## 7. Buyers in the Harbor

The trouble with being invisible, I've learned, is that it only works until someone decides to look.

I'd spent six years perfecting the art of not being seen. Casper Whitlock, salvage diver, daughter of a dead man with expensive habits. I kept my head down at the fuel dock, paid cash for diesel, nodded to the harbor master and never lingered. The *Kestrel* — my father's boat, my father's debt, my father's ghost in every rivet — sat at the end of the pier where the tourists didn't wander and the fishermen had long since stopped gossiping about her. Invisibility was a skill, like knot-tying or holding your breath. I was good at it.

Then came the man in the gray coat who ordered coffee he never drank.

I noticed him on Tuesday, three days after the cave, two days after Rennick's interview. He sat at the window table of the Salt & Battery — the only café in town pretentious enough to charge four dollars for what Marta Voss called "burnt water with ambitions" — and he read the same page of a newspaper for forty minutes. Nobody reads the shipping forecasts that slowly unless they're memorizing them.

I told myself it was coincidence. Gray Coat was a birdwatcher. Gray Coat was waiting for a ferry that didn't run on Tuesdays. Gray Coat was anyone but what he clearly was: someone watching me watch him through the reflection in the pastry case while pretending to deliberate between a cinnamon roll and a cardamom bun.

I bought neither. I walked out with my coffee in a paper cup and my heart going like a bilge pump with a cracked impeller.

* * *

Marta found me that afternoon at the fuel dock, where I was doing something useful with my hands so my brain couldn't keep replaying the compass needle swinging toward the glacier like it had somewhere urgent to be.

"You've got a shadow," she said, without preamble. Marta Voss did not believe in preamble. She believed in herring, hard weather, and saying the thing.

"I noticed."

"And you're standing here instead of leaving?"

"Leaving where?" I wiped diesel off my fingers. "He's not following me yet. He's *watching* me. There's a difference. One's a nuisance. The other's an invoice."

Marta leaned against the fuel pump, which she was allowed to do because she'd been leaning against things in this harbor since before the harbor master was born. "My brother used to say there are two kinds of men who come asking questions in coastal towns. The kind who want answers, and the kind who want confirmation." She looked out at the water, at the low gray swell beyond the breakwater, at the escarpment rising black and indifferent to the north. "The second kind are worse. They already know. They just want to know if *you* know they know."

"That's a lot of knowing."

"It's a lot of trouble," she corrected. "And you're swimming in it, girl. Whatever you pulled up off that wreck — whatever you saw when the tide went out — somebody else saw it too."

I said nothing, which is its own confession if you're talking to Marta Voss.

She pushed off from the pump. "Quill's boat came in this morning. You know the one. Black hull, no name painted, the kind of clean that costs money."

I knew it. Everyone knew it. It had been sitting out past the moorings for two days like a shark deciding whether the bay was worth the effort.

"Quill himself is ashore," Marta said. "Asking after you."

The coffee in my cup had gone cold, which felt symbolic. "What does a salvage broker want with me?"

"What do brokers always want?" Marta shrugged into her coat. "To buy low from people who don't know what they're holding. Be careful, Casper. Men like Quill don't knock. They just arrive, and afterward you count what's missing."

* * *

He found me at dusk, which I suppose he considered dramatic and I considered inconvenient, because dusk is when I check the *Kestrel*'s bilge and I resent interruptions to my suffering.

He was already aboard.

I want that understood: he was *already aboard*, sitting on my father's bench seat in the wheelhouse like he'd been invited, his coat folded beside him, his hands clasped, everything about him arranged. Quill was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, with the soft hands of a man who hadn't hauled a line in decades and the eyes of a man who'd watched plenty of other people haul them. He smiled the way appraisers smile.

"Casper Whitlock." He pronounced it carefully, like a word in a language he'd studied but didn't speak. "You have your father's shoulders."

"You have my father's bench seat. Get off it."

He laughed — a real laugh, warm even, which made it worse. "Direct. Good. I hate negotiating with polite people; they take forever to say no." He stood, unhurried, and gestured at the wheelhouse around us. "Fine boat. Older than me, I'd wager. Kept well, though. You can tell when a boat's loved versus maintained. This one's both."

"She's mortgaged to her rivets. Is that why you're here? To gloat? Because the bank does that for free."

"I'm here to do business." He reached into his coat — I shifted my weight toward the flare locker, old habit — and produced not a weapon but a photograph. He laid it on the chart table between us, face up, and smoothed it flat with two fingers.

It was a picture of the escarpment coastline taken from the water, telephoto, professional. And there, circled in grease pencil, was the sea cave. My sea cave. The one that had been sealed behind forty feet of ocean since before I was born, until the lowest tide in forty years peeled the water back like a bandage.

"Interesting geology," Quill said mildly. "Wouldn't you say?"

"Rocks are rocks."

"Mm." He tapped the circle once. "There are stories about this coast, Ms. Whitlock. Old ones. Fishermen's stories, which means they're ninety percent nonsense and ten percent true, and the ten percent is always the interesting part. A lost expedition. Government men. Something carried up the glacier that never came back down." His eyes came up to mine, pleasant and bottomless. "And now, in a year of freak tides, a cave opens that hasn't seen air since before those stories were written. You were diving the wreck offshore that same week. You were seen near the point at low water. And yesterday, a navy lieutenant flew into a town that hasn't seen a navy officer since the last war, asking questions about anomalies."

"I get asked about anomalies all the time," I said. "I'm a woman with a boat and debts. Practically a walking anomaly."

"Ms. Whitlock." He said my name again, softer now, the way you'd speak to a spooked animal or a slow client. "Let me save us both the theater. I'm not Rennick. I don't care about regulations, chain of custody, or whose flag flies over what. I care about objects. Rare ones. Recoverable ones. And I pay —" he named a number.

I'll be honest with you, because nobody else will be: the number hit me like cold water hitting the lungs. That number wasn't a fortune. It was several fortunes. It was the *Kestrel* owned outright, the creditors paid, the fuel dock's ledger closed, and a life after all this where I could dive for things I chose instead of things I owed. For one full second I stood there and let myself live in that number, warm as bathwater.

Which, I suspect, is exactly what Quill was counting on. He watched my face the way I watch a depth gauge.

"Anything recovered from the site," he continued smoothly. "Whatever it is. Metal, instruments, documents. Condition irrelevant. Provenance irrelevant. I handle disposal, I handle discretion, and you handle a bank account that stops being a source of nightmares." He smiled again. "You'd be free, Ms. Whitlock. That's what I'm actually selling. Not money. Freedom."

Here's the thing about freedom when someone offers it to you in a package: it isn't freedom. It's a lease with hidden terms.

"The cave was empty," I said.

It was the wrong thing to say, and I knew it was the wrong thing to say approximately half a syllable after I said it, because Quill's smile didn't move but something behind it did — a small recalibration, a gear engaging.

"Empty," he repeated.

"Tide pools and barnacles. I went for a look because the tide was historic and I'm a diver and divers look at things. There was nothing." I shrugged, and my shrug has been called many things by many people, none of them convincing. "If there'd been something, Lieutenant Rennick would have it, wouldn't he? Navy divers were all over that point within the day."

"Were they." Quill picked up his photograph, folded it along the grease-pencil circle, and put it away. "You know what I find interesting about you, Ms. Whitlock? Your file says you're careful. Six years of careful. No incidents, no disputes, no debts beyond the inherited kind. A woman who understands that the safest place in any storm is below notice." He collected his coat. "But careful people only lie badly when the stakes have gotten personal. And you, just now, lied like a woman with something personal in her pocket."

The compass sat in the false bottom of my tool locker not six feet from where he stood, and I became acutely aware of my own heartbeat and whether it could be heard.

"I think we got off on the wrong foot," I managed.

"We got off on the correct foot. You simply didn't like where it pointed." At the wheelhouse door he paused, and the last light off the water caught the silver at his temple. "The offer stands for seventy-two hours. After that, the price changes. Not down." He glanced back at the wheelhouse, at the bench seat, at some small detail I couldn't follow. "Your father was offered a similar arrangement once, you know. By different men. He also refused badly."

The wheelhouse got very quiet.

"He turned down money to protect something that was never his," Quill said, almost gently. "Sentimental men make excellent guardians and terrible businessmen. I do wonder which trait is hereditary." He stepped down onto the dock, straightened his coat, and added, without turning around: "Sleep well, Ms. Whitlock. And do check your mooring lines. Harbors are unpredictable places."

Then he was gone, walking up the pier at an unhurried pace, nodding to the fisherman mending nets as though they were old friends, and the fisherman nodded back, and I understood that Quill had been in this town for days and everyone knew him except me.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the dark of the wheelhouse with a mug of tea going cold and did the arithmetic of disaster.

Seventy-two hours. Then the price changes. Not down.

I thought about the compass under the tool locker floor, wrapped in an oilcloth, lying in the dark like a sleeping animal I'd agreed to feed. I thought about Rennick's interview, his soft questions about anomalies, the way he'd signed my father's death report with the same pen he probably used to sign lunch checks. Two predators now, circling the same wreck — one wearing a uniform, one wearing cologne — and me in the middle with a piece of brass that pointed at a glacier.

I should give it to one of them. That was the rational play. Give it to Rennick, take whatever mercy the navy dispenses, go back to diving scrap and hating every minute. Or call Quill, take the number, be free. Either hand would close over mine and either future would be decided by someone else, and God help me, both options looked easier than the third one, which was the one where I kept the compass and my mouth shut and waited to see which wolf got tired first.

The problem with the third option is that it requires believing you're smarter than wolves. I am not smarter than wolves. I am, at best, faster in water.

Around midnight I heard footsteps on the dock.

Not Quill's — lighter, wrong rhythm, pausing too often. I killed the wheelhouse lamp and moved to the window, and in the sodium glow of the harbor lights I watched a man walk the length of the pier past the *Kestrel*, slow, casual, looking at nothing, looking at everything. He stopped at the end of the dock. He lit a cigarette. And then he simply stood there, smoking, facing the water, for twenty minutes, in weather no one stands in for twenty minutes.

When he finally left, a second man took his place near the fuel shed.

They weren't watching the cave anymore. The tide had come back in; the cave was gone, sealed under the sea like it had never existed. They weren't watching the wreck; the navy had that stitched up tight. They were watching the only loose thread left on this entire coast.

They were watching me.

I sat back down in the dark and laughed — quietly, so as not to disturb the surveillance — because it was that or scream, and screaming attracts attention, and attention was the one thing I had left to lose. Six years of careful invisibility, undone in a week. My father's debts had made me poor, but this — this had made me *visible*, and visible, I was learning, is so much worse than broke. Broke, they send letters. Visible, they send men.

I got out the chart of the coast and spread it under the red of my night lamp, and I looked north, past the harbor, past the escarpment, to where the mountains went white and the glacier came down to meet the world. Somewhere up there was whatever the compass wanted. Somewhere up there was whatever my father had refused to sell and died protecting. And somewhere between here and there were seventy-two hours, two watchers, one lieutenant, and a broker whose price changes in directions I didn't want to imagine.

In the morning, Marta Voss knocked on my hull at an hour decent people consider still night, climbed aboard without invitation — apparently that runs in this town — and set a covered basket on my chart table.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Thank you. I've been practicing."

"Pack a bag," Marta said, and pulled the cloth off the basket, and underneath it wasn't food at all but a rolled oilskin map, soft with age, tied with string. "And listen carefully, because I'm only telling this story once, and it starts with my brother."
