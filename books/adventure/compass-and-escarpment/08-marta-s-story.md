## 8. Marta's Story

The oilskin map was older than my father's boat, and my father's boat had been old enough to have opinions about weather.

I untied the string with the care you'd give a thing that might bite. Inside was a survey chart of the escarpment range — hand-drawn, pre-war paper stock by the watermark, the kind of document that wasn't supposed to leave an archive. The glacier above Halden-at-the-Wall was inked in with a confidence the cartographer probably regretted later; half the icefall was annotated in pencil in at least three different hands, corrections layered over corrections like sediment.

And there, near the upper col, a small circle in red grease pencil, and beside it two words in a hand that shook: *they stopped here.*

"Whose map?" I asked.

Marta settled onto my bench like she'd been issued it at birth. She took a boiled egg out of her pocket, cracked it on the table edge, and began peeling it with the unhurried precision of a woman who intended to be comfortable for a while.

"The government's," she said. "Before they decided they'd never had one."

* * *

She told it the way old people tell stories in coastal towns — sideways, circling, making you work for the center of it. I learned more about herring quotas in 1951 than I strictly needed to. But I'd grown up around salvagers and sailors, and I knew the technique. You don't hand someone a secret. You let them dig for it, so they remember where they found it.

So I dug.

"Your brother," I said, when she paused for breath. "You said it starts with him."

"Aksel." She said the name the way you'd set something fragile down. "My younger brother. Big shoulders, terrible singing voice, convinced he was going to be a famous mountaineer because he'd climbed the church roof twice without dying." She ate a piece of egg. "He was twenty-three when he signed on with the Halvard Expedition."

"Halvard." I looked down at the chart. The name was stamped in the corner, faded but legible: *Halvard Range Survey — Joint Civil-Military Commission.*

"They told the town it was a mapping exercise," Marta said. "Twelve men. Scientists, mostly, a couple of army engineers who were supposedly there to drive supply sledges and couldn't have driven a sledge out of a barn. They went up onto the glacier in late summer, when the calving opens the high route — same window Eirik Solheim will tell you about if you ask him, though he'll charge you for the telling. And they were due back in six weeks."

"And?"

"And they came back in eleven weeks," Marta said, "as four men and a closed crate."

The cabin was quiet except for the water slapping the hull. I found I was holding the edge of the chart very still, as if movement might make her stop.

"The official report said crevasse field, bad weather, equipment losses," she went on. "Eight dead, presumed lost on the ice. No bodies recovered — which happens, up there, the glacier takes what it takes. The four survivors were flown out from the military airfield instead of coming home through the harbor, which the town noticed, because everything gets noticed here eventually, just slowly. And the crate went onto the plane with them, under guard, and nobody ever said what was in it."

"You think the eight didn't die of weather."

"I think," Marta said, "that my mother got a letter six months later. Army stationery. It expressed condolences again — the second set, mind you — and enclosed a payment, and asked her to sign a document confirming she would not inquire further into the circumstances of her son's death. My mother couldn't read well. She signed it because a priest told her it would bring peace." Her thumbnail tapped the table once, a small sharp sound. "It did not bring peace."

I thought about my father's last voyage. About the navy report that cleared it — the report whose signature I'd spent one long sleepless night memorizing. Different coast, different decade, possibly the same pen.

"What do you believe happened to them?" I asked.

Mara finished her egg and folded the shells into a scrap of newspaper with the tidiness of a woman raised to waste nothing, including grief.

"I believe the expedition found something they weren't supposed to find," she said. "Or were sent to find and then unfind. I believe some of those twelve men understood, partway up that mountain, exactly why the army engineers were carrying more rope than maps. And I believe Aksel — my loud, ridiculous, brave brother — was one of the ones who objected."

"Objected how?"

"There was a man who came back. Søren Lunde, the radio operator. He lived another thirty years in the next fjord over and drank himself through most of them, and toward the end, when he was past caring what the government thought or anyone else either, he talked. Not much. A few evenings' worth, to people he trusted, and my family was one." She looked out my porthole at the gray water. "He said the crate wasn't brought down from the mountain. He said the mountain refused to give it up. He said the eight who died didn't all die at once, and they didn't all die of the ice."

"That's not much to go on."

"It's more than the navy gave my mother," Marta said, "and she built forty years of Sundays on less."

* * *

She unrolled the chart fully across my table and weighted the corners with the egg shells' newspaper, a salt cellar, and my one clean mug. Her finger traced the route up — harbor, moraine, icefall, col — and stopped at the red circle.

"This is where the pencil says they stopped. This is where Søren said the argument happened. And this—" her finger moved downslope, west, to a fold of ice beneath the escarpment's shoulder, "—is where he said the rest of them turned. Off the mapped route. Carrying things."

"Things."

"Sledges," Marta said. "Søren said two sledges went west with six men and came back with none. He said the officers counted heads at the col and the arithmetic was wrong and everyone pretended it wasn't." She sat back. "Now. I'm an old woman who talks to fish for a living, so you can decide how much of that is memory and how much is thirty years of aquavit. But here's what I know that isn't story."

She leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the network of lines around her eyes, and for a moment she looked every one of her years and then some — a woman who had been carrying a specific weight for a very long time.

"I know that in the spring after the expedition, men came to this harbor in a navy launch. Civilians, they claimed, but they stood like navy. They surveyed the sea caves under the escarpment for three days. They lowered instruments into the water. And when they left, they left behind a notice posted at the harbormaster's office declaring the stretch of coast below the glacier a restricted zone, on account of — and I quote the notice, because my father kept a copy until the day he died — 'unstable geological conditions.'"

"Caves aren't unstable geology."

"No," Marta agreed. "They're caves. But a restricted zone keeps divers out, and fishermen honest, and questions unwelcome. And it has stayed restricted, off and on, under one legal pretext or another, for fifty years. Your classified wreck sits inside it. So does the cave the tide is going to show us tomorrow." She tapped the chart. "The government didn't bury its secret in the glacier, girl. It buried it in paperwork, and the paperwork points at the ice."

I sat with that. Outside, the tide was already beginning its long withdrawal — I could feel it in the way the boat sat, a little lighter, a little more list to starboard, the harbor bottom rising to meet us inch by inch. Tomorrow it would drop further than it had in forty years. Marta had told me that two days ago and I'd filed it under local color. Now it sounded like a schedule.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. It seemed like a fair question. We'd known each other a week. She'd fed me fish stew, called me too thin, and now she was handing me a fifty-year-old state secret over my own chart table.

Marta was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its storytelling cadence and gone plain, which from her was more intimate than any softness.

"Because tomorrow you're going to walk into that cave, or you're not. I've watched you since you tied up — watched you count coins, watch the navy launch, keep your mouth shut and your eyes working. You've got your father's face, you know. And his habit of standing slightly apart from a room as if measuring it for exits." She gathered the egg shells into her palm. "Aksel stood like that."

"I'm not your brother."

"No," she said. "But you're somebody's child, standing alone in a boat full of debts, about to do something dangerous for money because the world made the money necessary. That's not rare, Casper Whitlock. That's practically the town crest." She stood, joints announcing themselves, and took her basket. "I'm telling you because if you go into that cave knowing nothing, you'll come out of it owning a curiosity. If you go in knowing this, you'll come out owning a responsibility. And responsibilities are heavier, but they float better."

At the companionway she paused.

"One more thing. Søren Lunde, before the end — he drew something. A bearing, he called it. Said the mountain itself would point the way back to what they hid, if you had the instrument to read it. Everyone thought it was the drink talking." Her eyes came to mine and held there, steady as a fixed light. "Then yesterday, old Brenner tells me a stranger dove the restricted wreck and came up asking odd questions, and today a navy lieutenant is in the harbormaster's office asking about anomalies, and tonight I hear a broker named Quill has been buying rounds at the Anchor and asking who's new in town."

"How do you hear all this?"

"I'm seventy-four and I run a fish stall," Marta said. "I am the intelligence service of this entire coast." She pulled the door open onto the cold. "Whatever you found down there — and don't insult me by saying nothing — you should know that three kinds of people are already looking for it. The kind who'll pay. The kind who'll take. And me." She stepped down onto the dock. "I only want to know where my brother is lying, so I can put a stone where his mother could have put one. Remember that when you decide who to trust."

Then she was gone up the dock, small and straight-backed against the wind, and I was alone with a chart, a legend, and a compass in my seabag that I had told absolutely no one about.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I told myself it was the tide — the whole harbor groaning as the water fell away, boats settling onto mud they hadn't touched in decades, the strange exposed smell of the seabed at night, iodine and old rope. But it wasn't the tide.

I spread my father's charts on the table — the real ones, the ones I'd been comparing against the compass since the night I pried it out of the rock — and laid Marta's oilskin beside them. Two generations of ink. His annotations crept up the glacier's flank in his cramped, impatient hand: soundings converted to elevations, tide tables marked against dates, and along the western route below the col, a series of bearings written and crossed out and rewritten, converging year after year on a single figure.

The compass bore. To within a degree.

He hadn't stumbled onto the cave. He'd been hunting it — or hunting what the cave pointed to — for years, quietly, between salvage contracts, between debts, between the lies a man tells his daughter about why they can never quite get ahead. And he'd died on a navy wreck inside a restricted zone at the foot of a glacier his charts obsessively measured, and the officer who signed off on his death had come to my harbor asking about anomalies.

I thought about what Marta said. Responsibilities float better.

It was nearly three in the morning when I finally blew out the lamp, and in the dark I heard the harbor exhale — the long, low suck of water pulling back and back and back, further than I'd ever heard it go, the seabed emerging into moonlight that had no business touching it.

Through the porthole I could see the escarpment, black against the stars, and the pale line of the glacier above it, and somewhere between the two, a cave that had been keeping a secret since before I was born, opening its mouth for the first time in forty years.

Tomorrow, three kinds of people would be looking.

I decided I'd rather be the one who arrived first.
