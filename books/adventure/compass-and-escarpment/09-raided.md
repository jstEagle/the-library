## 9. Raided

I want it on the record that I did not sleep well. Not because of premonition — I don't believe in premonitions, I believe in weather and arithmetic — but because of a sound. Somewhere past midnight, out in the harbor, an engine idled too long at anchor. Boats do that sometimes. Engines are moody things. But this one held its note like a man holding his breath, and then it went away, and that was worse.

I lay in my bunk aboard the *Kestrel's Wake* — my father's boat, my father's debt, my father's name still lettered on the stern under three coats of someone else's paint — and I listened to the water slap the hull and told myself the story I always tell: *You're nobody, Casper. Nobody watches nobody.*

The compass sat in the bilge pump housing, wrapped in oilcloth, where I'd stowed it after comparing it to Dad's charts. I'd had four days to hand it over to Rennick. Four days to sell it to Quill. Four days to throw it into deep water off the escarpment and let the sea keep its secrets the way it keeps everything else.

Instead I'd slept with it twelve feet below my spine.

That tells you something about me, though I'm not sure what. Stubbornness, probably. Or the particular stupidity of people who inherit questions instead of answers.

* * *

They came at low tide, which was either poetic or practical, and with Quill's crew I'd bet on practical every time.

I'd gone up to Marta Voss's house before dawn to return a borrowed net-mending awl, and I was standing in her kitchen drinking coffee that could strip varnish when her grandson Tomas came through the door without knocking, which he never did.

"Your boat," he said. "There's men on your boat."

Marta set down her cup with a click that sounded like a decision being made. "How many?"

"Three I can see. One on the dock walking a slow patrol. Big fellow, gray coat."

Quill's colors. Of course they were. Quill himself wouldn't come — Quill never dirtied his hands with anything heavier than a pen — but his men moved like they'd been briefed by someone who'd read my file, if I had a file, which apparently I now did.

"They'll have broken the cabin lock already," I said. My voice came out flat, which surprised me. I'd expected fear. What I got instead was something colder and more useful, a kind of inventory-taking calm, as if I were assessing a wreck from above before deciding what was worth diving for. "The chart table drawer sticks. They'll think there's nothing in it. The compass isn't in the cabin anyway."

"You put it ashore?" Marta asked.

"No." I looked at her, and she looked back, and I watched her understand. Her eyes went narrow and then, oddly, amused.

"The bilge housing," she said. "My husband used to hide his tobacco money from himself in the float switch box. Said if he couldn't find it, neither could anyone else."

"Tobacco money," said Tomas, "is not a brass instrument that points at glaciers."

"No," Marta agreed. "But stupid hiding places work precisely because they're stupid. Smart searchers skip them." She turned to me. "What will you do?"

And there it was, the question, arriving ahead of schedule. Because here's the thing about ransacking a person's boat: it ends a conversation. Up until this morning I could still pretend I was deciding whether to give the compass back, sell it, or sink it. I was a woman weighing options. Now I was a woman whose options were being weighed for her by men with gray coats and crowbars.

"I'm going to go down there," I said, "and look surprised."

"Casper—"

"Marta. If I run, they know I have it. If I fight, they know I have it. If I walk down that dock looking annoyed about my ruined lock like any other diver with bad luck, maybe they leave believing they missed it." I drained the coffee. It tasted like punishment. "Then I decide what comes next."

Tomas shifted by the door. He was nineteen and built like a bollard, and he had his grandmother's habit of going very still when he was thinking hard. "I can come down behind you," he offered. "Just to stand around."

"No." I said it more sharply than I meant to. "If this goes wrong, it goes wrong for me. You two stay clear of it. Please."

Marta looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her nod once, slow, like a verdict. "Go, then. And Casper — boats can be replaced."

"That boat is all I have left of him."

"Aye," she said quietly. "I know. But you're not. Go."

* * *

The dock boards were slick with morning frost, and I made myself walk them slowly, boots ringing, hands in pockets, a woman with nothing on her mind but the cost of a new hasp.

They'd done tidy work, Quill's men. The cabin door hung open on splintered wood, the lock punched clean through rather than picked — no patience in these ones, or else a schedule. Inside, my father's cabin had been turned inside out with a thoroughness that hurt worse than violence would have. Charts unrolled and dropped. His old logbooks fanned across the bunk like dead birds. The tin where I kept spare shackles upended in the sink. Someone had even opened the icebox and stared at my butter, apparently suspecting it of conspiracy.

The big one in the gray coat stood at the chart table, and the third man — young, wiry, gloves despite the cold — was on his knees going through the lazarette.

"Morning," I said.

Three heads turned with the smooth synchronization of men who'd rehearsed not being startled. The big one recovered first. He had a face like a closed fist and the polite emptiness of hired muscle.

"Miss Whitlock."

"That's mine you're standing in."

"Mr. Quill extends his apologies for the intrusion." He didn't apologize. He stated the apology the way you state coordinates. "There was concern that certain items recovered from the wreck site might be... mishandled. Mr. Quill is offering protection of assets."

"Protection." I stepped into the cabin, over the threshold, into the smell of my own disrupted life — diesel, old paper, my father's pipe tar soaked so deep in the woodwork that no amount of sanding would ever kill it. "This is protection."

"An unfortunate necessity." His eyes moved over my face, reading it. I gave him annoyance, and only annoyance, the way you'd serve a plain meal. "We'll replace the lock. And the door."

"Do that." I started gathering charts off the bunk, squaring their edges with exaggerated care, because busy hands are honest hands. "Find anything interesting?"

"Nothing of value."

"Well." A laugh, dry as rust. "Welcome to salvage."

The wiry one by the lazarette glanced up at his boss, and something passed between them — a question asked and answered. Had we checked everywhere? Everywhere that mattered. And that was the whole gamble, right there, playing out in a glance: smart men skip stupid places. The bilge pump housing was under the floor plates, in the lowest, filthiest corner of the hull, bolted shut, smelling of grease. No one hides treasure in a sump. Treasure hides in false drawers and hollow masts and chart tubes. I knew because I'd spent my childhood finding my father's hiding places, and they were all clever, and every clever one had been found this morning.

He'd taught me that too, without meaning to. The last lesson of a careful man: *the best place is the one that insults you.*

"Miss Whitlock." The big one hadn't moved. "One more matter. Mr. Quill asks — respectfully — whether anything was recovered from the cave during the low tide."

Here it was. I let my hands stop moving. I let a silence stretch just long enough to be human.

"What cave?"

His expression didn't change, which was itself an answer. "The cave beneath the escarpment. Exposed by the spring tide. Local children talk about it."

"Local children talk about everything." I resumed squaring charts. "I dive wrecks. Wrecks pay. Caves full of wet rock pay nothing. If Quill wants to fund an expedition to look at barnacles, he should take it up with the tourist board."

He watched me a moment longer. Then he nodded to the others, and they filed out past me, close enough that I caught cheap wool and cigarette smoke, and the big one paused at the broken door.

"We'll send a carpenter."

"Don't bother." I heard my own voice drop, and I let it. "Send money. For the lock, the door, and the hour of my life you've cost me. Quill knows my rates."

Something flickered behind his eyes — respect, maybe, or the professional's appreciation for another professional — and then he was gone, boots heavy on the dock, and I stood alone in the wreck of my cabin listening to their launch pull away from the pier.

I waited until the engine faded. Then I sat down on my father's bunk, among his scattered logbooks, and shook. Not much. Just enough. Hands, mostly. Fear arrives late with me; it always has. It stands outside the door while the work is happening and slips in afterward, cold and punctual, and sits down heavily and says, *well?*

Well.

They hadn't found it. But they'd come, which meant Quill believed it existed, which meant someone had talked — Rennick's divers, or a loose tongue at the harbor, or Quill simply reading the same tide tables everyone else could read and doing the same arithmetic. It didn't matter. The result was the same: I was no longer a woman who might have something. I was a woman who was believed to have something, and belief, in my experience, is far more dangerous than fact. Facts can be denied. Belief gets people searched, followed, and eventually introduced to the inside of a hold.

And Rennick. I hadn't forgotten Rennick. He'd interviewed me with those patient gray eyes and asked about anomalies, and I'd lied to him smoothly, and he'd signed the report that cleared my father's last voyage — the report that said *no mechanical fault, no negligence*, the report that let the navy wash its hands while my mother sold the house to cover the inquiry costs. If Rennick learned the compass existed, he wouldn't offer to buy it. He'd requisition it. And requisitioned things, in my family's experience, came with requisitioned silences attached.

Two hunters. One compass. One of me.

I got up and began to pack.

* * *

Packing a life is a strange exercise. Everything I owned fit into two duffel bags and a tool roll, and most of what I owned turned out to be equipment, because I'd spent six years converting grief into gear. Dive masks. Spools of line. The good knife. Spare regulators. Photographs — three, that was all, Dad at the helm, Dad laughing at some joke the camera missed, Dad and me aged nine on his shoulders at the regatta, both of us sunburnt and immortal.

The rest — the furniture, the spare anchor, the winter tarp, the books — stayed. Things aren't lives. I knew that. I repeated it to myself like a catechism while I pried up the floor plates in the stern and knelt in the bilge slime and pulled the oilcloth bundle from the pump housing.

The compass sat in my palm, warm from the engine heat seeping through the hull, absurdly small for the amount of trouble it was causing. Brass case, glass clouded with age, needle swinging in its lazy impossible arc toward the glacier whenever I tilted it north. Toward the ice. Away from every map ever printed.

Dad, what did you get us into?

The needle swung. It didn't answer. Compasses never do; that's the deal. They tell you where to go and leave the why entirely up to you.

I rewrapped it and stowed it in the bottom of my tool roll, under the spanners, because if I was going to carry my whole inheritance on my back it might as well travel incognito.

Then I did the thing I'd been circling since dawn, the thing that had been waiting for me at the end of every thought like a reef at the edge of a channel.

I opened the fuel line to the engine compartment and cracked the valve on the spare tank, and I let diesel pool in the bilge where it had leaked a hundred times before, innocently, over years, so that the smell of it lived in the wood grain itself. Anyone investigating later would find what they expected to find: an old boat, badly maintained, a tired owner, an accident. The sea is full of burned-out hulks. Nobody investigates the sea.

I'm not going to pretend my hands were steady. I'm not going to pretend I didn't stand in that cabin for a long time, breathing the pipe-tar smell, touching the worn spot on the chart table where his elbow had rubbed the varnish pale over thirty years of plotting courses he never finished.

But here's what I understood, kneeling there: they had already taken the boat from me. Quill's men had turned it into evidence, Rennick had turned it into leverage, the creditors had turned it into collateral. The *Kestrel's Wake* wasn't a home anymore; it was a trap with a hull. Every night I slept aboard, I slept inside the search radius of every man hunting me. The only way to keep anything of my father's was to stop keeping the thing that bore his name.

So I took the photographs, and the journals I'd salvaged from the floor, and the compass, and I struck the match.

Diesel doesn't catch pretty. It catches *all at once*. By the time I reached the head of the dock the smoke was standing up off the stern in a black column, and the first flames were licking through the cabin windows, and somewhere behind me a harbor alarm began to ring, thin and outraged.

I didn't run. Running draws eyes. I walked — fast, purposeful, a woman late for an appointment — and I let the fire announce me instead. Let them find the ashes and argue over them. Let Quill wonder whether the compass had gone up with the boat, and let Rennick wonder harder, because Rennick was smarter and would take longer to stop wondering.

Let my old life burn. It had been drowning for years anyway. I just declined to go down with it.

* * *

Marta was waiting at the top of the seawall with a canvas pack already packed — food, flasks, wool — because of course she was. She'd known before I had. Women like Marta Voss don't wait for events; they simply arrive slightly ahead of them and pretend otherwise.

"The mountain road," she said, not a question.

"The mountain road."

"There's a bus at noon as far as the quarry turnoff. After that you walk." She pressed the pack against my chest and held it there a beat longer than necessary, her hands rough and certain on my shoulders. "Halden-at-the-Wall. Ask for Eirik Solheim at the guide house. Tell him I sent you, and tell him—" she paused, and something old moved through her face like weather, "—tell him his father's name is spoken kindly in my house."

I wanted to ask. There was no time to ask. Behind us the fire crews' bells were multiplying, and down the hill a gray-coat figure had stopped at the corner and was watching the smoke with his head tilted, doing arithmetic of his own.

"One more thing." Marta dug in her apron pocket and pressed something small and cold into my palm: a key on a leather fob, worn smooth. "My brother's. He kept a room in Halden, before the expedition. The landlady's daughter still holds it. Say it's from Voss." Her voice didn't break. It just went quiet, the way deep water is quiet. "Bring me back what he left there, if there's anything. Or don't bring anything, and tell me the room still exists. Either will do."

I closed my fist around the key. "Marta—"

"Go." She pushed me, actually pushed me, toward the road. "The tide's turning, girl. It always does. Be somewhere higher when it does."

So I went. Down the coast road with the smoke rising behind me and the compass riding silent against my hip, pointing, always pointing, at the white wall of the range where the glacier held whatever my father had died keeping secret.

Behind me: debt, ashes, a harbor full of watchers, and a lieutenant who signed reports over drowned men.

Ahead of me: snow, and a village I'd never seen, and a pass that wouldn't open for weeks.

It was, I reflected, boarding the bus with two duffel bags and a tool roll, the worst plan I'd ever had.

It was also, for the first time in six years, entirely my own.

The bus pulled out. In the window's reflection the smoke column bent eastward on the wind, toward the sea, away from the mountains — as if even the burning had decided which direction the future lay.

I didn't look back again.
