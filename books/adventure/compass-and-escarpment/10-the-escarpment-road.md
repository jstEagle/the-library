## 10. The Escarpment Road

The bus that climbed out of the coast town cost me the last of my cash and most of my dignity, because I spent four hours wedged against a window with a crate of live hens who had opinions about my elbow. The road switchbacked up through pine and then scrub and then nothing much at all, until the escarpment itself rose on our left like a wall somebody had built out of spite and granite, and the driver said, without turning around, "Halden-at-the-Wall. Last stop before you need wings."

I got off with a duffel bag, a brass compass wrapped in a wool sock, and a face I'd been practicing in the bus window — the face of a woman with nowhere particular to be and no story worth asking about.

The village sat in the fold between mountain and sky like a coin dropped in a pocket. Grey stone roofs, smoke standing straight up in the cold air, a church spire wearing its lightning rod like a hatpin. Maybe two hundred people, maybe fewer. The kind of place where a stranger is weather: noticed, discussed, eventually absorbed or blown through.

I'd never been here. But my father's charts had been here, in their way — his annotations climbed this valley in pencil, small and certain, the way he wrote about things he trusted. *Halden. Ask for Solheim.* That was all it said. Ask for Solheim. No explanation of who Solheim was or why a man who trusted nobody would leave me an instruction like a breadcrumb.

I hadn't decided yet whether to follow it. First I needed food, and food needed a plan, and plans were easier on an empty stomach than they should be.

The harbor — if you could call six moorings and a slipway a harbor — held a dozen boats, half of them hauled out for winter already. Snowline was high on the peaks but coming down week by week, patient as a creditor. I stood at the slipway edge doing arithmetic I didn't like when a boy of maybe fifteen came down the ramp carrying a coil of rope and stopped dead, staring at me with the frank rudeness of the young.

"You're not from here," he said.

"Sharp eyes. You'll go far."

"What's wrong with your hands?"

I looked at them. Rope burns, salt cracks, one nail gone black where Quill's man had stomped a crate shut on my fingers while I was pretending to be somewhere else. "Occupational," I said. "I'm a diver."

That changed his face entirely, which interested me. In the coast towns, saying *diver* got you pity or suspicion. Here it got me something closer to hunger.

"Tomas!" a woman called from up the lane, and the boy went, but backward, watching me the whole way, like I might do something worth seeing if he blinked.

* * *

The inn was called the Kettle, which turned out to be accurate in every respect except comfort. The woman behind the bar — broad, grey-braided, with forearms that suggested she'd arm-wrestled the escarpment and won on points — looked me over the way Marta Voss used to look over fish at market: for quality, for rot, for what might be hiding inside.

"Room's forty a night," she said. "Meals extra."

"I have eleven."

She waited. She was good at waiting. I got the feeling the whole village was good at waiting, the way people are who live somewhere winter takes nine months to say what it means.

"I'm a salvage diver," I said. "Twenty years' experience, commercial certs, my own gear. I hear your water intake runs off the tarn above the village, and I hear it's been silted since the spring flood, and I hear you've been hauling buckets up that hill ever since because none of your men will dive water that cold."

"Who told you that?"

"The boy. Indirectly. He told me I wasn't from here, and then he looked at the intake pipe on the hillside the way you look at a tooth that needs pulling."

The barwoman's mouth did something that in a softer climate might have become a smile. "Eirik won't allow it. He says the current under the intake will pin a diver and drown her, and he's usually right about the mountain."

"He's probably right about the mountain," I agreed. "But I'm not offering to fight the mountain. I'm offering to fix a pipe. Eleven crowns, a room, and meals until it's done. If I fail, you're exactly where you started, minus eleven crowns and plus a drowned stranger, which I'm told improves the soil."

That did get a smile, brief as a fish flashing in dark water. "You'll want to talk to Solheim anyway," she said. "Everyone who wants anything from this valley talks to Solheim eventually." She slid a bowl of stew across the bar, unasked, unpaid. "Eat first. Negotiating hungry makes people agreeable, and I don't trust agreeable strangers."

I ate. It was lamb and barley and something rooty, and it was the best thing I'd put in my mouth since the last meal Marta Voss had bullied into me on the dock, and I found my eyes stinging over the steam, which was embarrassing, so I blamed the pepper and kept my head down.

Marta. I'd left her a note that said *gone north, don't wait up,* which was the coward's version of goodbye, and I knew it, and I'd written it anyway because the true version — *they burned my boat, they know about the compass, I'm going up the glacier to finish what Dad started* — would have made her either stop me or come with me, and both options ended with Marta dead in a snowfield. Some inheritances you carry alone until you find someone strong enough to share the weight. I was hoping Halden might be that someone. Hoping is a bad strategy. It was the only one I had.

* * *

Eirik Solheim found me before I found him, which I suppose is what guides are for.

I was on the slipway at dawn, checking the intake line with binoculars borrowed off the bar wall, when a man came down the ramp with the unhurried gait of someone who has never once been late for anything because everything waits for him. Mid-forties, maybe. Weathered to the color of the rock behind him. Eyes pale as glacial melt and about as warm.

"You're the diver," he said.

"Casper Whitlock."

"Eirik Solheim." He didn't offer a hand, and I noted that; men who work ropes judge hands before they shake them, and mine currently looked like a losing hand. "Greta says you want to dive the intake."

"Greta says you say it'll kill me."

"It killed Sven Aune's cousin in '09. Not the cold. The current. Water goes over that sill and funnels under the pipe, and it holds you there like a hand. We tried three times that spring. Lost a line, nearly lost a man." He looked at the tarn road, then back at me. "You've read the water?"

"From here? No. Give me an hour on the sill and I'll tell you whether you're right."

"And if I am?"

"Then I'll tell you how to beat it anyway, and you can decide whether to believe me."

Something moved behind those pale eyes — not warmth, exactly. Interest. The look of a man finding an unfamiliar knot and wanting to see how it's tied. "You've dived current like that?"

"Dived under a wreck that was rolling in surge, inside a cargo hold, on a hook line, at night, to cut loose a case I wasn't supposed to be able to lift." All true. None of it wise to say. I said it anyway, flatly, because I was tired of lying to everyone and this man looked like the kind who could smell a lie through neoprene. "I'm good at my job, Mr. Solheim. My job is going into places that don't want me and coming back out."

"Why?" he asked. Just that. Why.

And there it was, the question I'd been dodging since the cave, since the compass, since the night I watched my father's boat burn at her moorings and understood that the past doesn't stay sunk just because you weight it down. I could have lied. I was, by then, quite good at it.

"My father dove these waters," I said instead, nodding south, toward the coast we'd both come from in different directions. "He charted this range. His notes say *Halden, ask for Solheim.* That's the whole reason I'm standing on your slipway freezing."

Eirik went very still. Stillness, on him, was louder than motion. "Your father's name."

"Jonas Whitlock."

He turned away and looked up the valley, at the glacier headwall showing white between the peaks, and for a long moment the only sound was the intake stream falling over rocks and a raven making its opinion known from the church roof.

"Come to my house at noon," he said finally. "Bring the charts."

* * *

Here is what I learned later, though I only saw pieces of it that morning, standing in Greta's kitchen being fed a second breakfast I hadn't earned:

Eirik Solheim had watched the road from the coast for three days, because Tomas had run up from the harbor saying a woman with burned hands and a diver's walk had gotten off the bus, and Eirik had spent thirty years watching that road for exactly one kind of stranger. He'd lost his father to the glacier when he was nine — the expedition, the one the valley didn't discuss above a whisper, the one whose names weren't carved anywhere because the government men who came afterward had made it clear that carving them would be remembered as ingratitude. He'd grown up in a house with an empty chair and a mother who set out coffee for a ghost every morning until the morning she died, and he'd become a guide because the ice had taken something from him and he intended, in some inarticulate way he'd never have admitted to anyone, to take it back.

When Greta told him the stranger's name was Whitlock, he'd had to sit down.

Because there was a Whitlock in the story the old ones told quietly, the story of the lost expedition. A young navy diver, attached to the support vessel, who'd turned back at the icefall with the wounded and carried down the journals. Who'd sworn to the dying expedition leader that the thing they'd buried would stay buried. Who'd vanished afterward into the coast towns and silence, and whom the valley had cursed and thanked in equal measure for thirty years, depending on who was drinking.

Eirik had grown up hearing that name too.

Neither of us knew any of this yet, standing in his kitchen at noon with my father's charts spread across a table scarred by fifty years of knives and maps. He looked at the penciled annotations — *Halden, ask for Solheim* — and I watched a man decide something large and slow, the way a serac decides, invisibly, over years, and then comes down all at once.

"These bearings," he said, tapping the compass rose my father had drawn freehand, tilted a few degrees off true north, toward the icefall. "You know what they point at."

"I know what a compass in a sea cave points at," I said. "Whether that's the same thing, I'm hoping you'll tell me."

"And if I tell you, what will you do?"

"Go there."

"Alone?"

"If I have to."

He almost smiled. It was like watching a crack form in rock. "They always say that. The ones who go alone die alone, and the ones who say *if I have to* mean *preferably.*" He rolled up the charts and handed them back, and his voice changed — became the voice, I'd learn, he used for clients on the moraine, the one that permitted no argument because it wasn't arguing, it was simply describing the future. "The pass doesn't open until calving season. Four weeks, if the winds are kind. Five if they aren't. Nobody climbs the icefall before it shifts — not me, not you, not any fool Quill sends up the other side."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards. "How do you know that name?"

"Quill's broker has a cousin in the valley, and the cousin likes money more than he likes us. Word came two days ago. A salvage crew staging at the far side of the pass, buying gear, hiring local porters." He let that land. "So you brought them with you, Ms. Whitlock. Whatever's under that ice, you didn't come alone, and neither did it."

I thought of my boat burning. Of the sock-wrapped compass against my ribs, ticking its needle toward the glacier even now, faithful as a dog. Of Rennick, somewhere behind me, patient as winter.

"Then we'd better be faster than they are," I said.

"We," Eirik repeated. He studied me for a long moment, and whatever he was weighing, it wasn't my climbing résumé. It was something older and heavier, some debt or promise written in a dead man's handwriting. "Fix the intake first. Prove you can do what you say you do. Then we'll talk about *we*."

* * *

I dove the intake the next morning, and it tried to kill me exactly as advertised.

The current took me at the sill like a hand closing, and for ninety seconds I hung in the funnel with the roar in my ears and my knife out, cutting the silted screen free while the water tried to fold me under the pipe, and I came up gasping into sunlight with the screen in one hand and blood thinning pink from my knuckles, and the whole village was on the bank — I counted later, forty-one people, including Greta and Tomas and a very old woman who informed me I swam like her late husband, which I chose to take as praise.

By evening the taps ran clear in Halden-at-the-Wall for the first time since spring, and I stood in Greta's doorway listening to the sound of it — water hitting basins, children shrieking under it, ordinary domestic noise — and felt something in my chest unclench that I hadn't known was clenched.

It wasn't payment. It wasn't gratitude, exactly. It was the sound of having been useful somewhere, and the somewhere noticing.

Greta pressed coins into my hand — real wages, more than eleven crowns' worth — and said, "Solheim wants you at his house at seven. Bring your gear inventory." And Tomas walked me partway up the lane, bursting with questions about wrecks and sharks and whether I'd ever found treasure, and I answered honestly — yes, once, and it was the worst thing I ever found — and he laughed like I'd joked, and I let him.

At Eirik's door, I paused. Through the window I could see the empty chair at his table, and the coffee cup beside it, and I understood, suddenly and completely, whose house this was and what I'd walked into.

I knocked anyway.

The mountains had my father's secret, and Quill had my scent, and Rennick had my name, and the snows were coming down the valleys like a deadline. But for one breath, standing on a stone step in a village that had watched me work and decided I belonged to it — just one breath —

I knocked, and the door opened, and Eirik Solheim said, "Sit down, Whitlock. You're going to need to hear what happened on that glacier thirty years ago. All of it. Before you climb one step higher."
