## 11. Eirik's Terms

Eirik Solheim's house sat at the end of the village like a full stop at the end of a sentence nobody wanted to finish. I found it by following the smell of woodsmoke and the sound of a whetstone against steel, which turned out to be the same sound, if you listened long enough, as patience being sharpened into a weapon.

He opened the door before I knocked. They do that here, I was learning — answer questions you haven't asked yet, notice things you thought were invisible. It was disorienting for someone whose entire survival strategy had been built on not being noticed.

"You're the diver," he said. Not a question either.

"I'm Casper Whitlock."

"Whitlock." He rolled it around like a stone in his mouth, tasting something. "Your father came through here once. Years ago. Bought rope and asked too many questions about the icefall."

The floor did not move, but I felt it move anyway.

"He didn't," I said, which was true, and also a lie, because the man Eirik described sounded exactly like my father, and my father had told me he'd never been north of the shipping lanes.

Eirik studied me with the unhurried attention of a man who reads weather for a living and finds people only slightly more complicated. "Come in or don't. The cold doesn't care which."

I went in.

* * *

His house smelled of paraffin, wool, and old coffee. Maps everywhere — papered over the walls like wallpaper, layered like sediment, decades of them, each one annotated in a small precise hand. The glacier route was drawn and redrawn so many times the ink had worn grooves in the paper.

I put the compass on his table between us.

I hadn't planned to. My hand just did it, the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. And I watched Eirik look at it — brass case, clouded glass, needle swinging with that slow wrong insistence toward the escarpment's white shoulder — and I watched him not touch it.

"That's not pointing north," he said.

"No."

"It never has?"

"Not once since I pulled it out of the cave."

He sat down across from me, slowly, the way men sit down when they've decided something. "Marta sent you."

"Marta mentioned you were the only guide who'd take the high route."

"Marta mentions a lot of things." He leaned back. "She also mentioned you'd come asking about the glacier eventually. Two years ago, she said. She was off by two years and one dead father, but her aim's still better than most rifles."

I didn't say anything to that. There wasn't anything to say that wouldn't crack something open, and I wasn't ready to crack open in front of a stranger, even a stranger who kept maps of my grief on his walls without knowing it.

"The compass," he said. "Where did you get it?"

"A cave below the escarpment. The lowest tide in forty years exposed it. I went in before the water came back."

"Alone?"

"Alone."

He nodded slowly, and I couldn't tell if the nod meant approval or diagnosis. With guides, I'd learn, it's usually both.

"And now you want to go up there." He tapped the table where the compass sat, its needle straining toward the ice like a dog at the end of a chain. "Toward whatever that thing is pointing at."

"I want to know why my father's handwriting is on charts that match its bearing. I want to know what he knew, and why he died keeping it quiet, and why the navy paid me to dive a wreck they pretend doesn't exist, and why an officer named Rennick signed the report that cleared his last voyage and then showed up asking me questions with eyes like a man counting money in a dark room." I heard my own voice climb and made myself stop. "Yes. I want to go up there."

Eirik was quiet for a long time. Outside, wind worried the eaves. Somewhere a dog gave up on barking at nothing.

"No," he said.

* * *

I should have expected it. Everything in this village worked on delay — the mail, the ferry, the seasons, the answers. But I'd walked up a mountain road on blistered feet carrying everything I owned in a waterproof bag, and I had run out of patience somewhere around the second switchback.

"Why not? You guide the route. Marta says you're the best—"

"Marta says a lot of things." He stood and went to the wall of maps, and I realized he wasn't looking at any of them; he was looking through them, at the mountain itself, the way you look through a photograph at the person who took it. "You want to climb in three weeks' time. Maybe four. The pass is shut. The icefall is compressed — frozen solid, no movement, no way through. The route opens during calving season, when warm winds come off the sea and the glacier starts shedding weight. Then the ice moves, cracks, shifts, and for a few days there's a window. A door that opens maybe twice a year and stays open long enough to regret it."

"So we wait for the window."

"We wait for the window." He turned around. "And while we wait, the snows close in. Storm season. Whiteouts that erase the difference between sky and ground until you can't tell if you're standing on the world or falling through it. People die out there in good conditions. In bad ones they die faster and are found later, sometimes much later, sometimes as a shape in the ice that their grandchildren come to see."

"That's very encouraging."

"It's free." He sat back down. "Encouragement costs extra."

I almost smiled. Almost. "Then teach me. Train me. Whatever the waiting requires. I'm not asking you to carry me up there."

"No," he agreed. "You're asking me to walk in front of you onto a glacier that eats people, following a needle that points the wrong direction, because your dead father left you a riddle and a debt and a navy officer who wants what you have." He folded his hands. "From where I sit, that's not a client. That's a weather system."

* * *

I got up to leave, because pride is cheaper than lodging and I had almost none of either. At the door, his voice stopped me.

"Show me your hands."

I turned. He nodded at them, so I held them out — diver's hands, scarred at the knuckles, nails cut short, the pale line where a watch used to be and the deeper lines where rope had taught me humility.

"Earned," he said, mostly to himself. "Not gymnasium hands. Working hands." He looked up. "Why diving? Why salvage?"

"Because it pays. Because I'm good at it. Because my father's boat was all he left me besides the debts, and boats need divers the way churches need sinners."

"What's the deepest you've gone?"

"Ninety meters. Air, not mixed gas. On a wreck off the southern capes, current running, visibility like tea."

"Alone?"

"With a tender above. Mostly alone."

He grunted. It might have been approval. It might have been heartburn. "And cold water?"

"Cold enough to make peace with God and renegotiate on the way up."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one.

"Sit down, Whitlock."

* * *

He laid out terms the way he probably laid out ropes — deliberately, with knots I wouldn't see until it mattered.

"One: we leave when the calving season opens the pass. Not before. If you ask me to leave early, I'll say no, and if you hire another guide to go early, I'll attend your funeral and complain about the flowers.

"Two: you train. Every day. Crampons, rope, crevasse rescue, self-arrest, how to read the ice, how to read me. You'll hate it. That's how you'll know it's working.

"Three: what's up there—" he touched the compass, one finger, briefly, like testing a stove, "—whatever it is, we decide together what happens to it. On the mountain, my word is final on matters of safety. Yours is final on matters of the object. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Four." He paused, and this pause had weight in it, ballast. "Tell me about your father. All of it. Before we rope together. I don't climb with strangers, and I especially don't climb with people who are strangers to themselves."

The room got very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"My father drowned eight years ago," I said. "Officially. His boat was found empty, engines running, forty miles offshore. The navy report cleared it as equipment failure. The officer who signed it was named Rennick. Last month I dove a classified wreck for the navy, and Rennick was the liaison, and he paid me triple rate to be quiet about it, and then he started asking questions about anomalies near the site, and then a broker named Quill offered me a fortune for anything from a cave I never mentioned to anyone, which means everyone already knows about the cave, which means the wreck, the cave, the compass, and my father's death are all the same story wearing different coats." I took a breath. "And yesterday I found his annotations on charts of this exact glacier, matching the bearing this needle won't drop. So either my father lied to me his whole life, or he was protecting me from something worth lying about. I've stopped caring which. I just want the truth, and I want it before Rennick or Quill gets there first."

Eirik listened without moving. When I finished, he looked at the compass for a long moment.

"My father was on the lost expedition," he said quietly. "Thirty years ago. Nine went up. None came down. The government called it bad weather and buried the reports." His jaw worked. "I've climbed that route eleven times looking for them. Found camps, rope fragments, a boot. Never them. Never the thing they carried."

Now it was my turn to be silent.

"So," he said, and the word had the finality of a carabiner locking shut. "We wait for the window. We train. And when the ice opens, we go up together — you for your father, me for mine." He extended his hand across the table, calloused palm, grip like a mooring cleat. "Those are my terms, Whitlock. Take them or find a fool with a rope."

Outside, the first flakes of the season's real snow were coming down, fat and slow, like the sky deciding something.

I took his hand.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep. I lay in the loft above his workshop — he'd given it to me without being asked, the way this village did everything, generously and without warning — listening to the house tick and settle, watching snow accumulate on the window ledge like a debt growing interest.

Weeks. He wanted weeks. And every one of those weeks was a week Rennick could spend finding me, a week Quill's men could spend driving mountain roads, a week the compass could spend pointing at the glacier through the roof of Eirik's house like a lighthouse for anyone who cared to look up.

I got up and checked it. The needle swung, steadied, strained — southeast, toward the icefall, patient as gravity.

Through the frost-feathered glass, the escarpment was a blacker black against the night, and above it, invisible but absolutely present, the glacier held whatever it held.

Somewhere down the valley, headlights swept the switchbacks. One vehicle. Slow. Stopping at the village edge, where no one stops at this hour unless they're lost, or lying, or looking for someone.

I watched the lights die behind the church.

Then came the knock — not at Eirik's door, but at Marta's, three houses down. And Marta's porch light snapped on, and her voice carried through the cold the way old voices do when they want to be heard:

"Well. The navy finally found our road."

I dressed in the dark, compass in my pocket, and went down the stairs two at a time.
