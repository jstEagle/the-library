## 12. Winter Comes Early

The first snow came on a Tuesday, which felt like a personal insult. I had been in Halden-at-the-Wall eleven days, long enough to learn the names of the dogs but not the people, and the sky went the color of dishwater at noon and by four o'clock the world was white.

I'd seen storms before. I'd been caught out in them on the *Magpie*, my father's boat, hove-to under a scrap of storm canvas while the sea tried to rearrange my internal organs. But sea storms announce themselves. They build for days, they send outriders of wind and cloud, they give you time to be afraid properly. Mountain storms are different. Mountain storms just decide.

By evening the lower passes were gone. Not closed — gone. Eirik stood in the doorway of Marta's house looking up at where the road to the coast used to be, and he said, "Well," in the tone other men use for eulogies.

"That's bad," I said.

"That's early." He let the door swing shut against the cold. "The pass doesn't usually seal until midwinter. If it's sealed now, it stays sealed. Nothing comes over. Nothing goes."

"Nothing?"

"Nothing with wheels." He gave me a look that was almost kind. "Helicopters don't care about weather the way roads do, though. Remember that."

I did remember it. I remembered it all night, lying in the narrow bed under quilts that smelled of lanolin and woodsmoke, listening to the wind lean its whole weight against the house and finding no purchase. Somewhere south of us, Rennick knew I'd come this way. Somewhere south of us, Quill's men were counting their losses from the ransacked *Magpie* and doing arithmetic that ended with my name. The mountains had shut the door behind me, which meant they'd also shut the door ahead of me, and doors that only open one way have a way of becoming walls.

But that was tomorrow's problem. That night, the problem was simpler: the fire needed feeding every two hours, and Marta Voss slept through anything short of artillery, so I got up each time myself, barefoot on cold boards, and fed it, and watched the snow come down past the window like the world being slowly erased and redrawn as something cleaner.

On one of those trips back to bed, I noticed the boots.

They were standing by Marta's door — her grandson Tomas's boots, I'd learned, enormous things with the toes worn round — except they were wet, and it was past midnight, and the boy was supposed to be asleep in the loft above the stable. I stood there dripping firelight and deciding whether to be the kind of person who checked. My whole life I'd been the kind who didn't. Checking is how you find things. Finding things is how you get involved.

I checked.

Tomas wasn't in the stable. He wasn't in the woodshed or the smokehouse. I found him at last at the top of the village track, sitting on the low stone wall that marked where Halden decided it had built enough, looking down into the white nothing where the valley fell away toward the sea.

"You'll freeze," I said.

"So will you." He didn't look at me. He was sixteen, maybe seventeen, with his grandmother's same stubborn jaw and none of her warmth yet — warmth I suspected was in there somewhere, banked like a coal. "Go back in, salvage woman."

"Casper."

"Go back in, Casper." He said my name like it cost him something. Then, quieter, not quite to me: "She's watching the road. She thinks I don't know. She's been watching it since you came."

"Marta?"

"She lost someone to that glacier." He pulled his collar up. "My great-uncle. She doesn't talk about it. She talks about everything else, fish and weather and whose roof needs mending, but not him. And now you show up asking about the ice, and she starts telling you stories she's kept shut for forty years, and I—" His breath fogged and tore away. "I don't know what you're going to do when you get up there. But if you bring her brother home, even just his bones—"

"Tomas—"

"Don't thank me." He slid off the wall, suddenly all elbows and embarrassment. "And don't tell her I said anything. She'll box my ears." He stomped past me toward the house, then stopped. "The snow's good, actually. Snow means tracks. Nobody moves in this without leaving tracks. So whoever you're hiding from — we'll see them coming."

He said *we*. I noticed that. I filed it away next to the boots and the firewood and the fact that someone had left dry socks outside my door two mornings ago, folded, anonymous. In the harbor towns where I'd grown up, nobody left you anonymous socks. They left you invoices.

* * *

Rennick came three days later, and of course he came by air, because Eirik had been right: helicopters don't care about weather the way roads do. I heard it first as a wrongness in the storm's rhythm, a flat mechanical pulse underneath the wind, and I was out of bed and at the window before my brain finished assembling the word *rotor*.

It came down in the square by the church, gray-green and arrogant, throwing snow in a blinding disc, and the whole village turned out to watch it land the way villages turn out for anything — with folded arms and expressions suggesting the visitor would be well advised to justify himself quickly.

I stayed inside. Obviously I stayed inside. I stood at Marta's window with the curtain pulled to a finger's width and watched Lieutenant Commander Rennick drop from the helicopter's belly into the snow, and I felt my heart do something complicated and unpleasant, part fear and part the old grief, because I knew that walk. I'd seen it across a naval pier six years ago, a man in dress whites carrying a folded report, and I'd hated him since before I knew his face belonged to a name.

"He can't stay," Marta said behind me. She'd come up without a sound, the way old women do, as if the floor were a courtesy rather than an obligation. "Storm like this, he can't fly out again tonight. He'll want shelter."

"Then he'll want it somewhere else."

"He'll ask for it here." She looked at me, and her eyes were shrewd and calm and very old. "Because he'll ask where the stranger is staying, and Halden has one stranger."

"Marta—"

"Hush." She was already pulling her coat off its peg. "You'll sit in the loft. You'll not make a sound. And you'll listen, because a man tells you more when he thinks the room is empty than when he knows it isn't."

* * *

The loft above Marta's kitchen was low and hot and smelled of stored apples. I lay among the crates with my cheek against rough planking, looking down through gaps between the floorboards at the top of Rennick's head.

He sat at Marta's table drinking tea he clearly didn't want, uniform immaculate despite the storm, posture suggesting he was doing the village an honor by occupying its furniture. Two of his men waited outside in the cold, which told me something: he hadn't brought many. This wasn't an operation. This was a fishing expedition, and he was the bait.

"This is a courtesy call," he was saying. "We're conducting a survey of coastal sites. One of our contractors has gone missing — a diver, hired for routine recovery work. We're concerned for her welfare."

"Concerned," Marta repeated. She poured herself tea with the unhurried ceremony of a woman who had decided the meeting would last exactly as long as she wanted it to. "In this weather. A diver. Missing."

"The conditions are regrettable, yes."

"Mm." She sipped. "What's her name?"

A pause, precisely measured. "Whitlock."

"And what makes you think she came here? It's a big coast."

"She had family connections to the area."

"Did she." Marta set down her cup with a small click. "Her father, maybe. Hal Whitlock. He used to run salvage out of the southern harbors. Good man. Careful man." She let that word sit on the table between them like a stone. "Died badly, I heard. Boat lost with all hands on a navy-chartered run."

"The loss of the vessel in question was investigated thoroughly—"

"I'm sure it was." Marta refilled his cup unasked, pinning his hands to the table with hospitality. "Lieutenant. I'm an old woman, and old women in mountain villages survive by knowing two things: weather, and men. The weather I can't help you with. But I'll tell you about men for free. When a powerful organization loses something and sends one polite officer instead of fifty soldiers, it means the something is worth hiding, not worth fighting for. And when that officer asks questions in a village that owes him nothing—" she smiled, and it was not a warm smile, "—the village answers the way the sea answers. Honestly, but not usefully."

Rennick's teacup paused halfway to his mouth. "You're refusing to cooperate with a naval inquiry."

"I'm an old woman making tea. What could I possibly know?" She leaned back. "There's no diver here. There's no stranger here. There's a girl from the coast helping me with my roof before the snow takes it, and she keeps to herself, and she's welcome to. If your missing contractor passes through, I'll tell her someone's looking for her. Now. Will you be wanting supper? Because supper costs extra for uniforms."

I pressed my forehead against the planks and breathed very slowly, because I was laughing and crying at the same time and neither could afford the noise.

Rennick stayed another twenty minutes. I heard him try three more approaches — the concerned-official voice, the veiled-threat voice, and once, briefly, something almost human, a question about her brother, about the old expedition, delivered softly, and Marta's silence in response was longer and colder than any answer. Then the chair scraped, boots crossed the floor, and the door opened onto a knife of cold and the whine of the storm.

At the threshold he stopped. "Mrs. Voss. If you're sheltering her, understand what you're sheltering her *from*. She's involved in matters far above this village's head."

"So is the weather," Marta said, and shut the door.

* * *

He didn't leave that night — the storm grounded the helicopter, and his men billeted in the church hall while Rennick himself took the inn's one proper room. For two days Halden performed a masterpiece of collective nothing. The baker sold him bread without gossiping. The innkeeper served his meals without lingering. Children stared at the helicopter with frank delight and answered his friendly questions with the devastating literalism of the young — yes, there was a stranger, she helped fix the roof, she was bad at sledding. No, they didn't know where she'd come from. No, they didn't know her name. Yes, they'd ask.

Nobody asked me. That was the miracle of it, the thing I kept turning over like a coin I suspected was counterfeit. Nobody asked, nobody hinted, nobody angled for a look at whatever the navy might be paying. In the harbor towns, Rennick's questions would have been answered within the hour, priced according to need. Here, the village simply absorbed me, the way snow absorbs a footprint — not erasing me, exactly, but holding me, keeping my shape safe until spring.

Eirik put it less poetically. He found me the second evening splitting wood behind Marta's house, because I'd discovered that if my hands were busy they couldn't shake, and he took the axe from me without asking and split three rounds in the time it took me to do one.

"You know he's not here for the survey," I said.

"I know."

"He's here for me. And when the weather lifts, he'll either fly out and come back with more men, or he'll keep peeling this village like an orange until he finds a soft spot."

"Some spots won't soften." Eirik set the axe head-down against the block, precise as a ritual. "You're waiting for the catch. I can see it. You keep checking whether the floor's going to open." He looked at me, and there was frost in his eyebrows and something steadier in his eyes. "Here's the catch, then, so you can stop looking for it. The village helps you because you're Marta's guest, and Marta's guests are the village's business. That's the whole contract. But contracts run both ways. When the pass opens — if it opens — and you go up that ice, you go carrying more than your own reasons. You carry ours too. Forty years of ours."

"I haven't agreed to go up anything."

"No," he said. "But you've got a compass in your coat that points at my glacier, and you've got her brother's name written somewhere in your father's hand, and you've got the look of a person doing sums they hate." He picked up the split wood, stacked it against his forearm. "Do your sums, Casper Whitlock. The mountain will wait. It's good at that. It's the best waiter in the world."

He crunched off through the blue dusk toward his own house, and I stood there with the axe cooling in my hands, watching my breath hang in the air like something I'd said and couldn't take back.

* * *

Rennick flew out on the fourth morning, in that hard clean light that follows a storm, and the whole village stood in the square to watch the helicopter shrink northward over the icefall — northward, I noted, not south, a detail I turned over all day like a worry-stone. Before boarding, he'd shaken hands with the innkeeper, thanked the priest, and paused beside Marta in the snow for a final exchange I was too far away to hear. Whatever it was, she nodded once, slowly, and afterward she wouldn't speak of it, and that frightened me more than the helicopter had.

That night she called me to the table after Tomas had gone up to the loft, and set down in front of me not tea but a tin box, dented, older than I was.

"If he comes back," she said, "he comes back with authority. Papers. Men. Things a village can't refuse, only delay." She opened the lid. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a bundle of letters tied with string, and beneath them a photograph: five young men and one young woman on a glacier, squinting into a sun thirty years dead. One of the men had my father's chin. Another had Marta's eyes. "These are yours to read tonight and mine to keep forever. And this—" she drew out a folded paper, a map, soft as cloth from handling, "—is why you'll stop pretending you haven't already decided."

I didn't touch it. "Marta, I—"

"My brother went up that ice with your father and never came down, and the government sent a man to tell us it was weather, and we swallowed it because swallowing was all we had." Her voice didn't rise at all, which made it worse. "Now the weather has buried the passes, and the man has come back wearing the same face, and you're sitting in my kitchen with the one thing in this world that knows where my brother lies. So." She pushed the map an inch closer. "Read. Then tell me what you've decided. Because I'm an old woman, Casper Whitlock, and I don't have forty more years to wait for the truth — and neither does that glacier."
