## 14. Quill Climbs Too

The trouble with waiting in a small village is that everyone knows what you're waiting for, and half of them are kind enough not to say it out loud, which somehow makes it worse.

I'd been in Halden-at-the-Wall eleven days when the news came up from the coast, and I knew it was bad before anyone said a word, because Marta Voss set down her tea without drinking any of it. In my experience, an old fisherman abandoning a hot drink mid-sip ranks somewhere between a tsunami siren and a bank calling about your father's debts on the list of sounds that mean your week is ruined.

"Boat came through the strait this morning," she said. "Charter out of Skarvholm. Not a fishing boat. Too clean."

I kept my hands around my own cup and my voice level, which took more effort than it should have. "Clean how?"

"New paint. New engines. The kind of boat that belongs to people who've never been cold." She looked at me over the rim of her glasses with the flat patience of a woman who had buried a husband, two brothers, and most of her illusions, and did not intend to be lied to by a diver young enough to be her granddaughter. "People asking after guides."

The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere outside, a dog was making its opinions known about the wind. I made myself take a sip of tea because my hands wanted to do something and shaking wasn't on the menu.

"What kind of people?"

"The kind," Marta said, "who pay for answers before they ask questions."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about being hunted: it doesn't start with men kicking down doors. It starts with arithmetic.

Quill had offered me a fortune for whatever came out of that cave — his number, delivered with a broker's smile in a harbor bar that smelled of diesel and fried onions, had been enough to clear my father's ledger twice over and buy me a new life with change left for a hat. I'd said no badly. Badly meaning: too fast, too loud, with a face like a struck match. A man like Quill doesn't need a yes. He needs only to know that the thing exists, that you've seen it, and that you're the kind of fool who says no to money. Everything after that is logistics.

So while Halden waited for the warm winds to crack the glacier open, I did sums at Marta's kitchen table with a pencil stub and the back of a supply invoice. How long would it take Quill to mount an expedition? He'd need a guide who knew the high route — there were maybe four people in the province with that knowledge and the nerve to sell it. He'd need gear, which money buys overnight. He'd need permits, which money buys faster. And he'd need to stage on the far side of the pass, because the near approaches were Halden's ground, and Halden's ground was already proving less hospitable than he'd like.

Eleven days. Maybe twelve. That was my estimate, penciled in the margin, and I hated how professional it looked. My father had taught me salvage math — tides, air, bottom time, margins of error — and here I was running the same equations on men instead of water. The variables were uglier. Water doesn't hold grudges.

"You're doing the thing where you go quiet and your jaw does that," Marta observed.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look like your father."

I put the pencil down. That landed harder than she could have known, or perhaps she knew exactly, because Marta Voss missed very little and pretended to miss slightly more.

"I'm trying to figure out how much time we have," I said.

"We," she repeated, and let it sit there between us like a stone she'd placed deliberately. Then she stood, joints announcing themselves, and went to the window. Beyond the glass, the escarpment rose in its long gray shoulder, the glacier above it catching the afternoon light the way a blade catches it — all edge, no warmth. "Eirik will want to hear this. And you should hear it from him, not from the harbor gossip, because the harbor gossip will have him fighting three guides and a helicopter by suppertime."

* * *

Eirik Solheim received the news the way he seemed to receive everything: standing very still, arms crossed, looking at the middle distance as though the middle distance owed him an explanation.

We found him at the equipment shed behind the guiding cooperative, checking ropes. That was his religion, I was learning. Ropes, anchors, knots — things that held. He ran a length of line through his hands link by link, feeling for damage no eye would catch, and when Marta finished talking he didn't stop, just kept working the rope like a rosary.

"A charter out of Skarvholm," he said finally. "That'll be Halvard Nilsen's boat."

"You know it?"

"Everyone knows it. It does scientific charters, oil survey work, occasionally things that aren't either." He coiled the rope with two efficient motions and set it down. "And the guide?"

"That's the part nobody knows yet," Marta said. "Or won't say."

Eirik looked at me then, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I'd done at the kitchen table, except his version had names in it. Four guides in the province who knew the high route. He'd told me that himself, back when agreeing to take me up the glacier had still been a negotiation rather than a fact. I saw him arrive at the same conclusion I had, and I watched him decide not to say it, which was its own kind of answer.

"It doesn't matter who," he said. "It matters when. If they're staging on the far side, they're waiting for the same window we are. The calving season opens the route or it doesn't. Nobody crosses the icefall before it shifts — not for money, not for anything." He paused. "Nobody sane."

"And Quill?"

"Quill hires sane people and pays them until they stop being sane." Eirik picked up another rope. "How much did he offer you? For the compass."

I hesitated. Telling him felt like handing him a lever. But I'd learned something in the past weeks about levers: the ones other people hold over you are dangerous; the ones you hand over willingly, to people standing beside you, are called trust, and they work differently.

"Enough to erase my father's debts three times," I said. "Plus interest I haven't even accrued yet."

He nodded slowly, unsurprised. "And now he's spending ten times that on boats and guides to take it from you for free."

"That's the part I keep tripping over," I admitted. "If he'd just — if the number had been bigger, or if I hadn't—"

"No." It was sharp, sharper than Eirik usually spent words. He set the rope down and faced me fully. "You didn't fail a negotiation. You understood something he didn't. Some things don't get better when you own them. They get worse. Whoever carries that compass up the mountain is carrying a debt of their own — one that doesn't clear." His eyes went briefly toward the glacier, and something moved behind them, some private weight I recognized because I was carrying its twin. "Believe me. I know what families hand down."

Marta, who had gone quiet in the doorway, cleared her throat with the delicacy of a landslide. "The boy Tomas heard something at the dock this morning, too. Before the charter news. Sailors talk when they think no one's listening."

"Tomas hears everything," Eirik said. "Half of it's true."

"He says the charter took on fuel and provisions for twelve. Twelve, Eirik. That's not a survey crew. That's an expedition with muscle attached."

Twelve. I turned the number over. Quill's harbor operation had run six, maybe seven men when they'd ransacked my boat and burned the last of my old life along with it. Twelve meant he'd scaled up. It meant he thought of this as a major operation now, worth proper investment, which meant he believed the prize justified it — which meant somewhere, someone had told him enough about what lay under the glacier to make the numbers sing.

And there was only one person who'd talked to Quill's people besides me.

I didn't say the name. But Eirik was watching my face, and whatever crossed it, he read it, and his jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. Tomas was Marta's grandson. This was not a thread we could pull casually.

"Twelve men eat a lot of food," Eirik said mildly, which in Eirik-language was practically a scream. "Food comes through Halden or it doesn't come at all. I'll make some calls."

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, so I did what I'd been doing most nights: sat by the window with the compass in my hands and let it lie to me.

It didn't lie, exactly. That was the maddening thing. Its needle swung with perfect confidence toward the icefall, toward the high white shoulder of the glacier where the lost expedition had walked into legend, and it held that bearing with the serene certainty of a thing that has never once doubted itself. North meant nothing to it. My father's initials meant everything, though I still couldn't say why he'd engraved them, or when, or what he'd been doing on this coast in the years before I was born with a brass compass that pointed at a mountain instead of the pole.

Outside, the wind had dropped, and the village was that particular deep quiet that only very small places achieve after dark — a quiet with texture, made of sleeping dogs and banked fires and the distant mutter of the sea working the rocks below the escarpment. Somewhere out in that darkness, past the headland, a clean white charter boat rode at anchor or ran dark toward the far side of the range, carrying twelve men and enough rope and steel to drag a fortune off a glacier.

I tried to imagine Quill's crew on the ice. I'd seen two of them up close, the night they tore apart my father's boat looking for the compass, and what I remembered wasn't menace exactly — it was efficiency. Men who worked quickly and didn't apologize. On a dock, that's frightening. On a crevasse field, it's something else. Efficiency kills people on glaciers. Glaciers reward patience, humility, and the willingness to turn back, and none of those qualities appear on any payroll I've ever heard of.

Then again, neither did they appear on mine, necessarily. Eirik had called me reckless during our first session on the moraine, and he'd been right, and I'd hated him for a full day before admitting it. Two teams on that ice — one paid to be careless, one too stubborn to be careful. The mountain wouldn't care which was which.

The needle trembled — just the vibration of my hands, probably, though I chose to read it as impatience — and I wrapped the compass back in its cloth and put it away.

* * *

The confirmation came two mornings later, and it came the way bad news always arrives in Halden: on foot, breathless, ahead of any official version of events.

Tomas Voss came up the hill from the harbor at a dead run, seventeen years old and built like his grandmother — all sinew and stubbornness — and burst into the cooperative's common room where Eirik and I were going over route notes that suddenly felt like historical documents.

"They're at the far col," he panted. "Sven Halvardson saw their camp from the ridge trail — tents, orange ones, six of them, and a cache site already dug in below the wind line. Professional job. And—" he caught his breath, and I watched him decide whether to say the next part, and watched him lose that fight, because Tomas had never once in his life declined to say a thing, "—and Sven recognized the guide. He says it's Kjell Brandt."

The room went still. Eirik closed his eyes for a moment, the way a man does when a long-feared diagnosis is confirmed.

"Brandt," Marta said from the doorway. "Astrid's boy?"

"Astrid's boy," Eirik confirmed quietly. "We roped together for six seasons. Before." He didn't explain the *before*. Everyone in Halden seemed to have a *before*, a year or an event that divided their lives cleanly, and I was learning not to ask. "He knows the high route as well as I do. Better on the seracs. Worse judgment." A pause. "Much worse judgment, lately. Since the divorce he guides for whoever wires the money first."

"So it's a race," I said. It came out flatter than I felt. Inside, something was doing a slow, cold roll, the way the sea goes before weather. "They're staged. They're provisioned. They've got a guide who knows the route, and they're sitting on the far side of the pass waiting for the same window we are. When the calving opens the icefall, both teams move at once, and the first one to the vault—"

"—isn't automatically the one that survives it," Eirik finished. "But yes. It's a race."

Marta came fully into the room and shut the door behind her, which in Marta's body language was equivalent to a war council being declared. "Then we stop waiting politely," she said. "Eirik. The window. How long?"

"When the winds come warm, the icefall shifts — big movements, the route opens through the middle seracs. Historically, eight days, sometimes ten, before the melt makes it suicide." He looked at me, and I saw him weighing something, and I realized with a lurch that he'd been weighing it for days and had just now decided. "Casper. You're not ready. Another month of training and you'd be ready. We may not have a month. We may not have a week."

"Then train me in a week."

"It doesn't work like—"

"It has to." I heard my own voice harden, and underneath the hardness, my father's voice, saying the thing he used to say when a dive went wrong and the air ran short and panic offered itself: *slow is smooth, Casper-girl, and smooth is alive.* I took a breath and started over. "Teach me fast, then. Teach me the parts that keep me alive and skip the parts that make me graceful. I don't need to be a mountaineer. I need to not die on your rope."

Eirik studied me for a long moment. Outside, the light was changing, the long northern evening sliding toward a night that never quite committed to darkness. Somewhere beyond the escarpment, twelve men sat around orange tents beside a cache of rope and steel, waiting for the same warm wind I was praying for, and hating that I was praying for it, because every degree of warmth that opened my road opened theirs.

"Tomorrow morning," Eirik said at last. "Five o'clock. Crampons. If you're going to learn fast, you'll learn tired, because that's how it happens on the ice anyway." He began rolling the ropes, each coil precise, each motion a small act of faith in things holding. "And Casper — when we go up there, we're not racing them. Say it back to me."

"We're not racing them."

"Racing is how Brandt's clients die. We climb our own climb, at our own pace, and if the mountain offers us a shortcut, we assume it's lying." He tied off the rope bundle and slung it over his shoulder. "Get some sleep. Starting tomorrow, you won't get much."

Tomas lingered after the others drifted out, hovering by the door with the specific restlessness of a young man who has news and wants credit for it.

"There's one more thing," he said. "Sven counted the tents. Six tents, twelve men — but he says there's a seventh tent, smaller, set apart. And a generator. And crates marked with shipping codes, not brand names." He grinned, pleased with the mystery, oblivious to the way it settled over the room like frost. "Whoever they are, they're not planning to come back down the same way they went up. You don't haul a generator up a col for a day trip."

After he left, I stood at the window a long time, watching the glacier hold its pale fire in the last light.

Seven tents. A generator. Crates with shipping codes. Quill hadn't sent a salvage crew. He'd sent an extraction team — men equipped to cut something enormous out of the ice and carry it away whole. Which meant Quill knew what was buried up there. Not guessed. *Knew.*

And the only person who could have told him was someone who'd seen inside that cave the same night I had.

Below the village, the sea worked patiently at the base of the escarpment, the same tide that had exposed the cave now erasing its own evidence twice a day, keeping secrets the way it always had. Above, the glacier waited with its cargo and its dead. And between them, on a narrow shelf of rock and snow, a village that had taken me in was about to find out what it cost to keep me.

Five o'clock tomorrow, crampons, tired. I laid out my gear in the dark by feel — rope, harness, the compass wrapped soft in its cloth — and told myself the cold knot in my stomach was anticipation.

It wasn't. But it would do.
