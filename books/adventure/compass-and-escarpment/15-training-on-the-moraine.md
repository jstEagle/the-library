## 15. Training on the Moraine

Eirik Solheim woke me at four in the morning by knocking on my door once and saying, "Glacier doesn't wait," through the wood, as if the glacier were an impatient creditor I'd somehow inherited along with everything else.

I'd had creditors before. None of them had ever been polite about it.

The moraine above Halden-at-the-Wall was a gray tongue of rock and rubble left behind by the glacier's slow retreat, like the shed skin of something enormous that had decided, on reflection, to stay. We climbed to it in the blue half-light while the village still slept, and Eirik dropped two coils of rope, a rack of carabiners, and a pair of crampons at my feet with the ceremony of a man feeding chickens.

"Put those on."

I looked at the crampons. Ten steel points apiece, strapped to stiff leather boots that smelled like other people's feet and other people's mountains. "I've worn fins for eleven hours straight in water cold enough to stop your heart," I said. "How hard can spikes be?"

Eirik gave me a look I would come to know very well over the following days — the look of a man revising his estimate of how long this was going to take. "Fins don't go through your leg when you step wrong."

* * *

He was right, of course. He was right about nearly everything, which was rapidly becoming the most irritating thing about him, ahead of even his habit of stating weather conditions aloud as though reporting them to some invisible authority.

The first hour was humiliation. Crampons are not difficult in principle; you walk flat-footed, slightly wider than you think, letting the front points bite. But my body had spent a decade learning the grammar of water — weightless turns, long patient kicks, the economy of a creature suspended in a medium that forgives everything. Ice forgives nothing. Ice is the sea's vindictive sister. On my fourth attempt at walking a thirty-meter stretch of polished glacier snout, I caught a front point in the crust, pitched forward, and saved myself with both hands, palms skidding on surface frost.

"You lead with your chin," Eirik said from somewhere above me. He hadn't moved to catch me. I noted that. Filed it under things to remember about men who watch instead of help.

"I lead with whatever keeps me upright."

"No." He came down to me, unhurried, boots crunching. "On ice you lead with your feet. Everything else follows. Divers forget this. You're used to falling being a suggestion."

I got up. My palms stung. Somewhere below us the fjord lay flat and pewter-colored, and beyond it the escarpment dropped into the sea where my father's boat sat at anchor with a hole in its cabin door from Quill's men, and I made myself look at it for exactly one second before turning back to the ice.

"Again," I said.

Something shifted in Eirik's face then — not warmth exactly, but a recalculation. He'd expected me to swear at him, or quit, or do what most lowlanders did when the mountain embarrassed them: blame the equipment, blame the guide, blame the mountain itself. I just wanted to go again. Grief makes some people fragile and some people stubborn, and I had never been given the luxury of choosing which one I was.

"Again," he agreed.

* * *

By midmorning I could walk. By noon I could walk fast, which Eirik said was worse than not walking at all.

"Speed is how the glacier eats people," he told me, sitting on a boulder with the ease of furniture while I stood roped to nothing, breathing hard. "They get confident. Confident people stop testing the snow. They walk over a bridge of snow across a crevasse because it held yesterday. The glacier doesn't have yesterdays. It only has now, and now it wants you at the bottom."

"Cheerful."

"It's true. Truth is cheerful if you survive it." He tossed me the rope end. "Show me your knots."

I showed him my knots. My father taught me bowline and figure-eight before he taught me to swim, back when his salvage boat was still solvent and his hands still shook from drink rather than from whatever the expedition had done to them. My fingers knew rope the way they knew line and chain — without asking permission from the rest of me. I tied a double fisherman's bend blind, behind my back, mostly to see what Eirik would do.

What he did was go quiet for a moment, watching my hands.

"Who taught you?"

"My father." I cinched the knot tight. "Salvage diver. Rope work was the difference between coming up and not."

"He was careful?"

I thought about the report Rennick signed. *Equipment failure. No fault attributable to crew.* I thought about the annotations on the chart, the bearing matching the compass needle, the initials scratched into brass. A man who spent thirty years being careful about one secret and careless about everything else, including me, including the drinking, including the debts that had followed me like gulls after a fishing boat.

"He was careful about the things that mattered to him," I said.

Eirik heard the rest of the sentence, the part underneath, the way people who live in small villages learn to hear. He didn't ask. That was another thing I was learning about him: he collected information the way the glacier collected rock, slowly, silently, and kept it all.

* * *

The crevasse work was where I stopped being a student and started being a problem.

There was a slot in the moraine-adjacent ice — not a real crevasse, Eirik said, a training crack, twenty meters deep with a gravel floor, safe as such things ever are. He rigged a belay, explained the drill: I would cross a snow bridge, he would drop me, I would arrest his fall with my boot-axe brake, we would build an anchor, and I would haul him out. Standard. The kind of thing every climber learns and most climbers hope never to use.

"Your weight against mine plus gear," he said. "You're what, sixty kilos?"

"Fifty-eight."

"So you'll need mechanical advantage. Don't be proud about pulleys. Pride is heavy too."

I crossed the bridge. He fell. I caught him — the jolt went up my arms and into my teeth and for one vertiginous second I was ten years old again catching my father's tool bag on a heaving deck, except this time the thing on the end of the rope was a living man who trusted me not to kill him, which is either better or worse, I still haven't decided.

Then I built the anchor. And here is where I confess: I didn't build the anchor he'd shown me.

His method was textbook — two screws, equalized, redundant, fifteen minutes of careful work. But I'd spent the morning looking at the ice the way I look at wrecks, and wrecks teach you shortcuts that keep you alive when the textbook is underwater somewhere. There was a natural ice horn ten meters back, thick as a fence post, load-tested in my head by eye and instinct. I ran the rope around it, backed it with one screw, and hauled Eirik out with a three-to-one I improvised from his own kit, faster than he could have clipped his own system together.

He came up over the lip, dusted off, and looked at the anchor. Then at me. Then at the anchor again.

"That horn," he said. "How did you know it would hold?"

"I didn't. I estimated."

"You *estimated.*" He said the word like it was a disease. "Casper. On the icefall there are no horns you've tested. There are no estimates. There is what holds and what doesn't, and the difference between those two things is a funeral."

"The difference between estimating and drowning is also a funeral," I shot back. "In the water you make a hundred calls a minute with incomplete information. You don't get to test the wreck before you enter it. You read the current, the rust lines, the way the silt sits, and you commit. That's the job. That's always been the job."

"And how many divers do you know who died making a good read?"

That landed harder than he probably intended, because we both knew the answer, and the answer had a name and a boat and a berth in a harbor forty kilometers south.

The silence stretched. Wind moved through the moraine rocks with a sound like dry breathing. I crouched and began coiling the rope, because my hands needed work the way other people's mouths need words.

"Eirik," I said finally. "I hear you. I do. But I need you to understand what you're taking up that mountain. I'm not reckless because I'm stupid. I'm reckless because careful is what killed everyone I've ever loved. My father was careful with the secret and it ate him alive. Careful divers die in silty water waiting for conditions that never come. Careful salvagers lose the wreck to the next bidder. Every good thing that has ever happened to me happened in the window between *this is insane* and *too late to turn back*. That's where I live. You're asking me to move out."

Eirik sat down on the boulder again. He was quiet long enough that I finished the coil and started another one I didn't need.

"My father," he said at last, "was careful."

I stopped coiling.

"He checked everything twice. He turned back twice on routes other men did for glory. He used to say the mountain respects patience." Eirik picked up a flat stone and turned it over in his fingers. "He went up onto the ice with that expedition thirty years ago and he never came down, and I have spent my whole life wondering whether one more check, one more hour of patience, would have brought him home. So when I tell you to be careful, I'm not teaching you. I'm arguing with a ghost."

We looked at each other across the training crack, two children of the lost, each convinced the other's religion had killed their parent.

"Both can be true," I said. "Careful and dead. Reckless and alive. The glacier doesn't grade on method."

"No," Eirik agreed. "It grades on results." He stood, shouldered the rope, and for the first time since I'd met him he smiled — a small, crooked, winter-surviving smile. "Which is why tomorrow we do it my way until you can do it asleep. And then, when the ice tries to kill us, you can improvise all you like. Improvisation earned is worth ten of the born kind."

"Tomas says you never compliment anyone."

"Tomas talks too much." He turned downhill. "Come. Marta will feed us, and you'll want sleep. Tomorrow I drop you into the real crevasse field, and I'd rather you hated me rested."

* * *

Marta fed us fish soup and dark bread and opinions, in roughly equal quantities. Tomas was there, seventeen and all elbows and appetite, and he listened to Eirik's account of the day with the naked envy of a boy who'd been told he was too young for the icefall run.

"She built a bad anchor," Eirik reported.

"A *fast* anchor," I corrected.

"A fast anchor is a bad anchor that hasn't failed yet." He tore bread. "But she arrested a fall clean, first time, no panic. And her knots—" he paused, and I realized he was choosing to give me this in front of Marta deliberately, building my standing in the house the way you'd shore up a wall, "—her knots are better than mine."

Tomas whistled. Marta looked at me over her spoon with an expression I couldn't parse — pride, or warning, or both braided together the way everything in this village seemed to be.

"Her father's hands," Marta said simply, and went back to her soup, and I had to leave the table for a minute on the pretext of checking the stove.

* * *

That night I lay in the narrow bed in my borrowed room and listened to the wind test the shutters, and I took stock the way I used to take stock hanging off a wreck at forty meters: what I had, what I owed, what could kill me.

What I had: rope calluses starting in the same grooves my father's had. A compass in a oilcloth wrap under the floorboard, its needle pointing faithfully at a glacier full of secrets. Two allies — one who argued with ghosts, one who talked too much. A village that had closed around me like a hand around something it intended to keep.

What I owed: everything, still. The debt didn't care that I was learning to climb. Debts don't train, don't wait, don't respect patience.

And beneath the floorboard, the compass sat pointing northeast, patient as a debt, faithful as a ghost, toward whatever my father had spent his whole careful life guarding.

Tomorrow, the real crevasse field. Tomorrow, Eirik would try to teach my body what my father's death had already taught my mind: that the ice gives nothing back, and takes interest.

I was almost asleep when I heard it — boots on the lane below my window, more than one set, moving quietly the way men move when they don't want to be counted. Not Eirik's tread. Not Tomas's.

I lay still and listened to them pass, and I counted: four sets of boots, heading uphill, toward the moraine where our ropes were staged.

Somebody was checking our work before we did.
