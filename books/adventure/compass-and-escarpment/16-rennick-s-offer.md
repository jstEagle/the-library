## 16. Rennick's Offer

The knock came at nine in the evening, which in Halden-at-the-Wall is roughly the middle of the social day and also the worst possible hour to be anyone new.

I knew it was Rennick before I opened the door. Not because of any sixth sense — though after the last few months I'd earned one — but because Marta Voss had come by an hour earlier with a pot of fish stew and a look on her face that said *the navy is at the guesthouse asking for you*, delivered with the same tone she used for *the herring are running* or *your roof leaks*. Information as weather. You don't argue with it. You just dress for it.

I'd had time to think on the walk back from Marta's kitchen. That was either a mercy or a mistake; I hadn't decided yet. The village lanes were narrow and cobbled with stones worn round by four hundred years of boots, and my breath hung in the air like small white confessions. Somewhere above us all, under two hundred feet of ice, sat a compass that pointed at nothing any map agreed to acknowledge, engraved with my father's initials, and I had been carrying it around like a stone in my coat pocket for so long now that I'd stopped noticing its weight except when I took the coat off and the absence made me stagger.

Rennick stood in the doorway of the room Eirik had lent me — a narrow cell with a bed, a chair, one window facing the escarpment, and a draft that came free of charge. He wasn't in uniform. That was the first thing. He wore a grey civilian jacket and no insignia, which meant this was off the books, which meant he thought he was being clever, which meant he was nervous. Navy men wear their uniforms into rooms where they want power. They take them off when they want something they can't order.

"Casper Whitlock." He said it like a man confirming a reservation.

"Lieutenant Rennick." I said it like a man reading a warrant.

"Commander, actually. Promotion came through in the spring." He smiled the way ice smiles — all surface, nothing underneath but more cold. "May I?"

He didn't wait for permission. He stepped past me into the room, looked around it with the quick inventory of a man who has searched rooms for a living, noted the bed, the chair, the window, the single candle, and seemed satisfied that I owned nothing worth taking. Which was almost true. Almost.

"You've settled in," he said. "Cozy."

"The hospitality of mountain people is legendary."

"It is." He turned. "That's actually what I wanted to talk about."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Commander Aldous Rennick, gathered over years the way you gather silt in a harbor — slowly, quietly, without wanting any of it:

Eleven years ago, the salvage vessel *Meridian Rose* went down off the escarpment coast with a crew of five. My father was aboard. So were four other men whose names I could recite faster than my own birthday. The official inquiry found equipment failure compounded by weather. The report clearing the voyage plan — the document that said, in effect, *nothing anyone did wrong here, terrible tragedy, moving on* — bore one signature at the bottom of the authorization page. A young lieutenant named A. Rennick.

My father died three weeks before the insurance payout would have cleared our debts. Instead the debt stayed, because a dead man's signature on a failed contract is worth exactly nothing, and the navy's compensation offer was insultingly precise: enough for the funeral, not enough for the boat. I was nineteen. I learned to dive his wrecks and hate his ghost and tell myself the sea doesn't take sides.

Then, this autumn, a classified wreck nobody would name started paying too well, and the man who signed my father's death warrant started paying me personally, and I began to understand that the sea takes sides constantly. It just bills you later.

"I'll be direct," Rennick said, settling onto my chair uninvited, which told me everything about how he saw rooms and the people in them. "You found something at the cave during the low tide."

"Did I?"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Please. I've read your file. You're good at lying — genuinely, it's one of your better qualities — but you're better at diving, and you spent ninety minutes longer in that cave than the tide window allowed. I know because I had men watching the waterline, and when they went in after you left, the rock face where the compass housing was fused had been freshly broken. Chisel marks. Your chisel, if I wanted to be tedious about it, which I do have the serial numbers for."

I kept my face still. Inside, something cold turned over. Not fear, exactly. More like the moment on a dive when you check your gauge and the number is lower than you'd budgeted for. Recalculation time.

"That's a lot of inference from chisel marks," I said.

"It is." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the performance dropped and I saw the actual man underneath — tired, sharp, and frightened in a way he'd never admit. "Whitlock. I'm going to make you an offer, and then I'm going to leave, and tomorrow we can both pretend this conversation happened or didn't, whichever serves you."

"Go on."

"The debts. All of them. Harrow & Finch, the marine mortgage on the *Rose*, the personal notes your father signed against the boat — every obligation attached to Casper Whitlock, extinguished. Not paid. Extinguished. There's a difference. Paid leaves records. Extinguished means the creditors receive their money from a source they won't question and you receive a clean slate from a source that doesn't exist." He let that sit. "In exchange: the compass, and the names of everyone who's helped you since you took it."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened inside me in that moment, because honesty is the only currency I have left that isn't counterfeit.

For about four seconds — I counted them later, lying awake — I wanted to say yes.

Not because I'm weak. Because I'm tired. There's a kind of exhaustion that comes from carrying a secret through a village that has been kinder to me in six weeks than the coast was in eleven years, knowing that kindness is built on a lie of omission, knowing that every warm loaf of bread from Marta's oven and every gruff nod from Eirik is extended to a woman who is, technically, hiding stolen government property in a flour sack under her floorboards. Four seconds of imagining the weight gone. The coat light. The creditors' letters turning to ash before they reached the mailbox. Four seconds of a life where I could sleep.

Then I thought about my father's initials cut into brass, and the bearing he'd annotated on charts he never showed me, and the fact that a man who loved the sea more than God had spent his last years diving alone in bad weather to check on something he refused to sell even when selling would have saved him.

My father was not a brave man in the ordinary ways. He was afraid of doctors and public speaking and my mother's family. But he had gone into the water one last time rather than give up whatever that compass pointed to, and the inquiry had called it equipment failure, and the signature at the bottom of that finding belonged to the man now sitting in my borrowed chair offering to buy my silence with my own inheritance.

"No," I said.

Rennick didn't move. "You haven't heard the alternative."

"Oh, I've done the arithmetic. I just like saying no while I still can."

"Whitlock—"

"Let me ask you something first." I sat down on the bed across from him, because standing felt like pleading and I refuse to plead. "The *Meridian Rose*. Eleven years ago. Did you know what my father was carrying when you cleared that voyage?"

Something flickered behind his eyes. It was fast, but I spend my life reading water, and water teaches you that the dangerous things are the ones that move fastest beneath a calm surface.

"I signed a routine clearance," he said.

"You signed a clearance for a dive site that didn't appear on any chart. For a crew that was told to keep radio silence. And when they died, you signed the report that buried the questions along with the bodies." I watched him. "You've been managing this secret your whole career, haven't you? You're not hunting the compass because it's valuable. You're hunting it because it's a loose thread, and you're the man who's supposed to make sure the garment stays sewn."

The silence stretched. Outside, wind moved down the escarpment and rattled the shutter, and somewhere below us a dog complained about it on principle.

"You sound like your father," Rennick said finally, and there was something in it that wasn't contempt. Something worse. Something like respect, which from him was an obscenity.

"You knew him."

"I supervised his clearances for six years. He was the best salvage diver I ever certified. Careful. Honest to the point of self-destruction." He stood, smoothing his jacket. "Which is why this disappoints me. I offered you a clean exit. He'd have taken it."

"My father," I said, "died rather than take it. Whatever you think you knew about him, you got that wrong."

* * *

He walked to the window then, and I understood he was giving me the speech he'd actually come to give, the one underneath the offer, and that the offer itself had been theater — a formality, a documented gesture of reasonableness. Men like Rennick build paper trails even for their threats. Especially for their threats.

"Here's what happens next," he said, to the glass. "Tomorrow morning I file a formal request with the Halden council for assistance in recovering government property. They'll comply — they always comply, it's cheaper than noncompliance. My men will search the village. Methodically. Every outbuilding, every boat, every floorboard." He turned back to me. "If the compass surfaces during that search, the offer stands. Full forgiveness, full anonymity. If it surfaces damaged, or if it turns out you've passed it to someone else—" he shrugged, a small economical motion "—then the charges become what they should have been from the start. Theft of classified materiel. Conspiracy. You'd do eight years, minimum, and your father's boat gets auctioned to cover legal costs, and the Voss woman's grandson gets charged as an accessory, and the Solheim guide loses his license, and the village learns what it costs to shelter strangers."

"There it is," I said.

"There it is."

"You know what the funny thing is?" I stood up. I'm shorter than him and always will be, but I've stared down worse than a commander from the low end of a look. "You came here without your uniform because you wanted deniability. But you just threatened an entire village in a rented room with no witnesses, which means you need me to hand it over voluntarily. Which means you can't find it yourself. Which means—" I smiled, and it felt good, like cracking a knuckle "—you're not as powerful as your paperwork says you are."

His jaw tightened. For a moment I thought he might actually do something stupid right there in the little room with the drafty window, and part of me — the reckless part Eirik kept yelling at on the moraine — wanted him to. It would have simplified things.

But Rennick hadn't gotten fat on the navy by losing his temper in cheap rooms. He straightened his cuffs instead.

"Ninety-six hours," he said. "The search begins Thursday. Sleep well, Miss Whitlock."

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and didn't turn around.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the cave was supposed to stay sealed until the glacier finished the job naturally. Another decade, maybe less, the way the ice is moving. Your tide pulled the curtain up early. Everything that happens now — everything — is downstream of that. Remember that when you're deciding what your principles cost."

Then he was gone, boots fading down the cobbles, and I was alone with the candle and the draft and the flour sack under the floorboard that suddenly weighed as much as the escarpment.

* * *

I gave him ten minutes, then went out the back way, down the stairs that smelled of tallow and old smoke, to the kitchen where Marta Voss was doing something violent to a cabbage and pretending she hadn't been waiting up.

"Well?" she said.

"He offered to erase my debts."

Marta's knife paused. "And?"

"And I said no."

The knife resumed. Whack. Whack. "Good," she said. Then, whack, "Stupid." Whack. "But good."

"He's going to search the village, Marta. Thursday. And if he finds it, he'll bury Eirik and Tomas and probably you along with me."

She set the knife down and looked at me with those pale fisherman's eyes that had watched sixty years of weather come off the glacier. "Child," she said, "do you know what my brother carried up that mountain? Do you know what the navy did when the expedition didn't come home? They sent one letter. One. Saying the matter was closed and further inquiries would be 'unhelpful.'" She picked the knife back up. "Aldous Rennick's kind has been telling this village what's helpful for forty years. We've developed opinions."

"So what do I do?"

"You stop keeping that thing under your floorboards, for a start. Any fool searching a room checks the flour." She jerked her chin toward the back door, toward the dark bulk of the escarpment rising against the stars. "And you go see Eirik. Tonight. Because if the navy's going to tear apart Halden looking for your compass, then you'd best be ready to tell him why you really need to climb his glacier — and you'd best pray the calving season opens before Rennick's patience does."

I stood in Marta's kitchen with the cabbage smell and the lamplight and the cold coming under the door, and I understood, finally and completely, what I had bought with my refusal. Not freedom. A countdown. Ninety-six hours, minus the ones already bleeding away, and somewhere out in the dark a man with paper for a soul was already writing the orders that would turn this village inside out looking for a piece of brass with my father's name on it.

I went to find Eirik.

The compass rode against my ribs the whole way, and for the first time since the cave, its needle wasn't pointing north, or at the glacier, or anywhere at all.

It was trembling.
