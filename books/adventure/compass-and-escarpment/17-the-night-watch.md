## 17. The Night Watch

The storm came in on a Tuesday, which felt about right. Storms in Halden-at-the-Wall didn't announce themselves with drama; they arrived the way tax collectors do — quietly, inevitably, and with ruinous consequences for anyone who hadn't prepared.

I'd been in the village eleven days by then, long enough to stop flinching at the wind's habit of rattling every shutter in Marta's house like it was conducting an inspection. Long enough to know that Eirik Solheim grunted when he meant yes, said nothing when he meant no, and made coffee so strong it could have dissolved a propeller shaft. Long enough to learn that Tomas — Marta's grandson, seventeen, all elbows and opinions — had appointed himself my unofficial shadow, ostensibly because he was "bored," actually because I'd once described a dive in too much detail and he'd been hooked ever since.

"Tell me again about the cave," he said that evening, sprawled across the kitchen floor with his boots on the woodstove's bench, which Marta permitted solely because she was pretending not to see it.

"No."

"You've told Eirik."

"I've told Eirik what Eirik needs to know."

"He needs to know everything if we're going up there." He said *we* with the casual entitlement of youth, as though his inclusion in an expedition that might kill us all was already settled business. Maybe it was. Nobody had voted me onto the glacier either; things just happened around here and then became true retroactively.

"Tomas." Marta's voice from the doorway carried the specific gravity of a woman who had raised children through winters that killed other people's children. "Leave her be. She'll tell you what matters when it matters."

"She never tells me anything that matters."

"That's because you haven't earned it yet," Marta said, and hung her coat on its peg with the finality of a judge closing court.

I should explain something about Marta Voss before this chapter of my life goes any further, because what happened that night doesn't make sense without it. She was seventy-three years old, built like the pilings under an old pier — weathered, bent slightly, but holding up more than anyone gave her credit for. She'd lost her brother to the glacier forty years ago and had spent every winter since keeping a lamp lit in the window facing the mountain, which she claimed was for the fishermen coming home late, though no fisherman had needed her lamp in thirty years. She took me in without asking why I'd come, without asking what I carried, without asking anything except whether I could gut a fish, and when I said yes she nodded like that settled my character completely.

In her house, for the first time since my father died, I slept without one ear open.

Which is, of course, exactly when you should keep both ears open.

* * *

The storm hit full force around nine. The wind stopped rattling shutters and started trying to remove them, and the snow came sideways, horizontal, occasionally upward, in defiance of several laws of physics. The power flickered twice and held. Marta banked the stove. Tomas argued for one more hour of staying up and lost. By ten the house had settled into the deep quiet of a building holding its breath against the weather.

I lay in the narrow bed in the back room — the room that used to belong to Marta's daughter before she moved to the coast — and listened to the storm work. My pack sat where it always sat, wedged between the bed frame and the wall, with the compass wrapped in oilcloth inside a spare sock inside a hollowed-out space in my dive log binder, because I had learned from Quill's men ransacking my boat that hiding places are only as good as their boringness. A dive log is the most boring object in the world to anyone who isn't a diver. It's a book of numbers. Numbers are invisible to people looking for treasure.

I was nearly asleep when I heard the dog.

Not barking. Barking would have woken the whole village and been fine. This was worse: Halvard, the old shepherd mix chained by the fish shed two doors down, had gone silent mid-complaint. Dogs in Halden complained all night during storms — it was practically a civic function. Silence from Halvard meant either he'd frozen solid or someone had given him something better to do than complain. A dried cod head, say. Or a hand that smelled of meat.

I lay still and did the arithmetic of the house. Marta's room at the front, door closed, her breathing audible only when the wind dropped. Tomas across the hall, snoring with the dedication of his age group. Me at the back, above the woodshed, with a window over the lean-to roof — the reason Marta had given me this room, I later suspected, was that it had always been the easiest to escape from and the hardest to enter.

Except tonight someone was testing that theory.

It wasn't a sound so much as an absence — the wind hitting the lean-to roof changed pitch, just slightly, as though something were crouched against the wall breaking the gusts. Then the faintest scrape, metal on ice, at the window frame.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about fear: it clarifies. All those months of debt and dread and running had left me exhausted, but exhaustion apparently makes excellent ballast. I didn't panic. I got out of the bed without moving the mattress springs, took the dive knife off the sill where I kept it — not hidden, because a knife hidden is a knife you die reaching for — and stood flat against the wall beside the window, where the room's darkness was deepest.

The latch lifted. Someone had done their homework; it was a sliding latch and they'd worked it with a blade, slow and patient, the way you open a lock you've practiced on. The window rose an inch. Cold poured in along the floor like water finding its level.

And then Tomas's voice, clear and carrying and absolutely delighted, said from the hallway:

"Marta! There's a man climbing into Casper's window!"

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and I've reconstructed it partly from memory and partly from the accounts of four different neighbors, each of whom told it differently and each of whom was certain.

The intruder did the sensible thing, which was to drop back off the roof and run. What he hadn't counted on — what nobody who isn't from these valleys counts on — is that a seventeen-year-old boy raised in Halden-at-the-Wall can move through a storm-blind village faster than a stranger can move through daylight. Tomas went out the front door in his socks, hit the drift off the porch like a swimmer, and cut the man off at the fish shed before the man had covered fifty meters. Halvard, restored to full vocal capacity by the excitement, announced events to the entire settlement.

By the time I got outside — knife in hand, boots unlaced, dignity abandoned — three men from neighboring houses had materialized out of the white with lanterns, the way villagers do, as though they'd been standing in the snow all along waiting for something to happen. Eirik Solheim was among them, which surprised me until I remembered that Eirik seemed to regard sleep as a rumor spread by southerners.

The intruder stood pinned between Tomas and the fish shed wall, hood back, snow to his knees, breathing hard. Young. Navy haircut growing out at the edges. I knew him — not by name, but by type. He'd been at Rennick's elbow in the harbor office the day of the interview, taking notes, wearing the expression of a man who found everyone around him mildly disappointing.

"Petty Officer," I said. "You're a long way from your boat."

He looked at me with the flat assessment of a professional. "Ma'am, I don't know what—"

"The window latch," Eirik said. He'd picked up the fallen blade from the snowdrift and turned it over once in his big hands. "You worked it with this. That's not a burglar's tool. That's military issue." He said it without heat, the way you'd note the weather. "You came up the coast road after dark, in a storm, to break into a guest's room. In Marta Voss's house."

Something shifted in the young officer's face at the name. Everyone in these parts had a Marta story, or knew someone who did, and even navy men apparently weren't immune.

"I'm authorized—" he started.

"Authorized by whom?" Marta asked.

She had come out last, in her coat and her good shawl, and she stood at the edge of the lantern light small and straight and terrible. The petty officer looked at her and I watched him make a mistake I suspect he'd regret for the rest of his career: he lied to her face.

"Ma'am, I was just seeking shelter from the storm. I got turned around and—"

"In a storm," Marta said, "you go to the church hall. Every soul in this valley knows it. There's a green light burning over the door that hasn't gone out in sixty years. You passed it to get here." She let that sit in the cold air. "My brother went up that glacier in 1971 and never came down, and in forty years no one has climbed through my window at night. You will tell Lieutenant Rennick — and he knows I know his name, because his father drank with mine — that the next person who touches this house answers to the whole valley, and the whole valley has long memories and shorter tempers."

The silence that followed had texture. Snow fell into it. Somewhere Halvard was still delivering his closing argument.

Then Eirik stepped forward, and what he did was very simple and very effective: he took the man's blade, snapped it in half — I want to stress that Eirik Solheim is not a large dramatic man, but he snapped that blade the way you'd snap a biscuit — and handed both halves back.

"You'll walk to the harbor office now," he said. "Tomas will watch you reach it. And tomorrow morning your party leaves Halden-at-the-Wall. All of it. The council meets at eight."

"We're guests of the municipality—"

"Not anymore," said old Anders from next door, who had thus far contributed nothing but presence and whose presence, I gathered, was considerable. "Council meets at eight. Bring your lieutenant."

They walked him out. I stood in the snow in my unlaced boots and watched the lanterns bob away toward the harbor, and I noticed two things. First, that my hands were shaking now, an hour too late, the way hands do. Second, that the compass was still in its sock in its binder under the loose floorboard where I'd moved it after dinner, because some part of me — the part my father built into me without ever saying a word about it — had heard the dog go quiet too.

* * *

The council met at eight, in the church hall, under the green light, and I was invited in a way that made clear I could attend but shouldn't enjoy it.

Halden-at-the-Wall's governing body consisted of seven people aged roughly fifty to ninety, seated at folding tables, with a thermos of coffee that was legally distinct from Eirik's coffee in that it was drinkable. They heard the facts from Tomas — who testified with such relish you'd have thought he'd wrestled the man rather than chased him — then from Eirik, then from the petty officer himself, who attended with Rennick and stuck to the shelter-from-the-storm story with a stubbornness that would have been admirable in a better cause.

Rennick wore civilian wool and his best reasonable face. He was good. I'll give him that. He expressed concern, offered cooperation, suggested misunderstandings, deployed the word *unfortunately* four times. He mentioned national security once, softly, like a man laying a card face-down. I watched the council listen with the particular patience of people who have heard outsiders explain things their whole lives.

Then Marta spoke, and it was over.

She didn't raise her voice. She told them about the green light and the church hall and the latch worked by a military blade, and then she said: "This girl came to us with nothing and asked for nothing. She guts fish. She fixed my guttering in a gale without being asked. Whatever she's carrying, whatever the navy wants, she came to this valley honest and she's lived among us honest, and I will not hand her to a man who sends boys through windows in the night." A pause, while the thermos steamed. "My brother is still on that mountain. If there's truth up there, I'd rather it came home in her hands than in theirs."

I sat very still and stared at the grain of the table and thought about how I had planned, for most of my adult life, to need no one, and how thoroughly that plan had failed, and how much better the failure tasted than the plan ever had.

The vote wasn't close. The navy party would leave by noon — their choice of direction, escorted to the valley mouth by a delegation that included Eirik and, over his grandmother's objection, Tomas. They could return as private individuals, as travelers, as guests. Not as an armed detail quartered at the harbor office with a boat in the bay and questions on their tongues.

Rennick took it well, which frightened me more than rage would have. He shook hands all around. He thanked the council for its hospitality. At the door he paused beside me, and for a moment the reasonable face slipped and I saw the thing underneath — not anger, exactly. Recalculation. A man discovering that the problem was harder than the paperwork suggested.

"You're making this difficult, Ms. Whitlock," he said quietly.

"Lieutenant," I said, "your report cleared my father's ship for that last voyage. I've read it. The weather window was closed and you signed it open anyway." His face didn't move, but something behind it did — a hairline crack, quickly plastered. "So yes. I am making it difficult. Consider it inherited."

He left at noon with his men and his escort, and the village watched them go from doorways and windows, not cheering, not waving, just watching, the way a body watches a splinter leave the skin.

That evening Marta put an extra lamp in the window facing the mountain. When I asked her why, she said, "For whoever's coming next," and went to bed, and I understood that in Halden-at-the-Wall, hope and warning were the same gesture.

Upstairs, I took the compass from the floorboard and set it on the sill. Through the storm-scoured gap in the clouds, the high glacier caught the moon — a white shoulder of the world, patient, keeping what it kept. The needle swung, steadied, and pointed at it, faithful as a dog.

Three days, maybe four, Eirik had said, before the calving winds. And somewhere on the far side of the pass, I now knew, Quill's crew was staging with hired guides of their own.

The race had spectators now. It was time to learn how to climb.
