## 18. Calving Season Opens

The wind changed in the night, and I woke to a sound I'd been waiting for without knowing it: the mountain clearing its throat.

It came up through the floorboards of Marta's house, through the stone and timber, a deep groan followed by a crack like a rifle shot fired somewhere above the clouds. Then another. Then a long, grinding exhalation, as if something enormous had shifted its weight after standing still for a hundred years.

I lay in the dark with my heart going like a diver's hammer and listened to the glacier calve.

I'd heard ice move before — bergs groaning offshore, the tick and sigh of pack ice in a cold snap. But this was different. This was structural. Somewhere up there in the dark, tons of ice were letting go of each other, and the sound carried down the valley the way a scream carries across water: flattened by distance, stripped of everything but its meaning.

*Move,* it said. *Everything moves.*

Downstairs, Marta was already at the window. She didn't startle when I came to stand beside her; she'd known I would come. The village below her window was still dark except for one lamp — Eirik's boathouse, where he'd been sleeping for three nights with his gear packed, waiting for exactly this sound.

"There it is," she said, in the tone other people used for *there's the mail.*

"That's it? That's the calving season?"

"The front edge of it." She poured coffee from a pot that had clearly been hot since midnight. "Warm wind comes up the fjord for two, three days. The icefall loosens. The route opens." She handed me a cup. "Then the warm wind dies, the cold comes back down off the summit, and the icefall closes again until next year. You don't get to be late."

"How long is the window?"

"Ask him." She nodded toward the single lamp burning down by the water. "He's counted it every spring for thirty years. He'll tell you to the hour, and then he'll tell you you're wrong anyway."

* * *

Eirik was on his dock when I got there, dressed, roped, and looking out at the glacier the way a man looks at an opponent he respects. He didn't turn around when my boots hit the planks.

"You heard it," he said.

"The whole valley heard it."

"The whole valley hears it every year." Now he turned, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before — not the reluctance I'd grown used to, not the flat professional caution. Something closer to hunger, carefully rationed. "Most years it means nothing to me. Ice opens, ice closes, tourists pay me to take them to the moraine and back. This year—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "This year it means the pass is open, and your compass points through it, and Quill's people are already on the far side."

"So we go now. Tonight."

"We go at dawn." He said it with the finality of a man who had argued this with himself all night and won. "Not tonight. You want to cross the icefall in the dark because you're excited? That's how people die up there. The ice has been moving for six hours. It hasn't finished moving. By dawn it will have settled into whatever shape it's decided on, and we read that shape in the light, and we go through it while it holds."

"And how long does it hold?"

He looked back at the mountain. The moon had gone behind cloud again, but the glacier was a pale presence above the black shoulder of the escarpment, and even from here, even in the dark, you could feel it working. A distant boom rolled down the valley, and a moment later the harbor water shivered against the pilings.

"Four days if we're lucky," Eirik said. "Three if we're honest. After that the temperature drops and the route pinches shut, and anyone still on it becomes a permanent feature of the landscape." He finally looked at me straight. "So here's what happens between now and dawn, Whitlock. You sleep. You eat everything Marta puts in front of you. You check your kit twice, and then you give it to me and I check it a third time, because you've been a diver for ten years and a mountaineer for eleven days, and the difference matters more than anything else about you."

"I've been training on the moraine for—"

"Eleven days. And you're strong, and you're stubborn, and you learn fast, which is the only reason I'm taking you at all. But strength doesn't matter on the icefall. Judgment matters. And judgment is the thing you can't cram into eleven days." He held up a hand before I could argue. "Which is why Tomas goes with us."

"Tomas already knows. He's been packing since yesterday."

"He knows, and he's packing, and I'm still going to say it out loud so you understand the arithmetic. Three ropes. Two experienced climbers and you. If you fall into a crevasse, it takes both of us to hold you. If one of us falls, it takes the other two. There is no scenario on that glacier where you are ever alone, and if you find yourself alone, that's not luck — that's failure, and you report it immediately." He said it without heat, the way he'd said everything since the day he agreed to guide me. "You spent your life trusting rope and water. Good. Same rules. The ice is just water that got impatient."

* * *

I slept badly, which surprised me, because I'd expected not to sleep at all.

The body, it turns out, keeps its own ledger. Ten years of diving had taught it that the night before work is for rest, whatever the mind is doing. So I went down into something half like sleep, and in it my father was steering the *Kestrel* through fog, and he kept checking a compass I couldn't see, and every time I asked him where we were going he said, *You'll know when the tide's right,* and I wanted to shake him and ask why everyone in my life spoke in riddles about tides—

—and then the wind gusted hard against the shutter and I was awake, and the groaning hadn't stopped. All night the glacier talked to itself. Cracks, booms, long tearing sounds like canvas ripping somewhere vast. Once there was a roar that went on for a full minute, and I heard Marta's footsteps cross the hall and pause outside my door, listening, before moving on.

At four in the morning I gave up and got dressed. The compass sat where I'd left it on the sill, needle steady, pointing at the mountain as if it had been awake all night too, keeping watch.

"Your father's initials," Marta said from the doorway. I hadn't heard her come. "On the case."

"C.W. Casper Whitlock. He signed his dive logs that way."

"Mm." She came and stood beside me at the window. Below, the sky over the escarpment was turning the color of old pewter, and the first light was finding the glacier's face. "My brother signed his letters the same way. Initials in the corner. Little habits you keep without knowing why, right up until they're the only proof you existed." She looked at me sideways. "You're afraid."

"Yes."

"Good. Fear is the mountain checking your knots. Only fools climb without it." She pressed something into my hand — a small cloth bundle, warm from her kitchen. "Flatbread and smoked fish. Eat it at the first rest stop, not before. Climbers who eat too early vomit, and vomit on a rope is its own emergency." At the door she paused. "Bring my grandson back. Bring yourself back. Everything else up there is just ice."

* * *

Dawn came up gray-gold and windless, which Eirik pronounced "a lie the weather tells to get you onto the ice."

We stood at the foot of the moraine with full packs and the whole route visible above us: the lower glacier, smooth and blue-gray; then the icefall, a jumbled white chaos of towers and crevasses where the glacier poured over a hidden step in the bedrock; then, beyond and above it, the high snowfields running toward the col, and past the col, invisible from here, the upper reaches where the compass pointed and where, presumably, Quill's crew was already staging.

Tomas arrived last, breathless, cheeks bright, twenty-two years old and vibrating with a excitement he was trying very hard to disguise as professionalism. He'd grown up under this mountain. He'd never been on the icefall. Both facts were written on him.

Eirik made us stand in a line and checked every knot, every buckle, every carabiner, handling our gear with the impersonal intimacy of a surgeon. When he reached me he paused at the compass, riding in its pouch on my chest strap.

"It stays zipped," he said. "If we have to navigate, I navigate. That thing is a last resort, not a toy."

"It found a snow hole in a whiteout."

"It got lucky in a whiteout. On the icefall, luck is a currency you spend once." He zipped the pouch himself, firmly, then met my eyes. "Last chance, Whitlock. Once we're on the ice, there's no boat to swim back to. Say it plain: are you ready?"

I looked up the route. The morning light was climbing the icefall tower by tower, lighting the seracs from within until they glowed like frozen surf, beautiful and murderous. Somewhere up there was a vault my father had helped bury, a device worth enough to erase my debts a thousand times over, and a truth worth more than either. Somewhere behind me were Rennick's questions and Quill's watchers and a harbor full of people who knew my name now, which was the one thing I'd spent ten years making sure never happened.

And somewhere in my chest, under the fear, was the strangest sensation: that I was finally swimming in the right direction.

"I'm ready," I said.

Eirik grunted — his version of a blessing — and turned to lead out. Tomas clipped in behind him. I took the last position on the rope, the way you take the last position on a dive line, watching the backs of the people whose lives were now mechanically joined to yours.

The glacier boomed again, closer now, a sound I felt in my teeth. Above us, high on the icefall, a serac the size of a church leaned, sighed, and shed a block the size of a truck. It fell in silence — distance eats sound — and then the crash arrived, rolling down the glacier like thunder with a grudge.

Eirik didn't even turn around. "That," he called back, "is the mountain telling us the window's open. Move fast, move smooth, and when I shout *ice*, you don't look up — you run."

We stepped off the moraine and onto the living ice, and the whole white massif above us seemed to lean in and take notice.

Behind us, far down the valley, I thought I heard the thin insect whine of an engine that had no business being in Halden-at-the-Wall — but the wind carried it away before I could be sure, and there was no time to be sure of anything anymore.

Only forward. Only up.
