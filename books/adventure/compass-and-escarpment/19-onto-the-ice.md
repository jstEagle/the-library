## 19. Onto the Ice

Dawn came up gray and cold over Halden-at-the-Wall, and I stood in Eirik Solheim's yard wearing more borrowed clothing than I had ever owned at one time, feeling like a scarecrow that had been dressed by committee.

"Too loose," Eirik said, tugging a strap at my hip. "The pack rides you, not the other way around. Again."

I tightened it. He watched me with the expression of a man who had already imagined every way I could die on his mountain and was deciding which ones were preventable.

Tomas Voss arrived as the light strengthened, seventeen years old and vibrating with a joy he was trying very hard to disguise as boredom. He had Marta's dark eyes and her stubborn jaw, and he carried a coil of rope over one shoulder like it weighed nothing, which for him it probably did.

"You're late," Eirik said.

"I'm exactly on time," Tomas said. "You said dawn. This is dawn."

"This is dawn *late*. Dawn is when the birds start arguing." He shouldered his own pack — enormous, immaculate, every strap worn to a shine from decades of use — and looked at me once more, head to boots. "Last chance, Whitlock. The pass is open. It will not stay open. Once we're on the ice, we're on the ice."

"The tide doesn't wait either," I said. "I've spent my life learning that."

Something flickered across his face — approval, or maybe just resignation that I hadn't come to my senses overnight. "Then we walk."

* * *

The first hours were almost kind. A goat track above the village, then a valley of gray scree where the meltwater ran loud and bright, then the long slow climb through dwarf birch that shrank as we rose until it gave up entirely and left us nothing but stone and lichen and sky. My legs remembered diving — the thighs, the lungs, the patience of moving under load — and they complained anyway, because legs are honest creatures and mine knew the difference between swimming and carrying forty pounds uphill.

Eirik set a pace that seemed insultingly slow until I understood it wasn't slowness at all. It was arithmetic. Every step calculated against the day's length, the weather's mood, the distance between water sources, the weight of our doubt. He didn't talk much. When he did, it was short and useful: "Drink now." "Step where I step." "That sound is just the mountain settling. Probably."

"Probably," Tomas repeated cheerfully, behind me. "He says probably when it's definitely fine."

"And when it isn't?"

"When it isn't, he says nothing at all. You'll know."

We ate cold food at midday with the escarpment falling away below us, the sea a hammered sheet of pewter far down, my father's boat a speck I couldn't actually see but looked for anyway. Somewhere out there was the wreck they don't name, and the cave, and the whole drowned architecture of secrets I'd been crawling through since the lowest tide in forty years. Up here it all looked small. That was either comforting or insulting; I hadn't decided.

The glacier appeared an hour later, and I stopped pretending I wasn't afraid.

It came down between two shoulders of black rock like something poured rather than placed — white going blue going green-white in its depths, cracked everywhere with fissures that caught the afternoon light and threw it back in colors ice has no business knowing. Above it, the icefall: a staircase of broken seracs climbing toward the col, chaotic as a shipwreck frozen mid-sinking. Which, I supposed, was roughly what it was.

"That's the route?" I asked.

"That's the route," Eirik said. "Today we only reach the lower tongue. Tomorrow, if the mountain permits, the icefall. The day after, the col." He glanced at me. "The compass. Is it pulling?"

I took it out. In all the weeks since the cave, the needle had done its impossible work — swinging off north, straining toward the glacier like a dog at the end of a chain. But up close, standing on the moraine with the ice breathing its cold at us, it did something new. It steadied. Not pointing vaguely anymore. Pointing. Locked onto something up there with the certainty of a thing that had been waiting a long time.

"It's pulling true," I said, and heard my own voice go quiet.

"Then it knows we're coming," Tomas said, delighted. Eirik did not look delighted. Eirik looked like a man doing arithmetic again, and this time the numbers included me.

* * *

Rope school began on the lower tongue, where the ice was old and gentle and the crevasses were mostly bridged by snow strong enough to hold a polite person walking carefully.

"Be impolite," Eirik said. "Assume every bridge is lying to you. Probe with your axe. Weight your steps. And when — not if, Whitlock, *when* — someone goes in, you fall into position, you dig in, and you do not move until I tell you. Your body is the anchor. If you panic, three people die instead of one. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Say it back."

"If someone goes in, I'm the anchor, and if I panic we all die."

"Good. Tomas, front. Whitlock, middle. I'll take rear, where I can watch you both lie to yourselves about being fine."

We roped up at fifty-foot intervals, and the rope changed everything. I'd spent my adult life alone underwater, alone on boats, alone with ledgers and debts and the particular solitude of a person nobody is coming to save. The rope was the opposite of all that. It was a live connection, warm even through gloves, transmitting every hesitation and every confidence between us. When Tomas moved, I felt it before I saw it. When Eirik stopped, I stopped, my body obeying him faster than my mind could ask why.

For two hours it went beautifully, which should have been my warning.

The crevasse opened without ceremony — no crack of warning, no dramatic groan. One moment the snow under my right foot was ground. The next it was a suggestion, and the suggestion failed.

I have fallen through things before. Decks, hatches, the surface tension of my own composure. But snow bridges fail differently than anything else. There's a heartbeat where the world is still holding you and also has already let you go, both at once, and in that heartbeat I understood with total clarity that below me was not water. Water forgives. Water slows you, accepts you, lets you fight. Ice does none of these things. Below me was blue dark going down further than my headlamp would ever reach, and walls that would break me on the way if the bottom didn't.

The rope snapped taut.

My harness bit into my hips and thighs hard enough to lift a shout out of me, and then I was hanging — half in, half out, chest against the broken lip of the bridge, one boot still uselessly planted on the surface side, the rest of me committed to the dark. Snow crumbled past my face in slow motion, glittering, going down and down without any sound of landing.

"*Anchor!*" Eirik's voice, flat and absolute.

I dug in. Axe shaft buried, elbows locked, boot wedged, everything I had driven into the slope behind me while the rope tried to feed me to the crevasse. My arms shook. My breath came in whoops. Somewhere below my dangling leg the blue dark waited with the patience of geology.

"Tomas — flank, ten o'clock, backup anchor." Eirik moved fast and economical, no wasted motion, rope paying through his hands. "Whitlock. Look at me."

I couldn't. I was looking down.

"*Look at me.*"

I looked at him. His face was calm in a way that I understood, suddenly, was not calm at all — it was discipline so old it had become texture.

"You are not falling," he said. "You fell. It's finished. Now you are a piece of equipment, and equipment does not flail. Breathe. Four counts in, four counts out."

I breathed. The shaking got worse before it got better, which apparently was how it worked.

"On my count, walk yourself backward up the slope. Small steps. The rope is yours as much as ours — use it, don't fight it. One."

I walked myself out of the mouth of the crevasse one humiliating inch at a time, Tomas hauling on the flank line, Eirik taking my weight in increments, until I collapsed on solid snow with my heart slamming and my hands useless claws inside my mitts.

Tomas crouched beside me, grinning. "First one's the worst."

"There's a second one?"

"There's always a second one."

Eirik knelt, checked my harness buckles with brisk fingers, then held my wrist and counted my pulse without asking permission. "Fast," he said. "But steady. Good." He sat back on his heels and studied me, and whatever he was looking for, he found enough of it. "Most people freeze their first time. Most people who freeze their first time shouldn't be here. You anchored."

"I screamed."

"You anchored *and* screamed. Both is allowed." He stood and offered a hand up. "The bridge lied to you. That's not your failure. Walking where I walk won't always save you — sometimes the mountain just takes its tax. Today it took a fright. Cheap, as these things go."

I got up. My legs were jelly and my dignity was somewhere down in the blue dark with the falling snow, but underneath the terror there was something else, something I recognized from the other side of my life — from surfacing after a deep dive with the case secured, from signing away another year of my life to creditors and feeling, absurdly, alive. Terror and exhilaration, braided tight. I'd been chasing that braid in cold water for years. Turns out it lived up here too.

"Again," I said.

Eirik's eyebrows went up a fraction of an inch, which from him was applause. "Again," he agreed.

* * *

We made the lower camp as the light went copper, a shelf of moraine gravel tucked against the glacier's shoulder where the wind couldn't quite get its teeth in. Tomas built the shelter with the speed of someone raised to it, and Eirik melted snow and spoke about stove fuel and hydration with the soothing monotony of a man keeping our minds on small survivable things.

I sat apart a little, where I could see the icefall in the last light. It burned orange and rose, impossibly beautiful, impossibly hostile. Somewhere up in that chaos was the route. Somewhere beyond the col was whatever the compass wanted, whatever my father had annotated on charts in a handwriting I'd known my whole life without ever really reading.

I took it out. The brass was cold enough to sting even through my glove's thin palm.

In the dying light the needle didn't waver. It pointed up the glacier, into the icefall, dead steady, and I thought about the cave beneath the escarpment, about the rock that had grown around this thing like a fist closing, about the navy divers and Rennick's soft questions and Quill's men tearing through my boat looking for exactly this. All of them wanted what pointed. None of them, I suspected, had ever asked what it was pointing *at*, or why it had been fused into stone in a sea cave like something deliberately hidden rather than accidentally lost.

"You keep looking at that thing like it's going to explain itself," Tomas said, dropping down beside me with two mugs.

"It's been explaining itself all along. I just haven't liked the translation."

"Marta says her brother carried something up there. Something heavy." He handed me a mug — sweet tea, scalding, the best thing I'd tasted in weeks. "She doesn't say what. She says the government men came twice, after. Asked questions with their hats in their hands and their eyes on the doors."

"Eirik's father too," I said quietly. "On the expedition."

"I know. He told me last night, after you'd gone to bed. Took him twenty years to say it out loud, I think." Tomas blew on his tea. "Funny kind of mountain. Collects fathers."

Across the camp, Eirik stood watching the icefall with his back to us, and I wondered what he saw up there — a route, or a grave, or both.

"We sleep in shifts," he called, not turning. "Storm window holds tomorrow. We move at first light, straight into the icefall while it's frozen solid. Afternoon sun loosens the seracs, and I don't fancy introducing any of us to one."

"And after the icefall?" I asked.

"After the icefall, the col. And after the col—" he finally turned, and the last light caught his face, "—after the col, we find out what your father died keeping, and whether the mountain means to let any of us carry the answer home."

I looked down at the compass. The needle lay against its stop, straining upward into the dark, patient as debt, certain as tide.

"Sleep," Eirik said. "Tomorrow the mountain starts charging real prices."

And high above us, in the frozen dark, something shifted — a deep, distant groan rolling down the glacier like a door testing its hinges.
