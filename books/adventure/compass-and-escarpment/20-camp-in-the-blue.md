## 20. Camp in the Blue

The storm found us an hour before we found shelter.

It came off the icefall like something with intent — not weather but a pursuit, a white animal that had scented us on the glacier and wanted us for supper. Eirik called it in two words, "Blue cave," and pointed his chin at a seam of deeper color in the wall of ice to our left, and we went at it like drowning people going at a plank.

Tomas reached it first. He's twenty-two and built like a stack of firewood, and he got his axe into the lip of the entrance and hauled himself up and in, then turned around and pulled me through by the pack straps hard enough to leave bruises I'd find later and be grateful for. Eirik came last, backwards, watching the whiteout swallow our tracks behind us as if he expected the mountain to be following.

Then we were inside, and the world was blue.

I've spent my life underwater. I know every shade the sea can make — the green-black of deep water, the silver-green of shallows over sand, the bruised violet just before the light gives out. But I had never seen blue like this. The cave walls were old compressed ice, centuries of winters pressed into glass, and the storm-light coming through them arrived already filtered, already changed, so that everything — Tomas's frost-white eyelashes, Eirik's grey beard, my own hands — glowed faintly sapphire, as if we had died somewhere better than we deserved and this was the waiting room.

"Beautiful," Tomas said, teeth chattering.

"Don't touch the walls with bare skin," Eirik said, which is guide-speak for *yes, beautiful, now stop admiring it before it kills you.*

We did what you do. We got out of the wind's reach, back far enough into the cave that the cold stopped trying to peel our faces off. We dug the stove out, melted snow, forced down food none of us tasted. We checked each other's faces for the white patches that mean frostbite is making its claim. My cheeks were fine. Two toes on my left foot were arguing about it, and I flexed them until they gave up their complaint.

Outside, the storm worked itself into a fury. The sound came through the ice like the sea heard from inside a wreck — vast, muffled, patient. It should have frightened me. Instead it made me homesick, which is a stupid thing to feel in a hole in a glacier, but there it was. A body gets used to certain noises meaning *shelter*, and mine had decided the roar of moving water outside solid walls counted.

"You're smiling," Eirik observed. He sat across the stove from me, steam rising between us, and looked like a man who had been carved by someone who only knew how to carve one expression and had gotten bored halfway through.

"It sounds like being under a boat."

"Everything about you sounds like being under a boat." He fed another tablet to the stove. "You walk like the ground might not hold."

"The ground usually doesn't."

* * *

We took first watch in shifts, because Eirik doesn't believe in sleeping all at once on a glacier any more than he believes in luck, mercy, or decaffeinated coffee. Tomas lost the coin toss and curled up against his pack like a dog turning three circles, and was gone inside a minute, snoring with the total commitment of the young.

Which left Eirik and me sitting in the blue dark with a stove the size of a coffee tin between us, listening to the mountain try to get in.

I had the compass out. I didn't plan to take it out; my hand just went to the inner pocket the way it always does now, the way some people touch a crucifix or a photograph. The needle lay steady in its housing, pointing — not north. Never north. Pointing up-glacier, into the heart of the icefall, toward whatever my father had known about and never told me and died without telling me anyway.

Eirik watched me look at it. He'd been watching me look at it for days, in the way of a man who counts things.

"My father carried a compass too," he said.

I looked up. The blue light made his face strange — younger, somehow, all the weathering washed out of it, leaving just the bones of the man underneath.

"He was Halvard Solheim," Eirik said. "Of Halden-at-the-Wall. Guide, second class. Married, one son." He paused. "One son who talks too much about other people's business and not enough about his own."

"Eirik—"

"He went up with the expedition. Thirty-one years ago. Nine men and one woman, government papers, sealed crates, and a guide contract that paid triple what it should have." His voice stayed level, the way water stays level right up until it goes over the edge. "My mother waited one winter. Then she stopped talking about him, which in Halden means she buried him without a body. And I grew up in a house where his gear hung on pegs by the door like he'd stepped out to check the weather and would be back before the light went."

I didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that wasn't either pity, which he'd hate, or questions, which could wait.

"I was four when he left," Eirik went on. "I remember exactly one thing about him. He knelt down in the doorway and he said, *Keep the stove fed, little man.* That's it. That's the whole inheritance. Keep the stove fed." He almost smiled. "And then thirty-one years later a diver walks into my village with her father's boat and her father's debts and a compass that points at my mountain, and asks me to take her up the route nobody takes, during the season nobody climbs."

"Tomas told you about the compass before I asked you to guide me."

"Tomas tells everyone everything. It's his best quality and his worst." Eirik leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the stove-light moved across his face. "But I'd have said yes even if he hadn't. Do you understand that? You didn't have to convince me. You walked into the tavern smelling of salt and trouble, and you asked about the icefall, and I thought: *there she is.* The other half of it. Because your father was on that expedition too, Casper. Wasn't he."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway. "The journals will tell us. If we find the camp."

"I know what the journals will tell us." He said it quietly. "I've had thirty-one years to imagine it. Every version. He froze. He fell. He ran. He—" The level voice finally cracked, one hairline fracture, and then he sealed it again, the way you'd seal a crevasse bridge, carefully, knowing what was underneath. "I need to know which version is true. Not for grief. Grief I've finished with. For the *shape* of it. A man can't spend his whole life keeping a stove fed for a ghost without wanting to know if the ghost kept anything fed for him."

The storm screamed against the cave mouth. Somewhere above us, deep in the icefall, something enormous shifted and settled, a groan that traveled down through the glacier and up through the floor and into our bones, the mountain clearing its throat.

"My father never talked about the glacier either," I said. "He talked about everything else. Fish prices. Engine tolerances. Which harbors cheated you on fuel. He was a talker. But there was a room in him that stayed shut, and I was too young and too stupid to wonder what he kept in it." I turned the compass over in my hands. In the blue light the brass looked almost black. "Now I'm going up there to open it."

"And if what's in the room is worse than the debt?"

"Then it's still mine to carry. That's what inheritance is." I looked at him. "Isn't it."

Eirik held my gaze for a long moment. Around us the cave glowed and the storm raged and Tomas snored like a small avalanche, and something passed between us that neither of us named — the recognition, maybe, of two people who had been carrying other people's secrets so long they'd forgotten the straps were cutting into their shoulders.

"We'll find the camp," he said at last. "If it's there to find. And then we'll both know whether we've been guarding something worth guarding, or just guarding." He reached over and tapped the compass housing once, gently, with one finger, the way you'd tap a stranger's shoulder. "Your father's needle points true. Mine pointed home. Let's hope they're pointing at the same place."

* * *

I slept after that, or something near it — the shallow, bright-eyed sleep of altitude, full of falling dreams from which I surfaced every hour to check the stove, the rope coiled between us, the shape of Tomas breathing.

In the deepest part of the night I woke to voices and lay still, listening, before I understood that Eirik was awake too and talking low, and that the other voice was Tomas, who was supposed to be asleep and evidently had been listening longer than I had.

"—my grandfather's brother," Tomas was saying, barely above the storm. "Marta's brother. Jon Voss. He was the surveyor. He drew the maps for the route." A pause. "Grandmother doesn't say his name. She says *the one who went up*. Like there's only one direction that matters."

"There is, on this mountain," Eirik said.

"She keeps his boots. Under her bed. Thirty-one years and she keeps his boots oiled." Tomas's laugh came out wrong, wet at the edges. "I used to think it was morbid. Now I think — I don't know what I think. That's why I came. Don't tell her I said that."

"Your secret's safe. I'm a professional."

"You're a grump with ropes."

"That's what I said."

I closed my eyes and let them talk, and let the blue hold us, and thought about boots oiled for thirty-one years by a woman who refused to say a name, and a stove kept fed by a boy who became a man who became our guide, and a compass that pointed at all of it — at the camp, at the truth, at whatever my father had shut inside the locked room of himself.

Two inheritances converging on one stretch of ice. Three families' dead, all walking uphill with us.

Somewhere below, I knew, Rennick was climbing too, or paying men to climb for him. Somewhere beyond the pass, Quill's crew was staging. The window was open and closing, and the mountain had opinions about all of us.

But that was tomorrow's weather. Tonight there was only the blue, and the stove, and the small human noise of people keeping faith with their ghosts.

I was nearly asleep again when Eirik shook my shoulder — gently, but Eirik's gentle is other men's urgent.

"Casper." His face was turned toward the cave mouth, toward the storm. "Listen."

I listened. Under the wind, under the groan of the ice, there was something else. A light. Faint, flickering, far up-glacier through the whiteout — orange, moving, wrong.

Headlamps. Someone else was on the icefall tonight, climbing in a storm no sane person would climb in.

Or being driven up it.
