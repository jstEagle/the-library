## 21. The Serac Field

The glacier changed its mind about us at dawn.

We'd camped on a shelf of blue ice above the icefall, and I woke to a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat — a deep, structural groan that came up through the ground and into my sleeping bag and into my teeth. Eirik was already up, standing at the edge of the shelf with his back to me, watching the light come up over the seracs.

"Good morning to you too," I said.

He didn't turn around. "The mountain is talking. We listen."

"It's saying what, exactly?"

"That we should hurry." He turned then, and his face had the particular flatness he got when he was worried and had decided not to spend energy on it. "Eat. Rope up in twenty minutes. Today we cross the serac field, and I want to be through it before the sun warms the ice."

Tomas was slower to wake. He'd been quiet since the col — quieter than even his usual careful quiet — and I'd caught him twice looking back down the route we'd climbed, as if checking whether retreat were still an option. I understood the impulse. I'd spent three years of my life checking exits. But there's a difference between a man who checks the door because he likes knowing where it is, and a man who checks it because he's already decided to use it. I wasn't sure yet which one Tomas was, and I didn't like not being sure.

We ate cold oats and harder bread. Eirik went over the rules for the serac field in the tone of a man reciting scripture he'd personally watched kill people.

"The ice towers are melting from below. They lean. They fall without warning — no groan first, sometimes no sound at all until they're falling. We move fast but we do not run. Running makes bad footwork, and bad footwork in the seracs is how you die tired." He looked at each of us in turn. "When I say move, you move. When I say freeze, you freeze so completely that your heart apologizes for beating. Yes?"

"Heart apologizes," Tomas said. "Got it."

I said nothing. My stomach had its own opinion about the whole enterprise and I saw no reason to dignify it out loud.

* * *

The serac field was everything the word promises and nothing you want.

Picture a city built by a drunk god: towers of ice the size of houses and churches, leaning against each other at angles architecture would forbid, blue-green in their depths and white-rimed on their shoulders, all of it creaking as the morning sun found it. The route threaded between them along a corridor maybe two rope-lengths wide, and the walls of the corridor were made of frozen collapse — jumbled blocks fused together, evidence that this passage had been carved by previous falls and would be re-carved by future ones. The glacier keeps a ledger here too. Everything gets written down eventually.

Eirik led. I came second, which he'd insisted on — "the middle position learns from both ends" — and Tomas took the rear with the slack management, which he did well. Whatever else Tomas was or wasn't, he was a competent mountaineer, and competence is its own kind of honesty. You can't fake a well-tended rope.

For the first hour it was almost beautiful. That's the trap of these places; I've thought about it since, and I think it's deliberate, if a glacier can be said to have intentions. The light comes down between the towers in long slanted columns full of drifting ice-dust, and the dust catches fire, and you're walking through burning air, and some animal part of your brain decides this is a temple and you've been invited. Then a tower the size of a grain silo settles six inches with a crack like a rifle, and the animal part revises its theology.

"Move," Eirik said, and we moved.

We crossed a snow bridge over a crevasse wide enough to swallow our whole camp. I didn't look down. Looking down in the seracs is a luxury for people with better odds.

It was near midday when the mountain stopped talking and started shouting.

We'd just come around the shoulder of a leaning tower Eirik had nicknamed — I don't know when, or why guides name the things trying to kill them, but they do — *the Bishop*, when the sound started. Not a crack this time. A roar, low and rising, coming from somewhere below and behind us, back toward the icefall we'd climbed through yesterday.

Eirik froze. We froze. My heart apologized.

"Calving," he said quietly. "Big one. On the lower face."

We couldn't see it. The seracs blocked everything except sky. But we could hear it — a sound like the world's largest door being torn off the world's largest hinge, and under it a grinding, tumbling thunder that went on and on, thirty seconds, forty, a minute, ice the weight of city blocks letting go of the glacier face and falling into the sea of the melt-lake below, and the whole time the ice under my boots trembled like a struck bell.

Then it stopped. And the silence after was worse, because silence in the seracs isn't peace. It's the pause while the mountain decides what else to say.

Eirik stood listening for a long moment, head tilted, reading the glacier the way Marta Voss read weather off the water. Then he said something in Norwegian, short and flat, and sat down right there on the ice and got out his map.

"We don't sit," I said. "You said—"

"We don't sit where things fall. Nothing falls here for the next hour. Everything behind us is currently rearranging itself." He spread the map on his knee. His hands were steady, but I noticed he smoothed the same corner twice. "Come. Look."

I knelt beside him. Tomas crowded in.

The calving zone marked the base of the icefall — the chaotic jumble we'd spent two days climbing, the only route down. Eirik's finger traced it. "That section has let go. All of it. The lower icefall is gone."

"Gone," I repeated.

"Recast. It will rebuild itself over years, new seracs, new chaos. But the route we came up—" He drew a line and then crossed it out with his thumbnail, a small decisive violence. "Is buried under a hundred thousand tons of fresh collapse. Nobody goes down that way now. Maybe nobody goes down that way for five years."

I sat back on my heels. The math arrived slowly and then all at once, the way cold water arrives when a boat starts taking it. Behind us: a wall of fresh rubble. Ahead of us: the high route, the col, the vault, whatever waited there. And beyond that — I looked at Eirik. "The descent on the far side?"

"Also through icefall country. Also seasonal." He met my eyes. "But the window holds for perhaps four more days. If we keep moving, we cross before it closes. If we stop—" He shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "Then we winter on the glacier, and we are not equipped to winter on the glacier."

"So it's forward," Tomas said. There was something in his voice I couldn't place at first, and then I could: relief. Carefully hidden relief, the kind a man lets out when the choice has been taken out of his hands. A man who'd been looking back down the route all morning had just been told the door behind him was bricked shut, and part of him — the part that had wanted permission — was glad.

I filed that away. I was getting a drawer full of things filed away about Tomas, and the drawer was getting heavy.

"There's another option," I said. "We wait here. Signal from the ridge — Rennick has helicopters. Quill's team is somewhere behind us on the mountain—"

"Quill's team is behind us on a route that no longer exists," Eirik said. "Or they took the eastern variation, in which case they are a day behind and climbing ice that is actively falling on them. Either way they are not a rescue party." He began rolling the map. "And I will not signal any helicopter to come collect us from a calving zone. You have seen what the navy does with things it collects."

I had. I'd been one of the things, once, briefly, and I'd spent years making sure I never fit in a crate again.

"Forward," I said.

"Forward," Eirik agreed, and stood, and coiled the map away, and there was something almost gentle in how quickly he did it, as if he'd known what I'd decide before I did and simply wanted to spare me the theater of deliberation. "Drink water. Ten minutes. Then the worst part of the field — the corridor under the towers we call the Nuns. After that, easier going to the col."

"Why the Nuns?" Tomas asked.

"Because they are tall, they are close together, and they are praying," Eirik said. "Pray faster than they do."

* * *

The Nuns were worse than advertised, and Eirik advertises honestly, which somehow made it worse still.

Three towers leaned together overhead like conspirators, leaving a slot of sky maybe ten feet wide. The floor of the slot was polished blue ice running downhill at an angle that would have been a fun slide in different company. We clipped into a fixed line Eirik rigged across the worst pitch, and we went through one at a time while the other two stood anchor in the open — relatively open — ground beyond.

I went second again. From inside the slot the world reduced itself to boot-fronts, rope, breath, and the blue glow of the ice walls, which was beautiful in the way that deep water is beautiful, which is to say it was beautiful the way drowning is peaceful. Halfway through, the tower on my left groaned — actually groaned, a long creaking moan like a ship rolling in heavy swell — and shed a boulder of ice the size of a washing machine that smashed down ten feet ahead of me and exploded into shards that rang off my helmet and filled the slot with cold smoke.

I did not run. I am proud of this. Every nerve I owned voted unanimously to run, and I stood there with my axe planted and my heart doing something medically inadvisable, and I waited for Eirik's voice.

"Move," he called, calm as a man discussing tides. "Now. Quickly now."

I moved. I came out the far side with my legs shaking and discovered Tomas anchoring my rope, and he gave me a look — quick, sideways, almost shy — and said, "Nice helmet."

"Nice anchor," I said.

It wasn't much. But it was the first thing Tomas had said to me in two days that wasn't procedural, and I clung to it the way you cling to anything warm in a cold place.

* * *

We cleared the main field by late afternoon and gained a shoulder of wind-scoured snow below the col, and that was where we stopped and looked back, because Eirik made us look back. "Always look," he said. "A mountaineer who doesn't look back doesn't know the mountain, and a person who doesn't know the mountain belongs to it instead."

So we looked.

The serac field lay below us, golden in the low sun, and beyond it the lower icefall — our road in, two days of work, two nights of cold, every meter of it paid for in sweat and terror — was unrecognizable. Where we had climbed, there was now a scar of fresh collapse half a mile wide, white blocks tumbled like dropped crockery, steam still rising faintly where new ice had ground against old. The melt-lake beyond it had a new iceberg archipelago, and even from this distance I could see the water still shivering around them, rings spreading out and out like the mountain was still speaking, in a language with only one word.

"No retreat," Tomas said. He said it lightly, but I heard the thing underneath it again, that same relieved note, and this time I let myself look at him properly while he said it. He was watching the ruins of our back-trail with an expression I can only call unburdened.

"You okay?" I asked.

He startled slightly. "Fine. Just — thinking that's a lot of ice."

"It's a lot of ice," I agreed, and let it go, because there was nothing else to do with it yet. Suspicion without proof is just a stone you carry. I had carried enough stones.

Eirik stood apart, facing the wreckage of the route, and when I joined him he spoke without turning. "My father's journal says they crossed this same field in June, forty years ago. He wrote that the Bishop fell the day after they passed. He wrote—" He paused, and I realized his voice had gone carefully level, the way mine went carefully level whenever anyone mentioned my father. "He wrote that they argued that night about whether to go on. Two wanted to turn back. The rest outvoted them."

"What happened to the two who wanted to turn back?"

"They kept going." He was quiet a moment. "They were roped to men who kept going. That is the arithmetic of the rope, Casper. You inherit other people's decisions."

I looked at the rope lying between us on the snow, our three lives braided into sixty meters of kernmantle, and thought about all the decisions I'd inherited — my father's debts, my father's secrets, my father's compass sitting in my pack pointing its impossible point. Somewhere below us, under a hundred thousand tons of fresh ice, lay the path home. Somewhere above us, past the col, lay whatever my father had spent his life guarding and dying rather than selling.

There was no version of this where I turned around. There hadn't been since the cave, maybe since the wreck, maybe since the day I signed for his boat and found the second ledger sewn into the chart table. Retreat hadn't been sealed by the calving. It had been sealed years ago, by a dead man who loved me enough to leave me a map and not enough to leave me a choice.

"Get some sleep," Eirik said. "We take the col at first light. The pass will hold maybe four days, and tomorrow we find out whether Quill's people are ahead of us or behind."

"And if they're ahead?"

He picked up his axe and looked up at the col, where the last light was going red on the snow.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we climb toward the sound of other people's ropes."
