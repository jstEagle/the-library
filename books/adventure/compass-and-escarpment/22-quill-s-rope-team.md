## 22. Quill's Rope Team

The morning after the calving that sealed our retreat, Eirik woke us by kicking the bottom of my sleeping bag with the tenderness of a man shoveling coal.

"Up. We have company."

I'd learned by then that when Eirik said *company*, he didn't mean a friendly face and a thermos. He meant trouble wearing crampons.

We broke camp in the blue half-light, the glacier still groaning from yesterday's collapse — a deep, tectonic complaint, like the mountain clearing its throat before saying something we wouldn't like. Behind us, where we'd crossed two days ago, the route was gone. A wall of fresh ice rubble stood where the fixed lines had been, fifty feet of shattered serac stacked like a god's abandoned masonry project. There was no going back. There was only up, and whatever was ahead.

And ahead, it turned out, was Quill.

Tomas spotted them first. He'd gone forward to probe the next stretch of icefall and came back down the rope at a speed that made my stomach drop for reasons that had nothing to do with technique.

"Six of them," he said, breath smoking. "Maybe seven. Moving fast. Too fast."

Eirik took the glasses from his chest pocket and lay flat on the ridge crest, elbows in snow, studying the upper glacier. I lay beside him and followed his gaze.

There they were: a rope team strung across the white like dark stitches in pale skin, moving with the ugly efficiency of people who consider the mountain an obstacle rather than an employer. Even at this distance I could pick out Quill — or I told myself I could, which amounts to the same thing. The lead figure moved with a certain economical arrogance, placing picks exactly where they'd do the most good and nowhere near where caution would have suggested.

"They're cutting the corner," Eirik said quietly. "Under the east seracs."

I looked. The route they'd chosen shaved perhaps forty minutes off the safe line — forty minutes bought by traveling directly beneath a row of ice cliffs that had been shedding house-sized blocks since the warm winds started.

"That's not a route," I said. "That's a suicide note with waypoints."

"It's a schedule." Eirik passed me the glasses. "Quill has a timetable and he intends to keep it. The mountain can file a complaint later."

Through the lenses I watched one of Quill's climbers cross a snow bridge that sagged visibly under him — a bridge Eirik would have probed for ten minutes and bypassed entirely. The man didn't even test it. He just walked across the way you'd cross a parking lot, because his boss was behind him, and bosses are heavier than crevasses.

"He'll kill someone doing that," I said.

"Yes." Eirik took the glasses back. "Probably himself last, though. Men like that always survive to bury the others."

* * *

We held a council of war over cold oatmeal, which is the traditional venue for such things.

"They're a day ahead of us," Tomas said. "Maybe less now, if they keep that pace."

"Then we don't catch them today," Eirik said. "Or tomorrow. We climb our line, at our speed, and let the mountain sort out their arithmetic."

"And if they reach the vault first?" I asked.

Eirik chewed his oatmeal as though it had personally wronged him. "Then they'll be standing on top of a hole in the ice waiting for us, and we'll have the advantage of arriving rested and unhaunted." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. "Casper. Listen to me. You cannot race them. The moment you start climbing angry, you start climbing stupid, and stupid on this ice has a very short learning curve."

"I know."

"You say you know. Your father's charts say you know. But I've watched you look at that compass every night like it owes you money, and men who climb to collect debts die on the first bad pitch."

He wasn't wrong, which annoyed me. I turned the compass over in my mittened hand — its needle steady, patient, pointing at the icefall above us as it had pointed since the cave, since the wreck, since possibly before I was born. My father's initials caught the early light. *J.W.* Two letters scratched into brass by a man who'd walked this same glacier thirty years ago and turned back, and spent the rest of his life paying for the turning.

"We climb smart," I said. "But we climb."

* * *

We climbed.

The day was hard and clean — the kind of cold that makes everything honest. We crossed the moraine shelf below the east seracs, taking the long way around precisely because Quill hadn't, and twice we heard the mountain discharge somewhere out of sight: flat cracks like rifle shots, then the deeper rumble of ice finding new opinions about gravity. Each time, Tomas flinched and looked toward the corner Quill's team had cut.

Nothing came down while we watched. That proved nothing. The glacier keeps its own books, and it settles accounts on its own schedule.

By midday we'd gained the shoulder of rock that split the icefall, and from there we could see the whole upper basin — and see that Quill's team had stopped.

They were gathered at the base of a steep headwall where someone, years ago, had fixed a line of rope: old expedition hardware, sun-rotted sheath over a core that might or might not still hold, anchored to pitons driven into the ice by hands long frozen elsewhere. It was the only reasonable way up the headwall without drilling new anchors, which takes hours nobody had.

Quill's team was clipping it. All six of them. Onto a rope that had been hanging in ultraviolet light and freeze-thaw cycles since before I could legally buy a drink.

"Oh no," Tomas said.

"Oh yes," Eirik said grimly. "Come on. We need to get there before they all commit."

"Why?" I asked, already moving. "If the rope fails, that's their problem."

"Because if the rope fails under the fourth man, the first three are stranded above a headwall with no descent, and they will do something desperate to get down. And because—" he paused, breathing hard from the traverse, "—because I refuse to watch six men die on my glacier and write a report about it afterward. Some things you don't get to be neutral about, Casper Whitlock. Not here."

So we went fast, which is to say we went carefully at the maximum speed careful allows, which is slower than fear wants and faster than wisdom prefers. The compass rode against my sternum, and I noticed — with the part of my brain not occupied by footwork — that its needle trembled slightly whenever I leaned toward Quill's bearing, then steadied again toward the icefall. As if it disapproved of detours. As if it knew whose hands were pulling toward it from the other side of the mountain.

* * *

We reached the base of the headwall twenty minutes behind them. Four of Quill's climbers were already up; two were mid-pitch on the rotten fixed line, and Quill himself stood at the anchor point directing traffic with the breezy authority of a man who has never once cleaned up after his own decisions.

He saw us coming and smiled. I felt the smile from thirty meters away, the way you feel a draft under a door.

"Well," he called, voice carrying flatly across the ice. "The diver. And her mountain nannies. Come to share the rope?"

"Come to tell you your rope is dead," Eirik answered, stopping well clear, hands visible, ice axe grounded. An old guide's posture: non-threatening and absolutely immovable.

"The rope got us four up already."

"The rope got four men up on luck. Sheaths rot from the inside. You can't see the damage until the core's powder." Eirik nodded upward. "Your fifth man is betting his life on thirty-year-old nylon right now. Pull him off."

Quill considered this with the expression of a man weighing whether the information was worth anything. Behind him, his fifth climber — a big, methodical man I'd seen once in Halden's harbor bar, buying rounds and asking questions — reached the midpoint and hung on the line to rest.

The rope creaked. I heard it from where I stood. A sound like an old ship complaining, which is a sound I know better than my own heartbeat, and it is never a sound you want to hear coming from equipment holding a human being.

"Pull him off," I said.

Quill turned to me fully for the first time. Up close he was smaller than his reputation and colder than his smile — a compact man with frost-nipped cheeks and eyes that did arithmetic constantly. Salvage brokers are a particular breed: they look at everything, including people, and see tonnage and price.

"Whitlock." He said my name like an appraisal. "You know what's funny? I offered you a fortune in a warm harbor, and you said no. Now look at us. Same destination, same weather, same terrible coffee. All that principle, and here you are anyway."

"Here I am telling you your rope is going to kill your man."

"Here you are," he agreed pleasantly, "and yet somehow I'm ahead of you." He raised his chin toward the pitch. "Keep climbing, Voss. Don't wait on our account."

The creak came again, louder, and this time everyone heard it. The big man on the line froze, spread-eagled against the ice, and looked down — not at Quill, but at us. At me. His face did something complicated: pride wrestling terror, and losing.

"Rennick's men are behind us," Quill added, conversational, monstrous. "Did you know? Helicopters landed at the icefall two days ago. They're moving up the standard route. So really, the question isn't whether you beat me, diver. It's who gets there first — the thief, the navy, or the girl with the dead father's toy." He smiled again. "No pressure."

And that was when the rope let go.

Not dramatically. That's the horror of it — no snap, no movie-moment crack. Just a soft giving-way, a sigh of parted fibers, and the big man dropped. He fell maybe eight meters before the pendulum swung him into the headwall and his axe bit and held, and then he was hanging from one tool with 200 feet of nothing below him and his teammates shouting uselessly from above.

Everything after that happened fast and wordlessly. Eirik was already moving — he'd been moving since the first creak, anchors out, our own rope running through his hands like it was alive. Tomas built a belay with the calm of a boy who'd grown up on these ropes. I ran the low angle and set a snow anchor, stamped it in, tested it with my whole weight.

"Line!" Eirik shouted, and threw the coil up the headwall. It landed across the hanging man's arm.

"Clip! Clip and lean back!"

The man fumbled — gloves, panic, the whole miserable physics of terror — and then the carabiner clicked home and his weight transferred and he hung there, swinging, sobbing once, quietly, like a man embarrassed to be alive.

It took another hour to bring him down and get him to the anchor point, where he sat in the snow shaking and refusing to look at Quill. Quill, for his part, watched the rescue with polite interest, as though observing a competitor's technique at a trade show.

"Efficient," he offered.

"Get off my mountain," Eirik said. No heat in it at all, which made it worse. "Take your people and go down. The window is closing. Every hour you spend ahead of us is an hour you spend under the seracs, and the seracs don't care whose payroll you're on."

"You know I can't do that."

"I know," Eirik said. "That's why I'm telling you anyway. So that afterward, when there's an afterward, no one can say you weren't warned."

* * *

They didn't go down. Of course they didn't. Quill re-rigged his own line — he had bolts and a hand drill, hours lost, fury barely contained — and by late afternoon both teams stood at the top of the headwall, sixty meters apart, regarding each other across a field of wind-carved sastrugi like two armies that had discovered they were marching to the same city.

There was no fighting. There was no shooting. There was only the peculiar, grinding misery of shared terrain: two rope teams within earshot for the rest of the day, close enough to hear each other's axes, far enough apart to pretend otherwise. We leapfrogged. They leapfrogged. When we stopped to drink, they stopped to drink, each side timing its halts so as never to appear to be resting on the other's schedule. It was the pettiest possible form of warfare, conducted at five thousand meters, and I hated every minute of it, and I caught myself checking their position every ninety seconds like a woman checking a wound.

Once, passing us on a traverse, Quill slowed beside me — deliberately, insolently, inside rope's reach — and nodded at my chest, at the shape of the compass under my jacket.

"Your father had good taste," he said. "In instruments. Poor taste in partners, but then, so did mine." Something flickered across his face — not quite grief, more like an invoice he'd never managed to collect. "Ask your guide what happened to the last man who climbed with him."

"Eirik's never lied to me."

"No," Quill said. "Liars are cheaper than that." And he crunched away up-slope, leaving me with a splinter I couldn't reach.

I asked Eirik that evening, in the tent, while the wind tried its keys in every seam. He was quiet a long time.

"There was a client," he said finally. "Years ago. Before Halden, before any of this. He wanted a route through the icefall in bad season, and I told him no, and he found a guide who said yes." Eirik's hands stayed busy with the stove, precise, unhurried. "The guide died. The client lived. The client's name was Quill."

"And you've hated him since."

"I've pitied him since. Hate is for people who might change." He looked at me across the stove's small orange light. "Sleep, Casper. Tomorrow we travel parallel to them, and the day after, the routes converge at the col, and after that there's only one line to the vault, and we will all be on it together — us, them, and Rennick's soldiers. Whatever this mountain is going to decide about us, it will decide soon."

Outside, the wind rose. And beneath the wind, faint and rhythmic, the glacier kept up its work — cracking, shifting, settling — like a ledger being balanced in the dark, page by page, name by name.

I slept with the compass in my fist, and dreamed of water rising in a cave, and woke once to hear, impossibly far off across the ice, the sound of Quill's team breaking camp hours before dawn.

They were moving again. And now, so were we.
