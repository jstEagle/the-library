## 23. Whiteout

The mountain took the sky away at half past noon.

One minute I was watching Eirik's broad back move up the slope ahead of me, his ice axe swinging in a rhythm I'd learned to trust like a heartbeat, and the next minute there was no Eirik, no slope, no Tomas behind me — just white. Not fog-white, not cloud-white, but a white so total it had texture, like being wrapped inside an unwound bandage. The horizon vanished. My own boots vanished. When I held my hand up to check I still had hands, it was a pale ghost hovering at the edge of nothing.

"Rope!" I shouted, and heard my voice come back to me from every direction at once, flattened and strange, as if the mountain were mocking me in stereo.

The rope was there. Thank God for the rope. It ran taut-ish from my harness up into the blankness where Eirik ought to be, and I gripped it with both mittens and felt — barely, faintly, like a pulse through a wall — the small tug-and-give of someone moving on the other end.

Then even that stopped.

* * *

Let me be honest about what happened next, because I've told this story enough times now that I know which parts people want prettied up, and I've decided I'm done prettying.

I panicked.

Not the screaming, flailing kind — the mountain doesn't reward that, and anyway screaming into a whiteout is like shouting into wet cotton; the sound dies two feet from your lips. No, mine was the quiet kind, the kind that happens somewhere behind the sternum. My breath went short and fast. My brain, which prides itself on being a salvage diver's brain, on staying level when the wreck is collapsing and your air line is snagged and the only way out is through, served up a single unhelpful thought on a loop: *you are going to stand here until you freeze, and they will find you in spring, standing up, like a fence post.*

Fence post Casper. Very dignified. Marta would have made a joke about it.

I did the things you're supposed to do. I planted my axe. I stamped out a platform so I wasn't slowly sinking. I counted my breathing down from panic-pace to something a human might use. I called out at intervals — Eirik's name, then Tomas's, then just a long hoot of sound — and listened between each one with everything I had.

Nothing came back. The white ate it all.

Here's the thing about whiteouts that nobody tells you: they don't feel dangerous. That's the trap. A storm you can see is a thing you fight. A whiteout is a thing that erases the difference between fighting and standing still. There was no wind to speak of, no cold biting at my face any worse than it had all morning. Just stillness, and silence, and the slow realization that "still" and "dead" look identical from the inside.

And in the middle of all that nothing, one thing moved.

In my chest pocket, against my heart where I'd carried it since Halden, the compass shifted. I felt it before I saw it — a faint tick against my ribs, like a knuckle rapping from inside. I worked my mitten off with my teeth — a mistake, my fingers screamed about it for the next hour — and pulled it out into the blind white world.

The needle was swinging. Not the lazy, dithering swing it did around camp, not the restless hunting it did when I pointed it anywhere but the icefall. This was urgent. Deliberate. It swept left, right, left, and settled — hard, like a dog hitting the end of its chain — toward a bearing I couldn't have named if you'd paid me, because there was no sun, no shadow, no ridge line, no anything to name it against. It pointed at white. It pointed at nothing.

It pointed, I realized, the way it always pointed. Toward the glacier's heart. Toward whatever my father had spent thirty years and finally his life keeping buried.

Except now it was pointing at something closer. Something alive, or I'd have bet my remaining nine fingers on it, because Eirik Solheim did not get separated from his clients by accident, and when Eirik got caught out he dug in, and when Eirik dug in he dug *down*, and down meant —

A snow hole. He'd have put Tomas in a snow hole. And a snow hole meant shelter, and shelter meant a place a compass needle could find if the compass in question didn't give a damn about magnetic north and had never once, in all the weeks I'd owned it, given me a reason to trust it except that it kept being right in ways that made no sense.

"Fence post," I said out loud, to myself, in Marta's dry tone. "You want to die standing in one spot waiting for spring? Or do you want to walk?"

The needle quivered, patient as a teacher waiting for the slow student.

I clipped the rope off. That was the hardest part — cutting myself loose from the last physical thread connecting me to another human being, trusting that Eirik would understand, that he'd anchor his end and read the absence correctly. We'd drilled it on the moraine until I hated him: *if the rope goes slack and stays slack, the other party has made a decision. Respect it.* I respected it. I hated respecting it. Then I buried the rope's end under a cairn of snow blocks so he could track me if he came looking, marked the spot with my spare wands, and stepped off into the white with nothing but a brass liar and my own two legs.

* * *

Walking in a whiteout is an act of faith dressed up as navigation.

You can't see the ground. You can't see your feet. You learn within ten steps to read the slope through your boots — the slight drag of uphill, the treacherous slickness of wind-loaded sastrugi, the hollow note your crampons strike on bridged snow that might be covering air. Every step is a question and the mountain answers in braille.

I kept the compass in my bare left hand — bare because mittens and needles don't negotiate — and checked it every twenty steps, because the needle drifted when I did. That was the eerie part, the part I still can't fully explain without sounding like a woman who's been at altitude too long. It didn't point at a fixed bearing. It pointed at a *place*. When I veered left, it swung right to correct me. When I stumbled and turned a full circle catching myself, it waited until I stopped, then swung smoothly back around as if to say, *no, that way, keep up.*

My father's initials were engraved on the case. C.W. — Casper Whitlock, named for him before either of us knew what that inheritance would cost. I'd stared at those letters through a whole winter trapped in Halden, turning them over like a coin, wondering what he'd been doing with a compass that ignored the poles. Now, alone in the largest blankness I'd ever stood inside, I found myself talking to him.

"If this is a joke," I said, breath tearing away instantly in the flat white, "it's a bad one. If you're laughing wherever you are —"

The needle ticked, firm, unchanged.

"— fine. Fine. Point the way, old man."

I thought about him a lot, walking. About the last time I'd seen him, loading the *Kestrel* at dawn, telling me the tide tables were wrong and to double-check them, which was his way of saying *be careful* without having to say it, because Whitlocks said careful things sideways or not at all. I'd spent years angry at him for dying — for taking a contract he shouldn't have, for leaving me a boat full of debts and a head full of questions. Somewhere between Halden and the icefall, the anger had started transmuting into something else, something heavier and more useful. He hadn't been reckless. He'd been *guarding*. All those years of small contracts and careful tides, and I'd read it as timidity. It had been a man keeping a low profile over a secret worth killing for, and doing it well enough that I never once suspected.

Until the navy hired his daughter to do the one job he'd died avoiding.

The slope steepened. I felt it in my calves before my boots confirmed it, and the compass needle lifted — actually lifted, the way a dog's nose lifts when it catches scent — and steadied on a line straight upslope. Snow holes don't go uphill, generally. But Eirik had been reading the terrain when the whiteout hit, same as me, and if there was a crevasse wall or a serac base anywhere near, that's where he'd have gone. Shelter follows structure. Structure rises.

I climbed. Twenty steps, check. Twenty steps, check. The needle never wavered. Somewhere in the third hundred steps my fear burned off — not because the situation improved, but because purpose is a fuel and I was running rich on it. This was the feeling I knew from diving, the one I'd been chasing since I was sixteen: the moment when the unknown stops being a threat and becomes a search pattern. Wreck diving is just this — a blankness, a bearing, and the discipline to follow the bearing past the point where your nerves tell you to surface.

Follow the bearing. Trust the instrument. Breathe.

The compass ticked against my palm like a second heartbeat, and for the first time since I'd pried it out of that cave beneath the escarpment, I stopped thinking of it as evidence, or leverage, or a curse. It was a tool. It was *his* tool. And it was working.

* * *

I nearly walked past it.

That's the joke I'll never tell at parties, because there aren't parties for this kind of thing, but it's true: after all that, after the rope and the bare hand and three hundred steps of blind faith, I came within six feet of the hole and registered it only as a slight flattening in the white, a place where the texture of the snow changed the way water changes over a submerged wreck. My boot found the edge of a trench and I went down to my waist, swore with feeling, and heard —

Sound. Actual sound. From below and ahead.

"Casper?" Eirik's voice, muffled, incredulous, coming out of the side of the mountain itself. "Casper Whitlock, is that you?"

"It's the fence post," I called back, and started digging.

The snow hole was a proper one, because of course it was — Eirik had cut it into the base of a crevasse wall where the snowpack was deep and stable, a chamber big enough for three, entrance angled away from the wind, sleeping platforms raised. Tomas was inside it, wrapped in both their spare layers, gray-faced and shivering but alive, alive, alive. Eirik filled the entrance, hauling me through, and for a moment none of us said anything at all. He gripped my shoulders hard enough to bruise. His beard was a solid mass of frost. He looked at me like a man looking at a ghost that had politely brought coffee.

"The rope went slack," he said finally. "Twenty minutes. I anchored it and I was coming after you. I was *just* coming after you."

"I know. I left you a trail."

"You left me a trail." He looked at me the way he looked at clients who did something reckless and brilliant in the same motion, which was most of the way he ever looked at me. "In a whiteout. Alone. Off rope."

"I had a bearing."

"You had —" He stopped. His eyes went to my left hand, blue and clumsy, and the brass compass sitting in it, needle still holding its impossible line even here, even underground, pointing through the snow wall toward something neither of us could name. "That thing again."

"That thing again," I agreed.

Tomas stirred on the platform. "Did she really navigate here with it?" His voice was thin but working. In the weeks since the col — since the betrayal, since we'd roped him in rather than leave him, since his shame had slowly, painfully converted into competence — he'd become the expedition's quietest believer in the compass. Maybe because he needed something to believe in that wasn't himself. "Eirik, she navigated here. In that." He nodded at the white wall of the entrance. "Nobody navigates in that."

"Somebody did," Eirik said.

He made tea — Eirik can make tea in conditions that would make a laboratory blush — and while the stove hissed its small brave hiss, I flexed feeling back into my fingers and told them the whole account: the rope going dead, the needle waking up, the walk. I told it plainly, without embellishment, because it didn't need any. The facts were strange enough.

When I finished, Eirik was quiet for a long moment, turning his spoon in his cup.

"My father's journal," he said at last. "From the old camp. There's a passage I've read forty times. Halvard writes that the device — the cargo — 'pulls at instruments the way iron pulls at lodestone, but wrong, always wrong, as though it remembers a different north.'" He looked up. "I always thought it was a man writing poetry about equipment he didn't understand. Expedition men did that. Filled their journals with weather and wonder." He tapped the compass case, gently, with one frost-crusted finger. "But maybe he understood better than we gave him credit for. Maybe your father's compass isn't broken, Casper. Maybe it's the only honest instrument on this glacier. Everything else points where the world says to point. That thing points where the *cargo* says."

"And the cargo," Tomas said quietly, "is under the icefall."

"And the cargo is under the icefall," Eirik agreed.

We sat with that. Outside, the whiteout pressed against our little pocket of warmth like a thumb testing glass. Inside, the stove ticked, and Tomas drank his tea in small sips, and the compass lay on my knee still pointing, patiently, faithfully, through a meter of snow and half a mountain, at a thing my father had died keeping buried and I had climbed a glacier intending to destroy.

Faith is a strange thing to build. Mine had been assembled piecemeal over months — a needle that wouldn't lie, initials in brass, a dead man's annotations matching a dead expedition's route — and I'd never quite committed to it, never quite let myself believe the whole impossible architecture. Sitting in that snow hole, watching Eirik study the compass with the expression of a man revising a lifetime of professional skepticism, I felt the last piece click home.

It had never lied to me. Not once. Through the cave, through the winter, through the col and the fall and the storm — it had pointed true at the one thing that mattered, and it had just done it again, in the worst conditions the mountain could manufacture, well enough to lead me across a featureless slope to two men buried in the snow.

Whatever else happened above the icefall — Quill, Rennick, the calving, the vault, the choice I could already feel waiting for me like a crevasse under powder — I knew this much now, the way you know a rope is good because you've hung your whole weight on it:

I could trust the bearing.

The whiteout howled — no, it didn't howl, that's the horror of it, it *silenced* — outside our walls for another four hours. We slept in shifts. And when at last the light changed, when the white peeled back and the world reassembled itself out of nothing — slope, sky, seracs stacked like the teeth of a broken comb — Eirik crawled to the entrance, looked at the terrain, looked at the compass in my hand, and let out a low whistle.

"You held a line," he said. "Through that. To within fifty meters of our position." He shook his head slowly. "Casper. Your father didn't leave you a debt and a boat. He left you a map."

Above us, half-hidden in the clearing sky, the icefall gleamed — and far up in its blue depths, something glinted back.
