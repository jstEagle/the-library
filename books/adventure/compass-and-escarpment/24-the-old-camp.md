## 24. The Old Camp

The storm that had tried to kill us two days earlier left behind a sky so clean it felt like an apology. Eirik said mountain weather worked that way — it broke your legs and then brought you flowers. He had a way of saying things like that while chewing pemmican, which made them harder to take seriously.

We'd climbed since first light through country that didn't have names so much as warnings. The glacier here was old and deep and moved in its sleep, and every few hours it groaned like a man dreaming of debts. I knew the sound. I'd been making it myself for three years.

It was Tomas who saw it first.

"Smoke," he said, then corrected himself. "No — not smoke. Color."

He was pointing at a shelf of ice maybe four hundred meters above our route, where the glacier bent around a shoulder of rock like water around a boulder. In the blue-white of the ice there was a patch of wrongness — a brown smear, a rectangle of shadow too regular to be anything nature had made.

Eirik got out his binoculars and studied it for a long time. When he lowered them his face had gone carefully blank, which for Eirik was the same as shouting.

"That's the camp," he said.

"What camp?" I asked, though my hands had already gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with altitude.

"The one nobody comes back from talking about." He passed me the glasses. "My father's expedition. They were last seen near this point on the icefall. The search parties found nothing. Officially, they walked off the face of the earth."

Through the lenses the rectangle resolved into canvas — a tent wall, half-buried, its fabric turned amber by decades of sun-scour. Beside it stood the skeletal frame of a sledge, and something tall and thin that might have been a mast or an antenna or a cross.

"They never found them," I said. "Six men, and they never found so much as a boot."

"The glacier keeps what it takes," Eirik said. "Sometimes it gives things back. Usually not in one piece."

Tomas looked between us. "You want to go up there."

"I want to go up there," Eirik said.

"I want to go up there," I said, and surprised myself with how fast it came out.

* * *

The detour cost us four hours. Eirik led us up a spine of wind-carved ice that he clearly knew from some previous life — he moved on it the way I moved underwater, without looking at his feet, reading the surface by some sense he couldn't have explained if you asked. I followed in his steps and tried not to think about how far we were now from any route a rescue party would bother searching, and failed completely, and climbed anyway.

The compass rode in the inner pocket of my jacket, against my ribs. For the last hour its needle had stopped its slow restless swing and settled into a steady pull toward the shelf above us, like a dog straining at a leash. I hadn't told the others. It seemed like the kind of information that changed how people looked at you.

The camp announced itself by smell before anything else — or rather by the absence of smell, the high sterile nothing of air that hasn't held a living thing in forty years. Then the details arrived one at a time, each worse than the last.

The tent was a pyramid of canvas over an aluminum frame, one pole snapped, the whole structure leaning like a drunk against the rock wall behind it. The fabric had gone the color of weak tea. A rope ran from its entrance down-slope and simply ended, frayed, in the ice. There was a cook stove with a pot still on it. There was a crate stenciled with letters I recognized from my father's charts — a government mark, the kind that meant *this box does not exist*. And there was snow inside the tent doorway, drifted smooth, undisturbed.

And there were the men.

Three of them. Two outside, one half-in. The cold had done what cold does at altitude when it works quickly — it had kept them. Not kindly. Their gear had rotted and their skin had darkened to leather, but their shapes were whole, their postures legible. One sat against the sledge with his back straight, hands in his lap, as if he'd only paused to rest and the world had agreed to let him. Another lay face-down with one arm flung forward, pointing down-glacier, toward home, or toward whatever he'd been crawling toward when the cold took the decision out of his hands.

The third was inside the tent, curled around a canvas bag like it was the last warm thing in the world.

I had seen drowned men pulled up in nets. I had seen what the sea leaves after a week. This was different. This was a room where time had been told to wait outside, and it had obeyed, and the waiting had become its own kind of horror.

"We should cover them," Tomas said quietly. His voice had gone young.

"After," Eirik said. His voice hadn't gone anywhere at all, which is how I knew what this was costing him. "First we find out who they are."

* * *

You learn things about people from how they die, and the camp told us its story in fragments.

The man against the sledge had a notebook in an oilskin sleeve tucked inside his jacket, and Eirik worked it free with the gentleness of a man handling a bird. The pages were frozen into a solid block; he wrapped it without opening it and put it inside his own coat, against his body, to thaw. "Slowly," he said. "Fast heat cracks the pages."

The man face-down in the snow wore a watch that had stopped at 3:40, and under his collar, when Tomas eased it aside with a trekking pole because none of us could bear to touch him bare-handed, a dog tag. Eirik read the number aloud and wrote it in his own book with fingers that shook only slightly. "I'll carry the names down," he said. "However many there are."

It was the third man, the one in the tent, who broke everything open.

His bag — the one he'd curled around — was a document case, government-stamped, and the lock had rusted open years ago. Inside were papers, frozen into a brick, and on top of the brick, where it had been when he died, lay a journal with its cover bent back and its pages fanned, as though its owner had been writing in it at the very end and simply stopped mid-word.

Eirik lifted it out. The pages nearest the cover had thawed and refrozen so many times they'd fused; but deeper in, protected by the mass of the block, the ink was still sharp. Black ink, small handwriting, slanting hard to the right like a man writing against a wind that wasn't there.

Eirik read the first legible page silently. I watched his lips move. Then he went very still, the way the glacier goes still just before it makes a decision.

"Eirik?" Tomas said.

"There are six names listed here," Eirik said slowly. "The expedition roster. Five of them are these men — I've matched three already by description." He looked up at me, and there was something in his face I couldn't read, and didn't like. "The sixth name isn't on the mountain."

"Who is it?"

He handed me the book without a word.

The page was headed in careful capitals: **PERSONNEL — FINAL AMENDMENT**. Below it, six lines. I read them the way you read a bill you already know you can't pay.

Five names meant nothing to me. The sixth line read:

*Whitlock, C. — civilian diver, contracted for sub-surface recovery. Withdrew at base camp, day nine. Reason recorded: equipment failure.*

Casper Whitlock. My father. Casper Whitlock the elder, the man whose debts I'd inherited along with his boat and his charts and his habit of keeping the important truths in pencil.

"He turned back," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "Day nine. Equipment failure."

"You're certain it's him?"

"Casper Whitlock, civilian diver." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, thin and high, skittering off the ice. "There aren't queues of us."

I crouched over the journal, holding it flat on my knees, and read the entries around the roster with my heart going like a dive computer counting down. The handwriting was not my father's — this was the expedition leader, a man named Halvard Rime, who wrote the way officers write, all nouns and no doubt. But he mentioned my father. Again and again.

*Whitlock completed the survey of the melt channel. Remarkable nerve for a contract man. Says the water under the ice is the clearest he's ever seen — "like diving in a cathedral."*

*Whitlock advises against the eastern approach. Says the ice there is rotten to a depth of thirty meters. Overruled. We are behind schedule and the navy wants results, not opinions.*

*Whitlock again. The man argues like a lawyer and dives like a fish. If he says the tunnel floods at thaw, the tunnel floods at thaw.*

And then, three pages later, the tone changed.

*Whitlock has withdrawn. Claims regulator failure. I do not believe him. I watched him test that regulator twice yesterday and it performed perfectly. Something has frightened him. He would not say what. He took the longboat down the fjord alone and refused the escort.*

*Day eleven. The eastern approach, as predicted, is lost. Whitlock was right. I have logged my objection to continuing and been ordered to continue. There is cargo aboard that must reach the cache site before the season closes, and there are men aboard whose instructions supersede mine. I begin to understand why the diver turned back. I begin to wish I had the courage to follow him.*

I sat back on my heels in the snow of a dead man's camp, forty years and a hundred miles from where my father had died at a winch handle, and I understood that I had never known him at all.

I had built my whole grief on a simple story: a salvage man, working past his luck, killed by a sea that doesn't forgive arithmetic errors. A story with no villains, which is the most comfortable kind. And underneath that story, all along, had been this — my father on a glacier, testing regulators that worked fine, finding something in the water or in the orders or in the faces of the navy men that scared him badly enough to break a contract and run, and running anyway being braver than staying.

"Equipment failure," I said again. "He lied to get off this mountain."

"Or lied to get onto it," Eirik said. "We don't know yet which direction the lie points."

"He was a diver," I said. "They hired him to go under the ice. Into the melt channels." I looked up at the glacier wall above us, all that blue compressed light, all that weight. "Whatever they were hiding, part of putting it away happened underwater. That's why they needed him. That's why—" I stopped, because the next part was arriving whether I said it or not. "That's why the compass points here. He wasn't following it. He helped make it point."

Tomas had drifted to the tent entrance and was standing very still, looking at the dead man curled around the document case. "This one," he said softly. "He didn't freeze sitting down. Look at his boots — the crampons are on. He was geared to move." He glanced back at us. "Why would a man curl up around a bag of papers instead of walking?"

Nobody answered, because the answer was obvious and terrible: because the papers mattered more than the walking. Because some things you guard even with the last heat in your body.

Eirik finished photographing the roster page with the little camera he carried — "for the families," he said, "for the proof" — and then he did something I didn't expect. He took off his glove and laid his bare hand flat on the tent canvas, just for a moment, the way you'd touch a coffin going past.

"My father trained with two of these men," he said. "He used to drink with Halvard Rime. When the search was called off, the official word was *avalanche, remains unrecoverable.* Everyone in Halden knew it was a polite lie, but a lie you could live beside." His hand came off the canvas. "Now I'm standing in the lie. It's smaller than I imagined. And colder."

* * *

We worked until the light went long and gold, and we worked respectfully. We recovered the notebooks — four of them, frozen shut, wrapped in Eirik's spare shirt and packed against our bodies to thaw. We recovered the dog tags, five sets. We photographed everything: the stove, the crate with its government stencil, the frayed rope ending in the ice, the position of each man, the bearing the dead crawler's outflung arm still pointed down-glacier.

It was while Tomas was photographing the crate that he made the last discovery of the day, and it was the one that rearranged all the others.

"There's writing under the stencil," he said. "Somebody marked over it, but the paint's thinner than the original."

We crowded in. Under the official stamp, in grease pencil, someone had written a string of characters — a designation, a serial, something — and beneath that, in smaller letters, almost apologetic:

***NAVY PROPERTY — RECOVERY PRIORITY ABSOLUTE — DO NOT OPEN IN FIELD***

"Do not open in field," I read aloud. "Which means somebody intended to open it somewhere else. Somewhere specific." I looked at the glacier wall, at the blue heart of it showing through a crevasse mouth ten meters away, glowing like a held breath. "Somewhere under it."

Eirik was quiet for a moment. Then he said, in the tone of a man laying cards face-up: "Your father surveyed the melt channels. Your father turned back the day before the eastern approach collapsed. Your father spent the rest of his life diving one stretch of coast beneath one escarpment, taking contracts nobody else wanted, asking no questions and answering none. And he died on a wreck the navy pretends doesn't exist, lifting sealed cases for a liaison who pays too well." He met my eyes. "Casper. He didn't turn back from this expedition. He turned *around*. He spent forty years guarding the door these men died trying to close."

The wind came up the icefall then, and the glacier groaned its long dreaming groan, and I stood in a dead man's camp holding a dead man's journal against my chest, and the compass against my ribs pulled toward the ice like a pulse.

The last entry in Rime's journal — the one frozen open under the dead man's hand, the one he'd been writing when the cold closed his account — was only half a sentence. Eirik had photographed it, but I'd read it, and it stayed in me like swallowed seawater:

*If anyone finds this, know that the cargo was never ours to deliver. Whitlock understood that before any of us. God keep the diver, wherever he—*

That was where it stopped. Mid-word. Mid-name.

Mid-blessing, forty years overdue, delivered at last to the only person left to receive it.

"We camp here tonight," Eirik said. "Below the shelf, out of the fall line. Tomorrow we move on the cache site." He looked at the journals bundled against his chest, then at me. "These need to thaw properly before we can read the rest. Yours especially. Rime wrote about your father for nine days, and men write differently when they're afraid." He paused. "I think what your father knew is in these pages. And I think it's the reason the navy is climbing this mountain behind us."

I looked down at the journal in my hands, at the leader's slanted, wind-fighting script, and felt the ground shift under everything I'd believed about the man who raised me.

Tomorrow, the ice would tell me the rest.
