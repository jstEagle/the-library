## 25. Father's Confession

The dead keep poor filing systems.

That was my first thought, crouched in the old camp with a frozen journal open in my mittened hands, because the man who'd written it — the expedition's leader, a name I now knew to be Halvard Brenner — had kept three separate notebooks and cross-referenced none of them. One for supplies. One for survey notes. One, the one thawing against my chest inside my jacket, for everything he apparently couldn't say out loud even to his own team.

Eirik had built a snow wall across the tent mouth and gotten the little spirit stove hissing. Tomas sat with his back against the packs, holding his hands near the flame like a man learning to pray again after a long absence. Nobody spoke. The glacier did enough talking for all of us — that deep, patient groaning it makes when it settles, like a house the size of a country shifting its weight in its sleep.

I'd taken Brenner's private journal from the body itself. That sounds worse than it felt. He'd been sitting against the camp wall for — what, forty years? Fifty? Frozen mid-thought, pen still clipped to the page, as if he'd meant to finish the sentence after dinner. The ice had him by the shoulders like a polite arrest. Taking the book from under his arm felt less like robbing a grave and more like being handed mail addressed to someone who never came home.

"Page by page," Eirik said quietly. "Let them warm before you flex them. Tear one and you may lose what's under it."

I nodded. My father's handwriting was already visible on the open page of the second journal — the survey log, the one we'd found wedged under a fuel tin, frozen open as if someone had pinned it there in a hurry. His name in the margin: *C. Whitlock, dive support.* Twenty-six years old. Younger than I was now.

My father had been here. Not near here. *Here*, at this exact latitude, on this exact ice, breathing air that had been waiting all this time for me to breathe it too.

I unwrapped the pages an inch at a time.

* * *

You have to understand what I thought I knew about my father, because the journal was about to take a crowbar to it.

I thought he was a man who ran. That was the story I'd built out of the wreckage of my childhood, plank by plank: he took one bad contract too many, one dive too deep, and when the sea took him it was taking a man who'd been swimming away from something for years. The debts. The drinking, in the bad stretch. The way he'd go quiet when anyone mentioned the northern range, change the subject like a man stepping over a hole in the floor. I'd catalogued all of it as evidence. Guilt, I'd decided. A guilty man's silences.

I'd spent six years resenting a ghost for dying broke and leaving me the bill.

The first pages of the survey log were almost boring. Soundings. Weather. Complaints about the cook. Brenner's team had been contracted — the word used was *contracted*, always capitalized in my head now, always with a question mark after it — to place "the cargo" at a site "approved at ministry level." No name for the cargo. Just *it*, and *the package*, and once, memorably, *the item (do not describe in writing)*.

Brenner had followed that instruction with the diligence of a man who expected his notebooks to be read someday. Which, Halvard, buddy — they were.

Then, a third of the way through, the entries changed. The handwriting got smaller. Tighter.

*Whitlock raised the question again tonight. The boy asks what we are burying and why it needs divers at all if the answer is nothing. I told him to mind his gauge. I do not like that he asks. I like less that I cannot answer him.*

I stopped. Read it twice. *The boy.*

Tomas looked up. "What?"

"Nothing." My voice came out strange. "Keep warming your hands."

But my father hadn't been minding his gauge. He'd been asking questions. At twenty-six, roped to a secret he didn't understand, my father — my cautious, debt-drowned, careful-invisible father — had been the one poking at the dark.

* * *

The temperature in the tent was climbing just enough. Outside, the light was doing that thing it does up here in the late afternoon, going gold and then copper and then a color that has no name and no business existing at this altitude. I turned pages with the delicacy of a bomb tech.

The entry that changed everything was dated eleven days before the last one.

*I will write this plainly, once, and then I will burn nothing but will trust to the ice to keep it better than any safe. If you are reading this, the ice has failed or you are family, and either way you deserve the truth more than the ministry deserves its silence.*

*The cargo is a navigation system. Not a compass. Not a radio aid. A guidance unit — self-correcting, terrain-independent, accurate to a margin the specifications call absurd. It was built to steer things that should never be steered by it. The men who commissioned it talk about shipping lanes and rescue beacons. The men who funded it do not talk at all; they listen. I have seen one of them, once, at the coastal depot, and I have not been able to forget his face. It was the face of a man pricing something.*

*Brenner believes we are burying a prototype ahead of a treaty review — that it is embarrassment, not menace. Perhaps. But I have watched the escort officers burn their own copies of the manifest, and embarrassed men do not burn paper with such relief.*

*Today Whitlock and I moved the secondary cache. He asked me, point blank, whether what we carried could kill people. I told him it could steer them. He considered this longer than any man I've known, and then he said: "Then the honest work is making sure nobody finds it."*

*He is twenty-six years old and he has understood the assignment better than the colonel.*

I had to put the journal down. I pressed it flat against my thigh and stared at the stove flame until my eyes watered, and Tomas pretended very hard to be interested in the tent seams, and Eirik — Eirik just watched me with those grey guide's eyes that miss nothing and comment on less.

My father hadn't stumbled into this. He hadn't been a hired hand who kept his head down and went home. He had stood on this ice at twenty-six years old, been asked whether the thing in the crate could kill people, given the only honest answer available, and then — at twenty-six — decided that the honest work was burial. Guarding. A life sentence accepted voluntarily, by a diver with no stake in it beyond being the kind of man who couldn't un-know a thing once he knew it.

And then he'd gone home and spent thirty years pretending to be a small man with small debts so that no one would ever look twice at him.

Every silence I'd resented reorganized itself in front of me. The quiet when the range came up. The charts hidden in the chart table's false bottom — the annotations matching the compass bearing, which I'd found in chapter and verse back in my harbor shack and pocketed like a thief. The way he'd taught me to dive, patiently, obsessively, insisting I learn to navigate without instruments, by feel, by current, by the lie of the light. I'd thought it was a quirk. A hobbyist's pedantry.

It was a curriculum. He was training his own replacement. Or — no. Worse and better. He was training someone who could find it if he ever failed to guard it. A failsafe with his own eyes and my stubbornness.

"You found something," Eirik said. Not a question.

"My father," I said. My throat was full of gravel. "He wasn't what I thought."

"No one ever is." He fed the stove another tablet. "Read the rest."

* * *

The last entries were the hardest, and not because of the handwriting, which deteriorated beautifully — Brenner's neat surveyor's script unraveling into a scrawl as the cold and whatever else got into the camp.

They'd been caught by the early winter. That much matched Marta Voss's telling: the expedition that went up and didn't come down. But the journals made it worse than weather. The escort officers — there were four, and Brenner clearly despised every one of them — had decided, somewhere around day nineteen of the storm, that the cargo's location mattered more than the crew's survival. Two of the team had been sent back down toward the col with the decoy sledge, deliberately, visibly, so that any pursuit would follow the wrong trail.

Marta's brother had been on that sledge.

*Voss argued. Of course he argued. He said the decoy plan spends lives like small coin. The major told him it was arithmetic. I have served under officers my whole career and I have never before heard murder called arithmetic to its face.*

*Voss went anyway. That is the worst of it. He argued, and lost, and shouldered the rope, because he would not let the younger ones walk that trail without him. If any goodness survives this mountain, let it attach itself to his name and follow him home.*

I looked up. Tomas was staring at me. He'd read enough over my shoulder, or maybe he'd simply seen it land in my face.

"Marta's brother," he said. "Aron."

"Aron Voss," I confirmed. "He volunteered to walk the decoy trail so the others might live. And the navy logged the whole expedition as lost due to navigational error." I heard my own voice go flat and cold. "Navigational error. Signed off by—" I stopped. Rennick's name sat in my mouth like a fishhook. The report clearing my father's fatal voyage had borne the same signature. Of course it had. You don't let one loose thread near the spool.

"The last entry," Eirik said gently. "There's always a last entry."

There was. Dated the final morning. The storm had broken briefly — a liar's gap of blue — and Brenner had used it to write fast.

*Decision made. The cargo goes into the deep cache, the one only Whitlock knows the approach to, and the tunnel mouth will close behind us if the season does its work. Whitlock is the only one of us going down. He dives the melt channel alone and sets the seals. Brave fool. He shook my hand this morning like a man closing a bank account — settled, complete.*

*I have instructed him to tell no one, including his family. Especially his family. Knowledge is a weight, and he has a daughter. Let her inherit his debts, God forgive me, rather than his duty. Debts can be paid. Duty only endures.*

*A daughter. He showed me her photograph. She is scowling at the camera with her arms crossed, aged perhaps nine, with the look of a person who intends to audit the universe and find it short.*

I laughed. It came out cracked and wet and entirely involuntary, a sound I hadn't made since the funeral, and it scared Tomas half out of his frostbitten skin.

Because I *had* scowled at that camera. I remembered the photograph — my mother kept it on the mantel until she didn't keep anything anymore. Arms crossed. Nine years old. Already auditing.

*If she reads this someday — and women like that always, eventually, read everything — then hear me, girl: your father did not fail you. He spent himself on the loneliest purchase a man can make, which is other people's safety, bought anonymously, at retail. He will die owing money and owing nothing else. Do not confuse the two ledgers.*

I closed the journal. Pressed it to my chest. Sat there in the blue-ice hush of a dead man's tent while the stove ticked and the glacier groaned its long slow syllables overhead.

Six years of resentment, and it dissolved not in a rush but the way ice melts — grain by grain, structure giving up its grip, until what had been solid was simply water, running where it wanted. He hadn't run from anything. He'd stood post. Thirty years, alone, unpaid, guarding a buried weapon with nothing but silence and a false-bottomed chart table and a daughter trained to navigate by feel. And when the navy finally sent someone to check on the post — some accident arranged or merely permitted, I'd never prove which — he'd died still holding it.

Dying rather than selling it. Because Quill's kind had come sniffing over the years, I'd bet the boat on it. Some broker, some buyer, some offer made in a harbor bar. And my father, drowning in debt, had looked at the one exit from his ruin and walked past it every single time.

"Casper." Eirik's voice, low. "The compass."

I'd almost forgotten it — the brass thing riding in my inner pocket like a second heartbeat. I drew it out. The needle swung, steadied, pointed up-glacier toward the vault with its idiot faithful certainty. And there, on the case back, worn nearly smooth but legible in the low sun slanting through the tent fabric: initials. *C.W.* His. Cut by his own hand, probably, on this same ice, while Brenner watched and wrote him down as a brave fool.

"He mapped his own grave goods," I said. "This isn't treasure. It's instructions. It was always instructions."

"For what?" Tomas asked.

I turned the compass over in my fingers and thought about the failsafe phrase from Brenner's pages, the mechanism hinted at in the margins of the survey log — the seals, the scuttling charge, the *deep cache designed to be unmade*. My father hadn't just hidden the device. He'd built the means to destroy it into the hiding, and then he'd built a map to the means, and then he'd waited his whole life for someone worth handing the map to.

"For finishing the job," I said. "For doing what he couldn't do twice."

Outside, the light was failing into that violet hour, and far up-slope — closer than yesterday, I was almost sure — the glacier gave a crack like a rifle, then another, the calving window opening its brief bright throat. Somewhere below us, Quill's rope team was climbing. Somewhere above, Rennick's helicopter had a range the mountains couldn't argue with forever.

Eirik began melting snow for tea. Tomas checked the ropes with hands that had stopped shaking. And I sat in my father's old camp, in the circle of light from a stove he'd never seen, reading his youth in a dead man's handwriting, and forgave him everything — the debts, the silences, the long lonely watch — because I finally understood the ledger he'd actually been keeping.

It had never been about money.

The next morning, we'd need to move before the window narrowed — and when I stepped out of the tent to look at the route, I saw figures far up on the serac field, tiny and wrong, moving fast, cutting the line straight through ground no sane guide would touch.

Quill wasn't behind us anymore.

He was ahead.
