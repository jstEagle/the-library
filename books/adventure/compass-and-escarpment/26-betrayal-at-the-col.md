## 26. Betrayal at the Col

The col looked, from below, like a door someone had left ajar in the sky.

A notch of grey light between two shoulders of black rock, maybe forty meters wide, with the glacier pouring through it in a frozen river of blue and white. Eirik had been pointing at it for two days the way other men point at home. "The col," he'd say, as if naming it were half of climbing it. "We sleep at the col."

I'd learned by then that when Eirik named a thing, he was also telling you how much it could hurt you. The col was going to hurt us. It was 5,300 meters of everything we had, and then some.

What I hadn't learned — what nobody can teach you, apparently, not even a guide who drills you until your hands bleed — is that the thing most likely to kill you on a mountain isn't the ice. It's the person sharing your rope.

* * *

It started with small things, the way these things always do.

Tomas had been quieter since the serac field. Not sullen — Tomas didn't have a sullen bone in him; his face was built for grinning even when it was lying — but quiet in a way I recognized, because I'd been quiet exactly like that for two years after my father died. It's the quiet of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want anyone to overhear.

I'd caught him once at camp, hunched over his pack with something in his hands that wasn't rope or stove or any of the hundred legitimate objects a rope team carries. He'd closed the flap before I could see what it was and grinned at me, and I'd grinned back, because that's what you do at altitude when your brain is starving and your teammate smiles at you. You smile back. You're family up here. That's the whole point of the rope.

That was the mistake I kept making. I kept thinking the rope made us family.

Eirik noticed more than I did, I think. On the morning of the col he checked Tomas's harness twice instead of once, running the buckles through his fingers with a frown like a man counting money in a dark room. "Something wrong?" Tomas asked, all injured innocence.

"Your tie-in knot looks tired," Eirik said.

"It's a figure-eight. They always look tired. It's their whole personality."

Eirik almost smiled. Almost. Then he re-tied it himself anyway, and I remember thinking: *good, careful man*, and thinking nothing else about it, because the wind had come up and the col was waiting and there is only so much suspicion a body has room for when every step costs a quarter of its breath.

* * *

The pitch below the col was the worst climbing of the trip. Steep ice, fifty degrees if it was anything, cut steps and front-point work with a drop beneath us that didn't end so much as lose interest around four hundred meters down. We climbed it roped in sequence: Eirik leading, me second, Tomas last, thirty meters between each of us, running belays off screws Eirik placed with the economy of a man who knew each one cost him minutes he might want back later.

Halfway up, the wind found us.

Not a gust — a *decision*. The kind of wind that arrives like it owns the lease. It came over the col from the far side and hit the slope broadside, and suddenly the world was white noise and sideways snow and my own breath tearing out of me in rags. Eirik's figure shrank to a smudge above. I clipped his last screw, shouted something that the wind took away unopened, and climbed.

I remember the exact moment I came over the lip of the col, because it was the first beautiful thing in hours: the notch opened up and there it was, the far side, the upper glacier spread out under a torn sky like a field of broken glass, and for three whole seconds I forgot to be exhausted.

Then I heard the sound.

You never forget the sound. A rope coming tight under load doesn't twang, exactly. It *sings* — one high, terrible note, a note no instrument should know — and then the anchor hardware starts screaming against the ice, and after that comes the part where people start shouting.

"TOMAS!" Eirik's voice, from somewhere above and behind me, stripped down to its bones. "TOMAS, WHAT DID YOU—"

I turned.

Tomas was standing at the anchor station ten meters below the col's lip, and he was holding a knife.

Not using it. Holding it — arm extended, blade against the fixed line they'd both been clipped into, the line that was supposed to hold Eirik while he moved across the col's shattered crest toward the next anchor. And the line was already cut. I saw it go, actually saw the last strands part, and the rope whipped away down the slope like something alive escaping a trap, and Eirik — Eirik who was mid-move, weight committed, tools set, nothing left to stand on —

Eirik fell.

* * *

There is a version of this story where I think clearly. Where I assess, decide, execute. I've read those versions in other people's books and I've even written one or two myself, sitting warm somewhere with tea, giving my heroes the presence of mind I have never once possessed in my actual life.

Here is what really happened: the world went very simple and very loud, and my body did things my brain signed off on afterward.

Eirik went past me on my right. Close enough that I felt the wind of him. His arms were wide, legs cycling, and his face — I'll carry his face — not screaming, just open, astonished, a man reading his own ending in the space of a heartbeat.

My axe was planted. My tether was clipped to the screw below me. There was no time to move the tether, no time to do anything correct, so I did the incorrect thing, the thing Eirik would have flayed me for in training, the thing that kills rescuers along with the fallen: I threw my arm down-slope and let the rope run through my gloved hand and clamped down on it.

The burn went through the glove like the glove was a rumor. Through the skin, into the meat of my palm, a white bar of pain so total it briefly replaced the rest of the universe. The rope jerked taut and my shoulder tried to leave my body and my crampons skated an inch, two inches, on the fifty-degree ice, and below me Eirik's weight hit the full stretch and *held* — held on a screw Eirik himself had placed, in ice he himself had tested, which is either a comfort or the cruelest irony I've ever personally witnessed.

Four hundred meters of air swung gently below him like a pendulum finding its rest.

"EIRIK!" My voice came out shredded. "Eirik, I've got you! I'VE GOT YOU!"

He looked up at me from the end of the rope, hanging in the wind, and said — I swear on my father's grave — he said, "Your brake hand is wrong."

Even now I don't know whether to laugh or weep about that. Both, probably. Everything worth feeling on that mountain came in both flavors.

* * *

Getting him back up took forty minutes and cost me whatever was left of my hands.

I couldn't haul him alone — physics doesn't care about your feelings — so I built a belay off two screws, transferred his rope to a friction hitch, and walked backward up the slope hand over hand while he prusiked, both of us moving in the slow, grinding rhythm of people who are too tired to talk and too scared to stop. The wind kept arriving in freight-train loads. Twice the rope jumped in my burned grip and twice I got it back, and each time I thought about the knife, and each time I made myself think about the rope instead. One problem at a time. First keep him alive. Then find out why.

Eirik came over the lip gray-faced and shaking, and the first thing he did — before checking his own gear, before touching his own frost-nipped cheeks — was grab my wrist and turn my hand over and look at the burn, and say nothing at all, which from Eirik was a eulogy.

Then he stood up, and turned around, and looked at Tomas.

Tomas hadn't run. That was the strange part. He was still standing at the anchor station, knife closed now, fist white around it, and he watched Eirik come toward him the way a man watches weather he knows is going to flatten his house.

"You cut the line." Eirik's voice was flat. Flat the way the glacier is flat — all the violence pressed down underneath, waiting. "While we were on it. While *I* was on it."

"Quill's team is half a day behind us," Tomas said. "Maybe less. They have the route beta and they're moving light and fast, and when they catch us—" His voice cracked and he hated it; I watched him hate it. "When they catch us, there are five of them and three of us and two of us are half dead already. I did the arithmetic, Eirik. Somebody had to do the arithmetic."

"The arithmetic," Eirik repeated, very quietly. "You cut a rope and you call it arithmetic."

"They weren't supposed to be on it!" It burst out of him, and with it, everything else. This is the thing about betrayal at altitude: there's nowhere to walk it back. No door to slam, no street to storm down. Just three people and the wind and four hundred meters of honest air listening in. "Quill has my sister's debt. All of it. She signed papers she didn't understand, the way everyone signs papers they don't understand, and Quill bought the paper, and he told me — he told me if I fed him the route, the camps, the timing, he'd tear it up. Tear it up, Eirik. Her kids. She's got two kids and a boat with a cracked hull and Quill owns her down to the nails, and all I had to trade was information, because information is the only thing I *have*—"

"And today?"

Tomas stopped.

"Today," Eirik said, taking one step closer, close enough that I could see Tomas swallow, "today the information ran out. Today Quill needed more than words. So you were going to slow us down. Cripple us at the col. Leave us here short a rope and let the mountain do the polite work, and then walk down to Quill's camp and collect your sister's freedom with our blood on your sleeve. Isn't that how it priced out?"

"I didn't cut *your* rope," Tomas whispered. "I cut the fixed line. I told myself — God help me, I told myself you'd be clipped to your own—"

"He was transitioning anchors," I said. My voice came out stranger than I expected, cold and level, a voice I didn't recognize as mine. "You knew the system. You've climbed with us for weeks. You knew exactly what state he'd be in when that line parted. Don't you dare launder it through bad luck."

Tomas looked at me then, and whatever he saw in my face made him sit down. Right there on the ice, gear and all, like a puppet with the strings let go. He sat down and put his head in his hands and the knife fell out of his fist and slid a few inches down the slope and stopped, and none of us moved to pick it up.

"My father climbed with yours," he said to Eirik, muffled, into his palms. "Did you know that? Everyone keeps saying it like it means something. Like blood remembers. Well, blood also owes, Eirik. Blood owes *fortunes*."

* * *

We didn't talk for a long time after that.

The wind talked enough for all of us. It came over the col in long white breaths and erased the far glacier and gave it back, erased it and gave it back, and I sat with my burned hand wrapped in a spare sock and watched Eirik stand at the edge of the col looking down the way we'd come, doing his own arithmetic now. I could see him doing it. The route behind was sealed by the calving — no retreat, we'd known that since the serac field. The route ahead was the upper glacier and the melt tunnel and whatever waited inside it. And in between sat the question of what to do with a man who had cut a rope with both of them attached to it.

Exile was impossible. There was no *anywhere* to exile him to. Leaving him was murder with extra steps. Taking him meant roping a knife back onto the team and pretending the rope meant what it used to mean.

And yet.

I looked at Tomas sitting in the snow with his father's grin gone out of him entirely, and I thought about debts. I thought about a ledger in a cabin in Halden-at-the-Wall, columns of numbers that had once seemed like the truest thing in my life, numbers that had driven me into a navy wreck and a sea cave and finally up a glacier to die among strangers. I thought about how easy it had been, every single step of my own long fall, to tell myself *I had no choice.* How warm that sentence is. How it fits the hand like a tool.

"You knew the approach," I said.

Tomas lifted his head.

"Past the col. The final approach to the tunnel. You've been up here before — you told us, first night in Halden, you and your uncle ran the high route when you were nineteen. Eirik's never done it in calving season. Neither have I." I flexed my burned hand inside the sock and the pain confirmed I was still alive and still angry, which were, at that point, my two leading qualifications. "So here's how this goes. You rope up with us. Front of the line, where I can see you. You get us through the approach, and when we're down off this mountain, you walk into Marta's kitchen and you tell her yourself what you did. Not Quill's version. Yours. And if you ever touch another rope with a knife in your pocket, I will personally feed you to the crevasse field and tell the crevasses you were delicious."

"That's not forgiveness," Eirik said quietly.

"No," I agreed. "It's not."

But Tomas was looking at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere — something between shame and disbelief, the look of a man who had braced for the axe and been handed a job instead. "Why?" he said. "After what I — Casper. *Why?*"

Because I know what desperation sounds like from the inside, I didn't say. Because the difference between you and me is one letter from a creditor and one bad winter, I didn't say. Because my father spent twenty years paying for a choice with silence and it killed him anyway, and I am not going to spend my life learning nothing from that, I didn't say.

"Because leaving you here makes me Quill," is what I said.

* * *

Eirik retied the rope team himself. Of course he did. New order: Tomas on point, me in the middle, Eirik at the back where he could watch us both — watch *me*, too, I understood; trust had taken casualties on both sides of that knife. He checked every knot twice. When he reached my harness he paused over my burned hand, turned it palm-up one more time, and wrapped it properly with tape from his own kit, tight and competent, his fingers quick and impersonal and — I don't know. Gentle at the very bottom of it, where gentleness lives when a man refuses to make a scene about anything.

"You should descend," he said. "Hand like that, you should descend."

"There's no down," I reminded him. "The glacier ate the road behind us, remember? Very dramatic. You were there."

His mouth twitched. One degree of thaw in a face that had been bedrock since the rope sang. "Reckless," he said, which from Eirik was practically a love letter.

Behind us, down the slope we'd climbed, the wind dropped for a moment — one of those sudden mountain silences that feels like the world holding its breath — and in the silence I heard it, faint and wrong: the whine of a motor. Not a helicopter. Smaller. Snow machines, maybe, on the lower glacier, or the distant grind of Quill's crew breaking camp and moving.

Half a day behind us, Tomas had said. Maybe less.

Tomas heard it too. He stood, and shouldered his pack, and looked up the col toward the upper glacier where the tunnel waited somewhere in all that blue, and when he spoke his voice was low and steady and stripped of everything except the truth:

"They'll know we're still moving. And they'll know what I did. Quill doesn't forgive a debt unpaid any more than the mountain does." He tightened his straps. "We need to reach the vault before they reach us. And Casper — if it comes to it — you should know I'm done doing arithmetic for other men."

Above us the sky was tearing open, and through the gap the upper glacier lay shining and enormous, hiding whatever my father had hidden, glowing faintly, if the journals were right, in the dark under all that ice.

We started up.
