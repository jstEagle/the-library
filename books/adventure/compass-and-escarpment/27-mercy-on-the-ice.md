## 27. Mercy on the Ice

The rope went through my hands twice before I understood what I was doing.

Tomas stood at the edge of the col with his harness half-buckled, looking like a man waiting to be told which way to die. Behind him the route we'd climbed lay sealed under fresh collapse; ahead of him the glacier rose in blue steps toward the melt tunnel Eirik had been watching all morning with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"You understand," Eirik said, not unkindly, "that the traditional response to what you did is a length of rope and a short walk."

"I understand it."

"And you understand that I am not a traditional man, or we would already be done talking."

Tomas nodded. His jaw worked. He was twenty-three years old and he had the face of someone who had aged forty in a single night, which, I supposed, he had. Betrayal does that. It had done it to me too, once, though my betrayal had been smaller — a lie told to a navy lieutenant across a table, smooth as varnish. Tomas's had been ropes cut at a col, and Eirik falling sixty feet into a crevasse before my ice axe caught him.

I still had the bruise across both forearms from holding that fall. I could feel it every time I coiled rope, which was constantly, because everything on a glacier is rope.

"Give me your reasons again," I said. "All of them. Slowly. If I'm going to tie my life to you, I want to know exactly what I'm tying it to."

He sat down on his pack like his legs had made the decision without consulting him. "Quill's broker found me in Halden before Rennick did. Before anyone did. My mother's medical bills — you know about those, everyone in Halden knows about those, that's the problem with villages, there's no such thing as a private debt." He looked at his boots. "They didn't threaten me. That's the thing nobody believes. They just showed me the numbers. Quill's people are very good at numbers. They showed me what my family owed and what they'd pay for word of where you were going and when, and they let me sit with the two figures until one of them ate the other." He laughed, a dry terrible sound. "It took four days. Four days of arithmetic. And then at the col I cut the fixed line and I told myself it wasn't murder, it was just — removing an obstacle, it was just business, and then Eirik fell and I heard the sound he made and I knew exactly what it was."

Eirik said nothing. He was watching the upper glacier while Tomas talked, but I'd climbed with him long enough now to know that Eirik's attention was never only in one place. His silence had weight and direction, and right now all of it was pointed at the boy on the pack.

"Here's my problem," I said. "I don't forgive you. I want to be clear about that. What you did at the col — if my arm had been an inch slower, or the axe placement bad, Eirik would be at the bottom of that crevasse and I'd be alone up here with Quill's team behind me and no guide. So no forgiveness. Not yet. Maybe not ever."

"Fair," Tomas whispered.

"But forgiveness isn't actually what this decision is about." I crouched so we were eye level. "This decision is about the fact that you've been to the far side of the pass. You've seen Quill's staging camp. You know their guide, their gear, their pace. And you know the final approach to the vault better than any of us, because you spent last summer running supply lines for the survey teams before they pulled out. Eirik can read ice better than anyone alive, but even he hasn't been past the serac field at this season." I held out the spare rope. "So here's the offer. You rope in with us, you lead us through the approach, and you spend whatever's left of your life being useful instead of being sorry. That's the trade. Sorry is cheap. Useful costs."

For a long moment he didn't move. Then he took the rope, and his hands were shaking so badly it took him three tries to clip it to his harness, and Eirik finally reached over and did it for him, checking the carabiner with two fingers, the way you'd check a child's seatbelt.

"Understand something," Eirik said quietly. "You fall, we arrest you. That's not mercy. That's arithmetic too. A dead body on a rope is worse than a live climber. But if you lead us wrong — if you take us onto a slope you know will slide, if you make one more calculation that spends our lives to save yours — then the mountain will do what I would otherwise have to do myself, and it will be slower." He patted the carabiner once, almost gently. "Climb well, Tomas Voss. Your grandmother is watching from Halden whether you can see her or not."

That landed harder than any threat. I watched it land. Tomas flinched as if struck, and then something in his spine reorganized itself, and he stood up, and when he spoke his voice had stopped shaking.

"The tunnel mouth is two hours' travel, maybe three with the new snow. There's a crevasse field first — it looks solid but the bridges are rotten, we cross wide, spaced out, no bunching. Then the slope under the east seracs. We stay left. Everyone wants to go right because it looks easier, and right is where the mountain eats people." He swallowed. "And after that, the melt channel. It opens widest at midday when the sun's on the ice. We time it or we don't get in."

"Then we move now," Eirik said, "and we're at the crevasse field by ten."

* * *

Roping a penitent man into a rope team is a strange kind of intimacy. Every few minutes the line between us went taut or slack and reminded me he was there — the living weight of him, the trust I hadn't given and couldn't take back. When he moved well, I felt it as relief. When he stumbled, I felt it as a small cold hand around my heart.

He moved well. That was the surprise. Grief and shame had burned away whatever softness he'd had, and what remained was precise. He called the rotten bridges before Eirik saw them, probing each snow bridge with his axe handle, reading the hollow drum-sound of thin ice over nothing. Twice he stopped us entirely, backed us off a line that looked perfect, and picked a uglier route that held.

"How do you know?" I asked him the second time, breathless, standing on a shelf of ice the width of a coffin while the glacier breathed somewhere beneath us.

"Because I helped mark the safe route for the survey teams, and then I watched Quill's advance man photograph our markers last August." He said it flatly, without drama. "Whatever route looks easiest over the next hour, assume Quill has a copy of it. We go ugly."

We went ugly. It cost us an hour and most of my remaining courage, including a traverse above a crevasse so deep and so blue that looking into it was like looking down a throat. The light came up out of it pale and ancient, and somewhere far below water was moving — I could hear it, a sound like the glacier swallowing.

At the far side, Eirik called a halt and we drank melted snow from a thermos, passing it down the rope without speaking. Tomas sat apart from us, which was fair, and ate a ration bar in small mechanical bites.

"They'll be behind us by now," he said eventually. "Quill's team moves fast and cuts corners. If they took the marked route—"

"Then they're an hour back and gaining," Eirik finished. "Yes. I've been assuming so since dawn." He screwed the thermos lid down and looked at me. "Which means the question isn't whether we reach the tunnel first. It's whether we're still inside when they arrive, and what the mountain does about it."

"What does it usually do?" I asked.

Eirik smiled without any humor in it at all. "In calving season? Everything."

* * *

The slope beneath the east seracs was the worst ground I had ever crossed, and I had crossed ground that killed my father.

The seracs themselves hung above us like a city of frozen architecture — towers and buttresses and leaning walls of ice the size of buildings, groaning in the warm wind, shedding small avalanches of debris at intervals that had no pattern I could find. The route stayed left, as Tomas had said, hugging the rock rib where the glacier scraped the mountainside, and even so the air was full of a fine cold dust that glittered in the sun and got into everything, my teeth, my eyes, the lining of my lungs.

Halfway across, the mountain made its opinion known.

It started as a sound like a door slamming, miles away, and then another, closer, and then the whole serac city began to speak at once — cracks like rifle shots overlapping, echoing, multiplying, until the noise was less a sound than a weather. Eirik's fist went up. We froze on the rope, three dots on a white diagonal, and watched a serac the size of a church lean, shudder, and come apart.

It didn't fall so much as unfold. Tons of ice dropped the first hundred feet as a single block, then burst, and the avalanche came down the slope toward us as a rolling gray-white flood — not toward us, I corrected myself, watching it, beside us, a hundred meters right, taking the easy line, the marked line, the line Quill's map would show.

It roared past below our rib for a full minute. When it stopped, the slope where it had passed simply wasn't there anymore. In its place: raw blue ice scoured clean, and a debris fan spreading out onto the lower glacier like something spilled.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That," Tomas said finally, in a voice like paper, "was the marked route."

"Yes," Eirik said.

"So they'll—"

"If they're on it." Eirik shouldered his pack. "Move. We don't stop to count other people's dead. That's how you join them."

But I looked back anyway, once, at the debris fan, and I thought about Quill's advance man photographing markers, and about arithmetic, and about how many ways there were on a glacier to pay a debt you hadn't borrowed.

* * *

We reached the melt channel at noon exactly, and Tomas's timing proved worth more than anything else he'd given us.

The channel was a slot in the glacier's flank, tall as a cathedral door and narrow as one, breathing out air that was strangely warm — warmer than the day, warmer than it had any right to be, smelling faintly of stone and old water. Meltwater ran out of it in a braided silver thread. Above the entrance, the ice had thinned to a foot of translucent blue, and sunlight came through it the color of deep sea, so that standing at the threshold felt like standing on the surface of water, about to dive.

I knew that feeling. I had built a life on that feeling. Something enormous and hidden below, and you, small and roped and afraid, about to go down to meet it.

"Tunnel runs maybe four hundred meters into the ice," Tomas said. "Survey teams went in with generators and lights. The cavity's at the end — big enough to hold a building. They cached the cargo there in the fifties and the army sealed it, and then the expedition—" He stopped. "You know the rest. You read the journals."

My father's journals. Frozen open on a table in a dead man's camp, his handwriting marching across the pages, young and furious and certain: *They want it buried and I am the only one who knows how deep.* He had turned back at the icefall, they'd said. The coward, the survivor. He hadn't turned back. He'd gone down instead, alone, and done the burying himself, and spent thirty years diving cold water and paying debts and keeping one secret so heavy it had bent his whole life around it like light around a mass.

"We go in roped?" I asked.

"No point." Eirik was peering into the gloom. "Inside the ice there's nothing to fall off of. And if it comes down, a rope won't save you, it'll just make sure we die together, which is sentimental." He clicked on his headlamp. "Single file. Tomas leads. Casper, you're second — your father's compass may want you close to the front."

The compass. I'd almost forgotten its weight against my ribs, which is its trick; it sits quiet until you need it, and then the needle swings toward the glacier like a dog straining at a leash. I pulled it out at the threshold. Even here, even this close, it was steady, insistent, pointing straight into the dark.

"It agrees," I said.

"Then it's smarter than the survey teams," Eirik said, "who needed a generator."

We went in.

The tunnel narrowed and widened like a throat working. The ice around us was layered with decades — ash bands from volcanoes long quiet, bubbles frozen mid-rise, a darkness that our three lamps pushed back only a few feet at a time. The meltwater underfoot made the going slick, and the sound of it echoed strangely, so that it was hard to tell whether water was dripping near us or far away or everywhere at once.

Two hundred meters in, Tomas stopped so suddenly I walked into him.

"Lights," he whispered. "Kill them. All of them."

Darkness, total, pressing. My heartbeat filled the whole world. And then, from deeper in the tunnel — ahead of us, downslope, where the cavity opened — a flicker. Faint, rhythmic, moving. Lamplight, reflected off ice, coming around a bend that shouldn't have held anyone.

Someone was already inside.

Eirik's hand found my shoulder in the black and squeezed once. In the reflected glow I could just see his face, and Tomas's, both turned toward that distant trembling light, and I understood that we had lost the race — that Quill, or what remained of Quill's team, had taken the marked route after all, or some other way, and had beaten us to the vault by hours or minutes, and that the only thing between them and my father's secret now was three divers and guides standing in a dark throat of ice with one compass and no plan.

The light bobbed closer. Voices carried along the tunnel, distorted by the ice into something low and wordless.

Eirik leaned in until his mouth was at my ear.

"Off the main channel," he breathed. "There'll be side galleries — melt channels branch. Find one, get deep, kill your lamp, and let them pass. Then we follow them in. Let Quill's men open what my father's generation sealed."

"And if what they open doesn't want opening?"

Eirik was silent for a moment. Somewhere above us, the glacier groaned — a long, tectonic sigh, the sound of a calving season reaching its peak, of a window beginning to close.

"Then," he said, "we'll be very glad we kept the boy who knows the way out."

The light swung nearer. I pulled Tomas into the black mouth of a side gallery, my thumb finding the compass in my pocket, its needle pulling past me, deeper in, toward the thing in the dark that my father had died keeping buried — and that, if the fates were cruel and the ice was honest, I was about to see glowing in the lamplight of the wrong hands.
