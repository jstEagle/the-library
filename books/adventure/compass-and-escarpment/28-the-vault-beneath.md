## 28. The Vault Beneath

The glacier woke us at four in the morning by trying to kill us.

That's not an exaggeration for effect. At four in the morning a serac the size of a church fell somewhere to the east of our camp, and the sound came through the ice like God clearing his throat, and the three of us sat up in the tent in the dark and listened to the mountain decide things without consulting us.

"Peak of the season," Eirik said, which was his way of saying good morning.

"That was close," Tomas said.

"That was *far*," Eirik said. "Close sounds different. Close sounds like the end of your life arriving early." He unzipped the tent and looked out at the grey-pink light on the icefall below us, and I watched his shoulders drop half an inch, which for Eirik Solheim was the equivalent of a child dancing. "The tunnel will be open."

I'd been dreaming about it — or dreading it, the line between those two having worn away sometime around day six on the ice. The melt tunnel. The route down into whatever my father had spent thirty years not telling me about. In my dream it had been a doorway in my father's boat, below the waterline, and I'd been diving toward it with empty tanks, and the compass needle had been spinning like a drunk compass should never spin, and I'd woken with my heart going like a bilge pump.

Now I took the compass out of its oilcloth wrap and checked it, because checking it had become a tic, like touching a crucifix. The needle lay steady as a nail, pointing down-slope, into the ice. Toward the thing.

"Still pointing?" Tomas asked. He asked every morning. I think he needed to hear that it was still impossible before breakfast, the way some people need coffee.

"Still pointing."

"Good," he said, and then, because Tomas was Tomas and shame lived in him like a splinter working toward the surface: "I still think you should have left me at the col."

"And I still think you should stop saying that," I said. "You're roped to us now. You fall, we all go over. It's not forgiveness, it's physics."

He almost smiled. That was progress. Two days ago he hadn't smiled at all; he'd sat at the edge of camp looking like a man doing arithmetic with his own worth and getting a negative number. Eirik had handled it the way Eirik handled everything — by handing Tomas the worst chores and pretending nothing had happened. There are worse theologies.

We ate cold oats and hard bread and drank water melted over the stove, and we packed, and we roped up, and we went down into the blue heart of the glacier to see what my father had died keeping buried.

* * *

The melt tunnel opened where the guidebooks would have said no tunnel could be, if glaciers had guidebooks, which they don't, which is why people keep dying on them. A season of warm winds had done what no drill could: eaten down through fifty feet of ice along an old crevasse seam and hollowed a passage that sloped into the glacier's interior like a throat.

Eirik stopped at the lip and looked down, and I watched him do the thing he did — reading ice the way Marta read weather, the way my father read water.

"It'll hold," he said finally. "Today. Maybe tomorrow. After that—" He shrugged. Glaciers don't do guarantees.

"After that it closes until next year," I said.

"Or forever. The calving's been violent this season. The whole lower face is retreating faster than I've seen." He looked at me. "Whatever's down there, Casper — once this closes, nobody gets to it again easily. Not Quill. Not Rennick. Not anyone. You understand what that means?"

I understood. It meant this was the door, and doors only open so long, and everything I'd climbed for was at the bottom of a melting throat in a mountain that wanted, on balance, to swallow us.

"I understand," I said.

"Then I go first," Eirik said. "Tomas anchors. Casper, you're between us. Nobody touches anything down there until we've looked at the walls. Ice caves kill people who touch things."

"Understood," I said, and meant it, mostly, though there was a brass compass in my pocket with my father's initials engraved on the case and a needle that had been leading me here since a record tide pulled the sea back off a cave beneath the escarpment, and I knew — I knew the way you know a current under calm water — that whatever discipline I promised on the surface, something in me was already climbing down.

* * *

Going into a glacier is not like going into a cave. Caves are old and patient and indifferent. Glaciers are alive. The tunnel breathed around us — a slow, tidal exhalation of cold air flowing out as the sun warmed the upper face, drawing warmer air in behind it. The walls were not white. They were blue, a blue so deep and saturated it seemed lit from inside, light entering through fissures far above and filtering down through decades of compressed snow until it arrived down here as pure color, pure depth, the blue you see behind your eyelids when you press them too hard.

I have spent my life underwater. I know blue. This was bluer than any water I'd ever dived, and silent in a way water never is, and I felt my lungs do something strange, some animal recognition that we were inside a living thing, walking down its gullet.

"Talk," Eirik said quietly. "Keep talking. If someone goes quiet, the others need to know immediately."

So we talked. Tomas told a story about a goat that got into his grandmother's kitchen and ate an entire loaf of bread and part of a wool sock, and Eirik told one about his father teaching him to read crevasses by the shape of the snow bridges, and I talked about my father's boat, the *Kestrel*, how she leaked at the stern gland and how he'd refused to fix it properly because, he said, a salvage man should always know exactly how much water is coming aboard. I talked and my voice echoed down the blue throat and the glacier carried it away somewhere deep, and I thought: it kept his voice too, maybe. Thirty years ago. Some version of him, young and scared, saying words into the ice.

The tunnel turned twice, dropped through a chamber where meltwater ran in a clear thread over sculpted floors, and narrowed to a slot we had to turn sideways to pass. Then it opened.

* * *

I want to describe it accurately, and I can't, because accuracy fails at thresholds. But I'll try.

The tunnel ended in a cavity the size of a chapel — that was Eirik's word later, chapel, and it stuck. Vaulted ceiling of blue ice, ribbed with ancient annual layers like the rings of a felled tree stood on end. The floor was bare glacier ice, swept clean by wind that must have moved through here in some earlier season, some earlier opening, long ago. And against the far wall, half cradled in a shelf of ice that had formed around it like a hand closing slowly over decades —

— a case.

Not a treasure chest. Nothing so honest. It was a metal container, military-grey, about the size of a sea chest, with latches and pressure seals and stenciled markings worn nearly to ghosts. It sat on a wooden pallet that had been dragged up here by men with ropes and sledges and desperation, and around it were the small archaeology of their passing: a rusted stove, a coil of rope fused into the ice, a tin cup standing upright as if set down yesterday by someone stepping out for a moment.

And the case glowed.

That's the part I keep failing to say right. It didn't glow like a lamp. It glowed faintly, from seams in the casing, a pale green-white shimmer, like the aurora had gotten bored of the sky and come indoors. Our headlamps swept across it and the light didn't just reflect — it answered, faintly, a breath of luminescence rising and falling like something breathing in its sleep.

Nobody spoke. Even the glacier seemed to hold its breath. Somewhere far above, ice settled with a sound like a long sigh.

"It's real," Tomas whispered. He sounded like a man in church who'd expected an empty building.

Eirik raised a hand — wait — and moved forward alone, low, careful, checking the floor, the ceiling, the shelf of ice holding the case. He touched nothing. He came back to us with his face doing something complicated.

"The pallet's been here a long time," he said. "But look at the snow drift near the entrance. It's been disturbed. Recently. Within a season or two." He let that sit. "Someone else has been down here."

Quill, I thought. Or Rennick's people. Or both, circling each other across the ice like dogs that haven't decided whether to fight. My hand found the compass through the oilcloth.

"Or the expedition came back," Tomas said. "Years later. To check on it."

"Maybe," Eirik said, in the voice of a man humoring a hopeful child.

I stepped past them both.

"Casper." Eirik's voice, sharp.

"I'm not touching it. I'm looking. You said look at the walls." I crossed the chapel floor, my boots ringing faintly on the ice, and stood in front of the case, and the glow washed up over me, green-white, cold, and I felt it on my face like moonlight off water.

There were markings on the casing. Stenciled numbers, mostly gone. And below them, scratched into the metal with something sharp — a knife point, maybe, or a screwdriver — in a hand I knew better than my own:

**C.W. — IF FOUND, DO NOT MOVE.**

My father's initials. My father's handwriting. Cut into steel in a vault of ice above the world, decades before I was born, or when I was a baby, or whenever it was — time had gone soft in here, the way it does underwater.

"He was here," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened by the ice. "He stood right here. He wrote this."

Eirik came and stood beside me. He didn't touch me — Eirik wasn't a touching man — but he stood close enough that his shoulder was a fact I could lean on if I needed to.

"Do not move," he read aloud. "He means the device itself?"

"I think he means exactly what he says." I crouched, keeping my hands well clear, and studied the seals, the seams, the source of the glow. Faint vents along the lower edge, hairline-thin, breathing that pale light. Whatever slept in the box, it wasn't inert. It was powered. After all these years, it was still *on*. "It's not cargo," I said slowly. "Cargo doesn't glow. Cargo doesn't need a warning carved into the case. This is equipment. Live equipment. Something that runs."

"A navigation system," Tomas said. "That's what my grandmother said they carried up. Something that could find its way anywhere. Through anything."

Through anything. I thought of Rennick in his pressed uniform asking his smooth questions about anomalies. I thought of the classified wreck off the escarpment, the sealed case I'd recovered for pay too good to be clean, the navy pretending the ship had never existed while paying divers to crawl over its bones. I thought of my father's charts, annotated with bearings that matched a compass that didn't point north, and of the report he'd signed — or that Rennick had signed — clearing his last fatal voyage.

Everything I'd been carrying separately in different pockets of my mind shifted, settled, and locked together with a click I felt in my teeth.

"This is why the navy's here," I said. "This is why the wreck is classified. This is why my father died." I stood up. "It was never about salvage. It was never about a lost expedition. They lost this — whatever it is — and they've been looking for it ever since, and my father found it, and instead of telling them, he hid it deeper. He built a map to it that looks like a compass and points like a lie, and he spent his whole life making sure the wrong people couldn't follow it."

"And the right people?" Eirik asked quietly.

"My father thought there weren't any."

The glow pulsed, slow, patient. Up close I could hear it now — or feel it, more than hear, a frequency at the bottom of hearing, like the hum of a power line heard through a wall. Like something waiting.

"We should document it and leave," Eirik said. "Photographs. Notes. Nothing touched. Then we get down off this mountain and decide what to do with the truth of it."

"Yes," I said. And I meant it. I swear I meant it.

But I was still crouched there, close enough to read the ghost-stencils on the casing, when Tomas, up by the entrance chamber, made a sound.

Not a word. A short, punched-out exhale, the sound of a man seeing something that rearranges the furniture of his understanding.

"Eirik," he said. "Casper. You need to see this."

* * *

It was ten meters back up the tunnel, tucked into a niche where the blue ice curved away, and if you weren't looking for it you'd have taken it for a shadow.

A body.

Preserved by the cold the way only a glacier preserves — not mummified, not decayed, simply *stopped*. A man in expedition clothing forty years out of date, sitting with his back against the ice, his pack beside him, his hands folded in his lap as if he'd sat down to rest and the mountain had gently declined to ever let him get up again. Frost had claimed his eyelashes and beard. His face was peaceful in a way faces on mountains rarely get to be.

And pinned to his chest, frozen into the fabric, was a notebook. Open. The pages fanned and welded together by years of freeze and thaw into a single block of paper, but the top page legible, the ink faded to brown, the handwriting slanted and hurried.

Eirik played his lamp across it carefully, not touching. We read it upside down, three heads bent together, our breath fogging the air and drifting up into the blue dark.

The last entry was dated — the date meant nothing to me then except that it was older than me — and it read:

*Left the device with Whitlock's plan. He takes the sea route. We stay to mislead them. If you are reading this, the compass is true. Trust the needle, not the men.*

Whitlock.

My name. My father's name. Written in a dead man's notebook in a vault of ice, in a sentence that assumed — that *planned for* — someone reading it someday.

Trust the needle, not the men.

Behind us, down in the chapel, the device glowed on in its cradle of ice, patient as a banked fire. Above us, somewhere beyond a hundred feet of blue, the sun was working on the glacier's face, widening the throat we'd come down, opening the mountain for a few more hours, a day at most.

And from far above — so far above it might have been imagination, might have been the ice settling, might have been anything — came a sound none of us mistook for anything natural.

Voices. More than one. Coming down.
