## 29. Quill Arrives

There is a particular quality to hearing voices in a place where no voices should be. It's not fear, exactly. Fear comes later, when the voices resolve into names and numbers and the specific cadence of men who have been paid to want something you have.

The first thing I felt was something older and stupider than fear — the animal urge to stand very still, as if the glacier were a game of statues and I might win it.

"Down," Eirik breathed. "Behind the serac. Now."

We pressed ourselves against the wall of blue ice at the mouth of the melt tunnel, Tomas's shoulder hard against mine, all three of us suddenly aware of how loud our breathing was, how loud everything was, in a cathedral built for silence.

The voices came down the throat of the tunnel in echoes, bouncing off the ice so that they seemed to come from everywhere at once — above us, below us, inside the walls. Four voices. Maybe five. One of them carried the flat, cheerful authority of a man reading a menu he already knew by heart.

Quill.

I had met him once, in the harbor at home, over a handshake that lasted two seconds too long and an offer that lasted a lifetime too long. *Anything recovered from the cave, Miss Whitlock. Anything at all. Name a figure that would embarrass you.* He'd had teeth like a piano and eyes like a man doing arithmetic on your face. I had refused badly — I remember that clearly, the way you remember stepping on ice that then cracks — and ever since, his people had been watching me the way gulls watch a boat with a full hold.

Now his voice was coming down a glacier toward a vault my father had spent thirty years keeping empty of exactly this kind of man.

"How many?" Tomas whispered.

"At least four," Eirik said. "Rope team moving fast. They'll have fixed lines." His jaw worked. "They came up the far side. They must have climbed through the night."

"They cut corners," Tomas said, and there was something strange in his voice — guilt wearing the mask of contempt. I looked at him. He looked away. There would be time for that accounting later, if there was a later. There usually isn't, but we're optimistic creatures, we humans. We keep scheduling later like it's a room we've booked.

Eirik made his decision the way he made all decisions: quickly, quietly, and with the air of a man who has already lived through the alternative. "We go back down. Into the chapel. If they're coming for the device, better we're standing next to it than hiding from them near it."

"That's insane," I said.

"Yes," he agreed. "Come on."

* *

The chapel — that's what we'd taken to calling the chamber where the expedition had cached their cargo, because of the way the light fell through the melt tunnel in long blue shafts, because of the hush, because of the bodies. Two of them, still roped together at the far wall, sixty years dead and perfectly preserved, kneeling as if in prayer or exhaustion, which in the end are the same thing on a glacier. The journals lay frozen open beside them. My father's name sat in one of them like a stone in my chest.

And in the center, cradled in ice that had grown around it like amber around an insect, sat the device.

It was smaller than I'd imagined, after all those years of my father's silences. A cylinder about the length of my forearm, dull grey metal gone faintly green with age, banded with clamps and stenciled markings that had been deliberately ground off — except one, a serial fragment that meant nothing to me and would mean everything to a man like Rennick. It didn't glow, not really. But in the blue light it seemed to hold a light of its own, the way deep water does, the way certain stones do when you lift them from a riverbed. A prototype navigation system, the journal had said. Guidance for things that fly. Worth more than every boat in every harbor I'd ever worked. Buried here instead, by men who chose a mountain over a fortune, and died for the choosing.

My father had been the diver who turned back. The young one. The one who carried the secret down the mountain and then carried it for a lifetime, letting it eat him alive, letting it cost him everything — his reputation, his marriage, eventually, in its own slow way, his life — rather than sell it or surrender it.

*Trust the needle, not the men.*

The brass compass hung at my throat now, on its cord, under my jacket. Its needle, even now, even here, strained gently toward the device like a dog toward a door.

We took up positions behind the ice cradle, which was about as much cover as a dinner table, and waited. Eirik held his ice axe in both hands. Tomas had a knife and the expression of someone recalculating every choice he'd ever made. I had a compass, a dead man's confession, and the growing certainty that whatever happened in the next hour, I was going to have to make a decision I couldn't take back.

The voices grew louder. Then boots — the squeak-and-crunch of crampons on wet ice, a sound I will hear for the rest of my life whether I want to or not. Light bloomed at the tunnel mouth: headlamps, four of them, sweeping the chamber in practiced arcs.

"There she is," said Quill's voice, warm as bathwater. "Ladies and gentlemen, look at that. Sixty years in the ice and not a spot of rot. They built things properly back then."

He came down into the chamber the way he'd shaken my hand — taking up more space than he occupied. He was older than I remembered, or the mountain had aged him; frost in his beard, a healing cut above one eye, but the arithmetic still running behind his face. Three men behind him with ropes and axes and the loose readiness of hired muscle. One of them I recognized distantly from the harbor — the night my boat was ransacked, probably. Probably the same hands that had gone through my father's charts.

Quill saw us immediately, of course. We were standing in plain sight behind insufficient cover, because Eirik's plan had been insane, and insane plans do not include concealment.

"Well," Quill said, spreading his arms. "Miss Whitlock. And the famous Solheim. And—" his gaze flicked to Tomas, and something passed between them, quick and private and ugly, "—young Voss. What a family reunion. You found it for us after all. I did say you had talent."

"I found it for nobody," I said.

"And yet here we all are, standing around it." He walked toward the device with the unhurried confidence of a man who believes the world is a series of transactions and he has the bigger ledger. "You should know I'm not a monster, Miss Whitlock. I pay well. I pay *extraordinarily* well. That thing is worth more than the navy's budget for the entire coast, and I only need a fraction of what it's worth to make everyone in this room rich enough to never work again. Even the guide. Even the boy." He smiled at Tomas. Tomas went the color of old ice.

"It's not for sale," I said. "It's not anything. It's a weapon, Quill. A guidance system. For missiles. That's what your fortune is — a way to point death more accurately. My father died keeping it buried. Four men in this room's history died keeping it buried."

"People die of everything," Quill said mildly. He reached the cradle and put his gloved hand on the ice above the device, almost tenderly. "They die of poverty most reliably of all. I've watched it happen. Haven't you? Your father's debts — I bought some of them, you know. Did the bank mention that? I own paper on your boat, Miss Whitlock. Or I did. Debts are so negotiable, when both parties are reasonable."

Something cold moved through me that had nothing to do with the glacier.

"You're going to step away from that," Eirik said. His voice was quiet, which from Eirik was the loudest warning there was.

Quill sighed, the sigh of a reasonable man surrounded by unreasonable ones. "Gentlemen," he said to his crew, "the rope, please. We're going to extract the item and be out of here before the window closes. Nobody needs to be a hero. Heroes freeze last, in my experience, because they're always the ones standing still arguing."

Two of his men came forward with a coil of line, meaning to sling the device out of its ice cradle whole. And that — that was the moment everything went wrong, though I suppose it had been going wrong since 1961, since my father signed a report he shouldn't have, since the tide dropped forty years' worth in a single afternoon and showed me a cave I should have swum past.

Because the ice cradle wasn't just ice. It was load-bearing. Sixty years of meltwater had grown around that cylinder like a knuckle of bone, and the chamber's roof — the hundred feet of glacier above us, groaning all morning as the calving season opened the mountain — leaned on that knuckle. I understood it in the half-second between the first swing of the ice axe and the sound it made, the way you understand a wave is going to break badly a moment before it breaks.

"No—" I said.

The axe bit. The ice cracked — a long, branching crack, wrong-sounding, like a bone breaking in a body. The second swing freed the device's upper clamp, and the cylinder shifted in its cradle, and the casing — old, brittle, ground-down, sealed by men in a hurry with the tools they had — the casing split along a seam with a sound like a struck bell.

A hiss began.

It was very quiet. That was the worst part. Not a roar, not an explosion. Just a thin, steady exhalation, like the glacier breathing through a wound, and a smell — sharp, chemical, wrong — cutting through the cold.

Everyone stopped. Even Quill. Especially Quill.

"What," he said, "was that."

"It's venting," I heard myself say. My voice sounded far away, calm, the voice I use underwater when things go bad and panic is a luxury. "The casing's breached. Whatever's inside — propellant, coolant, I don't know — it's coming out. That thing has been sealed for sixty years and you just broke the seal with an ice axe."

Quill stared at the device. The thin hiss continued, patient as the tide. Frost was already forming in a feathered plume around the split seam, white vapor curling in the lamplight like incense.

"Then we take it faster," Quill said.

"Are you deaf?" Eirik's composure finally cracked. "That could be toxic. That could be volatile. You don't know what's in it—"

"I know what it's *worth*," Quill snapped, and for the first time I saw the real man under the bathwater warmth, and the real man was not reasonable at all. The real man was a hole shaped like a person, and nothing would ever fill it, and he would climb into a venting weapon with his bare hands if the price was right. "Whole. I said whole and I meant whole. Wrap it. Move it. Now."

One of his men — the harbor one, the one with my father's charts in his memory — hesitated. Just a fraction. His lamp wavered off the device and toward the tunnel mouth, toward up, toward out. "Boss," he said. "The smell. Maybe we—"

"Maybe you wrap the *item*," Quill said, "or maybe you find out what the back side of a glacier pays, which is nothing."

And that was when the glacier answered for all of us.

The crack from the ice axe hadn't stopped. It had traveled — up through the cradle, out across the chamber floor, a seam of white racing through the blue like lightning frozen mid-strike. Somewhere above us, deep in the hundred feet of ice that was our sky, something enormous let go with a groan that I felt in my teeth, in my sternum, in the soles of my feet.

"Roof's shifting," Eirik said. All the blood had left his face. "Casper. Casper, listen to me. When it goes, get to the tunnel. Don't stop for anything. Don't stop for *me*."

"Don't be stupid."

"It's my job to be stupid professionally," he said. "Go when it goes."

Tomas grabbed my arm. His hand was shaking. "The needle," he said. "Your father wrote — trust the needle. Does it still—"

I put my hand over the compass at my throat. Through the jacket, through the brass, I could feel — or believed I could feel, which on a glacier amounts to the same thing — the needle straining, steady now, steadier than it had ever been, pointing not north but at the broken thing in the ice. At the wound. At the truth.

Above us, the glacier groaned again, longer this time, a sound like a ship tearing loose from its moorings. Snow sifted down through the lamplight. The hiss of the venting device rose slightly, as if it too were waking up, as if sixty years of patience had curdled into urgency.

Quill's men were wrapping the cylinder in rope with shaking hands, because greed is strong but stronger still is greed with a boss watching. Quill himself stood over them, shouting about speed, about the window closing, about money, always money, his voice climbing over the glacier's voice as if volume were a form of power.

And then, cutting through everything — through the hiss and the groan and the falling snow and Quill's arithmetic — came a new sound from the tunnel mouth above.

A different rhythm of boots. More of them. And a voice, amplified, official, carrying the particular flat authority of a man who has never once doubted his right to give orders in any room, on any mountain, in any world:

"This is Lieutenant Rennick, Royal Navy. Everyone in the chamber will cease movement and remain where you are. The site is under military jurisdiction."

Eirik closed his eyes. "Of course," he said. "Of course he tracked us. Of course he's *here*."

Quill stopped shouting. For one moment, thief and officer and diver and guide and traitor all stood together in the blue light, listening to the glacier groan overhead and the device hiss below, three kinds of ruin converging on one small chamber at the top of the world.

The needle pulled true against my chest.

The ice above us began, very softly, to sing.
