## 30. Rennick on the Mountain

The sound reached us before the shadow did.

It came down the glacier like a rumor — a flat, rhythmic hammering that didn't belong to ice or wind or anything the mountain had ever made. Eirik heard it first. He stopped mid-stride, one crampon still planted in the crust, and tilted his head the way he did when reading weather, except this wasn't weather.

"Helicopter," he said.

Tomas swore in three languages, none of them politely.

We were maybe forty minutes below the melt tunnel, roped and tired and raw from the col, from Tomas, from everything, when the machine itself came around the shoulder of the escarpment — black against the white, flying low and arrogant, the way things fly when they've never once had to worry about crashing. It banked over the serac field we'd spent half a day crossing, and I watched its shadow slide across the blue ice like a hand feeling for us.

"They tracked us," I said. "The whole way."

Eirik didn't answer. He was already moving us off the open slope, toward a pressure ridge that would at least break the rotor wash. But there was nowhere to hide on a glacier. That's the thing about glaciers. They are honest terrain. Every crevasse is visible to anyone who knows how to look, and every person on them is a dark speck on a white page.

The helicopter set down above the icefall, on a shelf of wind-scoured snow that some pilot with more nerve than sense had decided was a landing zone. Skids bit in. The rotors kept turning. And men came out of it — six of them, in cold-weather gear that looked new enough to still have its price tags folded inside, carrying rifles slung across their backs like they'd read about mountains in a brochure.

And at the front of them, picking his way down the slope with the careful steps of a man who hated every second of being on ice but refused to admit it, came Lieutenant Rennick.

* * *

I want to tell you I felt fear. What I actually felt, standing there with my rope team and my dead father's compass in my jacket, was something colder and more useful: recognition. Of course it was Rennick. It had always been going to be Rennick. The man had signed the report that cleared my father's fatal last voyage — *cleared*, as if a drowned boat were a clerical matter — and he had been circling me ever since like a debt collector with a uniform. The harbor. Halden. The night intruder Marta's grandson caught going through my things. All of it, threads leading up this mountain to him.

He stopped ten meters away, close enough to talk without shouting, far enough to be safe from anything we might do, which told me he'd thought about what we might do. His breath fogged. His eyes went past me to Eirik, to Tomas, cataloguing them the way you catalogue equipment.

"Casper Whitlock." He said my name like a receipt. "You're a long way from your boat."

"You're a long way from your paperwork," I said.

Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger. Disappointment, almost, as if I'd failed an exam he'd expected me to pass. Behind him his men spread out along the ridge with the unhurried confidence of people who have never once been outnumbered on their own say-so.

"Here's how this goes," Rennick said. "Nobody's hurt today. Nobody has to be. You're all detained pending investigation — that's Eirik Solheim, unlicensed guiding activity in a restricted zone; that's Tomas Voss, who I suspect has a story worth hearing about how you found this route. And you—" his gaze settled back on me, and for a moment the officer's mask thinned and I saw the arithmetic underneath, the same arithmetic that had priced my father's life at nothing— "you're in possession of government property."

"The compass?"

"The device."

So he knew. He knew about the vault, the tunnel, the thing glowing faintly under the ice. Which meant someone had talked. My stomach did a slow, sick roll as I looked at Tomas — and Tomas looked back at me with an expression I couldn't read, guilt or terror or both braided together, and I remembered Quill, and the fixed line, and every conversation Tomas had ever walked away from too quietly.

Or maybe Rennick had simply followed the compass himself. Maybe the navy had known where its buried secret pointed all along and had just been waiting for someone else to dig it up. With Rennick I genuinely couldn't say which possibility was worse.

"We haven't reached any device," Eirik said evenly. He stepped slightly forward, putting his shoulder into the space between Rennick and me, and I could feel the mountain guide in him doing calculations — slope angle, distance, the shelf edge fifty meters below. "We're recreational climbers. Lost."

Rennick smiled. It was the worst smile I'd seen in weeks, and I'd recently watched a salvage broker count money over my father's grave of a boat. "Mr. Solheim. Do you know what happens to guides who take clients into restricted military zones? Because I do. I write the reports." He let that sit. "Everyone walks to the helicopter. Everyone gets warm. Everyone tells the truth eventually. That's the whole plan. It's a good plan."

"And if we don't?" Tomas asked.

Rennick turned to him with genuine mildness. "Then my men walk you to the helicopter. There's a difference, but it's mostly medical."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about power: it doesn't shout. It doesn't need to. It arrives by helicopter on a glacier no one can own and starts giving orders as though the ice itself had signed a contract. Rennick's men moved us upslope with polite hands and rifles held low, and the terrible thing was how easy it was. We were exhausted, roped to each other, and surrounded. Eirik tested the situation once — a shift of weight, a glance at the drop — and one of the soldiers said, "Don't," in a voice so flat that even Eirik recalculated.

They marched us toward the idling helicopter, and I made myself look at it properly. Big enough for all of us plus cargo. Cargo. That word arrived in my head wearing boots. They weren't here to arrest us. Arrests happen on land, in rooms, with lawyers. They were here to extract something, and we were incidental — packaging to be cleared away once the contents were secured.

My hand found the compass through my jacket. The brass was body-warm after days against my chest, and I thought of my father's annotations on those old charts, the bearing repeated in his cramped handwriting until it looked like prayer. *He knew they'd come someday.* Maybe that was the whole design of his quiet, stubborn, ruinous life — not to use the failsafe, but to keep it out of reach until someone with clean hands found it.

I did not have clean hands. But I had free ones, and Rennick hadn't searched me yet, because searching a woman on an open glacier in front of witnesses was the kind of detail officers leave to rooms.

"Whitlock." Rennick fell into step beside me. Up close he smelled of jet fuel and coffee, absurdly civilized. "Last chance for the version of this where you benefit. The device, voluntarily surrendered. Full cooperation. In exchange—" he glanced back at the helicopter, at the debt that had chased me across a country— "your father's obligations die today. Every creditor. Every lien. Signed before you're back at sea level. You'd be free."

"I am free," I said.

"You're standing on a glacier in handcuff-adjacent circumstances."

"I mean generally."

He exhaled through his nose, almost amused. "Your father wasn't free either. You know that now, don't you? He understood exactly what he was carrying. And look what it bought him." He gestured at the emptiness around us — the ice, the sky, the whole indifferent white. "A hole in the water and a daughter who dives wrecks for men like me. Whatever's under that ice ruined your family once already. Hand it over and be done with the past."

And that was the moment I understood him completely. Not evil. Worse: sincere. Rennick believed every word. The device was government property, the government was order, order was safety, and my father had died of disorder — his own, for keeping secrets. In Rennick's ledger, my father was a cautionary tale.

In mine, he was the only honest man in the story.

"He didn't drown because of the past," I said. "He drowned because of men who sign reports without reading them."

Rennick's jaw tightened, just once, and I knew I'd landed somewhere real. Good. Let him carry that up the mountain too.

* * *

Twenty meters from the helicopter, the mountain intervened.

It started as a sound under our feet — a deep, structural groan, the glacier clearing its throat — and then the icefall below us spoke properly: a crack like the sky splitting, followed by the long thunder of a serac letting go somewhere in the blue maze. The ground shuddered. Snow hissed off the slope above. One of the soldiers stumbled; another grabbed the helicopter's skid as the pilot lifted off instinctively, rotors flaring snow into a blinding curtain.

For perhaps ten seconds, nobody was in command of anything. The world was noise and white.

Eirik moved first. I felt it more than saw it — a hard tug on the rope, three sharp pulls, the signal we'd drilled on the moraine until it lived in my hands. *Run.* He cut Tomas loose with his knife, seized my harness, and hauled me away from the rotor wash downslope, into the smoke of displaced snow, and the last thing I saw clearly was Rennick shouting orders into the chaos, his voice swallowed whole, his men scattered and blind, the helicopter hovering above its own dust like a wasp that had lost the thread.

Then we were running — really running, crampons biting, lungs burning, the compass a small hard heart against my ribs — down into the blue labyrinth where helicopters can't follow and rifles are just expensive ice axes.

Behind us, the thunder kept rolling. Ahead, the melt tunnel waited, and whatever the calving season had opened, it was closing by the hour.

Rennick had brought the full weight of the government to the top of the world, and the world had simply shrugged him off. But I knew better than to celebrate. Men like Rennick don't leave. They regroup.

And we had left him holding nothing but a landing zone and a grudge.
