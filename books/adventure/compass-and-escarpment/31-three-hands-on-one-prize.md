## 31. Three Hands on One Prize

The device was still hissing when the sky filled with men.

That's how it felt, anyway — like the mountain had exhaled and everything that had been circling us for months finally dropped out of the air at once. Rennick's team above, rappelling down the melt tunnel with the crisp economy of people who had rehearsed this. Quill's crew at the far mouth of the cavity, ice axes in hand, breathing hard from a climb they'd clearly made faster than any sane guide would allow. And us — me, Eirik, Tomas — standing in the blue glow of something my father had spent thirty years making sure nobody would ever stand next to.

Three hands on one prize. Except the prize was leaking, and the glacier holding it was groaning like an old ship in weather, and every one of those hands thought it belonged to them.

"Nobody moves," Rennick said.

He'd said it twice now. The first time, backlit at the tunnel mouth with his breath steaming, he'd sounded like authority. The second time, with the glacier's rumble rolling through the ice beneath our boots, he sounded like a man shouting instructions at a tide.

I'll give him this: he didn't waste time on speeches. He'd tracked us across forty miles of icefall, landed a helicopter on a calving glacier in a closing weather window, and now he stood six meters away with four armed sailors fanning out behind him, and all he wanted was the thing in the middle of the room. The thing that was, at that moment, ticking.

Not ticking like a clock. Ticking like a kettle deciding.

"Casper." He used my first name the way he always did, gently, as if we were colleagues. As if he hadn't signed the report that cleared my father's death and ruined my family's name in the same week. "Step away from the casing."

"It's venting," I said. "You can hear it."

"I can hear the glacier. That's normal for calving season."

"That," Eirik said quietly, nodding at the thin ribbon of vapor curling off the seam in the device's housing, "is not the glacier."

Eirik doesn't waste words. When he spends one, people listen. Even Rennick's sailors glanced at the vapor, then at their officer, then back, the way men do when they've noticed the water rising past the mark someone drew and nobody official has mentioned it yet.

Quill laughed.

It was a terrible sound in that space — too loud, bouncing off the blue walls, coming back wrong. He'd lost weight since Halden, or maybe the mountain had simply pared him down to what he actually was: a man built entirely of appetite, standing in a cathedral of ice with frost in his beard and greed doing all his thinking for him.

"You hear that, Lieutenant?" Quill called. "Your own people are nervous. Here's my offer, since yours is so obviously 'shoot everyone': my crew walks out with the unit, you get to write your report, everybody's rich except the dead."

"There is no deal," Rennick said. "That equipment is government property—"

"It's *my* salvage," Quill said. "Found under a glacier by *my* guide's information—"

Tomas made a small sound beside me. I put my hand on his arm without looking. His confession was two days old and three days raw; he'd sold our route to Quill to pay his mother's debts, disabled our ropes at the col, nearly killed Eirik, and then — because shame is a strange rope too — told us everything and led us here through approaches only he knew. We hadn't forgiven him. We'd roped him in anyway. On the ice, those are closer to the same thing than they should be.

"The point," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the only kind of voice worth having, "is that it's leaking. Whatever's inside that casing has been sealed since before I was born, and the seal just cracked, and we are all standing in a hole in a glacier that is actively trying to close."

The glacier chose that moment to agree with me.

It wasn't dramatic — not yet. Just a long, deep groan rolling up through the floor, the sound of a hundred thousand tons of ice shifting its weight, followed by a crack somewhere overhead that sent a fine snow of ice crystals sifting down through our headlamp beams. Everyone looked up. Everyone looked down again quickly, because looking up at cracking ice is a habit you lose fast.

"Window's closing," Eirik said. "Hours. Maybe less if that serac field lets go."

"Then we secure the device and extract," Rennick said. "Corporal, bag it."

One of the sailors started forward, and Quill's nearest man — a slab-faced climber I'd last seen ransacking my boat — shifted his axe in a way that could have been read as casual or as a promise. The sailor stopped. Rennick's jaw did something tight and expensive.

And I stood there in the middle of it, between the navy and the thieves, next to the thing both of them wanted, and I understood something with terrible clarity:

They were going to fight over it. Right here. In a melting cave under a collapsing icefall, with the window closing and the casing venting, two groups of grown men were going to kill each other over a box, because neither of them had asked the only question that mattered.

"What is it?" I said.

Rennick looked at me. "That's classified."

"I know it's classified. I'm asking *you* — do *you* know? Have you ever actually been told what's in it? Or do you just have orders?" I took a step toward him, away from the device, which felt like walking away from a lit fuse. "Because I've read the journals. I know what expedition carried it. I know what it was designed to do. And I don't think you know, Lieutenant, because if you knew, you wouldn't want it within a hundred miles of a city."

Something moved behind Rennick's eyes. Not doubt exactly. Doubt's cousin — the one who shows up uninvited and stands in the corner not saying anything.

"Ms. Whitlock," he said carefully, "you are in no position to lecture anyone about—"

"My father died keeping this buried." My voice cracked on *died*, which annoyed me, but there it was. "He turned back from this exact glacier with it hidden in his boat, and he spent the rest of his life poor and watched and alone because he wouldn't sell it. And the report you signed called it an accident. So yes, I'm going to stand here and lecture you, and you're going to listen, because the alternative is that we all die in here arguing about ownership of something that shouldn't belong to anybody."

The vapor off the seam thickened. It caught the lamplight and hung there, faintly iridescent, like breath in cold air that refused to disperse. Eirik edged closer to it, sniffed, and stepped back with an expression I'd learned to read on the moraine: the face he made when the mountain told him something he didn't like.

"We need to go," he said. "All of us. Now."

"Not without the unit," Rennick and Quill said, almost in unison, and then glared at each other for having agreed.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about standoffs: they're mostly waiting. The movies give you shouted ultimatums and slow zooms, but real confrontation, on ice, in the cold, is mostly people standing very still while their bodies burn calories they can't afford and their minds run the same arithmetic over and over. Who moves first. Who's fastest. Who's bluffing.

I was done with arithmetic. I'd spent five years doing it — debts against earnings, risk against reward, my father's ledger with its patient columns of numbers that never once added up to enough. Standing in that blue cave with the device hissing behind me, I found I'd run out of columns.

"Here's what happens now," I said, loudly enough for both ends of the cave. "In a few hours, at most, this tunnel closes. Either the melt refreezes or the whole mouth comes down — ask Eirik, he's climbed this ice his whole life, he'll tell you I'm right. Nobody is carrying that thing out of here. It weighs more than it looks, the route behind us is gone, and the route ahead—" I gestured vaguely upward, toward the serac field that had eaten our retreat two days ago, "—is a graveyard with delusions of stability. So the choice isn't who gets it. The choice is whether we all stand here until the glacier makes the decision for us."

"Sentimental," Quill said. "And irrelevant. My crew is roped and ready. We take it, we move, and anyone who objects can explain themselves to the crevasse."

"Your guide cut corners on the ascent," Eirik said flatly. "I watched your fixed lines. Half of them wouldn't hold a fall. You're not getting that thing down an icefall. You're not getting yourselves down an icefall."

Quill's smile thinned. Behind him, one of his climbers — the smart-looking one, the one who'd been doing the actual navigation — glanced at their anchor line and then very deliberately at nothing. He'd counted the same half.

"And you, Lieutenant?" I said. "Helicopter's not landing in the tunnel. Your team rappelled in. How exactly were you planning to extract a two-hundred-kilo guidance system up a vertical ice wall during active calving?"

Rennick didn't answer, which was its own answer. He'd come with force instead of a plan, because force had always worked for him — on my father's paperwork, on my family's reputation, on every problem he'd ever met that could be solved by being the one holding the gun. But the glacier doesn't read rank. The glacier was going to do what it was going to do, and all the insignia in the world wouldn't slow it down by one minute.

The device hissed. Louder now. Or maybe I was just listening harder.

"Whitlock." Rennick's voice dropped, went private, though nothing in that cave could possibly be private. "Last offer. Walk away. All three of you. Full debt clearance — I have the authority, I've had it since Halden. Your father's boat, free and clear. You never see me again."

It was a good offer. That's what I want to be honest about, here at the end of things, when honesty is the only currency I've got left. It was a genuinely good offer, and some tired, frozen, debt-hollowed part of me wanted it the way you want air.

But I'd read my father's journal by headlamp in a blue-ice cave, his handwriting slanting drunk with cold across pages frozen open for decades, and I knew now what he'd chosen, and why, and what it had cost him, and I knew that the debt he'd really been paying all those years wasn't the one in the ledger. It was this one. This exact moment, pre-paid, thirty years in advance, left to me like an inheritance I'd mistaken for a burden.

"No," I said.

"Casper—"

"It's *not mine to sell*." And the strange thing was how good it felt to say it. Five years of drowning and the relief wasn't in the offer I was refusing — it was in finally knowing, absolutely, what I'd refuse it *for*. "And it's not yours either. It's not anyone's. That's the entire point."

Quill snorted. "Touching. Truly. Someone hand her a violin before the ice does." He turned to his crew. "Take it."

Everything happened at once after that, the way everything does — not in sequence but in a single ugly tangle.

Quill's two nearest men lunged for the device. Rennick shouted something official and unfollowable. A sailor raised his rifle halfway — not aiming, not yet, but the gesture crossed some line that couldn't be uncrossed — and Eirik stepped into the gap between the barrel and Quill's men with his arms slightly out and his voice low and absolute, saying something in the tone guides use in avalanches, the tone that says *stop moving and everyone lives*.

And the glacier spoke.

Not a groan this time. A *crack* — enormous, intimate, directly overhead — and a chunk of the ceiling the size of a rowboat sheared off and hit the floor ten meters from the device, shattering into shards that skated across the ice and stopped against someone's boot. The whole cavity rang with it. Snow sifted down through every headlamp beam like the inside of a shaken globe.

Everyone froze. Even Quill. Especially Quill.

When the ringing faded, the vapor from the casing's seam had changed. Thicker. Faster. And underneath the hiss, faint but unmistakable, a new sound — a rhythmic clicking, like something inside counting.

Eirik looked at me across the device, across the rifles and the axes and the greed, and his face had gone very calm, which on Eirik meant the opposite of calm.

"It's accelerating," he said. "Whatever's venting — heat, pressure, I don't know — the crack let it loose and it's building. We are standing inside a kettle."

"Then we leave," Rennick said. "All of us. Now. Device stays."

"Now he sees reason," I muttered.

Quill didn't move. That was the terrible thing — even now, with the ceiling shedding rowboats and the device clicking like a bomb, Quill stood there with his hand on the casing, feeling the vibration of it through his glove, and I watched him try to do the math one more time. Value against risk. Fortune against survival. Men like Quill always believe they'll be the exception, that the mountain will make an allowance for them personally.

"Voss," he said suddenly, turning to Tomas. "You know the lower exit. The one your grandfather's generation used. Tell me there's a lower exit."

Tomas's face did something complicated. "There's a crack," he admitted. "Maybe. In warm years. It comes out below the seracs."

"Show me."

"It might not be open—"

"*Show me.*"

And that was when the second crack came, longer than the first, running away into the dark of the ceiling like lightning written in ice, and the glacier's groan rose around us and didn't stop — a long ascending note, the sound of a decision being made at a scale where none of us were a factor.

Rennick grabbed my sleeve. For one absurd second, the officer who'd ruined my family and the daughter whose family he'd ruined were just two small cold people holding onto each other in a shaking room.

"Whitlock," he said, and for the first time since the harbor, since the wreck, since the report with my father's name in it, he sounded young. "How do we get out of here?"

I looked at the device, clicking and hissing in the middle of the cave, glowing faintly in the lamplight like something alive. I looked at Eirik, already coiling rope. At Tomas, pointing toward the dark lower crack. At Quill, still gripping his fortune with both hands.

Above us, the ice groaned again — deeper, closer, final.

"Follow me," I said, "and pray the glacier gives us the minutes."

Behind us, the clicking got faster.
