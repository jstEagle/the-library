## 35. Descent

The mountain did not want us to leave. That was the first thing I understood about the descent, standing at the lip of the collapsed tunnel with my ears still ringing from the calving and my right hand bleeding through its wrappings where the compass had come apart in it. The glacier had swallowed its treasure, sealed its own throat, and now it seemed inclined to swallow us too, as a kind of tip.

"Route's gone," Eirik said. He said it the way a man reads a weather report about his own funeral.

Behind us, where the melt tunnel had opened onto the vault, there was now a wall of shattered blue ice forty meters high, steaming faintly in the cold like something freshly killed. Ahead of us, the route we'd climbed — the fixed lines, the anchors Tomas had cut at the col, the whole ladder of our ascent — was either buried under new serac debris or hanging in pieces over crevasses that hadn't existed three days ago. Somewhere below all of it, the storm was coming up the valley to meet us, a gray smear on the southern horizon moving with the unhurried confidence of a thing that has never once been late.

"We go down the east flank," Eirik said. "The old survey line. It's longer. It's steeper. It's not a route so much as a rumor of one." He looked at me, then at Tomas, then back at me. "But it's what we have."

I had a journal wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, a canvas bag of bones — I'll come back to that, because it deserves its own paragraph and possibly its own grief — half a rope, two ice axes between three people because Quill's men had left one of theirs jammed in a crevasse bridge along with considerably more than an ice axe, and no compass at all, which should have been the strangest part of the inventory but somehow wasn't. I'd spent weeks navigating by a needle that pointed at a glacier instead of north. Now the glacier and I were quits, and I found I missed the thing anyway. You can get attached to anything that tells you where you are. Even when what it's telling you is: you are here, and here is terrible.

The bones. Right. We'd carried them up from the old camp in two duffel bags, wrapped in the expedition's own frozen tarps, because Marta Voss had made me promise — her hand on my wrist, her eyes wet and furious at once — that if I found her brother I would bring him home, and I had said yes before I understood what yes would cost. A body is heavy. A body that has been ice for thirty years is heavier, because it carries the weight of every year someone waited for it. We had divided the remains among us by weight and by some arithmetic none of us spoke aloud. I carried the man whose initials had been engraved inside a brass compass case. My father had turned back from this mountain alive, and had spent the rest of his life carrying these people in a different way. It seemed only fair I carry them literally for a while.

Tomas carried Marta's brother. He hadn't said much since the col. Since he'd cut our ropes and dropped Eirik into a crevasse and watched me do the single hardest physical thing I have ever done or expect to do, which was arrest a falling man on a friction hitch while my arms tore and my soul filed formal complaints. Tomas hadn't apologized, either. Not properly. What he'd done was worse, in a way: he'd started pulling. Every rest stop, every anchor, every kilometer of white horror, Tomas was first on the rope, breaking trail, taking the worst of it, silent as a penitent. Guilt is a fuel. It burns dirty but it burns hot, and I suppose if you're going to cross a glacier in a dying season you take your propulsion where you find it.

* * *

The first day down, we lost the light at noon.

That's not poetry. The storm front came over the shoulder of the mountain like a lid closing, and the world went from gray to nothing — no horizon, no up, no distinction between snow and sky, just a milky blindness pressing on all sides. Whiteout. Eirik had taught me the word in Halden, back when it was an abstraction, a piece of vocabulary belonging to other people's misfortunes. Now it was a room, and we were locked in it, and the room was the size of everything.

"Rope length," Eirik shouted, which in a whiteout means: tie in tight, move only when I say, and if you feel the rope go slack in a wrong way, scream.

We moved by count. Twenty steps, probe, twenty steps, probe. The probing was mine mostly, because my arms were shot and probing is more about patience than strength, and also because Eirik had decided somewhere around the col that keeping my hands busy kept my mind off the fact that we were descending a glacier that was actively rearranging itself beneath us like a drunk rearranging furniture.

At some point my boot went through a snow bridge to the knee, and I felt the hollowness under me, the vast cold nothing, and I did scream, a little, and Eirik's hand closed on my pack strap and hauled me back onto solidness without a word. That's the thing about him I was learning to love, though I wasn't ready to use that word yet even in the privacy of my own skull: he didn't waste breath. Everything he had — his warmth, his anger, his fear — went into motion.

We stopped when stopping became more dangerous than continuing, which is a sentence I never expected to write, and camped in a dugout behind a serac the size of a cathedral, three bodies zipped into two bags because we'd abandoned one bag at the col to lighten load, and I lay awake listening to the glacier groan. It sounds like whales. Like enormous slow whales singing under the floor of the world. Except whales aren't deciding whether to kill you.

"You should sleep," Eirik murmured.

"I'm sleeping."

"You're narrating."

"That's how I sleep," I said. "Ask anyone. Ask my father." And then I stopped talking, because his name had gotten heavier lately, ever since the journals, ever since I'd read his handwriting describing this very icefall in 1979, young and scared and turning back, turning back, always the one who turned back, and I finally understood that turning back had been the bravest thing he ever did, and that he'd paid for it across thirty years in installments.

Eirik's hand found mine in the dark, the bandaged one, and held it with great care, the way you hold something that might shatter. "Tomorrow," he said, "we reach the moraine. After the moraine it's just walking. Ugly walking, but walking."

"And after the walking?"

"After the walking," he said, "there's Halden. There's soup. There's Marta Voss, who will feed us until we beg for mercy and then tell us we're too thin." A pause. "There's the rest of your life, Casper Whitlock. Try to arrive alive for it."

I slept.

* * *

The second day, the mountain took payment.

It happened fast, the way the glacier does everything that matters. We were traversing below the serac field — the same field we'd crossed on the way up, before the calving sealed our retreat, back when the ice still had manners — and I heard it before any of us saw it, a crack like the sky clearing its throat, and Eirik screamed a word in Norwegian that needed no translation, and we ran.

You cannot outrun an avalanche. Everyone knows this. But you can outrun the edge of one, sometimes, if the edge is lazy and you are desperate, and we were the most desperate people on the face of the earth. I remember Tomas ahead of me, actually weeping, tears freezing on his face, running with the duffel of bones strapped to his back like a man fleeing a burning house with the family portraits. I remember the sound behind us becoming a sound inside us, entering through our boots, our teeth, our ribs. I remember diving into the lee of a boulder and the world going white and loud and then, abruptly, quiet, the way a room goes quiet after a door slams.

When the powder settled, the route behind us was gone. Not damaged. Gone. Two hundred meters of slope converted into a smooth white rampart, featureless, final. Whatever had been back there — our tracks, our camps, the last physical evidence of our ascent — was compressed into the glacier's memory now, and the glacier keeps its records the way banks keep yours: indefinitely, and against your interest.

Tomas sat down in the snow and laughed. It was a broken sound, high and helpless. "It's following us," he said. "The whole mountain is following us down."

"It's not following," Eirik said, checking each of us over with brisk hands, counting fingers, looking into eyes. "It's finishing. There's a difference." But he said it gently, and I noticed his hands weren't quite steady, and I loved him a little more for the unsteadiness, if I'm honest. Fearlessness I could get from a textbook. A brave man whose hands shake and who climbs anyway — that you have to climb beside to understand.

We took inventory again, because that's what you do when the world shrinks: you count what's left. Three climbers. One and a half ropes. Two axes, one stove, fuel for perhaps four days if we were stingy, which we were experts at being. The journals — six of them, recovered from the old camp, frozen open like the books of some drowned library, including my father's, including the expedition leader's, including a slim notebook in a woman's handwriting that I hadn't let myself read yet because I suspected it was Marta's brother's and I wanted to be somewhere warm and safe when it broke my heart. And the bones. All accounted for. The mountain could keep its ice; it was not getting its dead back.

"Four days of fuel," Eirik said, "and two days of travel if nothing else comes down. So we don't stop unless the mountain votes."

"The mountain," I said, "has been voting all week, and it keeps voting no."

"Then we outlast the electorate," he said, and for the first time in two days, all three of us laughed — short, shocked laughs, the kind that escape rather than arrive — and I thought: this is what endurance is. Not gritting teeth. Finding the joke in the jaws of the thing, and laughing so the thing knows it hasn't closed yet.

* * *

The third day, the storm caught us properly, and we stopped being travelers and became survivors, and I learned what I am when everything is stripped away.

Here is what I am, apparently: cold. Extremely, comprehensively cold. We holed up in a snow cave Tomas carved — and I will say this for the boy, whatever court eventually weighs his crimes, and courts will, because Rennick will see to it: the man can build shelter like nobody I've ever seen, some inherited Sámi cunning in his hands — and we sat hip to hip in the blue dark while the wind outside reached speeds that made the fabric of the world hum. The stove burned between our knees. We melted snow. We ate half-rations and lied to each other about not being hungry.

And we talked. Because there was nothing else to do, and because the storm had stripped away not just our gear and our route and our futures but our silences, and what was underneath turned out to be words.

Tomas told us about the debts. His mother's medical bills, the loans with interest rates that should be classified as weapons, the broker named Quill who had appeared in his life like a solution and revealed himself as a second debt. "He didn't threaten me," Tomas said, staring into the stove flame. "That's the thing everyone gets wrong about men like Quill. They don't threaten. They offer. Over and over. Until offering is all you are." He looked at me. "Your father knew that. In the journal. He wrote that they offer, and the only defense is to already belong somewhere they can't buy."

"He wrote that?" I said. My voice came out strange.

"Page forty-something," Tomas said. "He wrote it about this valley. About Halden. He'd never been here. He wrote it like a prayer for a place he hoped existed."

I put my head back against the snow wall and let that settle into me. My father, thirty years ago, praying for a village he'd never seen, and me arriving in it half-dead and friendless and being fed, sheltered, defended by strangers who asked nothing except that I stay for soup. The debt collectors had taken everything else. They couldn't take that. It had never been on their ledger.

Eirik talked too, which surprised me. About his father, on the lost expedition, about growing up with a photograph and an absence, about how he'd guided this route eleven times hoping the mountain would give something back and it never had, until a salvage diver with salt-damaged hands walked into Halden carrying a compass that pointed at his father's grave. "I thought I came up here for you," he said quietly. "Somewhere on the ice I realized you came up here for me. We were each other's errand." Then he said something in Norwegian, soft, and wouldn't translate it, and I've decided I don't need it translated. Some sentences work better as music.

We slept in shifts, roped together even in the cave, because trust on a glacier is a rope and we had all learned what happens when the rope fails. Once, in the black hours, I woke to hear Tomas crying, silently, the way people cry when they've practiced, and I reached across and gripped his wrist, and he gripped back, and neither of us said anything, and it was enough. Mercy, it turns out, isn't a decision you make once at the col. It's a grip you renew every hour, in the dark, without comment.

* * *

The fourth day, the storm broke like a fever, all at once, mid-morning, and the sun came down the valley so bright and clean it hurt, and there below us — God, there below us — was the moraine, gray and rocky and gloriously solid, and beyond it the green lower slopes, and beyond those, small as a toy, the smoke of Halden-at-the-Wall rising straight up into still air.

None of us said anything for a long moment. Then Tomas sat down on a rock and put his face in his hands. Eirik stood with the wind pulling at his beard, and he was smiling, and I realized I had never once seen him smile on the mountain. It changed his whole face. It made him look like the boy he must have been before the glacier took his father.

"Two hours," he said. "Then soup."

"Then testimony," I said, because I'd had four days in a snow cave to think about it, and I knew what waited at the bottom besides soup. Six journals full of names — navy names, government names, the whole architecture of the secret my father died guarding. Rennick would be waiting too, somewhere below, with his helicopter and his rank and his offers. Let him wait. I had bones to deliver and truth to spend, and I had learned on this mountain that truth is the only currency that doesn't lose value in a storm.

We roped up one last time — habit now, affection really — and started down the moraine, three figures and two duffels of the honored dead, walking out of the ice and into whatever came next.

The journals rode against my chest, and the lowest slopes rose to meet us, and somewhere ahead a kettle was already going on Marta Voss's stove, though she didn't know it yet.

She would know soon. She'd known everything else first.
