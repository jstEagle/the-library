## 37. Bones Home

They came down off the mountain on a Thursday, which felt right, because Thursdays in Halden-at-the-Wall were the day Marta Voss cleaned her windows and cursed the gulls, and I had decided somewhere above the serac field that if I ever saw her windows again I would never take their smug streaked cleanliness for granted.

We came down half-dead, as promised. Eirik's leg was strapped and splinted and complaining loudly about the splint. Tomas hadn't spoken a full sentence since the col — he'd said *sorry* eleven times, once per rope-length, like a rosary, and then gone quiet in a way that wasn't sulking but something heavier, some interior weather system still deciding whether to break. And me, I had frost-nipped fingertips, a compass-shaped bruise on my palm where it had shattered, and nothing to show for six weeks of hell except three journals wrapped in oilcloth against my chest like a stolen infant.

Which, I suppose, is exactly what they were.

The village met us at the lower landing, where the trail spat you out between two fish sheds and the smell of the harbor hit you like an old friend who hugs too hard. Half of Halden was there. The other half was presumably holding the fort, boiling water, or standing on rooftops with spyglasses pretending not to watch.

Marta pushed through the crowd before anyone else could reach us. She was seventy-something and built like a mooring bollard, and she took one look at the bundle in my arms and knew. She didn't ask. She just put out both hands, palms up, the way you'd receive bread from an oven or a verdict from a doctor.

"Is he—"

"Whole," I said. My voice cracked on it, embarrassing, unavoidable. "Frozen whole. He looks like he's sleeping. He looks like he's about to complain about the coffee."

Something went through her face then — grief and relief wrestling in a small room — and she nodded, twice, sharp, and turned to the crowd and started issuing orders like a harbormaster in a gale. Blankets for Solheim. A sledge for the remains. Hot broth, and if Pastor Elling made another joke about resurrection she'd personally drown him in the shallows.

I stood there swaying, watching a village organize itself around death and return the way water organizes around a dropped stone, and thought: *so this is what it looks like when people belong to each other.* I'd read about it. I'd seen it from the outside, through boat windows, across harbor fences. It looked different up close. Messier. Louder. Better.

Eirik leaned on my shoulder — carefully, favoring the good side — and murmured, "You're crying."

"I'm experiencing precipitation."

"You're crying."

"Altitude sickness," I said. "It can strike at sea level. Look it up."

He laughed, which hurt him, which made me laugh, which hurt me, and for a moment the two of us stood there in the fish-shed wind, wheezing and leaking, while Halden carried its dead home.

* * *

The journals changed everything, though I didn't know how much until the days after.

I'd read them on the glacier, by headlamp, in a melt tunnel that no longer existed, with a cracked guidance system venting its last secrets into the ice behind me. I'd read them again during the descent, when we were storm-bound for thirty hours in a snow hole the size of a coffin and reading was the only thing keeping Tomas's shame and my own exhaustion from eating us alive. But reading them alone was one thing. Reading them into the record was another.

Because here's the arithmetic nobody tells you about truth: it doesn't pay debts all at once. It pays them in installments, and the first installment is having to say the words out loud to people whose faces you have to keep seeing afterward.

Pastor Elling's back room served as the hearing chamber, because Halden didn't have a courthouse, it had a room with a stove and forty chairs and a teapot that had survived three generations of crisis. The naval inquiry sent a representative — not Rennick; Rennick was already being recalled in disgrace, his career folding up around him like a chart in a wet wind, and the man they sent instead was younger, greyer around the eyes, with the careful voice of someone trained to apologize without admitting anything.

I told it all. The wreck they don't name. The cave at the lowest tide in forty years. The compass that pointed at the glacier instead of north. My father's annotations, matching bearing for bearing. Quill, and his men, and the ransacked boat. Rennick's offer — debt forgiveness for silence — and the searches, and the intruder Marta's grandson caught going through my things. The expedition. The camp. The bodies preserved in blue ice, journals frozen open, my father's name in a young man's handwriting: *turned back today. Someone has to carry this home.*

And then the vault, and the device, and what it actually was — not treasure, not salvage, not anything any government should have been allowed to dig out of a glacier and point at the world. And the choice. I told them about the choice plainly, because there was no version of it that made me look clever or heroic. I'd held a fortune in my hands and put it under ten thousand tons of ice, and shattered the only map to it, and I would do it again before breakfast if asked.

The navy man wrote for a long time. Then he looked up and said, "You understand the equipment was state property."

"I understand," I said, "that your lieutenant tried to buy my silence with my father's debts, and that those debts were incurred because your service cleared a voyage it knew was doomed." I set my father's journal on the table, open to the page where he described the order to abandon the expedition and seal the cargo, and the page after, where he wrote: *If they come asking, tell them the mountain keeps its own accounts.* "So yes. State property. And the state can invoice the glacier."

Silence. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed its one note.

Then old Henrik Sorel — who owned the chandlery and hadn't spoken a full sentence in my presence since I'd arrived in Halden — cleared his throat and said, "The girl dove our waters for two seasons and never once shorted a delivery. That's more character reference than most of us can produce."

And that was somehow the thing that broke it open. Testimony rolled in like tide after that — Eirik from his sickbed, hoarse and absolute; Tomas, standing up with his confession already written out in a hand that shook, naming every message he'd passed to Quill and every debt that had squeezed it out of him; Marta, telling the legend entire, her brother Halvard's name spoken aloud in a warm room for the first time in forty years. Even the pastor managed to be useful, producing the parish records that showed when the original expedition had last been seen and by whom.

The inquiry lasted four days. On the fifth, the grey-eyed man stood, closed his notebook, and informed the room that the court's findings would follow by post, that Lieutenant Rennick had been recalled pending investigation, and that — this part he delivered looking directly at me, and I'll grant him the decency of meaning it — "the debts referenced in testimony are voided by order of the admiralty court. Paid in full."

Paid in full. Three words I'd been diving toward for years, hauling other people's losses off the seafloor, and they arrived not with treasure but with a courtroom smelling of woodsmoke and herring.

I walked out onto the harbor wall and laughed until I cried, or cried until I laughed, and couldn't have told you the difference under oath.

* * *

They buried Halvard Voss on a bright cold morning beside the sea, in the little graveyard above the north beach where the escarpment drops away and you can hear the water working the rocks below like a patient craftsman.

The navy repatriated the remains — all of them, eventually, though Halvard came first because Marta had written letters with the force of artillery. They brought him down in a plain box, and six men of Halden carried him up from the landing, and the whole village walked behind. I walked behind too, carrying the journals, because Marta had asked that they rest on the coffin lid for the length of the procession. "He kept the words," she said. "Let the words walk with him."

Halvard Voss had been twenty-four years old when he walked onto that glacier. In the photographs Marta showed me — grainy, silver-faded — he had her same stubborn jaw and a grin that suggested he'd never once considered the possibility of losing an argument. The journals caught him mid-sentence, mid-joke, mid-complaint about the cold, and then simply stopped, because ice doesn't do epilogues.

Pastor Elling said the words over the grave, decent ones, unhurried. Then Marta stepped forward, and everyone understood that the service proper was beginning now.

She talked about her brother. Not the lost boy, not the legend — the actual nuisance. How he'd once rowed the family dory to the far islands on a dare and come back with a boatload of puffins he refused to explain. How he'd taught her to read the tide tables upside down before she could read them right way up. How the last letter he ever sent said, *This mountain is bigger than they told us, Marta, and I think we're going to be famous,* and how she'd wanted, for forty years, to write back: *You idiot. You beautiful idiot. Come home.*

She didn't cry until the very end, and even then it was brief and furious, wiped away with the heel of her hand like weather she resented. Then she took a handful of earth — real earth, dark and thawing, carted up special because the ground here holds frost like a grudge — and scattered it, and said, "There. Now you're anchored."

One by one, the village followed. Fishermen and shopkeepers and children too young to know why they were there, each taking a handful of the dark soil, each saying something — a word, a name, sometimes nothing at all. It took an hour. Nobody left early.

When my turn came, I knelt at the graveside with my ruined fingertips and my father's journals under my arm, and I found I had nothing prepared. So I said the truest thing I had.

"He waited for you," I told Halvard Voss. "All these years, somebody's been keeping your place. She cleaned her windows every Thursday so you'd know the house from the sea."

Behind me, I heard Marta make a sound — half laugh, half sob — and I got up and gave her my handful of earth and my hand, and she held both hard enough to leave marks, which I counted as a blessing.

My father's bones came home the following week. There was less ceremony, because there was less mystery — we'd known where he died, more or less, even if we'd never known why. But I buried him in the same ground, a few rows from Halvard, and I put the broken compass housing in with him. All that remained of it was the brass shell and the hinge and the little engraved initials — C.W., his, not mine, though for a strange season it had answered to both. It seemed right that it should stop pointing at anything. It had done its work. Let it rest the way he never got to.

I said at the graveside, quietly, so mostly only the gulls heard: "You kept it. Forty years, you kept it, and everyone thought you were just a bad businessman." The wind moved the grass. "Worst-kept secret in the family, Dad. You were the best one."

* * *

The berth came without ceremony, which is how the important things arrive in Halden.

I'd been living in Marta's spare room since the descent — a narrow bed, a window facing the water, a quilt that weighed roughly as much as the boat I slept beneath it dreaming of. My own boat, the *Kestrel*, my father's boat, had spent the winter tied at the public dock with a tarp over her and a slow leak in her heart, and I'd assumed that after everything — the inquiry, the debts voided, the wreckage of the season — I'd refit her slowly, alone, the way I did everything.

Instead, on a morning in late spring, I came down to the dock and found Henrik Sorel waiting for me with a clipboard and the expression of a man about to win an argument.

"Berth fourteen," he said.

"That's the deep-water berth. That's for the big boats."

"That's berth fourteen," he repeated, with the patience of a man explaining tides to a tourist, "and it's yours. Harbor council voted Tuesday. Unanimous, if you don't count Old Peder, who votes against everything on principle."

"Henrik, I can't afford—"

"It's paid through the season. A gift from the village." He handed me the clipboard. "Don't make a speech. Peder will retract his vote."

I looked at the paper. I looked at the berth — wide, sheltered, close to the chandlery, with a cleat rated for a vessel three times the *Kestrel*'s size. I looked at the small crowd of people who had absolutely failed to disperse after their errands and were studying the harbor with tremendous interest.

"Why?" I asked, and hated how young my voice sounded.

Henrik shrugged, which for him was a soliloquy. "You brought our dead home. You stood in front of the navy and told the truth when lying would've been cheaper. And your father—" he paused, and something old moved behind his eyes, "—your father ran supplies up the valley road the winter of the great snows, when the pass was closed and half the village was eating stockfish and pride. He never let us pay him. Said he was saving up."

"Saving up for what?"

"For a berth, maybe. For a place." He tapped the clipboard. "Consider the account settled."

I signed. My hand shook. Henrik pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness, and walked away whistling, and the crowd immediately abandoned its pretense and descended on me with advice about caulking, engine oil, and the correct way to insult a marine surveyor.

That afternoon, Eirik showed up on crutches with a paint tin and no explanation, and began painting the *Kestrel*'s name fresh along her bow. His leg was still mending; the doctors said he'd climb again by autumn, maybe sooner, though he'd have a limp in cold weather forever, a souvenir of the col. He painted badly and cheerfully, getting white all over everything including himself.

"You know," he said, not looking up, "my father's name is on the memorial stone now too. Next to Halvard's. The whole expedition." He blew a stray hair off his face, leaving a white streak. "Forty years I thought he'd abandoned us. Walked off into the mountains chasing something worth more than his family. Turns out he was guarding something worth more than anybody. Same thing, I suppose, from the outside."

"Yeah," I said. "From the outside."

"My mother cried when she read the journal. Then she laughed. Then she made me read the part about the pemmican complaint out loud three times." He finally looked at me, and his expression was the one I'd first seen across a harbor fence in Halden, appraising a stranger — except now the appraisal was finished, and the verdict was in. "Your father turned back. Mine didn't. Between the two of them, they kept it buried. Strange arithmetic."

"The strangest." I picked up a brush and joined him on the second coat, our strokes overlapping, sloppy and doubled. "Hey, Eirik."

"Mm?"

"Thank you. For the guide work. For the rope. For not leaving me in the whiteout, even though I navigated better than you."

He snorted. "Even though. You're insufferable. You'll fit in fine here."

* * *

By midsummer the *Kestrel* floated like she meant it — new caulking, new paint, the engine rebuilt by Tomas, who had taken to mechanical work the way penitents take to prayer, silently and with total commitment. His debts were being handled; the court had been harder on Quill's estate than on a frightened grandson, and Marta had declared, in front of witnesses, that whatever Tomas owed the world, he'd paid a portion of it on the glacier, and the rest he could work off hauling her firewood until further notice. He seemed relieved to have terms.

On the evening the boat was finished, the whole village came down to the dock. There was food — an amount of food that suggested coordinated conspiracy — and somebody produced a fiddle, and Pastor Elling made exactly one joke about resurrection before remembering Marta's threat and retiring to safer material.

I stood at the rail of my father's boat, in my father's berth, in a village that had decided I belonged to it the way it decides everything — by vote, by unanimous count, by showing up with food — and I watched the light go long and gold across the water toward the escarpment. High above, the glacier sat white and silent in the evening sun, keeping what it keeps, keeping what we'd given it. From here you couldn't see the scar. From here it was just the mountain, beautiful and permanent, holding its accounts.

Marta appeared at my elbow with two cups of something strong and pressed one into my hand.

"To Halvard," she said.

"To Halvard. To Dad." We drank. Below us, the tide was turning, the water sliding out soft and silver, and I thought about the lowest tide in forty years, and the cave, and the brass needle swinging toward the ice, and how every road I'd walked since had started there — at the moment the sea pulled back far enough to show me what it had been hiding.

"You'll dive tomorrow?" Marta asked. "The season's good. Henrik says the crab are thick past the point."

"Tomorrow," I agreed. "First run of the new hull."

She patted the rail once, like blessing a grandchild, and went off to argue with the fiddler about tempo, and I stayed at the rail a while longer, watching the water.

That was when Eirik climbed aboard — no crutches anymore, just the faint cold-weather hitch in his stride — and leaned next to me, and followed my gaze up to the glacier, gleaming pink now in the last light.

"So," he said. "The *Kestrel*'s fit. The debts are dead. The navy's recalled its ghosts." He turned to me, and there was a chart folded in his hand — I recognized the fold-pattern, the soft worn creases of a map consulted many times. "What will you chart next?"

I took the chart. Opened it. And felt my breath catch, because there, in fresh pencil in Eirik's careful hand, was a coastline I'd never surveyed — the far islands, the deep water beyond the point, waters my father had marked with a single annotation I'd never understood until this exact moment.

Waters marked, in his writing: *ask the mountain.*
