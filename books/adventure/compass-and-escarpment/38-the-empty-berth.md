## 38. The Empty Berth

Spring came to Halden-at-the-Wall the way forgiveness comes to a stubborn man — slowly, grudgingly, and with a great deal of meltwater.

I noticed it first in the harbor ice, which had spent four months pretending to be land and finally admitted it wasn't. One morning in late April I looked out Marta's kitchen window and the bay was breathing again, dark seams opening between white plates, and by noon the whole surface was groaning like an old floor under someone's careful weight. By the following week there were boats in the water that hadn't been in the water since autumn, and the smell of tar and diesel hung over the quay like a promise nobody wanted to say out loud for fear of jinxing it.

My father's boat sat at the end of the pier in berth fourteen, where the village council had put her without being asked, which is how this village does most things worth doing. The *Kestrel*. Forty-one feet of stubborn Norwegian-built steel that my father had bought secondhand when I was nine and had never once fully repainted, so that she wore her history in patches — green over rust over primer over whatever color she'd been born. She'd taken on water over the winter, as boats do when nobody loves them enough, and her decks were gray with gull leavings and one long winter's neglect.

She was also, as far as I could tell, entirely sound beneath the mess. Which felt like a metaphor, and I resolved not to think about that too hard, because metaphors have a way of becoming obligations.

"You're going to do it, then." Marta Voss stood on the quay with two mugs of coffee and the expression of a woman who had already decided the answer and was merely allowing me the dignity of agreement. She handed me a mug. It was too hot to hold properly, so I held it anyway, because refusing coffee from Marta Voss is not among the available options in Halden.

"I'm going to look at her," I said. "Looking is not doing."

"Your father's boat, your father's daughter." She sipped. "You've been walking down to stare at that berth every morning for three weeks. The whole village has a betting pool."

"On whether I'll refit her?"

"On how long you'll pretend you haven't decided. Eirik has money on Thursday."

"It's Thursday."

"I know," said Marta, and drank her coffee with the serenity of a woman who had bet on Wednesday.

* * *

The inside of the *Kestrel* smelled like my childhood, which is to say diesel, brine, and the particular ghost-smell of pipe tobacco that no amount of scrubbing ever kills. My father had smoked a pipe for exactly six years — the years he owned this boat — and then quit cold when the doctor frowned at him, but the smell had soaked into the wood of the wheelhouse and stayed, patient as everything else about him.

I found his logbooks in the chart drawer, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, because my father wrapped everything in oilcloth against damp. He'd wrapped me in oilcloth once, figuratively speaking, for twenty-six years, and look how well that turned out: I'd gone diving for the navy into a wreck they didn't name, pulled a compass out of a cave that pointed at a glacier instead of north, climbed said glacier, buried a weapon-grade guidance system under several hundred thousand tons of calving ice, shattered the compass in my own hand, and come down the mountain half-dead carrying journals and bones. All of which, I now understood, had been arranged for me in advance by a dead man with a pipe and a talent for oilcloth.

Thanks, Dad.

The logbooks were mostly ordinary — depths, tides, engine hours, the small liturgy of a working salvage diver's life. But tucked into the 1987 volume, between a page recording a routine recovery off Skarvskerry and a page recording nothing at all, was a folded chart of the escarpment coast annotated in his cramped hand. Depths I'd never dived. A dotted line running from the sea cave beneath the headland up and inland, past the tree line, onto the ice itself. And beside the cave, in letters pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*If anything happens to me, C. — it stays down. Promise me.*

He'd written it before I was old enough to read it. He'd written it knowing I'd find it someday, because men like my father don't leave messages; they leave traps made of love, sprung decades later. I sat in the wheelhouse with the chart on my knees while the harbor ice groaned outside, and I did not cry, or if I did, the gulls were discreet about it.

Then I went to find Halvard Nilsen, who runs the boatyard, and told him I wanted the *Kestrel* seaworthy by June, and watched him smile the way a man smiles when a bet pays out.

* * *

Refitting a boat, it turns out, is grief work disguised as labor. Nobody tells you this. There should be a pamphlet.

Every task came with its attendant ghost. Scrapbing the deck, I found the place where he'd dropped a wrench and sworn with such creativity that I'd memorized the phrase at age eleven and deployed it, to catastrophic effect, at school. Replacing the zinc anodes, I remembered hanging off the swim ladder at twelve while he talked me through what they were for — "sacrificial metal, Casper, it corrodes so the propeller doesn't" — which is either marine engineering or parenting, depending on the day. Rewiring the wheelhouse, I found his spare reading glasses wedged behind the switch panel, exactly where he'd been looking for them the last summer of his life, and I put them on the shelf above the helm where he'd see them first thing, in whatever way dead fathers see things, and I did not take them back down.

Eirik Solheim showed up on the third day, ostensibly to help, actually to supervise, and brought with him a thermos, a bag of tools that were better than mine, and Tomas, who was ostensibly helping Eirik and actually avoiding his grandmother's kitchen, where the accounting of his winter betrayal was still, apparently, ongoing.

Tomas had come back from the glacier thinner and quieter and with a limp that the doctor said would fade by summer. What didn't fade was the way he looked at ropes now — checking knots twice, three times, running his thumb along every carabiner gate like a man counting rosary beads. On the mountain we'd roped him in when we had every right to cut him loose, and he knew it, and the knowledge had settled into him the way sediment settles: slowly, permanently, at the bottom of everything. He didn't talk about Quill's crew, lost in the white when the glacier decided. He didn't talk about the col, or the cut ropes, or the debts that had made him Quill's man in the first place. He just showed up every morning and worked until his hands were raw, and if you asked him why, he'd shrug and say the boat needed doing.

Which was true. But it wasn't the reason, and none of us pretended otherwise, because in Halden you don't strip a man's penance off him before he's finished wearing it.

"Port side seacock's shot," Eirik announced from the bilge, his voice echoing up through the deck plates. "Everything else is honest work."

"Honest work," I repeated. "Says the man who charges by the hour to walk uphill."

"Uphill is honest. Downhill is where people die." His head appeared through the hatch, hair full of sawdust, eyes crinkled. "Your father kept her well. Better than the outside suggests."

"He kept everything well. That was the problem. He kept secrets the same way he kept engines — serviced, sealed, and never discussed."

Eirik considered this. "Some machines run better closed," he said. "You just have to know when to open them."

"And when's that?"

"When they start making noise you can't ignore." He disappeared back into the bilge. From below came the clank of a wrench, and then, more quietly, almost to himself: "Or when someone you trust asks nicely."

* * *

Marta's grandson caught me at dusk that week, sitting on the *Kestrel*'s foredeck with my legs dangling over the anchor well, watching the last light go copper on the escarpment. The headland that hid the cave. The glacier above it, still white, still holding what we'd left there — the collapsed cavity, the buried device, the bones of the expedition that had carried it up and the bones of the men Quill's greed had added to the mountain's ledger. Somewhere under all that ice was the thing my father had spent thirty years guarding and I had spent one bad winter burying deeper.

I visited it the way you visit a grave. Not often. But sometimes necessary.

"You're staying, then." Tomas dropped onto the deck beside me with the uninvited confidence of the young, which I suppose I'd earned by not letting him fall into a crevasse, even after he'd earned the opposite.

"Nobody's said anything about staying."

"The council gave you berth fourteen. Permanent moorage. Halvard's ordered lumber. Marta's telling everyone you're taking over your father's salvage runs come herring season." He shrugged. "In this village, other people decide you're staying about six months before you do."

"That's not ominous at all."

"It's how belonging works here. You don't apply. You get assumed." He picked at a flake of old paint. "It took me nineteen years to figure that out. You did it in one winter. Show-off."

I laughed, and the laugh surprised me, because I hadn't done much of it since the mountain. We sat in the quiet while the tide came in, filling the harbor with that soft underwater whisper of water finding its old channels, and I thought about what Tomas had said, and about how for ten years — since the phone call, since the report, since Lieutenant Rennick's tidy signature clearing my father's fatal voyage — I had lived like a diver between wrecks: moving, salvaging, surfacing nowhere. Debt will do that. Grief will do that. Rennick will do that, if you let him.

Rennick was gone now. Recalled in disgrace, the journals having done their slow work in rooms I'd never see, his name attached forever to a cover-up and a drowned crew and a young diver named Whitlock who'd turned back and lived thirty years with it. I'd testified in a room smelling of floor wax, laid the journals on the table, and answered questions for eleven hours, and when it was done a court had voided my father's debts — paid in truth, the lawyer said, and meant it kindly — and I had walked out into spring with nothing owed to anyone for the first time since I was sixteen.

Nothing owed. I was still getting used to the weight of it, which is to say the absence of weight, which takes practice, like swimming in fresh water after a lifetime of salt.

"Tomas," I said. "When the herring come in — want a job? Deckhand. Terrible pay, worse hours, occasional mortal danger."

He grinned, and for a second he looked fifteen instead of ninety. "Occasional?"

"I can't promise frequent. The good wrecks are all classified."

* * *

June arrived the way it does at that latitude — all at once, overnight, as if someone had flipped a switch marked SUMMER and stepped back before the sparks. The light stopped leaving. At midnight the sky held a pale gold residue along the northern horizon, and the glacier above the village glowed faintly pink at two in the morning, and nobody slept properly, and everybody pretended this was normal.

We launched the *Kestrel* on a flat calm Sunday with half the village on the quay, because in Halden a launching is a holiday and any excuse for Marta's cinnamon buns is seized upon with naval discipline. Halvard had done beautiful work — new paint, proper green, the hull fair and black below the waterline — and when she slid off the slings and took the harbor water, she rode high and level and right, the way a boat rides when she's been loved back into shape.

I stood at the helm, my hands on my father's wheel, and steered her out past the breakwater into open water for the first time in six years, and the engine note was clean, and the wake unfurled behind us straight as a ruled line, and I thought: *he'd have hated the paint.* He'd have said green shows every streak. And then he'd have run his hand along the rail the way I was running mine, and said nothing else, because some approvals are silent.

We ran her down the coast, Eirik and Tomas and I, past Skarvskerry, past the seal rocks, around the shoulder of the escarpment itself. I slowed her there — not stopping, because some places you don't stop, you just acknowledge — and looked up at the headland where the lowest tide in forty years had opened a door in the rock, and at the white mass above it where the door had led. The cave mouth was underwater again, sealed, ordinary, a legend returned to legend. The glacier gleamed. Somewhere in all that blue depth, a compass lay in pieces in a collapsed vault, its needle finally pointing nowhere, its work finished.

"Depth here's forty fathoms," Tomas said, reading the sounder. "Bottom's clean sand. Good holding."

"You've been studying."

"Marta makes me. She says if I'm going to be a deckhand I should know what the numbers mean." He paused. "She says a lot of things."

"She does," Eirik agreed solemnly. "Most of them correct. It's infuriating."

I brought the *Kestrel* around and headed for home, and the wind came up just enough to raise a small sea, and the boat took it the way she'd always taken it — patiently, without complaint, a little old-fashioned in her motion, utterly sure of herself. My father's boat. My father's waters. My father's long, silent, sacrificial vigil, which I had inherited without knowing and discharged without meaning to, and which was, at last, complete.

* * *

That evening, tied up in berth fourteen with the deck washed and the tools stowed, I sat in the wheelhouse with a blank chart spread on the table and a pencil in my hand, because a diver who owns a boat and owes nothing to anyone eventually has to answer a question she's been avoiding since the ice: *now what?*

The herring would come in July. The wreck work — honest wreck work, permits and insurance surveys and the occasional lost outboard motor — would keep the accounts level. There were scallop beds to the south that Halvard claimed nobody had touched since the nineties. There was the coast itself, fifty miles of it, shoals and skerries and unnamed depths that my father had charted in fragments across thirty years and never finished, because he'd been busy guarding something, and the unfinished charts had sat in his drawer like an apology.

I could finish them.

Eirik found me there at dusk, leaning in the wheelhouse doorway with the particular patience of a man who has climbed enough mountains to know that the last hundred meters are the ones you don't rush.

"So," he said. "What will you chart next?"

I looked down at the blank water where the pencil hovered. South, toward the scallop grounds and the warm currents. North, toward the deep channels where the old charts gave up. Or out — out past the escarpment, into the open sea where the navy's classified dots used to sit and now, thanks to eleven hours of testimony, sat labeled and public and harmless.

"All of it," I said. "That's the trouble. I keep looking at the chart and seeing everything at once. Fifty miles of coast nobody's mapped properly since my father died trying to map it. Shoals that aren't on any chart. Wrecks that are only legends until somebody goes down and looks." I tapped the pencil against the table. "He left half a life's work in this drawer, Eirik. Somebody should finish it. Might as well be the person who inherited the drawer."

"Sounds like a lot of dives."

"Sounds like a lot of summers."

"And winters?" He came into the wheelhouse and leaned on the chart table beside me, close enough that I could smell rope and spruce and the cold-clean smell that clings to mountain men no matter the season. "Boats need harbors. Divers need villages. You've got a berth now. A permanent one."

"I noticed," I said. "I'm told the decision was made for me six months ago."

"Eight," said Eirik. "I had money on it."

"Of course you did." I looked at him — really looked, the way I'd learned to look at ice, reading what's underneath — and he looked back, unhurried, certain, waiting the way the mountain waits: not going anywhere, not asking for anything, simply present and available to anyone willing to make the climb. "There's a shoal south of Skarvskerry," I said. "Dad marked it 'unverified, dangerous, check.' Thirty years unchecked. First dive of the season. Tomorrow, weather permitting."

"Weather permitting," Eirik agreed. "And after the shoal?"

"After the shoal, the rest of the chart. After the chart—" I looked out the wheelhouse window at the harbor, at the lights coming on along the quay, at Marta's kitchen window glowing gold, at the escarpment standing black and permanent against the last of the light with its white burden gleaming above it. "After the chart, I expect there'll be another chart. That's generally how charts work."

He nodded slowly, and something in his face suggested the conversation had contained, somewhere beneath the cartography, an entirely different question and answer, both of which we'd handle in due time, at altitude, without rushing.

"Tomorrow, then," he said, and squeezed my shoulder once on his way out, and went down the gangway whistling.

I sat alone in the wheelhouse as the light faded to that pale gold that doesn't quite become night, and I picked up the pencil, and over the shoal south of Skarvskerry I wrote, in my father's cramped hand, which I seem to have inherited along with everything else:

*Unverified. Dangerous. Check.*

And underneath it, smaller, in my own:

*Checking.*

Outside, the tide turned, and the *Kestrel* shifted gently against her lines in berth fourteen — her empty berth, filled now — and the mountain kept its silence, and the water kept its secrets, and for the first time in longer than I could measure, I slept without dreaming of drowning.
