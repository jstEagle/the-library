## 1. Fuel, Weather, Gossip

The river woke before Coral did, the way it always did in flood season—groaning against its banks, hauling whole trees downstream like a man dragging furniture he'd lost a bet over. He lay in his cot under the tin roof of the hangar office and listened to it, the way another man might listen to a wife breathing beside him. Twenty years of mornings, and the river had never once said anything new.

He got up anyway.

The hangar smelled of kerosene and cut grass and the particular sourness of old canvas that had been wet more times than it had been dry. The *Kestrel* sat where he'd left her the night before, nose into the wind, a 1931 bush plane held together by wire, prayer, and Bo's increasingly personal feud with her engine. Coral walked her length with his coffee mug in one hand, running his thumb along the leading edge of the port wing the way a man checks a horse's leg. There was a new chip in the fabric near the strut. He'd find out who'd done it later, or he wouldn't. The plane forgave everything eventually. That was more than most things did.

"You're up early," said a voice from under the wing.

"I'm up on time. You're up late."

Bo slid out on the creeper, grease to the elbows, a smear of it across one cheekbone like war paint applied by someone who didn't care about the outcome of the war. He was sixty if he was a day, built like something left out in weather, and he looked at the engine housing the way other men looked at difficult sons.

"Number three cylinder's drinking oil again," Bo said. "She'll make your run. She won't thank you for it."

"She never thanks me for anything."

"That's because you talk to her like she's cargo." Bo hauled himself upright, knees popping like green wood in a fire. "Planes know."

Coral let that stand, because arguing with Bo about whether machines had feelings was a longer conversation than either of them had fuel for. He drained his coffee, checked the sky—a high gray ceiling, no thunderheads yet but the kind of light in the east that promised them by afternoon—and went inside to wake Wren.

He knocked on her door, which was a formality. The door had been a formality since she was six and had declared, with the flat authority children reserve for their own sovereignty, that knocking was "a thing you do so I can pretend I have privacy." She was fourteen now. The pretense had hardened into law.

"Wren. Medicine run. Up."

"I'm up."

"You're not."

"I'm *vertically adjacent*."

Coral leaned his forehead against the doorframe and permitted himself, briefly, the smile he would not permit her to see. Two minutes later she emerged: boots unlaced, hair like a crow's nest after a storm, a paperback jammed in her back pocket and a look on her face that said whatever he was about to ask, the answer was no.

"Cargo's at the mission dock," he said. "Quinine, penicillin, iron tablets, and a crate of something Ferro at Two Rivers says is 'medical supplies' but is probably cigarettes. You're on the manifest."

"I'm always on the manifest."

"Because you're good at it."

That stopped her mid-sulk, the way it always did. Praise from Coral Underhill arrived roughly as often as rain in the dry season, and when it came, it fell hard enough to leave a mark. She laced her boots without being told twice.

* * *

The strips along this stretch of the river were less airstrips than arguments—flat places where the ground had been persuaded, temporarily, to cooperate. Coral flew them the way an old woman walks a path she's worn herself: without looking much, feeling every rut through the stick before the wheels found it.

The first stop was Sainte-Anne-de-l'Eau, which everyone called Sanny-Loo because nobody had spoken French there in forty years. The strip ran between the church and the cane fields, and by the time Coral killed the engine, half the town's population had materialized at the edge of the grass with the unhurried inevitability of water finding low ground.

This was the true economy of the river country. Not medicine, not freight, not the government's precious survey contracts. Gossip. It moved upriver faster than the current and downriver faster than any plane, and it obeyed laws stricter than gravity: nothing traveled far without changing weight, and everything eventually came back around wearing different clothes.

"Mornin', Captain," said Madame Ossé, who ran the mission dispensary and weighed every word she spoke as though words were produce and she was charging by the pound. "You hear about the Delacroix boat?"

"What about it?"

"Sank."

"It sank last March."

"Sank *again*. In the same place. Second time." She let that sit, watching him. "Same place, Captain. River don't sink the same boat twice in the same hole unless the hole wants something."

"The hole wants a better boat," Coral said, signing her manifest. "Anything for me?"

"News travels slower than sickness up past the bend. Old Tomas's boy took fever. And—" she lowered her voice, which meant the information was either valuable or worthless, "—there's a surveyor come through on the mail boat Tuesday. Government man. Asking questions about salvage rights."

Coral kept his eyes on the paperwork. "Salvage rights for what?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She smiled with all the warmth of a fish hook. "Nobody knew what to tell him."

Wren was already hauling crates with the two dock boys, and Coral watched her out of the corner of his eye while he fueled from the drum—watched her haggle with the boys over who carried what, watched her pocket a mango one of them offered and pretend she hadn't, watched her glance back at him exactly once, quickly, the way she still checked that he was there. She thought he didn't notice. He noticed everything. It was the only skill twenty years of flying had perfected in him: seeing the whole river while pretending to look at none of it.

They made three more stops before noon. At Two Rivers, Ferro the cook pressed a wrapped parcel into Wren's hands and winked at Coral over her head, and Coral pretended not to see that either. At Caño Seco, the strip had gone soft at the north end and Coral landed long and walked the ground himself before letting anyone unload, prodding the earth with a stick like a doctor palpating a belly. At the leper station above the falls—he was one of three pilots in the country who'd still land there—the sisters gave him bread and news in equal measure, and the news was thinner than the bread.

By the time they turned for home, the afternoon light had gone the color of brass, and the thunderheads Bo had predicted were stacking up over the eastern hills like furniture being moved upstairs by giants.

"Storm's coming," Wren said from the right seat. She'd been quiet an hour, which for Wren was a geological event.

"Mm."

"You've been quiet too."

"Flying."

"You fly quieter than anyone I ever met. It's like sitting next to a locked room." She picked at the fabric of the seat. "Madame Ossé told the dock boys about the surveyor. Asking about salvage rights."

"Madame Ossé tells everybody everything. That's how she keeps from telling anybody anything important."

"What's salvage rights?"

"When something sinks, who gets to say what happens to it."

"And who does?"

"Depends who's asking." Coral eased the stick forward as they dropped toward the river valley, threading under the cloud base. "Government says it's the government. River people say it's whoever pulls it out. Lawyers say it's whoever pays them. Everybody's right once in a while, like a broken clock."

"Is that why you never dive the wrecks? Other pilots do. Tomas dives, Bo says. There's money down there."

"There's rust down there. Rust and river mud." He checked the fuel gauge, which was lying to him gently, the way old instruments do. "Everything the river takes, it takes for a reason."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Coral agreed. "It isn't."

She glared at him, and he felt the glare land and stay, the way her glares did. Fourteen years old and she could hold a grudge like a compass holds north. He sometimes wondered where she'd learned it, and then remembered, and stopped wondering.

* * *

They beat the storm home by ten minutes, which was either skill or luck, and Coral had stopped believing in the difference somewhere around his fortieth year—a year he had noted, filed, and refused to observe, on the grounds that a man who counts years starts measuring what's left instead of what's here.

Bo met them at the hangar with a lantern, because the light had already failed. Rain hit the tin roof like applause from a huge indifferent audience.

"Strips?" Bo asked.

"Caño Seco's going soft. Somebody should tell somebody official."

"Somebody official will write it down and file it. That's how they fix things." Bo helped him tie the Kestrel down, hands moving in the dark without needing light. "Surveyor came through on the mail boat yesterday. While you were out."

Coral's hands paused on the tie-down rope—one second, maybe less, but Bo had known him twenty years and read him the way he read engines.

"Yeah," Coral said. "I heard."

"Asking about salvage. And about you, they say. Whether you ever pull cargo out of the river."

"I haul medicine and gossip. Gossip pays better."

Bo grunted. From Bo, a grunt was a paragraph. This one said: *you're lying, and I've decided to let it pass, and we both know both those things.*

Inside, the hangar office was warm and lamplit, and Wren had already claimed the table with her book and the mango, eating it over the pages with the concentration of a surgeon. Coral put water on for rice and listened to the rain and the river and the small domestic sounds of a life he had assembled without ever admitting he'd done it on purpose.

"Cor." Wren didn't look up from her book. She only used his name like that, shortened, bare, when she wanted something and wanted him to know she knew it was a big something. "The flood's going down. Bo says lowest water in nine years. Low enough to see things."

"See what things?"

"Things the river took." She turned a page she hadn't read. "Down past the bend. Where the old outpost was. The drowned one. The maps just say *ruins, submerged*, like the river ate it and that was that."

Coral stood at the stove with his back to her and let the water come to a boil and did not turn around, because turning around would have shown her his face, and his face at that moment was twenty years old and standing on a dock in the rain.

"Maps are mostly wrong," he said finally. "That's why I make my living."

"Not that part." She closed the book. "The part where the river's dropping lower than anyone remembers. Bo heard it from the ferryman, who heard it from the dredge crew, who heard it from—" 

"—the usual parliament," Coral said. "Yes."

"So if things are down there. Things the river took." She let it hang, fourteen years old and patient as a fisherman. "Wouldn't now be the time they'd come up?"

Outside, the storm worked itself out over the hills, and the river kept talking to itself in the dark, hauling the year's wreckage toward the sea, keeping what it wanted.

Coral stirred the rice and said, "Eat your dinner," and neither of them believed that was an answer, and both of them knew the river had started asking questions he'd spent twenty years making sure it couldn't.
