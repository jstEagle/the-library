## 2. What the River Keeps

The river gave things back the way a miser gives change—slowly, grudgingly, and never quite what you were owed.

Coral Underhill had learned that in his first year on the water, back when he still counted seasons instead of pretending they didn't matter. The flood would come up brown and swollen in March, take fences, goats, a church roof once, whole stretches of bank with the nonchalance of a man brushing crumbs from his shirt. Then by August it would fall again, dropping a foot a week, and what it left behind was never what it took. A canoe for a fence. A dead cow for a church roof. Once, memorably, a piano, upright and swollen beyond saving, sitting in the mud of the lower bend like a drowned man's last joke.

So when the water started down that August, Coral did what he always did: he flew low along the channel with the window open and one eye on the exposed banks, cataloguing. Not salvage, exactly. Salvage implied intent. He called it *taking inventory of the river's debts*, and he told himself it was habit, nothing more—a pilot's way of knowing his country. But he knew every stretch of shoreline where something interesting might surface the way other men knew where their mistresses lived.

The drowned outpost was one of those places.

It sat at the confluence where the Sable met the main channel, forty minutes south of Perdition Strip if you flew it lazy, twenty if you flew it like Coral flew everything. On the charts—the current ones, the ones the Ministry printed—it was marked as *ruins, uninhabited*, which was the government's way of saying *nothing here, move along*. Two hundred years ago it had been a colonial river station: warehouses, a mission, a customs house, a bell tower. Then the empire that built it had collapsed into itself like a lung giving out, the garrison had been withdrawn, the records burned or boxed or lost, and the river had done the rest. Two floods in the 1940s ate the lower town whole. What remained now lived underwater most of the year—a ghost grid of foundations visible only in the driest weeks, stone walls furred with weed, the mission bell tower's stump breaking the surface like a rotten tooth.

Coral had been flying over it for twenty years and had landed beside it exactly four times, each time for reasons he could justify to himself: fuel emergency, weather, a sick passenger, once just to stretch his legs on ground no one else wanted. Four times in twenty years. He was proud of that arithmetic. It meant discipline.

On the third Tuesday of August, flying back empty from the medicine run to San Felix, he saw the mud around the outpost breathing.

That was the only word for it. The floodwater, retreating, left the clay soft and shining, and as it dried it cracked and shifted, and sometimes—rarely—something buried would work its way up through the shrinking earth like a seedling in reverse. He'd seen fence posts rise. He'd seen a wagon. He'd seen, one unforgettable morning, an entire rowboat standing upright on its keel in the middle of what had been open water in June, as though the river had set it down carefully and walked away.

Today it was a glint. Just that—a dull metallic flash off to the east side of the customs house foundation, where the silt ran deepest. Most men would have logged it and moved on. Coral circled twice, telling himself he was checking for stranded fishermen, because that was the kind of lie that worked best when you told it to yourself in your own voice.

Then he put the plane down on the gravel bar downstream and walked in.

* * *

The heat came off the mud in waves you could almost see, and the smell was the smell the river made every August—rot and mineral and green things dying in reverse order. His boots sucked at the clay. Mosquitoes found him inside a minute, as they always did, and he let them. Complaining about mosquitoes was like complaining about gravity; it identified you as a stranger.

The glint turned out to be a corner. A brass corner fitting, green with age, protruding from the mud at the angle of something large lying flat. Coral crouched over it for a long time without touching it, the way he'd crouch over a strange animal—respect first, curiosity second, contact last.

He dug with his hands. There was no dignity in it and he didn't look for any. The clay gave reluctantly, peeling away in wet sheets, and after twenty minutes he had outlined the shape: a chest. A real one, not a fisherman's trunk, not a supply crate. Iron-banded, brass-cornered, maybe four feet long, and heavy in the way that things are heavy when they're full of themselves rather than full of water. The lid bore a stamped plate, and Coral wiped the mud from it with his sleeve and felt the day tilt slightly under him.

A seal. An imperial seal—the double anchor and crossed keys of the old colonial administration, the one that had run this river when the maps still called it by names nobody used anymore. A nation that hadn't existed in seventy years, stamped into brass on a chest that had spent God knew how many decades under the silt of a drowned outpost.

Coral sat back on his heels in the mud and looked at it for a long time.

Here was the thing about Coral Underhill that the gossip economy of the river towns had never quite managed to price correctly: everyone agreed he knew things, but nobody could agree what. They said he'd flown contraband in the border wars. They said he'd been a government pilot who'd walked away from something bad. They said he had money buried, or a wife buried, or a name that wasn't his. All of it was wrong in its details and right in its shape, which was the nature of gossip, and Coral had spent two decades making sure the shape stayed vague. You stayed invisible in this country not by hiding but by being so consistently unremarkable that looking at you became boring. He flew cargo. He charged fair rates. He drank two beers and never three. He was polite to ministry men and short with smugglers and generous, quietly, with widows. After enough years of that, people stopped looking.

And here, gleaming up out of the mud like an accusation, was the single most interesting object to surface from the Sable in his lifetime.

He should have covered it back over. That was the disciplined move, the invisible-man move: kick some clay across the brass, mark the spot in his head, fly home, say nothing, let the next flood decide whether the world needed to see it. In twenty years he had built a life on exactly that kind of decision—the small surrenders, the things left unsaid and undug.

Instead he heard himself say, out loud, to nobody: "Well."

The word hung in the hot air. Somewhere across the flats a heron complained and went silent.

Coral looked at the seal a while longer. Then he stood, walked back to the plane, and returned with the tow strap, a spade from the survival kit, and the particular expression of a man who has already lost an argument with himself and is simply finishing the paperwork.

* * *

It took him two hours. The chest weighed more than it should have—waterlogged wood, iron, and whatever sat inside it dense as ore—and the mud fought him the whole way, sucking at his knees, filling every hole he dug faster than he could dig it. Twice he stopped and considered quitting, and both times his eyes went to the brass plate, and both times he kept digging. By the end his hands were shaking and his shirt was plastered to his back and he had invented and discarded six different cover stories for anyone who might ask, which was its own kind of confession, because honest men don't rehearse.

He rigged the strap to the chest, dragged it to the gravel bar on a sled of split bamboo he cut from the stand upstream, and winched it into the cargo bay with the hoist he kept for engine blocks. The plane sat low on its gear when he loaded it. He didn't care. Nobody flew this stretch in August except fools and him.

He was airborne by late afternoon, the outpost falling away behind him in the long gold light, its bell tower stump casting a shadow across the mud like the hand of a clock pointing at nothing. In the mirror he could see the scar he'd left in the clay—a raw wound of a hole, already slumping closed at the edges. By October the river would take it back. By November there would be no evidence he'd ever been there, except the thing itself, riding in his hold.

He caught himself, somewhere over the middle channel, composing the sale pitch.

That was the plan. That had been the plan since the moment he'd touched the brass, if he was honest, and he was trying very hard not to be honest about anything today. Antique dealers existed in the capital—two of them worth dealing with, three if you counted the one who'd cheat you politely instead of criminally. A sealed imperial chest, provenance the drowned outpost, recovered from documented flood silt: that was a story collectors paid for. Enough to re-cover the Cessna's cylinders. Enough to put a year of Wren's school fees in a jar and stop doing the arithmetic he pretended he wasn't doing every month. Enough, maybe, to buy the kind of distance that made invisibility permanent.

Sell it unopened. That was the rule he set himself somewhere over the Sable Bend, speaking it silently like a vow: *unopened*. Because opened things created questions, and questions created answers, and answers were the one cargo Coral Underhill had spent twenty years refusing to carry.

The chest rode in the hold behind him, silent, patient, smelling of river mud and old iron. He didn't look back at it. Looking back at it felt like letting it win.

* * *

Perdition Strip at dusk was a study in small ambitions: fuel drums, a hangar with a roof that leaked in philosophically interesting patterns, Bo's workshop glowing at the far end with the blue light of a soldering iron, and the long tin-roofed cantina where the town conducted its true business, which was talk. Coral taxied in, unloaded the medicine crates' return empties in plain view, waited until the light went the way he wanted, and then rolled the plane into the hangar and shut the doors.

Moving the chest from plane to storage loft took another hour and cost him a bruised shoulder and one nearly catastrophic encounter with the doorframe. He stowed it under canvas behind the spare propeller and the crate of Bo's unclaimed parts, in the corner of the loft where he kept the things that were technically nobody's business: a case of medical supplies he dispensed free and billed to no one, a rifle that belonged to a man who'd died owing him money, and a shoebox of photographs he had never once looked at in nineteen years.

He climbed down the ladder, pulled the cord on the loft light, and stood in the dark of the hangar listening to the tin roof tick as it cooled.

Wren was going to know. That was the thought that arrived, unwelcome as a bill collector, before he'd even reached the hangar door. Twelve years old and sharp as a fishhook, the girl missed nothing that happened on that strip—not arrivals, not departures, not the weight distribution of a plane taxiing heavier than it had taken off. She'd have heard the engine come in heavy. She'd have watched him unload through whatever gap in the world she was currently inhabiting. By tomorrow morning she'd have asked Bo, and Ferro, and the postmistress, and assembled a theory better than the truth.

He'd give her a job, he decided. Something with her hands and her back and her attention fully occupied. That usually bought him a week.

Coral locked the hangar, walked across the strip to his house—a raised timber box with a porch and a view of the channel—and lit the lamp. He ate rice and tinned fish standing up, the way he did when he wasn't hungry but knew he ought to be. Through the window the river ran dark and quiet, carrying its secrets south, giving nothing back tonight.

He should sleep. He had a charter at seven, a load of roofing tin to the mission landing, and a man named Delacroix waiting on quinine.

Instead he found himself standing in the yard in the dark, looking back at the hangar, feeling the pull of it like a current. The chest sat fifty yards away under canvas, and it was full—he was certain it was full, had felt the density of it against his shoulder all afternoon—and full meant papers, most likely, or coin, or instruments, or letters. Letters. Outposts wrote letters. Reports, requisitions, complaints, confessions. Twenty years of a man's careful silence, and fifty yards away sat a box that might contain somebody else's entire voice.

"Unopened," he said aloud, to the yard, to the river, to himself. "You sell it unopened."

The river said nothing. The river was very good at that.

He went inside, trimmed the lamp low, and lay down on his bunk with his boots off and his clothes on, which was how he slept on nights when sleep was a negotiation rather than a fact. Above the strip the stars came out in their usual indifferent thousands. Downriver, forty minutes south if you flew it lazy, the mud was closing over the hole he'd dug, and the bell tower stump stood in the dark keeping its own counsel, the way it had for seventy years.

Coral Underhill fell asleep eventually, and dreamed of nothing he remembered—which he would have told you, if anyone had asked, was the finest thing a night could offer a man like him.

He woke once, near midnight, to a sound he couldn't place, and lay listening to the wind move through the tin roofs of the town. It took him a long moment to understand that the sound was nothing—that it was only the river, running low and honest over its stones, getting quieter every night as the dry season came on, dropping toward the beds it hadn't shown in years.

Dropping, he thought, toward things.

He slept no better after that.
