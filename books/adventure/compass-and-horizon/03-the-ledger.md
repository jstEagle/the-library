## 3. The Ledger

The chest sat on his workbench for three days before he touched it.

Coral told himself it was prudence. A man didn't open a sealed strongbox fished out of a drowned colonial outpost without thinking about what might be inside—brass fittings gone green with a century of river, sure, but also the possibility of things that had waited too long to stay quiet. He'd seen men open graves with less ceremony and pay for it. He told himself he was being careful.

He was being afraid, and he knew the difference, even if he wouldn't have said the word aloud.

So for three days the chest sat where he'd put it, under a tarp behind the hangar, while he flew his runs and drank his coffee and let Wren chatter at him about the schoolteacher's new husband and whether the mail plane would bring her the books she'd ordered from the coast. Three days of ordinary life, laid over the thing like a tarpaulin over a body. And on the third night, after Wren had gone to bed in the room off the office and the town's last generator had coughed itself silent and the only light left on the strip was the moon doing its indifferent work, Coral came out to the hangar with a crowbar, a lamp, and no good reason at all.

* * *

The lock was the first surprise. He'd expected rust, expected to shear it off without effort, but the mechanism turned under the keyhole's persuasion—a flat blade from his toolkit, worked slow—and gave with a click so clean it sounded recent. The interior of the lock had been packed with grease. Somebody had maintained this box. Somebody had intended it to open again someday, or never to be opened by anyone who hadn't earned the grease.

The lid came up on hinges that whispered instead of screamed.

Inside, under a layer of river silt fine as flour, lay paper. That was all. No coin, no plate, no jewels—the kind of cargo that would have made this easy. Paper, bundled in oilcloth tied with tarred cord, and beneath the bundles, wedged into the corners like ballast, a set of brass weights stamped with the same seal as the chest: a crowned bird over crossed anchors. The arms of a nation that had stopped existing before Coral was born, its flag lowered somewhere in a rain-soaked ceremony nobody had bothered to photograph.

He lifted the first bundle out and set it on the bench. The oilcloth crackled. His hands were steady—he'd landed in crosswinds that would have folded lesser men's hands into fists—but there was a feeling in his chest like the moment before an engine catches, that held-breath interval between turning the key and hearing it turn over.

He cut the cord with his pocketknife.

Shipping ledgers. Six of them, bound in board covers gone soft as felt, their spines numbered in a clerk's precise hand. He opened the earliest one and the smell rose up out of it—mold and iron-gall ink and something older underneath, something like a church. The pages were ruled in columns: dates, vessel names, tonnage, manifests. *Schooner Meridian, 14 tons, rice, kerosene, quinine, 40 bales.* The handwriting changed every few pages as clerks came and went, but the columns stayed true, decade after decade, an outpost keeping its accounts against the jungle the way a man keeps his ledger against his conscience.

Coral turned pages. He wasn't reading, not exactly. He was listening—for anything that sounded like him.

Because here was the thing he had never said to anyone, the thing he kept below his own waterline: he had been born in a place like this. Not this outpost, maybe. He couldn't swear to that anymore; memory in a child is a river that keeps moving its banks. But he had been born somewhere upriver of anywhere, in a settlement with a church bell and a ration store and a clerk with neat handwriting, and he had been carried out of it at an age too young to carry the name they gave him, and the name they gave him instead was Underhill, and the man who gave it had said, *that's all you get, boy, a hill to hide under,* and had laughed like it was a joke.

So he turned the pages and listened.

Ration tallies next. These were thinner volumes, and crueler in their way, because rations tell you who was actually there. Rice by the sack, kerosene by the drum, quinine by the hundred tablets—and names. The tallies were signed out against family heads, and the clerk had recorded them all: Okafor. Lindqvist. Marchetti. Duarte. Ferreira. Names from everywhere, the whole scattered congregation of empire washed up on this one bend of brown water, planting rubber and cassava and children. He read the names the way you read a passenger manifest for a flight you weren't on, feeling the strange grief of strangers.

Then he reached a page where the entries stopped mid-month.

Not tapered. Stopped. One week the tallies ran full and regular—rice, salt, lamp oil, a birth registered in the margin in the clerk's hand, *M. Okonkwo, delivered safely, mother well*—and then nothing. The next dated entry was three months later, in different ink, different hand, terser: *Store closed. Inventory transferred to district office.* And below that, in yet another hand, official and bloodless as a form: *Site decommissioned per directive. Personnel reassigned.*

No departure lists. No destination. Forty-odd families' worth of signatures, and then a closed door drawn in two lines of bureaucratic penmanship.

Coral sat back on his stool and looked at the lamp flame for a while.

He had grown up around men who talked about the old postings the way sailors talk about reefs—there but not discussed, marked on no chart anyone would sell you. He'd assumed it was the usual shame of empire, the ordinary forgetting. But this wasn't forgetting. Forgetting leaves smudges. This was erasure, and erasure takes work, and work takes orders, and orders come from someone's desk.

*They didn't evacuate,* he thought, and the thought arrived with the certainty of a compass needle. *Evacuations have manifests.*

* * *

There were letters. Two packets, one tied with ribbon gone brittle as insect wing, one loose. He saved the loose ones for last, which was not discipline so much as dread wearing discipline's clothes.

The ribboned packet was official correspondence: the outpost superintendent writing to the district commissioner, year after year, in a hand that started confident and grew cramped. Requests for medical supplies. Complaints about shipping schedules. Then, in the later years, a change of subject that Coral read twice because he could not at first believe what he was reading.

*...the survey party under Mr. E. Vale has completed triangulation of the lower reaches and will proceed upstream as far as the falls. Mr. Vale continues to record matters beyond his commission, and I must again request instruction as to how I am to answer his questions regarding the disposition of the settlement's population...*

Coral read the name again. Vale.

It meant nothing. It was a common enough name; the river country had Vales the way it had Silvas and Santoses. He told himself that with the flat voice he used on passengers who panicked at turbulence, the voice that said *nothing is wrong* whether or not it was true.

He untied the loose letters.

These were private—not the superintendent's official voice but a man's actual hand, hurried, written on the backs of requisition forms when proper stationery ran out. Letters to a brother downcoast, it seemed, though the address line had been torn away. The first few were small talk: weather, prices, a mule that had died stupidly. Then the tone shifted the way weather shifts, and Coral found himself leaning into the lamplight, reading a dead man's fear in fading ink.

*...they have come for the ledgers, Tomás, and I have copied what I can. You will think me mad but I tell you the boats that took them away carried no families. I stood on the bank and watched them load and there were no women wailing and no children asking questions and a boat full of people who go quietly is a boat full of people who were made quiet. The soldiers said reassignment. Reassignment to where? There is nowhere. I have flown the whole of this country in my mind and there is nowhere they could have been taken that anyone would not have seen them arrive...*

Coral's mouth had gone dry. He swallowed and read on.

*...I have hidden the originals where the water guards them better than any lock, and if I do not come back you will know why, and you will know that I tried to make a map of the truth even when they burned the surveys. A man can drown a bell but the ringing stays in the air a while...*

The rest was fragmentary—an inventory of what he'd copied, a list of family names he begged his brother to remember, instructions wrapped in the kind of code that only means something once it's too late. Coral turned the last sheet over. Blank. The letter simply ended, mid-thought, the way lives do.

He set it down on the bench very carefully, as if it were sleeping.

* * *

The lamp had burned low. Outside, the river murmured over its stones, quieter every night, going down and down toward the beds it hadn't shown in years, and Coral understood suddenly why the chest had surfaced now, why the floods had pried it loose after all this time and laid it on the shingle like an offering—or a summons. Low water showed things. That was the whole nature of the country. Every dry season the river confessed a little more, and every wet season it took the confession back, and everyone who lived along it had learned to build their houses above the high-water mark of truth.

He had built his whole life above that mark. Twenty years of cargo runs and gossip and competent solitude, a man with no past worth the freight, and he had believed—right up until tonight—that the arrangement would hold. That the river would keep what it kept and he would keep what he kept and the two secrets would lie side by side on the bottom, undisturbed, until he was done flying and done remembering and done.

But the river had other ideas, and now there was a name sitting on his workbench in a dead man's handwriting, and it was not a common name after all, or if it was common everywhere else, it was singular here, because he knew it. He knew it the way you know a face across a crowded airstrip—half recognition, half warning.

Vale.

There was a cartographer named Ines Vale due on the mail flight inside the week. He'd heard it from the fuel agent, gossip riding its usual current: a woman from the coast, professional, serious, hunting her grandfather's last survey, carrying half a map and a head full of questions. Everyone in the town already knew her business; that was how the rumor economy worked, value flowing downhill like water.

And her grandfather, the gossip said, had disappeared upriver. Disappeared, the word did its soft work, the way it always did, smoothing a hard edge into mystery. Men like the one who wrote these letters didn't disappear. They were disappeared, and the grammar of the crime got buried with them.

Coral looked at the letters, at the ledger with its mid-month silence, at the brass seal with its crowned bird, and he felt the old instinct rise in him like floodwater: pack it up, sink it deep, fly somewhere the mail doesn't reach. Twenty years of practice. He could have the chest back under the tarp in five minutes and a story ready by morning—salvage rights, scrap brass, nothing of interest, ask the ministry.

But the letters were addressed to a brother. Somewhere downcoast there might be descendants of a man who had stood on a bank and watched boats load quietly and had spent his last weeks copying truth by lamplight, begging someone to remember. And those forty families in the ration tallies—Okafor, Lindqvist, Marchetti, Duarte—somewhere their blood had continued, scattered thin, probably telling themselves the same comfortable stories everyone told about the old postings.

And one of those descendants was getting off a mail plane in four days with half a map and her grandfather's eyes, looking for a man who had vanished trying to testify.

Coral put out the lamp. Not because he was finished—because he couldn't look at it anymore. He sat in the dark of the hangar with the river running low outside, and he understood that the choice he'd been avoiding for three days had just made itself. He could not sell this. He could not sink it. He could not leave it lying where the ministry's surveyors—another piece of gossip, another current—would eventually find it. Which meant he was going to have to move it, and moving it meant a crew, and a crew meant lies, and lies meant weight, and weight at altitude was the one thing every pilot feared.

He gathered the letters, squared their edges, and slid them back into the oilcloth with hands that were almost steady.

Four days, he thought. Four days to decide what to tell a woman named Vale about her own grandfather—and about the boy some boat had carried out of a place like this, renamed, and set loose on twenty years of miles.

The mail plane, he remembered, was due Thursday.
