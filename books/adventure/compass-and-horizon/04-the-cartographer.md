## 4. The Cartographer

The mail plane came in low over the town at half past noon, and Ines Vale watched it the way she'd learned to watch weather in her grandfather's study: not with hope, exactly, but with a professional's attention to what was coming.

She had been in Port Meridian for six days. Six days of a boarding house that smelled of diesel and dried fish, of asking questions at the harbormaster's office and the surveyor's guild annex and the one bar with electric lights, and of receiving in return the particular blankness that river people gave to outsiders who asked about things that happened before they were born. Six days of her half-map spread on her cot at night, its torn edge ghosting across her dreams like a coastline that kept moving.

The mail flight was her last lead. The pilot who flew the upper river—Coral Underhill, the harbormaster had said, waving a hand upriver as if the whole country out there were one undifferentiated green—was the only man who went everywhere and answered to no schedule but the water's.

She stood at the edge of the landing strip as the plane flared and rolled out, a battered high-wing freighter with paint gone chalky and a cargo pod slung where passengers ought to be. It taxied to a stop near the fuel drums with the economy of a machine whose pilot resented every liter it burned. The engine ticked. A door swung up.

The man who climbed down was harder to read than his aircraft. Forty-something, maybe older—the river aged people strangely, she'd noticed, sun and water taking turns. Lean without being thin, in a canvas jacket faded to the color of old rope. He moved around the plane checking tie-downs with his eyes rather than his hands, the way a man checks a horse he trusts. When he finally looked at her, his face did something brief and complicated—recognition of a stranger, assessment, dismissal—and settled into polite neutrality.

"Mail for Port Meridian?" he said, though the sacks were already being dragged out by a boy from the trading post.

"Actually, I'm hoping to hire you."

That got her a longer look. Up close his eyes were a gray-brown the color of the river after rain, and there was a scar through one eyebrow old enough to have gone silver. He didn't smile. She found herself annoyed at how much attention she was paying to whether he would.

"Hire me for what?"

"A survey flight. Or several." She had rehearsed this on the dock that morning, and now heard how it sounded—too eager, too much information offered unasked. She slowed down. "I'm a cartographer. My grandfather surveyed the upper river twenty-two years ago. His final expedition never filed a report. I'm trying to find where he stopped."

Something moved behind his eyes—not surprise, she thought later, remembering. Something more like a man hearing a floorboard creak in a house he thought was empty.

"Names?" he said.

"Elias Vale."

He turned back to the plane and began coiling a tie-down rope with great attention. "Don't know it."

"It would have been before your time, maybe. He was working out of Port Meridian, contracted through the colonial survey office—"

"I don't fly contracts," Underhill said. "I fly medicine, mail, and whatever Bo needs hauled. If your grandfather left a report somewhere, it'll be in an archive. Try the capital."

"The archive burned."

"Then try harder archives."

It was so flatly absurd that she almost laughed. He glanced at her then, caught her expression, and something at the corner of his mouth shifted—a millimeter of movement that might, in a more generous man, have been the beginning of humor.

"You're telling me no," she said.

"I'm telling you I've got cargo waiting and a weather window closing. Both of those are real. Your map isn't my problem."

"My map." She hadn't mentioned a map.

His hands paused on the rope—one beat, no more—and resumed. "Everyone who comes upriver hunting something has a map. Half of them are wrong and the other half are forged."

"And which is mine?"

"I wouldn't know. I haven't seen it."

*You looked,* she thought. *When I said cartographer, you looked at my satchel.* But she let it go, because pressing him here, on the strip, in front of the fuel boy and the mail clerk, would get her exactly what the last six days had gotten her: nothing, delivered politely.

"When's your next run upriver?" she asked instead.

"Depends on the river."

"Then I'll ask every day until the answer changes."

He finished the coil, hung it on its hook inside the door, and looked at her fully for the first time. She felt the look like a hand laid flat against her sternum—weighing, not warm. Whatever he found there seemed to decide something, though what, she couldn't have said.

"There's a woman two doors down from the boarding house sells cold coffee," he said. "It's terrible. Drink it slow and stop standing on my strip."

He swung up into the cockpit. The engine coughed twice and caught. And Ines Vale stood in the prop wash with dust in her teeth, watching a man fly away from her questions, and understood two things with equal certainty: that Coral Underhill knew something about her grandfather's last survey.

And that she had just spent four minutes looking at a stranger and thinking about nothing else, which was inconvenient, professionally speaking, in roughly equal measure to everything else.

* * *

The cold coffee was, as promised, terrible. She drank it slowly anyway, at a table outside the trader's, with the half-map weighted down by her cup and her notebook open to the page where she'd been keeping a ledger of refusals.

*Harbormaster: never heard of Elias Vale (lied—he signed the guild register, 19 years ago, renewal lapsed). Survey annex: files transferred to capital (capital: burned). Bar owner: too young, too drunk, or both.*

*Coral Underhill: doesn't know the name. Watched my satchel when I said map.*

Her grandfather had taught her to read terrain the way other people read faces—the way a ridge lied about its height, the way a river's color confessed its depth. She'd inherited the habit and aimed it at people, sometimes, when the stakes were high enough. Underhill had a tell. It wasn't in his face; his face was a locked hangar. It was in his hands. They were still and competent right up until a subject got close to something, and then they found work to do. Ropes. Buckles. Coffee cups, if she bought him one.

She turned the half-map ninety degrees and studied it again for the hundredth time.

Elias Vale had come home from his final expedition with nothing—no report, no instruments, no explanation, and, according to her mother, no sleep. He'd spent the last year of his life redrawing sections of the upper river from memory, obsessively, on butcher paper, and burning most of them. What survived was this: the eastern half of a chart covering perhaps sixty miles of river above Sable Bend, drawn in his precise drafting hand, cut raggedly down the center fold. Her grandmother had found it sewn into the lining of his coat after the funeral, along with a note in his smallest script: *Whoever finishes this, finish it honestly.*

The western half—the destination, the reason, whatever he'd seen that made a careful man tear up his own work—was gone. And the eastern half ended at a stretch of river her grandfather had labeled, in letters smaller than any other annotation on the sheet, with a single word: *ASK.*

Not a place name. An instruction.

She'd asked everyone. She was running out of everyone.

"Ines Vale."

She looked up. The boy from the trading post stood beside her table, holding out a folded slip of paper. "Pilot said give you this."

Underhill's handwriting was small and slanted, a mechanic's hand. She unfolded it.

*Thursday. Fuel run to Two Rivers, then upriver if the weather holds. Fare is forty marks, paid forward, no refunds, no detours. You ride as cargo. Don't talk above five hundred feet.*

No signature. No mention of her grandfather, the survey, or the word *ask*. Just a window, opened a crack, and the distinct impression that opening it had cost him something.

She read it three times. Then she took out her notebook and wrote beneath her ledger of refusals:

*Underhill: knows something. Lying badly, which means lying scared. Thursday.*

And then, because honesty was a discipline her grandfather had died practicing imperfectly, she added a third line:

*Also: don't. Whatever it is. Don't.*

* * *

Thursday came in gray and windless, the kind of sky that made the river look like poured metal. The plane sat on the strip already loaded—crates marked with a clinic's stencil, a wrapped engine part, mail sacks—and Underhill was crouched at the open cowling with a wrench and an expression of grievance, arguing with the engine in a low mutter she chose not to eavesdrop on.

A girl of ten or eleven was sitting on the wheel chock, swinging her legs, watching Ines with the frank appraisal children used on strangers and adults pretended not to.

"You're the map lady," the girl said.

"I'm a cartographer."

"That's what I said." She hopped off the chock and stuck out a hand, solemn as a magistrate. "Wren. I load the cargo. That's my job."

Ines shook the small hand. "Do you fly often?"

"I live on the plane. Not legally." Wren glanced toward the cowling, lowered her voice with the delicacy of long practice. "He'll say yes to the fare but he'll pretend it's a favor to the clinic. He does that. Pretends things are favors."

"Wren." Underhill, without looking up. "Cargo check."

"See?" said Wren, and went to do her job.

They flew out at nine. Ines rode in the jump seat behind the pilots' bench with the mail sacks wedged around her, and true to his written word, Underhill said nothing above five hundred feet—or below it either. He flew the way he did everything else, she was learning: with a competence so total it functioned as a wall. His hands on the yoke were quiet. His eyes moved in a constant unhurried circuit—engine gauges, horizon, river, sky, river again—as if the world were a document he was proofreading.

She watched the country unroll below and tried to read it as her grandfather would have. The river braided and rejoined itself. Oxbow lakes lay in the forest like dropped mirrors. Once, far off the line of flight, she saw a straightness in the treeline that wasn't geology—a canal, or a levee, or the skeleton of some works abandoned—and her pen was in her hand before she'd decided to move it.

"Don't mark that," Underhill said.

She started. "You're watching me, not the river?"

"Somebody has to. You're sitting next to the mail." A pause. "That clearing's a timber camp. It's on every chart. Marking it makes you look like you don't know charts."

It was reasonable. It was even probably true. It was also, she noted, the second time he'd told her what not to write down, and the first time he'd done it before she'd touched pen to anything.

At Two Rivers they dropped crates at a mission clinic—she watched a nun argue him down from his invoice with the ease of someone who'd won that argument weekly for years—and took on a passenger, a silent old man with a caged bird who rode with his eyes shut the whole leg. And then the river narrowed, the banks closed in, and the country changed its grammar. Hills shouldered up through the forest. The water ran darker. There were fewer roofs.

Ines leaned toward the gap between the seats. "Below Sable Bend," she said, "there's a reach my grandfather's half-chart covers. Sixty miles. He drew it from memory after he came back, which means he lost his originals, which means whatever happened to them happened up there. I'm not asking you to take me anywhere. I'm asking you to look at a drawing."

Silence, except for the engine.

"He annotated it," she went on. "One word. *Ask.* As if he expected whoever found the map to go asking, and as if he knew—" she stopped, recalibrated. "As if he knew the answer was a person, not a place."

The plane droned. Below them the river made a long green bend around a hill with a bare crown, and Underhill banked slightly—not toward it, she noticed, but enough to keep it in view a moment longer than the flight path required.

"Cargo flies free," he said at last. "Questions cost extra."

"How much?"

"More than you've got."

"I've got forty marks and a guild stipend."

"Then you can't afford the answer."

Frustration rose in her like floodwater, and with it, unwelcome and unmistakable, something adjacent to admiration. He was good at this. He was very, very good at this, and the goodness of it was itself the confession. Innocent men got bored. This man was managing her.

"All right," she said. "Then I'll pay with something else. Look at the map tonight. If you see anything you recognize, tell me. If you don't—" she shrugged. "Tell me that. I'll believe you. Probably."

"Why would I look at it at all?"

"Because you already have. Twice. Once on the strip when I said *cartographer*, and once about ten minutes ago when we passed that bald hill and you flew like a man checking on something he owns."

The silence that followed had texture. She felt him deciding, the way she could feel weather deciding.

"Tonight," he said finally. "After the engine cools. One look."

* * *

He came to the boarding house at dusk, which surprised her; she'd had him pegged as a man who slept in his plane. He sat across from her at the little table by the window, refused coffee with a shake of the head, and held out one hand for the map with the resignation of a man accepting a summons.

He was careful with it. That was the first thing she noticed—that he handled the tracing paper like something that could bruise, turning it toward the lamplight, and that his eyes went not to the grand sweep of the river but immediately, hungrily, to the annotations. The soundings. The tiny marginal notes in her grandfather's cramped hand. He read them the way a man reads letters.

"This reach," he said, tapping a section. "Your grandfather has the channel on the west side of the island shoal. It runs east in dry season. He drew this from memory, you said?"

"After the fact. Why?"

"Because memory drifts." He set the map down with the same care. "Rivers move. A chart is a photograph of a corpse." He stood. "I don't recognize anything."

The lie was smooth. That was the thing she would remember afterward, lying awake with the lamp off: how smooth it was. No flicker, no overcorrection, no excess of denial. Just a mild, professional regret, delivered while handing back her inheritance.

But his hands, taking the tracing paper by its edges to return it, had trembled. Barely. The tremor of a man carrying something heavier than paper.

"Thank you," she said, and meant none of it, and watched him understand that she meant none of it, and watched him leave anyway, because there was nothing else for either of them to do.

Through the window she saw him pause in the street under the last of the light, look back once at her window—not at her, she told herself, at the lamp, at the map-shaped shadow the lamp threw—and stand there a moment with his head tilted, like a man listening to a bell that had stopped ringing years ago.

Then he walked on toward the strip, and Ines Vale sat down at the table, pulled her notebook toward her, and wrote the entry she'd been circling all evening:

*He recognized the island shoal. He corrected it without thinking—which means he knows that reach in dry season, personally, recently. Nobody knows a dead man's river that well unless they've flown it.*

*Elias, what did you find?*

*And you, Coral Underhill—what are you keeping?*

Outside, far off toward the strip, an engine turned over in the dark, idled, and shut down again, as if its pilot couldn't decide whether to stay or go, and had chosen, for tonight, neither.
