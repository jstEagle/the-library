## 5. Wren's Question

Wren had three rules for the hangar, and she'd made them herself, which meant they were law.

One: never touch Coral's tools without putting them back where your fingers found them. Two: if a plane came in low over the water at dusk and it wasn't his, you got under the workbench until the engine note told you whose it was. Three: when Coral said *not now*, you counted to a hundred in your head, and then you asked again.

She was on eighty-nine when he came out of the back room with the oil rag over his shoulder and that look on his face — the one like a door pulled shut from the inside.

He'd been in there two hours. The lamp under the door had burned past midnight, and Wren had lain in her cot above the office listening to him move things around in there, slow and careful, the way nobody moved anything unless it mattered. Once there had been a sound she couldn't name. Metal on wood. A lid settling, maybe. Or a lock turning home.

*Not now* was what he'd said that morning, when she'd asked about the chest sitting in the corner of the hangar behind the tarpaulin, the one he'd hauled up from the river flats on the handcart, sweating through his shirt like the thing weighed more than iron had any right to weigh.

So she'd counted. And now she asked again.

"What's in the box?"

Coral didn't look up from the carburetor he was rebuilding with his big careful hands, each part laid out on the bench in a row like words waiting to be read in order. "A box."

"A box you carried four miles by hand instead of waiting for the tide to come back and float it."

"The tide takes its time."

"The tide," Wren said, "was coming in. I watched it."

That got her a glance. Not much of one — just enough to tell her he'd heard, and that hearing it cost him something small. Coral Underhill could lie to a customs officer without blinking, could tell a passenger the weather would hold while looking straight at a sky the color of a bruise, but he had never once managed to lie to her with his whole face. Part of it always stayed honest, standing off to the side like a man who wouldn't leave the room.

"School tomorrow," he said. "You've got sums."

"I've got sums every night of my life. What's in the box?"

"Wren."

"That's not an answer, that's my name."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Closer to surrender being considered and declined. He set down the float needle and wiped his hands, and for a second she thought — this is it, he's going to tell me — and then he reached up and took down the inventory ledger from its nail, which was worse than any answer, because it meant he was going to give her *work*, and Coral gave her work exactly when he wanted her to stop asking questions.

"Fuel run Thursday," he said. "Up to Pelican Cut and the mission strips beyond. We're low on everything and the wet season ate half the dock at Serrano's landing. You'll do the manifest."

"I did the manifest last time."

"You'll do it again. Every drum, every crate, every weight and balance figure in your own hand. If we're overweight by so much as a kilo I want to know before the wheels leave the water, not after." He tapped the ledger. "Start tonight. Count what's in the stores room. All of it."

The stores room was next to the back room. The back room with the lamp and the sound of metal on wood.

Wren looked at him for a long moment, twelve years old and certain she could read a man better than most grown women read weather. "You're giving me a job so I'll stop asking."

"I'm giving you a job because the job needs doing."

"And the box?"

"Is cargo," Coral said, "that hasn't been manifested yet. When it's manifested, you'll know what it is. Until then—" he picked the float needle back up, conversation over in the language of his hands— "count drums."

* * *

She counted drums. Forty-one of them, avgas and diesel both, stenciled numbers fading, seams she checked with a rag and a thumb for the weep of fuel that meant a drum was lying about how full it was. She counted crates of tinned milk and quinine and the schoolbooks for the mission at Pelican Cut that always arrived water-stained no matter how high they were stacked. She wrote it all in her round, stubborn handwriting, and she did it well, because doing things well was the only argument she had ever found that Coral couldn't answer.

And the whole time, the back room door stayed shut, and the chest sat behind the tarpaulin in the corner of the hangar like a dog that had been told to stay.

She'd seen it uncovered, once. The morning he'd brought it up. She'd come down to the flats with the thermos the way she always did when he worked the riverbank, and she'd stood on the mud-slick stones watching him lever it out of the silt with the handcart, and for a moment — just a moment, before the tarp went over — she'd seen the top of it. Dark wood gone black with decades of river. Iron bands. And stamped into the wood, worn shallow but still legible, a seal: an eagle inside a ring of stars, wings spread over a crown.

Wren knew that seal. Everybody in the river country knew it, the way you knew a scar on your own family. It was on the old bridge pilings. On the rusted boundary posts back in the cane. On the faded certificate Father Anselm kept framed in the mission office, the one granting the outpost lands to a nation that hadn't existed since before Wren was born.

Nobody used that seal anymore. Nobody *talked* about the time it was used. It was like asking about the year of the great flood — adults went quiet and changed the subject to the weather, and the weather was always changing, so the subject never came back.

She finished the manifest at nearly midnight and climbed down to find Coral still at the bench, though the carburetor was long since done. He was just sitting, hands empty, looking at nothing, which for Coral was the same as looking at something very hard.

"Manifest's done," she said, laying it on the bench. "Forty-one drums, twenty-six crates, total load nine hundred and forty kilos. We'll be light enough to take the mail sacks too if Serrano's got any."

He read it. He always actually read it, ran his finger down the columns, and she'd learned to feel proud of the way his finger never stopped or doubled back. "Good figures," he said.

"Coral."

"Mm."

"The seal on the box. The eagle with the crown." She watched his hands go still on the paper. "I know what country that was. Father Anselm showed us on the old maps. It owned everything upriver once. Everything." She paused. "Including the outpost that drowned."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out on the water a night bird called, three notes, like a question asked twice too many.

"Where'd you learn all that?" Coral said finally, and his voice was easy, lazy even, and that was how she knew it wasn't.

"School. And the mission office. And the fact that you went stiff as a mooring post the second I said the word *outpost*."

"I'm tired, is what I am."

"You're not."

"Wren." He set the manifest down and looked at her then, full-on, and she braced herself, because when Coral looked at you like that you felt measured — weighed against something only he could see. "There are things I keep because keeping them is the job. Not because I like it. Not because it's fun. Because somebody has to be the one who carries a thing without setting it down, and I decided a long time ago that somebody was me. That's all the box is. A thing I'm carrying."

"Carrying where?"

"Wherever it doesn't get us hurt."

"*Us*," she repeated, and saw him hear it, see the word land. Us. Her included. Always included, though he never said it straight out; he built it instead, out of cot beds and flying lessons and the exact right amount of sugar in her tea, and she had learned to live in the house he built without either of them naming it.

"There's a story in that box," she said. "Isn't there. A true one."

Coral was quiet long enough that she thought he might actually do it — open the door he kept pulling shut. Then he said, "Every crate on this river has a story. The milk tins came from somebody's farm. The quinine saved somebody's life. Doesn't mean you open them in the hangar at midnight."

"That's not the same."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

Thursday they flew north, and Wren did the loading herself, checking her own figures twice, and Coral watched her sling the tie-downs with a nod that in his currency was a parade. The chest rode in the back room of the hangar, tarpaulined, locked — she'd checked the lock, because checking locks was rule number four, which she'd invented that week — and the whole flight upriver she turned the problem over the way you turn a stone in your hand looking for what's underneath.

Because here was the thing Wren knew that Coral maybe didn't know she knew: he talked in his sleep. Not often. But some nights, in the bad-weather season, when thunder walked around the sky all night, he said words. River words. Names, sometimes, in a language that wasn't the trade tongue and wasn't the mission Latin either. And once, a word she'd written down secretly in the back of her sums book because it had sounded like a key sounds, small and shaped to fit something:

*Aldous.*

She'd asked Father Anselm, casual as Sunday, whether Aldous was a saint. The old priest had frowned and said it wasn't a saint's name, it was an *outpost* name — a family name from the drowned station, from before. There had been Vales at the outpost, he said. And Aldouses. And others. Families that were *removed*, the priest said, and then he'd pressed his lips together the way adults did, and changed the subject to the weather.

Removed. Like furniture. Like a stain.

So when they landed back at dusk and Coral went straight to the back room and the lamp burned late again, Wren didn't ask. Asking was pushing, and pushing made Coral plant his feet. Instead she did what she did best, which was pay attention.

She noticed that he'd started locking the hangar's outer doors at night, which he'd never bothered with in eleven years. She noticed the government survey boat that had come upriver Tuesday, white hull, official stripe, two men in clean shirts taking photographs of the flood line, and the way Coral had stood in the doorway of Serrano's store watching them with an expression she'd never seen on him before — not fear exactly. Recognition. Like a man seeing, far off across the water, a shape he'd hoped was gone forever.

She noticed that when the cartographer woman arrived on the mail flight — Ines Vale, the manifest said, and Wren had read that name three times because Vale was an outpost name too — Coral lied to her. Smoothly, pleasantly, with his whole pilot's face on. Wren had been in the office filing receipts and heard every word through the shutter. *No wrecks upriver worth surveying. Nothing surfaces after flood season but driftwood and dead fish.* And the woman had smiled and thanked him and written something in her notebook anyway, and Coral had closed the shutter like a man closing a window against rain that was already inside the house.

That night Wren sat on the hangar steps with her knees drawn up, watching the river run dark and endless toward the sea, and she put it all together the way she'd learned to put together engines from parts: chest, seal, outpost, removed families, Vale, ministry men, lies.

Whatever Coral was carrying, it wasn't cargo. Cargo you could sell. This was the other kind of thing. The kind you carried because setting it down would break something.

She heard his boots on the boards behind her. He sat down beside her without asking, the way he always did, leaving exactly one person-width of space between them, which was how he hugged.

"Sums done?" he said.

"Done."

"Manifest for tomorrow?"

"In my head already."

They sat. The river breathed. Far off, thunder muttered over the hills like an old man complaining in his sleep.

"Coral," she said, and didn't look at him, because this part was easier sideways. "You don't have to tell me what it is. I know you won't. You've got your rules and your reasons and I've counted to a hundred more times than a hundred." She turned to look at him then, and made her voice steady the way he'd taught her to make her voice steady over the radio when the wind was shoving the plane around. "But whatever it is — whatever you're carrying — you should know I can carry too. That's all. I'm not a kid you have to keep things from. I'm crew."

For a moment he said nothing. Then he reached over and knocked his knuckles gently against hers, once, the way pilots touched a good instrument panel. Something crossed his face that had a lot of weather in it.

"I know you're crew," he said quietly. "Best manifest hand on the river."

"And?"

"And," Coral said, standing, brushing off his trousers, already halfway back behind the door he lived behind, "tomorrow you're learning weight-and-balance calculations for real, because if we ever have to fly heavy out of a short strip in a hurry, I need to trust the figures without checking them."

Which was not an answer. Which was a job.

But it was also — and Wren heard it, tucked inside the practical words like a coin sewn into a coat lining — *I trust you*. And *if we ever have to fly heavy out of a short strip in a hurry* meant he was thinking about running. From the survey boat. From the woman named Vale. From the box with the dead nation's eagle on it.

She climbed the stairs to her cot and lay in the dark listening to the river, and made herself a fifth rule, silent, unwritten, hers alone:

*Find out what he's carrying. Then help him carry it, whether he asks or not.*

Down in the back room, the lamp went out. And in the morning, when the mail flight droned in over the water and the cartographer named Vale stepped down onto the pontoon with her half-map and her patient eyes, Wren watched Coral walk out to meet her wearing his smoothest face — and watched the woman look past him, briefly, directly at Wren, as if she already knew the girl on the steps was the one person on this river who might tell her the truth.

Wren met her eyes. And did not look away.
