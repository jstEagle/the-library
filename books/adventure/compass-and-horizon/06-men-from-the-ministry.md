## 6. Men from the Ministry

They came on a Thursday, which was itself a kind of warning. Government men did not travel on Thursdays. They traveled when the budget allowed, and budgets, like floods, arrived without regard for anyone's schedule.

Coral was under the Cessna's port wing with a socket wrench in his fist and grease to his elbows when he heard the boat first — not the loose, easy slap of a trading skiff but the disciplined throb of an outboard run at regulation speed by someone who had read the manual. He slid out from under the wing and stood up slowly, wiping his hands on a rag that had been clean sometime during the previous administration.

Two men in the boat. The one at the tiller wore khaki pressed into creases sharp enough to draw blood, and the other sat in the bow with a leather document case on his knees like it was a sleeping child he didn't quite trust. Sunglasses. Short sleeves buttoned to the wrist despite the heat. A man dressed for a climate he refused to admit existed.

"Underhill," the tiller man called as they came alongside the strip's low dock. Not a question. That was the second warning. Men who already knew your name before they'd landed were men who'd read your file, and Coral had spent twenty years making sure his file was boring enough to put a clerk to sleep.

"That's me," he said. "You're lost."

The creased one smiled the way a form smiles. "Mr. Ferro said you'd say that."

"Ferro talks too much."

"He said you'd say that too." The man stepped onto the dock and offered a hand Coral did not take. "Senior Surveyor Okonkwo. Ministry of Interior Resources. This is Mr. Adeyemi." A nod toward the document case. "We're conducting a review of salvage rights along the lower reaches."

"Lower reaches are a long river," Coral said. "I fly medicine and mail. I don't salvage."

"No," Okonkwo agreed pleasantly. "But you know everyone who does."

* * *

He made them wait while he finished nothing in particular on the airplane, because making ministry men wait was the only currency of resistance a man with a wrench possessed. Then he walked them up to the house, where Wren was — thank every indifferent god — absent, sent downriver an hour earlier to Ferro's for flour and, though she didn't know it yet, for distance.

Ines Vale was another matter. She sat on his porch with her half-map spread across her knees like an open book she was pretending not to read, and when the two government men climbed his steps she folded it away with the unhurried calm of a woman who had learned that the best place to hide a thing was in plain sight of officials, who never looked at what was offered freely.

"Gentlemen," she said.

"Ma'am." Okonkwo tipped an imaginary hat. "On holiday?"

"Surveying." She smiled. "Old family habit."

Something moved behind Okonkwo's sunglasses — quick, professional interest, there and gone like a fish turning under brown water. "A cartographer."

"Ines Vale." She extended her hand and gave her name the way you hand someone a receipt. "My grandfather surveyed this reach. Before the consolidation."

"Before," Okonkwo repeated, and took her hand, and held it perhaps half a second longer than courtesy required. "There were a great many things before the consolidation."

"There were," Ines said, and let go.

Coral watched this exchange the way a pilot watches weather building on a horizon he can't outrun: two storm cells, neither of them his, drifting toward each other over his own roof.

He got them inside, got them tea he didn't offer and they didn't refuse, and then Okonkwo opened the document case and the interview began in earnest, which is to say it began as a conversation about paperwork, which is how all serious violence begins in a country governed by forms.

"We're compiling an inventory," Okonkwo said, "of materials recovered from the flood season. There are regulations regarding artifacts of colonial provenance. Anything bearing the seal of the former administration is technically state property."

"Is it."

"It is." Adeyemi spoke for the first time. His voice was soft, almost apologetic, which made it worse. "Salvage recovered within three miles of a registered waterway must be declared within thirty days. Failure to declare carries penalties."

"Penalties," Coral said. "For picking up what the river throws at you."

"For keeping what the state has an interest in." Adeyemi opened the case fully now, and Coral saw the folder inside — buff-colored, thick, a red string wound around two buttons to keep it shut. Stamped across its face, faded but legible, was a classification mark and a date twenty-two years old. "We're also reviewing legacy files. Files sealed at the time of the consolidation and never formally closed."

The room got very quiet. Outside, a kingfisher hit the water and came up with something silver and doomed.

"What file?" Coral asked, and heard his own voice do what it always did — flatten out, go mild, go nowhere. Twenty years of practice in four words.

Okonkwo removed his sunglasses. His eyes were pale and patient and entirely without warmth. "File 114-Corvid. Concerning the administrative outpost at—" he consulted a card, though Coral would have bet his airplane the man knew it by heart— "Sable Bend. Closed pending resolution. Never resolved."

"I don't know anything about Sable Bend."

"No," Okonkwo said. "Very few people do. That was rather the point of closing the file." He set it on the table between them, unopened, like a card played face down. "Here's what we know, Mr. Underhill, so we don't waste each other's time. This season's flood was the worst in eleven years. When floods like that recede, they expose things. The river gives back. And people along this reach talk — about salvage, about finds, about a chest, if you'll forgive the word, that some salvager may or may not have hauled out of the mud near the old outpost channel."

Coral drank his tea. It had gone bitter and he drank it anyway, because his hands needed something official to do.

"People talk," he agreed. "Half of what they say is wrong and the other half is mine."

"Then you'll have no objection to assisting us." Okonkwo leaned back. "If such a chest exists, it should be surrendered for archival review. If it doesn't exist, no harm done. Either way, we're offering compensation for cooperation. Fair market value. Plus a confidentiality stipend, if the matter stays quiet."

*A stipend.* Coral felt the word land somewhere behind his ribs. They weren't here for history. History was what you told people who asked too many questions. They were here to buy the ending before anyone else could write it.

"And if I find something," Coral said slowly, "and turn it over — what happens to it?"

"Archival review," Adeyemi said gently. "Cataloguing. Assessment. Standard procedure."

"How long does standard procedure take?"

Adeyemi smiled his apologetic smile. "Files of this nature have been under review for decades."

*There it is,* Coral thought. *The whole machine, laid out on my table between the teacups.* Find it, surrender it, watch it disappear into a warehouse and a red string and a hundred years of procedure, until every name in it was dust and every family that might have claimed it had scattered or died or forgotten there was ever anything to claim. The empire hadn't burned the records. It had done something far more efficient. It had filed them.

"The dry season's coming," Coral said aloud. "River drops. Strips soften. You gentlemen will want to be out of the interior before your boat can't float."

"We've booked passage," Okonkwo said. "On the mail flight, end of the month. Yours, I understand."

"My plane doesn't carry passengers."

"Then I misread the schedule." Okonkwo rose, unhurried, and buttoned his jacket against a heat he still wouldn't acknowledge. "We'll be staying at Ferro's. Nice establishment. Good gossip." At the door he paused. "One more thing, Mr. Underhill. The Vale survey. File 114-Corvid references a cartographer attached to the Sable Bend station. Last name Vale. Disappeared during the removal. You wouldn't know anything about that either, I suppose."

Behind him, Coral heard Ines Vale stop breathing.

"No," Coral said. "Can't help you there."

"Mm." Okonkwo put his sunglasses back on. "Everyone on this river says that. It's remarkable, really. Such a consistent answer from such inconsistent people." He stepped off the porch, then turned. "Thirty days, Mr. Underhill. The declaration window closes whether or not you find anything. We'll see you at the flight."

* * *

He watched their boat diminish downriver until it was a smudge, then a rumor, then gone. Ines hadn't moved from her chair. Her half-map lay in her lap, refolded, her knuckles white around its edges.

"Sable Bend," she said quietly. "That's on my grandfather's last route. That's where his survey ends. Mid-line. Like he just — stopped writing."

Coral said nothing. There was nothing safe to say, and everything true to say, and the distance between those two things had just become the width of a government file.

"They knew his name," she went on. "Before I said it. They knew he disappeared. They've known for twenty years and the file was never closed and nobody—" She stopped herself. Looked at him. "Coral. What aren't you telling me?"

"The same thing I'm not telling them," he said, and hated how smooth it sounded, and hated more that it worked — that she nodded slowly, accepting it, filing it, a cartographer logging an unmapped region for later.

Downriver, at Ferro's, two ministry men would sleep tonight behind walls of good gossip. Somewhere in his back room, under a tarp and a false floor panel, a chest stamped with a dead nation's seal sat holding a ledger full of names — names the state wanted re-buried, names a woman on his porch was bleeding to find, names that included, though she didn't know it yet, her own.

Thirty days. The declaration window. The mail flight. The dry season clock already ticking in the falling water line.

Coral looked at the river, low and honest and giving back what it had kept, and understood at last that the chest was no treasure at all.

It was a debt. And the collectors had just checked in down the road.
