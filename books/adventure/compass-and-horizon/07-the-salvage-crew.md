## 7. The Salvage Crew

The trouble with lying to a river town, Coral had learned over twenty years of practice, was not the lie itself. Lies were cheap. The trouble was that a small town kept its own ledger, and every entry was written in gossip ink that never dried.

So when he walked into Ferro's kitchen at six in the morning and said he needed a crew for a salvage run upriver, he did it loudly, in front of the two deckhands eating breakfast, because a salvage run was the one story nobody would question. The river gave and the river took, and men who pulled what it took were a breed as old as the water itself.

Ferro didn't even look up from his pan. "What wreck?"

"Old barge, reported snagged above Sable Bend. Ministry wants it cleared before the dry season muddies everything." He let the word *ministry* do its work. Nobody argued with paperwork.

"And you need me because—"

"Because your cooking keeps Bo from killing Tomas, and Tomas from killing himself, and I can't afford to replace either of them."

That got a grunt that in Ferro's language meant yes. Coral had hired him three years ago off a mail boat, a wide, slow man with burn-scarred forearms and a memory for every grudge on the river except his own. He cooked the way he did everything else: without hurry, without complaint, and better than he had any right to.

* * *

Bo was harder.

He found her under her tarp shelter at the edge of the strips, halfway into the guts of a generator, a wrench in one hand and a cigarette burning down between two fingers she'd forgotten about. She was sixty if she was a day, built like an engine block herself, and she'd been fixing things since before Coral could name the parts.

"I heard," she said, before he'd finished his first sentence. "Deckhands talk faster than radio."

"Then you know what I'm asking."

She slid out from under the generator, sat up, and looked at him with eyes the color of old oil — flat until you tilted them toward the light, and then something moved down deep. "I know what you're saying. Saying's not asking." She tapped ash onto the concrete. "A barge clearance doesn't need a mechanic of my particular talents, Coral. A barge clearance needs a boy with a toolkit and a strong back. You're asking for me because whatever you're pulling out of that river, you want it to keep working after you've got it."

Coral held her look. Twenty years of flying cargo and gossip had taught him that most people filled silence with truth if you gave them room enough.

"You don't have to come," he said.

"No," she agreed. "I don't." She stood, joints announcing themselves one at a time, and looked past him at the strips, where the morning haze was burning off the water and the ministry surveyors' tent still stood white and neat by the fuel depot, like a tooth that hadn't decided whether to ache yet. "But you've got that look. Had it since the chest came up. My husband had it once, the week before he flew us into a canyon to dodge a customs boat. We lived. Barely. And I never once regretted going." She picked up her wrench and pointed it at him like a doctor delivering a diagnosis. "I'll come. But hear me, Coral Underhill: I'll fly with you anywhere. What I won't do is pretend I don't see what I see. You're carrying something heavier than salvage, and when it gets too heavy — and it will — you tell this crew the truth or I walk off the job wherever we're standing."

"That's fair," he said, and hated how easily the agreement came out of his mouth, and how little he meant it.

* * *

Tomas found *him*, which was how it always went.

Coral was checking the Otter's tie-downs that afternoon when the diver came up the bank from his houseboat, drying his hair with a rag, walking with the loose-hipped ease of a man who spent more time underwater than on land. Tomas was thirty, maybe, all tendon and grin, the kind of fool who thought decompression tables were suggestions and depth was a personal challenge.

"Heard we've got a wreck," Tomas said. "Above Sable Bend."

"News travels."

"News flies. It rides in your plane." He dropped onto the pontoon, uninvited, comfortable. "Here's my question, captain. Every wreck on this river, I know. I've dived half of them and drunk with the other half. There's no barge snagged above Sable Bend. I checked with the lock keeper this morning."

The river went very quiet around them, the way it did when it was listening.

"So either the ministry's chasing a ghost," Tomas went on, "or you are. And I don't much care which." He grinned, and there was nothing foolish in it at all. "I care about the second thing, though. Whatever it is, it came up in the floods, near the old outpost water. And my grandmother used to say the outpost water keeps what it's given. So I'll dive where you point me, captain, and I'll ask nothing. But I'll be watching what you do with what I bring up. That's the price. Not questions. Watching."

Coral looked at him for a long moment. Twenty years ago, someone had watched a boy get loaded onto a plane at night and told no one. It had saved the boy's life. He had never decided whether watching was mercy or cowardice, and he suspected now he never would.

"Done," he said.

* * *

They gathered at Ferro's kitchen that evening, the four of them around a table scarred by twenty years of elbows, and Coral laid it out the way he'd rehearsed it on the flight back from the strips — the true bones wrapped in false skin.

"Officially: wreck recovery above Sable Bend, ministry contract, salvage rights standard split. Unofficially—" he set his hands flat on the table, "—we go upriver ahead of the surveyors' schedule, and we move some property of mine to a place I'll show Bo only. Somewhere it stays."

"Property," Bo repeated.

"A chest. Contents personal."

"Personal," Tomas said, tasting the word. "Like a dead man's letters, or like a live man's trouble?"

"Both," Coral said, because they'd smell a cleaner lie than that.

Silence, except for Ferro's knife working through onions behind them, unhurried, metronomic. Then Ferro spoke without turning. "My mother ran a ferry crossing down at the delta. When the soldiers came through in the bad years, she hid people under the cargo deck and told the officers the boards were rotten, couldn't take the weight. They never checked twice. You know why?" He turned, knife still in hand, a big gentle man with murder in his hands and none in his face. "Because she never once looked afraid. That's the whole trick of it, Coral. Whatever you're hauling upriver — don't look afraid."

"I don't look anything," Coral said. "That's the problem. People say so."

"They say worse," Bo muttered, and lit a fresh cigarette off the stove flame, which Ferro allowed exactly once a year and this was apparently the day.

Wren came in with the dishes bucket, fourteen years old and all elbows and watchfulness, and stopped dead when she saw the table full. Her eyes went around the crew — Bo, Tomas, Ferro — and landed on Coral with the weight of a question she'd been carrying for days now, ever since the chest, ever since the night light burned late in his shack. He'd given her a job instead of an answer: inventory the stores, count the fuel drums, log the spare parts. She'd done it perfectly and hated every minute of it, and he loved her for hating it.

"Wren," he said. "You'll run the manifest. Stores, fuel, weights and balances. If Bo says she needs it, it goes on your list, and nothing goes on that plane that isn't on your list. Nothing."

Her chin came up. "And if something tries?"

"Then you come find me before it's on board. Not after."

It wasn't trust, exactly, and it wasn't an answer, and they both knew it. But it was a rope thrown across a gap, and she took it, the way family takes things — without thanking you, without letting go.

* * *

Loading happened two mornings later, in the gray hour before the heat came up, because Coral wanted them gone before the ministry men finished their coffee.

The Otter sat heavy on the water, floats riding low. Fuel drums lashed amidships. Dive gear. Tools. Ferro's galley box, which weighed more than sin. The chest itself, wrapped in canvas and tarped, riding in the cabin like a passenger who'd paid for silence. Wren stood at the ramp with her clipboard, ticking items, and when Bo carried aboard a crate marked SPARES that clanked wrong — too many loose pieces, too much rattle — Wren looked inside, looked at Bo, and wrote it on the list anyway.

"What is it?" Coral asked.

"Tools," Bo said.

"It's tools," Wren said, and ticked the box, and Coral understood that the girl had just chosen a side without knowing what side it was, and that someday soon that choice would need explaining, and that he was running out of days on which he could postpone explaining anything to anyone.

Ines Vale came down the bank as the engines started.

She'd been in town nine days now, hunting her grandfather's last survey with half a map and a cartographer's patience, and Coral had lied to her twice — smoothly, professionally, with the specific fluency of a man protecting something. She stood at the water's edge with her satchel and her sun hat and her terrible timing, and called over the prop wash:

"Underhill! Where does a salvage crew go above Sable Bend? There's no wreck there. I checked the charts."

Of course she had. She'd probably drawn her own.

"Ministry ghost," he shouted back. "Paperwork wreck. We clear it, they file it, everybody's happy."

She studied him for a moment, wind flattening the grass, water slapping the floats, and he watched her decide — visibly, deliberately — to pocket the lie rather than spend it. "When you're up there," she called, "keep your eyes open. Old surveys put structures along that bend that aren't on any modern chart. If your ghost has foundations, I'd pay for photographs."

"Photographs cost extra."

"Everything worth having does." She touched her hat brim — a salute, or a promise, or a warning; with her it was always all three — and turned back up the bank.

Bo leaned close as Coral throttled up. "That woman's going to follow us."

"I know."

"You're going to let her."

He didn't answer. The Otter lifted off the brown water into the smoke-hazed morning, and below them the town shrank to toy size, the ministry tent a white grain, Ines Vale a smaller grain still, standing where the grass met the river, watching them go.

Behind him in the cabin, Tomas was showing Wren how the dive mask worked, Ferro was already arguing with the galley stove, and Bo was looking at Coral with those oil-dark eyes that saw everything and forgave less.

"Course heading?" she asked.

"Sable Bend first. Then somewhere no chart agrees on."

She nodded slowly, and lit one more cigarette against regulations, and said, mostly to the windshield, "My husband flew us into a canyon once. Lived. Barely." A drag. A long exhale of smoke. "This feels familiar."

Ahead, the river bent north into country the maps gave up on, and Coral flew into it carrying four people who trusted him, one girl who almost did, and a lie heavy enough to sink them all — telling himself, as he had for twenty years, that keeping people safe and keeping them ignorant were the same work.

The compass needle swung and settled. The horizon waited, patient as water.

He should have known better than to believe either one.
