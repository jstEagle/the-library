## 8. Half a Map

Ines Vale had learned to read men the way she read contour lines: by what they concealed. A ridge that showed no shadow was a plateau; a man who never paused before answering was lying. Coral Underhill did both things at once—answered too fast and too smooth, like a river running over something deep enough to drown in.

She'd been in Port Meridian four days now, sleeping in the loft above the chandlery because the hotel flooded every afternoon at high water, and she had spent those four days watching him the way he probably watched weather. He flew his medicine run out at dawn and came back smelling of kerosene and river mud. He ate alone at the corner table of Ferro's cookhouse with his back to the wall. He spoke to everyone and told no one anything.

And he had a crate in the back of his hangar that he'd covered with a tarpaulin the day after low water exposed it, and which he checked every evening like a man checking on a sick animal he intended to eat.

Ines spread her grandfather's half-map across her cot that night and let herself admit it plainly: she needed him. Not wanted—needed. The map stopped at the Sable Bend escarpment as if someone had torn the world in half along a fault line, and everything east of it—the drowned outpost, the survey her grandfather had been flying toward when he vanished—lived on the missing piece. Twenty years of asking archivists and old pilots had gotten her nothing but shrugs and one drunk who wept into his beer and said, *You don't want to find that place, girl.*

Coral Underhill knew where it was. She could see it in the way he'd gone still when she mentioned the escarpment's eastern face, stillness like a heron before the strike, or before being struck.

So on the fifth morning, when he came out of the hangar wiping grease from his hands, she was sitting on an oil drum outside his office door with two cups of coffee and her grandfather's map rolled in her fist.

"Captain Underhill."

He stopped. His eyes went to the map first—not to her face, not to the coffee—and that told her more than any interrogation would have.

"Miss Vale." He took the coffee anyway, because pilots always take free fuel. "You're up early."

"I've been up since three." She unrolled the map across her knees, weighting the corners with her elbows. "I want to show you something."

"I'm not a cartographer."

"No," she said. "But you fly this river like you drew it."

* * *

She walked him through it slowly, testing him. Her grandfather Elias Vale had been a survey pilot for the colonial administration back when the maps were still drawn in ink and argument. This sheet was the western half of his last flight plan, recovered from his landlady's effects after the administration declared him missing and his room cleared. It showed the river from Port Meridian up to the bend, annotated in his small, precise hand: *sandbar shifts seasonally — unreliable*, *mission bell audible 2 mi (verify)*, *outpost supply strip — status unknown, do not attempt without ground confirmation.*

"Do not attempt without ground confirmation," Ines said, tapping the last note. "Which means he intended to attempt it."

"It means he was careful," Coral said.

"It means he was flying somewhere the administration didn't want confirmed." She looked up at him. "The eastern half is gone. Torn off. Someone removed it deliberately—there are pinholes where it was once pinned flat, and the tear follows the fold line. You don't accidentally lose half a map. You destroy half a map."

Coral drank his coffee. Beyond the hangar doors the river ran brown and slow, dragging the dry season down its throat. "Maybe he tore it himself. Split the route between two sheets so losing one wouldn't lose the whole flight."

"That's exactly what a careful surveyor would do," Ines agreed. "And then he lost anyway."

Something moved behind Coral's eyes—she caught it, brief as a fish turning under the surface. Pity, maybe. Or recognition.

"You've asked around town," he said. "What did they tell you?"

"That the outpost flooded out twenty-two years ago. That there was nothing worth salvaging. That my grandfather probably put down on a bad strip in the wet season and the river took him." She folded her hands so they wouldn't shake. "What I want to know is why nobody will say the outpost's name."

The silence stretched long enough for a barge horn to sound far downriver, mournful and stupid.

"Carrow's Reach," Coral said finally.

The name landed in her chest like a dropped tool. "Say it again."

"Carrow's Reach." He set the empty cup on the drum. "It's not a secret, Miss Vale. It's just a place nobody has a reason to say out loud. There's nothing there. Foundations, maybe, at dead-low water. A bell tower if the mud's been kind. Your grandfather flew out in the late wet looking for higher ground for a strip relocation and didn't come back. The administration wrote it up as weather. Weather writes a lot of reports out here."

"And you know all this how? You've only been flying this river twenty years. He disappeared twenty-two."

For the first time since she'd met him, Coral Underhill hesitated before answering.

That hesitation was worth more than the name.

"I read the old reports when I started flying cargo," he said. "A pilot likes to know what killed the ones before him."

It was a good answer. It was even probably true. But it came a half-second late, and Ines Vale had spent six years in government map rooms learning that the half-second was where the truth lived.

* * *

She tried a different angle that afternoon. Bo, the mechanic—a woman built like a dock crane with forearms to match—was rebuilding a carburetor on the hangar floor and willing to talk to anyone who held a wrench steady while she worked.

"Your captain," Ines said, passing pliers. "How long have you known him?"

"Twelve years." Bo torqued something that clanked. "Why?"

"He ever mention Carrow's Reach?"

The wrench stopped. Bo looked up, and her face did something complicated—went carefully blank in a way that Ines recognized, because she'd just spent five days watching Coral do the same thing. They'd learned it from each other, or from the same school.

"Why would he?"

"Because I think he knows where it is. Exactly where. And I think he knows more about it than old reports."

Bo set the wrench down and wiped her hands on a rag that was mostly oil already. "Look, miss. I don't know what your business is here. But I'll tell you what I know, which isn't much, and it's this: Coral Underhill doesn't lie. He just doesn't finish sentences. There's a difference, and out here the difference keeps people alive." She picked the wrench back up. "If he hasn't finished a sentence about Carrow's Reach, there's a reason, and it's not the reason you think."

"What reason do I think?"

"You think he's hiding treasure." Bo nodded at the tarpaulined crate visible through the hangar's back bay, where Tomas was coiling line nearby and pretending not to listen. "Half the town thinks that. Salvage men dream about the drowned places. But that man—" she jerked her chin toward the office, where Coral stood silhouetted against the window, talking to Wren, who was perched on his desk swinging her legs, "—that man buries things for a living. He doesn't dig them up."

Ines turned that over all through dinner and most of the night. *Buries things.* Bo hadn't meant it as a clue. That was what made it one.

* * *

She cornered him properly on the sixth morning, at the fuel drums behind the hangar, where he went at first light to check the stores before anyone else was awake. She'd learned his schedule the way she learned terrain: patiently, from elevation.

"Before you say good morning," she said, "I want to make you a proposal."

He looked at her over the drum lid. Up close, in the gray light, he looked older than his file said—he'd shown her a license once, casually, the way honest men do, and she'd memorized the birthdate and done the arithmetic and found it didn't quite fit the face. Another half-second. Another unfinished sentence.

"I'm listening."

"My grandfather's western sheet ends at Sable Bend. Everything past it is blank paper." She pulled the map from inside her jacket, protected from the damp, and held it up between them. "But look at the annotations. He marked a bell tower. He marked a supply strip. He marked current lines around what he called 'the drowned quarter.' Captain, I've spent six years in archives. Those aren't guesses. He'd been to Carrow's Reach before. At least twice. And the eastern half of this map doesn't just show where he was going—it shows what he found."

"And you think I have it."

"I think you know what's on it."

The river filled the silence. Somewhere upstream a kingfisher rattled like a loose bolt.

"If I did," Coral said slowly, "why would I give it to you?"

"Because you haven't destroyed it." She stepped closer. "Twenty years, Captain. If there were papers in that crate you hauled out of the mud that you wanted gone, they'd be ash by now. You're a practical man—I've watched you jury-rig a fuel pump with a spoon. Practical men burn what they fear. You've kept it. Which means part of you wants it found."

His jaw tightened. For a moment he looked, not angry, but exhausted—the specific exhaustion of a man who has been holding a door shut for a very long time and can hear footsteps on the other side.

"You don't know what you're asking for," he said.

"I'm asking for my grandfather. That's all. Not treasure, not scandal, not whatever the ministry men who came through last week were sniffing after." She saw him flinch at that—so he knew about the surveyors too, of course he did, the whole town knew—and pressed harder. "Elias Vale flew into that country and never flew out. My grandmother died not knowing. My mother died not knowing. I am the last person alive whose whole life has a hole in it shaped like a man, and I am asking you to help me fill it in."

Something in her voice cracked on the last words, and she hated it, and couldn't stop it. Six years of composure, undone by a fuel drum and a man who wouldn't finish his sentences.

Coral set down the drum lid. When he spoke, his voice was quieter than she'd ever heard it.

"The eastern half of your grandfather's map," he said, "if it exists—it won't tell you what you want to know. Maps show where. They don't show what happened."

"Then give me the where, and I'll find the what myself."

"You can't go alone. The channels shift. The strips are soft. The dry season's closing the window—" He stopped himself, jaw working, furious at how much he'd revealed.

"The window," Ines repeated softly. "You know the window. You know the channels. You know the strips." She let him sit in it for a moment, then laid her cards down, all of them, because she'd calculated that the only way through this man's defenses was over the top, in daylight, with everything showing. "Here's my proposal. I have money—grant money, expedition money, enough to fund a proper salvage run. You have a boat, a crew, and knowledge of that country that no chart contains. I want to hire you to take me to Carrow's Reach. Officially, we're recovering a wreck—you're already assembling a crew for a wreck, I've heard the talk at Ferro's. Unofficially, I follow my grandfather's coordinates, and you keep us alive doing it. You don't have to tell me what's in your crate. You don't have to tell me anything you don't want to. I only need the terrain."

"And when we get there," Coral said, "and there's nothing but foundations and a bell tower and river mud—what then?"

"Then I'll have stood where he stood, and I'll go home." She held his eyes. "But we both know there's more than foundations."

He looked away, out at the river, and she watched him weigh it—watched the scales move behind his face, duty on one pan and something else on the other, something he wouldn't name. Two secrets circling each other, she thought. Hers with a hole in the middle. His with a weight. Both of them flying blind in the same weather, close enough now that their wakes crossed.

"One condition," he said at last.

"Name it."

"When we go upriver, you follow my calls. On the water, in the air, on foot—if I say turn, you turn, and you ask why after. Not before." He turned back to her, and his eyes were the flat gray-green of the river in dry season, hiding everything. "Out there, curiosity kills faster than crocodiles."

"Accepted." She stuck out her hand. "Partners?"

He took it. His grip was calloused and careful, a pilot's grip, and he held it one beat longer than a contract required.

"Partners," he said. "We leave when the river drops another hand's width. Three days, maybe four."

"Three days." She smiled, and meant it, and hated how much she meant it. "I'll be ready."

He released her hand and turned back to the fuel drums, and she walked away across the wet boards feeling the strange double pulse of triumph and dread, because she had gotten almost everything she'd asked for—

—and he had agreed too easily.

She stopped at the corner of the chandlery and looked back. He was still standing at the drums, motionless, staring at nothing, and even at this distance she could see him mouthing something to himself, over and over, like a man rehearsing a confession or a denial.

Three days, she thought. Whatever he was carrying, she'd have it out of him before they reached Sable Bend.

Behind the hangar, unseen, Coral Underhill stood a moment longer, then went inside and woke Wren with a hand on her shoulder and a question that made her sit bolt upright:

"How would you feel about a boat trip?"
