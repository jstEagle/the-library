## 9. Departure Window

The river told you first.

Long before the rain stopped falling in the high country, long before the radio men started using the word *drought* with that careful, official hush they saved for things they couldn't fix, the water itself began to confess. It pulled back from the roots of the flooded fig trees along the north bank. It exposed a shelf of red mud below the fuel dock that hadn't seen sun since the last dry season, and then another shelf above it, and then a third, each one a tidemark of what had been and would not be again for months. By the time I walked down to the strips on the morning this chapter of my life turned over, the river had dropped four feet from its flood crest, and it moved with the low, muscular mutter of something conserving its strength.

I stood at the edge of the main strip with a cup of Ferro's coffee—strong enough to strip paint, which was the point—and watched the ground lie to me.

That's what a dry season does to an airfield built on river silt. The surface looks fine. Looks like any other day of packed earth and sparse grass, hard as a road. But the water table underneath is sinking with the river, and the clay beneath the silt dries at a different rate than the crust above it, and somewhere between the two, voids open up like pockets in a coat. You can land on a strip that looks perfect and have your port wheel punch through into soft ground at forty knots and go up on your nose in front of God and everybody. Every pilot who'd worked this country longer than one season knew about the softening. We called it the strips going quiet, because that's what it felt like—the ground stopping its argument with you, going agreeable, going treacherous.

Bo was already out there, walking the main strip heel-to-toe like a man counting his own sins, prodding the surface every ten paces with a length of rebar he'd cut down for exactly this purpose. He'd drive it in, pull it out, squint at the clay clinging to the metal, and move on. He did this every morning from the first week of the drop until the rains came back. Twenty years I'd flown out of this town and I'd never once seen him skip it.

"Main strip's good for another eight days," he said when I reached him. "Maybe ten if we keep traffic light. The crosswind strip's already gone. I sank to the ankle by marker three." He held up the rebar as evidence, six inches of wet red clay clinging to it like something alive. "After that, everything heavier than a mail sack lands on the sandbar at Kilometre Forty or doesn't land at all."

Eight days. I filed the number away next to the other numbers I'd been keeping since the night I broke the seal on that chest: four feet of river drop, six days until the ministry surveyors' resupply flight was due, one ledger full of names that could burn down half the history of this country if it fell into the wrong hands—or the right ones, depending on how you felt about the truth, which was a question I'd been successfully avoiding for twenty years.

"You're doing the math," Bo said. He wasn't looking at me. He was looking at the strip, but I could feel him doing the math too, the way mechanics do, everything reduced to loads and tolerances and margins of safety.

"I'm drinking coffee."

"You're drinking coffee *and* doing math. Your jaw does this thing."

I drank the coffee. Downriver, a heron stood in the shallows where a month ago there'd been twelve feet of brown water, patient as a debt collector, waiting for the receding flood to give up whatever it had been holding.

* * *

The rumor economy of the river country ran faster than any mail plane, and by noon the news had reached me through three separate channels, none of them official. First it was Ferro, sliding a plate of rice and dried fish across the counter of the cantina and saying, too casually, that a boy had come up from the landing stage saying two government men had booked berths on the *Marisol*, the packet boat that ran the lower river on Thursdays. Then it was old Sefu at the parts counter, who heard everything worth hearing and most things that weren't, telling me the same two men had also asked about charter flights—specifically, whether anyone in town flew heavy salvage work upriver, and specifically specifically, whether anyone named Underhill still had a license.

By the third telling, from Wren herself, breathless, bursting through the hangar door with her boots unlaced and her hair full of burrs, the story had grown a file number.

"They asked at the telegraph office," she said. "The clerk told Mama Odile, and Mama Odile told me because she says I've got a face people talk to. They sent a wire this morning asking Port Authority to confirm the status of a sealed salvage claim. There's a *file*, Coral. A sealed file with a number on it." She said the word *file* the way other people said *treasure*, eyes lit up, and I felt the floor of my stomach tilt a degree, the way it does when a plane drops into air pocket you should have seen coming.

"What did Mama Odile tell them?" I asked, keeping my voice at the level of a man discussing spark plugs.

"That nobody named Underhill keeps records. She said—" Wren grinned— "she said you were illiterate and superstitious and probably smuggled cigarettes."

"Mama Odile is a national treasure."

"But it's true, isn't it? There's a file. About the chest." And there it was, the question under the question, the one she'd been circling since the night she caught me with the ledger open and I gave her a job cataloging spare parts instead of an answer. She was fourteen and sharp as broken glass and she loved me, and all three of those facts meant she wasn't going to let it go. But she was also mine, in the way that mattered more than blood, and she knew when to bank a fire instead of feeding it. She stood there with the question in her mouth, looked at my face, and swallowed it. For now.

"Wren," I said. "Go tell Bo I need the Cessna's weight and balance sheet redone. Full fuel, four souls, cargo bay loaded heavy aft. Tonight."

Her eyebrows went up. "We're going somewhere?"

"We're going somewhere."

She ran. I watched her go and thought, not for the first time, that I was a liar and a coward and that the only honest thing I'd ever built was standing in that hangar with burrs in her hair, and that someday—soon, maybe sooner than I wanted—she was going to ask her question again and I was going to have to decide what kind of man answered it.

* * *

The ministry men arrived at dusk, and they arrived wrong in every particular, the way government men always do in river country, wearing city shoes on a mud bank and carrying leather briefcases in a place where everything you own eventually grows mold.

There were two of them. The tall one was called Ostrer—I got that much off the manifest the packet boat's mate showed me, along with the fact that they'd paid cash for priority berthing—and he had the smooth, unhurried manner of a man who had never once been rained on without choosing it. The short one, Reyes, was younger, quieter, with ink stains on his fingers and the look of someone who did the actual reading while the tall one did the actual talking. They took the corner table at the cantina, ordered beer, and made no effort whatsoever to be inconspicuous, which was its own kind of message. We're here. We're watching. We can wait.

I gave them twenty minutes, then walked in like a man with nothing on his mind but a drink.

Ostrer saw me coming before I'd crossed half the room. Of course he did. He'd have had my description, my route schedule, probably my blood type. "Captain Underhill," he said, rising slightly, extending a hand that was cool and dry as paper money. "I was hoping our paths would cross. Aurel Ostrer, Ministry of Interior, Survey and Records Division. My colleague, Mr. Reyes."

"Captain," Reyes said, with a nod. His handshake was quick and he didn't hold it, and something in his face—some flicker of apology, maybe, or just discomfort—made me mark him as the more dangerous of the two. Zealots you can outrun. Men with consciences you have to out-think.

"Heard you boys are taking the packet upriver Thursday," I said, signaling Ferro for a beer I didn't want.

"The *Marisol*, yes." Ostrer smiled. "Though we understand the upper river becomes unreliable as the season turns. Shallows, sandbars, the strips going soft. A man could get stranded up there for weeks if he mistimed his departure." He let that sit in the smoky air between us like bait on a hook. "A man with cargo to move might want to leave early. Before the window closes."

"And what cargo would that be?"

"Oh, I imagine the usual. Salvage. Rivermen are always pulling something out of the water this time of year." He turned his glass a quarter turn on the table, precise as a man adjusting an instrument. "You know, Captain, there's a regulation—old one, hardly ever invoked—that requires notification of the Ministry when significant historical materials are recovered from navigable waters. Artifacts. Documents. That sort of thing. Most people don't know it exists. Most people, frankly, we'd never bother. But every so often something comes up that has archival value beyond its weight." His eyes were pleasant and absolutely empty. "You'd be amazed what the floods cough up. And what the low water uncovers."

The beer arrived. I drank some of it. Somewhere behind the bar Ferro was very carefully not listening, which meant he was memorizing every word.

"River gives and river takes," I said. "Mostly takes. Anything it gives me usually isn't worth the paperwork."

"No," Ostrer agreed pleasantly. "Usually not." He finished his beer, set coins on the table with a small precise click, and stood. "Thursday, then, Captain. Enjoy the season. It goes quickly, doesn't it? The dry season. One moment the river's full and generous, and the next—" he snapped his fingers softly— "everything hidden is standing out in the open, wondering how it got there."

They went upstairs to the rooms they'd taken facing the strips. Reyes glanced back once, at the door, at me, at nothing, before the stairwell swallowed him.

Ferro appeared at my elbow with a rag over his shoulder. "Charming fellow," he murmured. "I liked the part where he threatened you with weather."

"He didn't threaten me."

"Coral. I run a cantina in a town with eleven buildings. Threatening is ninety percent of my entertainment, and I know it when I hear it." He wiped a glass that didn't need wiping. "So. Are you leaving before Thursday?"

I looked at the window, where the last light was going copper on the water, and at the dark line of the strips beyond, and at the hangar where a girl was redoing a weight and balance sheet for a load I hadn't fully admitted to yet, and at the river, dropping, always dropping, four feet down and falling, uncovering shelf after shelf of red mud and everything the flood had promised to keep.

"Ferro," I said, "how fast can you pack me food for six?"

* * *

We left Wednesday, an hour before dawn, and I'll say this for the crew Bo and I assembled in one frantic evening: they moved like they'd done it before, though half of them hadn't. Tomas had the dive gear crated and lashed by moonlight. Ferro showed up at the hangar with provisions packed in oilcloth against the damp, plus a sack of limes "for morale," plus, unasked, a second sack of the cheap black tobacco Bo smoked, which told me Ferro understood exactly what kind of trip this was going to be. Bo torqued the last of the engine baffles at 4:40, wiped his hands, and said only, "Main strip'll hold us. Barely. Don't brake hard past the midpoint."

Wren was in the cargo bay before anyone thought to stop her, strapped into the jump seat with her chin up and her arms crossed, radiating the specific defiance of a person who has decided that permission is a technicality.

"This is a salvage run," I told her. "It's boring. It's mud and mosquitoes and Tomas complaining about his back."

"It's a salvage run you're doing at midnight to beat two government men onto the river," she said. "Boring trips don't leave at midnight."

I opened my mouth to build the lie and found, to my surprise, that the lie wouldn't come—not to her, not this time, not with the whole dark sky waiting. So I gave her the truth sideways, which is the family dialect. "There's a thing I have to move before someone else finds it. And there are people it belongs to, even if they don't know it yet. And I need someone smart keeping the log and working the radio, because everyone else on this plane is either old, hungover, or me." I buckled the cargo net down over the chest, wrapped in tarpaulin at the bottom of the stack, looking for all the world like engine parts. "That's all you get. For now."

She studied me for a long moment, and I watched her weigh it—the same weighing she'd been doing since the night of the ledger—and then she nodded once, sharp, and said, "For now," in a tone that put a date on it.

Ines Vale came down the strip path at 5:15 with her half-map rolled in a bamboo tube and a duffel over one shoulder, moving fast, having clearly heard through the same grapevine that moves everything in this town except official information. She didn't ask if there was room. She stood at the cabin door with the dawn coming up gray-gold behind her and said, "Your mechanic told me you're flying upriver today instead of Thursday, and that the ministry men are on the packet tomorrow, and that you're loading heavy aft like a man running from something." Her eyes went past me into the bay, quick and cartographer-precise, cataloguing crates and crew and one suspiciously rectangular shape under canvas. "I'm coming. I have half a map, Captain, and I'm starting to think you have the other half."

"Ines—"

"Or throw me off," she said, and climbed aboard, and sat down beside Wren, and started checking the radio frequencies like she'd been born in the right seat.

Bo swung up into the cockpit beside me, pulled his harness tight, and looked out at the strips—gray in the pre-dawn, soft-going-hard, the whole airfield holding its breath between seasons. "Window's closing," he said. Not a question. A reading of instruments.

I ran up the engine, and the sound filled the hangar and rolled out across the dropping river, and somewhere up the hill two men in city shoes slept through the sound of their quarry leaving, or didn't sleep, and lay listening, and decided the packet on Thursday would be soon enough.

The strip held. Barely, like Bo said—we were light on the wheels by the midpoint, and I lifted off over the fig trees with the first true light hitting the water, and the river ran below us, low and honest and giving up its dead, and behind us the town shrank to a scatter of roofs and smoke, and ahead of us the drowned outpost waited with its bell and its foundations and its names, and I flew north with a full plane, a closing window, and the growing certainty that the truth I'd spent twenty years outrunning had finally learned to fly.
