## 10. Downriver

The river at Sable Bend was the color of strong tea left out overnight, and it smelled like everything the flood had taken and not yet given back—mud, rot, diesel, and underneath it all something green and patient that I would come to think of as the river's own breath.

We loaded the boat in the last hour of dark. Bo backed the truck down the mud ramp with the headlights off, because Coral said lights on the water at night drew questions, and questions were cargo he refused to carry. I stood in the shallows passing crates hand to hand—Tomas taking them from me, Ferro stacking them below, Wren supposedly asleep back at the house—and I remember thinking that I had never in my life worked beside people who moved like this, without instruction, without complaint, as if loading a boat before dawn were a language they'd all been raised speaking.

Coral counted crates with a pencil stub behind his ear and didn't look at me once. He hadn't looked at me properly since the strip, since I'd told him about my grandfather's half-map and watched him decide—visibly, the way a man decides whether to swallow or spit—not to tell me whatever it was sitting behind his eyes.

"Sixteen," he said. "That's fuel, water, food for nine days if we're careful, five if Ferro cooks like he means it."

"I always cook like I mean it," Ferro said from the dark.

"That's what worries me."

I climbed aboard. The boat was a long-bellied river freighter gone soft with age, its cabin a low wooden box with windows fogged by salt air, its deck scarred by twenty years of crates and boots. Someone had painted a name on the stern in letters now mostly flaked away: *MARGUERITE*. I ran my thumb over the M and felt absurdly pleased about it, though I couldn't have said why.

"She was named before me," Coral said, appearing at my shoulder with the silence of a man who had spent his life not being heard coming. "The boat. Before you ask."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to ask."

I was. I turned and found him closer than I expected, close enough that I could smell engine oil and woodsmoke and, beneath both, soap—the ordinary human smell of him, which unsettled me more than any of his silences had. In the gray light his face was all planes and weather, and there was a crease between his brows that deepened when he looked at me, as though looking at me were work.

"The survey," he said. "Your grandfather's. You've shown me half of it. What does the other half show?"

"That's what we're going upriver to find out."

"Ines." He said my name carefully, like a word in a language he was still learning. "When we get where we're going, some of what's there isn't going to be what you want it to be. Rivers keep things badly. They keep them wet, and they keep them broken, and they give them back wrong."

"And yet here you are," I said, "taking me to look."

He held my gaze for three seconds—I counted; I'm a cartographer, counting is what I do—and then he stepped past me to the wheel and started the engine, and the conversation closed like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

Wren came out of the hold an hour after dawn, when the banks had opened into reed marsh and the first herons stood in the shallows like sentries who had forgotten their orders.

She climbed through the hatch with straw in her hair and Ferro's spare jacket around her shoulders, and she stood on the deck in the strengthening light with her chin up, daring us to object.

Coral objected. At length. I stood at the rail pretending to study the channel markers and listened to the two of them go at each other in the shorthand of people who have argued before and will argue again, and what struck me wasn't the anger—it was the tenderness under it, so poorly disguised on both sides that it might as well have been painted on.

"You're turning this boat around," Coral said.

"No, I'm not."

"Wren—"

"You need someone keeping the log. Bo's got engines, Tomas has diving, Ferro has food, and Ines has maps. Nobody has logs." She crossed her arms. "Also I already ate two days of Ferro's rations, so turning around doesn't un-eat them."

"That's not how rationing works."

"It's exactly how rationing works."

He looked at her for a long moment, and I saw the exact instant he lost—the softening around his mouth, the surrender he tried to hide by turning it into a scowl. "Sable Bend and back. You sleep in the cabin. You wear the vest on open water."

"I hate the vest."

"The vest or the turn-around."

She put on the vest. She put it on with the theatrical suffering of a girl who knew precisely how much leverage she had, and then she came and sat next to me at the rail with her knees drawn up, and after a while she said, without looking at me: "He'll tell you eventually. Whatever it is. He tells things eventually, like a slow leak."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Sure," said Wren, who was eleven going on forty, and fell asleep against my arm within ten minutes, trusting me completely, which is a thing children do that adults have forgotten how to survive.

* * *

We made the first night at a mooring called Halfway Tree—a fig grown enormous out of a mound of old ballast stone, its roots holding the bank together the way good stories hold families together, invisibly and under strain.

Ferro cooked rice with dried fish and something sharp and green he wouldn't name, and we ate on deck while the mosquitoes rose off the water in columns and the frogs started their evening argument. Bo told a story about an engine he'd rebuilt in a monsoon using a spoon. Tomas told one about a crocodile that had followed his dive boat for three days out of what he insisted was loneliness and everyone else insisted was appetite. And Coral—

Coral told nothing. He ate, he listened, he laughed once, briefly, at something Bo said, and the laugh transformed his whole face for a second before he folded it away again like a letter he'd read by mistake.

But afterward, when the others had turned in and the fire had burned down to a red eye, he came and sat near me at the rail, and I decided to spend some of the trust Wren had handed me like loose change.

"My grandfather flew survey for the colonial administration," I said, to the water rather than to him. It was easier to say things to water. "Everyone thinks cartographers sit in offices. He was in the air most of his working life. Photographed half this country from a plane with an open cockpit and a camera bolted to the floor." I turned the half-map over in my hands. I'd been carrying it in an oilcloth sleeve since I was nineteen. "This is the northern sheet of his last run. The southern sheet never came back. Neither did he. The administration wrote *lost with aircraft* and paid my grandmother a sum so small it was almost an insult, and the file was sealed, and everyone agreed to stop asking."

"And you didn't agree."

"I was four." I looked at him. Firelight did strange generous things to his face. "My grandmother asked until she died. Then I inherited the asking."

He was quiet a long time. A fish broke the surface somewhere out in the dark, a sound like a hand slapping water.

"What was his name?" Coral asked.

"Elias Vale."

Something moved across his face—fast, gone before I could name it, a shadow crossing a field. If I hadn't been watching I'd have missed it entirely. Later, much later, I would understand that I had missed nothing at all, that I had seen it perfectly and simply failed to translate it. That night I read it as sympathy, and sympathy it may even have been, in part.

"Vale," he repeated. "It's a good name for a mapmaker."

"Why?"

"Because a vale is a low place between hills," he said. "A place rivers gather." He stood, brushing off his trousers. "Get some sleep. We make the narrows tomorrow, and the narrows don't forgive early mistakes."

"Coral." He paused. "You know this country better than anyone I've met. Better than the charts, even. How?"

For a moment I thought he wouldn't answer. Then he said, "I've been flying it a long time," and went aft, and the lie—if it was a lie—sat between us on the deck like a third passenger neither of us acknowledged.

* * *

The narrows took all of the second day. The river pinched between bluffs where the current ran fast and brown and muscular, and Coral steered us through standing at the wheel with his whole body reading the water, weight shifting before each eddy as if he could feel the river thinking. Twice he killed the engine and let us drift while he studied something ahead—a submerged snag, a shift in the current's color—and twice his judgment proved exact. Bo watched him with the expression of a craftsman watching another craftsman work, and finally said to me, quietly, "Twenty years I've flown with him. Never seen him take a bad line. Not once."

"Where did he learn the river?"

Bo shrugged. "He was just always good at it. You know how some men are with country? Like they were poured into it instead of born?" He wiped his hands on a rag that had been black longer than it had been cloth. "He doesn't talk about before. None of us push. You'll figure out why, or you won't."

*That* was the thing about Coral Underhill's crew, I was learning. They loved him in the specific way you love a man who has carried you through weather, and they trusted him in the specific way you trust a lock you've never seen opened—and both loves had a wall built into them, load-bearing, invisible, and no one seemed to wonder what the wall was for except me, and lately, sometimes, in the dark, Wren.

* * *

On the fourth day we left the boat.

Tomas knew a tributary mouth—a slot in the reeds you'd miss at fifty meters—and above it, a path the flood had half-erased, climbing through bamboo toward a ridge line that matched, feature for feature, the western edge of my grandfather's half-sheet. We walked single file, Coral first cutting trail with a machete he handled like an extension of his arm, Wren in the middle where he'd put her, me behind her, the others strung out back with the dive gear and the crates.

By midday the heat was a physical weight. We stopped at a stream and drank, and Wren lay flat on her back in the shallows fully dressed, vest and all, and announced that she lived here now.

And Coral—hot, tired, machete over his shoulder—laughed. Really laughed, head back, helpless, and for a moment the wall wasn't there at all, and I saw the man the wall was built around, and I wanted, with a suddenness that frightened me, to know everything about him. Where he'd learned the river. What he'd been before he was a pilot. Why a man who was so good at finding things had arranged his entire life around not being found.

He caught me watching him. The laugh died down but the warmth stayed in his eyes a beat longer than usual, and he crossed the stream and crouched by Wren and said, "Up, fish-girl. Ridge by four or we camp in leech country."

"There are leeches?"

"There are always leeches."

She scrambled up. He offered me a hand next, and I took it, and his grip was dry and strong and brief, and he let go a half-second later than he needed to, and neither of us mentioned it, and I thought about it for the rest of the afternoon.

* * *

We camped that night on the ridge itself, on a shelf of ground where the bamboo opened onto a view downriver—all the way back, faintly, to the far-off glitter of the main channel, and forward into country no chart I owned described. My grandfather's half-map ended at a fold-line maybe six kilometers north of where we sat. Beyond it, the paper simply stopped, and the world kept going.

I spread the map on a crate and weighted the corners with stones, and Coral came and stood over it, and this time he didn't ask questions. This time he studied it the way a man studies his own handwriting—head tilted, lips slightly parted, remembering.

"This bend," he said, touching a curve of blue ink. "There's a rock bar under it now. Wasn't on any chart I ever bought."

"You've been here." It wasn't a question.

"Everyone's been everywhere, Ines. It's a small country."

"Not here. Not this far up, not off every chart." I looked up at him. The firelight and the starlight mixed on his face. "You said you fly cargo and gossip. There's no cargo up here. There's no one to gossip about."

For a long moment he said nothing, and the river talked instead, far below, patient and endless. Then he reached out and touched the map again, at the very edge of the fold, where my grandfather's ink faded into blankness.

"Tomorrow," he said quietly, "we cross this line. And I need you to hear me on this. What's past it—some of it belongs to other people. Families. Living ones. They've kept their heads down a long time, and they've earned the right to keep them down, and whatever we find, we find it gently. Can you promise me that?"

"Gently," I repeated. "You're asking a cartographer to map gently."

"I'm asking you to care more about the living than the record. Just for a few days. Just until we know what we're carrying."

And because I liked him—because I was already, stupidly, inconveniently halfway to loving him, and because his hand was still resting on the edge of my grandfather's map as though anchoring it to the earth—I said yes.

I said yes knowing he was withholding something. I told myself it was grief, or privacy, or the ordinary damage of a man who'd lived alone too long. I told myself the truth would come out on its own schedule, the way truth does, slowly, like water rising.

I was right about the water. Everything else, I had backwards.

Below us, far downriver, a light appeared on the black water—two lights, actually, running without running-lights discipline, moving fast against the current, heading the same direction we were headed. Coral saw them at the same moment I did. His whole body changed, the way a plane changes when its pilot's hands tighten.

"Ministry boat," he said, almost to himself. Then, louder, waking the camp: "Bo. Kill that fire. Everybody up. We move at first light—and this time, nobody waits for anybody."
