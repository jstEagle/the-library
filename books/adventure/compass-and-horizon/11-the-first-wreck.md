## 11. The First Wreck

Tomas had been diving the river for nineteen years, and he could tell you this much: every wreck has a voice. You hear it through the water before you ever see it—a hum in your teeth, a pressure against your ears, like something down there still trying to say its name. The good wrecks, the ones full of brass and history, speak clearly. The bad ones, the ones that took people, speak in a lower register. This one spoke low.

He floated on his back beside the boat and let the current argue with him while he listened to it.

The *Marta's Pride* sat at anchor off a bend called Kilometre Forty by the surveyors and nothing at all by everyone else, because there was no kilometre marker anymore and hadn't been for sixty years. The river here ran wide and brown-green, opaque as tea left standing, and somewhere under that opacity lay the stern section of a paddle steamer that had burned and sunk in the year of Tomas's grandfather's wedding. Bo had found it on the depth sounder two days ago—a shadow with a spine—and Coral had looked at the readout for a long time before saying, quietly, "That'll do."

Tomas had understood immediately what *that'll do* meant. It meant: we need the ministry men behind us to believe we are doing exactly what we said we would do, which is salvage a wreck, and not what we are actually doing, which is something Coral will not name. It meant: go down there, come up with something interesting enough to justify three more days at anchor, and don't ask questions with your mouth or your eyes.

It was a fair trade, as trades went. Coral paid in cash, never asked about the money Tomas sent downriver to his sister's kids, and had once flown a doctor ninety miles through weather that grounded the mail plane for a week because Ferro's cousin's baby was turning blue. A man like that earned a certain amount of diving without questions.

But the river didn't care about debts between men. The river only cared about the river.

"Tomas." Wren's voice, from the bow, where she'd appointed herself line-tender and signal-watcher with the seriousness of a girl who had decided her job was the most important one aboard. She held the rope in both fists, wrapped twice around her palms the way he'd taught her. "You going down or going to sleep?"

"Going down," he said. "Watch the line. Three pulls means I'm coming up slow. Six fast means haul me up and don't be gentle about it."

"I know." She rolled her eyes, thirteen years old and already fluent in the language of men who thought they were teaching her things she already knew. "Six fast. Don't be gentle."

Coral stood at the wheel, arms folded, watching the water the way he always watched it—like it owed him an explanation. "Current's slack," he said. "You've got maybe forty minutes of decent light down there. Don't be a hero. There's nothing in that hull worth your lungs."

"There might be a bell," Tomas said, and saw something flicker across Coral's face—quick, gone, like a fish turning under the surface. Interesting, Tomas thought, and filed it away with all the other small unexplained things he'd filed since leaving home: the chest lashed under tarpaulin in the hold that nobody opened; the way Coral checked the mirror aft every ten minutes without appearing to; the half-map Ines carried folded into her shirt pocket like a letter she was afraid to finish reading.

He spat over the side, pulled his mask down, and went under.

* * *

The world closed over him and became green silence.

Twenty feet down the light turned the color of old bottle glass. Thirty feet and it was dusk. He followed the shot line hand over hand, equalizing, watching his bubbles climb away from him like a string of silver birds released from a cage, and at thirty-eight feet the steamer rose out of the murk to meet him.

She was bigger than the sounder had suggested. The stern wheel hung collapsed against the hull like a broken fan, its paddles fanned out into the silt, each slat furred with sixty years of grey sediment. The hull itself listed hard to port, buried to the rub rail on that side, and the fire damage showed even now—the wood above the waterline charred to the texture of charcoal briquettes, soft when he pressed a glove against it. Fish had colonized the deckhouse. A catfish the length of his forearm regarded him from the doorway of what had probably been the purser's office with the flat indifference of a landlord.

Tomas swam the length of what remained, maybe eighty feet of hull, the rest of the ship either burned to ash or carried off downstream in some flood year before he was born. He worked methodically, the way you learn to work when the water is your office: check the structure first, then the cargo spaces, then the places where people keep things they value. The engine room was a cathedral of rusted iron, beautiful in the way ruins are beautiful, worthless in the way ruins are worthless. The cargo hold amidships had already been emptied—either by the fire's crew or by salvagers decades ago. Silt had drifted in and set like concrete.

But the purser's safe—that was another matter. Riverboats carried safes. Payrolls, passenger valuables, company papers. And safes were heavy, which meant they sank straight down instead of wandering, and they were built to keep water out, which meant that sometimes, if you were lucky, they kept time out too.

He found it wedged beneath the collapsed deckhouse frame, a squat iron box furred with the same grey fur as everything else, its door hanging open at an angle that told him someone had beaten him to it by fifty years or more. Empty, then. Probably empty. But inside the door, when he swung it, he found the thing that made his heart do a strange double-beat against his ribs:

A ledger. Water-swollen, its leather cover gone soft as skin, but whole. And beneath it, tangled in the roots of something that had once been a canvas bag, a bundle of papers tied with wire, and a brass object he couldn't identify at first—cylindrical, engraved, until he turned it in his glove and the light caught letters around its rim.

A bell. A hand bell, small, the kind they rang on riverboats to mark departures. The engraving was deep enough to have survived the water. He couldn't read it through the murk and the muck, but he could feel the letters under his thumb, deliberate, machine-cut, made to last.

Made to last. Like everything else the dead left behind.

His air gauge said he had twenty minutes. His head said take the ledger and the papers and leave the bell, it's brass, it's heavy, it's not worth the swim. But his hands had already closed around it, and there is a particular stupidity that lives in the hands of men who work the water, a greed that isn't about money—it's about bringing something up. Proof. A story. Something to put in Ferro's galley and watch the whole crew crowd around.

He stuffed the ledger and papers into his mesh bag, clipped the bell to his belt, and started up the shot line.

That was when the deckhouse frame moved.

* * *

Later—he would think about this later, in the dark of his bunk, in the weeks after, every time he closed his eyes—he would reconstruct it precisely. The frame had been resting on the safe. Fifty years of equilibrium, silt settling, current scouring, the whole wreck finding its balance the way wrecks do, the way grief does, the way secrets do. And he had swung the safe door open, and the door had levered against the frame, and the frame—rotted through, charred, holding nothing but habit—had let go.

Not all at once. That was the horror of it. It came down slowly, a wall of black timber tilting through the water with terrible patience, and Tomas had time to see it coming and no time at all to get clear. It caught his left leg below the knee and drove him down into the silt, and the impact knocked his regulator out of his mouth.

The river poured in.

Cold, brown, tasting of iron and rot. He got the regulator back on the second try—hands shaking, breath rasping—and the air came, sweet and mechanical, and he lay pinned in the dark under a beam that had killed men before him, probably, in the burning, and thought very clearly: *six pulls. Don't be gentle.*

His free hand found the signal line. He pulled it six times, fast, and felt the tension change as Wren took it up, felt the boat's weight come onto the rope, and then felt—nothing. The line went slack.

No. Not slack. *Cut.* No—frayed, parting strand by strand against the broken edge of the deckhouse where the collapse had dragged it across jagged timber. He could feel the vibration of the engine above, the pull, the hope, and he could feel the rope dying in his fist one fiber at a time.

The gauge said fourteen minutes of air. The beam weighed, he guessed, four hundred pounds, pinning him at an angle across a deck that sloped into deeper water. His dive knife was on his right hip. His right arm was free.

Tomas did what river divers do. He stopped thinking about the surface. The surface was a rumor. The surface was somebody else's country. There was only the beam, and the knife, and the next breath.

He cut the wreck the way he'd learned to cut snags off prop lines: short strokes, angled, working the deepest fibers first. The charred wood was soft and the knife went through it almost insultingly easily—but underneath the char was heartwood, dense, waterlogged, stubborn as sin. Dust clouded the water around him until he was cutting blind, working by feel, by the geometry of the beam against his leg. Somewhere above, the engine note changed—Coral swinging the boat, repositioning, trying to find an angle on the line. Smart. Hopeless, but smart.

Ten minutes of air. The knife hit metal—a spike, a nail, something—and skated, and he nearly lost his grip on it, and the panic came up his throat like floodwater. He made himself breathe. Four counts in, six counts out, the rhythm Ferro used for kneading bread, the rhythm Wren used when she was scared of thunder and pretending not to be. Breathe like a man who intends to keep breathing.

Eight minutes. The beam groaned. Shifted. Settled worse.

And then, through the brown murk, a shape. A second diver, fins first, descending fast and graceless—no technique at all, all hurry, bubbles boiling off him like he was venting steam. Bo. Of course. Bo, who couldn't swim properly until Tomas taught him at sixteen, Bo who was afraid of open water and would deny it with his last breath, Bo coming down anyway with a pry bar in one fist and no plan except the obvious one.

Bo jammed the bar under the beam, braced his shoulders against the hull, and heaved. The beam lifted an inch. Two inches. Enough. Tomas dragged his leg free—something tore in the calf, a hot bright line of pain—and the two of them came apart from the wreck together, kicking for the light, for the green, for the silver ceiling of the world.

They broke the surface gasping, and the sky had never looked so improbably, ridiculously blue.

* * *

They hauled him over the gunwale and Ferro had a blanket around him before he'd finished coughing, and Wren—Wren had both hands pressed over her mouth, and her eyes were wet, and she said, "The line broke, the line broke, I had it, I *had* it—" and Coral said, quietly, "I know you did," and that was all, but the way he said it settled her like an anchor.

Then Coral crouched in front of Tomas, and his face was the face Tomas had seen once before, years ago, on a man whose son had gone under at the confluence and stayed there. Not panic. Something past panic. The face of a man recalculating everything.

"Talk," Coral said.

"Leg's cut. Not the bone—I can flex it." He demonstrated, and hissed, and Ferro clucked and started cleaning the gash with boiled water and the good rum, the medicinal rum, which was how Tomas knew it was serious. "Beam came down when I opened the safe. Bo pulled me out." He looked at Bo, dripping and gray-faced and furious with relief, pretending to fuss with the dive gear. "I owe him."

"You owe him nothing," Bo said roughly. "You owe me a new pry bar. It bent."

"The safe?"

Ferro's hands paused. Coral's eyes didn't move from Tomas's face, and Tomas understood suddenly that the question wasn't really about the safe. It was about the story. What story was Tomas going to tell? Here, on the deck, with Wren listening and the ministry boat—no, wait, the ministry boat was a day behind them, that was the whole point of anchoring here, buying time—

"The safe was already opened," Tomas said. "Somebody got there decades ago. But there was a ledger inside the door, and papers, and—" he reached for the mesh bag, which Ferro had already brought aboard, and lifted out the hand bell.

The brass caught the afternoon sun and threw it back, warm as a coin. Everyone leaned in. Even Coral leaned in, and Tomas watched him read the engraving and watched the color leave his face by degrees, like water draining.

Ines came out of the cabin where she'd been charting, drawn by the commotion, and Coral moved—not obviously, just a half-step, angling himself so the bell was less visible—and failed, because Ines was already there, already looking, and her hand rose toward the engraving and stopped in the air an inch away, the way you stop before touching something holy or something wounded.

"That's a foundry mark," she said. Her voice had gone strange. Careful. "Vale et Fils. That's my family's foundry. That mark is my grandfather's."

Nobody said anything. The river said it for them, slapping against the hull, patient, saying the low-voiced thing it had been saying all day.

"It's a common pattern," Coral said finally, and his voice was level, and it was the best lie Tomas had ever heard him tell, smooth as varnish, and it fooled absolutely no one, least of all Ines, whose eyes went from the bell to Coral and stayed there like a compass needle finding north. "Riverboats carried bells from everywhere. It doesn't mean anything."

"No," Ines said softly. "Of course not."

Ferro bandaged Tomas's leg in silence. Wren coiled the ruined signal line, strand by strand, with the concentration of someone committing a lesson to memory. Bo sat down heavily and didn't pretend to work anymore.

And Tomas, wrapped in his blanket with the rum warming his blood and the torn muscle throbbing in time with his pulse, thought about the beam. About how it hadn't fallen until he touched the safe. About how the wreck had held its balance for sixty years and given way the moment a living hand reached in.

The river keeps what it keeps, his grandmother used to say, and it charges interest on whatever you borrow back.

Below the keel, in the green dark, the steamer settled a little further into the mud, and somewhere in the flooded dark of the purser's office the catfish resumed its tenancy, and the wreck went on saying its low-voiced thing to anyone who would listen.

That night the storm front came down out of the west earlier than any forecast promised, and Coral ordered the anchor doubled and the radio antenna rigged, and Ines carried a tin box of letters to the fire in the galley and asked, in a voice that permitted no refusal, whether anyone wanted to hear what her grandfather wrote the year before he disappeared.

Tomas, leg propped on a crate, watched Coral's face across the flames.

"Yes," Coral said.
