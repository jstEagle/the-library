## 12. Night Radio

The storm found them an hour after dark, the way storms do in that country—not arriving so much as assembling itself out of the heat, thunder stacking up over the western hills like cargo nobody had claimed. We had made camp on a shelf of clay above a side channel, the boat pulled up and tied with three lines because Tomas didn't trust two, and by the time the first rain hit we were all under the tarp Ferro had rigged between two poles, listening to the world turn to water.

I had never heard rain like it. Not weather—weather you could wait out. This was a sound with weight, a sound that made the river beneath us change its mind about which way it flowed.

"Grounded," Bo said, with the satisfaction of a man who enjoyed being right about bad things. "Nobody flies in this. Nobody boats in this either, if they've got any sense."

"Then we're all equally senseless," Coral said, "since we're already here."

He said it mildly, crouched at the edge of the tarp checking the tie-offs, and I watched him work in the lightning-flash stroboscope of the storm: hands sure, movements economical, a man who had done this ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more. He didn't look at me. He had mostly stopped looking at me since the night at Sable Bend, or rather—he looked, but only when he thought I wouldn't catch him, and when I caught him he turned to some task as if the task had been calling his name.

Wren was asleep against Ferro's duffel, her mouth open, one hand still curled around the tin cup she'd refused to surrender at dinner. The girl slept like the dead anywhere, anytime, a talent I envied. The rest of us sat in the lamplight while the storm leaned on us, and the radio—the little battery set Bo kept wrapped in oilcloth for reasons he'd never explained—hissed and spat static through the wet air.

"Try the forecast," Coral said.

Bo unwrapped the set, thumbed it on, and worked the dial through a swamp of static until a voice surfaced, thin and tinny, reading barometric numbers from a station so far away it sounded like it was broadcasting from the moon. Storm cell moving east-southeast. Heavy rain through morning. Small craft advised off the main channel. Advised, Bo repeated, like it was funny. As if the river took advice.

The voice faded back into static. And then, just before Bo killed the set, another signal bled through underneath—a second station, weaker, closer, speaking Spanish in a low urgent register, words half-drowned. I caught *río*, caught *no pasar*, caught something that might have been a place name.

Coral's head came up. "Turn it back."

Bo turned it back. The signal swam, dissolved, reassembled. A man's voice, tired, repeating something—a notice, a position report?—and then it cut clean off, whether by distance or by hand none of us could say.

"Ministry band," Coral said quietly.

"You can tell that from three words?" I asked.

"I can tell that from the boredom." He almost smiled. "Government men read the weather like it's a eulogy." He nodded at the rain hammering the tarp. "If they're on the water tonight, they're hating their lives. If they waited it out somewhere, they'll move at first light."

"And we?"

"We move too. Earlier." He said it to Bo, not to me, and something in my chest tightened—not jealousy exactly, though there was some of that, but the older, colder feeling of being managed. Of being told the shape of the truth instead of the truth itself.

Bo killed the radio. The storm filled the silence it left behind.

* * *

We kept a watch rotation anyway, more from habit than need, and my shift came at midnight, paired—as Coral arranged it, always arranging it—with Ferro, who slept sitting up and could be woken by saying his name twice. But Ferro, once woken, established immediately that he intended to sleep again, and so I sat alone under the tarp's dripping lip with the lantern turned low, watching lightning walk along the far ridge like something searching for a door.

That was when I took out the letters.

I carried them the way other people carry knives—in an inner pocket, close to the body, where a sudden movement couldn't take them from me. Eleven letters, four photographs, and half a map drawn in ink gone brown as old blood. My grandfather's last mail home, sent over three years from survey camps upriver, each envelope stamped and franked and addressed in his small precise hand to my grandmother, who had kept every one in a biscuit tin until the day she died, and then I had kept them, and now they had crossed an ocean and a mountain range and half a continent to be here, in the rain, in the country where he wrote them and did not come home from.

I hadn't planned to read them aloud. That happened the way most true things happen—accidentally, and because someone was listening.

Coral wasn't asleep. I knew it without looking; he had a way of not sleeping that you could feel across a camp, a held-breath quality to the darkness where he lay. And maybe it was the storm stripping away everything decorative, or maybe it was eleven days of guarded conversation wearing me down to the quick, but I unfolded the first letter and began, quietly, to read it into the rain.

*My dear Rosa—*

*The river here is the color of strong tea and moves like it has somewhere important to be. I have been three weeks establishing the base line and the insects have established themselves upon me. Tell your mother the quinine works. Tell your father I have not fallen in the water yet, which he will agree is a personal record.*

*The people upriver are extraordinary navigators. I mean to learn from them before I presume to map anything. A man who draws a river he does not understand is not a cartographer, he is a liar with good penmanship.*

I laughed—actually laughed, alone at midnight—and heard the small sound get swallowed by the storm, and kept going.

*They have a word for the dry channels. It translates roughly as "the river's memory"—the paths the water remembers even when it has forgotten to fill them. I think of your father, who remembers everything and forgives selectively.*

I read four letters that night. The jokes got shorter as the years in them advanced. The surveys got longer, more detailed, more obsessive—depths, channels, villages, and then, in the fifth year, a new category of notation that appeared without explanation: small crosses beside certain names, and beside the crosses, dates.

By the seventh letter he had stopped performing cheerfulness altogether.

*Rosa. Something is wrong here. I am not permitted to write what I know, which is itself information. The outpost at [the ink had bloomed; the name was gone] has been struck from the shipping schedules. I asked why and was told it was a clerical matter. Clerical matters do not require soldiers.*

*I have begun keeping my own record. If anything happens to me, it is in the bell.*

I stopped. Rain on canvas. Lightning. The river talking to itself in the dark.

"In the bell," I said aloud, to no one, and heard my own voice come out strange.

Behind me, in the dark past the lantern's reach, Coral shifted his weight. Just slightly. The kind of movement a man makes when he has been still too long and his body betrays him—or when a sentence has landed somewhere he didn't want it to land.

I should have turned around. I didn't, not yet. I opened the eighth letter instead, the last one, dated six weeks before the date my grandmother always gave when she spoke of him, which was never, if she could help it.

*Rosa—*

*Forgive the short letter. Hands are watched now. I have sent the survey south in pieces, disguised as routine filings, and I trust the ministry's own boredom to bury it safely. There are forty-one names I cannot send. They live in my head and in God's, and soon, I hope, in stone.*

*Tell the children the river story. You know the one. About how the water keeps what it is given and gives back what it keeps. I used to think it was a fable. It is a procedure.*

*All my love, longer than the river. —Emil*

I sat with the paper trembling very slightly in my fingers, and the storm went on, indifferent, enormous, and I understood that I had just read my grandfather's death warrant aloud into the dark, and that the man lying ten feet behind me had heard every word, and that he had known—had known something, had known enough—since the night at Sable Bend, since the graves, since the moment Wren asked her question and the whole crew looked anywhere but at each other.

"Coral," I said.

A pause long enough to count raindrops in.

"You're awake."

Another pause. Then, quietly: "Yes."

I turned. He was sitting up now, blanket around his shoulders, face half-lit and half-not, and he looked—this was the thing I couldn't stop noticing, the thing that made the anger complicated—he looked like a man standing in a doorway between rooms, unable to commit to either.

"How long have you known about the outpost?" I asked. "Not guessed. Known."

The lightning chose that moment to flood the whole sky white, and in that flat merciless light I saw his face do something I had never seen it do: fail to decide. For one full second he was going to answer me. I watched the answer form. And then the light died, and with it the answer, and what he said instead was:

"It's late, Ines."

"Don't." My voice cracked on it, which infuriated me. "Don't do that. Don't manage me. You've been doing it since the airstrip—deciding what I can carry, steering me like weather. I just read you a man's last letter. Forty-one names, Coral. He hid them in a bell. And you knew there was a bell down there, didn't you. At the site. You knew what we'd find before we ever launched this boat."

He didn't deny it. That was the worst part. He sat there in the dark and let the accusation stand, and the rain came down, and somewhere out on the flooded channel a branch went by, grinding against the current, gone.

"What I know," he said finally, slowly, like a man walking on river ice, "is that there are families upriver who have survived twenty years precisely because no one dug this up. Not the government—they'd bury it deeper. Not the papers—they'd make it a spectacle. And not—" he stopped.

"Not me," I said.

"You I'm less sure about," he said, and there was something raw under the dryness, something that sounded almost like a confession trying to get out sideways. "You I keep underestimating. It hasn't worked yet."

I hugged my knees, the letters pressed to my chest, and for a while neither of us spoke, and the strangest thing happened, which is that the silence wasn't hostile. It was the silence of two people in the same small boat—which, tonight, was literal—both looking at the same black water, knowing there was something under it.

"My grandmother waited thirty years for a body," I said. "There wasn't one. There was a file marked *relocated, records incomplete*. She died with that phrase in her house. It was the last thing she asked me to find out about. Whether incomplete meant lost." I looked at him. "Does it mean lost, Coral?"

His jaw worked. I watched him weigh it—I actually watched the scales move behind his eyes, duty on one pan and whatever was growing between us on the other, and I hated him a little and understood him completely, both at once, which is a combination I don't recommend.

"No," he said. "It doesn't mean lost."

And then he lay back down, and turned his face away, and gave me nothing else, and I sat up the rest of my watch with my grandfather's letters against my heart, doing arithmetic I didn't want to do: forty-one names, a bell, a man who knew the river's memory better than anyone alive, and a chest I had glimpsed exactly once, in the back of his plane, sealed with wax and cord, that he had never once mentioned since.

* * *

The storm broke near dawn, the way they do—all at once, like a debt paid. The clouds tore open over the eastern hills and the light came through in shafts, gold and filthy with suspended river, and the whole world steamed. Bo was up first, testing the air like a baker testing dough, declaring it flyable, boat-able, survivable. Ferro produced coffee that tasted like boiled tires and we drank it gratefully. Wren woke, assessed the adult faces around the fire, and correctly concluded that something had happened in the night, and wisely said nothing, which is more than most adults manage.

We broke camp fast. Coral moved through it all with his usual economy, coiling lines, distributing weight, and if his eyes were red-rimmed, well, everyone's were; nobody sleeps in a storm.

But as I handed him the last of the gear, he held on to it—one extra second, no more—and said, low, under the noise of the crew:

"The bell. When we reach the site. You should hear it from me first."

"From you," I repeated. "Not from the bell."

"From me," he said. "What I can."

And he turned away to shove the boat off the clay shelf, and I stood there holding my grandfather's letters, understanding two things with total clarity: that he was finally, clumsily, honestly trying to give me something—and that whatever it was, it was still not the whole.

Downstream, around the first bend, the channel ran high and brown and fast, carrying last night's storm toward the sea. And far behind us, faint under the birdsong, I thought I heard it—an engine, low-throated, patient, keeping our speed.

Tomas heard it too. He looked at Coral. Coral looked at the water, and said nothing, and pushed us out into the current.
