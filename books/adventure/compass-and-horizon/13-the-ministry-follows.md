## 13. The Ministry Follows

I saw their boat for the third time at the mouth of the Cazador channel, and by then I'd stopped pretending it was coincidence.

It sat low in the water the way government boats always do—too much steel, too many men, not enough cargo to justify either. White hull, grey canopy, a radio mast whip-thin against the afternoon glare. The kind of boat that never carries anything heavier than paperwork and still rides like it's hauling sin.

"Same one from Two Rivers," Bo said beside me, not looking at it directly, which was a courtesy we extended each other in this crew. You didn't stare at trouble; you let trouble think it hadn't been seen.

"Could be a different boat."

"It's got the same dent in the bow."

"You've got good eyes for a man who claims he can't read a chart."

"I can read dents." He spat over the rail, unhurried. "That dent was there when they tied up behind us at Two Rivers, and it was there when they passed us slow yesterday like they were taking our picture."

Tomas came up from the hold wiping his hands on a rag that had once been white, and Ferro leaned out of the galley with a wooden spoon held like a scepter. Wren was on the roof of the cabin with Ines, both of them bent over her half-map, and I made a decision about how much of this conversation they needed to hear.

"Below," I said to Bo and Tomas, quietly. "Ferro, keep cooking. Loudly."

The spoon went back to work. We gathered at the engine housing where the noise would eat our words.

"They're following us," Bo said. It wasn't a question.

"They're surveying," I said. "That's what surveyors do. They follow rivers."

"Coral." Bo wiped his hands slow, the way he did when he was choosing words like bolts, checking each thread before he trusted it. "I flew supply runs out of Kestrel Field for eleven years. I know what a tail looks like on water same as I know one in the sky. That boat has turned when we've turned. Held back when we anchored. And last night somebody ran a light along our hull at anchor, and it wasn't a fisherman, because fishermen don't count the crates."

Tomas nodded. "They counted twice."

"So here's my question," Bo said, and now he looked at me straight, because he was a man who believed trouble should be faced square or not flown into at all. "What are we carrying that a ministry boat wants counted?"

And there it was—the moment every captain knows is coming, the moment the lie you built gets weighed against the people standing on your deck. I could tell them nothing. I could tell them half. Or I could tell them enough to keep them alive, which is a different amount than enough to keep them comfortable.

"We're carrying salvage," I said. "Legitimate salvage, off a wreck I have rights to. But the ministry has an old file, and old files make clerks nervous, and nervous clerks send boats. They don't want our cargo. They want to know where we're going, so they can go there first and put a seal on it."

"And are we going where they think we're going?"

"No."

Bo waited. He was good at waiting. It was one of the reasons I'd hired him and one of the reasons he scared me.

"Then where are we going?"

"Somewhere else entirely," I said. "Tonight."

* * *

I laid the chart out in the cabin after supper, weighted at the corners with coffee mugs, and let them all crowd around it—Wren included, because she'd earned the inclusion and because leaving her out would only guarantee she eavesdropped anyway. Ines stood at my shoulder. I was aware of her shoulder the way a man is aware of weather.

"The Serrano Rapids," I said, and tapped the inked squiggle that cartographers draw when they want to say *here be grief*. "Everyone runs the Cazador channel. It's wide, it's marked, it's slow—and it's exactly where that boat will be sitting tonight with its searchlight, because there's nowhere else to go."

"There's always somewhere else," Ines said. Not challenging. Testing. She had a way of saying things that were questions without question marks, and I was starting to learn that those were the dangerous ones.

"Serrano," I said. "Six kilometers of whitewater through a granite cut. Nobody runs it at night. Nobody runs it loaded. The charts mark three wrecks in it and the charts are wrong—it's five. I know, because I put two of them there myself, twenty years ago, in boats I had no business driving."

Silence around the chart. Ferro crossed himself with the hand that wasn't holding his pipe, which for Ferro was practically a formal objection.

"You've run it?" Tomas asked.

"Twice. Daylight, empty, younger." I looked at each of them. "Here's the truth of it, since you're owed some: that boat isn't following us for fun. If they reach the site before us, everything we came for goes into a crate with a government stamp and nobody living sees it again for fifty years. Maybe ever. So tonight we go through Serrano, in the dark, and tomorrow morning that surveyor wakes up at the mouth of the Cazador wondering where we went, and by the time he works it out we'll be two days gone and the river will be doing our lying for us."

Wren was staring at the rapids mark like it might stare back. "You said the river drops four meters through there."

"Four and a half, this time of year."

"And the rocks—"

"The rocks are where they've always been. That's the one honest thing about rocks." I rolled the chart closed. "Bo, I need the engine perfect. Not good—perfect. Tomas, everything lashed down double, and the chest tripled. Ferro, feed everybody, because nobody eats scared and everybody needs eating anyway. Wren—" and she looked up at me with her chin already set to argue, "—you're on the lamp. Forward lamp, my signals only. Nothing else. Can you do that?"

She could. That was the thing about Wren; she could do nearly anything I asked except stop asking questions, and I loved her for it in the way you love a fire in a dry season—warmth and worry in the same breath.

Ines stayed behind when the others moved. She put her finger on the chart roll before I could stow it.

"How many times have you lied to me since Two Rivers?"

I met her eyes. "Fewer than I'm going to."

Something moved across her face—not anger, something more tired than anger, something that recognized the shape of a secret because she was carrying one herself. "Come back through those rapids, Captain. I have questions that need you alive to answer them."

* * *

We entered the cut at 2300, moonless, running on the forward lamp and forty years of a river writing itself into my hands.

I'll tell you what a night rapid sounds like before it looks like anything: it sounds like the world clearing its throat. A long rolling growl under the hull, getting deeper, getting personal. Then the spray comes, cold as a hand on the neck, and after that you're inside the sound and there's no outside anymore.

"Standby," I said, and felt the word travel down the boat the way current travels down a rope.

Wren's lamp swung left—one flash, two. Rock shelf on the port side, right where my memory said it would be. Good girl. I eased the wheel starboard and the river took us.

There's a moment in every rapid—I've known pilots who call it the prayer point, though none of them pray out loud—where the water decides how much of your plan it intends to honor. You come off the lip of the drop and for one full second the boat isn't in the water, it's above it, flying the way a stone flies, and every choice you made is already spent and the river is just telling you the price.

We dropped. The hull slammed. Somewhere aft a crate sang against its lashings and Ferro swore in three languages, two of them liturgical.

"Lamp right!" I called, and Wren swung it, and the beam found the tongue of smooth water I wanted between two black teeth of granite, and I threaded us through a gap I'd have sworn on a Bible was wider than it was.

Behind us, faint but unmistakable, an engine note changed pitch. Then a white finger of searchlight swept the mouth of the cut behind us, found nothing but spray, and swept away.

"They followed us to the entrance," Tomas called. "They're holding at the pool."

"Of course they are." I grinned, and I heard it in my own voice, that ugly bright thing adrenaline makes of a man. "Nobody follows anybody into Serrano at night. That's the whole point of Serrano."

The second kilometer was the killer. The river pinched, the gradient steepened, and the water stopped being a surface and became a staircase with the steps missing. I drove by feel and by Wren's lamp and by a memory of a night twenty years old that I'd never told a living soul about, and the boat climbed and fell and climbed, and Bo's engine never missed a stroke—that man had tuned it like a choir—and then the lamp caught something that shouldn't have been there.

A sweeper. A whole tree, flood-carried, wedged across the channel at the exact height of our superstructure, invisible until the light hit it, and by then it was thirty meters away and closing.

"Hard left! Everybody DOWN!"

The wheel went over. The river argued. For a heartbeat I thought we'd make it under—thought it the way you think things in the half-second before physics files its report. The branch caught the roof of the cabin with a crack like the world's largest knuckle rapping a door, tore the lamp mount half loose, and flung us sideways into the current, and the current, insulted, took its payment.

We came through the last kilometer sideways, backwards, and once, briefly, entirely airborne, and I will carry the sound of my crew's voices in that kilometer to whatever grave gets me—Wren shouting the rocks, Ferro praying, Bo yelling engine readings nobody could use, Ines silent, absolutely silent, which frightened me more than all of them together.

Then the cut opened, the water lay down flat and black as spilled ink, and we were through.

* * *

We grounded on the shingle bar below the rapids at 0140, mostly because the alternative was drifting, and drifting required an engine, and the engine had a problem.

The problem announced itself the moment Bo opened the housing: a smell like burnt sugar and hot metal, and a shaft that turned with a grinding complaint no mechanic would forgive.

"Prop's done," Bo said. He said it calmly, which from Bo meant fury. "Cavitation damage from running half-submerged, plus whatever that tree did to the alignment. I can patch it. I can't fix it here."

"How far to Sable Bend?"

He looked upriver, then downriver, doing the arithmetic men like him do with distances the way accountants do with money. "Eight hours drifting if we had power. Three days poling and towing if we don't. There's the old mission station at the Bend—your chart shows a slipway."

"It shows a ruin."

"A ruin with a slipway." He stood, wiped his hands, and looked at me with the lamplight making hollows of his face. "Coral. That tree. We should be at the bottom of the river."

"But we're not."

"That's not skill. That's arithmetic failing in our favor." He stepped closer, voice dropping to the register he saved for things that mattered. "I've flown with captains who lie well. Smooth ones. Charming ones. Men who could talk a customs officer out of his own uniform. And every single one of them, sooner or later, asked a crew to pay for the lie with their lives, and paid it, and called it bad luck." He glanced back toward the boat, where Wren was re-lashing the forward lamp with a length of cord, small and stubborn in the dark. "So I'm not asking what you're hiding. I'm asking you to remember, every hour from now on, that the hiding has a price tag, and I can see whose names are written on it."

I watched Wren work. I thought about a boy flown out of a burning country with a new name sewn into his collar like a label.

"I remember," I said.

Bo studied me a long moment, then nodded once, the way a man signs a contract he hopes he won't regret. "Then get some sleep, Captain. I'll need you fresh to help me beg parts off a ruin."

* * *

I couldn't sleep. I stood on the shingle while the crew bedded down under tarps, and looked back upriver at the black wall of the granite cut, listening to Serrano growl to itself in the dark, satisfied.

We'd lost them. The surveyors were sitting at the mouth of the Cazador with their searchlight and their paperwork, watching an empty channel, and by dawn they'd be arguing about charts. Two days' lead. Maybe three.

But Bo's voice kept pace with me along the bank like a second heartbeat. *Liars get crews killed.* And underneath it, another sound I couldn't shake: the engine note behind us at the cut's entrance, changing pitch. Holding at the pool.

Because here was the thing I hadn't told my crew, the thing I'd known since Two Rivers and hadn't said aloud even to myself: that boat's skipper wasn't a clerk. Clerks turn back at rapids. Clerks wait for orders. But a man who holds his boat at the mouth of a night passage, quiet, lights doused, listening—that's a man who's been upriver before. A man who knows there's a second way through, even if he doesn't dare take it in the dark.

A man who'll take it at first light.

I looked at the stars through the smoke-haze, did the arithmetic I didn't want to do, and got the same answer twice: our lead was real, and it was melting, and eight hours downstream an abandoned mission station was the only place on this stretch of river with a slipway, tools, and walls—which meant it was also the only place we could be found.

Behind me, Wren stirred under her tarp and murmured something in her sleep—a word I almost recognized, in a language I hadn't heard spoken since I was smaller than she was.

I stood very still in the dark, listening to it fade, and understood that the river had brought us exactly where it had always meant to bring us.

Sable Bend. Where the dead children were buried.

We'd arrive by noon.
