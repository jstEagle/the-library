## 14. Stranded at Sable Bend

The propeller was a ruin. Everyone said so in their own way—Bo by kicking the landing gear, Tomas by whistling low through his teeth, Coral by saying nothing at all, which was the worst of the three. Wren had learned to read Coral's silences the way other children learned letters, and this one spelled *we are not going anywhere soon*.

Sable Bend rose out of the morning haze like something the river had tried to swallow and failed. The mission station sat on a shelf of high ground above the water, its whitewashed walls gone the color of old bone, its tin roof half-lifted and rusted through in places so that light fell through it in long dusty blades. A bell tower stood at the center of the compound, leaning slightly, patient as a heron.

"Three days," Bo said, spitting into the current. "Maybe four, if I can't find metal worth bending."

"Then we have three days," Coral said. He was already hauling crates up the bank, moving like a man who had decided that work was a form of prayer. "Wren. You're with me. We clear the big room before the damp gets into everything."

She went, because she always went when he called, but she looked back once at the mission, at the way the jungle pressed against its edges like a crowd pressing against a door.

Nobody had lived here for a very long time. She could feel it—the particular emptiness of a place that hadn't just been abandoned but *left*, quickly, with things still in it. In the big room there were desks. Small ones. Child-sized, their legs swollen with rot, inkwells still sitting in their holes like dark little eyes. A chalkboard hung crooked on one wall, and across it someone had written a lesson decades ago in a hand that slanted upward, hopeful: *The river gives and the river takes. Today we learn the names of clouds.*

Wren stood in front of it for a long time.

"Wren." Coral, from the doorway, arms full of canvas. "Crate first. Clouds later."

But she touched the board anyway, lightly, and came away with chalk dust on her fingertip, white as bone meal, and she didn't wipe it off for hours.

* * *

By afternoon the camp had organized itself the way it always did around Coral: Ferro's cookfire smoking at the edge of the compound, Tomas's dive gear drying on the chapel rail, Bo already elbow-deep in the engine cowling they'd hauled up from the boat, swearing at it with real affection. Ines sat on the mission steps with her maps spread out, drawing, always drawing, her pencil making small precise sounds like an insect working.

Wren carried water. It was her job now—Coral had given it to her that morning with a look that meant *stay busy, stay near, don't ask*. She'd been getting a lot of jobs lately. Fetch, carry, hold, count. Jobs were how Coral answered questions he didn't want to answer, and she was starting to keep a ledger of her own, a mental column of all the jobs against all the questions, and the columns did not balance.

The well was behind the chapel, past a stand of bamboo that whispered even when there was no wind. The path to it was overgrown, and she had to push through ferns that came up past her waist, wet and cold against her arms.

That was how she found the graves.

They weren't marked the way graves were supposed to be marked. There was no fence, no proper stones. Just a clearing where the ferns grew shorter and the ground rose in small, regular mounds—dozens of them, rows of them, each mound capped with a flat piece of coral rock, and on many of the rocks, scratched deep with something sharp, small marks. Crosses, some of them. And names.

She knelt beside the nearest one and brushed the moss away with her sleeve.

**TOMÁS JUNIOR — 1931–1938**

Her breath went strange. She looked at the next stone.

**MARISOL V. — 1934–1939**

And the next. **ANSELMO. 1936–1939.** **LUCIA P., 1932–1938.** Row after row after row, the dates running together like rain down a window, and every child dead before their tenth year, most of them within the same span—1938, 1939, 1939, 1938—as if the years themselves had reached down and closed a hand.

Wren counted. She was good at counting; Coral had taught her navigation math on long flights, made her do fuel calculations in her head until numbers felt like friends. There were forty-one mounds she could see, and the ferns hid more beyond them, bumps in the green like sleeping animals.

Forty-one children. Buried behind a chapel, in no order anyone had bothered to explain, in a place nobody had come back to.

She sat down hard in the wet grass and stayed there a long time, the water jug forgotten beside her, the bamboo whispering its windless whisper overhead.

* * *

She didn't tell anyone right away. That surprised her about herself. Part of her wanted to run straight back and shout it—but another part, a newer part, the part that had been keeping the ledger of jobs and questions, understood that this clearing was not the kind of thing you shouted. This was the kind of thing you carried a while first, to see if your arms were strong enough.

She went back at golden hour, when the light came low through the trees and turned the coral rocks the color of embers. She brought paper this time, and the stub of pencil she kept in her shirt pocket for sketching cloud shapes, and she copied the names. All the ones she could read. Some of the scratches had weathered to ghosts, but others were deep and clear, cut by hands that must have been shaking, or crying, or both.

Marisol V. Anselmo. Lucia P. Tomás Junior. A stone that said only **BEBÉ**, no name at all, no dates, just the word and a cross, and Wren had to stop copying for a while after that one.

Near the far row she found one different from the rest. Newer—not new, but newer, the coral rock unweathered, the letters cut clean and recent-looking, though the date carved into it was older than any of them:

**AQUI DESCANSAN LOS QUE EL RÍO LLEVÓ**
*Here rest those the river carried*

Someone had put that stone there recently. Within years, maybe. Someone had come back.

She thought about Coral then, standing in the doorway of the schoolroom saying *crate first, clouds later*, and something cold moved through her stomach, slow as the river itself.

He knew. He knew about this place. Maybe not these exact graves, maybe not this exact bend—but he knew what had happened here, whatever it was, the thing that filled forty-one small mounds and made him give her jobs instead of answers and lie smoothly to strangers with half a map. He'd known since before the chest. Maybe long before the chest. Maybe her whole life.

The evening bells of nowhere rang in her head, silent, forty-one times.

* * *

At supper nobody talked much. The fire cracked, Ferro ladled out stew, and the adults spoke in the short code of expeditions—fuel, current, bearing, sleep. Wren ate with her bowl balanced on her knees and watched Coral across the flames. He was cleaning his knife the way he always did, slow strokes along the leather strop, his face lit orange and unreadable.

Ines was showing him something on her map, leaning close, and he was nodding, and neither of them was looking at each other quite directly, which was also a kind of language Wren had started learning.

"I found the well path," Wren said, into a gap in the talk.

Coral's knife stopped. Just for a beat. Then it kept moving.

"It goes past the chapel," she went on, watching him. "There's a clearing back there."

The fire popped. Tomas glanced up. Bo kept working a wrench, but slower.

"There are graves," Wren said. "Children's graves. Forty-one that I counted. Most of them died the same two years." She took the folded paper from her pocket and held it out. "I wrote down the names."

Nobody moved. The river murmured below the bank, going about its business, giving and taking.

Ines reached for the paper first. Her eyes went down the list, and Wren watched her face change—watched the cartographer's calm composure crack and re-form, crack and re-form, like ice on a warming current. When Ines got to the bottom of the page her finger stopped on a name and stayed there, pressing into the paper as though she could press through it to something underneath.

"Vale," she said quietly. "Marisol V. My grandfather's survey party would have—" She stopped. Started again. "These dates. Thirty-eight, thirty-nine. That's when the outpost was supposedly evacuated. That's what every record says. *Evacuated.*"

"Graves aren't evacuation," Tomas said softly.

"No," Ines said. "They're not."

Wren looked at Coral. He had set the knife down. His face in the firelight was the face of a man standing very still on ground he knew was about to move.

"Coral." She used his name the way he hated—directly, without a job attached to it. "You knew. Didn't you. About what happened here."

The whole camp was looking at him now. Even Bo had set down the wrench. Somewhere out on the black water a night bird called, twice, and got no answer.

Coral picked up the knife again. Put it down again. When he finally spoke, his voice was level, and that was how Wren knew how much it was costing him, because Coral only sounded calm when he was holding something down with both hands.

"Some places on this river," he said, "the dead outnumber the living. And the living who remember are fewer than either." He looked at her, and his eyes weren't hard—they were tired, tired in a way that went back further than she'd ever let herself imagine. "I'll answer your question, Wren. One day soon. Not tonight. Tonight I need you to do me one more job."

Her jaw tightened. Another job. Always another job.

"What," she said.

"Keep the names safe." He nodded at the paper in Ines's hands. "All of them. Don't lose that page. Because I promise you—" and here his voice did something she'd never heard it do, dropped down into something raw and certain, "—there are people who will want those children forgotten twice. First by what was done to them. Then by paperwork. And we're going to be the ones who don't let that happen."

Silence. The fire settled. Ines folded the list with great care, like a flag, and put it inside her map case, against the waterproof backing.

Wren lay awake long after the others slept, listening to the river and the bamboo, thinking about the stone that said *Here rest those the river carried*. Someone had come back to raise it. Someone who remembered.

And in the morning, when she walked past the chapel with the water jug, she saw Coral standing alone at the edge of the grave clearing, hat in his hands, head bowed—not praying, she didn't think, or not exactly. More like reporting in.

Behind him, unnoticed by everyone but her, Ines stood at the treeline watching—and in her hand, open, was a letter she'd taken from her grandfather's papers, its edges trembling in the dawn breeze.
