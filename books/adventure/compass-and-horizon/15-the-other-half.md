## 15. The Other Half

The mission station at Sable Bend had been dead longer than Wren had been alive, and it made a better camp than most living towns. The roof of the chapel still held over one corner, enough to keep the rain off the crates and the radio. Bo had the engine cowling off the plane and spread across three oilcloths like a surgeon's tray. Ferro was arguing with a pot. And Ines Vale was sitting on the chapel steps with her grandfather's half-map flattened across her knees, waiting for me the way weather waits.

I'd been avoiding her for two days. On a boat that's a skill; on foot, in a camp the size of a churchyard, it's a discipline. I'd kept to the engine, to the waterline, to Tomas's dive gear, to anything with my hands in it. But the river had dropped another hand's width overnight, and the sandbar at Sable Bend was growing by the hour, and every hour I didn't tell her something, the telling got heavier.

She looked up when my boots crunched on the shell path. Didn't smile. Just patted the step beside her, once, like she'd been doing it all evening and I'd finally arrived late to my own appointment.

"Coral."

"Ines."

"Sit down or stand there," she said. "But either way, we're going to talk about the map."

I sat. It seemed the cheaper option.

* * *

She turned the half-map so it faced me. Her grandfather's drafting hand was fine and sure, the kind of line you only get from someone who drew for a living and loved it — contour hatching like combed hair, soundings written small and neat along the channel. The paper was foxed brown at the folds and soft as cloth from handling. It covered the lower river: the delta towns, the fuel strips, the drowned sugar country. All the places that were easy.

And then it stopped. Cleanly, deliberately, at a longitude that ran through nothing but blank paper and a single inked notation in a cramped old hand: *survey suspended — see continuation.*

"I've had this since I was nineteen," Ines said. "My grandmother kept it in a biscuit tin with his medals. She used to say he'd finish it when he came back." She smoothed the edge where the map ended, where sixty years ago someone had cut or torn away the rest. "He never came back. So I learned to draw maps myself, and I came out here, and I've spent four years walking this river with half a document, asking everyone who'd talk to me whether they'd seen the other half."

"You've asked me twice."

"And both times you told me you'd never seen any survey work from the outpost era." She said it without heat, which was worse than heat. "Coral, I've flown with you for six weeks now. I've watched you navigate channels that aren't on any chart I own. You know bends that silted over before I was born. Nobody learns a river that well from a cargo run. You know it because somebody taught it to you, or because you drew it yourself, or—" she tapped the torn edge, gently, "—because you're holding the piece I'm missing."

The fire cracked somewhere behind us. Ferro had stopped clattering the pot, which meant the whole crew was listening, which meant they'd all known before I did what this conversation was going to be.

"There are things on this river," I said, "that people keep private. Fishing holes. Landing strips. Graves."

"Graves," she repeated.

"Families have their dead out here. Their history. Some of them don't want it surveyed, drawn, published, put in a government file with a stamp on it. I've carried their mail twenty years without reading it. That's not deception. That's discretion."

"That would be a beautiful answer," Ines said, "if I believed it." She folded the half-map closed, slowly, along its old creases, and held it in both hands like something that could be taken from her. "Here's what I know, and you can correct me. Two nights ago, at the fire, when I read my grandfather's letters aloud — you went pale when I got to the one about the bell foundry. Not sad. Pale. Like a man hearing a floorboard creak over a room he thought was empty. And yesterday, when Wren asked you straight out what you were hiding, you gave her a job instead of an answer, which is what you do when the truth costs more than the lie. And today, when I mentioned the continuation sheet, your eyes went to the supply tent. Twice."

I hadn't known about the tent. That was the trouble with being watched by a cartographer — they noticed everything, and they remembered where everything was.

"The chest is in the tent," I said.

It came out quieter than I meant it to. She didn't gloat. She just waited, the way she waited on survey stations, until the instrument settled.

"It washed up after the floods," I said. "Low water near the old outpost. Stamped with the colonial seal. I hauled it home meaning to sell it unopened — there's always a buyer for sealed imperial salvage, and an unopened one pays double." I rubbed my jaw. My beard needed cutting. Everything about me needed cutting, lately. "Then I opened it."

"Why?"

Because I'd wanted to know. Because twenty years of flying over that drowned ground had built up a pressure in me that no amount of miles relieved. Because curiosity is just loneliness wearing a work shirt. I said none of that.

"Because it was sitting in my house," I said, "and I don't keep things in my house I can't account for."

"What's in it?"

"Ledgers. Shipping manifests. Ration tallies. The administrative spine of the outpost — who was stationed there, what moved upriver, what got stockpiled." I heard my own voice go flat and official, reciting inventory, and hated it. "And letters."

Her breath changed. Small thing. A cartographer notices small things.

"Letters," she said.

"Official correspondence. Dispatches. The kind of paper that gets boxed and buried when an administration closes down." I stood up, because sitting felt too much like being deposed. The chapel loomed behind her, its dead bell tower black against the early stars. "Ines, I'll show you the ledgers. Tonight, if you want. Every page."

"But not the letters."

"The letters are—"

"Don't." She rose too, and she wasn't tall but she stood like she was, chin level, hands still wrapped around that folded half-map as if it were the only honest object left between us. "Don't tell me they're fragile. Don't tell me they're ministry property. Don't tell me they're nothing. You opened a sealed chest from a place where my grandfather disappeared, you read papers from the last months of his life, and you've decided — *you*, Coral, alone, in your kitchen — which parts of my family's history I'm allowed to see."

"It's not only yours."

"No," she said. "Whose else?"

And there it was, the trap I'd built myself, door swinging shut. I looked past her at the fire, at Bo's silhouette bent over the engine, at Wren's smaller shape sitting close to the flames pretending to mend a net. Whose else indeed.

"The families," I said. "There are families upriver whose people came out of that outpost. What happened there isn't a mystery to solve. It's a wound some of them are still bleeding from. Those letters name names. They describe—" I stopped. Recalibrated. "They describe things that were done to those people, and then erased. If I hand that paper to the first cartographer who comes asking, it ends up in a file, and files get stamped, and stamped files get shelved, and shelved files get forgotten, and the forgetting happens all over again. I've watched this country do it before."

"So your plan was to sit on it forever."

"My plan was to move it somewhere no one would look."

"That's not a plan, that's a hiding place."

"Yes," I said. "It is."

Something moved through her face then — not anger. Anger I could have handled. This was the other thing, the thing underneath, and it was worse. She looked at me the way you look at a chart that's been accurate right up until the moment it kills you.

"I trusted you," she said. "Six weeks. I told you things on that boat I haven't told anyone. About my grandmother keeping the biscuit tin dusted for thirty years. About how I used to write letters to a dead man and mail them to the last address on his survey. And the whole time you were sitting on the end of his story in a locked box."

"He might not be in it."

"He might be," she said, "and you know, and you won't say." Her voice didn't break. It narrowed. "Do you understand what that is? That's not protecting anyone. That's you deciding my grief gets to stay unfinished because finishing it inconveniences your—your *discretion*."

The word landed where she'd aimed it. I let it.

* * *

We walked to the supply tent together, and I unhooked the lantern, and I lifted the chest down from under the tarpaulin where it had ridden since Sable Bend, and I set it on the crate between us. The seal was already broken — had been broken for weeks now, by my own hands, in my own kitchen, by lamplight, alone. I'd told myself that night that opening it was prudence. Know your cargo. Now, with her watching, I understood it had been the first theft. Everything since was just fencing the goods.

I took out the ledgers and laid them on the crate one by one. Shipping manifests. Ration tallies. The dry bones of the place, columns of numbers in faded iron-gall ink, the outpost reduced to what it received and what it consumed and what it shipped away.

Ines read the way she did everything — thoroughly, silently, fast. Her finger tracked down the columns. I watched her stop at certain entries. I knew which ones. The entries where the tonnage didn't match the population. Where boats left loaded and came back empty. Where the ration counts dropped month over month in ways that had nothing to do with supply and everything to do with mouths.

"These tallies," she said quietly. "Fourth column. The headcounts."

"Yes."

"They fall."

"Yes."

"By two hundred and eleven people over fourteen months." She looked up. "You don't lose two hundred and eleven people to emigration. Not from a station this size. Not in that pattern." Her finger rested on the page. "This isn't a ledger. This is a confession that doesn't know it is one."

"That's why it can't go in a government file," I said. "That's why."

She absorbed that. I watched her weigh it, actually weigh it, because she was fair-minded in a way that made deceiving her feel like a crime with a specific victim. Then she said, "And the letters?"

I reached into the chest. My hand closed on the packet — oilcloth-wrapped, tied with the waxed cord I'd replaced after the floodwater rotted the original — and I felt the whole weight of the thing, the paper weight and the other kind, the kind I'd been carrying since the night I read the name *Vale* in a dead man's dispatch and had to set the letter down and walk out into my own yard and breathe at the stars like a man surfacing from deep water.

*Re: removal of persons, Vale among them.* A grandfather's name in a bureaucrat's sentence. A family erased in eleven words and a signature.

If I gave her that packet tonight, she'd read it by this lantern, here, with the crew pretending not to watch, and she'd learn her grandfather's fate in a shell-heap camp forty miles from anywhere, alone except for a man who'd lied to her for six weeks. Or she'd learn it later, better, with time to prepare — that was what I told myself. That was the kindness. I handed her kindness the way I handed Wren jobs instead of answers, and I was beginning to understand it was the same coin, and it was counterfeit.

"Not yet," I said.

Her face closed like a border.

"When?" she said.

"When we reach the site. When there's context. Letters like these—" I made myself hold her eyes, because lying flat-on is easier than lying sideways and I owed her at least a straight posture. "They reference places. Documents. Things at the outpost itself that make sense of them. Read apart, they're just cruelty on paper. Read there, they mean something."

"You've read them there? In context?" A beat. "You've read them at all."

"Ines—"

"Answer the question. Have you read them?"

"Yes."

"All of them?"

The lantern hissed. Somewhere down the bank the river ran low and quiet over the bar, dropping, always dropping, the season closing around us like a fist.

"Yes," I said.

She nodded, once, slow, and I watched her decide something, though I couldn't read what. She gathered the ledgers back into a neat stack, squared their edges with a precision that hurt to watch, and set them inside the chest beside the packet she didn't touch, didn't look at, refused with her whole body.

"Then here's how this goes," she said. "I fly with you to the site. I help you do whatever it is you're actually doing out here, because I'm not a fool, Coral, and I've known since the ministry men boarded that this expedition has two purposes and only one of them has a wreck in it. I'll carry your secret as far as the site." She picked up her half-map and tucked it inside her coat, against her heart, where she'd carried it across an ocean and four years of river country. "But when we get there, I read everything. Not the ledgers. Everything. And if I find out you've removed pages, or burned pages, or that you've decided ahead of time what I can't survive knowing — then whatever this is between us, whatever I thought it was becoming, ends on that ground, and I will walk out of here on foot if I have to."

"That's fair," I said, and my voice almost held.

"It's not fair," she said. "Fair would have been six weeks ago." She paused at the tent flap, lantern light catching the tired lines of her face, and for one moment the anger went out of her and something older and sadder looked through. "My grandmother died not knowing. Thirty years of dusting a biscuit tin. I swore I wouldn't die the same way, and I will not let a man who measures his life in miles take that from me too."

She left. The flap fell. I stood alone with the chest and the lantern and the packet I hadn't given her, and I told myself, one more time, the story about kindness. About context. About the right moment.

Down by the fire, Wren was watching me through the gap in the canvas. She didn't ask anything. She just looked, the way kids look, straight through the part of a man that does the talking, and then she went back to her net.

I doused the lantern and sat in the dark with the chest between my knees, and for the first time since the floods coughed it up, I was afraid of what was inside it — not because of what it would do to the families, or the ministry, or the river.

Because in three days, maybe four, we'd reach the site. And she'd read everything. Including the letters I hadn't shown her. Including the ones that didn't just name her family.

Including the one that named mine.
