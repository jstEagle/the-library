## 16. Ines's Bargain

Ines Vale had spent her life learning to read the space between lines—on maps, in letters, in the silences of men who flew rivers for a living. Her grandfather had taught her that, though he hadn't meant to. His surveys were full of marginalia, small annotations that said more than the neat contours ever did: *sandbar shifts after flood*, *villagers distrustful of officials*, *ask for Marisol at the second bend*. He believed a map was a confession. Every line you drew was something you'd seen, and everything you left out was something you couldn't bear to.

So when Coral Underhill set the ledger down between them on the deck of the boat and did not set the letters beside it, she saw the omission the way a pilot sees weather building on a horizon—not yet upon them, but coming, and coming fast.

They were anchored in a backwater below Sable Bend, the engine apart under Bo's tarp, the propeller a ruined sculpture of bent bronze lashed to the rail as a reminder. The night passage through the rapids had cost them a day and nearly cost them Tomas twice—once in the water, once in the fever that followed—and it had cost Ines something quieter and harder to name: the last of her willingness to be managed.

Coral sat across from her with his forearms on his knees, the way he always sat, as if ready to stand and leave at any moment. Twenty years of leaving folded into his posture. The lantern between them threw his face into planes and shadow, and she hated how much she still wanted to look at it.

"You showed me the shipping manifests," she said. "The ration tallies. You showed me the columns of numbers."

"Yes."

"And you kept the letters in your coat."

He didn't flinch. That was the thing about him—the honesty arrived late but it arrived whole. "I did."

"Give me one reason that isn't kindness. Because if you say you were protecting me, I'm going to remind you that my grandfather disappeared somewhere up this river, and every day you don't let me look is a day you've decided my grief is yours to schedule."

The river moved against the hull in long, patient strokes. Somewhere aft, Ferro was arguing with Wren about whether beans could be burned twice, and losing. Bo's radio murmured static and half a weather report from a station two hundred miles east, a voice out of another country.

"I'll give you two reasons," Coral said. "And then I want to hear your terms."

That surprised her. She'd prepared for deflection, for the slow river-guide patience that wore questions down like water wears stone. Instead he was meeting her halfway, or claiming to, and she distrusted it even as she leaned toward it.

"Go on."

"The first reason is the one I told you. Those letters name families. Families that are still alive, still living along this river, still fishing the same bends their grandparents fished. What happened at the outpost didn't end at the outpost. It ended in a government office that stamped a file closed and paid nothing to no one. If those pages come out wrong—if they come out through me, a stranger with no standing and less proof—they won't free anyone. They'll just make the ministry careful. Careful men burn things." He turned his hands over, palms up, an old man's gesture on a man who wasn't old. "I have watched this country eat its own history for twenty years. I know how it chews."

"And the second reason?"

He was quiet long enough that the lantern flame leaned in the draft and righted itself.

"The second reason is that some of what's in there would hurt you before it helped you, and I told myself I could choose the order of your hurting." His eyes came up and held hers, and there was no smoothness in them now, none of the cargo-runner charm she'd catalogued that first day on the mail strip. "That was arrogance. I see that. A man doesn't get to curate another person's truth. But I need you to understand it wasn't contempt. It was the opposite. It was the only kind of protecting I know how to do, and I did it badly, to someone I—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "To someone whose good opinion has started to matter more to me than my own safety. Which is a stupid way for a man my age to live."

Ines looked away first, out at the black water, because looking at him while he said things like that felt like reading someone's diary by accident.

Her grandfather's last letter—the one that had brought her here, the one with the half-map pressed flat inside it—had contained a single sentence she'd read perhaps four hundred times: *If anything happens to me, the river will tell you where I couldn't.* She had come three thousand miles on that sentence. She had not come to be handled.

"All right," she said. "Here are my terms."

She took her notebook from her jacket—the surveyor's notebook, gridded and oilcloth-wrapped, the one tool she owned that she'd defend with her body—and opened it flat on the crate between them. She uncapped her pen. The small ceremony of it mattered; she wanted him to see that these were not feelings being negotiated. They were coordinates.

"Term one," she said. "When we reach the site—at dead-low water, wherever the foundations are, wherever your chest came from—I read everything. Not summaries. Not selected documents. Everything. Letters, ledgers, whatever else the river gave you. I read it myself, with my own eyes, before anyone else touches it. Before the crew, before the ministry, before God."

"Ines—"

"That's not negotiable, so don't spend breath on it. Term two." She wrote as she spoke, her hand steady, because the steadiness of her hand was the only argument she fully trusted herself to make. "You don't decide anymore what I can carry. Whatever you think will break me—you show it to me and let me break or not break, and either way it's mine. My grandfather went into this country believing someone would finish his work. If his work ends with a granddaughter who was fed her own history in safe portions, then they got him twice, and I will not allow it."

The static on the radio swelled and faded. Downriver, faint as memory, thunder worked at the edge of hearing.

"Term three." Now her pen slowed. This was the term she'd drafted and redrafted all afternoon while pretending to check Tomas's charts, and she made herself say it plainly, without decoration, because decoration was how liars talked and she refused to sound like one. "Whatever is in those letters about my family—the Vales, my grandfather, whatever the empire did to them or erased about them—you tell me yourself. Before I find it. You don't let me discover it alone at midnight with a lantern. You sit across from me the way you're sitting now, and you say it out loud, and you let me be angry at a person instead of at paper. Can you do that?"

Coral looked at her for a long moment. In the lantern light she watched him do the arithmetic she'd seen him do a dozen times since the rapids—the constant, tireless calculation of a man weighing what a truth costs against what its absence costs—and for the first time since she'd known him, she watched the calculation come out the other way.

"Yes," he said. "I can do that."

"Then those are my terms. And in exchange—"

"You don't have to offer anything. The terms aren't a trade. You're not buying the truth off me."

"Aren't you abandoning the negotiation, then? Because I had more."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where smiles lived in him, gated and rarely open. "Say it anyway."

"In exchange," she said, "when this is over—however it ends, whatever the ministry does, whoever gets the chest—I stay in the country until the families have been told. All of them. And I put the site on the map. Properly. Named, surveyed, documented, in the record where no office can unmake it. Your drowned outpost becomes a place again instead of a rumor. That's my work. That's what I'm for." She capped the pen. "A map is a confession, Captain. Let's confess the whole thing."

He was silent. She'd learned his silences the way she'd learned weather—there were silences that meant *no* wearing the costume of *maybe*, and silences that meant he was counting costs, and silences that meant something in him had shifted and he needed a moment before the shift showed on the surface. This one was the third kind. She was getting better at him. She wasn't sure that was wise.

"My grandmother used to say," he said finally, "that the river keeps what people can't afford to hold. I used to think that meant it was kind." He shook his head slowly. "It's not kind. It's just patient. And patience isn't the same as mercy—it just means the truth comes up on its own schedule instead of yours. Low water comes every year. Sooner or later everything drowns or surfaces, and you don't get to pick which."

"So you agree."

"I agree." He reached across the crate and turned her notebook toward himself, and before she understood what he was doing he'd written beneath her three terms, in a hand surprisingly fine for a man who lived by his grip—*Agreed. C.U.*—and signed it like a charter, like a bill of lading, like the contracts he'd spent twenty years making and keeping with strangers. Then he pushed it back. "Witnessed and binding. Ask Bo; he'll tell you I've never broken a signed manifest in my life. It's the one honest religion I have."

Ines looked down at the ink drying in the lantern light, her terms and his signature, and felt something in her chest loosen that had been tight since the mail strip, since the first lie about salvage rights, since every guarded conversation on the water. It wasn't trust—not yet, maybe not entirely ever—but it was the frame of it, the surveyor's stakes driven into uncertain ground. You could build on stakes. You could navigate by them.

"There's one more thing," she said. "Not a term. A question."

"Those are usually the expensive ones."

"Why did you keep the letters in your coat? Not the ledger—the letters. You could have buried them, burned them, sunk them back where you found them. You carried them against your body for two hundred miles of river. Why?"

Coral looked at the lantern for a while. When he answered, his voice had gone somewhere she hadn't heard it go before—down past the guide's calm, past the captain's competence, into a room she suspected almost no one visited.

"Because paper burns and rivers forget, and somebody in this world ought to remember those names even if nobody says them out loud," he said. "Carrying them was the closest I could come to saying them." He rose, joints cracking, twenty years of miles audible in the sound. "Get some sleep, Miss Vale. Dead-low water won't wait for us, and neither will the ministry."

He moved aft toward the engine tarp and Bo's low swearing, and Ines sat alone with the notebook and the lantern and the river's long breathing against the hull.

She should have felt victorious. She had her terms, signed; she had her access; she had, if she was honest with herself, something warmer than access growing in her that she hadn't planned for and didn't intend to examine tonight. But instead she kept returning to the moment before he stood—the pause before *someone I—*, the sentence he'd swallowed, and the deeper pause before *carrying them was the closest I could come to saying them.*

Nobody carried other men's sins against their body for two decades. Nobody learned the exact depth of every channel above Sable Bend unless they'd grown up learning it. There was a shape in Coral Underhill's silences, and she was beginning to trace its outline, and the outline frightened her—not because of what it might mean for her expedition, but because of what it might mean for him, and for whatever this was between them that neither of them had named.

She clicked the lantern down to a ember-glow and lay back on the bench beneath the awning, the notebook on her chest like a breastplate.

Above the awning, past the black weave of the trees, the sky had cleared after the storm, and the stars over the interior were the old stars, the surveyor's stars, the ones her grandfather had steered by. She found the southern cross and held it, the way he'd taught her, and let the boat rock her.

She was nearly asleep when she heard voices aft—Bo's, low and hard, pitched beneath the wind, and Coral's answering, equally low. She caught only fragments through the canvas: *...flown with men who lied to crews before...* and *...you don't get to ask me that...* and then a silence with weight in it, the kind of silence that follows a door closing.

Ines lay awake a long time after that, listening to the river, holding her signed terms against her heart, understanding that the hardest negotiations of this voyage had not happened at her lantern at all—and that tomorrow, in the grey light before departure, she would have to decide whether the man she'd just bound himself to her was a man she could follow into the drowned places, or a man the river was still keeping secrets for.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of tin. Bo was already at the engine, sleeves rolled, and when Ines climbed out of the stern with her chart roll under her arm, the mechanic looked up at her with an expression she'd never seen him wear—something between apology and warning.

"Captain says we make Sable Bend by noon," Bo said. "Tide tables say dead-low water comes day after tomorrow." He wiped his hands slowly on a rag that was cleaner than his hands. "Miss Vale. When we get there—whatever the captain shows you—make sure he shows you all of it. I've hauled cargo for liars and I've hauled cargo for honest men, and the difference ain't the lying. It's whether they tell you before the water rises or after."

He turned back to the engine before she could answer, and Ines stood in the tin-colored dawn with her grandfather's half-map against her ribs, watching the river run low and shallow and honest toward the place where everything would surface—whether Coral carried it there, or the water did.
