## 17. Bo's Doubt

Bo Okonkwo had a rule about engines and a rule about men, and both rules said the same thing: listen to them idle before you trust them under load.

An engine idling rough told you everything. A man idling rough did too. And Coral Underhill had been idling rough for eleven days now—smooth on the surface, sure, hands steady on the yoke, voice even over the radio—but underneath there was a knock. A faint metallic irregularity, way down in the case, the kind of sound a lazy mechanic would write off as valve lash and a careful one would tear the whole bottom end open to find.

Bo was not a lazy mechanic.

He sat on an upturned fuel drum at the edge of camp with the generator's spare carburetor in his lap, not working on it, just holding it, watching Coral across the firelight. The captain stood at the water's edge where the boat was tied up, talking low with Ines Vale. Or not talking. Standing near her while she looked at her grandfather's half-map by flashlight, the two of them arranged like a diagram of two things that hadn't decided yet whether they were going to collide.

Eleven days downriver from the strips. Eleven days of Coral's cover story—the wreck of the *Marta C.*, a freight barge lost with a cargo manifest worth recovering, coordinates he'd produced from memory so fast it had made Bo's neck prickle. Eleven days of Tomas diving decoy holes while the real current of the trip ran somewhere else entirely, somewhere Coral steered them toward with corrections that came too quick and too certain for a man who claimed to be guessing.

And tonight, Bo had finally put his finger on the sound.

It had happened an hour after sundown, when he'd gone to check the tie-downs before the rain came. He'd come around the stern of the boat and stopped dead, because Coral and Ines were there in the dark, close enough that Bo could hear every word, and what he heard was this:

"The other half," Ines said. "You've seen it."

A long silence. Long enough for the river to say three things against the hull.

"I know the country," Coral said. "That's all you need."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got tonight."

And then—Bo would swear this on his torque wrench—he'd heard something else in the captain's voice. Not the smooth radio voice. Not the strip-side gossip voice. Something older and rawer, like a bearing that had been running dry for twenty years.

"Ask me again when we get there," Coral had said. "I'll answer. I swear it, Ines. When we get there, I'll answer everything."

*When we get there.* Not *if*. Not *wherever the current takes us*. *There.* As if there were a specific there, fixed in his head like a rivet, and had been since before they ever left the strips.

Bo had backed away into the treeline without making a sound, which was a skill you learned growing up the youngest of six in a house with one working radio and a father who drank, and he'd come back to the fire with the carburetor in his lap and the knock in his ears getting louder every minute.

Now Wren dropped down onto the drum beside him, knees first, the way she did everything—like gravity was a suggestion she'd decided to honor out of politeness. She smelled of woodsmoke and river mud and the cheap soap Ferro doled out in squares the size of postage stamps.

"You're doing the face," she said.

"What face?"

"The face you do when an engine's lying to you. Jaw all set. You did it in Port Ansel when the Otter's number two was eating itself and everybody else said it sounded fine."

Bo turned the carburetor over in his hands. "Engine's fine."

"So it's the other thing." Wren watched the fire, not him. She'd learned that trick from him, probably. Watching sideways was how you got the truth out of people who knew you were looking. "You think I don't notice anything because I'm fifteen. But I count too. We've been going the wrong direction off the *Marta C.*'s last known position for four days, and every time somebody asks why, Coral says 'current,' and currents don't argue back but rivers don't run uphill either."

Bo looked at her properly then. Fifteen years old, hair tied back with a strip of inner tube, eyes that had already catalogued more of the adult world than any child's should. He'd helped raise her the way everyone in the salvage outfit had helped raise her—Coral most of all, though he'd deny it with his dying breath—and sometimes she still caught him off guard like a door swinging open into a room he thought was empty.

"What do you want me to say, small fry?"

"I want you to say whether we're okay." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands found each other in her lap and held on. "You're the one who knows when things are okay. That's your whole job. So. Are we?"

The question went through Bo like a drill bit through thin sheet metal—no resistance until suddenly all of it at once.

He thought about the answer he wanted to give. *Sure we're okay. Captain knows the river. Eat your beans.*

He thought about the answer that might be true. *I don't know. Your captain is carrying something heavy enough to bend his frame, and I haven't figured out yet whether it's going to hold or shear.*

What he said was: "Eat your beans. Then go help Ferro with the tarp before the rain."

Wren went. But she looked back once from the firelight's edge, and the look said she'd filed his non-answer exactly where she filed everything—in the same drawer as Coral's, which was getting full.

* * *

Bo waited until the camp settled.

Ferro banked the fire and took the middle watch. Tomas, whose ribs were still strapped from the dive at the first wreck, slept the heavy drugged sleep of someone who'd taken the last of their codeine and earned it twice over. Wren curled in the boat under a tarp, one hand on the camera she'd bought off a dead man's estate in Port Ansel and treated ever since like a religious object. Ines sat up late with her letters, reading by flashlight, and Bo saw the moment Coral drifted over and stood near her without speaking, and the moment she let him, and the moment he moved on toward the treeline to check the perimeter—which was what he always called it when he needed to be alone, and which fooled nobody, least of all Bo.

Bo gave it twenty minutes. Then he picked up two mugs of Ferro's coffee—which was less coffee than a threat of coffee, boiled down to something you could patch a fuel line with—and walked into the trees.

He found Coral standing at the top of the cutbank, looking downriver. The moon was up behind cloud, giving everything the flat gray light of a photograph developing wrong. Below them the river ran narrow and quick over its gravel bars, dropping by the day, exposing ribs of sandstone that had been underwater since the floods. Somewhere downstream, past the dark, was the place Coral wouldn't name.

"Coffee," Bo said.

Coral took the mug without looking at him. They drank. Downriver a night bird made its sound like a hinge that needed oil, and neither of them mentioned it, because mentioning birds was for men who had nothing better between them.

"Perimeter's quiet," Coral said finally.

"It's a forest, Captain. Nothing's been out here to perimeter against since the war."

"Old habit."

"Yeah." Bo drank. "You've got a lot of those."

Coral's profile didn't move. But Bo had spent years reading faces across engine bays, in the bad light of failing generators, through smoke and spray and the particular darkness of a hangar at 3 a.m. when a part has failed and the flight leaves in four hours and the only question is whether the mechanic will lie about it. He knew what a jaw looked like when it tightened around something.

"Say what you came to say," Coral said.

"All right." Bo set his mug on a root. "I flew eight years with a man named Denny Okafor, out of the coastal strips, before you knew me. Best pilot I ever saw. Better than you, and I'll deny saying that too. Denny had a secret. Never told me what it was—money, a woman, a warrant, I never found out. But he carried it the way you carry yours. Smooth on top. Knock underneath. And every time we flew into weather he shouldn't have flown into, every time he pushed a leg longer than the fuel justified, every time he waved off my concerns with that easy smile—I could hear it. The knock. Getting louder."

Coral said nothing. The river said nothing louder.

"Denny died at Cape Verity with five souls aboard because he wouldn't divert, wouldn't land, wouldn't tell me why we absolutely had to be somewhere by a certain hour. Board of inquiry said fuel exhaustion. I say the cause was whatever he wouldn't say out loud. Secrets are heavy, Captain. They shift the center of gravity. And I have been listening to your knock for eleven days."

For a long moment Coral just breathed. Then he set his own mug down beside Bo's, deliberate, the way he did everything, and turned to face him fully for the first time.

"How loud?" he asked.

"Loud enough that Wren hears it. And that girl doesn't miss much, but she's fifteen, and she trusts you the way kids trust gravity. She doesn't check it. She just steps off the edge believing it'll hold." Bo's voice stayed level, which cost him. "Tomas is diving on busted ribs because you asked. Ferro left a kitchen and a woman who says yes to him sometimes, for this. Vale gave up her survey contract. And me—I signed on to recover a wreck, same as the rest. So the question isn't whether you've got a secret. Everybody's got a secret; mine's a divorce and a debt, and I'll show you the paperwork if it makes you feel better. The question is whether your secret weighs more than this crew can lift."

"And if it does?"

"Then I want to know before it shears, not after. That's all. I'm not asking you to open the case in front of everybody. I'm asking you to tell me what I'm bolted to."

Coral looked at him for a long time. And Bo watched something happen in the man's face that he'd only seen a few times in twenty years of knowing him—a crack, fine as a hairline fracture in a crankcase, running from the eye down through the jaw. Not weakness. Load. The look of a structure bearing more than it was rated for and holding anyway, barely, and knowing it.

"There's a place," Coral said slowly, "downriver. Where the water goes low enough this year to show something that hasn't seen sun in sixty years. What we're really doing isn't salvage. It's—" He stopped. Started again. "It's a delivery. Of something that belongs to people who were never paid for it. And there are men behind us, and men ahead of us, and some of them would burn the whole valley to keep what's in that delivery from seeing daylight."

"In the boat? Now?"

"No." Coral's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "In my head. Where it's been for twenty years."

Bo absorbed that. He looked downriver, at the black water running over its bars, honest and shallow and getting shallower, and he thought about center of gravity, and he thought about Denny Okafor's last transmission, calm as a weather report, and he thought about Wren asleep in the boat with her hand on her camera.

"Does Vale know?"

"She knows there's a there. She doesn't know what's in it."

"But you promised her."

"I promised her."

"And the promise is good?"

Coral was quiet long enough that the bird called again, its hinge-squeak up and down the dark. "The promise is good," he said. "Everything else, I'm still—" His hand opened and closed at his side, a mechanic's gesture, a pilot's gesture, a man testing a grip. "I'm still flying it, Bo. Day by day. That's the truth. It's not a good truth. But it's the one I've got."

Bo nodded slowly. It wasn't everything. It wasn't even most of everything. But it was more than Coral had given anyone in twenty years, and Bo had learned the hard way that you judged a repair not by whether it was perfect but by whether the man making it kept working at it instead of painting over the crack.

"Okay," Bo said. "Here's my terms. One: no more course changes I don't get explained, even in general terms. Two: if it comes apart—if the ministry boys catch us, if the chest surfaces, if anything goes loud—you tell the crew the truth before you tell anybody else anything. They've earned that. Three—" he picked up his mug, drained the tar at the bottom, grimaced—"you let Wren grow up inside whatever this is instead of outside it. She's already in it, Captain. She counted the miles off the *Marta C.*'s track before I did. Kids always do. The only choice you get is whether she finds out from you or without you."

Coral flinched. Actually flinched, small, like a man touching a hot manifold. Because Bo had put his finger on the exact bearing, the one deep in the case, and they both knew it.

"You've been talking to her," Coral said.

"I've been listening to her. There's a difference, and it's the difference between a mechanic and a parts-swapper." Bo stood, joints announcing themselves the way forty-year-old joints do. "We're good, Captain. For now. But I'm telling you straight, the way I'd tell you about an engine: I can keep this running. I can't keep it running clean. Every mile we go, the knock gets louder, and there's a point past which no amount of my skill fixes what's worn inside. When we get to your there—whatever's waiting in that water—you deal with it all the way. All of it. Or I will, and you won't like my methods."

Coral almost smiled. "Your methods involve a wrench."

"My methods involve a wrench," Bo agreed, "applied with love."

They walked back to camp together through the wet dark, two men carrying different weights toward the same fire. Ines's flashlight was still on under her tarp, a small yellow planet. Wren's breathing came slow and even from the boat. The river ran lower than yesterday. Tomorrow it would run lower than today.

Bo lay down on his pad with his boots on, because old habit, and stared up at the tarp, and listened—not to the forest, not to the water, but to the shape of the camp, the way he'd listen to an engine at idle, hunting the knock.

It was quieter tonight. Not gone. Quieter. Like a bearing that had been given oil, temporarily, by a man who knew it wouldn't last.

He was nearly asleep when Wren's voice came out of the boat, soft, pitched for him alone across the embers:

"Bo? Did you fix it?"

He smiled in the dark. Smart kid. Listening at idle herself.

"Not yet, small fry," he murmured. "But I got the panel off."

Downriver, past the last bend, past the gravel bars rising out of the falling water like the backs of sleeping animals, the drowned outpost waited with its bell and its bones and its sixty years of held breath—and tomorrow, God help them all, they'd fly the last leg straight into it.
