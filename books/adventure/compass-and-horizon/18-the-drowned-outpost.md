## 18. The Drowned Outpost

The river told us we were close before anything else did.

It changed its voice. For six days it had been talking to us—gurgling over gravel bars, slapping the hull, muttering through the reeds in that endless low conversation water has with itself. Then, on the morning of the seventh day, it went quiet. Not calm. Quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone stops breathing.

I stood at the bow while Tomas poled us through the shallows and felt that silence come up through my boot soles like a current of cold air from a cellar door left open.

"Captain," Bo said behind me. She'd been running her thumb along the edge of her wrench for an hour, a habit she thought nobody noticed. "Water's wrong."

"I know."

"Wrong how?"

"Too clear." I nodded at the surface ahead of us. All the way upriver the water had been the color of steeped tea, thick with silt the dry season hadn't yet settled out. Here it ran glass-bright, so transparent I could see the sand rippling forty feet down, and shadows among the sand that weren't fish.

Ines came forward to stand beside me. She had her grandfather's half-map folded into her shirt pocket where she kept it, and she had not slept much—I could see it in the bruised skin under her eyes—but her voice was steady. "The tributary," she said. "It should be here. On his survey there's a tributary feeding in from the east."

"There is." I pointed with my chin at a wall of cane two hundred yards downstream. "It's underground this time of year. Comes back with the floods. He mapped it in the wet season, so his river's fatter than this one."

She looked at me sideways. She'd been doing that more often since Sable Bend, since the graves, since I'd shown her the ledger and kept the letters shut inside the chest like a hand pressed over a mouth. Every look was a question now. Every look cost me something to meet.

"How far?" Wren asked. She was crouched on the cargo crate amidships, elbows on knees, watching the bottom slide past beneath us with the unblinking attention she gave everything. She'd barely spoken since the mission station. Children's graves will do that to a person. They'd done it to me once, though I'd had twenty years to bury the memory deeper than any grave.

"Half a day," I said. "If the channel holds."

The channel held. The river narrowed, the banks rose into clay cliffs stitched with tree roots, and by early afternoon we were poling through water no deeper than a man's chest, so still and clear that our own reflections traveled beneath us like drowned twins. Ferro cooked nothing. Nobody asked him to. Even he understood that some approaches you make hungry and silent.

I had been here before. That was the thing none of them knew—not Ines, not Wren, not Bo with her sharp eyes and sharper questions. I had stood on these banks as a boy whose name wasn't Coral, and I had watched men in government grey load families onto boats, and I had been lifted onto a plane by hands I never saw again. Twenty years I'd flown over this bend of the river at altitudes where it was only a brown ribbon in green cloth, and I had never once let myself look down.

Now the river itself was making me look.

* * *

We made camp on the eastern bank where the clay shelved down flat, because I remembered—it came back to me the way a scar itches—that the western bank flooded first and fastest. Tomas set the dive lines. Bo stripped the outboard and began the ritual argument she always had with its carburetor. Ferro built a fire small enough to hide and started rice. And Ines stood at the water's edge with her boots off and her trousers rolled, staring across at the far bank as if she could read something written there.

I went to stand beside her. It was becoming a habit I didn't trust.

"You've been here before," she said. Not a question. She said it quietly, so the others wouldn't hear.

"I told you. I fly this country."

"No." She turned to face me. The late light caught the river behind her and turned it to hammered brass. "You *knew* the tributary goes underground. You knew which bank floods first. You knew the channel would hold at dead-low water, and you've been steering us like a man reading a book he wrote himself." Her eyes held mine, and they weren't angry. That was the worst part. They were patient. "I'm not asking you to tell me what you know, Coral. I'm telling you that I know you know."

The truth sat in my chest like a stone I'd swallowed years ago and had finally begun to work its way up. I could feel its edges.

"When we find it," I said, "you'll understand why I don't say things."

"That's what frightens me," she said. "That sounds like a man planning an apology instead of an answer."

Before I could decide whether that deserved a reply—and I still don't know if it did—Tomas shouted from the shallows.

* * *

He was standing waist-deep with his dive mask pushed up on his forehead, pointing downstream, and his voice had gone strange. "Coral. Captain. You need to see this."

We waded out to him. The water dropped away fast past the third step, going from knee-deep to over-my-head within the length of a boat, and Tomas anchored himself on a submerged ridge of stone and gestured down.

I put my face in the water.

The clarity that had unsettled Bo was a gift now. Forty feet below us, lit by the afternoon sun falling straight down through the still water, lay the foundations of a town.

Walls. Streets. A grid of pale stone laid out neat as a hymnal, softened by two decades of silt but unmistakable—a street running north-south, another crossing it, the rectangles of house-foundation after house-foundation, a long low shape that could only have been a warehouse, and at the center of it all, rising from the floodplain floor like a finger pointing at the sky, a square tower of dressed stone with arched openings on every side.

A bell tower.

Nobody spoke. I think none of us breathed. Above us the river went on being a river, indifferent, glittering, carrying its leaves and its light, and beneath us a whole community lay drowned in its sleep, streets and all, preserved in cold water like a fly in amber.

"The charts show nothing here," Ines whispered. Her voice came out cracked. "Nothing. My grandfather's survey shows a trading post, a fuel depot, a mission. A dot. This is—" She stopped. Started again. "This is a town."

"It was," I said. My own voice sounded like it belonged to somebody else. "San Cristóbal del Río. That's what they called it." The name came out of me whole, unbidden, in a child's accent I hadn't used in twenty years, and I heard Ines go very still beside me.

"You said you'd never heard of it," she said softly.

"I said a lot of things."

Tomas dove. We watched his shadow detach from ours and sink, arms pulling easy, down toward the street grid, and we watched him land in the middle of what had been a road and walk—actually walk, upright, kicking gently—along a street where people had once carried water and argued about prices and taught children their letters. He stopped at one of the foundations. Reached down. Came up holding something pale and elongated.

When he surfaced he was holding a door. A wooden door, waterlogged black, with an iron handle, and he cradled it in both arms like something rescued from a fire.

"Houses are intact," he said, panting, treading water. "Roofs collapsed but walls standing. There's furniture, Coral. There's a table down there with bowls on it. Bowls." His voice climbed. "Who evacuates a town and leaves the dishes?"

Nobody answered him. The question just hung there over the bright water, and I felt the awe that had been swelling in my chest all afternoon begin, very slowly, to curdle.

Because I knew the answer. I had known it since I was seven years old, standing on the western bank in the rain, watching. Evacuees pack. Evacuees argue about what to take. Evacuees leave in daylight, on schedules, with lists.

These people had not evacuated. These people had been *removed*—taken off in the night or in haste, taken without warning, taken so completely that the bowls were still on the tables. And the difference between those two words, *evacuated* and *removed*, was the difference between a tragedy and a crime, and the old empire had spent twenty years and an entire archive making sure the second word never got written down.

"Get the lines rigged," I heard myself say. "Tomorrow at first light we bring up what we can. Carefully. Everything gets catalogued before it leaves the water."

"Coral." Bo had come down to the waterline, wiping her hands. She was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read. "What is this place?"

"A grave," I said. "That's what it is. Treat it like one."

* * *

That night the fire burned low and nobody sang, which for Ferro's crew was unprecedented; the man hummed when he stirred porridge, hummed when he mended nets, hummed in his sleep sometimes. Tonight he sat with his big hands wrapped around a tin mug and stared into the flames, and around him the rest of us did the same, each of us privately drowning in the image of that street, that table, those bowls.

Wren broke the silence. She'd been quiet all evening, sitting with her knees drawn up, and when she spoke her voice was careful, the way it got when she was working up to something. "The bell tower. Could we get the bell up?"

"Why?" Tomas asked.

"Because bells are for calling people." She said it simply, the way children say devastating things. "If the people were taken away, maybe they're still somewhere they can hear it."

I looked at her—at this girl I had found half-starved on a dock and raised without ever once saying the word *daughter*—and my throat closed so hard I couldn't have answered her if my life depended on it. Somewhere they can hear it. As if the removed might still be listening. As if the dead and the scattered were owed a summons home.

"We'll try," Bo said, when I couldn't. She said it looking at me, daring me to contradict her. "Bell's iron. Heavy, but we've got blocks and line. If the tower's sound, we can rig it."

"If the tower's sound," Tomas said, "there's a belfry. Bells have inscriptions. Foundry marks, dates, sometimes dedications." He glanced at Ines. "Your grandfather was a surveyor, not a founder?"

"He was an engineer before the war," she said. "He apprenticed at a foundry in his youth. Why?"

"Lucky guess," Tomas said, and rolled into his blanket.

I didn't roll anywhere. I sat up long after the others slept, watching the moon come up over the drowned town and silver the water above it, and I thought about the chest lashed under the supply tarp ten feet from where I sat—the chest with the ledger and the letters, the letters I had not shown Ines, the letters with the name Vale written in a clerk's careful hand on a list headed REMOVALS, THIRD CONVOY. Tomorrow we would raise a bell with a foundry mark, and Ines would read it, and she would start asking questions I had spent twenty years making sure no one could ask.

And underneath all of it, underneath the dread like sediment in still water, something else was moving. Because tomorrow I would swim down into the street where I had learned to walk, and I would pass the foundation of the house where a boy named something other than Coral had slept, and I did not know—could not know—which of those dark doorways had been mine.

At dawn, the river was lower still. The top course of the tower's stonework stood clear of the water, black and dripping, and on its weathered face, carved deep by some mason who'd wanted it remembered, were words I had spent two decades pretending I couldn't read.

Ines was already wading toward them.
