## 23. The Surveyors Arrive

Wren had learned to read boats the way other children learned to read faces.

She'd grown up on the strips and landings, half-hidden behind fuel drums and cargo nets, watching vessels come in. A trader's boat came in loud and loose, everyone talking at once. A smuggler's boat came in quiet, riding low, pretending to be empty. But a government boat—Wren knew this better than her own handwriting—came in *patient*. It didn't hurry because it didn't have to. It arrived like a signature already dried on a page, like a thing that had happened somewhere above your head, in an office you would never see, before you ever heard its engine.

So when she stood at the treeline with the others and watched the launch put ashore at dead-low water, she felt the fear arrive in her stomach before it reached her head: not the fear of men with guns, but the fear of paperwork. The kind that could make a person into a line item. The kind that could make a massacre into a *file*.

There were four of them coming up the mud-slick bank. Three wore the gray of the Ministry of Interior Surveys, and one—the big one at the rear, carrying nothing but a canvas satchel he held like it might bite him—wore no uniform at all.

"That's them," Bo said quietly. "The two from the airstrip office. The tall one's called Havelock. He does the talking."

"And the other?" Ines asked.

"Junior officer," Bo said. "Reyes. He mostly writes."

Wren watched the junior officer as they climbed. He was younger than she'd expected—younger than Coral, certainly, maybe not much older than twenty-five—and he climbed the bank like a man who hadn't been told what he was climbing toward. His eyes went everywhere: the exposed foundations of the drowned outpost, black and skeletal in the morning light; the mission bell lying half-buried where Tomas had levered it free; the chest itself, sitting open under a tarp between two supply crates like a patient waiting for a doctor who was late.

His eyes stopped on the chest. Stayed there. Something moved across his face that Wren couldn't name—not greed, not fear. Closer to recognition. Like a man seeing a photograph of a house he'd lived in before he could remember.

Coral stepped forward to meet them, and Wren saw him do the thing he did when he was most afraid: he became very still, and very polite, and very boring.

"Morning," Coral said. "You're a long way from the survey office."

"We're precisely where we intend to be, Mr.—" The tall one consulted a card he clearly didn't need to consult. "Lockridge. Senior Surveyor Havelock, Ministry of Interior. This is Officer Reyes. Our divers and our records clerk are aboard. You'll forgive us if we don't present credentials twice—we understand you've had a copy of the salvage notification since the airstrip."

"I got a piece of paper," Coral said. "I didn't get an explanation."

"The explanation is standing behind you." Havelock's gaze traveled past Coral, unhurried, cataloguing: Ines with her arms crossed, Bo with his wrench still in his hand like a habit rather than a threat, Tomas dripping river water onto the stones, Ferro holding a pot handle like a weapon he was disappointed not to use. And Wren. His eyes paused on Wren a half-second longer than on anyone else, and something in the pause made her want to step backward into the trees.

"You've located the outpost site," Havelock said. "You've raised artifacts of colonial-era provenance, including—" he nodded at the tarp, "—a sealed document chest bearing the imperial registry mark. Under Statute 44 of the Rivers and Reclamation Act, all such materials are property of the Crown-in-Right-of-the-Ministry, pending archival review. I'm instructed to take possession."

"Instructed by whom?"

"By the file." Havelock said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that was worse than if he'd said *by the Minister*. "File designation Kestrel-Nine. Opened in the year of the evacuation. Never closed."

The words landed strangely. Wren watched Coral's shoulders—not moving, exactly, but settling, the way a house settles when something heavy is placed upstairs.

"Never closed," Coral repeated.

"It's not unusual," Reyes said suddenly, and then seemed surprised to hear his own voice. He cleared his throat. "Files like this—they're supposed to be closed within five years. Compensation assessed, claims settled, record sealed. Most of them were. This one wasn't." He looked down at his satchel. "It's been sitting open for sixty years. Nobody wanted to be the one to sign it shut."

"Then sign it shut now," Bo said. "Seems easy. You're here, you've got a pen."

"It isn't a matter of a pen." For the first time, Havelock looked tired. Not performative-tired, the way officials got tired when they wanted sympathy. Actually tired, in the deep-set way of a man who had been reading something for a long time in bad light. "May we sit? It's early, and what I have to say is long, and I'd rather say it once."

Nobody moved to offer seats. Ferro, magnificently, turned and began banging around the cook fire as though breakfast were the only emergency in existence, which meant there was nowhere formal to sit and everyone had to stand, which Wren understood was Ferro's way of fighting.

Havelock took the lack of hospitality without offense. He walked a few steps to the edge of the exposed foundations and looked out over them—the grid of drowned walls emerging from the falling river like the ribs of something enormous—and when he spoke again, his voice had changed register. It was lower. It was almost careful.

"Do any of you know why this outpost doesn't appear on maps?"

"Rivers move," Coral said. "Towns drown. It happens."

"Towns drown," Havelock agreed. "And when they drown, someone draws the new shoreline. Someone notes the abandonment. There's a form for it—it's a small form, honestly, a half-page. *Settlement decommissioned, date, cause.*" He crouched, touched the black stone of a foundation wall, and stood again with wet fingers. "For this settlement, that form was never filed. Instead there is a file six hundred pages thick, and in sixty years, eleven officers have been assigned to close it, and eleven officers have requested reassignment instead. I am the twelfth."

"Why?" Ines asked. Her voice was steady but Wren saw her hands weren't. "Why couldn't they close it?"

"Because closing it requires stating what happened here." Havelock turned to face them fully. "And what happened here, according to the file, is not an evacuation. It is listed internally as a *removal*. Forty-one households. One hundred and ninety-three people, of whom sixty-two were children. They were promised transport, resettlement land, and compensation payable upon arrival. The transports left. The compensation was"—he paused, and Wren watched him choose the word with visible distaste—"deferred. Then deferred again. Then the ledger was transferred to an archive that flooded, and the claims were transferred to an office that burned, and by the time anyone reconstructed the paper trail, the statute of limitations had expired on every claim except the ministry's own right to possess the evidence of what it owed."

The silence after that was total. Even the river seemed to go quiet, sliding past the foundations without comment.

"They never paid," Ines said. It wasn't a question.

"The file contains signed receipts from every household head acknowledging the promise," Havelock said. "One hundred and ninety-three names. The payments were authorized three times. Three separate ministers declined to release funds, each time citing the pending review. The review has been pending for sixty years." He looked at the chest under the tarp. "That chest, Mr. Lockridge, is the only complete set of original documents in existence. Everything else burned or flooded, conveniently, twice. If that chest comes back with me, it goes into archival review, and archival review, historically, takes"—he almost smiled, and it was a terrible smile—"as long as it needs to."

"And if it doesn't come back with you?" Coral asked.

"Then I write a report saying it doesn't exist, and the file stays open another decade, and some thirteenth officer inherits it." Havelock shrugged. "Or I write a report saying it was destroyed in transit. That one closes files fastest. Everyone signs, nobody reads, and the hundred and ninety-three stay exactly where they've always been, which is nowhere."

Wren's chest hurt. She realized she was gripping the strap of the camera she'd carried since Sable Bend—the salvaged camera, the one with the film she'd already loaded, already begun. She forced her hands to loosen.

"But you're the twelfth," she said.

Every head turned to her. She hadn't meant to speak. Havelock regarded her with an expression she couldn't parse—surprise, maybe, or the look of a man noticing, for the first time, how young some of the people standing in front of him were.

"I'm the twelfth," he agreed.

"What makes you different?"

He considered the question seriously, which she hadn't expected. Officials didn't take questions from girls seriously. This one did.

"Honestly? I read the whole file," he said. "All six hundred pages. My predecessor read the summary. The summary says *administrative irregularities*. The full file says names. Birthdates. A school register with attendance marks through the last week. Ration tallies. Letters home that were never delivered because the postal run stopped." He glanced at Reyes. "Officer Reyes read it too. He's the one who found the compensation authorizations. All three of them. Signed, funded, and countermanded."

Reyes had gone very red. "They were going to pay," he said, to no one in particular, as though he'd been carrying the sentence a long way and finally set it down. "The money existed. It was appropriated. Somebody just—kept not releasing it. For sixty years. Because releasing it meant admitting the removal happened the way it happened."

"And the centenary," Ines said slowly. Wren saw her mind working, fast and cold. "The ceremonies next year. Sixty years since the removal. The ministry wants the file closed *before* the anniversary."

Havelock didn't insult her by denying it. "A closed file makes a clean ceremony," he said. "An open file makes questions. Questions at a centenary make careers end. Including mine." He let that sit. "I'm telling you this because you're going to figure it out anyway, and because I've spent nine months reading about these people, and I find I'd rather not lie about them on top of everything else that was done to them."

Ferro had stopped banging the pots. Even Ferro was listening.

"So what happens now?" Coral asked. His voice was level, but Wren knew him well enough—well enough to know she didn't know him at all, really—to hear the strain underneath, like a wire humming. "You take the chest. We get a receipt. The file closes. The ceremony happens. The river keeps the rest."

"That's the instruction I was given, yes."

"And?"

Havelock looked at him for a long moment. "And I haven't decided yet whether I follow instructions," he said. "Which is a sentence I never expected to say aloud, and which my career will very likely not survive either way." He turned to Reyes. "Show them."

Reyes opened the satchel with hands that weren't quite steady and drew out a folder—thin, typed, official—and laid it on a crate. Inside, Wren could see, even from where she stood, rows of names. Columns. Amounts. A number beside each name that had never been paid, printed in a font designed to look permanent.

Ines stepped forward first. Of course she did. She scanned the list top to bottom, and Wren watched her finger rise, travel down the column of names, and stop.

Her finger stayed there a long time.

"Vale," Ines said. "Elias Vale, surveyor. Household of four." She looked up at Havelock, and her voice had gone strange and bright. "He was promised forty pounds and resettlement land. He sent letters for two years afterward asking where it was. The file has his letters?"

"The file has his letters," Havelock said gently. "Unanswered."

Ines closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked at Coral, and whatever passed between them was private and enormous, and Wren looked away from it, because some things even a girl who read boats shouldn't watch.

Coral stepped up to the crate. He looked down at the list of names—the hundred and ninety-three, the sixty-two children—and Wren watched his face do something she had never seen it do in all her years of watching him, which was to stop hiding entirely. Just for a second. Just long enough.

Then Havelock straightened, and the official came back into his spine like a rod.

"Here is what happens now," he said. "I am required to secure the chest. I will do so—under guard, aboard my vessel, logged properly, so that no one can later claim it vanished. What I am *not* required to do is hurry." He looked out at the falling river, at the sandbars emerging daily, at the dry season tightening its grip on the water like a fist. "The river drops another foot by week's end. After that, this boat can't leave, and neither can your plane carry full load. We are all stranded together until the rains. So." He turned back to them. "I propose we use the time. All of it. Every page, every name, read properly, witnessed properly, recorded properly—by all of us, together, so that whatever happens to the originals, the truth exists in more than one place. I can't promise the ministry will thank me. I can't promise any of us will escape this cleanly. But I did not fly nine hundred miles to bury these people a second time."

Silence again. Wren found she was breathing hard, though she hadn't run anywhere.

Bo spoke first, gruff, suspicious. "Why should we believe you?"

"You shouldn't," Havelock said. "Believe the documents. They've waited sixty years to be believed. I'm just the boat they came in on."

Coral looked at the chest. At Ines. At the folder of unpaid promises lying open on the crate, the numbers marching down the page like a debt the country had learned to sleep on.

"Then we do it in the open," he said again, and this time it sounded less like an agreement and more like a vow. "Everything. Every name."

"Every name," Havelock agreed. He extended his hand, and after a moment Coral took it.

Wren slipped back toward the plane while the adults arranged the day—tables, witnesses, order of reading. Her heart was hammering, but her hands, when she checked the camera, were perfectly steady. The film in the tail compartment was already half-exposed. There was room for more.

Because she had listened carefully, harder than anyone, the way she'd taught herself to listen to boats. And underneath Havelock's honesty, underneath Reyes's shame, she had heard the thing they hadn't said:

Three officers had already reported this site's location by radio before they ever put ashore.

She'd heard the transmission crackle through the launch's open hatch while the men climbed the bank. Short. Coded. *Site confirmed. Chest intact. Requesting further instruction.*

Further instruction—from someone above Havelock, someone who hadn't come, someone whose answer hadn't arrived yet.

Whatever that answer was, it was coming upriver. And Wren intended to have every page photographed before it did.
