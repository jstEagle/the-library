## 24. Confiscation Order

They came at first light, the way official things always do—when the mist is still on the water and a man's defenses are still folded up inside him like a chart he hasn't opened yet.

Coral heard the outboard before he saw the boat: a government engine, well-maintained, running at exactly the speed regulations prescribed for approach to an archaeological site. Nobody in the river country ran an engine that politely unless they were being paid to.

He was already awake. He'd been awake most of the night, sitting by the cold fire pit with the chest between his feet like a dog he couldn't decide whether to keep or shoot. The surveyors' camp across the shallows had been quiet too—too quiet—and he'd learned long ago that quiet from officials meant paper moving somewhere upstream of him, forms being signed by men whose signatures weighed more than his whole airplane.

The boat grounded on the sandbar with a soft crunch. Reyes stepped out first, boots in the water, and behind him came Halloran—the lead surveyor—with a leather document case under one arm and the expression of a man who had rehearsed this on the ride over and still didn't like the taste of it.

"Captain Underhill." Halloran's voice carried flat across the water. "I'd ask you to gather your people."

"They're gathering," Coral said. They were. Bo had come out of the tent with her wrench already in her hand, which was Bo's version of a formal greeting. Tomas stood by the wrecked propeller they'd been rebuilding, arms crossed. Ferro emerged last, wiping his hands on his apron, and Wren—

Wren came out slow, eyes going straight to the chest, then to Coral. He gave her the smallest shake of his head. *Not now. Not here.* She held his gaze for three seconds longer than she needed to, and he understood that she was telling him something back. He just didn't know what yet.

Halloran opened the document case on a folding table Ferro hadn't offered but set up anyway, because Ferro believed you fed people even when you were about to fight them, and fed men argued slower.

"By authority of the Ministry of Interior Surveys, Section Nine," Halloran read, "all materials recovered from the site designated Outpost K-7 are hereby placed under confiscation for archival review pending determination of historical custody."

Coral listened to the words the way he listened to weather reports: for what wasn't said. *Archival review.* He turned the phrase over. It had the smooth, closed feel of language built to end conversations rather than begin them.

"You mean you're taking it," Bo said.

"I mean the ministry is assuming temporary custodianship," Halloran said, without looking at her.

"For how long?"

"That will be determined by the review."

"And who conducts the review?"

"The review is conducted internally." Halloran finally looked up. He was not, Coral decided, a cruel man. He was worse than that: a tired man doing a job that required him to be precise about things that deserved to be spoken plainly. "Miss—Bo, is it? I've spent eleven days on this river watching your captain run rapids at night to lose us, and I'm not going to pretend we're all friends here. The ministry takes the chest. You get a receipt. That's the honest version."

Coral almost respected him for it.

* * *

The receipt took twenty minutes to fill out because Halloran insisted on itemizing. Coral watched him write *one sealed strongbox, brass-bound, bearing the seal of a defunct colonial administration* and thought about how many words it took to describe a thing without saying what it was. A ledger full of names. Letters written by dying men. Proof that two hundred people had been loaded onto barges in 1911 and never arrived anywhere.

"Sign here," Halloran said, "acknowledging receipt of compensation schedule C-4."

"There's no compensation listed," Coral said.

"The schedule is attached."

Coral read the attachment. It was one page. It said, in effect, that any persons able to demonstrate descent from residents of Outpost K-7 might, upon application through proper channels, be considered for discretionary remuneration subject to budgetary availability in the relevant fiscal period. It was the most elegant sentence he had ever seen constructed entirely out of nothing.

"My grandfather died waiting for a page like that," Ines said.

She had come to stand beside him, and Coral felt her there the way you feel a change in the wind—not seeing it, knowing it. Her voice was level. That frightened him more than shouting would have.

"Then your family has standing to apply," Halloran said.

"We applied. In 1938. In 1952. In 1961." She tapped the page. "There are letters in my grandfather's file answering each application. Do you know what they say? They say the records were lost in a flood. Your ministry has been losing the same flood for fifty years."

Reyes shifted his weight. Coral watched the junior officer look at the river, then at the chest, then at nothing at all, and thought: *there's a crack in that man, and someone poured water into it.*

"Captain," Halloran said, turning to him, and now his voice dropped into something quieter, meant only for the space between them. "A word."

They walked down to the waterline, out of earshot—or what passed for earshot in a camp where Bo could lip-read a conversation at forty paces and everyone knew it.

"I'll say this once, off the record," Halloran said. "You know what happens after archival review. The chest goes to a warehouse in the capital. The warehouse floods, or burns, or gets reorganized by a deputy minister with a new filing system. In fifteen years a researcher requests the file and is told it cannot be located. I have seen it done. I have probably done it, twice, without knowing what I was burying."

"Then why are you telling me?"

"Because I read the manifest last night." Halloran looked out at the water. His jaw worked once. "Two hundred and six names, Captain. Ration tallies that stop mid-month. A requisition order for lime dated four days after the outpost supposedly evacuated peacefully. Lime, for the pits." He exhaled through his nose. "I signed on to survey riverbeds. Somewhere along the way the surveys started having bodies in them, and nobody upstairs wants the maps redrawn."

"So don't take it."

"I have orders." He said it simply, without excuse, and Coral found he couldn't hate him for it. That was the trap of men like Halloran—they made hatred impossible and cooperation worse than hatred. "There's a launch coming tomorrow morning. It takes the chest and it takes you. All of you, downriver to Puerto Verde. You'll be flown home from there at ministry expense. Consider it severance."

"And if we're not on the sandbar when it comes?"

Halloran finally turned and looked at him fully, and Coral saw the other man measuring him—the same way Coral measured weather, reading pressure, deciding whether a front was worth flying around or through.

"Then I write in my report that the salvage crew departed voluntarily ahead of the launch, and I note that the confiscated materials were secured aboard the ministry vessel without incident." A pause. "I am not offering you a way to steal government property, Captain. I am telling you what my report will say regardless of what actually happens, because my report needs to be simple and my career needs to continue. What you do with that information is between you and the river."

He walked back to the table. Coral stood at the waterline a moment longer, looking at the brown current sliding past, carrying everything downstream and keeping nothing.

* * *

They argued in the tent with the flap tied shut and Ferro's radio playing low, tuned between stations so the static would blur their voices to anyone listening with a parabolic microphone—which Bo insisted existed, and Tomas insisted did not, and which they kept anyway.

"They'll inventory it in the capital," Bo said. "Then it goes in a crate. Then the crate goes in a row. I've worked airfields my whole life; I know how crates die. Slowly, in the dark, next to crates nobody remembers paying for."

"So we take it back," Tomas said. "Tonight. Reyes sleeps forty feet from it. One man, one crowbar."

"One man with a sidearm and a radio," Coral said.

"Then we take the radio man too."

"And then we're thieves fleeing a government site, and every launch on this river hunts us, and the story becomes about us instead of them." Coral rubbed his eyes. Twenty years of staying invisible, and now invisibility was the one thing he couldn't afford. "Listen to me. All of you. The chest isn't the cargo anymore. It stopped being the cargo the moment Halloran wrote that manifest. Two hundred and six names on paper in a government warehouse—that's a file. Two hundred and six names in the mouths of the families on this river—that's a reckoning. We don't need the paper. We need what's *in* the paper."

Ines sat very still. "You want copies."

"I want the truth loose enough that no warehouse can hold all of it." He looked at Wren, and Wren looked back, and neither of them blinked, and something passed between them that he would only understand two days later, standing on a sandbar with letters scattered in the sand. "We have until the launch comes tomorrow morning. Bo, how's the tail section?"

Bo frowned. "Why?"

"Humor me."

"It's sound. Why?"

"No reason. Keep it that way."

* * *

Evening came down purple and hot. The ministry camp cooked rice over a smokeless fire, disciplined as everything else about them. Ferro sent Reyes a plate of fried plantain anyway, carried over by Wren, because Ferro believed hunger softened men faster than argument, and also because Wren wanted to look at the junior officer up close, and Coral pretended not to notice either thing.

He sat by the dead fire with Ines while the light went out of the sky.

"He's right, you know," she said. "About the warehouse. My grandfather's survey disappeared the same way. Fifty years of families writing letters into a hole."

"Then we stop mailing letters."

"What does that mean, Coral?" She turned to face him. In the last light her eyes were nearly black. "Because I've watched you carry that chest for three hundred miles like it was made of glass, and today you handed it over in twenty minutes, and now you're talking in riddles about stopping mailing letters. Tell me what you're planning. I've earned that much. We both know I've earned more than that."

He wanted to tell her. God, he wanted to. There was a whole speech in him—about the tail compartment, about the camera he suspected Wren had salvaged off the wreck at Sable Bend, about a strip of high ground upriver that existed on no map because he had cut it himself with a machete when he was nineteen years old and still answering to another name. But the words caught where words always caught, in the old scar tissue, and what came out instead was:

"When the time comes, you'll be standing next to me."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got tonight."

She studied him for a long moment, and he braced for anger, and instead she did something worse. She reached over and put her hand on his forearm, briefly, and left it there.

"Tomorrow, then," she said. "But Coral—if you hand these people's dead over to a filing cabinet and call it duty, I will spend the rest of my life making sure the world knows exactly what was in that chest. With you or without you."

"Yes," he said. "I know. That's why I love you."

It was out before he could weigh it, the way cargo shifts in a turn. Ines went still. The river went on talking to itself. Neither of them took it back, and neither of them picked it up, and they sat together in the dark holding the words between them like a live wire, until the watch bell rang and the night swallowed everything.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He lay in the tent listening to the camp breathe—Tomas's snore, Bo turning over, Wren's breathing suspiciously absent from her bedroll for the better part of an hour—and he thought about duty, that word Halloran had used like a key. Duty to the state. Duty to the dead. Everyone claimed the dead. Nobody asked them.

Near midnight he rose, quietly, and walked past the ministry sentry—who nodded at him, because Captain Underhill walking toward the latrine trench at midnight was the most predictable event on this river—and paused beside the plane.

The tail section sat in the moonlight, riveted and sound, exactly as Bo had promised.

He laid his palm flat against the fabric, feeling the cool metal underneath, and thought about a boy on a barge in 1911, and a boy in a mail plane in 1931, and the forty years of miles between them, and how every mile had been a way of not arriving anywhere.

Tomorrow the launch would come. Tomorrow the paperwork would close its jaws around the truth and swallow, and some clerk in the capital would stamp a drawer shut on two hundred and six lives.

Unless the tail section of a crippled cargo plane was heavier than it looked.

Coral took his hand off the metal, went back to his bedroll, and lay staring at the canvas until the sky went grey.

At dawn, Wren was missing, and so was the camera.
