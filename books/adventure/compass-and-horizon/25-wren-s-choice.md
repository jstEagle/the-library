## 25. Wren's Choice

Wren had learned to steal the way other children learned to whistle—by imitation, by practice, by a certain shameless delight in the doing. Not from Coral, who would deny it under oath. From watching him. From twenty years of watching him move through rooms where he wasn't supposed to be, pockets filling themselves with spark plugs and tinned peaches and other men's secrets, his face arranged into the particular blankness of a man thinking about nothing at all.

She used that face now, walking out of camp before the sky had finished deciding whether to be grey or gold, the camera wrapped in her spare shirt against her chest like something with a heartbeat.

The camera was Tomas's. It had come off a wreck two seasons back—a Leica with a cracked leather case and a shutter that stuck unless you coaxed it, which meant warming it in your hands first and pressing gently, gently, the way you'd press on a bird's chest to feel it breathing. Tomas had fixed the winding mechanism with a sliver of clock spring and declared it good for maybe forty exposures, and then he'd mostly forgotten about it, because Tomas forgot about everything that didn't float or sink. Wren had not forgotten. Wren kept a mental inventory of every object within a mile of any camp they made, the way Coral kept a mental inventory of every sandbar between here and the delta. It was how you survived being small: you knew where everything was, and everyone underestimated what knowing was worth.

She knew where the ledger was, too. That was the problem with grown-ups. They thought hiding a thing was the same as making it disappear. But Coral had moved the chest from the mission station to the plane yesterday afternoon, and she had watched him do it, and she had watched him choose the aft compartment—the one behind the baggage net, the one whose access panel sat directly beneath the tail section, the one he thought nobody remembered existed because he'd told Wren, three years ago, that it was rusted shut.

It wasn't rusted shut. She'd opened it herself when she was fourteen, looking for Christmas presents. She hadn't found presents. She'd found a coil of new brake cable and a tin of emergency rations and the clear understanding that Coral Underhill lied to her constantly, tenderly, and for her own good, and that this was simply what love looked like when it came from a man built out of locked compartments.

Well. Two could play at compartments.

* * *

The ledger lay inside the chest under a false bottom of oilcloth, and the chest itself sat wedged behind the netting where the confiscation order couldn't see it without crawling into the fuselage on its belly. The ministry men had photographed the chest's exterior, catalogued its dimensions, and slapped a paper seal across the lid—a seal that would have been more impressive if Bo hadn't already shown Wren how to duplicate one with a candle stub and a spoon handle, back when they'd needed to open Ferro's medicine crate without explaining why.

Wren did not break the seal. That mattered to her, though she couldn't have said why. Maybe because breaking it would make her a thief, and she wasn't stealing. She was copying. There was a difference, and the difference was going to matter later, when the questions came, and the questions were always going to come. Coral had taught her that too, without meaning to: *Never take what you can't explain having taken.*

She lifted the ledger out with both hands—it was heavier than it looked, swollen with river years, its boards furred green at the corners—and carried it up onto the wing where the light was coming up strong and clean off the water. The sandbar stretched away in both directions, pale as a held breath. Downstream, the ministry launch sat at anchor, its masthead light still burning stupidly in the daylight. Upstream, the drowned outpost waited under a hand's depth of water, its foundations dark shapes like teeth. Somewhere beyond the tree line, Ines was asleep, or pretending to be, and Coral was awake, because Coral was always awake now, lying in his bedroll staring at canvas the way he'd been doing since the surveyors arrived.

He'd been staring at the ceiling of the plane last night too. She'd seen him do it—seen him stand there in the dark with his palm flat against the tail section, holding still the way he held still when he was deciding something enormous, like whether to fly into weather or around it.

And then at dawn he'd gone looking for her, and she hadn't been in her bedroll, because she'd never been in her bedroll, because she'd been sitting in the shadow of the fuel drums since four o'clock waiting for exactly this kind of morning: the kind where everyone else was exhausted, and guilty, and busy with their own calculations.

She warmed the camera in her hands. Coaxed the shutter. Pressed gently.

*Click.*

The first page of the ledger, angled so the rising sun raked across the columns and threw every inked line into relief. Names down the left margin. Numbers down the right. Ration tallies, shipping manifests, dates going back further than Wren had been alive, further than Coral had been alive, back to a world that had officially never existed.

*Click.* Page two. *Click.* Page three.

Her grandfather—no. Not her grandfather. Her nothing; her no-one; the man whose blood she didn't share and whose silences she'd inherited whole. He'd taught her to read a manifest the way priests taught scripture: *the cargo list is the truest document a country keeps, because nobody lies to a customs man twice.* She understood now, in the cold way you understand things at five in the morning, that he'd been teaching her how to read this exact book. Every lesson about bills of lading and tonnage declarations had been a lesson in how to catch an empire forgetting what it had done.

*Click.* A page of names grouped under a heading she had to hold sideways to read: *REMOVED PERSONNEL — OUTPOST STAFF AND DEPENDENTS.* Removed. As if they were furniture. As if they were stones from a field.

She counted them without meaning to. It was a habit from the salvage work—you counted everything, twice, because your life depended on the count. Two hundred and six. She counted again. Two hundred and six.

*Click.*

The shutter stuck on the seventh exposure. She breathed on it, warmed it, waited. A heron stalked the shallows downstream, patient as a clerk. The ministry launch's engine turned over, coughed, died. Somebody aboard was having a bad morning, and Wren found she didn't care even slightly.

*Click.* *Click.* *Click.*

There were letters in the chest too. She'd seen them—bundles of them, tied with wire, soft as moth wings. She did not photograph those. Not because she didn't want to. Because there wasn't time, and because some part of her, the part Coral had raised, understood that letters were different from ledgers. A ledger was evidence. Letters were people talking, and people talking deserved better than to be stolen in the dark by a girl with a borrowed camera. When those letters got read—and they were going to get read, she'd make sure of it—they'd be read in the open, aloud, by someone with the right to read them.

Though she paused over one bundle anyway. The topmost envelope was addressed in a careful schoolroom hand, and the name on it stopped her breathing for a second:

*Vale.*

Ines's name. Ines's family, right there in the chest, tied up with wire like fish. And underneath the Vale bundle, another bundle, older, water-stained past reading except along one edge where a single word survived: *children.*

Wren looked at that word for a long time. Then she put the bundles back exactly as they'd lain, squared the oilcloth, lowered the false bottom, and closed the chest, and her hands were steady, and she was proud of them for it.

*Click.* The last frame went to the tally page—the master summary, the one that totaled everything, the page a court would want first. Forty exposures. She'd used thirty-eight. She rewound the film with the crank, slow, feeling the tension build, and when the spool slipped free into her palm it weighed almost nothing. A cylinder of celluloid an inch long. Two hundred and six lives, rolled up smaller than a cigarette.

That was the thing about paper, she thought. That was the thing nobody in the capital understood. You could seize a chest. You could stamp a drawer. You could order a crew downriver and put a seal on a lid and write *archival review* in beautiful ministerial handwriting. But you could not seize what you didn't know existed, and you could not burn a negative that was already somewhere else, and somewhere else was the only place anything true ever survived. Coral had survived by being somewhere else for forty years. Well. So would the truth.

* * *

Hiding the film took longer than taking it, because the plane's tail was Coral's territory and Coral missed nothing—but even Coral had blind spots, and his blind spots were all shaped like the past. He checked the tail section obsessively for structural cracks, for corrosion, for the ghost of some old repair. He never checked it for additions, because in his mind the tail was a place where things fell apart, not a place where things were put.

Wren crawled in through the access panel with a flashlight clamped in her teeth and a grease pencil behind her ear. The fuselage narrowed to a crawl, skin warm already in the climbing sun, smelling of dope and fabric and old rain. She worked her way back past the baggage bay, past the elevator cables hanging slack in their guides, to the junction where the tailcone met the last former—a seam Bo had re-skinned two seasons ago after the porcupine incident, a story Wren intended to carry to her grave because it was the only time she'd ever seen Bo laugh until he cried.

The re-skinned panel had a gap at its leading edge. Not a flaw—an allowance, for expansion, the kind of gap every riveted skin had. Wide enough for a film canister wrapped in waxed cloth and bound with safety wire. She fed the wire through two existing rivet holes, twisted it tight with her fingers, and pulled. Nothing shifted. Nothing rattled. She shook the skin with both hands, hard, the way Bo shook things, and the canister rode silent in its pocket of air.

Then she did the thing that made it hers instead of just hidden: she took out the grease pencil and wrote, inside the skin where no one would ever look unless they already knew, a single set of characters. Coordinates. The ones Ines had plotted for the drowned outpost, the ones Wren had memorized off the chart two nights ago while pretending to sleep. Latitude, longitude, and beneath them, small: *206.*

Not a message for anyone. A message for the future. If the plane burned, if the river took it, if the ministry stripped it for parts—someday, someone cutting into this skin would find numbers where numbers shouldn't be, and they'd wonder, and wondering was the seed the whole crop grew from.

She wrote her own mark under it, the little wading-bird glyph she'd been scratching into driftwood since she was six, and closed the panel, and slid back down the fuselage into the ordinary morning.

* * *

Coral was waiting for her at the wing root.

She saw him from thirty feet and made herself keep walking at the same pace, camera cradled openly in her arms like a cat, because the one thing you never did was change your gait when a man who read gaits was watching. His face was doing the blank thing. His eyes were doing the arithmetic thing.

"Morning," she said.

"Where were you?"

"Watching the birds." True, technically. There had been a heron. "Tomas's camera was in my kit. I wanted to test the shutter."

"Did you."

"I took two pictures." Also true, technically, if you rounded thirty-eight down generously. "Of the sunrise. Want to see?"

She held the camera up and worked the advance lever, empty and honest, and let him look at her face while she did it, and his eyes went from the camera to her hands to her eyes and back, running the calculation she could almost hear: *she's mine, I taught her, therefore she's capable of anything, therefore I know nothing.*

"You were gone two hours," he said.

"Birds are shy." She tucked the camera under her arm. "You were gone twenty years. We've all got our habits."

Something moved behind his face—not anger. Something wearier and more complicated, the look of a man realizing the lock he'd spent a lifetime maintaining had been picked by the person he'd installed it to protect. He looked past her at the plane, at the tail, and for one suspended second she thought he'd walk straight to it and put his hand on the panel and know.

Instead he said, quietly, "Whatever you're carrying, Wren—when the weather comes tonight, and it's coming, Bo says the front's turned—I need to know what's loose in this aircraft. Loose things kill us at altitude."

"Nothing's loose," she said. "Everything I care about is wired down tight."

He held her gaze a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the nod he gave when a decision had been made that he wasn't going to share yet, and walked away toward the fire where Ferro was burning the last of the good coffee, and Wren stood alone on the sandbar with her heart going like a sewing machine.

Downstream, the ministry launch's engine caught properly this time and held, a dirty diesel stutter rolling across the flat water. Figures moved on its deck. One of them raised a hand—not waving. Signaling. Measuring, maybe, the wind, or the distance, or them.

Bo appeared at the engine cowling, wrench in hand, squinting upriver at a smudge on the horizon that hadn't been there yesterday and was definitely not cloud. "Wren," he called, without turning around, in the voice he saved for bad news delivered calm. "Get your kit stowed. We're flying out ahead of that front, and we're taking passengers."

She looked at the tail of the plane—her tail, her secret, two hundred and six names riding in a waxed cloth behind the re-skinned panel—and felt the strangest sensation, half terror and half triumph, of a truth finally airborne.

The storm smudge thickened on the horizon, and the launch began to raise its anchor.
