## 26. The Long Climb

I knew the engine was going to quit on us before we ever left the sandbar. That's the thing about engines—they tell you everything if you're willing to listen, and I'd spent three days with my head inside that radial like a doctor listening to a bad heart. The number three cylinder had been running hot since the repair, the rings were shot, and she was burning oil at a rate that made my stomach tighten every time I checked the level. We'd rebuilt her from scrap—two dead engines along the bar, cannibalized for pistons and valves—and what we'd built was a machine that would run.

For a while.

"Tell me straight," Coral said, standing beside me in the gray light of dawn while the river slid past below the bank, low and brown and honest about how little water it had left. "Can she make it over the front?"

The front sat on the horizon to the west like a bruise coming up through the skin—a wall of cloud stacked purple-black from treetop level to somewhere above where anything alive wanted to be. Storm fronts in this country didn't drift. They marched. And between us and home was ninety miles of that, plus the heat thermals rising off the savanna like invisible chimneys, plus the smoke haze already thickening from fires nobody would ever fight.

"She'll make it or she won't," I said. "There's no straighter answer than that."

Coral looked at me for a long moment. He's got a face that doesn't give much away—I've flown with him eleven years and I can count the times I've seen him actually surprised—but there was something working behind his eyes that morning that I hadn't seen before. Not fear exactly. More like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like the sum of.

"We could wait out the front," he said. "Two days, maybe three."

"The strip won't hold three days. River's dropping an inch a night. You saw what happened to Tomas when he tried to walk out to the boat yesterday—he went in to his chest." I wiped my hands on a rag that was more grease than rag. "We go now or we walk. And the ministry men aren't walking anywhere."

That was the other arithmetic, the one none of us said out loud but all of us were doing. Reyes and Okafor stood at the edge of the camp with their kit bags, the junior officer pale and quiet, the senior one checking his watch like the weather owed him punctuality. Two government men aboard a plane carrying a salvaged ledger, a chest full of letters that could end careers, and a crew that had spent the last week lying to them by omission. If the engine quit over the forest, we'd all be in the same life raft, so to speak. There wasn't one. There was never one. Coral's philosophy on emergency equipment was that the best emergency equipment was not crashing, and mostly I'd agreed with him.

Mostly.

* * *

Loading the plane took an hour because everything weighed double what it should have. The chest went in last, lashed down in the cabin behind the seats where Wren usually rode, and I watched Okafor watch it go in. He didn't say anything. He'd stopped saying anything about the chest two days ago, which worried me more than if he'd been demanding it. A man who's given up arguing has either accepted something or decided to wait.

Wren climbed aboard without being told to, which was its own kind of statement. Coral opened his mouth—I saw the objection forming, the same one he'd been making since Sable Bend—and she looked at him with that flat stare she does, the one that makes her look about forty years old, and he closed his mouth again. Eleven years I've watched those two have entire arguments without words. She stowed herself against the fuselage wall with the camera bag between her feet, arms wrapped around her knees, and gave me a nod like a co-pilot confirming ready.

Ines took the seat behind Coral. She'd been quiet since the sandbar—since the letters spilled across it and the surveyors saw everything and the whole architecture of lies we'd been living inside came down around our ears. Quiet wasn't the word, maybe. Settled. Like someone who'd finally put down a weight she'd been carrying so long she'd forgotten it was heavy. She caught me looking at her and said, "Bo. How bad is the engine, really?"

Everyone asks the pilot's mechanic that question and everyone hopes for a lie. I liked her too much to give her one.

"It'll get us up," I said. "After that, we're negotiating."

She nodded like that was fair and buckled her harness.

Coral ran the start procedure with his usual economy—no wasted motion, no wasted words, primer, mags, clear—and the old girl coughed twice and caught. I stood on the float strut with my hand on the cowling and listened. Good compression on most cylinders, a faint metallic tick from number three that sounded like a clock counting down, oil pressure climbing slow but climbing. She sounded rough. She sounded alive. In this country that's often the same thing.

"Talk to me," Coral said through the window.

"She's angry but she's running. Don't lean her out on the climb. Keep the power smooth. And whatever you do, don't let her get cold at altitude—if you have to come down, come down hot."

He nodded once and turned forward. Through the cabin windows I saw the passengers settle: Reyes gripping the seat frame with both hands, Okafor reading some folded document like the flight was a bus route, Tomas grinning like this was the fun part, which for Tomas it probably was. Ferro wedged himself in back next to the chest with the serenity of a man who had made his peace with dying fat and happy.

I swung up into the right seat. Coral taxied us out onto the river channel, did his run-up facing into the wind, and then looked west at the front one more time. It had grown while we loaded. It always grows while you load.

"Everybody breathe," he said, not loudly, and pushed the throttle up.

* * *

The takeoff was the longest thirty seconds of my professional life.

Low water meant a short run. Low water meant the river was shallow enough that the floats started dragging sand before we were near flying speed. I felt it through the airframe—that grinding shudder as the boards kissed bottom—and Coral held her down anyway, holding every knot she'd give us, until the channel bent and he hauled her off with maybe five knots to spare. We cleared the far bank by less than the height of a man standing on another man's shoulders. Wren, who does not scare easily, made a sound like a kettle starting to think about boiling.

Then we were up, and climbing, and the engine was telling me everything.

She was pulling unevenly—you could feel it in the seat of your pants, that rhythmic hiccup every second revolution where number three wasn't contributing what it should. Oil temperature crept. I watched it the way you watch a fuse burn. Coral flew the climb exactly the way I'd told him: gentle bank, no steep turns, power smooth, mixture rich. Below us the river unspooled behind, brown and thin, and ahead the savanna rose toward the front in waves of heat shimmer and smoke.

"Oil temp's going to be our problem," I said, close to his ear under the engine noise. "Not the front. Me."

"How long?"

"If she stays like this? Ninety minutes. Maybe less if we climb high—the air's cooler up there, but thinner, and she's already starving."

"And if the front makes her worse?"

I didn't answer, which was an answer.

The front was closer than it looked, the way fronts always are. From ten miles out you could see the structure of it—this wasn't a squall line, it was a proper system, towering cumulus stacked into an anvil that spread east like a fist opening. Lightning flickered inside it, silent at this distance, like a lamp switching on and off in a house down the road. Between us and it, the air was already roughening. The thermals coming off the scorched ground hit us in random slaps, first from the left, then underneath, then a downdraft that dropped us fifty feet in two seconds and made Reyes yelp.

"Sorry about the ride," Coral called back, without turning around. "Tickets are nonrefundable."

Ferro laughed. Nobody else did.

We climbed to stay ahead of the terrain and the turbulence got worse instead of better, which is the deal you sign when you fly in this country in the wet season's last gasp. At four thousand feet the smoke haze thickened into a brown ceiling and the light went the color of weak tea. I had my head half-turned, watching the gauges, when the engine spoke to me directly for the first time.

A bang. Not a backfire—a bang, deep, like someone hitting the crankcase with a hammer from the inside. The whole airframe flinched. Oil pressure, which had been sitting at a comfortable sixty, dropped to forty-five and kept sliding.

"There it is," I said.

"What is it?" Coral's voice didn't change pitch. That's the thing about him—in eleven years I've heard him frightened exactly twice, and both times it sounded like boredom.

"I don't know yet." I leaned down toward the little inspection window where you can see the oil screen. The glass was dark. Too dark. "Either she threw a rod cap or she's pumping metal. Either way, pressure's falling and I can't fix it from here."

"Options."

"Turn back—we're twenty minutes behind us, and the sandbar strip might still be there, but we'd be landing downwind on a soft bar with a sick engine. Or keep going—sixty minutes to the strips at home, over the front, and pray she holds together."

Behind us, Okafor had lowered his document. He was looking at the oil gauge over my shoulder with the expression of a man discovering that airplanes are not, in fact, buses.

Coral was quiet for maybe four seconds. Then he pushed the nose down a few degrees, trading altitude for speed, and turned us west, straight at the wall of cloud.

"We go through," he said. "Home's past it. Everything's past it."

* * *

Flying into a storm front is not flying. It's being swallowed.

The first rain hit us like gravel—big warm drops that went to hail for about ninety seconds and then to a deluge so solid the windshield became a river. Coral had us on instruments inside a minute, which for Coral means flying by a pocket watch, a compass, and a lifetime of refusing to die. The turbulence in the cloud was nothing like the thermals outside. This was the sky itself convulsing, updrafts that slammed us upward and shoved the nose down, downdrafts that fell away beneath us like trapdoors. Lightning lit the cabin white through the windows, once, twice, close enough that I could smell it—that ozone sharpness cutting through the oil stink.

And through all of it, the engine kept talking.

Bang. Every few seconds now. The oil pressure needle lay down at twenty-five and trembled there like a drunk holding onto a lamppost. Temperature was off the scale. I could hear the change in her voice—she'd gone from rough to wrong, the difference between a man breathing hard and a man drowning.

"Bo." Coral's hand found my shoulder and squeezed once. He didn't have to say the rest. *You're the mechanic. Tell me what she needs.*

What she needed was a hangar and a miracle. What I could give her was less. I reached over and eased the mixture richer, feathered the cowl flaps wide open even though the rain was doing that job for free, and put my palm flat against the firewall to feel what the engine was feeling. The vibration had changed character. It wasn't the tick-tick-tick of a tired cylinder anymore. It was a heavy, loose knocking, the sound of metal finding metal where metal should never meet.

"She's finishing," I shouted over the roar. "It's not a question of if. It's when. Minutes, not hours."

"Where do you want to be when she quits?"

I looked at the compass, at the clock, at the gray chaos outside the glass, and did the arithmetic I'd been dreading. Downriver of the front, maybe eight miles, the river bent north around a ridge I knew from a supply run years ago. On the outside of that bend there was a sandbar—long, wide, firm enough that cattle crossed it in the dry months. If the water was low enough. If the light held. If the engine gave us the six or seven minutes we needed.

"Bear right, ten degrees. There's a bar on the river bend. Get us over it and I'll get us onto it."

"You sure?"

"No," I said. "But it's the only sure thing on offer."

He brought us around. Behind me I heard Ferro say, calm as ordering dinner, "Everyone brace. Hands and feet inside the ride." I heard Wren say something to Ines, quick and low, and Ines answer, and whatever it was it made Wren laugh—one short bark of a laugh, terrified and defiant, the bravest sound I heard that whole day. Even Reyes unbuckled his document case and wedged it under the seat like a man accepting that the paperwork could sink with the rest of us.

We broke out of the worst of the cell into hard rain and dying light, and there it was below us: the ridge, the bend, the river running thin and braided around a bar of pale sand maybe four hundred meters long. The wind was quartering across it from the storm at our backs, which meant we'd land downwind, fast, with an engine that was now knocking loud enough to hear over the rain.

"One pass," Coral said. "No second tries."

"Flaps half. Come in flat and fast and hold her off as long as you dare. When she touches, I'll kill the mags and you stand on the brakes till your boots go through the floor."

The engine banged—a real bang this time, and the propeller visibly stuttered, and for one full second the only sound was the rain and the wind in the wires. Then she caught again, snarling, furious, alive out of pure spite. I've rebuilt forty engines in my life and I will swear on any book you like that in that moment that motor was not running on fuel and spark. It was running on something else. Call it what you want.

The bar rushed up at us. Coral flared her with the gentleness of a man setting down something breakable, the floats kissed sand with a long tearing hiss, the tail came around a half-point in the crosswind—and the engine died. Not sputtered. Died, mid-beat, like a heart stopping between one pulse and the next, so that we coasted the last hundred meters in silence except for the rain drumming the roof and the sand hissing under the floats and, faintly, Wren's breath going out in a long shaky stream.

We rolled to a stop. The prop hung still. Steam curled up from around the cowling seams into the wet air.

For a moment nobody moved. Then Tomas started laughing, that silent shaking laugh of his, and Ferro said, "Well. That's the last time I complain about Ferro's cooking," which made no sense and made everyone laugh anyway, the way things do after you almost die.

Coral sat with his hands on the wheel for a moment longer than he needed to. Then he turned and looked back at the cabin—at Wren, at Ines, at the two ministry men who had just shared a crash landing with the people whose secret they'd come to bury—and said, quietly:

"Nobody's hurt. The chest is intact. And Bo—" he glanced at me, and there it was, the ghost of a grin, "—tell me honestly. Could she have made it home?"

I pulled the cowling cover loose and looked at the truth inside: a connecting rod bearing gone to blue steel, metal glittering in the drained oil like crushed glass.

"Honestly?" I said. "She died a mile ago. Something just forgot to tell us."

And that was when Okafor stood up in the rocking cabin, dripping, pale, and said the sentence that turned a survival story into something else entirely:

"Then I suppose," he said, "we'd better talk about what happens when the ministry finds that chest in the wreckage."
