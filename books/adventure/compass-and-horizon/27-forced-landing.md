## 27. Forced Landing

The engine had been telling me the truth for an hour, and I'd been lying to it the whole way.

That's what I did, I suppose. Lied to engines, lied to ministry men, lied to a woman I loved with half of everything and called it kindness. The Lycoming ran hot through the smoke haze, oil pressure sagging like a tired man against a doorframe, and every time Bo glanced back from the copilot seat I gave her the same shrug: she'll hold. She knew I was lying. She'd told me so in fewer words than that. But there was nothing to do about it except keep flying, because behind us the river bent away north and ahead of us the storm front sat on the horizon like a wall somebody had built overnight out of slate and spite.

Seven souls aboard. Me at the yoke, Bo beside me. Ines and Wren wedged between crates in the cabin, Tomas dozing against the fuselage wall, Ferro holding a pot of coffee like it was ballast. And in the last row, strapped in with their document cases and their government-issue calm, Surveyor Halloway and his junior officer, Reyes—the man who'd spent nine days watching us like we were cargo he hadn't inventoried yet.

The chest was lashed under the netting amidships. Or what was left of the chest. It had taken water in the rapids, taken a beating when we'd dragged it up the bank at Sable Bend, and its iron bands had gone to rust and its lid no longer sat true. I could smell it, faintly, under the coffee and the hot oil—old paper, old iron, the smell of a thing that had waited forty years underwater and didn't intend to wait much longer.

"Oil pressure's still dropping," Bo said. Not a question. Bo didn't ask questions she already knew the answers to.

"I heard it."

"You're not hearing it right if you think she'll hold to the far strip."

"The far strip is gravel and downhill," I said. "Everything else is trees."

"Then land on the river."

"The river's got a two-foot chop and a crosswind."

"Coral." Bo turned in her seat, and in the gray light through the windshield her face was all business and none of forgiveness. "I've rebuilt this engine twice. She's done. You put her down on the first flat thing you see, or you put her down wherever God decides, and God has never once picked a good spot in this country."

Behind me, Wren said something low to Ines. I caught my name in it. I caught fear in it too, the particular quiet fear of a girl who'd grown up around broken airplanes and knew exactly what the sound of a sick engine meant. I'd taught her that sound myself, one summer, pointing out planes on approach: *hear that? That one's flying on prayer and a cracked cylinder.* I'd thought I was giving her knowledge. Turns out I was giving her nightmares with a curriculum.

The storm front came up to meet us. The haze thickened, went brown, went the color of wet cardboard, and the light in the cockpit dimmed like someone turning down a lamp in a room where important things were being said. Rain hit the windshield in fat, scattered drops, then in sheets. The wiper on my side had died somewhere over the middle years of my life, along with most of my other illusions, and I flew by leaning toward the glass and squinting, which is a technique, if not a good one.

Halloway leaned forward from the back. "How far to the strip?"

"Twenty minutes," I said.

"How far to anywhere?"

I did the arithmetic without wanting to. "Twelve."

He sat back. He was a careful man, Halloway. Careful men don't panic; they calculate, and you can hear the calculating going on behind you like a clock in another room.

The engine made its decision before I made mine.

It came as a shudder first—a deep, structural shudder, the whole airframe ringing like a struck bell—and then a bang from the cowling that I felt through my boots and my teeth, and then the oil pressure needle fell off the bottom of the dial like it had been pushed. The prop kept windmilling, but the sound changed, went hollow, went wrong, went from *engine* to *machine pretending to be an engine*, and every soul aboard knew the difference even the ones who'd never heard it before.

"Brace," I said, and my voice came out level, which surprised me, because inside I was doing what I always did: counting. Counting altitude, counting airspeed, counting the seconds since the bang, counting the people behind me whose names I knew and whose futures I was suddenly holding in two hands along with the yoke.

Below us, the river unspooled through the rain, wide and brown and furious with the storm, and along its eastern edge lay a long pale smear—a sandbar, exposed by the dry season, maybe four hundred meters of it, maybe less, streaked with driftwood and cut by channels that might be ankle-deep mud or might be the kind that eats landing gear and keeps it.

It wasn't a strip. It was a suggestion. But Bo had said *first flat thing*, and the sandbar was listening.

"Sandbar, east bank," I said over my shoulder. "Everybody brace. Wren—"

"I know," she said. "Head down, hands crossed, mouth shut."

"That's my girl."

I wish I'd said something better. I've had twenty years to compose last words for people I love and I've never once used them, because the trick of staying alive is refusing to believe any flight is the last one. So instead I gave her the same instruction I'd give a stranger, and I put the nose down, and I traded altitude for distance the way you trade a coat for a blanket—not enough, never enough, but warmer than nothing.

The dead engine gave me maybe ninety seconds of glide, and I used every one of them. Flaps half. Trim nose-up. The crosswind shoved us sideways and I crabbed into it, and the river came up fast, and the sandbar came up faster, and in the last hundred feet I saw the channel cutting across the near end of it, black water sliding over pale sand, and I knew I couldn't clear it and couldn't go around, so I did the only thing left, which was to pick the smoothest-looking stretch beyond it and accept whatever came.

The wheels touched sand at just under seventy knots and the world became a fight.

The sand held, mostly. Then a driftwood log took the left gear and the plane slewed hard right, wing down, and I stood on the rudder and hauled the yoke into my chest and the tail came around and the right wingtip dug a furrow you could have planted cassava in, and then we were spinning, slow and grinding and terrible, sand fountaining past the windows like brown snow, and then we stopped.

The silence afterward was enormous. Storm-rain on the roof, the tick of cooling metal, Ferro's coffee pot rolling in a slow circle on the cabin floor. That was all.

"Count off," I said.

"Bo."

"Tomas."

"Ferro."

"Ines Vale."

"Wren." A beat. "Coral, say it back."

"Coral," I said. "Halloway?"

"Present." A cough. "Reyes?"

"Here." The junior officer's voice was thin but whole. "Everyone's—I checked. Everyone's whole."

*Whole.* Not unhurt. Tomas was already swearing softly in three languages about his shoulder, which had found the crate frame. Wren had a split lip she was licking like it was dessert. But nobody had died. Forty years of flying this river and I'd finally bought myself a crash, and the bill came due at zero lives. I'd have paid it gladly at double.

Bo was already out of her harness and through the door before I'd finished unstrapping, walking the wings with her hand flat on the fabric, reading damage through her palm the way other people read weather. I followed her out into the rain.

She was standing at the left main gear when I reached her, and she didn't turn around.

"Axle's bent through," she said. "Strut's collapsed. Prop's a sculpture. And I won't know about the crankcase until I open her up, but I'm telling you now, Coral, so you can start believing it: she doesn't fly out of here. Not today, maybe not ever."

"Three days," I said. "You said once you could rebuild anything in three days if there was scrap."

"I said I could rebuild anything in three days if there was scrap *and a machine shop*. This—" she gestured at the sandbar, the storm, the wreck of our situation "—this is scrap and rain."

"Then it'll take longer."

She looked at me then, and under all that grease and anger there was something else, something that had been growing in her since Sable Bend, and I recognized it because I'd been carrying it too. Doubt. Not of each other—of me. Of the story. Of the chest under the netting and the reason we were all on a sandbar in a storm instead of home drinking Ferro's terrible coffee.

"Longer means they find us," she said quietly, nodding back at the cabin, where Halloway was helping Reyes down onto the sand. "Longer means whatever's in that chest stops being yours to carry."

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't the truth, and I'd been rationing truth like a man rationing water in a drought, and the drought was over.

* * *

We got the tarp rigged over the fuselage using the wing strut Tomas's bad shoulder refused to help with, and Ferro got a fire going under the wing with driftwood that smoked like a signal fire, which it was, though not one anyone wanted. The storm passed through in the late afternoon the way storms do here—all fury and then apology—and the sky opened into that washed, bruised gold that makes even a crash site look painted.

Wren and Ines worked the lashings off the cargo while I watched the river. That was supposed to be my job—watching, weighing, deciding—but my hands needed work more than my eyes needed vigilance, so I ended up hauling crates with them, and it was Wren who reached the chest first.

It had shifted in the spin. The netting had held, but the lid—that lid that hadn't sat true since Sable Bend—had come the rest of the way loose, and one rusted band had snapped clean through. When Wren lifted it by the rope handle, the bottom boards flexed, and the seam gaped, and the letters came out.

Not all of them. Maybe thirty. Maybe forty. Thin envelopes gone soft and gray with forty years of river, tied in bundles that had rotted apart, and they spilled across the sand between the fire and the waterline like a hand of cards dropped by a dying gambler.

Wren froze. Ines went down on her knees in the sand.

I moved without deciding to move. Twenty years of instinct says *gather, conceal, bury, deny*, and my body obeyed it before my heart could file an objection. I dropped down and started sweeping the envelopes in, armfuls of them, sand sticking to the damp paper, and I was halfway through the second armful before I understood what I was doing and who was watching me do it.

Ines was watching me. So was Wren. So, from the fire, were Halloway and Reyes, and Bo, and Tomas, and Ferro, and the whole assembled population of my life, and every one of them had just seen Captain Coral Underhill—salvager, ferryman, honest dealer in medicine runs and gossip—fall on spilled letters like a man smothering a fire with his own coat.

"Coral," Ines said. Not loud. The river was loud enough. "Let me see them."

"They're damaged. They need to dry flat or they'll—"

"*Let me see them.*"

There it was. The bargain she'd drawn at the drowned outpost, the terms I'd agreed to with a nod and a coward's hope that the finding would somehow never come. *When we find the site, I read everything.* We'd found the site. We'd crashed on top of it, more or less. And here was everything, blowing across a sandbar in the gold light, addressed in faded ink to names I'd read once by lamplight and never spoken aloud.

I set the armfuls down. All of them. Even the ones my hands wanted to keep.

Ines knelt in the sand and opened the nearest envelope with the care of a surgeon and the stillness of someone bracing for a blow. She read it. Her face did something small and terrible, and then she smoothed it flat, because she was Ines, and she read the next one, and the next, laying them out in rows on the sand like evidence, like a map, like a graveyard.

Halloway came and stood at the edge of the rows. Reyes came and stood beside him. Neither of them spoke. Government men are trained to let documents do the talking, and these documents talked—about requisitions, about removals, about families counted and loaded and erased from the record, and the handwriting on some of them was neat and official and the handwriting on others was hurried and human, and both kinds lay drying on the sand of a river that had kept them safe from every eye for forty years until I came along and dug them up and carried them into the sky and dropped them here.

"This is material relevant to a closed file," Halloway said at last, and his voice had lost its surveyor's smoothness. He sounded like a man reading his own sentence.

"It's mail," Ines said, without looking up. "It's mail that never got delivered. Somebody should deliver it."

"The ministry will determine—"

"The ministry had forty years." Now she looked up, and I have seen Ines angry at me, angry at the river, angry at the dead, but I had never seen her look at a stranger like this, with the full weight of her grandfather's empty grave behind her eyes. "Forty years, Mr. Halloway. My family has been missing from the record for forty years, and here they are, and you want to *determine* something. Sit down."

And he sat down. That surprised me more than the crash. The careful man folded himself onto a driftwood log across the fire from the rows of letters, and Reyes sat beside him, and for a long time nobody spoke, and the letters dried in the evening air, curling slightly at the edges like living things.

Wren came and stood next to me. She didn't take my hand. She was past the age for that, or I was past the age for allowing it, or both. But she stood close, shoulder to shoulder, the way she'd stood since she was six and scared of thunder, and she watched Ines read.

"You knew what those said," Wren said quietly. "Before today. You knew."

"Yes."

"And you were going to—" She stopped. Started again, choosing her words the way I'd taught her to choose anchor points, carefully, where they'd hold. "You were going to decide for everybody. Again."

"It's what I'm good at."

"It's what you do instead of letting anybody love you," she said, and walked away to help Ferro with the fire, leaving me standing alone in the middle of the biggest thing I'd ever wrecked, and it wasn't the airplane.

* * *

Full dark came down, and we made camp in the lee of the fuselage, and the fire burned low and orange, and Ines was still reading. She'd gotten a lantern from the survival kit and she sat cross-legged with the bundles around her, sorting, and every so often she'd make a small sound—not a word, just a sound, the sound of a person being struck softly and repeatedly by the past—and every so often she'd look up at me across the fire with an expression I couldn't survive looking at for long.

Near midnight, Halloway stirred. He'd been writing in his case log by firelight for an hour, and now he closed the book and looked at me over the flames.

"Underhill. Your manifest listed the chest as salvage, contents unknown."

"It was."

"It isn't anymore." He rubbed his face. He looked, suddenly, very tired, and older than his file photo, and less like a wall and more like a door. "Tomorrow, when we can raise the relay station, I'm required to report a forced landing and request recovery support. What I report about the contents of that chest is—" he paused, choosing, and I knew the feeling "—is going to matter to a great many people. Some of them wear suits. Some of them are sitting around this fire."

"And which are you?" I asked.

He didn't answer. Across the fire, Reyes was looking at the letters, at Ines, at the dark line of the river, and his face in the lantern light was the face of a young man standing on the edge of something with a long drop on either side.

Ines chose that moment to lift one envelope from the row, the oldest-looking one, the softest, and she held it out—not to Halloway, not to the fire, but to me.

"This one's different," she said. "Different paper. Different hand. It's not outpost correspondence." Her voice was steady the way a wire is steady under load. "It's addressed to a child. To a boy. And the name on it, Coral—it's not Underhill."

The fire popped. Downriver, somewhere in the dark, a bird cried out once and quit.

"Read it," Ines said, and held it out further, and everyone at the fire was looking at me now, and the river ran on past us, low and honest, keeping nothing.
