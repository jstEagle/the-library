## 29. The Repair

The engine had died at forty feet over open water, and Bo had spent the first hour after the crash landing wanting to kill it properly—with a wrench, personally, cylinder by cylinder.

Instead she knelt on the sandbar in the white heat of midmorning, sleeves rolled past her elbows, hands black to the wrist, and listened to the Lycoming the way you'd listen to a patient who'd lied about his symptoms. The sandbar was maybe two hundred meters of baked river clay and shell grit, an island that hadn't existed three weeks ago and wouldn't exist after the next flood. The plane sat canted on its left gear, propeller bent into a question mark, tail still heavy with the film Wren had hidden there. The ministry men stood at the far end of the bar under the one scraggly algarrobo, pretending they weren't prisoners of the same falling river everyone else was.

"Talk to me," Bo said to the engine.

It didn't answer, which was normal. Engines only talked through their corpses. She pulled the number-three jug's rocker cover and found what she'd already known she'd find: a connecting rod bearing gone to copper, spun dry because the oil line had been starved when the prop strike shuddered the whole forward end. Below it, in the sump, the oil carried glitter like a snow globe full of bad news. Metal in the oil meant metal in everything. You didn't fix that. You replaced it, or you walked.

Except there was no walking off a sandbar in the middle of the Sable Reach, and the river had dropped another handspan overnight. She could see it in the mud lines on the stakes Tomas had driven—three fresh bands of darker clay, each one a day of water giving up on them. At this rate they had four days, maybe five, before the bar joined the bank and became just another stretch of bad ground no pilot would ever pick out from the air. Which cut both ways. Nobody would find them. Nobody would come.

Nobody except whoever came.

"You're smiling," Coral said behind her. "That worries me."

"I'm not smiling. I'm doing arithmetic." She sat back on her heels and wiped her forehead with the back of her wrist, which just moved the grease around. "Number three rod is scrap. Number one's scored. The sump's full of confetti. I need a crankshaft, two rods, four jugs minimum, and a machine shop."

"And we have?"

Bo looked down the length of the sandbar, then upriver, where the water ran thin and braided around a chain of old wrecks she'd marked on her chart years ago and never had a reason to visit. A sternwheeler's bones. A barge that had gone down with a load of railway iron. And half-buried in the point, visible only now, at dead-low water, the engine block of a wrecked freighter plane—the same vintage as theirs, same era, same factory, probably flown by some other fool chasing the same weather.

"We have a graveyard," she said. "And three days."

Coral followed her gaze. Something crossed his face—not hope exactly; Bo had learned that Coral did hope the way other men did penance, quietly and without admitting it—but something adjacent. He nodded once.

"Tomas can dive the shallow ones. Ferro can feed anyone. Wren—" he paused, and Bo watched him do the thing he always did, the calculation where the girl was concerned, weighing risk against the fact that keeping her idle would rot her faster than any danger. "Wren runs parts. Reyes too, if he volunteers. The junior officer has hands, apparently."

"He fixed our radio last night while everyone slept," Bo said. "Didn't ask permission. Just did it."

Coral grunted. That was approval, in his currency.

* * *

By noon the sandbar had become a town of six people and two reluctant guests, organized around a single idea: take the dead engine apart without losing a single bolt.

Ferro set up his cook fire at the upstream edge, where the smoke would blow away from the work, and produced the miracle of fried plantains and tinned beef from a larder that should have been exhausted days ago. He fed the ministry men without being asked, and Bo noticed the lead surveyor hesitate before accepting the plate—a hesitation that lasted exactly long enough for Ferro to say, "Hungry men make poor decisions, señor. Eat." The man ate.

Tomas went into the water at the sternwheeler with a pry bar and a mesh bag, coming up gasping with fittings, a valve cover, and a story about a catfish the size of a canoe that Bo chose not to verify. Wren ferried everything to Bo's work area on a piece of tarpaulin, sorting bolts by size into divots pressed in the sand, and if her hands shook a little every time she passed the bent propeller, nobody mentioned it. They'd all heard the engine quit. They'd all felt the sandbar come up. Some things you worked off rather than talked off.

Reyes appeared at the edge of the tarp around midafternoon, sleeves rolled, holding a wrench like a man who'd been issued one in basic training and never expected to use it again.

"Tell me where," he said.

Bo studied him for a second. Young, sunburned, carrying the particular exhaustion of a man whose career had begun dying somewhere upriver. "You know what this is?" She held up a piston, ring grooves packed with carbon.

"A piston."

"You know why it matters?"

He thought about it honestly, which she appreciated. "Because everything else depends on it working and nobody noticing it's there."

Bo snorted. "Congratulations. You understand engines better than most ministers." She pointed him at the accessory section. "Start there. Anything with a wire or a gasket comes to me. Don't force anything. If it fights you, it wins, and then I lose."

They worked through the heat of the afternoon in a rhythm that surprised her. The sandbar rang with small sounds—wrench on casting, the tick of hot metal cooling, Ferro's knife on a cutting board, Wren's voice counting bolts under her breath like a rosary. Ines sat apart with the ledger open on her knees, but even she drifted closer as the light went gold, holding pieces while Bo fitted others, asking questions that were actually good questions, the kind a cartographer would ask: how does it fit, what holds it, what's underneath.

At dusk Bo stood, cracked her back, and surveyed the day's harvest laid out on the tarp: two usable cylinders, a carburetor, a magnetos' worth of points and condensers, a fuel pump, and enough hardware to fill Wren's sand-divots twice over. What she didn't have was the heart of it. Crankshaft. Rods. The parts that took the explosion and turned it into motion.

Those were downriver, in the drowned freighter, under a meter of water that was becoming half a meter, which meant tomorrow was the day.

* * *

The second morning, the river gave them its gift and charged them for it.

Dead-low water had exposed the freighter's engine block fully—two radial banks rising from the mud like a coral head, and bolted among them, intact, a crankshaft half the length of a man. Tomas dove the cold channels around it while Bo directed from the bank, and by ten o'clock they had the shaft free, sledded across the mud on a piece of wing skin, and scrubbed clean enough to read the part numbers.

Same family as theirs. Not identical—twenty years and a factory revision separated them—but close enough that a machinist's daughter with a file, a stone, and no other options could make it true.

"It'll need journals dressed," she told Coral, who had come to stand over it the way men stand over coffins. "I can do it by hand if I have to. It won't be pretty. It'll run."

"How long?"

"Two days if nothing goes wrong. Three if something does. Four if the river keeps dropping and I have to move the whole operation." She looked at him. "How long do we have?"

Coral looked at the mud lines on the stakes. He didn't answer, which was an answer.

That afternoon the sandbar became a workshop in earnest. Bo built a workstand from the cargo tie-downs and a section of wing spar, and they mounted the crank across it on blocks, and she began the slow, meditative violence of dressing metal by hand. Stones and crocus cloth and oil, stroke after stroke after stroke, checking with micrometer calipers worn smooth from her father's hands, measuring to tolerances thinner than a hair. The sun crawled. Her shoulders burned. She let herself fall into it—the one prayer she knew, which was repetition.

Around her, the crew became a body.

She saw it happen the way you see a tide turn, not all at once. Ferro cooking and hauling water and, when Bo's arms gave out, taking the stone without being asked and continuing the strokes in the same rhythm she'd set, because he'd been watching. Tomas diving the wrecks again for gaskets and hose, coming back with a coil of copper tubing that solved the oil line problem outright. Wren running the bolt inventory, then the tool inventory, then—when Bo caught her sketching the crankshaft assembly in the margin of her notebook with correct labels—quietly promoted to apprentice. Ines reading the river like she read maps, walking the bar each evening to mark where the water had gone, drawing the sandbar itself onto paper so that if they had to move, they'd move knowing the ground.

And Reyes. Reyes worked like a man doing penance, and maybe he was. He stripped the accessory case, cleaned every fitting, rebuilt the fuel pump to Bo's specs, and on the second night, sitting by the fire with his hands blistered, he said to no one in particular: "My father was a mechanic. Buses, in the capital. I used to think I'd escaped him."

"Did you?" Wren asked.

Reyes turned his hands over in the firelight. "No," he said. "I just stopped visiting."

Nobody said anything after that, but Bo noticed that Ferro refilled his plate without being asked, and that Wren scooted her log closer to his, and that the circle around the fire had, without any decision being made, made room.

* * *

The third day, the river called in its debt.

It came at dawn, as a sound—a deep, grinding shift downstream where the current, funneled by the falling water, began cutting the tail of the sandbar away. By breakfast, five meters of their island had become water. By midmorning, ten. The plane sat on ground that was now a peninsula, and by tomorrow it would be a reef.

Bo stood at the tail of the aircraft with the crankshaft half-assembled in the case beside her, two rods torqued, jugs waiting, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding. The engine needed a full day more—assembly, rigging, oil prime, mag check, run-up. The ground needed them gone in half that.

"Coral," she called. "We have a problem with a deadline."

He came fast. She laid it out plain: finish the engine and lose the ground under the plane, or move the plane now to the high point—which was soft clay, worse footing, worse for a takeoff run—and finish there with the river chewing at their heels.

"So we do both," Coral said.

"We're eleven people and we've slept four hours a night for three days."

"Then we stop sleeping." He said it without drama, the way he said everything, and Bo realized she was looking at a different man than the one who'd hired her in the river town a lifetime ago. This one wasn't hiding. Whatever had happened between him and Ines at low water, whatever the letters had burned out of him, it had left someone willing to spend himself openly. "You build. We move. Tell us how."

So she told them how, and the sandbar became one body with a single nervous system.

They jacked the tail, dug the gear free, and winched the plane fifty meters up the bar on rollers made from wreck timber, everyone hauling on ropes in the manner of people pulling something sacred out of a flood—because that's what it was, though nobody said so. The chest rode in the cabin, lashed where the cargo net used to be. The ministry men hauled too; the lead surveyor had stopped pretending otherwise sometime yesterday, and now worked the ropes with his jacket off and his file folders weighted under a rock, and Bo decided that history would sort out what to call him later.

While they moved the plane, Ferro cooked double rations. While they ate, Wren and Reyes prepped the workstand at the new site. While the sun climbed, Bo assembled.

It was the best work of her life and she barely noticed. Rod caps torqued in sequence, journals oiled, jugs drawn down over new rings, pushrods timed, the whole engine closing around its borrowed heart like a fist. Ines held the manual pages flat against the wind. Tomas handed tools before she asked for them—he'd learned her language in three days, the order of her needs. Wren kept the bolt count perfect, every fastener accounted for, and when Bo asked her at midafternoon how many were left, the girl answered without checking: "Fourteen. All castle nuts. They're in the blue tin."

Late light. Oil in. Fuel in. Bo primed the master and looked at Coral across the cowling.

"Together?" she said.

He put his hand on the prop. Everyone else stepped back except Wren, who refused to, standing at Bo's shoulder with her fists clenched, and Bo let her stay.

"Clear!" Coral called, and swung.

The engine coughed. Coughed again. Caught on the third blade with a ragged, smoking roar that smoothed—lumped, smoothed again, lumped—and then settled into a beat that was ugly and uneven and absolutely alive. Twenty-five hundred miles from any machine shop, built from a drowned ship and a dead man's tolerances, the engine ran.

Bo killed it before it could teach them humility, and the silence afterward was so total she could hear the river eating the far end of the sandbar.

Wren was laughing. Actually laughing, high and cracked, and Reyes was grinning like a schoolboy, and Ferro had both hands raised to the sky in thanks to whatever saints handled internal combustion. Ines had her palm flat against the cowling, feeling the ticking heat, looking at Coral with an expression Bo pretended not to see.

Coral just nodded at Bo. Once. His whole vocabulary of gratitude, and somehow sufficient.

"One problem," Bo said, when she could speak steadily. "Run-up needs a hundred meters of clear ground. Our takeoff roll, loaded, at this weight, in this heat—" she looked down the bar, at the shrinking runway of clay and shell, at the water closing from both ends like a book shutting, "—we get one attempt. Maybe two hundred meters of ground left by morning. We need three-fifty."

Silence. Then Ines, quietly: "Then we leave at first light, and we take nothing we don't need."

Her eyes went to the chest. So did everyone else's.

Coral followed the look, and Bo watched him weigh it—all of it, the ledger, the letters, twenty years of sealed silence sitting in a cabin that also had to carry eleven people against a failing margin of ground. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"The chest flies," he said. "Everything else is negotiable."

Behind him, at the water's edge, the lead surveyor straightened up from his rock of file folders and said, in the tone of a man reading his own orders aloud for the first time:

"Not everything. There's a confiscation order in here with my name on it. And as of tonight, Captain Lockridge, I'm deciding whether it applies."
