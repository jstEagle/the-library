## 30. The Junior Officer's Decision

Reyes had been awake since the sandbar went quiet, which was to say he had not slept at all.

He lay on his back on a groundsheet spread over warm sand, hands folded on his chest like a man in a coffin rehearsing, and watched the stars wheel behind the smoke haze. The river murmured against the hull of the beached survey boat. Somewhere down the bar, one of Lockridge's crew snored with the untroubled rhythm of a man who had made his peace with something. Reyes envied him with a violence that surprised him.

Three days. Three days since the forced landing, three days since the letters had spilled across the sand like a deck of cards thrown by an angry god, and three days since Senior Surveyor Aldous Vane had stood over them in the wind and read—silently, his lips moving—the name of a village that did not appear on any map Reyes had ever filed.

Vane had gathered every page. He'd done it carefully, almost tenderly, kneeling in the wet sand in his pressed khaki while the pilot's crew watched from twenty meters off with the stillness of people witnessing an arrest. He'd stacked them, squared them, weighted them under a stone, and then he'd looked up at Reyes with an expression Reyes was still trying to decode.

Not triumph. Not anger. Something worse: relief. The relief of a man who has found the thing he was sent to find and can now stop pretending he wasn't sent to find it.

*Archival review,* the order said. *Pending centenary preparations.* Reyes had typed the phrase himself, six weeks ago, in a ministry office that smelled of carbon paper and floor wax, and he had thought nothing of it. It was a filing phrase. It was furniture. He had typed hundreds of phrases like it into hundreds of orders, and each one had gone out into the country wearing his initials like a collar tag, and none of them—not one—had ever come back to show him what they meant when they arrived somewhere real.

Now he knew. They meant this: a sandbar going soft underfoot, a crippled plane being rebuilt from scrap by people who sang work songs in two languages, a chest of evidence that a government had spent eighty years pretending a massacre hadn't happened—and a senior officer deciding, alone, at night, whether history would fit back inside its box.

Reyes turned onto his side. Across the dying fire, Vane slept sitting up against the boat's gunwale, hat over his eyes, one hand resting on the document case buckled to his belt. Even asleep, the man guarded it. Reyes had watched him do it for three days—eat with one hand, write with the other, never once letting the case sit farther than an arm's reach away.

The case held everything. The ledger. The ration tallies. The letters. The manifest of removed families, forty-one names in a clerk's careful hand, each one followed by a destination column that said only *interior transfer* as if human beings were crates of ammunition being moved forward for an offensive that had already been won.

And the case held the confiscation order. Signed. Dated. Waiting only for Vane's countersignature and the words *executed as directed* in the space Reyes was supposed to fill in himself.

That was the part nobody had told him about fieldwork. In the office, you wrote the sentence. Out here, you had to watch the sentence happen.

He got up quietly, pulled on his boots, and walked to the far end of the sandbar where the plane sat propped on its belly like a wounded bird, lamplight leaking from its cabin door. The mechanic—Bo, they called her, a woman built like an anchor with forearms to match—was working under the cowling even at this hour, and she looked up when his boots crunched close.

"Ministry hours," she said. "Don't you people sleep?"

"Not well."

"Good." She went back to the engine. "Sleeping well is how they get you."

He should have laughed. Instead he stood there in the lamplight spill, and after a while Bo wiped her hands and looked at him again, longer this time, the way a person looks at weather coming in.

"You want something," she said. "People who don't want anything walk past. You're standing."

"I wanted—" He stopped. Started over. "The girl. Wren. She has a camera. A salvaged one. I've seen her carrying it."

Bo's face did something very small and very final, the way a door does when it closes but doesn't lock. "You've seen a lot, junior officer."

"It's my job to see."

"And hers is to carry a camera." Bo picked up her wrench. "Anything else you saw?"

"No," Reyes said, and heard how it sounded, and knew she heard it too, and knew that she would say nothing, and understood—in a single cold drop of clarity—that he had just asked a stranger to help him betray his own government without using any of the words for it.

Bo turned back to the engine. "Tell your boss," she said to the cylinders, "that the river's dropping another hand-span tonight. Whatever anybody's planning to decide, they'd better decide it soon."

* * *

The decision came for him the next morning, wearing Vane's voice.

They stood at the water's edge while the crew worked the plane, and Vane laid the documents out on a flat rock with the ceremony of a man dealing cards he'd already counted. The ledger. The letters, bundled and tied. The manifest. Last, the order itself, its signature block blank and patient.

"Countersigned today," Vane said. "We fly out with the first load whenever the mechanic gives us wings. The chest goes to the regional archive. Sealed. Catalogued. Forgotten properly, this time, with paperwork."

"Forgetten properly," Reyes repeated. He hadn't meant to say it aloud.

Vane looked at him. Up close, in daylight, the man was older than his file photograph—seventy percent gray at the temples, a scar through one eyebrow that no personnel form had ever explained. "You have an objection, Junior Officer?"

"I have a question." Reyes kept his eyes on the river. "What happens to the families? Forty-one names on that manifest. Compensation was authorized. I've read the authorization. It was never paid. Is it being paid now?"

The silence lasted four seconds. Reyes counted them, because counting was what he did instead of panicking.

"The centenary ceremonies," Vane said finally, "will honor the settlement era with appropriate dignity. Historical grievances are addressed through established channels."

"There are no established channels. I've checked. There's a closed file number and a dead man's signature and nothing else."

"Then the channels will be established." Vane began squaring the papers, tapping their edges flush, and his voice dropped into something quieter and more dangerous than authority: candor. "Reyes. Sit down."

Reyes didn't sit. Vane didn't seem to care.

"When I was younger than you," Vane said, "I was posted to a district office upriver. My job was to process land claims. Every claim from the interior came across my desk stamped with the same reference code, and every one of them was denied, and I processed the denials, and I told myself it was administration. It took me eleven years to learn what the code meant. Eleven years, and a drunk old clerk who owed me money and paid me in truth instead." He tapped the manifest once, lightly, the way you'd touch a scar. "This isn't the first time I've held this story in my hands. It's the third. The other two times, I did my duty, and the story went into a drawer, and the drawer went into a room, and the room is in a building where I could take you today and show you exactly which shelf."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're going to make a choice in the next day or so, and I want you to make it knowing that I know what choices look like." Vane buckled the document case. "I chose the shelf twice. I'm not choosing it a third time. But understand what I'm offering, because it's narrow: the archive gets the original, sealed, catalogued, *real*—and I get a written undertaking, from above both our heads, that the compensation file is reopened before the centenary. That is the best outcome available to us. That is the *only* outcome available to us that doesn't end with both of us on a charge sheet and these pages in a furnace, because there are people above me, Reyes, who would prefer a furnace, and I am not strong enough to fight them and neither are you."

It was a good speech. It was probably even a sincere speech. Reyes stood there hating how much he wanted to believe it, because that was the mechanism—he could see the mechanism now, plain as a trap's sprung jaw. Men like Vane were not the machine's teeth. They were its lubricant. They kept the gears turning gently enough that decent men could keep calling it administration.

"Sir," Reyes said. "May I think on it?"

"You may think on it until the wheels leave the sand." Vane picked up his hat. "That's more thinking time than I got, either time."

* * *

He thought on it all day, and the day gave him material.

He watched Ines Vale sit with the old pilot for an hour in the shade of the wing, heads together over a half-map gone soft at the folds, and he watched the pilot's hands—scarred, steady, a working man's hands—point at places on the paper and then stop pointing, deliberately, at the place where the drowned outpost must lie, and he watched Vale notice the stopping and let it go, and he understood that everyone on this sandbar was holding a piece of the same secret and passing it around like a flask.

He watched Wren photograph the ledger.

She thought she was clever about it—waited until the salvage crew drew Vane into an argument about propeller shafts, waited until Reyes himself was visibly occupied copying tide notes—but he had grown up in a ministry dormitory where survival meant seeing everything, and he saw her flat little camera come out of her shirt, and he saw the pages turn, and he saw her lips move as she read the names, silently, the way Vane had read them on the sand three days ago. Everyone read those names the same way. Like a prayer they'd been taught too late.

He could report it. That was the clean path: report the camera, secure the originals, execute the order, fly home, initial the file, become the kind of man whose initials meant nothing. The clean path ran straight and level and ended exactly where Vane's shelf ended.

Or.

Or there was the thing he'd been turning over since midnight, the thing that had brought him to Bo's lamplight like a moth with a bad sense of self-preservation. Because here was what three days on a sandbar had taught him that six years of office work never could: paper was not the truth. Paper was only the truth's body, and bodies could be buried, but a copied thing—a thing that existed in many hands at once—could not be buried, only argued with. Vane wanted to seal the original and negotiate for justice. Negotiation required the other side to hold nothing. And the other side—the forty-one names, the descendants, the old pilot who had been one of them—held nothing but memory, and memory died with its holders, politely, one funeral at a time, which was how empires preferred their history delivered.

So: copies. Copies in many hands. Copies the ministry couldn't furnace.

And the original?

Reyes stood at the river's edge at dusk, the document case heavy under his arm where Vane had finally, fatally, handed it to him—*carry it to the boat, Junior Officer, my knees are done*—and looked at the water running low and brown and honest over the sandbar's edge, and felt the whole shape of the choice settle onto him with the lightness of something that had always been inevitable.

The river had kept this secret for eighty years. The river had coughed it up once already. The river, if asked, would keep it again—or rather, would keep it *from* the furnace, which was not the same as keeping it from the world. Things sank in this river and came back. He'd seen the chest prove it. Whole nations proved it. The river was not a grave; it was a slow post office.

He copied the manifest that night by lamplight in the boat's cabin, Vane sleeping on the bank ten meters away, the pen scratching louder in his ears than the river. Name by name. Village by village. Destination by euphemism. His handwriting shook on the ninth name and steadied by the twentieth, and by the thirty-eighth it was the calmest hand he'd ever produced, because terror, he discovered, burns off like morning fog once you pass through it and find the other side is simply resolve.

He copied the authorization for compensation too—the one that had never been honored—with its dead minister's signature and its file number, because a promise in writing was a weapon, and weapons should not be left lying in drawers.

Then he rewrapped the originals in their oilcloth, exactly as they'd been, and carried them up the sandbar in the blue hour before dawn, and stood at the cut bank where the current ran fastest, and stood there for a long moment feeling the weight of eighty years and forty-one names and one career balanced on his two hands.

*You're going to make a choice,* Vane had said. *I want you to make it knowing what choices look like.*

Reyes knew what they looked like. They looked like a shelf. They looked like a furnace. They looked like a sealed archive where dignity went to be forgotten with appropriate honors.

He thought of the girl's lips moving over the names. He thought of Bo's door closing without locking. He thought of the old pilot's hands stopping at the place on the map where his own childhood was buried.

"Appropriate dignity," Reyes said quietly, to the river, to the names, to whatever clerk in the great bureaucracy of the dead was on shift tonight. "Filed for retrieval."

He let it fall.

The oilcloth bundle turned once in the air, caught the last starlight, and went into the brown water without a splash worth the name. The current took it the way the current takes everything here—gently, thoroughly, without comment. Downriver. Toward the town. Toward the families. Toward wherever the river decided the reading should happen.

In the cabin, under his mattress, wrapped in a spare shirt: the copies. The manifest. The authorization. The truth's body double, alive and breathing in many hands.

When Vane woke and discovered the loss, there would be rage, then investigation, then—because Vane was Vane, because Vane had chosen the shelf twice and meant it when he said not a third time—perhaps something else entirely. Reyes would stand at attention and report a handling accident during loading. He would accept the charge sheet. Six years, maybe. He found, examining himself honestly in the black water's reflection, that he could carry six years the way this river carried a chest: slowly, patiently, certain of surfacing.

Behind him, boots on sand. He turned.

Wren stood at the edge of the firelight with her camera in her hand and her chin up, and she looked at him the way you look at a co-conspirator you haven't formally met yet, and she said:

"I saw where it went in. I can mark it on a chart."

Reyes looked at the girl—sixteen years old, orphan-raised, keeper of photographs—and felt the future change direction like a boat catching current.

"Get the chart," he said.
