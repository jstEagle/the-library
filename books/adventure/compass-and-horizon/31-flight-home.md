## 31. Flight Home

The engine caught on the fourth try.

Bo had her hand flat against the cowling like she was blessing it, or holding it shut, and when the old radial coughed itself into a ragged rhythm she didn't celebrate—she just nodded once, the way a surgeon nods when the heart starts again, and climbed aboard without looking at any of us. That was how I knew what the three days had cost her. Bo celebrated everything. A good weld, a clean landing, Ferro's beans not burning. She celebrated with her whole body. This time she just nodded, and I understood that we were all of us carrying something too heavy for celebration.

We were overloaded. There was no polite way to say it. The Sandpiper was built for cargo and a pilot, and she'd flown heavier in her day, but never with this much weight in the wrong places: the chest lashed amidships where the center of gravity wanted it, the film canisters Wren had taped into the tail as ballast that weren't ballast at all, six adults, a girl, tools, water, and the particular gravity of what we knew. Weight isn't only mass. Any bush pilot learns that. You can feel a plane full of grief before you ever roll it onto the strip.

The sandbar had shrunk overnight. The river kept dropping—we could hear it dropping, almost, a long whisper of water letting go of ground it had held all season—and what had been a runway wide enough for three passes was now a brown ribbon maybe four hundred meters long with soft mud at both ends. Bo stood at the upwind end with a stick she'd cut, measuring our roll by eye, chewing the inside of her cheek.

"Four hundred," she said. "Maybe three-eighty if the far end holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then you swim, Captain, and I'll tell everyone you died a hero."

"Tell them I died an idiot. It'll be more useful to Wren."

Wren, at that, looked away. She'd been looking away from me since the night the letters spilled across the sand. Not angry—I'd have preferred angry. Angry has a shape you can work with. What she wore instead was something older and more careful, the look of a person re-measuring a room they thought they knew, finding the walls farther out than they'd believed. She hadn't said much. She'd helped Ferro boil water, helped Tomas coil line, helped Bo hold parts, and through all of it she'd been doing arithmetic I couldn't see. I'd raised her to be careful with numbers. It was coming back on me now, the way all my better lessons eventually did.

Reyes came down to the bar alone. His senior man, Ostrander, stayed by the ministry boat with his arms crossed, watching us load like a man watching money walk out of a bank. But Reyes—he carried a canvas satchel and he set it down in the sand between us without ceremony, and inside it were notebooks, and I didn't ask and he didn't say, but the way he held it told me everything: he'd spent the night copying. Pages of the ledger. Names, tonnages, dates, the cold bookkeeping of removal rendered in a junior officer's careful hand. He'd made a second truth and put it in canvas, and now he was handing it to a smuggler because somewhere between the sandbar and the sunrise he'd decided which side of the file he stood on.

"You understand I can't be seen giving you this," he said.

"I understand you're not giving it to me." I nodded toward the satchel. "You're giving it to them."

He looked upriver, toward the drowned outpost, invisible now under its skin of low water. "My grandfather worked customs at Port Meridian," he said. "Forty years. He used to say the empire never lost a document, it only misplaced them in advance." He handed me the satchel. "Don't lose it."

"I'm very good at losing things," I said. "It's finding them I've never managed."

Almost, he smiled. Then Ostrander called his name from the boat, one syllable with a whole career inside it, and Reyes walked back down the sand with his shoulders square and his face arranged into obedience, and I put the satchel under my seat where the weight would do the most good.

* * *

We lifted off the sandbar with fifty meters to spare.

I don't want to make it sound clean. It wasn't. The Sandpiper wallowed off the mud like something being born unwillingly, wheels kissing the wet sand twice, the engine howling at a pitch Bo later described as "a cry for help," and then the drag let go all at once and we were up, skimming the falling river so low the prop wash bent the water, and the far bank came up fast and I hauled her over it and we were flying. Behind us the sandbar shrank to a comma in the current. Ahead of us lay two hundred miles of smoke haze and thermals and the longest short flight of my life.

Nobody cheered. On a normal run, clearing a strip like that, Tomas would have whooped, Ferro would have crossed himself and immediately un-crossed himself on principle, Wren would have been pressed to the window narrating our altitude to no one. Instead the cabin sat quiet as a chapel. Ines had the ledger wrapped in oilcloth in her lap, both hands on it, not reading it—she'd read every word twice over on the sand—but holding it the way you hold a sleeping child, palm flat, feeling it breathe. Tomas stared at his hands. His hands had pulled him out of the river once already this trip and they seemed to fascinate him still, turning them over, flexing the fingers, a man renegotiating terms with his own body. Ferro dozed sitting up, which was his gift, his head nodding in time with some private music. And Wren—

Wren sat beside Ines, and watched me fly.

Not accusingly. That was the thing I couldn't shake. She watched me the way she used to watch me when she was seven and I was teaching her to read the river from the air—which bend lied about its depth, which channel would be gone by August—and there was the same hunger in it, the same absolute trust in my competence, wrapped around a new and terrible question: *what else did you know?*

I flew and let her watch. It was the least I owed her, and the least was enormous.

The haze thickened as we turned south. The dry season fires were burning in the high country, as they did every year, but this year the smoke hung lower and browner, a ceiling you could almost touch, and the sun went the color of a coin left too long in water. Flying in it was like flying inside a closed fist. Visibility fell to two miles, then less, and I climbed us above the worst of it where the air was smoother and the world below became a suggestion—river as a dull pewter seam, forest as texture without detail, the whole country I'd spent twenty years memorizing reduced to rumor beneath me.

There's a kind of tiredness that comes after fear, and it is not restful. We'd all been afraid for days—the rapids, the wrecked prop, the storm, the crash, the long repair with the river audibly leaving us—and now that the fear had nothing immediate to chew on it had turned inward and started eating slower, and everyone in that cabin was digesting their own portion of it. I could feel the silence changing character mile by mile. At first it was exhaustion. Then it was privacy. By the time we crossed the Meridian confluence it had become something close to communion—a cabin full of people who had seen the same letters, buried the same dead man's bell in their memories, agreed without a word to the same refusal, all of us flying home inside one secret instead of six separate ones.

Ines moved forward sometime past the second hour and settled into the jump seat beside me, shoulder nearly touching mine, and for a while neither of us spoke. Below us the river ran low and honest, every sandbar exposed, every drowned snag standing clear of the water like a warning finally spoken aloud.

"You're flying differently," she said.

"Am I."

"Slower. No—you're not rushing. You always rush. Even your landings rush." She watched the horizon. "Today you're just going somewhere."

I thought about arguing. I'd gotten very good at arguing with her over twenty chapters of knowing her, and I'd lost every round, which should have taught me something earlier than it did. "Twenty years," I said, "every mile I flew was a mile away from something. Away from the outpost, away from the name I was born with, away from the men who'd want to know how a boy got out of a place that officially never existed. You get so good at away-from that you forget there's another direction." I checked the manifold pressure, mostly to have something to do with my hands. "This is the first flight I can remember where I'm flying toward."

"Toward what?"

"Toward saying it out loud. In front of people." My throat tightened around it. "I've rehearsed it my whole life, Ines. In the cockpit, at night, over the noise. *My name was—*" and I stopped, because even now, even here, the name wouldn't come all the way up. It sat in me like a stone at the bottom of a well. "Tomorrow. With the families. I'll say it where it counts."

She put her hand over mine on the yoke. Just briefly. Just enough weight to mean something. "They're going to need to hear it from you before they hear it from anyone else," she said. "And Coral—they're going to need you after, too. Being one of them doesn't end the story. It starts it."

"That's what frightens me."

"I know," she said, and smiled, and it was the first smile in that cabin in a hundred miles. "It frightened me too. Right up until it didn't."

* * *

The thermals found us at the edge of the highlands, and they were vicious.

Dry-season heat off the bare slopes sent columns of rising air punching up through the haze, and the Sandpiper, heavy as she was, took each one like a blow. We'd be climbing hard in one column and falling out of the next, the wings flexing, loose cargo shifting in the back, and twice the engine stumbled on the heat-thinned air and my heart stopped with it both times. Bo wedged herself against the chest and rode it with her arms spread like a mother corralling children, muttering to the airplane in a language that was half mechanic's profanity and half genuine prayer. Once, dropping through a hole in the haze, we passed directly over a burn—a black scar miles across, still smoking, the fire's edge crawling slow as a tide—and Wren said, very quietly, "It looks like the maps of it. The outpost. Before the water came back."

"It does," I said.

"Everything burned there too, didn't it. Before the flooding. They burned the records first."

"They burned what they could carry to the fire," I said. "That's why the chest matters. Somebody hid what couldn't be burned. Somebody paid for hiding it, most likely." I thought of the ledger's last entries, the handwriting deteriorating entry by entry, the final page a list of names and a single word—*removed*—and beneath it, in different ink, different hand, smaller: *God forgive the surveyor who wrote this and the pilot who flew them out.* My grandfather's hand. The only sentence of his I owned besides the map.

"Pilot," Wren repeated, from the back. "There was a pilot."

"There's always a pilot," I said. "Somebody flies the dirty errands. Usually the same somebody, year after year, until the flying is all he knows how to be." I felt Ines look at me. I kept my eyes on the haze ahead. "The file will say the evacuation was orderly. Files always say that. The truth is the pilot took out eleven children in a plane rated for four, in weather he shouldn't have flown in, and made nine trips when he was ordered to make three, and the last trip he was seen making, and that's the whole of the record. Eleven children. One of them grew up and changed his name and learned to keep moving."

The cabin was absolutely silent except for the engine, which droned on indifferent as scripture.

"That pilot," Tomas said at last, slowly, "was your grandfather."

"Yes."

"And you—" Ferro had woken, and his voice was gentle in the way of big men handling small things. "You were one of the eleven."

"One of the eleven," I said. And saying it aloud, in the air, in front of the people who'd bled and dived and machined and cooked alongside me for weeks under a false flag—it didn't break anything. That was the surprise. Twenty years of treating the truth like a load beyond the plane's rating, and here it was, declared, and the wings held. The wings always held. It was only ever me that buckled.

Wren leaned forward until her chin nearly touched my shoulder. "What was your name?" she asked. "Your real one. I want to know it before tomorrow. I want to already know it when they say it."

So I told her. I said it quietly, into the engine noise, a boy's name from a drowned place, a name that meant *heron* in a language only four hundred people ever spoke and perhaps forty do now, and Wren repeated it once, carefully, the way she'd repeat a coordinates fix, storing it, and then she sat back and said, "Good. Now nobody gets to take it again."

* * *

We came down out of the highlands in late afternoon, chasing the river's silver thread through thinning haze, and the light turned gold and long across the floodplain. Home country. I knew every bend of it the way you know the lines of your own palm, and for the first time in twenty years the knowing didn't feel like a burden or a hiding place. It felt like an address.

The strips came up one by one as we descended—Kettle Reach, with its fuel drums; the mission field, mowed crooked; the long grass strip at Halfway where Ferro's cousin sold ice that was mostly hope. And at each one, I saw it before we were close enough to properly see it: boats. People. Small clusters along the banks, standing, looking up as we passed. Word travels faster than aircraft in the river country—it always has, it moves on the current and the radio and the simple human appetite for news—and somehow, impossibly, the story was already ahead of us. The chest. The names. The families. Somewhere between the sandbar and home, someone had talked, or guessed, or dreamed it true, and the river towns had turned out along the banks like spectators along a racecourse, except nobody was racing. We were bringing something home, and they knew it, and they were waiting.

"Look at that," Bo said softly. "Whole river's awake."

Ferro pressed his face to the window. "That's my cousin's dock at Halfway. That's—Captain, that's half the town down there."

Below us, the evening light caught the water, and the water carried our shadow inland at tremendous speed, a little dark airplane-shape running ahead of us over the fields and the roofs and the faces, arriving everywhere first, the way news does, the way names do once you finally speak them.

I keyed the radio and called the field at Kettle Reach, and the reply came back in old Amos's cracked voice, and behind his voice, faintly, I could hear a crowd—a lot of voices, a murmur like the river itself had come indoors. He gave me the wind, gave me the altimeter setting, and then he paused, and I heard him choose his words with visible care even across the static.

"Coral. You're cleared to land. And Captain—there's better'n sixty people down here waiting on you. Families. Upriver families, come down by boat since noon." A breath. "They know what you're carrying, son. They're asking for the names to be read tonight."

I looked at Ines. She was already unwrapping the oilcloth.

"Tell them," I said, "we're ten minutes out. And tell them we're coming in heavy."

"Ain't you always," Amos said, and signed off.

I trimmed her out for the approach, heavy and slow and steady, and the whole cabin leaned forward as one body toward the strip, toward the crowd, toward the evening that was waiting for us with its ears open. The miles behind us—every one of them, the smuggled ones and the honest ones, the twenty years' worth and the two weeks' worth—all of them suddenly counted as years, the way distance does when you're finally going the right direction.

Ahead, the field lights were already lit against the dusk, and the crowd stood among them like stars that had come down to earth to wait for us, and I put the Sandpiper gently down on the ground I'd been flying away from my whole life.
