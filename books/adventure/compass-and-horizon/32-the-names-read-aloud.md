## 32. The Names Read Aloud

The river town had one street and three hundred opinions, and by the time the sun cleared the ridge on the morning of the reading, every one of them had found its way to the green.

Ines stood at the edge of it with her grandfather's half-map folded in her jacket pocket—the half that mattered now less than the paper in Bo's hands, the ledger itself, carried up from the plane like something that might still be breathing. The green was a wide sweep of grass between the church and the fuel depot, hemmed by mango trees, and it had filled the way a river basin fills: quietly at first, then all at once. Families she'd never seen before had come down from the upper tributaries in longboats and dugouts, in trucks with no doors, on foot along paths that didn't appear on any map she owned. Old women in their Sunday clothes. Men who took off their hats when they saw the chest—because that's what it was now, not a crate but a chest, the word changing everything it touched—and set their hats over their hearts.

Wren had strung a line between two mango trunks and hung the photographs from it with wooden clothespins. Forty-one pages, front and back, curling slightly in the heat, each one pinned like laundry, like evidence, like prayer flags. People walked the line slowly, the way you walk past graves. Some stopped. Some reached out and touched the images without quite touching them, fingers hovering a centimeter above the ink.

Ines watched an old man stand in front of page seventeen for so long that she crossed the grass to see what he was looking at. It was a ration tally. Columns of numbers. But his lips were moving, and when she got close enough she understood: he wasn't reading the numbers. He was counting them against something he remembered.

"My mother," he said, without turning around. "She talked about the rice ration. Sixty years, she talked about it. I thought it was just a story." His hand finally came down and rested flat against the photograph, careful as touching a sleeping child. "It wasn't a story."

"No," Ines said. "It wasn't."

He looked at her then, and his eyes were wet and furious at once, and she recognized the expression because she'd been wearing a version of it since the night she read the letters alone in the tent at Sable Bend. Grief and vindication sharing one face, neither willing to give ground.

"You're the Vale girl," he said. "The surveyor's granddaughter."

"I'm the surveyor's granddaughter."

"He drew my father's ferry crossing. Marked it on his chart. Nobody else ever bothered." The old man nodded at her, once, formal as a treaty. "Your family did us one courtesy. Seems right yours should hear the names too."

* * *

They'd argued about the reading for two days, and the argument had been conducted in the particular dialect of people who love each other and are frightened.

Ferro wanted to do it at the church, with the priest, order and liturgy and shade. Bo wanted to do it at the airstrip, because "the government men will come by air and they can damn well land into the sight of it." Tomas said nothing for a long time and then said, very quietly, that it should be done where the water could hear it, because the water had carried what happened, and the water owed the dead an apology it had never been allowed to make.

In the end it was the families themselves who decided, through a woman named Odile Ferran who had a voice like a boat horn and the bearing of someone who had buried more relatives than she could count and had run out of patience for being gentle about it. The green. Midmorning. Everyone. And the names would be read by whoever among the descendants wished to read them—not by officials, not by the crew, not by Coral Underhill, though God knew he'd offered, standing there with his hat in his hands like a man asking permission to enter his own childhood.

"They'll be read by us," Odile had said. "By blood. That's the whole point. For twenty years other people have spoken *about* these people. Today we speak *for* them."

Coral had taken that like a blow to the sternum, and Ines had watched him take it, and had understood that he agreed completely and that agreement hurt worse than refusal would have.

Now she found him at the far edge of the gathering, near the fuel drums, doing what he always did when emotion exceeded his hull capacity—checking things that didn't need checking. A tie-down strap. The tension on a cargo net. His hands were steady. His face was the problem; it had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere twenty years deep, and Ines had learned enough of his geography by now to know he wasn't running from this. He was walking toward it, slowly, the way you wade into cold water.

"Odile's going to call your name," she said.

"I know."

"Not the name you go by."

"I know, Ines." He looked at her then, and there was no flinch in him, which was new, which was maybe three weeks old, which she intended to protect like a flame in wind. "I've been saying it to myself all morning. Trying to remember how it fits in my mouth."

"Does it?"

He considered that honestly, the way he considered everything—like a load that had to be balanced before takeoff. "Like a coat I outgrew. It still fits the shoulders."

Bo came across the grass at a walk that was almost a march, the ledger held against her chest with both arms, and behind her came Wren carrying the bell rope—no, not the rope, Ines realized, a small folding table salvaged from the mission station, wiped clean, set down in the center of the green with the care of people handling something consecrated. Bo laid the ledger on it. Opened it to the first page. And then stepped back, because it wasn't hers either.

The crowd quieted the way weather quiets before a change.

* * *

Odile Ferran stood beside the table and did not begin with the names.

She began with the place. "Sable Bend," she said, and the word went through the crowd like current through wire. "Before it was Sable Bend it had another name, in the language of the people who fished there, and that name meant *where the water rests*. The empire called it Outpost Seven. The ledgers called it a supply station. What it was, was home—to two hundred and six people, counting children, counting the ferryman, counting the schoolteacher, counting the dogs if you want the whole truth, and I think today we want the whole truth."

A sound moved through the green. Not a murmur. Something lower, older, felt in the ribs.

"The government says the outpost was evacuated before the flood season of that year. The government has said this for sixty years. The government is—" and here Odile paused, and Ines saw her choose her next word with the precision of a woman laying a charge of explosive, "—mistaken. There was no evacuation. There was a removal. There were boats that came at night, and lists, and families put on those boats separately from their belongings, and belongings put on separate boats that sank, conveniently, or burned, conveniently, or were sold, conveniently, in the capital. Two hundred and six people went onto the water. Fewer than forty are accounted for afterward. This book"—she laid her palm flat on the open ledger—"is the arithmetic of the difference. Today we read the names. All of them. However long it takes. However hot it gets. However many government boats happen to be on the river."

At that, heads turned toward the water, and Ines followed them, and saw what everyone saw: a white hull with a ministry pennant, anchored midstream, half a kilometer down. It had been there since dawn. It did not approach. It waited, the way paperwork waits.

"Let them watch," Odile said, without even looking. "Let them write it down. It saves Wren the film."

That got the only laugh of the morning, brief and startled, and it broke something loose in the crowd—not tension exactly, but the stiffness that had been holding everyone upright. People settled onto the grass. Children were lifted onto shoulders. Someone unfolded a parasol. And Odile nodded to the first reader, a young man in a mechanic's coveralls with a family resemblance to half the upper river, and the reading began.

* * *

It took four hours.

Ines had thought she understood what that would mean. She had read the ledger herself, alone, by lamplight, and had believed that the private version was the hard version. She had been wrong in the way you're wrong about a storm seen from shore.

Because the names weren't just names. Each one arrived attached to a living person's voice, and each voice did something different with it. One woman read her names fast, clipped, like pulling teeth. An old fisherman read his slow, giving each syllable its full weight, and after certain ones he would add a single sentence—"He taught me the net cast I still use"—and then move on. A teenage girl got halfway through a name and stopped, and her mother's arm came around her, and the girl finished it anyway, and Ines heard her own heartbeat in the silence around that finished name.

And between the official entries, the descendants added the record the ledger couldn't hold. *She was pregnant.* *He had a laugh you could hear across the river.* *They never found her shoes.* The ledger gave the count. The voices gave the cost. Together they built, hour by hour, in the heat, under the mango trees, a thing no archive in the capital possessed: a true inventory of Sable Bend.

Tomas cried openly from the second hour on and made no attempt to hide it. Ferro cooked through the whole thing—someone had to feed three hundred people, grief or no grief—and the smell of rice and smoked fish drifted over the green, and Ines understood that this too was part of it: the dead being remembered amid the ordinary business of staying alive, which was, she suspected, the only memorial any of them would have trusted.

Wren sat cross-legged at the front with the camera in her lap, not shooting. Just holding it. Ines caught her eye once and the girl shook her head slightly—*not today, today isn't for film*—and Ines could have kissed her.

Coral stood at the back. He had refused a chair. He stood with his hat off—he never took the hat off, she'd known him two months and had catalogued the hat as permanent as his hands—and he listened to every name with his eyes fixed on the middle distance, on some coordinates only he could see, and his face did not crumble and did not go blank but did something harder: it stayed present. He let every name hit him and kept his feet. Once, near the third hour, a name landed that made his jaw work sideways, and Ines, watching from ten feet away, saw his lips shape a word—a small word, a child's word, maybe *mama*, maybe something in the outpost dialect she'd never heard—and then he breathed, and stood, and stayed.

* * *

Then Odile Ferran reached the final page of the removal manifest, and the green went so quiet Ines could hear the river.

"Last entry," Odile said. "Children transported under separate authorization. Survivors' register." She adjusted her glasses. "These are the ones who lived. We read them too. We read them loudest."

And she began, and the names of the living came out one by one, and people in the crowd turned to look at the elders among them, matching names to faces, sixty years late, and there was weeping of a different texture now—not grief alone but its strange twin, the grief of survival, which no one ever builds monuments to.

Then Odile stopped. Looked up over her glasses. Found the back of the crowd the way a searchlight finds a boat.

"Coral Underhill," she said. "Step forward."

The whole green turned.

He didn't move for a moment. Then he came, hat in hand, through the parted crowd, and Ines walked the last stretch beside him because she was not going to let him walk it alone, and neither, she saw, was Wren, who fell in on his other side with her chin up like a soldier.

"Read your entry yourself," Odile said. "Nobody speaks a man's birth name but him, the day he takes it back."

Coral stood at the table. The ledger lay open. He looked down at the page and Ines watched him find the line—watched his finger rise, hesitate, touch it—and when he spoke, his voice came out low and roughened, a voice dredged from below the waterline.

"Coral Antero Vale," he read. "Age nine. Transported north, foster placement, records sealed by administrative order." He stopped. Swallowed. "Vale," he said again, to the crowd, to the river, to himself. "My mother's name. They changed mine when they changed my papers. I was nine years old and a man with a clipboard told me my name was Underhill now, and I was too scared to argue, and I spent twenty years making sure nobody asked."

Silence. Then the old man from page seventeen—the rice-ration man—called out from the crowd, clear as a bell:

"Vale! There's Vales here! There's been Vales on this river two hundred years!"

And the crowd turned, and Ines felt it turn toward her too, felt the connection complete itself in three hundred minds at once—the cartographer, the pilot, the same bloodline braided through the drowned outpost and the letters and the half-map—and she heard someone say, wonderingly, *"Her grandfather flew the boy out,"* and someone else say, *"No—her grandfather wrote the testimony, the boy WAS the testimony,"* and the truth assembling itself publicly, piece by piece, faster than she could have managed privately, the way a crowd sometimes understands a thing before any individual in it does.

Coral found her eyes across the space between them. Neither of them smiled. It wasn't that kind of moment. It was the other kind—the kind where two people realize, simultaneously and in front of everyone, that they were never strangers at all, that the river had been introducing them for years and they'd only just gotten around to listening.

Odile Ferran closed the ledger with both hands, gently, like shutting a coffin that had finally been allowed to stay shut—or opening one. Ines couldn't decide which, and suspected it was both.

"That's the roll," Odile said. "Two hundred and six. Read entire. Witnessed entire." She raised her voice to carry to the white hull on the river. "Witnessed ENTIRE."

And the crowd answered—not words, just sound, a long low affirmation that rolled across the green and out over the water, and Ines saw, midstream, figures on the ministry boat's deck, standing very still, one of them raising a hand to his forehead in something that might have been a salute or might have been a man shading his eyes from a light he hadn't expected.

* * *

Afterward, the green dissolved into food and argument and children, the way gatherings do, grief handing the baton to life without either one breaking stride. Ferro's rice went fast. Somebody produced a guitar. The old ration man told stories to a ring of listeners and embellished freely and was permitted to.

Ines found Coral at the table, repacking the ledger into its oilcloth with the tenderness he usually reserved for engines.

"So," she said. "Vale."

"Don't start."

"I'm not starting. I'm observing that I've been attracted to a relative for two months and I'd like to know how distant."

That got the closest thing to a laugh she'd seen from him all day—short, surprised, real. "Third cousins, maybe. Maybe fourth. River families tangle." He cinched the oilcloth. "My mother used to talk about a cousin who went off to the capital to draw maps. Said he'd come back someday and put us on the chart properly, so nobody could pretend we'd never been there." He looked up at her. "He never came back. But I guess he sent you."

"I guess he did."

They stood there a moment, in the noise and the heat and the smell of woodsmoke, and Ines reached over and straightened his collar, an ordinary gesture, a wife's gesture or a sister's, and he let her, and that letting said more than the whole morning's testimony.

Then Wren came running across the grass, camera bouncing on its strap, and her face had a look Ines had learned to dread—the look of a girl bringing bad news she'd already decided not to soften.

"Boat's coming in," Wren said. "The white one. And it's not just the surveyors." She caught her breath. "There's a launch ahead of it flying the ministry colors proper, and a man on deck in a gray suit, and Reyes is standing next to him looking like he's going to be sick. Bo says the gray-suit man has a document case chained to his wrist."

Coral went still. Ines watched the day's grace drain out of his posture and something harder pour in behind it.

"A document case," he repeated.

"Chained," Wren confirmed. "Ferro says that means confiscation orders. Ferro reads the river news."

Down at the landing, the white hull was already swinging toward the dock, its wake unrolling across the low bright water, and the crowd on the green was turning toward it name by name, the laughter dying, the guitar falling silent, three hundred people who had just finished speaking their dead aloud watching the machinery of silence come up the river to answer them.

Coral picked up the ledger and held it against his chest.

"Well," he said. "They heard it. Now let's see if they can take it back."
