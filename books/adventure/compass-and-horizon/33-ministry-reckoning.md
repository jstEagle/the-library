## 33. Ministry Reckoning

They came on a Thursday, which felt right. Bureaucracy always arrived midweek, when the river was too low for excuses and the town too busy to pretend surprise.

Coral saw the boat first from the strip—a government launch with the ministry's grey paint and the particular way it sat in the water, heavy with men who hadn't packed their own gear. He was re-tying the Cessna's tail wire, or pretending to, because Wren had been up there since dawn and he'd learned that the safest place to stand during her engine work was anywhere she could see him being useful.

"Three of them," Wren called down from the wing strut, wiping grease across her forearm like war paint. "Grey suits. One's got a briefcase chained to his wrist—no, I'm joking, but he's holding it like it's chained."

"You count fast."

"I count what matters." She dropped to the grass, light on her feet the way she'd never been as a small girl, all elbows then, all precision now. "You going to meet them at the dock?"

"No."

"Good. I'll tell Ines you said that with your whole chest while standing right here where they can see you not moving."

He almost smiled. Almost. The grey suits were coming up the hill from the dock now, three of them plus Reyes walking half a step behind like a man escorting prisoners he'd grown fond of. The lead one was broad through the shoulders in the way of men who lifted weights instead of cargo, and his shoes were wrong for the mud—shiny things that would be ruined by noon. Coral had spent twenty years reading shoes. Shiny shoes meant headquarters. Headquarters meant orders written before anyone left the capital, which meant flexibility was already dead on arrival.

Which suited him fine. Flexibility was how they'd buried the outpost the first time.

* * *

The meeting happened in Ferro's cookhouse, because it was the only room in town big enough for an audience and the only one with coffee worth drinking. By the time the officials settled at the long table, half the river town had found reasons to drift in—Bo leaning against the doorframe with his arms crossed, Tomas dripping lake water onto the boards, Ines sitting front and center with her grandfather's survey rolled beside her like a drawn sword.

The lead official introduced himself as Undersecretary Halloway, and Coral gave him credit for one thing: he didn't pretend this was a courtesy call.

"The file concerning the Sable Bend outpost," Halloway began, "has been under archival review for some time. Certain materials recovered from the site are government property under the Salvage Act of—"

"The ledger," Ines said.

Halloway paused. "I'm sorry?"

"You mean the ledger. And the letters. You can say the words. Everyone in this room has read them." She didn't raise her voice. She never needed to. "We've read them aloud, actually. All of them. Twice."

Coral watched the man recalibrate. It was a small thing, a flicker behind the eyes, but Coral had flown enough nervous passengers to know the look of someone whose plan had just met weather it wasn't briefed for.

"There are protocols," Halloway said, "regarding sensitive historical materials. Protocols designed to protect—"

"The families," Reyes said quietly, from the end of the table.

Everyone turned. The junior officer looked tired in a way that went past his skin into something structural, but his voice held steady. "That's who the protocols were supposed to protect, sir. That's what the original file says. Compensation authorized, disbursement pending, pending, pending—for sixty years." He set a document on the table, squared its edges with two fingers. "I've prepared a summary. Cross-referenced against the manifest."

Halloway looked at the document the way a man looks at a snake in his boot.

"That copy is unauthorized."

"Yes, sir."

"It will need to be surrendered."

"It's already been distributed, sir." Reyes didn't blink. "To every descendant family on the manifest. Fourteen households. I mailed them Tuesday, before we came upriver. There's a second set with the district registrar, a third with the mission archive at Puerto Verde, and—" he glanced at Wren, and something almost like a smile touched the corner of his mouth, "—a fourth location I'm not authorized to disclose."

Wren, sitting cross-legged on the counter next to the coffee urn, examined her fingernails with tremendous innocence.

Coral felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight for twenty years. The machinery of silence ran on a simple fuel: one copy, one custodian, one point of failure. They'd built the old empire's erasure that way—one file, one sealed room, one chain of custody. And these children of the new empire had just scattered the truth into fourteen kitchens and a church basement and wherever Wren kept her secrets, which was everywhere.

Halloway's jaw worked. "Mr.—Lockridge, is it?" He turned to Coral as if the pilot might be more reasonable than the cartographer. "You recovered the chest. You're the salvage operator of record. I'm authorized to offer you compensation for your cooperation. Substantial compensation. The materials would go to the capital for proper archival treatment, and the matter would be resolved quietly, without further distress to—"

"To whom?" Coral asked.

It was the first thing he'd said, and the room went still around it.

"To whom," he repeated, standing, "would you be sparing distress? Because I've read those letters, Undersecretary. I've read them more times than I can count, and I was there before I could read at all. There's no distress left to spare. There's only the debt. Sixty years of interest on it." He let that sit. "You want to buy the ledger so you can close the account without paying."

Halloway's face did something complicated. For a moment—just a moment—the official mask slipped and Coral saw underneath it a man who had probably read the file himself, probably late at night in some ministry annex, probably alone with the same letters, and had chosen his career over the knot in his stomach. Coral knew that choice from the inside. He'd made it for twenty years, flying miles instead of years, keeping his name changed and his mouth shut.

He wasn't angry at the man. That was the strange part. He was angry at the machine, and the man was just a part that moved when the machine moved.

"The centenary ceremonies," Halloway said finally, and his voice had lost its briefing-room polish. "In eight months. There are delegations, treaties, investments—the government cannot have a massacre narrative circulating—"

"A massacre narrative," Bo said from the doorframe, flat as sheet metal. "You mean a history."

"With photographs," Wren added sweetly, hopping off the counter. She produced the salvaged camera and held it up. "Forty-one exposures. Ledger pages, letter pages, the bell, the foundations, the graves at the mission station. Developed twice. Stored in two places." She tilted her head. "Want to see my contact sheets? I do good work. Everybody says so."

Tomas snorted into his coffee.

Halloway looked around the room—at the mechanic, the diver, the cook, the girl with the camera, the cartographer with the survey, the junior officer with his distributed copies—and Coral watched him arrive, visibly, at the arithmetic of it. There was no single throat to cut. There was no file to seal, because the file had become people, and people had become inconveniently numerous and inconveniently unafraid.

"Undersecretary," Ines said, and her voice gentled in a way that was somehow worse than sharpness, "nobody in this room wants a scandal. We want what the file promised. Compensation. Names restored. The site marked honestly. Give us that, and the centenary can include the truth instead of hiding from it, and I promise you the truth reads better than you think. My grandfather thought so. He died believing somebody would eventually publish his survey." She rested her hand on the rolled map. "I'd rather it happened with the ministry's name on it than without."

The silence stretched. Outside, the river ran low and clear over its bars, honest as it ever got.

Halloway looked at Reyes. Reyes looked back, and whatever passed between them wasn't insubordination anymore—it was succession. The younger man had simply gotten ahead of the older one, the way current gets ahead of a boat.

"I'll need to consult the ministry," Halloway said at last.

"Of course," Ines said. "There's a radio at the airstrip office. Coral will show you. He keeps it in better order than the ministry keeps its files."

* * *

They consulted for two days. Coral knew because he flew the mail run Wednesday morning and came back Wednesday afternoon to find the launch still tied at the dock and the shiny shoes ruined anyway, because Halloway had gone down to the waterline at dawn and stood looking at the exposed foundations across the channel, mud to his ankles, briefcase forgotten on the bank.

Coral walked out and stood beside him. Neither spoke for a while.

"My grandmother came from a river country," Halloway said eventually. "Different river. Same story, probably. Nobody wrote hers down either." He wiped mud from his shoe with a stick, uselessly. "I told myself, coming up here, that I'd find a way to make this quiet. Contain it. It's what I'm paid for." A pause. "Then the junior officer handed me a copy of the manifest last night, and my own great-aunt's maiden name is on page six. Removed families. I checked the parish records this morning at the mission. She survived. Her line survived. Me."

The river ticked against the pilings.

"What are you going to do?" Coral asked.

"What Reyes did, I expect. Get ahead of it." Halloway straightened, and for the first time his shoulders looked less like a bureaucrat's and more like a man carrying something he'd decided to carry. "Restoration of names. Compensation per the original authorization, indexed. The site marked in the national survey." He glanced sideways. "Your cartographer will want language approval on the marker text. Fair warning: she'll fight you over every comma."

"She fights me over commas when I'm not even writing anything."

"And the pilot," Halloway added, quieter. "The file lists a boy evacuated the week before the removals. Birth name, foster placement, subsequent record. It's all there. If the names are restored publicly—"

"He'll be on the list," Coral said. "All of him. Both names."

Halloway studied him for a long moment. "That's not what I expected you to say."

"Yeah." Coral looked out at the water, at the drowned outpost lying patient under the low current, at the bell they'd raised and hung on its frame at Sable Bend waiting for Saturday. "Took me forty chapters to say it. Don't waste it."

* * *

The ministry boat left Friday evening with Halloway aboard and Reyes staying behind—"to verify conditions on site," per the paperwork, though everyone understood he was staying because he belonged here now, or soon would. Wren walked him up from the dock personally, chattering about camera apertures, and Coral watched them go and felt the last load shift off the plane's center of gravity.

Bo found him at the hangar as the sun went down, both of them doing nothing in particular, which was how the two of them talked best.

"So that's it," Bo said. "Twenty years of keeping your head down, and the whole thing comes apart because a teenage girl with a salvaged camera wouldn't shut up."

"Don't let her hear you say it like that. She'll want a plaque."

"She'll build the plaque herself. Out of wreck aluminum." Bo was quiet a moment, turning a wrench over in his hands. "You know what I keep thinking about? The day you hired me. You said, 'I don't ask questions and I don't answer them.' Remember?"

"I remember hoping you'd forget."

"Never forget anything, me. That's the curse of mechanics." He set the wrench down. "Glad you started answering them, Captain. Took you long enough."

From the open door of the house, lamplight spilled gold onto the grass, and inside it Coral could see Ines at the table with her maps spread wide, and Wren beside her arguing happily about lettering styles for the memorial wall, and beyond them the river running low and silver toward Sable Bend, where tomorrow morning the bell would ring—once for each name, all of them, including a boy named for a saint whose feast day fell in flood season, a boy who had been flown out of the smoke and renamed and taught to keep moving until a cartographer with half a map taught him to stop.

Saturday was coming. The whole town was coming. And Coral Lockridge—who had spent two decades measuring his life in miles—discovered he was nervous as a first-flight cadet about standing still in front of everyone he loved.

Ines looked up through the doorway and caught him watching. She didn't wave. She just smiled, slow and certain, and pointed at the empty chair beside her.

He went.
