## 34. Ines's Answer

The printing press smelled of hot lead and ink, and standing beside it while the first copies came off the rollers, Ines Vale felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since she was nine years old.

*The Vale Survey: The Drowned Outpost at Sable Bend — Final Report of Elias Vale, Cartographer, with Supplementary Testimony of the Survivor Families.*

Not her title. She had wanted to call it something plainer, something like *What Was Found*, but the families had voted on it at the second meeting in the river town hall, and old Marta Okonkwo—whose grandmother's name sat in the ledger between two children who never grew up—had said, "Let him have his name back too. He earned it twice." So it was the Vale Survey, and Elias Vale's hand-drawn maps were reproduced at full scale in the appendix, every contour line he'd ever inked now legible against the terrain Coral had helped them verify by air.

The pressman handed her the first copy, still warm.

She held it the way you hold a sleeping child you're afraid of waking. Her grandfather had been a rumor for thirty years—a name on a half-map, a man who walked into the interior with his instruments and his questions and never walked out. Now he was a book. Two hundred pages. Forty-one names in the testimony section, read aloud at the gathering under the bell, each one followed by silence long enough to honor it. And in the appendix, in the surveyor's own cramped notation, a line she had read so many times she could recite it:

*The removal was not an evacuation. I counted the boats going downriver. There were not enough boats.*

He had known. He had written it down, sealed it, sent it toward the coast, and then gone back upriver one more time—to get the rest of the families out, Coral said, or to document what couldn't be stopped, no one would ever know which—and the river had kept him.

Ines ran her thumb along the cover's edge. Somewhere in this town, in a room above Ferro's kitchen where the light came through salt-fogged windows, Coral Underhill was packing a duffel bag.

She'd seen it that morning. She hadn't said anything. She'd watched him fold a shirt the way pilots fold everything—tight, efficient, made to disappear into small spaces—and she'd drunk her coffee and let him do it, because she understood that men like Coral didn't announce departures. They simply became weather. One morning the sky was clear and the next there was a plane climbing out over the strips, and everyone left behind squinted up at it and told themselves they'd always known he wouldn't stay.

Twenty years of miles. That was how Bo described him once, not unkindly, turning a wrench in the hangar heat. *Some men measure their lives in miles, miss. He's got twenty years of 'em and not one of them is a place.*

Well. She had published a dead man's map. She could publish one more thing.

* * *

The print run was five hundred copies. It sounded absurd until Reyes pointed out, dry as a customs form, that there were perhaps three hundred people alive who descended from the outpost families, scattered across four river towns and two coastal cities, and that the ministry's centenary committee had already ordered forty copies "for archival distribution," which meant they intended to shelve them somewhere dark and call it closure.

"Let them order a hundred," Marta had said. "Paper remembers longer than clerks do."

The families' blessing had come formally, in a meeting Ines chaired with her heart hammering the whole time—not because she feared refusal, but because she feared the opposite. When Marta stood and said, "Elias Vale died carrying our names. His granddaughter carried them home. Print it," Ines had to look out the window at the river for a full minute before she could speak.

Now the boxes were stacked in the print shop, addressed in her own hand. She spent the afternoon sealing them, and the work steadied her the way drafting always had—the geometry of it, the right angles, the certainty that a thing done carefully stays done.

Wren appeared around four o'clock, slipping through the door with the particular silence of a girl who had learned to move unheard around adults with secrets.

"You're staring at the boxes."

"I'm checking the addresses."

"You've checked that one three times." Wren leaned against the stack, arms crossed, thirteen going on forty. "He's leaving tomorrow."

Ines set down the sealing brush. "Did he tell you that?"

"He doesn't have to tell me. He starts folding things." Wren picked at the twine on a box, not looking at her. "He did it when I was eight. Before the flood season. I hid his boots for two days. Didn't work. He flew barefoot to the coast and bought new ones."

Despite everything, Ines laughed. "That sounds like him."

"He'll fly upriver after the delivery. To the strip he cut. You know about the strip?"

"I know about the strip."

"He never took anyone there. Not even me." Wren said it without accusation, which somehow made it heavier. Then she straightened, and looked at Ines with those flat, assessing eyes that missed nothing, and said: "You could hide his boots. If you wanted."

"Wren—"

"It worked about as well as mine did," the girl admitted. "But it's the principle of the thing." And she was gone through the door, quick as a bird, leaving Ines alone with five hundred books and a decision.

* * *

She found him at the strips at dusk.

Of course he was at the strips. He was sitting on the wheel chock of the old cargo plane—Bo's rebuilt engine ticking as it cooled, the airframe still wearing its scars from the sandbar landing like medals nobody asked for—and he was looking west, where the sun was going down the color of brass behind the smoke haze that hung over the dry country every year now, thin and far off, but present. A reminder that the world kept burning whether or not anyone was keeping records.

He heard her boots on the gravel—of course he did; the man heard everything except the things said directly to him—and didn't turn.

"Boxes all sealed?" he asked.

"All sealed."

"Good. I can take the upriver ones tomorrow. Save the mail flight the weight."

And there it was. Tomorrow. Already scheduled, already folded tight enough to fit in a small space.

"Coral."

"Mm."

"Walk with me."

He glanced at her then, and she saw him weigh it—the instinct to stay seated, to keep the conversation at propeller distance—and lose. He stood, brushed off his trousers, and fell into step beside her, hands in his pockets, a tall man making himself smaller out of habit.

They walked the length of the strips. The gravel crunched. Somewhere behind them Ferro's kitchen was sending up woodsmoke and the smell of onions, and ahead the runway lights were just beginning to flicker on, one by one, the way they did at civil dusk, little orange eyes opening along the ground.

"Ines." He said it carefully, the way a man tests ice. "Whatever you're going to say—"

"You don't know what I'm going to say."

"I know you walked me away from the hangar to say it. That's cartography. I can read a route."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Then read this one." She stopped walking and turned to face him, and the last light caught the planes behind her, and she thought: *say it plainly. He has spent twenty years being lied to and lying. Give him one plain sentence.* "I love you."

The words went out of her like birds startled off water.

Coral went very still. Down the strips, a night bird called. The runway lights buzzed.

"I'm not asking you to say it back," she continued, before the silence could harden into something either of them would regret. "I'm not asking you to promise anything. I've spent eight months learning how you operate, Coral Underhill, and here's what I've learned: you give people jobs instead of answers. You tell half-truths as kindness until the kindness curdles. You love people by hauling their cargo and memorizing their routes and never once saying out loud that they matter to you, because saying it makes it a thing that can be taken away." She held his gaze. His eyes were dark and wide and utterly defenseless, and it cost him visibly to keep them open. "So I'm not taking anything away. I'm putting something down in front of you, and you can pick it up or leave it where it lies, and either way I will still be here tomorrow, and the day after, working on the memorial commission, and correcting the survey plates, and drinking terrible coffee in Ferro's kitchen. No conditions. That's the whole offer. Love without a flight plan attached."

His jaw worked. For a long moment he said nothing, and she watched him do the arithmetic he'd been doing his whole life—the subtraction, the leaving-before-you're-left, the twenty years of miles measured precisely so that no single mile would ever be a place where losing him would hurt anyone.

"The survey's published," he said finally, hoarse. "Your grandfather's found. Your work here—" He gestured vaguely at the town, the river, the whole finished architecture of her quest. "There's nothing keeping you."

"That's your answer? An argument?"

"It's a good argument."

"It's a coward's argument dressed in a good argument's clothes." She stepped closer. Close enough to see the gray coming into his temples, the scar on his jaw from the rapids, the exhaustion of a man who had spoken his childhood name aloud in front of forty-one families and hadn't slept properly since. "Coral. Listen to me. I chased a ghost across this country for six years, and what I found instead was a chest, a crew, a girl who photographs ledgers, a mechanic who threatens captains, a cook who feeds everyone's grief, and a pilot who turned out to be the last living child of the place my grandfather died trying to save." Her voice cracked and she let it. "You are not the consolation prize. You are the finding."

Something moved through his face then—something breaking open, tectonic, slow, the way low water reveals foundations that have been drowned for decades.

"I don't know how to stay," he said quietly. "I want you to understand that. It's not that I won't. I genuinely don't—I've been leaving since I was seven years old. It's the only maneuver I know. Every other one I'd be learning in the air, with no instructor, and I'd make mistakes, and you'd be there for the mistakes, and—" He stopped. Breathed. "You deserve better than a man's first attempt at landing."

"I deserve the truth," Ines said. "Which is what you just gave me. That's the first honest instrument reading you've ever filed, and I'm telling you it's enough. We'll learn the approach together. I'm a cartographer, Coral. Making maps of places nobody's charted is literally my profession. Uncharted territory doesn't frighten me. It's the only kind worth drawing."

A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't gotten so rusty. He looked down at the gravel between them, then up, and when he spoke again his voice was low and rough and real.

"If I stay," he said, "it'll be the first time in my life I've chosen a place instead of a heading. I need you to know what you're asking. Some nights I'll wake up hearing the engines. Some mornings I'll be halfway to the strips before I remember there's nowhere I'm going. Twenty years of habit doesn't burn off in one landing."

"I know."

"And Wren—"

"Is hiding your boots as we speak. Probably. Check under your bunk."

That did it. The laugh broke free this time, startled out of him, and he pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes for a moment, and when he lowered it they were bright, and Ines thought she had seen this man fly through a storm front on a failing engine with more composure than he was showing right now, and loved him infinitely more for it.

"Come here," she said.

He came. She pulled him in by the collar of his shirt, and he held her the way he did everything at first—carefully, like something that might be repossessed—and then less carefully, and then completely, his face buried in her hair, his breath uneven against her temple, both of them standing on the gravel between the runway lights while the dark came down soft over the river country.

"No conditions," he murmured into her hair.

"None."

"I'll still fly. The medicine runs. The mail. The upriver deliveries."

"I'd be worried if you didn't. But you'll come home to a hangar with your name painted over the door. Bo's already designing the lettering. She showed me the stencil."

He pulled back just far enough to look at her, scandalized. "She what?"

"She says a man needs an address or he turns into weather." Ines smiled up at him. "Her words."

"God help me. The whole crew knew before I did."

"The whole crew has known since the sandbar. Possibly since the night radio. Tomas had money on it."

"There was *money*—"

"Reyes won, actually. Don't ask me how a junior ministry officer ended up in the pool. Ask him about the manifest copy sometime; he gets cagey."

Coral shook his head, laughing again, helpless, and Ines realized she was cataloguing the sound—filing it, the way she'd file a newly surveyed peak, marking it on the map of him as territory confirmed, here, present, existing. He cupped her face in one calloused hand, thumb brushing her cheekbone, and looked at her with an expression she suspected no one had seen on him in twenty years: a man with nowhere to be.

"Ask me," he said.

"Ask you what?"

"What I would have said, if you'd asked me to stay with conditions. With promises. All the formal instruments." His mouth curved. "I want you to know what you didn't need."

Ines considered him. The lights buzzed. The river murmured somewhere past the tree line, running low and honest, and upriver, on a strip cut by a boy who no longer existed, a chest's last contents waited for tomorrow's flight.

"No," she said. "I don't need it. But I'll take it anyway, if you're offering."

"I'm offering." He drew a breath, and she watched him choose the words like footholds. "I, Coral Underhill—born with another name, at a place that's going on the map now, properly, in the open—intend to stay. Not because the flying's done. Because for the first time in my life, the horizon and home point the same direction, and it took a cartographer to draw it that way." His voice dropped. "I love you, Ines Vale. I think I have since you cornered me with half a map and refused to be lied to. I just didn't have the courage to file the report."

"Late filing accepted," she said, and kissed him, and the runway lights burned steady all down the strips, guiding nothing home, because everything that mattered had already landed.

* * *

They walked back together, unhurried, and at the hangar door Ines stopped him with a hand on his arm.

"One condition after all," she said, and watched him tense, and grinned. "Tomorrow, when you fly the chest upriver to the strip—you take me with you. I've surveyed that site from the water and from the archive. I want to see it from your altitude. From *your* strip. The one you cut and never told anyone about."

Coral looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached into the duffel by the door—half-packed, shirts folded tight and small—and pulled out a folded sheet of paper, worn soft at the creases, and handed it to her.

It was a weight-and-balance worksheet. Two seats marked occupied. Her initials already entered in the passenger column, in his careful pilot's hand, dated tomorrow.

"I folded things," he said, "but apparently I wasn't planning to go alone."

Down the row of hangars, Wren's silhouette detached itself from the shadows, having witnessed everything, and called out across the gravel with the triumphant tone of a girl whose principles had been vindicated:

*"I TOLD YOU THE BOOTS WOULD WORK!"*

"We didn't hide any boots!" Ines shouted back, laughing.

"I hid them ANYWAY! Check under his bunk!"

Coral closed his eyes. "Twenty years of miles," he muttered, "and I get blackmailed by a thirteen-year-old in my own hangar."

"Our hangar," Ines corrected, lacing her fingers through his. "Get used to the plural."

Above the strips, the first stars were coming out over the smoke haze, and tomorrow the compass would swing upriver one last time this season—but tonight, for the first time in either of their lives, every heading pointed home.
