## 36. The Last Flight of the Season

The chest rode in the cargo bay like a passenger, strapped down with the same care Bo gave a live engine, and I kept checking on it in the mirror the way you check on someone sleeping.

Not all of it went upriver. Ines had been clear about that, and the families had been clearer still when we sat with them under the mission awning two nights ago and laid it all out—ledger, letters, photographs, the survey plates her grandfather had hidden inside the bell housing a century too early to be believed. The originals would go to the archive now, the real one, the one Reyes was building out of his copied manifest and Wren's film and the ministry's own embarrassed paperwork. But some things belonged to no archive.

The letters written by hand, from mothers, from the outpost schoolteacher, from a boy who signed himself with a name I hadn't spoken aloud since the night a floatplane lifted me off the water and a kind stranger told me I'd be called something else now. Those went home. Wherever home still was.

"Weight and balance," Bo said, walking the wing with his palm flat against the fabric like he was taking its pulse. "You're nose-heavy with that chest forward. Shift it back a frame."

"It rides better where I can see it."

"It rides better where the airplane says it rides." He said it without heat. Bo had spent twenty years not asking me questions and three weeks learning that the answers were worse than the questions, and somewhere in there he'd decided the flying was the only part he trusted me to do without argument. "Tomas checked your floats twice. Ferro packed food for four days in case the weather sits on you. You've got a crew that loves you, Lockridge, and every one of them thinks this flight is a bad idea."

"And you?"

"I think it's the best bad idea you've ever had." He patted the wing twice, the way he did before every engine start, a superstition he'd deny under torture. "Go put the mail where it belongs."

Wren came around the tail carrying the panel she'd machined—a patch over the old bullet scar from the rapids run, sanded smooth, painted the same faded blue as the rest of us. She held it up like a diploma.

"Custodian approves," she said. "Barely. Your rivet spacing is criminal, by the way. I fixed it while you were brooding."

"I don't brood."

"You absolutely brood. It's your whole personality. It's just that lately you brood facing forward instead of backward, so nobody noticed the difference." She climbed onto the pontoon and handed the panel up to me herself, and for a second she stood there in the morning light with grease on her jaw and her chin set exactly the way mine sets in mirrors, and neither of us said anything about it, because we didn't have to anymore. That was the strange gift of the last month: the things we'd spent years not saying had finally been said, and what was left was just—us. Plain as weather.

"You could come," I said. Not for the first time.

"You could let me come. Not for the first time." She jumped down. "But this one's yours. I get it. Take the pictures I loaded in the camera, though. If there's anything left of the strip, I want to see whether your engineering has aged better than your riveting."

Ines was waiting at the edge of the strips with her map case and the look she wore when she was trying not to give orders. She'd gotten better at it. So had I, at receiving them.

"Coordinates are marked," she said, tapping the case. "Your strip, my survey grid, the village site, the bell cairn. When you're done up there, fly the line back along the east channel—the light's good at evening and I want the shoreline updated. The drowned country deserves an honest map before the water comes back and takes it again."

"Yes, cartographer."

"And Coral." She stepped closer, lowered her voice below the wind. "Whatever you find when you land—whoever's left, whatever they say or don't say—you don't have to carry it alone after today. That's the whole point of the flight. Remember that when your hands want to take the yoke and your heart wants to stay."

I kissed her, brief and certain, in front of God and the fuel drums and half the town, because a man who'd spent twenty years hiding in plain sight could afford one public act.

Then I fired the engine and took the river north.

* * *

The drowned country opened beneath me the way it always did, green water braiding through green forest, the old channels showing their bones in the dry season like veins in an old man's hand. I'd flown this line ten thousand times and told myself it meant nothing, that it was just terrain, just work. Flying it now, with the chest behind me full of other people's handwriting, I understood I'd been lying the way rivers lie—smoothly, continuously, out of pure necessity.

Forty minutes up. The smoke haze thinned. The hills shouldered in closer, and then there it was, exactly where I'd left it in my memory because I'd never once flown over it in daylight with anyone watching.

The strip.

I'd cut it the autumn after they brought me downriver, sixteen years old and mute with everything I'd seen, working with a borrowed machete and a borrowed mule and a borrowed anger, clearing a shelf of high ground above a bend where the current ran clean. Two hundred meters of crushed termite mound and rolled clay, hemmed in by kapok trees I'd left standing on purpose so no government pilot doing a photo pass would ever read it as a runway. From the air it looked like nothing. From the air it looked like a clearing where somebody once thought about building something and changed his mind.

That was more or less the truth.

I brought her in slow, flaps down, watching the tree line slide past the wingtips close enough to count the orchids on the bark. The strip surface held. Twenty years of rain and root-press and not one wheel on it since the day I rolled it flat—and it held, firm as a promise, and the tires kissed it and we were down.

I cut the engine and the silence came in like floodwater.

Kapok leaves ticking. A hawk somewhere up the channel. My own breathing, loud in the cockpit, and under it the river saying the only thing it had ever said, which was *keep moving*, except that today I'd stopped.

I sat there a long time. Long enough for the sweat to cool. Then I unstrapped the chest, carried it down the ladder, and walked the strip end to end, boots on clay I had shaped with my own hands when I was a boy who couldn't say his own name.

At the far end, half-swallowed by vine, stood the marker I'd carved the first week and never told anyone about—not even myself, really; I'd told myself it was a boundary post. A flat stone with letters cut shallow, because a sixteen-year-old with a chisel and no right to grieve publicly does his grieving in millimeters:

FOR THE ONES THE RIVER TOOK.
FOR THE ONES IT GAVE BACK WRONG.

I'd forgotten how badly I'd spelled *wrong*. I hadn't forgotten cutting it.

* * *

They were waiting at the bend below the strip. I'd radioed ahead through the mission relay—Ines's idea, everything now was Ines's idea, the whole architecture of my new life built on her stubbornness—and word travels upriver faster than aircraft when the news is family.

Eleven of them. Old men and women mostly, and two middle-aged sisters who had the Vale height and the Vale jaw, and a boy of maybe nine who stared at the airplane the way I'd once stared at the sky, like it was either salvation or the thing that ate your family, depending on the day.

They knew the chest by its seal. One of the old men, Ambo, who must have been ninety if he was a day, put his palm flat on the lid the way Bo touched wings, and closed his eyes.

"My mother packed this," he said. "I carried it to the storehouse the night before. I was twelve. She said, *hold it flat, the ink will smear.*" He opened his eyes. "I held it flat. They took her anyway."

There was nothing to say to that, so I didn't try. I unbuckled the straps and opened the lid in the shade of the kapoks, and I handed the letters out one at a time, and the eleven of them read their dead aloud in voices that cracked and steadied and cracked again, and the boy who wasn't reading anything sat next to me on the stone marker and asked, eventually, the question children ask and adults spend lifetimes dodging:

"Were you here? Before?"

"Yes," I said. Because lying to children was the trade I'd learned first and abandoned last, and I was done with it. "I was one of the ones it gave back wrong."

He considered that with the gravity of a customs inspector. "What was your name?"

And here was the thing I'd flown forty minutes to do and hadn't known I was flying toward: I told him. My birth name, the one from the ledger, the one Ines had read aloud in the town square with ninety others, the one that had sat in my chest heavier than any cargo until the day it didn't. I said it out loud on the strip I'd cut myself, and the name fit the way the strip fit the tires—worn but true, holding after all these years.

"That's a good name," the boy said. "Why'd you change it?"

"Because changing it felt like surviving. And then keeping the old one secret felt like surviving too. Turns out only one of those was true."

He nodded like this made perfect sense, which it did, and went back to watching the hawk.

Ambo finished with the last letter and folded it along creases worn soft by a century of almost-being-read, and looked at me across the open chest with eyes gone pale as river fog.

"The government men came through in the dry season, year before last," he said. "Asked if we remembered anything. We said no." A pause. "We learned saying no from the best. But you—" he tapped the ledger, the manifest page with the column of names, my old name among them, "—you remembered enough for all of us. You flew it home. Whatever else you are, boy, you're the receipt."

I'm not ashamed of what happened then, though I wouldn't have allowed witnesses a year ago. I sat down on my own marker stone, in front of my own people, on my own secret strip, and I cried like the sixteen-year-old they'd flown away never got to.

Nobody made it strange. The sisters sat on either side of me. Ambo kept talking, low, about the storehouse and the bell and the night the boats came, filling in the map of it piece by piece, and the boy leaned against my shoulder, and the river ran past below the bend saying *keep moving, keep moving,* and for the first time in twenty years I answered it out loud:

"Not yet."

* * *

We buried what needed burying. There were letters that were prayers, and those went into the ground at the head of the strip with a stone over them, and the sisters sang something old in a language the empire had tried to tax out of existence, and the boy helped me fill the earth, and I took the photographs Wren wanted, including one of my terrible spelling, which she would never let me hear the end of.

Then Ambo walked me to the edge of the strip and pointed up-channel, past the first bend, to where a thread of woodsmoke rose thin and blue through the kapok crowns.

"There's a settlement up there," he said. "Nine families. Children. They've got no strip, no radio, no medicine past what Ferro packs in tins. Every wet season the river decides whether they eat." He looked at me sideways, sly as a boy himself. "A man with a plane and a cleared shelf of high ground could do worse than learn the way."

"I know the way," I said. "I cut the way."

"I know," Ambo said. "Who do you think sold the mule?"

I laughed until my ribs hurt, there at the end of the strip, and then I shook his hand the way you shake the hand of a man who has been waiting twenty years for you to knock on a door he never locked.

Loading up, I found the boy standing at the ladder, holding something out. A flat stone, small, with letters cut shallow and crooked in a child's hand. I turned it over. My birth name. His spelling was worse than mine had been.

"So you don't lose it again," he said.

I put it in my shirt pocket, over the heart, where pilots keep the things they can't afford to jettison, and I promised him I'd be back before the rains, and this time the promise didn't taste like a lie, because promises made on ground you've admitted is home have a different weight.

Takeoff was long and slow off the soft clay, the kapok branches combing the antenna, and then the ground fell away and the drowned country unrolled north and south and every direction, honest in the low water, all its secrets showing.

I keyed the radio once, on the frequency only four people in the world listened to.

"Lockridge. Cargo delivered. Strip holds." A breath. "Coming home."

Static, then Wren, crackling and delighted: *"Copy, custodian. Ines says fly the east channel. Also she says you're late and also she's smiling, I'm just reporting."*

I banked south, into the evening light, the little stone riding warm against my chest, and set course for the river town—for the woman drawing the map, the girl machining the future, the crew that loved me, the season turning over like a page.

Behind me the strip stayed where I'd put it, cut by a boy, landed on by a man, waiting now for the next flight.

Ahead, the horizon did what horizons do.

For once, I followed it instead of running from it—and it felt, at last, like going somewhere rather than leaving everywhere.
