## 37. Dry Season

The river told the truth in October.

That was what Ferro said, anyway, standing on the mud stairs of the landing with his arms crossed and his cook's apron already stained with the day's first argument. *The river lies all wet season*, he said. *Comes down to its bones and tells you everything.* Wren had heard him say it three times now, once to Bo, once to nobody, once to the river itself, which was the kind of thing you could do in this town without anyone locking you up.

She stood beside him on the stairs and looked at what the water had given back.

Sandbars everywhere, pale as peeled fruit. The skeleton of a barge that had gone down before she was born, ribs showing like something that had died mid-run. And downstream, past the bend where the current went quiet, the tops of the drowned outpost's foundations breaking the surface for the first time in living memory — gray stone shoulders above brown water, patient as old men waiting for a ferry that was never coming.

People came down to look at them every evening now. They didn't talk much when they did. They just stood on the mud stairs or out on the sandbar with their shoes off, looking at the stones, and then they walked home in the dark, and Wren had started to understand that this was its own kind of meeting. The kind where nothing got decided except that everyone knew.

The town had been arguing for eleven days.

Wren kept count because arguing was the town's second-favorite weather, after rain, and this season it hadn't stopped. It happened mostly at Ferro's kitchen table, which was the biggest table in the settlement and therefore the government, the church, the court, and the theater, depending on the hour. It started the morning after the names were read aloud — all two hundred and six of them, Ines's voice going hoarse by the fourth page, Coral standing at the edge of the crowd with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral he'd been late to for twenty years — and it had not stopped since.

The argument was about the bell.

* * *

"It belongs in the square," said Marta Osei, who ran the fuel depot and half the gossip and all of the opinions worth having. She said it the way she said most things, as though anyone who disagreed was welcome to try. "In the open. Where people pass it every day. A bell in a museum is a dead bell."

"A bell in the square is a bell nobody rings," said old Sefu, who was ninety-one and had been a boy on the upriver plantations when the outpost still had a name on the maps. He sat at the head of Ferro's table because nobody had ever been brave enough to tell him not to. "You put it where people live, they'll get used to it inside a month. It'll be a coat rack. You want it rung, you put it where people have to walk to it."

"Where, then? The mission station?" Marta snorted. "Half fallen down."

"So we build it up again."

"That's a year's work."

"We've waited eighty years," Sefu said. "We can wait one more."

Wren sat near the door with her knees pulled up on a crate, doing what she did best in these meetings, which was listening while appearing not to. She'd gotten good at it over the years — Coral had taught her without meaning to, the way he listened to engines, hearing the note that was wrong underneath the note that was loud. Right now the wrong note under the argument was simple: everybody wanted the same thing. Everybody wanted the names remembered somewhere real. They were only fighting about the address.

Bo wasn't fighting about anything. Bo sat at the far end of the table with a mug of tea he never drank, turning it slowly, and Wren watched him the way you watch weather. Three days ago he'd cornered her by the hangar and asked her, in his flat mechanic's way, whether she thought the captain was sleeping. She'd said no. Bo had nodded and said, *Good. Because I've seen him sleep, and it looks like work.* Then he'd gone back to rebuilding the number-two cylinder, which was his way of saying a prayer.

Tomas was there too, sitting quiet, his left hand still stiff from the wreck dive. He didn't argue either. He'd done his arguing underwater, he said, and lost.

It was Ines who broke the deadlock, the way she always did — not by being louder but by being more precise.

"The families should decide," she said. "Not the town. Not us." She had her grandfather's survey rolled open on the table between the teacups, weighted with wrenches and a tin of sugar, and she smoothed it flat with both hands. "There are forty-one descendant households on this list. Forty-one. We know because I spent four days knocking on doors and getting tea I didn't want. Ask them. All of them. One question: where does the bell go?"

"And if they disagree with each other?" Marta asked.

"Then they disagree," Ines said. "Out loud, where everyone can hear it. That's the whole point of what we're doing, isn't it?"

Nobody had an answer to that, so Sefu laughed, a dry rattle like a propeller winding down, and said the cartographer girl talked too much sense for her own safety, and the meeting dissolved into the usual chaos of chairs scraping and someone accusing someone else of borrowing tools, and Wren slipped out the door into the blue evening before anyone could give her a job.

* * *

Coral was at the plane.

He was almost always at the plane these days, or at least within sight of it, the way some men stay near a sick relative. The Cessna sat on the strip with its cowling off and its tail tied down against the coming winds, and Coral lay half under the fuselage with a rag in one hand, doing nothing, which Wren recognized as his version of thinking.

She sat down on the wheel chock and waited. That was the other thing he'd taught her: with Coral, silence worked faster than questions.

After a while he slid out, sat up against the tire, and looked at her. His face had changed since the reading of the names. Not older exactly — worn, the way a tool wears, down to the bright metal underneath. There was a version of him she'd grown up with, the man who answered every question with a job, and that man was mostly gone now. What was left answered things.

"You were at the meeting," he said.

"Somebody has to keep count of how many times Marta threatens to run for council."

"Eleven days, eleven threats. She's slowing down." He wiped his hands, though they weren't dirty. "What do you think? About the bell."

"I think Sefu's right," Wren said. "And I think Marta's right. And I think that's why it's taking forever."

Coral huffed something that was almost a laugh. Above them the sky was doing its dry-season trick, going from copper to violet in the space of a cigarette's length, the stars arriving early and shameless. The air smelled like dust and woodsmoke and the green rot smell of the exposed riverbed, which was the smell of the world telling the truth.

"There's something I haven't told you," he said.

Wren's stomach did the drop it always did when he started a sentence that way. But his voice wasn't heavy. It was careful, the way it was careful when he handed her something breakable.

"When I was sixteen," he said, "I cut a strip into the ridge above the north channel. Cleared it myself, two seasons of Sundays. Nobody knows it's there. I never filed it, never marked it, never landed anybody on it but me." He turned the rag over in his hands. "I built it because I needed one place in the world that was mine. That nobody could take away or find. And I've been thinking—" He stopped. Started again. "I've been thinking maybe that was the wrong lesson. Maybe a place isn't yours until you give it away."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. "You want to land the bell there."

"I want to ask the families if they want it there. High ground. Dry all year. Room for stone, room for people, room for whatever comes after us." He shrugged, and the shrug was the oldest thing about him, twenty years of miles compressed into one small motion. "It's a suggestion. Ines will probably make a map of it. Maps make things official."

"Coral." Wren leaned forward, elbows on her knees. "You're giving away your secret place."

"I'm giving away a lot of things this season," he said. "Might as well be thorough."

She wanted to say something big back to him — something worthy of it — but what came out was smaller and truer: "You'll need help clearing the approach. The south wind comes over that ridge sideways. I've seen the trees lean."

Something moved through his face then, quick as a fish under water, and she realized she'd surprised him — that he'd expected her to argue, or cry, or ask why, and instead she'd given him a wind report. That was love in their family, she supposed. Wind reports. Jobs instead of answers. Showing up with the right tool.

"Sunday," he said. "Bring gloves."

* * *

The work took over the town the way floodwater takes over a field — completely, and without anyone quite agreeing to it.

By the middle of the week there were three separate work parties and a dispute about lumber that Marta resolved by simply buying the lumber herself and daring anyone to object. Bo commandeered the mission station's fallen roof beams, tested each one by ear, and rejected nine of ten with the word *rot* delivered like a diagnosis. Tomas, whose shoulder still wouldn't carry a beam, organized the younger kids into a chain gang hauling stone from the sandbar, and paid them in Ferro's cinnamon buns, which Wren privately considered the strongest currency in the territory.

Wren worked. That was the strange gift of those weeks — grief and labor braided together so tight you couldn't always tell which one your hands were doing. She hauled and hammered and dug post holes in ground that hadn't seen sun in eighty years, ground that came up black and rich and smelling of everything that had ever settled into it. Sometimes she found things. A hinge. A doll's arm, porcelain, small enough to cup in her palm. A coin with a face rubbed blank. Each time, she carried it to Ines, who catalogued it in a ledger of her own — a new ledger, clean pages, the opposite kind from the one in the chest — and each time Ines wrote the date and the object and the place found, and looked at Wren with an expression Wren couldn't name yet but was beginning to suspect was the future.

The children's graves at Sable Bend had changed something in the town's argument, Wren thought. Before, people fought about the bell like it was furniture. After the story of the little cemetery spread — thirty-one graves, unmarked, that Wren herself had found and photographed and refused to let go quiet — nobody called it furniture anymore. Even Marta went quiet for a full day, which Bo said was a medical event.

On Saturday evening, Wren climbed the ridge road alone to look at the site.

The strip was better than she'd expected. Twenty years of jungle regrowth had narrowed it, but the bones were there — the long scar of level ground, Coral's old clearing work still visible in the way the trees grew around a absence. From the top of the ridge you could see the whole river country laid out below: the town's tin roofs catching the last light, the sandbars pale and honest, the drowned foundations showing their shoulders, and beyond all of it the haze-blue line where the maps gave up and the horizon started.

She understood him suddenly, standing there. Understood the boy who'd come up here with a machete and a grudge against the whole world and carved himself a runway pointing at the exit. Every strip he'd ever cut pointed away. This one pointed away too — but from up here, looking down at everything he'd spent twenty years flying over and never landing in, she could see the other thing it pointed at.

You could aim a runway at home if you flew it backward.

* * *

The answers came back from the forty-one households over eight days, carried by boat, by foot, by one very crowded mail flight, and finally by Wren herself, who volunteered for the upriver loop partly out of duty and partly because she wanted to see the faces when people said it out loud.

They said it out loud. That was the thing that undid her, door after door. Old women who'd been girls when the soldiers came, men who'd grown up on stories their mothers told in whispers — they held the question paper in their hands and read it twice, and then they spoke the word *bell* like it was a person's name, carefully, with the weight you give a name you haven't been allowed to say.

Thirty-three votes for the ridge. Five for the town square. Three abstained, one of them, Wren suspected, just to keep the argument alive, because a town without an argument dries up worse than the river.

When she brought the tally back to Ferro's table, she set it down in the middle like a verdict, and Marta read it, and everyone braced for the appeal. Instead Marta was quiet a moment, then said, "Ridge it is. But we ring it in the square first. Once. On the day it leaves. So the town hears it go." And Sefu, who had argued for the mission station and lost, nodded slowly and said, "A bell should be heard leaving and heard arriving. Everything else is just carrying." And that was that — eleven days of war ended by two sentences, which was, Wren reflected, roughly how all wars actually ended, when they ended well.

That night the town did something it hadn't done in years. Ferro cooked for everyone, out of doors, over three fires, and people brought chairs and blankets down to the sandbar, and somebody had salvaged a gramophone from the barge skeleton and got it playing something scratchy and slow, and the whole settlement sat under the early stars facing the river like an audience facing a stage.

The river ran low and silver. The foundations stood in it, black against the shine. Somebody had floated lanterns out on the current earlier — Wren saw them still, a loose constellation drifting down toward the bend, toward the stones, small fires going to meet the dark water.

Coral sat on the sand with his back against a drift log, and Ines sat beside him with her shoulder touching his, and neither of them pretended it was an accident anymore. Wren watched them from a distance, from her blanket with Bo and Tomas and Ferro, and felt the specific ache of seeing a thing become true that you'd been rooting for so long it felt like yours.

"He's different," Bo said quietly, beside her. Not a question.

"Yeah," Wren said.

"Better?"

Wren thought about it. Watched Coral laugh at something Ines said — actually laugh, head back, a sound she could count the occurrences of per year on one hand.

"Worse at hiding," she said finally. "Which is the same thing."

Bo made a sound that in Bo-language was a full speech, and passed her the last cinnamon bun, which in any language was love.

* * *

Later, when the fires had burned to orange cores and the gramophone had given up and most of the town had drifted home, Wren walked the sandbar alone, the way she did sometimes, checking the world.

The lanterns had all gone out or gone around the bend. The river whispered against the sand in its low-water voice, honest, unhurried. Out at the edge of the shallows the foundation stones rose dripping, and she stopped near them, and stood, and let the night be as big as it wanted.

Two hundred and six names. Thirty-one small graves. One bell, crated in the mission station, waiting. One secret place on a ridge, about to stop being secret. One family — hers, theirs, however you drew the lines now, and the lines had gone soft and generous lately — one family rebuilding itself out of salvage, which was, she supposed, the only material anyone had ever really had.

Her camera hung at her hip, the salvaged one, the one that had carried the truth in its belly down a storm front and survived a sandbar. She raised it and took one photograph of the stones in the starlight, not for evidence this time. Just because it was beautiful, and beauty deserved a record too.

Behind her, footsteps on the sand. She turned, expecting Coral.

It was Ines, wrapped in a blanket, hair loose, holding something flat and rectangular against her chest. She stopped beside Wren and looked at the stones a moment before speaking.

"The survey's at the printer's," she said. "My grandfather's. With the families' names in the margin, where they should have been all along." She shifted the thing she was carrying, and moonlight caught it — a long wooden case, old brass fittings, the kind of case that travels with instruments. "And this came up on the mail flight today. From the foundry archive. I wrote to them three weeks ago."

"What is it?"

Ines knelt and set the case on the sand between them, and opened it, and inside, nested in rotted velvet, lay a bell clapper. Heavy, hand-forged, stamped with the same mark that was on the bell itself — her grandfather's mark, the Vale mark, the one that meant testimony.

"They kept it separate," Ines said softly. "Eighty years ago, somebody at the foundry took the clapper out before shipping the bell, and kept it, and wrote on the box: *until it can be rung honestly.*" She looked up at Wren, and her eyes were shining and fierce at once. "It can be rung honestly now. Sunday. At dawn. Before we carry it up the ridge."

Wren's heart was pounding, though she couldn't have said at what — the bell, or the dawn, or the way Ines was looking at her like she was handing her something, which she was.

"I want you to do it," Ines said. "Ring it first. You're the one who wouldn't let it stay quiet."

Downriver, the honest river ran on over its secrets, and the first light was still hours away, and Wren reached out and touched the cold iron of the clapper, and felt the whole next morning gathering itself around that single point of contact like a storm deciding where to strike.
