## 38. The Bell Rung

They hung the bell from a frame of greenheart timber on the third morning of the dry season, when the river had dropped enough to show its bones and the town had run out of arguments and started, haltingly, to build.

Ines watched from the edge of the square as Bo and Tomas raised it with block and tackle, the two of them sweating through their shirts, Ferro calling measurements from below in the voice he used for everything—orders, jokes, prayers. The bell came up slowly, swinging in its slings, brown with a century of river silt except where they'd scrubbed it clean. The foundry mark caught the light. *Vale & Sons.* Her grandfather's hands had poured this metal, or supervised the pouring, or at least signed his name to it, and now his name hung above a river town that had spent a hundred years pretending not to remember him.

She had not slept much. Nobody had. But there was a difference between her sleeplessness and Coral's, she thought. Hers was the sleeplessness of a woman about to finish something. His was the sleeplessness of a man about to stand in a place he had spent twenty years flying away from.

The list was pinned to the mission station's old door, which Wren had salvaged from Sable Bend and hauled home on her own insistence, riding upriver in the plane's cargo bay with the door lashed between fuel drums like a coffin being returned for burial. On it, in Ines's careful cartographer's hand, were one hundred and twelve names. The removed families. The dead of the outpost. The children smuggled out in the bottoms of boats and the bellies of aircraft, renamed before they could object.

Coral's birth name was ninth from the bottom. She had written it herself, and she had asked him first—not because she needed permission, but because she wanted him to know it would be there, in ink, in public, and that no one would take it back.

He had looked at it for a long time. Then he'd said, "You spelled it right," which was as close as he came, some days, to weeping.

* * *

By midmorning the square was fuller than Ines had ever seen it. Word had traveled the way word always traveled here—by radio, by rumor, by the gossip economy that ran on Coral Underhill's flights the way the town ran on diesel—and families had come down from the headwaters and across from the far bank in boats of every vintage. Old women in their good dresses. Men who'd flown with Coral for years and never known what he carried. Children too young to understand, chasing each other between the legs of adults who stood very still.

Wren moved through the crowd with the camera case strapped to her chest, taking photographs with the seriousness of a court recorder. She was fourteen now by anyone's count and older than that by her own, and she had appointed herself the expedition's historian without asking anyone's leave. Ines watched her crouch to photograph an old woman's hands, then the woman's face, then the woman's granddaughter standing beside her—the same hands, one generation downstream.

"Senhora Vale." The junior officer appeared at her elbow—Reyes, still in his ministry khaki though he'd stopped wearing the tie, looking like a man who had jumped and was waiting to find out how deep the water was. "The manifest copies arrived in the capital yesterday. My director wants to know who authorized the reading."

"What did you tell him?"

"That the document fell into the river." Reyes almost smiled. "Along with my career, probably. Though—" He glanced at the bell, the crowd, the list on the door. "It's hard to bury a hundred and twelve names once they've been read aloud in a town square. Paper is quiet. People aren't."

"No," Ines said. "People aren't."

He nodded, excused himself, and went to stand at the edge of things, which Ines suspected was where he would live for a while—a man between governments, belonging nowhere yet, which was its own kind of honesty.

* * *

Coral came to find her an hour before the reading, when the heat had settled in and the crowd had grown quiet in the way crowds do before something irreversible.

He was dressed differently. She noticed it before she understood it: no flight jacket, no cap, none of the uniform of motion he wore like armor. Just a plain shirt, sleeves rolled, and his hair combed, which she had maybe seen twice in all the months she'd known him. He looked younger and older at once. He looked like a man who had landed.

"They want me to fly over," he said. "Some of the old families. Drop flowers from the plane, the way you do for the drowned." He said it lightly, but she heard the weight under it. "Tomas offered to take the controls."

"And?"

"And I told them I'd be on the ground." He turned the words over like stones, testing each one. "Twenty years I've been above this place. Looking down. It's easy to love something from eight hundred feet. You don't have to smell it. You don't have to be seen by it." He looked at her. "I think maybe I've been flying away so long I forgot you can also arrive."

Ines reached up and straightened his collar, an excuse to touch him, though she didn't need an excuse anymore and hadn't for weeks. "Your name is ninth from the bottom," she said. "When they read it, everyone will know who you are. Not Coral Underhill. The other one."

"I know."

"The boy from the outpost."

"I know, Ines."

"And you're staying on the ground anyway."

He smiled then, small and crooked, the smile she had fallen for half a map ago. "I'm staying on the ground," he said. "Turns out I've got family here. Never met most of them. Figure it's time."

* * *

The reading began at four o'clock, when the shadows went long and the heat broke, because the elders had decided that names should be spoken in the cool of the day, the hour when the living come home.

An old woman named Marta Ochoa read first—she was ninety-one, the oldest surviving daughter of the outpost, evacuated as a child on the last boat before the soldiers came, and she stood at the mission door with the list in hands that shook until she began, and then did not shake at all.

She read the names slowly. That was the agreement: slowly, so each one could land. Between every tenth name she paused, and the crowd answered with a single low tone, a hum that wasn't quite singing and wasn't quite silence, a sound Ines had never heard before and understood immediately—the sound of a community holding its grief together instead of letting each person carry it alone.

Ines stood near the front with Wren on one side and Coral on the other. She had expected him to be rigid. Instead he was very still, the stillness of deep water, and when the names of the removed families came—the Vales among them, her grandfather's survey party listed not as casualties but as witnesses, taken for testimony and erased—he closed his eyes.

Her grandfather's name came. *Elias Vale, surveyor.* Spoken aloud in a river town he had vanished from a century ago, and Ines felt it go through her like current through water, and Wren's hand found hers without either of them deciding it.

Then the survivors' children. The smuggled ones. The renamed ones.

Marta Ochoa read the ninth name from the bottom.

And Coral Underhill stepped forward.

Not far. One step, out of the line, into the space between the reader and the crowd, and he said, in a clear voice that carried to the far bank, "Here."

One word. The old answering-word, the one the elders had explained to them that morning—the word the outpost people had used at muster, at prayer, at the naming of children. *Present. Accounted for. Alive.*

A sound went through the crowd like wind through cane. An old man near the front began to weep without any noise at all. And somewhere in the middle distance, Ines saw, a woman in her seventies was staring at Coral with an expression Ines would spend years learning to read—the expression of someone recognizing a face they had last seen as a child's face, forty years gone, walking out of a burning world and into a new name.

His cousin. One of the few. She did not come forward—not yet; that reckoning would take longer than an afternoon—but she pressed her hand flat over her heart, and Coral saw it, and pressed his own the same way, and that was enough for today. Today was for the names.

Marta Ochoa continued. The last name was read. The hum rose one final time, held, and faded into the river sounds—the water, the birds, the ordinary blessed noise of a world that had finally stopped pretending.

* * *

They rang the bell at dusk.

That had been Wren's idea, argued for with the full force of her fourteen-year-old certainty: one ring for each name, struck by descendants, the rope passed hand to hand down a line of families until every name had its note. Bo rigged the rope. Ferro cooked for two hundred. Tomas, whose shoulder still ached from the wreck dive, took the first turn because he insisted the river owed him a debt and he meant to pay it in bronze.

The first strike rolled out over the water and kept going, out across the flats and the drowned foundations and the far dark hills, and Ines felt it in her sternum, in her teeth, in the soles of her feet. A century of silence ending in a single note.

The rope went down the line. Strike after strike after strike. Some rings were steady, some cracked, one boy pulled so hard the bell swung wild and the note wavered like a living thing, and nobody minded, because the dead do not require perfect pitch. They require remembering.

Coral did not ring it. He stood in the crowd, among the descendants, ground-level, dust on his boots, and when his name's turn came he did not step to the rope. The cousin rang it for him—one clean note—and he bowed his head to it like a man receiving something long owed.

Ines watched him from a few feet away and understood, finally, completely, what she had been circling since the mail flight, since the half-map, since the night radio and the storm and the sandbar: she had fallen in love with a man in flight, and the man had chosen to land. Not for her. Not only for her. For the boy ninth from the bottom, who had waited twenty years for someone to say *here* on his behalf, and who had finally said it himself.

Afterward, when the bell was done and the lamps were lit and Ferro's food was going around, Coral found her at the edge of the square. Behind him the crowd murmured, the river glittered, and the bell hung silent and full in the lamplight, finished with its work for tonight.

"One hundred and twelve," he said. "I counted."

"You always count."

"Not anymore." He looked out at the water, at the dark line of the horizon where the drowned outpost lay sleeping under the dropping river. "Starting tomorrow, I'm counting years."

Ines took his hand. Down the square, Wren was showing her photographs to Marta Ochoa, and the old woman was laughing and crying at once, pointing at faces, naming names, adding to the record. There was work ahead—maps to finish, a survey to publish, a memorial to build, a government to outlast. But that was tomorrow's cargo.

Tonight the bell had said everything, and the river had carried it, and for the first time in either of their lives, neither of them wanted to be anywhere else.

"Come on," Coral said quietly. "There's someone I want you to meet. My cousin. She's been asking about the woman who made me tell the truth."

Ines laughed, and let him lead her into the lamplight, toward the family she was only beginning to learn she already had.
