## 39. New Coordinates

The dry season had settled over the river country like an old man settling into a chair he'd earned—slowly, creaking, entirely without apology. The water ran low and glass-clear in the mornings, and where the floods of spring had hidden everything, now the river showed its bones: sandbars like the vertebrae of some enormous patient animal, the black stumps of drowned forest, and there, at Sable Bend, the pale geometry of foundations rising out of the shallows like a city remembering itself.

Coral stood on the mission station's old parade ground—it wasn't a parade ground anymore, it was a survey bench—with a plane table in front of him and Ines Vale's hands moving over it, sure and quick as a surgeon's.

"Hold that," she said, not looking up. "If you breathe on it again I'm going to put you on cook duty."

"I've been threatened by better."

"No, you haven't." She glanced up, and the morning light caught the ink smudge on her cheekbone, and he felt the old familiar turn in his chest—the one that used to feel like trouble and now just felt like home. "There. Alidade's level. Read me the bearing to the bell tower."

Coral lifted his eyes from the sight vane to the bell tower, which was no longer a tower at all but a brick stump standing hip-deep in the river, the bell itself already crated on the bank for the trip down to the town that would hang it. He read off the bearing. She wrote it down in her small, exact hand.

They'd been at this for six days.

The idea had been hers, though she'd framed it as if it were obvious, as if it had been sitting in the air since the night the names were read aloud and nobody had come to make them stop. *Nobody has ever surveyed this site properly,* she'd said, spreading her grandfather's half-map across Ferro's galley table while the crew ate around it, unbothered, as if maps were condiments. *Half of what your government men wanted buried was the location itself. So we put it back. Every foundation, every grave, every coordinate—in the record, under our names, where no one can ever lose it again.*

Bo had grunted. Tomas had said something about diving conditions being excellent in dead-low water. Wren had already been clearing plates to make room for the drawing instruments before anyone voted on anything.

And Coral—who had spent twenty years making sure this place existed on no map at all—had heard himself say, "I know where the well is. It's under the fig tree. Or the fig tree is under the well. One of those."

Ines had looked at him then, a long look, the kind that used to make him want to be airborne within the hour. Now it just made him stay.

* * *

The work was slow and it was good. That was the thing about surveying that Coral hadn't expected—that it was a form of attention, and attention, it turned out, was a form of grief, and grief, done in company, was a form of love.

They worked the site in strips, north to south, the way her grandfather's field notes said he had. Ines ran the plane table; Coral walked the ground with a ranging pole, calling out features; Wren kept the log book with a seriousness that would have frightened a customs officer. Bo had rigged a line-and-sounding arrangement off the boat so they could plot the submerged foundations in the deeper channel, and Tomas spent half of each day in the water with a weighted tape, surfacing to shout numbers through the spray.

"Foundation wall!" he called on the third morning, treading water beside a dark shape the low water had finally uncovered. "Limestone. It runs—Coral, it runs right up onto the bank. It's continuous."

"That'll be the hospital," Coral said, and heard his own voice go flat and careful.

Ines looked at him. She didn't say anything. She wrote *hospital (probable)* in the margin, and later, when Wren was down at the boat, she came and stood next to him among the fig roots and said quietly, "You don't have to narrate it, you know. You can just stand in it."

"I don't know how to do that yet."

"I know." She took his hand. Her palm was ink-stained and rope-roughened and it fit his like it had been surveyed for the purpose. "That's why I'm here."

They found the well on the fourth day, exactly where he'd said—under the fig tree, or the other way around—and they found the schoolhouse foundations with their little rows of post-holes, and they found the graves at the edge of the compound, the ones Wren had discovered months ago, and Ines plotted each one individually, each with its own coordinate, because, she said, "a mass grave is a lie told by arithmetic. They were people. People get coordinates."

On the fifth day she plotted the spot where the chest had surfaced, twenty years after the flood first coughed it toward the light, and Coral watched her draw the small square symbol and label it, and felt something in his chest unlock that he hadn't known was locked.

*Here,* the map said. *This happened here. Here is where here is.*

* * *

The ministry man came on the sixth day.

Coral saw the boat first—a government launch, white hull, too clean for the river—and felt the old reflex fire, the one that had him calculating wind and load and escape headings before he'd finished his coffee. Twenty years of miles had built that reflex plank by plank. He set down his cup and started walking toward the landing, and only realized halfway there that Ines was walking beside him, and Wren behind them both, and that Bo had appeared at the woodpile holding an axe in a way that was technically casual.

The launch carried two men. The older one Coral knew: Senior Surveyor Aldous Crane, who had signed the confiscation order in the spring with an expression like a man signing for registered mail. The younger one was Reyes.

Reyes looked different. Out of uniform, or nearly—field clothes, a canvas satchel, sunburn. He came up the bank first and stopped in front of Coral, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the river filled the silence the way it always did, patiently, with its own business.

"Mr. Lockridge," Reyes said. "Or—" he hesitated, delicately, "—whichever name you prefer."

"Coral's fine. It's the name I grew into."

Crane came ashore last, carrying a document case, and Coral braced for the paper, the seal, the machinery of silence grinding forward one more time. But Crane opened the case and took out—not an order. A letter. He held it out awkwardly, like a man returning something borrowed long ago.

"The ministry has reviewed the file," Crane said. His voice had the flatness of a man reading words he didn't believe in. "In light of... recent developments. Public interest. The centenary committee's position." He cleared his throat. "The site is to be entered into the national register as a historical monument. Compensation claims from descendant families are to be processed. There will be an inquiry." A pause. "A real one. With witnesses."

"And the file?" Ines asked.

"Closed," Crane said, and then, visibly forcing himself: "Closed means closed. Not buried. There's a difference. I'm given to understand—" and here his eyes flicked to Reyes, and something passed between them that Coral couldn't read and didn't need to, "—that certain copies exist which would make any further burial efforts both futile and embarrassing."

"Futile and embarrassing," Bo repeated from the woodpile, with deep satisfaction.

Crane ignored her. He turned to Ines. "Your publication, Miss Vale. The survey. The ministry requests—requests—the opportunity to print it as the official record."

"You can print it," Ines said, "exactly as I drew it. All of it. Including the graves, including the names, including the note about the removal order. If one line changes, you print nothing, because I'll publish it myself in three newspapers before your printers warm up."

Something that might have been respect moved briefly across Crane's face, like a fish under ice. "That was my understanding of the terms," he said.

He left the letter on the survey table, weighted with a stone, and he and his launch went back downriver, and only when the sound of the engine faded did Coral let out a breath he felt like he'd been holding since spring.

Reyes lingered. He stood looking at the plane table, at the map taking shape there—the outpost rendered in ink, whole, located, undeniable—and his jaw worked once.

"My grandmother," he said, not turning around, "came from upriver. From one of the villages that took people in, afterward. She never said from what." He picked up a pencil, set it down again. "When I copied that manifest, I wasn't thinking about careers. I was thinking I might find her family's name."

"Did you?" Wren asked, from the log book, blunt as a hammer.

"Not on the removed list." Reyes smiled, and it was a tired, real smile. "On the survivors' list. Under a different spelling. Names change when people run. I understand that better now than I did." He nodded to Coral—to all of them—and walked down to the launch, which waited, and took him away downriver toward whatever his own reckoning would look like.

Coral watched the wake flatten against the bank.

"He'll be at the inquiry," Ines said. "As a witness. You watch."

"I don't have to watch," Coral said. "I'm going to be one."

* * *

They finished the map on the ninth day.

It happened the way the best things in Coral's life had happened—without ceremony, in bad light, with everyone slightly sunburned. Ines made the last fix on the bell tower stump, checked it twice against her grandfather's half-map, and then laid the old man's fragile paper alongside her new sheet, and the two halves of the terrain met along a fold-line like a scar closing.

"It matches," Wren said softly. "His half and yours. It matches exactly."

"He was good," Ines said. Her voice caught on it. "He was so good, and they made his work into a weapon and then erased him, and the whole time the truth was just—sitting here. Waiting for someone to hold a ruler over it."

"Not waiting," Coral said. "The dead don't wait. That's the living's job."

She looked at him then, and he saw her decide not to argue, and he loved her for it, and he loved her grandfather too, this stranger whose blood ran in her hands, whose foundry mark was cast into a bell that had spoken forty-one names over the water three nights ago.

They titled the sheet together, kneeling over it in the last light. Ines wrote the formal designation, the coordinates, the datum, her own name as surveyor. Then she held the pen out to him.

"Assistant surveyor signs too," she said. "Standard practice."

"I'm not a surveyor."

"You cut an airstrip into a ridge above this valley twenty years ago and never told a soul. You've been keeping this site's secret coordinates longer than I've been alive. As far as I'm concerned you're the senior cartographer of the invisible." She pressed the pen into his hand. "Sign it."

Coral looked at the blank line for a long moment. Then he wrote his name—*Coral Lockridge*—and beneath it, smaller, in letters he'd last written as a boy on a slate in a schoolhouse whose foundations lay thirty feet away under the fig tree, he wrote the name he'd been born with.

Ines read it. She didn't say anything. She reached over and blotted it carefully, the way you handle something wet and living, and then she rolled the map, and it was done.

The outpost was on the map. In the record. In the open.

Nothing could drown it twice.

* * *

That evening they flew the sheet home.

Coral had offered the boat, but Ines had looked at the sky—high, clear, the dry-season blue that pilots pray to—and said, "You should see it from above. Your grandfather never got to," and that settled it. They loaded the plane at first light: the rolled map in its tube lashed into the cabin, the crate with the bell strapped amidships where Bo had calculated it would sit best, Wren in the right seat with the log book on her knees and her chin up in a way that made Coral's throat tight.

He ran up the strip he'd cut himself, twenty years ago, on a ridge above the valley—the strip no one had known about until last month, the strip that was going to be on the next edition of the charts under its proper name, because Wren had already decided the name and had not consulted him. He took off into the clean morning air and banked south, and below him the river unspooled, low and honest, silver in its channel, showing its bones.

"There," Wren said, pointing.

Sable Bend slid past beneath the wing: the pale foundations, the fig tree, the stump of the bell tower, the graves at the edge—all of it legible now, all of it *located*, a wound that had finally been given its true coordinates instead of being left to bleed invisibly into the current. Coral held the heading a moment longer than the flight plan required. Ines, in the seat behind him, leaned forward and put her hand on his shoulder, and didn't say anything, because she'd learned that about him: when to fill the silence and when to let the river carry it.

Then he turned for home, and the bend fell astern, and ahead the smoke haze of the far horizon glowed gold where the sun was climbing, and Wren opened the log book and began, in her fierce careful hand, to write down the day.

"Read me the time," Coral said. "We'll log it properly. Everything logs properly now."

Wren checked her watch and gave him the minute, and he wrote it in the corner of his kneeboard, and thought: *there. That's the minute it stopped being a secret.* Somewhere in the future, some child doing homework, some old woman claiming compensation, some pilot studying her charts, would find these coordinates and know that people had lived here, and been taken from here, and come home here. That was all the monument anyone needed. The rest was bells and stone.

The town appeared ahead, tin roofs catching the light, the riverfront already crowded—it seemed half the district had heard the plane coming, or maybe they just gathered now, these days, whenever his wheels touched earth, as if landings had become occasions. He could pick out figures on the dock. Bo's red kerchief. Ferro waving a spoon at nothing. And at the end of the dock, apart from the others, a woman in a traveling coat with a leather case at her feet, standing very still, watching them come in with the particular patience of someone who has traveled a very long way to ask a question.

Coral felt Ines go still behind him.

"That's—" she began.

"I see her," Coral said.

He brought the plane in low over the water, gentle as he knew how, and began to wonder who else, in this new open world where nothing stayed buried, had finally learned the coordinates.
