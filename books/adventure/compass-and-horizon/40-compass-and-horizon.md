## 40. Compass and Horizon

The new plane smells of cedar and fresh dope, of linen stretched tight over her wings, and underneath it all the faint chemical sweetness of paint that hasn't yet cured. Coral runs his palm along her cowling the way he used to check a horse's flank, and she is warm already, the morning sun doing its slow work on the metal, warming her the way it warms everything in this country that sits still long enough to be warmed.

He has named her *Bellwether*, which Bo says is a ridiculous name for an aircraft and Ines says is exactly right, and which Wren has already painted in small careful letters under the port-side cockpit rail where only a person kneeling on the grass would ever see it. That was the condition. Wren got the name if she kept it small. She kept it small. She has kept every bargain she has ever made with him, which is more than he can say for himself at any point in the last twenty years.

A year. He turns the phrase over the way he turns a coin he doesn't quite trust. A year since the sandbar. A year since the letters lay scattered across the wet sand like a flock of white birds that had come down all at once, and the surveyors stood among them with their hats in their hands, and there was nothing left in the world to hide behind. A year since he stopped counting miles and started counting something else.

He is not good at counting that something else yet. But he is learning.

* * *

Wren is in the front cockpit with her checklist, and she is doing it properly, which still surprises him every time. She is fourteen now, suddenly and without permission, all elbows and certainty, her hair tied back with the same faded red ribbon she has worn since she was nine and he told her it was lucky. She reads each item aloud under her breath — fuel, controls, trim, instruments — and taps each gauge with one finger, and when she gets to the magnetos she looks back over her shoulder at him with an expression that is pure challenge.

"Clear prop," she calls, and her voice cracks in the middle of it, and she glares at the sky as though the sky is responsible, which, in a way, Coral supposes it is.

"Clear," he calls back, and Ines, standing by the hangar door with a rolled chart under her arm, raises one hand in the lazy half-wave of a person who has watched this ritual a hundred mornings now and still hasn't gotten tired of it.

He swings the prop himself. He could have a starter; Bo has offered to fit one three times, and each time Coral has said not yet, and each time Bo has looked at him with the particular patience of a man who has decided to let the old fool keep one ritual. The engine catches on the third pull, that familiar stagger into a run, the whole airframe shivering like something waking from a dream it would rather not have been having. Coral climbs in behind Wren and settles his hands on the dual controls and does not touch them. Not yet. This morning is hers.

"Your airplane," he says.

"My airplane," she says, and taxis her out across the grass with the exaggerated care of a girl steering the most precious thing in the world, which is exactly what she is doing.

The strip is the old one — his strip, the one he cut himself twenty years ago and told no one about, the one that appears on no chart except the one Ines drew, and Ines drew it because he asked her to, in ink, with proper coordinates, with the note *Coral Lockridge, surveyed* in her small precise hand. His name on a map. It still does something strange to his chest, seeing it. For twenty years his name appeared nowhere. He had thought that was safety. It turns out it was just another kind of drowning.

Wren lines up, checks her heading against the strip's axis the way he taught her, and opens the throttle, and *Bellwether* rolls and lifts the way new aircraft do — eagerly, almost offended at the ground — and the river country unrolls beneath them in the early light.

* * *

From the air, a year after the floods, the drowned outpost is a shape in the water that the water has almost finished arguing with. The dry season has been long and hard this year, the river at its lowest in a decade, and where the mission station stood you can see the foundations clearly now, the pale rectangles of the old compound, the line of the bell tower's footing like a broken tooth. The bell itself is gone from there — it lives in the river town now, on a timber frame the families built with their own hands, on ground that used to be a government depot and is now something else. But its shadow is here. Everything's shadow is here.

Wren banks gently, the way he showed her, using the horizon as her reference, and brings them around in a slow circle over the site. Neither of them speaks. This is part of the ritual too, the one they fell into without ever discussing it. Once a month, when the weather allows, they fly out here and look down at the place where he was a child before he was a cargo. Wren looks for the graves. Coral looks for the shape of the kitchen garden, which he can still find, which he will always be able to find, the way you can always find the shape of your own hand in the dark.

"Reyes's memorial stone went in last week," Wren says over the engine noise, pitching her voice the way pilots do. "Ines showed me the photograph. The one with the names on both sides."

"I saw it."

"There were more names on the back than the front."

"There usually are," Coral says. "The front is who died. The back is who it cost."

She considers this with the seriousness she gives everything now, this girl who photographed a forbidden ledger with a salvaged camera at fifteen thousand millimeters of nerve, who hid the film in the tail of a dying aircraft because she had decided, at thirteen, that silence was not going to be her inheritance the way it had been his. He thinks sometimes that she saved the story more than he did. He carried the chest. She carried the proof. Between them, and Ines, and Bo, and Tomas, and Ferro, and a junior officer who let a ledger fall into a river on purpose — between all of them, the dead got their names back.

He looks at the back of Wren's head, at the red ribbon, and he thinks: *I would have flown another twenty years of miles rather than let anything happen to you, and I would have been wrong, and I would never have known it.* That is the arithmetic he is still learning. That keeping a person safe and keeping a person are not the same thing. That he had kept Wren safe for ten years and only began keeping her the day she asked him what he was hiding and he gave her a job instead of an answer, and she took the job, and then, in her own time and her own way, she took the answer too.

"Turn for home?" she asks.

"Turn for home," he says, and the words sit in his mouth like a coin of solid gold.

* * *

Ines is waiting on the strip with the chart spread on the bonnet of Bo's truck, weighted at the corners with four stones she has chosen, he is fairly sure, for their beauty rather than their function. She looks up as they taxi in, and the sun is behind her and the wind is moving her hair off her face, and Coral, who has flown through storms and engine fires and a night passage through rapids that still visits him in his sleep, feels the same small vertigo he has felt every morning for a year. It has not dulled. He has begun to suspect it isn't going to.

Wren shuts the engine down and climbs out and immediately begins telling Ines about her landing, which was, by Coral's professional assessment, very good, and by Wren's own assessment, flawless, and by the honest assessment of the ground crew — meaning Ferro, who wandered over from the hangar with a rag in his hand — *adequate, with room to be humble about it.* Wren informs Ferro that humility is for people with bad landings. Ferro laughs the way he always laughs, like a man who has decided the world is a kitchen and everything in it is eventually food, and Coral leaves them to it.

Ines smooths the chart flat. "New survey's back from the printer," she says. "The full one. The outpost, the mission, the old depot, your strip, all of it. The ministry wanted to review it first." Her mouth does the thing it does — the small dry curve that is not quite a smile and is entirely a victory. "I told them it was already published. Which it was. I published it Tuesday."

"You're going to get us both arrested."

"I'm going to get us both *invited to things*," she says. "It's different. There's better food." She turns the chart so he can see it, and there it is, the drowned outpost with its true name and its true coordinates, the memorial marked, the strip marked, the whole river country rendered honest at last, every secret given its place in the open like a bird set down on a branch to rest. "The centenary committee wants us to fly the families up for the dedication. All of them. It'll take three trips in *Bellwether*."

"Four," Coral says. "You've forgotten the Almeidas. There are nine of them and they all bring more luggage than a family of nine should legally own."

"I never forget the Almeidas," Ines says. "I married into knowing about the Almeidas."

The word goes through him the way it goes through him every time, which is to say: completely, and without any of the damage he once would have expected from a word that size. Married. Four months now. There was no church — there was the river, and Ferro's cooking, and Bo crying in a way he insisted was engine fumes, and Wren holding the ring on a ribbon because Coral had dropped it in the grass the night before and Wren had found it and extracted, in exchange for its return, a solemn promise of front-seat flying lessons in perpetuity. It was, everyone agreed, the best wedding any of them had attended, which was a low bar, since Bo had attended one other and described it as *an ambush with cake.*

"Three trips, then," Ines says. "Four."

"Four," he agrees. "And we'll need to carry water for the old people, and Ferro will insist on coming, and Tomas will dive off the floats at every stop whether there's anything to dive for or not."

"Yes."

"And Bo will find something wrong with the engine that isn't wrong."

"Yes."

"And it'll take a week and we'll come home tired and sunburned and half-deaf from Wren's singing."

Ines looks at him. The wind moves. Somewhere behind them, Wren has begun, at volume, to teach Ferro a song about a mule.

"Coral," she says, "that's not a list of problems. That's a description of the rest of our lives. I've already drawn it on the chart."

He looks down. There, in the margin, in her small precise hand, under the compass rose she always draws by hand because she says printed ones have no soul: a little sketch of an aircraft, and beside it, *us*, and beside that, an arrow pointing at the horizon, and under the arrow: *this way, together, for years.*

* * *

He takes the chart inside and pins it to the hangar wall, next to the photograph of the bell and the framed page of the ledger that Wren insisted on framing — the page with the children's names, the survivors, the ones carried out in the night and renamed and scattered like seed. His birth name is on that page. He has read it so many times now that it has stopped being a stranger. It has become what it always was: a boy's name, a boy who was put on an aircraft by people who loved him and flown away from everything, and who spent twenty years trying to outrun the fact that he had survived.

He doesn't outrun anything anymore. There's nowhere to run that Ines couldn't chart and Wren couldn't fly to.

Bo appears at the hangar door, wiping his hands, wearing the expression he wears when he has inspected something and found it, against all his expectations, good. "She'll be ready for the family runs by Thursday," he says. "New engine mounts, new fabric on the lower wing, and I fixed that fuel gauge that's been lying to you since March."

"It wasn't lying. It was estimating."

"It was *lying*, Coral, the way you used to lie, which is to say plausibly and with eventual catastrophic results."

Coral laughs. It comes easily now. That is the strangest thing of all — not the love, not the family, not even the names read aloud and the bell rung once for each one until his voice gave out and Ines's took over and then Wren's, all three of them passing the names back and forth like a bucket line passing water. The strangest thing is how easy it all became, once he stopped holding it. Twenty years of gripping, and the moment his hands opened, everything he'd been afraid of losing walked over and sat down beside him.

"Thursday," he says. "Four trips. Tell Ferro to start salting things."

"Ferro's already started salting things," Bo says. "Ferro started salting things in March. He says dedication ceremonies are just funerals with better attendance and he intends to be prepared for both." Bo pauses at the door. He looks back, and for a moment the mechanic's armor slips and there is just the man who confronted his captain in a tent a year ago and said *liars get crews killed*, and who has since watched that same captain land a crippled aircraft on a sandbar with everyone alive and the truth spilling out across the sand like spilled light. "It's a good plane," Bo says. "It's a good life. Don't tell anyone I said so."

"I'd never tell anyone anything again," Coral says solemnly. "I'm famously discreet."

Bo throws the rag at him.

* * *

Evening. The strip is gold, then rose, then that deep violet the river country does better than anywhere else in the world, and Wren has gone to the town with Ferro to help hang lanterns for the memorial's first anniversary, and Ines is at the table under the hangar's eave with her charts and her lamp, and Coral stands at the edge of the grass with a cup of coffee going cold in his hand, looking at the horizon.

The horizon. He has spent his whole life measuring himself against it. For twenty years it was the thing he fled toward — always away, always downriver, always the next strip, the next season, the next cargo of medicine and gossip and other people's secrets. The horizon was where his past couldn't follow. He believed that. He built a whole life on that belief, the way you build a house on a sandbar, and the river rose one year and took it, and what was left standing when the water went down was the truth: that you cannot fly away from what you are. You can only fly toward it.

He looks at the horizon now and it is not an escape. It is an address. Somewhere out there past the last line of hills is the town where the families live, and the bell on its frame, and the graves at Sable Bend with their small stones that Wren tends whenever they pass, and the outpost under the water giving up its foundations a little more each dry season like a sleeper turning over and showing its face. Somewhere out there is everything he spent twenty years not looking at, and it is not a wound anymore. It is a country. He lives in it. He is going to fly all over it for the rest of his life, with his wife's maps and his daughter's hands on the controls, and every mile will be a mile *to* something instead of away.

Ines looks up from her charts. "You're doing the horizon thing," she says.

"What horizon thing?"

"The thing where you stand there and look at it like it owes you money."

"It used to," he says. "We've settled."

She comes to stand beside him, and she does not take his arm or lean on him; she just stands, shoulder to shoulder, the way they stood on the sandbar while the letters dried in the sun and the surveyors read what the empire had buried and nobody, that whole long afternoon, said a single unnecessary word. They are good at that still, the two of them. Saying only what's needed. Letting the silence hold what it holds.

"Wren wants to fly the first family run herself," Ines says.

"Front seat, me behind her, you in the back with the Almeidas' luggage."

"The Almeidas' luggage gets its own aircraft."

"The Almeidas' luggage gets its own *airline*." He drinks the cold coffee anyway. It tastes like morning, held over. "She'll be ready by Thursday."

"She's ready now. You know she's ready now. You're the one who isn't."

"I'm the one who wants one more month of being the only pilot in the family," he says. "Is that so criminal?"

"It's human," Ines says. "It's the most human thing about you. Well." She considers. "Top three."

He laughs, and the sound goes out across the darkening strip and up into the first stars, and somewhere far downriver a bell is being polished for tomorrow's anniversary, and somewhere upriver the water is low and honest over the bones of the old world, and here, on a strip he cut with his own hands and hid for twenty years and finally gave a name and a place on the map, a man stands with his family's lamp burning behind him and the horizon ahead of him, and for the first time in his life he is not measuring the distance to it.

He is measuring the years.

There are more of them than he ever planned on. He intends to use every one.

"Thursday," Ines says, and takes his hand.

"Thursday," he says. "Four trips. Full tanks. And Ines—" he looks at her, at the lamplight and the charts and the twenty years of miles behind him and the uncharted country ahead, "—set a course for home first. Just once. Before we go anywhere else. I want to see what it looks like from the air when we're already there."

Ines looks at him for a long moment, and then she smiles — the full one, the rare one, the one he flew twenty years to earn without knowing it existed.

"I already drew it," she says. "Come inside. I'll show you the way."
