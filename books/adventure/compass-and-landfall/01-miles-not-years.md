## 1. Miles, Not Years

The sun came up over the San Rafael Swell like a coin dropped into a dry well, and by the time its light reached the survey camp, Mirabel Sterling had already been awake for two hours.

She stood at the edge of the mesa with her transit case open at her feet, drinking coffee that had gone bitter in the tin cup, and watched the light walk down the canyon walls the way she watched everything: with a notebook in one hand and a pencil behind her ear, cataloguing. Shadow line at 6:14. Wind out of the northeast, light, carrying sand off the rim. The mules restless in the picket line — something had come through in the night, probably a coyote, nothing worth waking Odile for.

Twenty years of mornings like this one, and she had never once tired of the arithmetic of them. Light, wind, water, distance. Numbers that behaved. A man could build a life on numbers that behaved.

Behind her, the camp was stirring. She heard the canvas of the cook tent snap in the breeze, heard Ruiz swearing cheerfully at the camp stove, which he swore at every morning with the devotion of a man who loved the argument more than the outcome. She heard the muleteers, the Okonkwo brothers, murmuring to their animals in the low river of a language she'd never learned but had come to understand by rhythm alone: *easy, easy, we have far to go.*

Far to go. That was the whole of it, wasn't it. There was always far to go.

She finished the coffee, set the cup on the transit case, and opened the notebook to the day's page. The line she'd ruled across it the night before was still there, straight as a horizon: **Day 41. Miles run: 312.6.**

Not *Day 41*. Well — that too, but the other number was the one she trusted. Days were slippery. Days stacked up into years, and years stacked up into a weight she preferred not to carry. But miles were honest. A mile was a mile whether you walked it at nineteen or at thirty-nine, and it never asked you what you'd done with the ones before it.

She ruled the fresh line, wrote the date because Odile would check, and closed the book.

"Boss." Odile's voice, from the cook tent, carrying the particular flatness that meant she had news and was deciding how to deliver it. "You want to see this."

Mirabel crossed the camp in eleven strides. She counted them without meaning to.

* * *

The cook tent smelled of woodsmoke and frying salt pork and, underneath it, something sweeter that Mirabel identified with a small internal frown and chose to ignore. Odile stood by the stove with a piece of paper in her hand — not a telegram blank, exactly, but the flimsy yellow paper that came out of the telegraph office at Green River, carried up to them by the weekly supply rider, who was at that moment watering his horse at the spring and pretending very hard not to be eavesdropping.

"From Vane," Odile said.

The name landed in Mirabel's chest like a stone dropped down a shaft. She heard it fall and fall and never quite hit bottom.

"Read it," she said.

"You read it. It's addressed to you." Odile held it out, and her eyes — dark, level, twenty years loyal — did the thing they did when she was watching Mirabel closely and pretending not to. "I'll just say that I read it already, since you'll accuse me of it anyway."

Mirabel took the paper. Elias Vane's name sat at the head of it in the telegraph clerk's blocky hand, and beneath it, the message itself, paid for at full rate, which meant Vane had wanted every word of it delivered whole:

**STERLING. HAVE CONTRACT STOP SURVEY CACHE RECOVERY KARAGOL RANGE STOP WINTER WAGES YOUR CREW NEEDS STOP KNOW YOUR FEES STOP REPLY BY MONTHS END STOP — E.V.**

The Karagol Range.

The canyon, the camp, the smell of salt pork — all of it receded, the way a shoreline recedes when you swim out past your depth. For a moment Mirabel was not standing in the Utah desert at all. She was nineteen years old, standing in a telegraph office in a lowland town whose name she had made herself forget, hearing a man she'd never met say words that would follow her across three continents: *lost, presumed dead, the expedition is lost.*

She had been nineteen, and the miles had started that day, and they had not stopped since.

"Boss." Odile's voice again, closer now. "You've gone white as the salt flats. Sit down before you fall down, and then tell me why."

"I'm fine." She was not fine. She sat down anyway, on the camp stool by the stove, because Odile had said to and because her knees had quietly voted the same way. The sweet smell under the woodsmoke was stronger here. She looked at the stove, and on the stove's iron shelf, cooling under a cloth, was a cake.

A small one. Ugly, honestly — lopsided, sun-cracked across the top, the kind of thing that happened to flour at altitude and in dry heat. Ruiz's work. Ruiz, who was at this moment standing in the tent flap with a look of elaborate innocence that fooled no one, least of all Mirabel.

"It's nothing," Ruiz said. "It's a geological formation. We found it this morning and thought you should examine it."

"Is that so."

"Very rare formation. Requires celebration."

"Ruiz." Mirabel set the telegram down on her knee, face up, and looked at him, and then at Odile, and understood, all at once and with a sensation like a hairline crack running through plaster, what day it was.

Thirty-nine.

She had known it, of course. She knew it every year the way she knew the date — wrote it in the notebook, moved on. But the crew knew it too, apparently, and this year they had decided to do something about it, and the crack in the plaster widened.

"I don't want a cake," she said.

"We know," Odile said placidly, slicing it anyway. "That's why we made one."

"That's not — Odile. I don't *celebrate* it. It's a day. It's a day like any other day, and we have a line to run, and the light is good until at least two o'clock, and I will not have this crew losing a morning to — to *cake*."

"Three hundred and twelve miles," Odile said. She held out a plate. The slice was enormous and sat on it like a small orange rockslide. "Forty-one days. That's what you count, isn't it? I've watched you do it for six years. You count everything except the thing that's actually happening to you."

The plate hung in the air between them. Behind Odile, Ruiz had stopped pretending to work. The supply rider had drifted closer with his horse, openly listening now, the absolute vulture. Somewhere out at the picket line one of the Okonkwo brothers laughed at something, the sound carrying clean across the morning air, and it was that — the ordinary, unguarded sound of people being happy — that made Mirabel's throat do something she had to swallow against.

"I had a father once," she said, before she could stop herself. It came out lower than she meant it to. "He counted years. Anniversaries. He believed time was something you could *mark*, that if you wrote the date on the right page the past would stay where you put it." She looked at the telegram on her knee. "The past doesn't stay where you put it. It comes looking for you. So I count miles instead. Miles go forward. That's all I want from a number."

The tent was quiet. The stove ticked. Ruiz had gone very still by the flap, and even the supply rider's horse seemed to be holding its breath.

Odile set the plate down on Mirabel's knee anyway — not offering now, just *placing*, the way you set a load on a mule that has decided to be stubborn — and said, "Then eat your geological formation, boss, and we'll run the line. And when you're ready to tell me why the name on that paper has put a look on your face I've only seen once before, the night you got drunk in Lander and told me half a story — I'll be listening."

Mirabel looked down at the cake. It was, she saw now, not entirely lopsided by accident. Ruiz had scored the top with a knife before baking: a rough, wandering line, and beside it, in careful strokes, a small arrow. A survey line. A bearing.

He had drawn her a route on a cake.

She picked up the fork, because there was no winning against Odile and they both knew it, and ate one bite, and then — because the crack in the plaster had already run and there was no pretending otherwise — she ate the whole slice, sitting on the camp stool in the cook tent on the morning of her thirty-ninth birthday, while her crew pretended very hard to be busy and failed completely.

It was, she noted with distant irritation, delicious.

* * *

They ran the line all morning, and it was the best morning of the season, and Mirabel hated how much she knew it.

The Swell opened up ahead of them in long red ramps of sandstone, and the transit sang under her hands, and Odile ran the stadia rod up the slickrock like a woman thirty years younger, and the numbers came off the instrument clean and true, and for three hours the world reduced itself to the only thing Mirabel had ever fully trusted: the work. Backsight, foresight, record. Backsight, foresight, record. The line unspooled across the mesa at four miles an hour, and every mile was a mile going forward, and forward was the only direction she had ever been able to bear.

But the telegram rode in her breast pocket the whole time. She could feel it there, a small yellow weight over her heart, like a coal that wouldn't cool.

At the noon halt she sat on a sandstone lip above a dry wash and took it out and read it again, though she had it by heart.

*Survey cache recovery. Karagol Range.*

Someone had left a cache in the Karagol Range. Someone had gone up into those passes twenty years ago with instruments and notebooks and a ledger, and had not come down, and the mountain had kept what it was given, and now Elias Vane — Vane, of all the names on the earth, Vane, her father's rival, the other half of the story she had spent two decades not telling — wanted to send a crew up to take it back.

He wanted her crew. He had said so. *Winter wages your crew needs.* He knew about the crew. He knew about the season — he knew that the Utah contract ended in six weeks and the winter contracts hadn't been signed, that the Okonkwo brothers had letters waiting from home, that Ruiz had a sister's wedding in the spring he'd already told Mirabel he couldn't afford to miss, that Odile was fifty-eight years old and had earned the right to a winter that didn't smell of canvas. Elias Vane had never met these people and he knew exactly what they needed, which meant he had done his research, which meant he had wanted *her*, specifically, and had gone at her through the people she loved, because he had guessed — correctly — that she would refuse for herself and not for them.

That was the part that frightened her. Not the mountain. Not the name. The *precision* of it. Vane had built the offer the way she built a survey line: backsight, foresight, record. He had sighted on her and he had not missed.

And underneath the fear, quieter, in the place she kept locked: the cache. Her father's instruments. Her father's notebooks. Whatever the Karagol had kept of him for twenty years, sealed in a cairn or a cave, waiting.

*He's dead,* she told herself, the way she had told herself ten thousand times. *He's dead, and dead men don't leave instructions, and whatever is in that cache belongs to the past, and the past doesn't stay where you put it but you don't have to go open its drawers.*

She stood up. She walked ten paces from the crew, out onto the bare slickrock where the wind came off the rim clean, and she took out her matches.

"Boss?" Ruiz, twenty feet back, watching.

"Burn barrel's full," she said.

It wasn't, and he knew it, and she struck the match anyway.

The telegram caught on the second try — the paper was thin and the wind was kind — and it flared up yellow, and she held it by one corner and watched Elias Vane's careful, expensive words curl and blacken and lift off the rock in flakes of ash that the wind took northeast, toward the mountains, toward the country she had sworn off at nineteen the way other people swore off drink.

She held it until the fire reached her fingers, then dropped the last corner and ground it under her boot heel.

"There," she said, to Ruiz, to Odile, to the whole red watching emptiness of the desert. "Answer sent."

Nobody laughed. Odile, down by the packs, just looked at her for a long moment with those level dark eyes, and then went back to cinching a mule's pack strap with a violence that said everything her silence didn't.

* * *

That night the camp was quiet in the way it always was after a hard line — the good tired, the mules dozing on their feet, the stove down to coals. Mirabel sat outside her own tent with the notebook on her knee, ruling tomorrow's page, and the stars over the Swell were so thick and so close it was like standing at the bottom of a well full of light.

Day 41. Miles run: 316.9.

She wrote it. She ruled the line. She closed the book.

And then she sat there, in the cold and the starlight, and did the thing she had not let herself do in twenty years, which was to stop counting and simply *feel* the day — the whole weight of it, thirty-nine years of it, the nineteen of them before the telegraph office and the twenty after, her father's voice in her memory saying *Mira, look, the bearing's true* for the ten-thousandth time, and the mountain above the tree line where that voice had stopped.

The past doesn't stay where you put it.

She had burned the telegram. She had ground it into the stone. And she knew — with the flat, sick certainty of a surveyor who has found the error in her own line — that it would not be enough. A man like Vane did not send one telegram. He sent two. He sent a second letter, with something in it you couldn't burn, because he had spent twenty years studying her the way she studied terrain, and he knew where the fault line ran.

The supply rider would come back through in seven days.

Mirabel sat with the closed notebook on her knee, under the too-many stars, and counted the days until then, and hated herself for counting.

Six.
