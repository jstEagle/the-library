## 2. The Rival's Letter

The rider came back in six days, not seven, which Mirabel chose to read as an accident of weather rather than an omen.

She heard him before she saw him — the drum of hooves on the hardpan, carrying a mile across the flat like sound carries over water — and by the time he swung down at the edge of camp she had already arranged her face into the expression her crews called the Sterling Mask: pleasant, attentive, entirely closed. She'd been wearing it for twenty years. It fit better than any of her jackets.

"Telegram and a letter, ma'am." The rider was young, sunburned through his kerchief, and he held out two items as if they might bite him. "The telegram's marked urgent. The letter's from the same party."

"I can see that."

He lingered. She didn't dismiss him so much as stop acknowledging his existence, and after a moment he remembered some urgent business involving his horse and withdrew to it.

The telegram was short. She read it standing in the open, because she refused to give Vane the dignity of privacy:

**SECOND LETTER FOLLOWS BY THIS RIDER. READ BEFORE DECIDING. THE ENCLOSED WILL EXPLAIN WHAT MY WORDS CANNOT. — E.V.**

*The enclosed.* As if he were sending her a specimen. A rock sample. Something to be catalogued.

Odile appeared at her shoulder the way Odile always did — no announcement, just presence, solid as a cairn. "You want me to take that?"

"No."

"You want to burn it out here where the wind can help?"

Mirabel almost smiled. "I want to read it first. Then we'll discuss burning."

She took it to her tent. Not because she wanted privacy — because whatever was inside deserved to meet her alone before it met anyone else. That was a lesson her father had taught her without meaning to: *never let them see you flinch at your own instruments.* He'd said it about barometers. He'd meant everything.

She sat on her cot, broke the seal — good paper, heavy stock, the kind of stationery that announced its own expense — and unfolded three pages in Elias Vane's narrow, precise hand.

The first page was business. Terms, generous to the point of insult: double the going rate for a winter contract, hazard pay written in, a bonus clause tied to delivery of the cache before the passes closed. He named the railhead — Copper Bend, eleven days' ride north — and a date. He named his representative: *my son, Tomas Vane, who will travel with your crew and answer for my side of this arrangement.* He wrote the word *arrangement* the way other men wrote *partnership*, and Mirabel noted the difference the way she noted a benchmark slightly off true.

The second page laid out the job. A survey cache, cached — the word did its grim little work twice — twenty years ago in the Karagol Range, above the tree line, below the summit pass. Contents unspecified beyond *instruments, records, and materials of scientific value.* Location given only as coordinates and a bearing line, which told her Vane didn't have a route, only a destination. Which meant he needed her not for her mules but for her mind. Fine. She'd known that since the telegram.

It was the third page where the trap showed its teeth.

*Miss Sterling,* it began, and then Vane set down his pen and picked up someone else's.

A single sheet, older, foxed at the edges, folded inside the third page like a knife inside a handkerchief. Mirabel knew what it was before she opened it. Some part of her — the part that ran survey lines across three continents specifically so that she would never again be in a room with her father's handwriting — knew it from the weight of the fold alone.

She opened it anyway. Of course she opened it. He'd known she would. That was the whole architecture of the thing.

Her father's hand. Unmistakable. He'd written the way he walked — fast, slanted forward, always leaning into the next thing, the descenders of his g's and y's trailing off like he couldn't be bothered to finish a thought when a better one was arriving. She had spent her childhood watching that hand draw maps in the margins of her schoolbooks, annotate her mother's recipes with elevation corrections, draft letters to Elias Vane that her mother made him copy out fair before sending because the drafts were *unfit for human eyes, Aldous.*

The page was dated. Nineteen years ago this autumn. Three weeks, her mind supplied unasked, before he died.

It was addressed to Vane.

And it was intimate. Not tenderly — there was nothing tender between those two men, there never had been — but intimate the way a wound is intimate. Her father wrote to Vane the way he spoke to him, she realized: as if the rivalry were a marriage neither could afford to leave. *You'll say I'm rushing the season again. You always say that. But I've found something up here, Elias, and I'm going to be the one who names it.* And lower, in a paragraph that seemed to have been written fast, angry, the ink dug deep enough to score the paper's back:

*Mirabel spotted the formation first. She's twelve and she reads ground better than men I've employed for thirty years. Whatever comes of this, that goes in the record or it goes nowhere.*

Mirabel read that sentence four times.

Then she understood what she was holding, and her hands went cold in the warm tent.

Because her father had *not* put it in the record. She knew that with the certainty of scar tissue. When the expedition's report came down the mountain — the report that made his name, the site that killed him, the discovery she had spent two decades refusing to think about — there had been no mention of a twelve-year-old girl. No mention of her at all. She had learned, at thirteen, at a memorial service full of solemn men in bad suits, that her father's greatest find belonged to her father alone, and she had swallowed that fact the way you swallow a fishhook, and she had built her whole life on the principle that names on pages were lies and distance was the only honest measurement left.

And now here was a page proving he had *meant* to correct it. Proving he had written it down, once, to the one man alive who hated him enough to keep every scrap of paper.

Vane hadn't sent her evidence of her father's glory. He'd sent her evidence of her father's broken promise — dangled, withheld, and now offered back at a price. *Read before deciding,* the telegram said. *The enclosed will explain what my words cannot.*

What it explained was that Elias Vane knew exactly where the fault line ran, and had driven a piton straight into it.

Mirabel sat very still for a long time. Outside, the Utah evening did its usual work — light going amber on the mesas, the crew's voices settling into supper sounds, someone laughing at Ruiz's card tricks. The world continued being ordinary, which felt like an obscenity.

She refolded the page along its original creases. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady and took no comfort in it; steadiness, in her experience, was just fear that had learned discipline.

* * *

She didn't burn anything. She called the crew together instead, which surprised even herself.

They gathered around the main fire with their tin plates balanced on knees — Odile, Ruiz, the Calloway twins, Petra Osei with her burned forearm wrapped and healing, the muleteers, the cook. Eleven faces, firelit, waiting. Mirabel stood with the letter in her jacket pocket and the telegram in her fist and gave them the shape of it, stripped clean.

"A contract offer. Winter work, high country, Karagol Range. Recovery of a survey cache left twenty years ago. Double rates, hazard pay, completion bonus. Client is Elias Vane."

Silence. She watched the name land on each face. Most of them knew it the way people know weather they've only read about — Vane Expeditions, the rival house, the man whose name sat opposite her father's in the old journals. Odile knew more than that. Mirabel saw it in the way Odile went still, plate forgotten, eyes fixed on her with a look that said *I know whose handwriting is in your pocket, girl, and I know why you haven't shown us.*

"The Karagol," Ruiz said slowly. "That's—"

"That's the range where Aldous Sterling died," Petra finished, blunt as ever. "Your father, boss."

"My father," Mirabel agreed. "Yes."

"And Vane wants *you* to go get his old cache." Ruiz turned his plate in his hands. "Why you?"

"Because I'm the best high-country surveyor he can hire, and he knows it."

"Or," said Odile quietly, "because of who you are."

Mirabel let that sit. She'd decided, walking from her tent to the fire, exactly how much truth to spend, and she spent it carefully. "There are reasons a man like Vane does things that have nothing to do with surveying. I won't pretend otherwise. What I'll tell you is what's in the terms, and what's in the season." She held up fingers. "One: the wages are real, and winter is coming, and half of you have families eating on what this crew makes. Two: the window closes with the snows — we're in or out by the end of the week. Three: if we take it, we do the job, we come home, and we don't ask the mountain for anything but a route through. It's work. That's all it is."

"It's not all it is," Odile said.

"No," Mirabel said. "But it's all it has to be."

She looked around the fire. "I've voted against this job. For the record: I vote against it. But this crew doesn't belong to me, and I promised when I formed it that contracts get decided the same way every time — together. So we vote. All in favor of taking the Vane contract."

Ruiz raised his hand first, then the Calloways, nearly in unison, glancing at each other and grinning the way twins grin when caught sharing a skull. Petra raised hers without hesitation — her sister's kids, Mirabel knew, needed new boots and a roof that didn't sing in the wind. The muleteers looked at each other and raised hands like men signing on for a season they'd already mentally spent. The cook raised both, which got a laugh, which loosened the circle's shoulders, which was probably why he did it.

Eleven hands. Twelve counting Odile's, which came up last, slow and deliberate, her eyes never leaving Mirabel's face.

"Carried," Odile said. "We're going to the Karagol."

"We leave for Copper Bend day after tomorrow," Mirabel said. "Railhead's eleven days north. We rendezvous with the client's representative there — a Tomas Vane. The son." She let a beat pass. "Anybody has a problem with any part of this, tonight's the night to say it. After Copper Bend, we're committed, and I don't run a crew that mutters."

Nobody muttered. They dispersed to their plates and their cards and the small economies of an evening, and Mirabel stayed by the fire long after it burned down to orange lace, turning the folded page in her pocket over and over like a stone she couldn't stop thumbing.

*Whatever comes of this, that goes in the record or it goes nowhere.*

He'd written it. He'd written it and then he'd climbed up into that white silence and he'd died, and the record had come down the mountain wearing his name and nobody else's, and she had spent twenty years making sure her own name appeared on nothing she couldn't walk away from.

Now Vane wanted to hire her to climb back into the exact center of the one story she'd sworn off, carrying a page that proved the lie she'd built her life around wasn't quite the lie she thought.

*Only business,* she told herself, watching the fire die. *Double rates. Winter wages. A route through and home again.*

She was very good at telling herself things. She'd had practice.

* * *

They broke camp at first light two days later, and the desert let them go grudgingly, the way it always did — a last mile of sand that pulled at the mules' shoes, a final ridge that showed them the whole empty world they were leaving for a colder one.

Mirabel rode point. Behind her the string stretched out in its practiced order, and behind the string the land flattened into haze, and somewhere ahead of all of it, eleven days north, a railhead town waited with a Vane in it.

Odile drew up beside her at the noon halt, water skin in hand, and offered it over. Mirabel drank. Neither of them spoke for a while. Twenty years of shared trail had taught them both when words were load-bearing and when they were just weight.

"He kept it," Odile said finally. "All these years. You know what that means."

"I know what it means."

"A man doesn't hold a page twenty years unless he's been waiting for the day it's worth something."

"A man holds a page twenty years," Mirabel said, handing back the water skin, "because he's been waiting for the day he can aim it."

Odile nodded slowly, unsurprised, unsurprising. Then: "You're going to tell the crew eventually. What's really in that letter. What really happened up there."

"Am I."

"You are. Because Tomas Vane is going to know his half, and you can't out-climb a story you won't tell." Odile swung back onto her mule with the economy of a woman who had been mounting animals since before Mirabel was born. "I was there, girl. Remember that. Whatever version gets told on this mountain, I'll know if it's true."

She rode back down the line, and Mirabel sat her horse a moment longer, looking north.

The Karagol wasn't visible from here — nothing was visible from here but heat and distance, the two materials her whole life was built from. But she could feel the range the way she sometimes felt a storm two days out: a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the light. Somewhere under all that ice lay a cache her father had packed with his own hands, and a page in her pocket proved he'd meant to give her credit before he took it away forever, and a man named Vane had spent two decades keeping both facts in a drawer against precisely this season.

*Only business.*

She turned her horse north and gave the signal, and the crew moved out behind her, twelve souls and twenty-six mules, walking steadily toward the one place on earth she had promised herself she would never see again.

Six days, she thought. Then the railhead. Then the son.

She counted the miles instead. It was easier. Miles never lied about what they cost.
