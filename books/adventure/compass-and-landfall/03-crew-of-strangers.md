## 3. Crew of Strangers

The train came in two hours late, coughing steam into a morning so cold the frost on the railhead boards crackled under Tomas Vane's boots like thin ice on a puddle. He had been waiting since before dawn, pacing the length of the platform, and he told himself the pacing was about logistics — mule lines, freight manifests, the weight distribution of the supply crates stacked under canvas at the platform's end — and not about the fact that somewhere behind him, up the switchback road from the valley town, Mirabel Sterling was walking toward him with twenty years of his father's stories stitched to her name.

He had grown up on those stories. That was the trouble.

*The Sterling woman*, his father called her, when he called her anything at all, which was rarely and always with a flatness Tomas had learned early to read as weather. *Cold as a surveyor's transit. Brilliant, God help us all. Cursed.* The last word his father said quietly, almost tenderly, the way other men spoke of drowned sailors or burned churches. Elias Vane did not believe in curses; he believed in consequences, and he believed Mirabel Sterling was one of them, walking around loose in the world with a compass in her hand and her father's ghost on her back.

Tomas had never seen her. He knew her by reputation the way you know a mountain you've only ever seen in photographs — the shape of it fixed in your mind long before your boots touch the scree. Twenty years of running lines across three continents. First woman to survey the upper Qorikancha basin. The desert work in Utah that had made three separate mining concerns rich and one geologist famous for refusing to be photographed. And beneath all of it, unspoken but present in every account like groundwater: the Karagol Range, and the expedition that went up and did not come down whole.

He checked his pocket watch against the station clock out of habit, though neither of them could agree on time this far into the mountains anyway. In his breast pocket, wrapped in oilcloth against sweat and snow alike, sat his father's sealed orders. He had carried them from the lowlands without opening them. This was not discipline, exactly, though his father would have called it that and been pleased. It was something Tomas didn't have a clean word for — a wish to meet this job the way you meet a stranger, without prejudice, without the old man's voice already dictating in his ear. Once he broke the seal, his father would be in the mountains with them, and Tomas wanted — just for a day, just for the first day — to be alone up here with his own judgment.

The train whistled twice at the bend below the gorge, a sound that bounced off the canyon walls and came back flattened. Tomas squared the manifests under his arm and went down to the platform's edge.

* * *

They came off the train in an order that told him everything before anyone spoke. Not the passengers first, as he'd expected, but the crew: four figures moving down the steps with the unhurried economy of people who had unloaded trains in worse places than this, and who were already, visibly, working. A broad-shouldered woman with iron-gray hair cut short took the tailgate and began counting crates by sight, her lips moving. A lean man with a climber's bowed legs went straight to the mule car and started talking to the animals before he talked to any human being. Two younger hands — a wiry half-Karakoram boy and a big quiet Swede — fell into position flanking the baggage car without being told, the way water finds its level.

Only then did Mirabel Sterling step down onto the platform.

She was smaller than the legend. That was Tomas's first thought, and it felt like a betrayal of something, though he couldn't have said what. Photographs had not prepared him — there were no photographs, that was the point — and so he had built her tall and severe, carved out of the same stone as the ranges she mapped. Instead she came down the steps like any traveler, medium height, dark hair bound back, a canvas jacket gone soft and pale at the elbows, and it was only when she stood still and looked at the range wall rising north of the station that he understood the photographs weren't missing anything. The legend wasn't in her size. It was in how she looked at the mountain: not admiring it, not bracing against it, but reading it. Her eyes moved along the ridgelines the way a literate person's eyes move along a page, and whatever she saw there made her mouth set in a line that had nothing to do with cold.

She turned and saw him, and he watched her take his measure in perhaps two seconds — his father's jaw, his father's coat, the Vane Expedition stamp on the crate stencils — and file him away.

"Vane," she said. Not a question. Not quite a greeting either.

"Tomas." He put out his hand. "My father sends his regrets. He's tied up with the financing through the winter."

"I'm sure he is." She shook his hand once, briefly, her grip dry and strong, and then her attention went past his shoulder to the crates. "That's our freight?"

"Yours and ours combined. I inventoried it Tuesday."

"Against what manifest?"

"Mine."

"Then we'll do it again against mine." She said it without heat, the way you'd correct a decimal error, and turned to signal her foreman. "Odile. Manifest."

So it began. Tomas stood aside while the gray-haired woman — Odile, and the name landed on him with a small strange weight, though he couldn't have said why, only that his father's voice went very quiet in his memory when he heard it — read out crate numbers while the Swede hauled and the boy checked seals. It took forty minutes. She found two discrepancies, both trivial, both real, and Tomas found himself nodding along rather than bristling, because she was right both times, and because she handled it without once making it about him.

By noon the mules were loaded and the whole caravan stood steaming in the yard like a living thing, forty legs and a hundred arguments waiting to happen. Tomas spread his maps on a plank table outside the station office and weighted the corners with stones.

"The gorge," he said, when Mirabel came over. "We go up the river gorge. Three days to the confluence, good gradient, my father's party used it on the approach in—"

"I know what your father used." She was looking at the map without touching it. "Twenty years ago. In September. With a different river."

"The gorge is still the fastest line."

"The gorge is a funnel." She tapped the map where the river pinched between two walls of shaded rock. "It's October. The headwaters are still open, which means the drainage is live. One hard rain on the high snowfields and everything that water has ever wanted to get rid of comes down that funnel at once — including, if we're unlucky, us."

"It hasn't rained in nine days."

"It rained in the high country this morning." She nodded north, at a thread of cloud snagged on the far peaks that he had looked at and dismissed as weather-that-was-somewhere-else. "You can't see it from here. You learn to smell it instead."

"And the alternative?" He kept his voice level. Around them, both crews had gone quiet in the way of people pretending to fuss with tack. "The low road adds four days. Four days against the first snows. My father's timetable—"

"Is your father's." She straightened. "This is my contract too, Mr. Vane, and my name goes on the route decision same as yours. We take the low road along the moraine. Slower, higher ground, no funnel over our heads."

"Four days is not nothing. The passes—"

"I know exactly what the passes do in October. Better than you do." Something moved behind her eyes when she said it, quick and deep, a door opening onto a lit room and shutting again. He understood suddenly that he had stepped on something without seeing it, the way you step on buried wire, and that the thing under the snow here was older and sharper than a disagreement about routes.

"The gorge," he said, "is a reasonable line."

"So is the moraine. Reasonable men can hold different maps." She rolled his chart closed and handed it back to him handle-first, a courtesy so precise it felt like a rebuke. "We'll put it to the crews if you like. Or you can trust the person who's spent twenty years learning what this kind of country does when the season turns."

"And if I say my father's orders specify the gorge?"

Something changed in her face. Not anger — colder than anger, an arithmetic. "Then your father," she said, "is spending money he doesn't have to buy speed he shouldn't trust. Which wouldn't be the first time."

The words dropped between them and neither picked them up. Down the platform, Odile had stopped working and was watching them with an expression Tomas couldn't read at all.

"We'll take it to the crews," Mirabel said at last, quieter. "Tonight, at camp. Majority rules. I don't outrank you and you don't outrank me, whatever the letterheads say."

"That's not how contracts usually work."

"No," she agreed. "Usually they work by whoever's willing to spend the most lives buying the least time. I've watched that arithmetic done before. I didn't care for the answer." She turned away, then paused. "For what it's worth, Mr. Vane — the gorge isn't a bad route. In June I'd take it myself. But we're not in June, and we're not your father, and I'd thank you to remember which of those facts is going to keep everyone breathing."

She walked off toward the mule lines, and Tomas stood at the plank table with his father's map in his hand, feeling as though he'd just been surveyed himself — measured, plotted, and found to sit slightly off the true line.

* * *

They made first camp two miles up the moraine road, in a fold of ground above the river, because Mirabel had won the vote — five to three, and Tomas noted without much comfort that two of his own hired hands had voted against his father's route. He told himself the sting of that was professional. He was less sure by the time the fires were lit.

Camp, he discovered, was a language, and the Sterling crew spoke it fluently. The tents went up in a geometry so exact it looked drawn. The mule lines were pitched with their tails to the wind. The cook fire was built in a dug pit lined with stone, smokeless, invisible from the trail — and when Tomas asked the boy why, the boy, whose name was Anselm and who had a smile like a cracked plate, said, "Miss Sterling says a camp should look like nobody was ever here. Says the mountain keeps better books than we do, and she hates owing."

"And you do what she says because?"

Anselm looked at him with genuine puzzlement. "Because she's never been wrong about a mountain yet. Ask Odile. Odile's been with her longest." A pause, and something flickered behind the boy's cheerfulness, some knowledge held carefully, like a coal carried in cupped hands. "Ask her about the Karagol sometime. If you want to see something."

"See what?"

But Anselm had found urgent business with the mule feed, and Tomas was left sitting on a crate with the firelight on his face and his father's sealed orders heavy in his breast pocket, heavier by the hour, it seemed, though the oilcloth hadn't gained an ounce.

Across the fire, Mirabel sat apart from both crews, mending a strap with small precise stitches, and Tomas caught himself watching her the way he'd been warned not to watch a fire in timber country — too long, too close, until you forgot which side of it you stood on. She was not cold, he decided. That was the lie in all his father's stories. Cold was absence, and there was nothing absent about her; she was the most entirely present person he had ever seen, every part of her aimed at the task in front of her like an instrument leveled on a target. What his father called cold was simply that she did not waste herself. Not on charm, not on comfort, not — he thought, watching her decline the Swede's offer of tea with a shake of the head that somehow cost her nothing and gave nothing — on warmth, either.

Cursed, his father said. Sitting there in the firelight with her needle going click-click-click against a thimble, she looked less cursed than haunted, and Tomas knew enough — growing up in his father's house, he knew more than enough — to know the difference. Haunted meant there was something back there. Something specific, with a date on it and a name.

His father's name, probably. His father's name was on most of the ghosts in their family's history, though Elias Vane would have listed them differently.

Mirabel looked up. Caught him watching. Held his gaze for a moment across the fire — level, unreadable, faintly questioning, as if he were a discrepancy in a manifest she intended to resolve — and then went back to her stitching.

He should have looked away sooner. He knew that the way you know a route is wrong before you can say why.

Later, when the camps had gone quiet and the stars over the moraine were hard and close as nailheads, Tomas sat up alone with the oilcloth packet in his hands and turned it over and over. His father's seal gleamed dully where the firelight reached it. *Read it when you're committed*, Elias had said, handing it over in the lowland office, and Tomas had assumed he meant committed to the contract. Now, two miles up a mountain road he had argued against and lost, he wondered if his father had meant something else entirely. Committed past turning back. Committed past honesty. His father had a genius for instructions that only revealed their full meaning after it was too late to follow them differently.

He weighed the packet in his palm. Inside it: the route, the timetable, the terms, and whatever else Elias Vane wanted done in the Karagol Range that he preferred his own son not to know until knowing could not be undone.

Tomorrow they would cross the river and enter the gorge country whether they took the gorge itself or not, and the low road would begin climbing in earnest, and there would be no more trains, no more votes, no more platform where a man could stand with his judgment still his own.

Tomas slid the packet back inside his coat, unopened, and lay down facing away from the fire.

He was nearly asleep when he heard footsteps stop near his bedroll — light ones, deliberate — and opened his eyes to find Odile standing over him, gray hair loose in the starlight, looking down with an expression he would remember for years: not hostile, not friendly. Appraising. The way you look at weather coming over a pass.

"You voted my father's route," he said. "Today. You and the others. Why lose, and stay?"

Odile crouched, forearms on knees, and considered him for a long moment.

"Because Miss Sterling was right about the gorge," she said. "And because in twenty years I've learned that being right is the cheapest thing she is." She rose, brushing off her knees. "Sleep, Mr. Vane. Tomorrow we climb. And tomorrow night, around the fire, somebody's going to tell you what we're really carrying up this mountain — or I will, if nobody else has the stomach for it. Your father's letter can wait one more day. Some things can't."

She walked back toward the fire, and Tomas lay staring up at the wheeling stars, the sealed orders pressing against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and understood that the vote he had lost today was the smallest thing he was going to lose on this mountain.
