## 4. What Was Sworn Off

The fire at Nine Mile Camp burned low and orange, the way Mirabel liked her fires: small enough to control, hot enough to trust. She sat apart from it by exactly one bedroll's length, mending a strap with waxed thread, listening to her crew settle into the rhythm of an evening she could have timed to the minute. Odile arguing elevation gain with the muleteers. Young Pell recounting his crossing of the scree field that afternoon as though he'd invented scree. The kettle ticking as it cooled.

Three days out of the railhead, and the Karagol Range had stopped being a word on a contract and become a fact of the body — a weight on the lungs, a cold that came up through boot soles, a silence between peaks so complete you could hear your own pulse admit it was afraid.

Mirabel pulled the thread tight. She had run this evening in her head a hundred times since the vote: how she would keep the crew's talk on routes and rations, away from the name Sterling and what it meant up here. She was good at this. She had been good at this for twenty years.

Then Odile said, "You knew my father."

Not to her. To Tomas Vane, who sat across the fire turning a tin cup he wasn't drinking from. But the words went through the camp like a dropped lantern.

Tomas looked up. "I didn't know him. I knew *of* him."

"Everyone knew of him." Odile fed a stick to the flames. She was not looking at anyone; she was looking at the fire the way surveyors look at instruments, reading something exact. "Aldous Sterling. The man who mapped the western approaches when everyone else said they couldn't be mapped." A pause, delicate as a plumb line. "The man who didn't come down."

Pell stopped talking mid-word. The kettle ticked once more and went quiet.

Mirabel kept sewing. Her hands were steady. She had trained them to be steady through worse than this, though at the moment she couldn't name what the worse had been.

"Your father talked about him," Tomas said. "Rarely. But when he drank—"

"When he drank," Odile agreed. "Mine too. That's how I know about the cache."

Now Mirabel's needle stopped.

Odile turned from the fire at last, and her eyes found Mirabel with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had been waiting three days for the right hour. "You didn't know I was on the original expedition, did you, Miss Sterling."

The fire popped. Somewhere up-valley, ice cracked off a serac with a sound like a rifle.

"You were twelve," Mirabel said.

"I was nineteen. Base camp cook's assistant. You'd never have seen me — base camp was below the moraine, and you were already above it by then, weren't you? Running lines for your father while the rest of us hauled flour." Odile's voice stayed level, almost gentle, which made it worse. "I remember the day the weather came in. I remember the runners going up and nothing coming back down but one man, half-frozen, carried on a sledge. And I remember Mr. Vane standing at the edge of camp all night, watching the mountain, saying nothing to anybody."

"Odile." Mirabel heard her own voice come out flat, clipped, the voice she used on green crew members who were about to do something stupid. "That's enough."

"It isn't, though. That's the trouble." Odile leaned forward, elbows on knees, and now she spoke to the whole fire, to Pell and the muleteers and young Anselm who kept the instruments, to Tomas most of all. "Mr. Vane hired us with a story. Recover a lost survey cache before the snows. Fair wages, fair risk. What he didn't tell any of you is that the cache is Aldous Sterling's cache. His final survey. His notes. Twenty years sealed under a cairn at the high pass because the man who put them there died getting to them." She looked back at Mirabel. "And he didn't tell you we knew, did he. He didn't tell you I'd stood in that base camp and watched the mountain take your father."

The strap hung slack in Mirabel's hands. Around the fire, faces had gone still in the way faces go still when a landscape shifts underneath them — Pell with his mouth open, Anselm clutching his notebook like a prayer book, the muleteers exchanging a look over the packsaddles they'd been oiling.

"He told me the job was a recovery," Mirabel said quietly. "He told me the notes were incidental."

"Did he tell you why he wants them?" Odile asked. "After twenty years? A man doesn't spend twenty years and a fortune hiring his dead rival's daughter to fetch a box of paper unless the paper says something he can't afford left unread."

"Tomas." Mirabel turned to him. He had gone very still, the tin cup motionless in his hands, and something in his face — some flinch behind the eyes — told her he knew more than the rest of them. Knew, or suspected, or had been carrying his father's sealed orders against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and had not read them, or had read them, or—

"What did he tell you?" she asked him.

Tomas set the cup down. "My father doesn't tell me things. He gives me orders."

"And have you read them?"

"Not yet."

The lie or the truth of it hung there. Mirabel couldn't tell which, and that frightened her more than the mountain did, because she had learned to read men's faces across rope-lengths and crevasse-widths where misreading meant dying, and Tomas Vane's face just then was the first face in years she couldn't parse.

"So none of us," Pell said slowly, "knows what we're actually climbing toward."

"You know what you're climbing toward," Mirabel said. "A cairn at the pass. Boxes. Paper. That part of the contract is true."

"And the part that isn't true?" Anselm asked. He was the youngest, and he asked it without accusation, which was somehow harder to bear.

Mirabel looked at the fire. Above the eastern ridge, the peak they called the Old Man — her father's name for it, though no map said so — caught the last light and held it like a coal. She had promised herself she would never speak of that night. She had built two decades of survey lines and precise distances on top of the promise, and here was Odile, digging through all of it with a cook's assistant's memory and a foreman's bluntness.

"Twenty years ago," Mirabel said, "my father led an expedition into this range to survey the western approaches. I was on it. I was his instrument runner. We found what we came to find. And on the way down—" She stopped. Started again, in the tone she used for coordinates, for facts. "On the way down, there was an argument. There was a fall. My father didn't come down. Elias Vane was on that expedition. So was Odile, at base camp. So was I, higher than any of you will ever be asked to go."

Nobody moved. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath along the moraine.

"That's what the job is," Mirabel finished. "That's what he didn't put in the contract. Now you know. If any of you want to turn back at the river fork tomorrow, I'll write you a full wage voucher myself and think no less of you."

Silence. Then Odile said, "We voted to come, Miss Sterling. We voted knowing winter wages were the truth of it. Knowing *this*—" she gestured at the darkening peaks, at the whole armed and waiting range, "—doesn't change the wages. It changes the reason. Those aren't the same thing, and I've followed you long enough to know you don't confuse them either."

"We're staying," Pell said simply, and Anselm nodded, and the muleteers went back to their saddles with the deliberate calm of men deciding not to decide tonight.

Mirabel looked at them — her crew, her clockwork, her chosen people — and felt the armor of routine crack along a seam she hadn't known was load-bearing.

But it was Tomas she watched. Tomas, who had not voted, whose father's orders pressed unread against his chest, whose face in the firelight held something that looked terribly like guilt wearing the mask of sympathy.

"You should read those orders," she told him, low, only for him. "Tonight. Before the past arrives faster than the mules."

Tomas met her eyes. "And if what's in them changes everything?"

"Then I'd rather know from you," Mirabel said, "than from the mountain."

She rose, rolled her strap around her fingers, and walked to the edge of the firelight, where the glacier's breath came down cold off the ice. Behind her the camp murmured, resettling, changed. Ahead, the pass waited under its snows with whatever her father had sealed inside it — and beside her, whether she willed it or no, the son of the man who had been there when her father fell.

Above the eastern ridge, the Old Man went dark.

* * *

She did not sleep. She told herself it was the altitude, and then she stopped lying to herself, because lying required energy she wanted for listening.

Past midnight, she heard Tomas move. Heard him cross the camp to the far rocks, where the wind broke and a man could open paper unheard. She gave him ten minutes. Then she followed, quiet as a surveyor, and stopped behind the boulder where the firelight couldn't reach.

He had the orders open. She saw the pale rectangle of the page, saw his face lit by the small shielded flame of a match he kept relighting, and she saw — she would swear afterward she saw — his shoulders change. The way a man's shoulders change when a weight becomes a different weight.

He read for a long time. Then he folded the page along its original creases, very carefully, the way one folds something that must look untouched, and he sat still in the dark for so long that she thought he might not move again until morning.

Mirabel withdrew the way she had come, silent, her heart beating hard and strange.

Whatever Elias Vane had written to his son, it was not a simple recovery order. And tomorrow they would reach the river fork, where the gorge narrowed and the real climbing began, and every mile upward would carry them all — the crew that stayed, the son who hesitated, the woman who counted distance instead of years — closer to a cairn of stones that held her father's last handwriting, and the truth of the night the mountain took him.

She stood in the cold and watched the stars wheel over the pass, and understood that the past had not waited politely at the trailhead to be dealt with on her terms.

It had been climbing with them all along.
