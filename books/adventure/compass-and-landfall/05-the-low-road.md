## 5. The Low Road

The gorge announced itself a mile before they reached it, as a sound like distant cloth tearing — the river working through stone it had no business moving. Tomas Vane reined his mule to the edge of the trail and looked down into the cut, and felt the same thing he always felt at the threshold of his father's projects: that he was entering a country where the rules he'd been raised on had been quietly repealed.

Below him, the Karagol River ran white and furious between walls of gray granite, sixty feet down and narrowing ahead until in places the two sides of the gorge nearly touched. The trail — if the word could be stretched that far — clung to the eastern wall like a scar, a shelf of broken rock barely wide enough for a loaded mule, dropping away on the left into water loud enough to swallow any cry for help.

Behind him, the Sterling crew was coming up the switchbacks in good order. He could hear them before he saw them: Odile Marchand's voice carrying over the noise of the river, low and even, correcting a muleteer's line with the economy of someone who never said anything twice. Then Mirabel Sterling herself rounded the last turn, and the whole procession came into view against the brown slope.

Tomas had been watching her for three days now, and he still hadn't settled what he was looking at.

He'd grown up on stories of Mirabel Sterling. His father told them at dinner when the whiskey was in him, and they were never quite the same story twice. In one version she was a prodigy who'd run her first survey line at nineteen and beaten Elias Vane to a copper strike in the Tavush hills by six days. In another she was a ghost who haunted the high country, cold as the passes, a woman who had buried her father in the Karagol and never once gone back to stand at the grave. In all of them she was brilliant, and in all of them she was alone, and his father always finished the telling the same way — with a slow smile and the observation that genius without warmth was just machinery, and machinery could be bought.

The woman climbing toward him now did not look like machinery. She looked tired, and competent, and watchful, and when her eyes found him at the trailhead she gave him a nod of exactly the length required to acknowledge his existence and no more.

"River route," she said, arriving beside his mule. Not a question. She was looking past him into the gorge, reading the water the way other people read a page.

"That's my recommendation," Tomas said. "The northern trail adds four days, and the weather window—"

"I know the weather window." She said it without heat, the way you'd correct a clerk's arithmetic. "I asked why you recommend the river."

Because my father's timetable demands it. Because every day we lose is a day the first snows gain. Because I have a sealed packet in my saddlebag with instructions I haven't read, and I am afraid of what happens when I do.

"The gorge is faster," he said instead, "and faster matters more than safer this late in the season."

Something moved behind her eyes — not agreement exactly. Assessment. "Faster matters more than safer," she repeated. "That's your father talking."

"It's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic has an author." She turned her mule down toward the trailhead. "Keep your people off the outer edge past the second bend. The shelf there is rotten. My father found that out twenty years ago, and so did I, and I'd rather neither of us proved it again."

She rode on before he could answer, and Tomas sat there a moment longer, listening to the river tear its cloth, wondering how a woman could invoke her dead father the way she did — flatly, precisely, like citing a survey mark — and give away nothing at all about what lay underneath.

* * *

They made the second bend by mid-afternoon, and the shelf was exactly as bad as she'd promised. The rock had shed its skin sometime in the intervening decades; the trail narrowed to eighteen inches of loose scree canted outward toward the drop, and the river below had organized itself into a single green tongue of current that sucked at the base of the wall with a sound like breathing.

Mirabel Sterling crossed it first, alone, testing each step with her staff, and then stood on the far side directing traffic while Odile fed the mules across one at a time, loads rebalanced, lead ropes doubled. It took two hours. Nobody spoke above the river except when necessary. Tomas watched the way the crew watched her — not the anxious watching of men waiting for orders, but the settled watching of men who trusted the order to already exist somewhere inside her — and he thought: my father would pay ten years of his life for a crew that looked at him like that.

His father had paid something else entirely, if half the old stories were true. Twenty years ago Elias Vane and Aldous Sterling had gone up into this same range together, rivals sharing a rope out of professional necessity, and only one of them had come down. The official account — the one in the journals, the one his father had let stand — was that Aldous Sterling fell during a storm on the upper glacier, that the expedition dissolved in grief, that the survey cache they'd established near the pass had been abandoned to the mountain. His father had built a reputation on the fragments Sterling left behind. He had spent two decades being the man who knew the Karagol better than anyone living, because the man who'd known it best was dead.

And now, twenty years later, Elias Vane had hired the dead man's daughter to go up and retrieve what the mountain had kept.

Tomas understood the surface logic. The cache held instruments, records, the original triangulation data from the first expedition — data his father needed to secure a claim before rival firms moved into the range. Hiring Mirabel Sterling was elegant: she knew her father's methods, she could read his field shorthand, and her name carried weight with the surveyors' guild. It was the kind of move his father made, the kind Tomas had admired since boyhood — turning an old wound into an instrument.

But there were things that didn't sit level. The contract specified the cache and nothing else. His father's letter announcing the partnership had been strangely specific about which members of the combined party would handle the recovered documents. And there was the packet in his saddlebag — oilskin-wrapped, sealed with wax, his father's seal pressed deep enough to leave dents in the leather beneath. *Read these at the gorge,* the covering note had said. *Not before. You'll want the whole picture.*

The whole picture. His father loved that phrase. It usually meant: the part I've decided you're ready for.

That evening they made camp on a gravel fan where a side creek came down to meet the river, and the two crews arranged themselves around fires with the careful diplomacy of nations sharing a border. The Sterling men kept to their own fire, mostly — Odile at the center of them, a broad gray-haired woman mending a pack strap with sinew and patience — but one of the young muleteers drifted over to the Vane fire asking after salt, and stayed for the conversation, and the border softened by a degree or two.

Tomas ate standing, the way he always did in new country, and watched Mirabel Sterling check the picket lines by lantern light. She moved through the camp the way water moves through cracks — everywhere at once, unobtrusive, finding the low places. When she passed the mules she touched each one's neck briefly, and he noticed that the animals turned their heads toward her hand.

*You'll want the whole picture.*

He walked out past the picket line, upstream along the side creek to where a fallen spruce made a bench above the water, and sat down with the packet in both hands. The wax broke cleanly. Inside were three pages in his father's upright, ruthless handwriting, and Tomas read them twice by the light of the moon on the creek, because the first time he couldn't believe them.

*Retrieve the cache intact. The instruments may be damaged; the documents may not. Everything recovered from the cairn comes to me sealed and unread — by anyone. Especially the field notes. Especially hers.*

*Burn anything else you find. Correspondence, personal effects, papers of any kind that are not survey records. Do not inventory them. Do not read them. Burn them where no one sees the smoke.*

*Tell no one what the notes contain. Not Marchand. Not your own men. And above all not Mirabel Sterling. If she asks what the documents concern, you will tell her they are triangulation records and nothing more. This is not a request, Tomas. This is the job.*

He sat with the pages for a long time. The creek talked to itself over its stones. Somewhere upslope a mule shifted and stamped.

*Especially hers.* His father had never met Mirabel Sterling — Tomas was nearly certain of it; the letters between the two houses had been formal and infrequent his whole life — and yet here was the possessive, dropped into instructions written for a retrieval operation like a knife left point-up on a table. His father knew what was in those notes. Had known, apparently, for twenty years. And whatever it was, it was important enough to burn other things over, important enough to lie about, important enough that the truth of it must never reach the daughter of Aldous Sterling.

There were only so many things a man might need hidden from a dead rival's daughter. Tomas turned them over one by one and disliked each of them. That the notes implicated his father somehow. That the fall — the storm, the grief, the clean tragedy his father had described at dinners with a sorrowful shake of the head — was not what the official account said. That the man who came down the mountain had done something up there that the mountain had witnessed, and the notes were the mountain's testimony.

Or — and this was the thought he flinched from, the thought he set down and picked up again like a hot coal — that the notes concerned Mirabel herself. That she was in them. That his father's interest in hiring her was not elegance but containment.

He looked back toward the camp. The fires had burned down to coals. At the nearest one, Mirabel Sterling had finally sat down, a tin cup in her hands, and Odile Marchand was saying something to her that made the younger men laugh, though Mirabel herself didn't quite — she smiled the way a person smiles in a language learned late, correctly but a half-beat behind.

*Tell no one what the notes contain.*

Here was the thing Tomas could not stop circling: he had spent his whole life obeying his father, and he had never once minded, because his father's instructions had always been legible. Ambitious, sometimes ruthless, but legible — aimed at some visible summit, explicable afterward even when they stung. These instructions were not legible. These instructions asked him to walk into the mountains beside a woman whose father had died there, carry out her father's last papers, and lie to her face about their contents. There was no summit in sight. There was only his father's voice saying *this is the job*, in the tone that ended discussions.

He folded the pages along their original creases. His hands were steady, which surprised him.

He should burn the instructions now — that was standard practice, his father insisted on it, no paper trail in the field. He held one corner to the coals of his own small fire and watched the edge catch, flame crawling up the first page, and stopped it there, pinching the flame out between thumb and forefinger before it reached the words.

The pain was sharp and ordinary and grounding. He looked at the scorched corner, at the black lace of char, at the sentence beneath it still legible: *This is not a request, Tomas.*

"Not yet," he said aloud, to the creek, to nobody.

It was the first time in twenty-nine years he had said *not yet* to Elias Vane, even alone, even in a whisper, and the sound of it frightened him more than the gorge had.

* * *

Morning came up gray and cold, the peaks above the gorge wearing fresh snow like a warning delivered early. They struck camp in the blue dark and were moving by first light, the trail climbing away from the river now, the roar fading below them into a constant undertone, the way a threat becomes a fact.

Tomas rode point for the Vane contingent, and somewhere in the middle of the morning Mirabel Sterling drew level with him and rode there, uninvited, matching his pace. For a long while she said nothing. She was looking up-valley, at the line of the range, and he followed her gaze and saw what she saw: the Karagol massif rising tier on tier, granite and ice, the high passes still white but the lower shoulders bare and brown, and above everything, far up at the head of the valley, a summit he recognized from his father's maps — the peak the first expedition had been climbing toward when it ended.

"You've been quiet since the gorge," she said at last.

"So have you."

"I'm paid to be quiet. You're not." She glanced at him. "Your father's orders. Did you read them?"

The question landed with the precision of everything she did. He felt the lie assemble itself in his mouth — *yes, routine stuff, timetables and supply manifests* — and heard, underneath it, the other instruction: *tell no one what the notes contain.* Technically, orders about the notes. Technically, he could answer questions about the orders however he liked.

Technically.

"They're thorough," he said.

Her mouth curved, not quite a smile. "Thorough. That's a word men use when the contents are embarrassing."

"My father isn't embarrassed by anything."

"No," she agreed. "I imagine shame requires imagining how you look from outside yourself." She said it mildly, almost absently, the way she'd corrected his arithmetic on the first day, and then she lifted her chin toward the peak at the head of the valley. "We'll make the confluence camp by tomorrow night if the weather holds. After that, the real climbing starts. Your father's timetable gives us eleven days to reach the pass. Mine gives us nine, and a day in reserve."

"Then we'll use yours."

"I know," she said. "I watched you recalculate the load distribution last night after everyone slept. You used my factors." She nudged her mule forward a step, drawing ahead, and threw the last of it back over her shoulder like a coin tossed to a beggar: "For what it's worth, Vane — whatever's in that packet of yours, you went pale reading it, and you've been riding like a man with a stone in his boot ever since. When you decide to say what it is, I'll listen. Until then, keep your people off my rope teams. I don't trust what I can't read, and right now you're the least legible thing in this outfit."

She rode on. Tomas sat his mule in the middle of the trail and watched her go, and felt the scorched corner of his father's letter pressing through the oilskin against his ribs like a brand.

Above them, far up the valley, a wind came off the high snowfields and dragged a plume of white off the summit — the mountain clearing its throat, or counting its guests. Eleven days, his father's timetable said. Nine, and a day in reserve, said hers.

And somewhere under the snow at the head of that valley, in a cairn twenty years silent, her father's notes waited — holding whatever it was that had made Elias Vane seal his own son's orders in wax, and made Tomas, for the first time in his life, hesitate at the edge of obedience like a man at a rotten shelf, testing whether the ground would hold.
