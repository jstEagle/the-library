## 6. Riverbend

The river told them a day early, if anyone had been listening.

Mirabel had been listening. She'd grown up on rivers the way other children grew up on lullabies — her father's voice in the dark of a hundred tents: *A river is a ledger, Mira. Every tributary an entry. Read it and it will never surprise you.* So when she stopped at midmorning and crouched at the water's edge instead of pushing on, Odile knew better than to ask why.

"Level's down," Mirabel said.

"It rained two nights ago."

"Upstream, then. Something's holding it back." She let a handful of water run through her fingers, cold enough to ache. "Snowmelt dammed in a gorge somewhere above us. Ice shelf, rockfall, doesn't matter. When it lets go, everything it's been saving comes down at once."

Tomas Vane had come up alongside them, silent as his father's letters. He looked at the river the way he seemed to look at most things — like a man doing arithmetic he didn't intend to share. "How long?"

"That's not how water works. It isn't a schedule." She stood, brushed grit from her knee. "But we're in the narrows for six more miles, and the supply train walks slow. I want the mules through the worst of it by tonight."

"The worst of it being?"

She pointed upstream, to where the gorge pinched between walls of gray granite and the river went white and furious through a gap no wider than a wagon. "That. If that bend floods, we lose the train. Everything — rope, flour, the stove plates, the medicine chest. We'd be eating boot leather and praying by Thursday."

Vane studied the bend. Then he did something she hadn't expected: he took out his notebook and drew it. Quick lines, sure hand, the profile of the gorge rendered in a dozen strokes. He was good. That annoyed her more than it should have.

"My father's timetable has us camping below the narrows," he said.

"I know what your father's timetable says."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile, not quite its opposite. "Then I'll say it plainly, since you won't: I think you're right and he's wrong. The low ground under that bend is a trap. I've been wanting to say so since dawn."

It was the first time he'd contradicted the wax-sealed orders he carried like scripture, and he'd done it to agree with *her*. Mirabel filed that away with the other small anomalies of Tomas Vane — the way he watched her crew work, the way he'd learned their names by the second day, the way he never once said *my father says* without a half-second pause, like a man checking whether the words still fit.

"We camp above the bend," she said. "High terrace, north side. Move the train now, while the light's good."

Odile's whistle cut the valley, and the crews came together like gears meshing — twenty-two people, forty-one mules, and eleven days of their lives strung along a rope of pack-frames. Mirabel rode point. Vane, unasked, took the drag position at the rear, where the trouble always started first.

The river stayed quiet all afternoon. That was the thing about rivers, Mirabel thought. They were patient liars.

* * *

They made the high terrace an hour before dusk, and Mirabel allowed herself ten minutes of feeling wrong about it. The camp sat pretty as a picture above the bend — too pretty, too safe, the kind of ground that made a person feel clever right up until the mountain proved otherwise. Below them the gorge muttered. Above them the peaks held their snow like a secret.

She was checking the mule pickets a second time when the sound came.

Not loud. That was the horror of it. A deep, structural groan from up-valley, felt in the soles of her boots before it reached her ears, like something enormous shifting its weight in its sleep. Then a silence with a texture to it — the river's muttering simply *stopping* — and every hair on her arms stood up, because a river that goes silent is a river that has somewhere else to be.

"ODILE!" Her voice cracked across the camp. "Water! Get them off the low side — NOW!"

The terrace wasn't low. But the trail they'd climbed by switchbacked down toward the bend, and the mules were picketed along that edge, and the flood, when it came around the corner of the gorge, would come looking for exactly that kind of easy ground.

It came like a wall that had decided to become a wave. Mirabel saw it first as motion in her peripheral vision — the whole canyon mouth surging, brown and violent, carrying whole trees upright like matchsticks, ice blocks the size of tables grinding against each other with a sound like teeth. The roar arrived a heartbeat later and erased every other sound in the world.

The near picket line went first. Three mules, screaming, dragged sideways as the leading tongue of the flood licked up the trail edge and found the lowest of the pickets. Odile was already moving, knife out, cutting the anchor line rather than fight it — but the third mule panicked, tore free of the frame, and bolted *down* the switchback with the cook tent's entire winter store strapped to its back.

And below it, cutting wood at the trailside where he shouldn't have been, was Pell, the youngest of Vane's men, seventeen if he was a day, standing frozen with an armful of kindling and his mouth open at the wall of water coming for him.

Mirabel didn't decide anything. Later she would reconstruct it in fragments: her boots on the switchback, taking the turns at a speed that should have broken her ankle; the mule ahead of her, white-eyed, hauling its burden down toward the water; Pell rooted to the spot like something planted. She got one hand in his collar and threw him bodily against the rock wall, pinned him there as the flood's leading spray hit them both like thrown gravel — and the mule, mercifully, shied from the roaring brown chaos and scrambled back up the slope, bawling, stores intact.

Which left the far side of the trail, where two of *her* mules — the ones carrying the survey instruments and the medical chest — had been picketed lower than she'd ordered, because Harkness had misread her hand signal in the failing light of the move. They were on a shelf of ground already going soft, water sheeting over their hocks, and there was no way down to them that didn't cross thirty feet of ground the river now owned.

Someone crossed it anyway.

She saw him come down the switchback on the outside of the railing-less edge, taking a line she would have called impossible, and she had time to think *that fool* before she recognized the set of the shoulders. Tomas Vane waded into the sheet-flow, hip-deep, leaning into the current at an angle her father would have drawn in a textbook, and got one hand on the first mule's halter.

After that it wasn't bravery, it was geometry. Mirabel came down the other side, took the second halter, and they worked the animals up-slope together — and this was the part she would remember years later, the part that kept surfacing at odd moments like a coin found in old clothing: they didn't speak. Not one word. He'd brace, she'd pull; she'd turn the mule's head, he'd take the lead rope over a rock horn and make fast. Two strangers' worth of instinct fitting together like a single mechanism someone had built years ago and never tested until tonight. The second mule came off the shelf four seconds before the shelf itself went, peeled away into the brown torrent like a page torn out.

They got the animals tied high, and then the flood did what floods do — spent its fury in twenty minutes and began, insultingly, to subside as though nothing had happened. The river resumed its muttering. Stars came out over the gorge, indifferent and clean.

Pell found Mirabel by the fire, still shaking, and tried to say something about owing her his life. She waved it off. "You owe me better firewood placement. Trailside, below the high-water mark? What were you thinking?"

He laughed, wet-eyed, and fled, and she let him.

Across the fire, Tomas Vane was wringing out his boots by the flames, and when he caught her looking he raised the tin cup someone had pressed into his hands — a salute, wordless again. She almost returned it. Almost.

*You saved my mules,* she thought. *My father's rival's son saved my father's daughter's mules, and neither of us said a word about it, and I don't know yet what that makes us.*

* * *

She woke past midnight to a smell she knew too well: paper burning.

Not the cook fire — this was sharper, inkish, the smell of a letter or a page giving itself up. The camp slept in the deep hush that follows catastrophe survived, even the watchman nodding against a rock. Mirabel rose without a sound, the way twenty years of solo camps had taught her, and followed the smell upwind to the rocks at the terrace's edge.

Tomas Vane sat with his back to a boulder, a small flame cupped between his palms, feeding it strips of paper one at a time. He burned each piece separately, waited for it to catch fully, watched it die. Careful. Methodical. A man destroying a document the way a surveyor paces a distance — in measured, deniable increments.

She stepped into the firelight. "Half a page burns louder than you think, up here. No river noise to cover it."

He didn't startle — credit where due — but the flame stilled, and in its light she saw his face do something complicated. "Couldn't sleep."

"People who can't sleep count stars. They don't burn correspondence in increments." She crouched across the flame from him, close enough to see the ash. "I've pulled three pieces out of the wind-trap behind that boulder already. Enough to know it's your father's hand. Enough to know the words *cache* and *no one* survived the draft stage."

His jaw worked. For a long moment the only sound was the fire eating.

"You saved my mules tonight," he said finally.

"And you saved my man. Neither of those purchases buys you a lie, Vane. They buy you the chance to tell me the truth while I'm still inclined to believe it."

He looked at her then, straight on, and she saw the war in him plainly — the son and the man fighting over the same ground, neither willing to yield. "There are orders," he said. "Sealed. From my father. I read them five days ago, in the gorge."

"I know. I've watched you carry them like a stone in your pocket."

"They say retrieve the cache. They say—" His voice caught on the next part, and she watched him choose, visibly, deliberately, to give her only the portion he could give. "They say some of what's in that cache isn't meant for anyone's eyes but his. And that anything else — any papers beyond the cache manifest — should be destroyed unread."

"Burned," she said quietly. "Like that page."

"Like that page." He fed the last strip to the flame and watched it go. "I've obeyed the first instruction. I'm deciding about the rest."

"Why burn it tonight? After the river. After—" she gestured back toward the camp, the sleeping crews, the strange machinery they'd become in the water. "Why not ride home and hand him whatever he asked for?"

"Because tonight I watched you throw yourself at a flood for a boy who works for my father, and I watched your crew cut their own picket lines rather than risk a mule, and I thought—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I thought my father wrote those orders before he ever met these people. And orders written blind shouldn't survive contact, any more than a route drawn blind should."

Mirabel sat back on her heels. Half the truth, he'd given her — the shape of it, the weight of it, without the contents. A sealed order, a forbidden reading, a father who wanted pages destroyed unread. Twenty years ago another man had gone up this range carrying papers, and the question of what was in them had killed him as surely as the fall did.

"Your father hired me," she said slowly, "knowing whose daughter I am. Knowing what that cache might hold. And he sent you — his own son — with instructions to keep certain papers from existing." She met his eyes across the small defiant flame. "What's in that cache, Tomas? What is Elias Vane so afraid I'll read?"

And here was the thing she would turn over for nights afterward: he didn't lie to her. He opened his mouth, and she watched him decide against it — watched the lie form and die somewhere behind his teeth.

"I don't know," he said. "That's the truth, Mirabel. I haven't read them. But my father sealed them twice, and he's never sealed anything twice in his life except the things he can't afford to be wrong about."

The fire guttered down to embers between them. Somewhere below, the river ran on, carrying the mountain's melted secrets to the sea, keeping none of them.

"Keep your half-truths," Mirabel said at last, standing. "But hear me, Vane. When we reach that cairn — and we will reach it — nothing gets burned before I've seen it. Not by your orders, not by your father's, not by love or duty or any rope that binds you. Whatever my father left in that ice, I'm the one who reads it first. That's not a request."

He looked up at her, ember-light in his eyes, and nodded once — a man signing something.

Above the gorge, far up the black valley, the high snowfields caught the moon and threw it back in a long white gleam, like a page turning. Eleven days, the timetable said. Nine, said hers.

And now, folded in a wax-sealed packet against Tomas Vane's ribs, lay the answer to a question both of them were finally asking aloud — waiting at the end of the route like the cache itself, sealed twice, and colder than the ice that kept it.
