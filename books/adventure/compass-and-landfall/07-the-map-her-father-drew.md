## 7. The Map Her Father Drew

The gorge narrowed on the third morning until the walls pressed close enough that Mirabel could have touched both sides with a mule-length of rope between them. The river ran loud and green below the trail, and the light came down in slats, and for two hours nobody spoke because there was no room in the canyon for anything but the next step.

She liked country like this. It asked nothing of her but attention.

It was when they climbed out of the narrows at midday and the valley opened into a long bench of pine and scree that the trouble started — not the loud kind, not yet, but the quiet kind that lived in her own chest and had been growing since Riverbend.

Tomas had given her half the truth by firelight: his father's orders said retrieve the cache, burn what remained, tell no one. He had not told her what *else* the orders might say, and she had not pushed him, because pushing a man on a rope team was how you put everyone in the river. But she had spent the night turning it over, and by dawn she had done what she always did when a problem would not sit still: she had gone to the survey data.

They made camp early on the bench — Odile's call, over Tomas's mild objection that they had light left, and Mirabel had backed her foreman without explanation, which earned her a look from Tomas that she pretended not to see. The crews raised the tents in their usual wordless choreography. Halvard set the cook fire going. The mules were hobbled and rubbed down. And Mirabel sat alone on a flat rock at the edge of camp with her field books open across her knees, comparing three days of compass bearings against the route Elias Vane had laid out in his contract.

The bearings matched.

That was not the strange part. A route up a gorge was a route up a gorge; there were only so many ways through the Karagol from the south, and any competent surveyor would have chosen this one. What was strange was the *corrections*. Vane's written itinerary called for a camp on the north bank of the river at a bend where the water cut hard against the cliff. Mirabel's crew had camped on the south bank instead, higher up, because Odile had looked at the cut bank and said one word — *"flood"* — and Mirabel had agreed without thinking about why she'd agreed so fast.

And then the flash flood had come down the north fork in the night, exactly where a camp on the north bank would have been sleeping.

Vane hadn't known about the flood. Nobody could have known about that particular flood. But somebody had once known that the north bank at that bend was bad ground, bad enough to mark it, bad enough to teach a child to avoid it — and that somebody had been dead for twenty years.

Mirabel closed her field book. Sat very still. Below her, the river went on saying the same thing it had been saying all week.

*You already know where he learned it,* she thought. *You've known since you were twelve.*

* * *

Her father had sewn maps into her clothes.

Not charts — she'd understood even then that these weren't charts, weren't meant for navigation, weren't meant for anyone's eyes but hers. They were small things, ink on linen, worked in his cramped left-slanting hand, folded to the size of a playing card and stitched into the linings of her jackets where no tailor would ever find them without cutting the seams. He'd done it four times that she knew of, starting the winter her mother died, when he'd begun to look at Mirabel sometimes as if she were the only other person in a country of one.

*In case,* he'd said, when she was eleven and had found the first one by accident, a pinprick of blue ink bleeding through the lining of her coat cuff. *In case you ever need to come find me.*

*Where would I be finding you?*

*Anywhere,* he'd said, and laughed, and ruffled her hair, and gone back to his tables. *Anywhere at all.*

The second map had gone into a jacket he gave her for her twelfth birthday — the last birthday they'd spent together, though neither of them could have known it. She remembered the evening precisely: the smell of paraffin and wet wool in the expedition house at the foot of the Karagol, her father hunched under the lamp with a needle in his teeth, cursing cheerfully at his own clumsiness while she watched from the bunk. He'd held the jacket out to her inside-out, the new seam crooked along the left breast.

*There. Now you carry the range with you.*

*Why would I need the range?*

*Because I'm going to be up in it,* he'd said, *and fathers are foolish, and if I'm late coming down I want you to know every way there is to come looking.* Then, quieter, in the voice he used only for true things: *I drew you the way through. Not the way the expeditions go. My way.*

She had worn that jacket for six years after, until the elbows went and the cuffs frayed and she retired it to the bottom of a trunk in a boarding house in Peshawar, because a working surveyor couldn't afford sentiment in her kit. She had not opened the trunk in fourteen years. She had never once taken the jacket out. But she had never thrown it away either, and she had carried the trunk itself across three continents and two wars' worth of border crossings, telling herself each time that it was because the trunk also held her mother's letters, which was true, and that the jacket was incidental, which was a lie so old it had worn smooth as river stone.

She knew the map by heart anyway. That was the thing. She had read it a hundred times as a girl, tracing the inked line with one finger while her father slept: the southern gorge, the bench of pines, the bend where the north fork came in — marked with a small careful symbol she had never seen in any textbook, a triangle with its point driven downward, which she understood, even at twelve, to mean *bad ground, do not camp*. Above the bench the line climbed west away from the main trail, through a notch below a peak her father had named, in the map's tiny margin, *the Old Man's Tooth*. And above that, drawn in a different, shakier hand — added later, she'd realized much later, after the map was already sewn in — a short dashed line running up to a small square with a cross in it, and beside the square, in letters so small she'd needed the lamp held close:

*C. — moved it. Only M. will look here. Forgive me.*

C. for cache. M. for Mirabel.

She had been twelve years old and had understood none of it except that her father loved her, which she had known, and that he was afraid of something, which she had not let herself know.

* * *

The fire was burning low when she finally went to her kit.

She did it methodically, the way she did everything, because method was the only armor that had never failed her. She waited until Halvard had turned in and Odile was on first watch and the camp had settled into the breathing rhythm of sleep — snores, mule-shift, wind in the pines. Then she knelt at her pack, unbuckled the flap, and lifted out the oilskin bundle at the bottom that contained, officially, her spare shirt and unofficially, the folded mass of a jacket twenty years out of fashion and thirty years too heavy for the altitude.

She carried it to the far side of the rock where she'd sat with her field books, out of the firelight, and turned it inside-out by feel.

The seam along the left breast was still crooked. Of course it was. Nothing else in her life had stayed where she'd left it; why should that stitch have moved?

She took out her knife and cut it.

The linen packet came free in one piece, stiff with age, soft as moth wing at the folds. She unfolded it under the stars, angling it to catch the last grey light off the snowfields, and there it was — the whole range, the whole childhood, the whole crime, drawn in her father's hand: the gorge, the bench, the downward-pointing triangle at the flood bend where Vane's itinerary had put a camp, the climb away west below the Old Man's Tooth, the dashed line, the crossed square.

*Only M. will look here.*

Every bearing matched the route they had walked for three days. Not approximately. Exactly. Vane's contract route was her father's route, correction for correction, avoidance for avoidance — including the flood bend, including a detour around a rockfall gully they'd scrambled past yesterday that Vane's written plan had routed straight through, including a spring marked with a small circle that their muleteer had found by smell two days ago and that appeared nowhere on any published chart of the range.

Elias Vane had not planned this route. Elias Vane could not have planned this route, because half of it existed nowhere but in the linen lining of a dead man's daughter's jacket.

Which meant Vane had never expected to follow his own itinerary at all.

Which meant the itinerary was bait, same as the wages, same as the telegram, same as the page of her father's handwriting he'd tucked into his second letter like a fisherman chumming water. Vane had written a route he knew she would correct. He had counted on her competence — on the specific, inherited, unrepeatable competence of a woman who had a stolen map stitched into her skin. He hadn't hired Sterling Surveys despite who Mirabel was.

He had hired her *because* of it. Because her father had moved the cache and left a riddle, and the riddle's only key was a twelve-year-old girl who was now forty-something and standing in the cold holding the lock.

The wind came down off the snowfields and moved the linen in her hands, and for a moment the little map breathed like something alive, and Mirabel felt the full weight of what she was holding: not a document. A message. Twenty years old, addressed to her, delivered at last by the one man alive with motive enough to spend a fortune getting it into her hands.

*He didn't want my crew,* she thought. *He wanted my eyes. He wanted the part of my father I've been trying to bury since I was eighteen.*

She thought of Vane's letter — the single page of her father's handwriting, unmistakable, intimate, chosen. She had assumed it was cruelty, or leverage, or an old man's spite. It was all of those. But it was also a test. *Show me you still recognize him,* the page had said. *Show me the key still fits.*

She folded the map along its old creases. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Somewhere behind her ribs, less steadily, something was coming apart — the clean story she had built in Utah and burned telegrams to protect: that this was business, that she was only the surveyor, that the past was a country she had emigrated from and owed no taxes to.

Odile knew. Odile had been on the original expedition and had said so around the fire, and Mirabel had forgiven her for the silence because there had been no choice in it — Vane had bought their silence with winter wages before Mirabel ever saw a telegram. But this was different. This was Vane building a trap out of her own inheritance and calling it a contract, and letting her walk into it step by corrected step, and she had walked willingly, gratefully, *relieved* to have a reason to come back to the range she'd sworn off —

Footsteps on scree. Soft ones, deliberate, the walk of someone announcing themselves.

She didn't startle. She folded the map into her palm and turned.

Tomas stood at the edge of the firelight with his bedroll under one arm, and she watched him take in the scene — her kneeling, the jacket inside-out beside her, the knife still open in the scree — and she watched him decide, visibly, to ask nothing.

"Your watch," he said. "Odile's turning in. She said to tell you the sky's wrong."

"The sky's wrong."

"Cirrus coming up from the southwest, thick and fast. She says it's too early in the season for it and she doesn't like it." He paused. "I told her weather doesn't keep your schedule."

"It doesn't keep anyone's schedule. That's the whole lesson of weather." Mirabel rose, sliding the folded linen into her breast pocket — the left one, over the crooked seam, where it had lived for twenty years in a different form. "We'll take the high ground tomorrow ahead of it. There's a notch below a peak the muleteers are calling the fang — we passed it today. Good shelter above treeline if we need it."

"You know a shelter above treeline on a route you've never run?"

"I know this route," Mirabel said.

Something moved through his face — not suspicion exactly; he was past pretending he wasn't curious about her, and she was past pretending she didn't notice. "From the old surveys," he said. "Your father's."

"Among others."

He nodded slowly, and she understood that he was filing it away next to the burned page and the half-truth he'd given her at the river, building his own map of her out of fragments, the way she was building hers of him. Two cartographers, she thought, surveying each other in the dark. Neither willing yet to publish.

"Get some sleep," she said. "If Odile's right about that sky, tomorrow will be long."

He hesitated. "Mirabel." Her name in his mouth still sounded new, like a place neither of them had properly surveyed. "Whatever you're carrying in that pocket — my father sent us both up here for reasons he didn't put in writing. When you're ready to compare notes—"

"I'll let you know," she said, "when I know whose notes they are."

He almost smiled at that. Then he went, and the camp swallowed him, and Mirabel stood alone under the wrongness of the sky with her father's map against her heart.

Below the notch, in the dark, the Old Man's Tooth caught the starlight like a blade. Tomorrow they would climb beneath it. Somewhere above, past treeline, past the storm that was even now assembling itself over the southwestern ranges, a cairn waited with her name hidden inside it — and a man down-valley had spent twenty years and a fortune making sure that she, and no one else, would be the one to dig.

She had spent two decades believing she'd escaped being her father's daughter.

Elias Vane had known better all along. He hadn't hired a surveyor.

He had hired the map.

Mirabel looked up at the cirrus streaming in from the southwest, thin and high and fast, feathers pulled across the stars, and felt the first cold edge of what Odile had already smelled: the season turning early, the mountain closing its hand.

They would need to move at first light. Whatever waited above treeline, they would reach it sooner than anyone had reckoned — and so, she suspected, would Vane.
