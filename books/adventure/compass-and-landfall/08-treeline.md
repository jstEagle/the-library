## 8. Treeline

The trees gave out the way a voice gives out at the end of a long speech — not all at once, but in failing phrases, stunted pines twisted sideways by wind, then scrub willow flattened to the ground like hair combed flat, then nothing at all but stone and lichen and the thin whistle of air that had crossed a hundred miles of ice without touching anything warm.

Tomas Vane had been above treeline before. He'd crossed three passes with his father's expeditions before he was old enough to shave, and he knew the particular loneliness of it, the way the world above the last tree felt less like a place and more like an argument the earth was having with itself. But he had never climbed into that country with two crews who hated each other's timetables, forty-one mules' worth of supplies, and a woman ahead of him on the trail who walked like she was trying to outrun something wearing her own name.

He watched Mirabel Sterling from twenty yards back, the way he had been watching her for nine days.

She moved well. That was the thing nobody warned you about — that competence could be beautiful, and worse, that you could notice it. She didn't walk so much as read the ground, each step placed where the rock would hold, her eyes always two moves ahead of her feet. Odile Marchand had taught her crew the same grammar; they moved as a sentence, no wasted words. His father's men moved like a committee arguing about punctuation.

The sky was the problem. It had been the problem since dawn, and only Tomas seemed willing to say so out loud.

It wasn't a storm sky yet. That was what made it dangerous. It was a *thinking* sky, high cloud boiling up over the western summits in towers the color of dirty wool, building faster than any September sky had a right to build. The air had gone still and heavy, the kind of stillness that pressed on your eardrums. The mules felt it first — they'd been jumpy since noon, ears swiveling back at nothing.

His father's timetable said they should make the Col de la Fenêtre by tomorrow night. The orders were specific: cross above the snowline before the equinox storms, reach the cache cairn by the twentieth, descend by the southern route before the passes closed. Every day accounted for. Every camp pre-named in Elias Vane's tight, certain hand.

But his father had written those dates in a study in Meridian City, off maps drawn thirty years ago by men who hadn't come back to correct them.

Tomas looked west again. The cloud towers had doubled since noon.

*You know what he'd say,* he told himself. *He'd say weather is an excuse made by weak leaders.* He'd said it often enough, usually while looking directly at whoever had raised the objection, in that voice that made disagreement feel like a personal failure. *The mountain doesn't care about your comfort. Neither do I.*

Ahead, Mirabel stopped. She stood on a shelf of granite with the whole western sky behind her, and even at this distance Tomas saw her go very still — the stillness of a woman listening to something the rest of them couldn't hear yet.

Then she turned and raised one arm, and Odile's crew began, without a word being passed, to look for camp.

* * *

The argument happened at four o'clock, on a bench of scree above a frozen tarn, and it happened fast, the way all real arguments do — no preamble, just the collision.

"We're making the col," Halvard said. He was his father's lead climber, a big Swede with a beard full of ice crystals and twenty years of Elias Vane's opinions stored up behind his eyes. "Vane's schedule has us crossing tomorrow afternoon. We lose a day now, we're climbing in the storm window."

"The storm window opened this morning." Mirabel didn't raise her voice. She never did, Tomas was learning. She saved volume the way other people saved money. "Look west. Look at the lenticulars forming over the shoulder of the massif. That's a foehn wall building, and when it breaks we'll have sixty-mile-an-hour wind and whiteout inside of an hour. We shelter here tonight, take the col in the morning clear."

"There won't be a morning clear. There'll be a morning storm, and then another, and then the passes close and we're spending winter on the wrong side of the range."

"Then we spend it alive."

"Your father would have pushed on."

The silence that followed had weight enough to bend the tent poles.

Tomas saw it land — saw the small flinch Mirabel didn't quite suppress, the way her jaw set, the way Odile Marchand took half a step forward with the unhurried menace of a woman who had been waiting nine days for someone to say exactly that. The Sterling crew went quiet in a way that wasn't deference. It was the quiet of a loaded weapon.

"Her father," Odile said, "is dead."

"I didn't mean—" Halvard began.

"You meant it. Vane's crew always means it." Odile wasn't tall, but she occupied space the way a keel occupies water. "Twenty years I've heard that sentence used like a whip. *Your father would have pushed on. Your father would have known better. Your father wouldn't have turned back.* You want to know what pushing on got him? Ask the Karagol Range. It kept him."

"Odile." Mirabel's voice was quiet. "That's enough."

"It isn't, but I'll stop."

Mirabel turned back to Halvard, and Tomas watched her do the thing she did — gather herself, fold the flinch away somewhere deep, become the legend instead of the woman. "Halvard. I've run survey lines on three continents. I have turned back exactly twice, and both times the people who called me cautious are under crosses somewhere. I'm not asking your opinion of my courage. I'm telling you the sky's opinion. We camp here."

"And if Vane asks why his contract lost a day?"

"Then Vane can ask me."

"He will."

"I know," Mirabel said. "I'm counting on it."

Halvard looked past her — and here it came, Tomas thought, here is where it turns — to the Vane contingent, to the son carrying the sealed orders, to the man whose signature held the purse strings of this entire enterprise.

Everyone looked at Tomas.

He had known, in some cold corner of himself, that this moment was coming. He'd felt it building for days, the way you feel a tooth going bad. His whole life had been an education in this exact posture: the son, standing in the wind, expected to be the echo. His father's voice lived in his head with terrible fidelity — every maxim, every contemptuous little laugh, every lesson delivered on ridgelines and riverbanks across twenty years. *A leader commits. A leader doesn't pollute his judgment with other men's fears.*

And Mirabel Sterling was looking at him too, and her face gave away nothing, because of course it didn't, because she had built that face out of granite years ago and mortared every crack. But her eyes — her eyes were doing something they almost never did. They were asking. Not pleading. Asking, the way you ask a witness, not a judge. As if his answer mattered to her as his answer, not his father's.

The cloud towers over the western massif rolled and stood up taller.

"We camp," Tomas said.

The word came out of him sounding strange, like someone else's voice played back at the wrong speed. He heard it leave his mouth and felt the ground shift under the whole expedition.

"Tomas." Halvard's tone dropped into warning. "Your father's orders—"

"My father's orders assume his dates are right. They aren't." He heard himself keep going, helpless as a man on ice. "He wrote this schedule against maps from before the last glaciation survey. He's guessing and calling it certainty, and we don't die for his certainty. We camp here, we double the anchor lines, and we take the col at first light if the sky gives us first light. If it doesn't, we wait. That's not cowardice. That's arithmetic."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, a mule complained, a thin bray swallowed by the thinning air.

Halvard held his gaze for a long five seconds, and Tomas understood that something had been decided between them that neither of them would ever name aloud — that the boy had become, in front of witnesses, a man with his own judgment, and that Halvard would obey him now and report everything later, and that both facts would arrive in his father's hands together.

"Double anchors," Halvard said finally, to the crew, not to Tomas. "Move."

* * *

They built the camp like people expecting a siege, which, Tomas thought, driving the last piton with numb hands, was exactly what they were doing. Two rope lines strung low between boulders. Tents pitched in the lee of a granite spine, kitchen fly doubled. The mules hobbled close and packed flank to flank, their breath steaming in the killing light of late afternoon, which had gone gold and strange, the sun showing through the western cloud like a coin behind smoked glass.

Mirabel worked the line beside him without speaking. That was another thing he'd learned about her: she could be silent in a way that included you rather than excluded you. Nine days of it. Nine days of hauling rope and reading water and sharing watch in the blue hours, and the silence between them had gotten so comfortable it frightened him more than the sky did.

"You sided against your own timetable," she said at last, coiling rope. Not looking at him.

"I sided against a bad forecast."

"That's not how it will read in Meridian City."

"No," Tomas said. "It isn't."

She glanced at him then, quick, and there was something in her face he couldn't parse — not gratitude exactly, or not only. Something warier and warmer at once, like a fire seen through a shuttered window. "Why?" she asked. "Genuinely. Your father's orders are explicit. I've watched you guard them like scripture for nine days. Why break ranks over a cloud?"

Because of the page I burned at Riverbend, he thought. Because I've read half of what my father wants buried up here, and every word of it is about you. Because I watched you pull Sefu out of the floodwater and then stand in the rain shaking afterward where you thought no one could see, and I understood that everything my father told me about you — the coldness, the curse, the ambition — was a story he needed true, and I am so tired of living inside his stories.

What he said was: "The sky doesn't care whose name is on the contract. When it breaks, it takes Vane men and Sterling men at the same rate. I'd rather be wrong in a tent than right on a ridge."

She studied him a moment longer, and whatever she was looking for, she seemed to find enough of it, because she nodded once and went back to the rope.

But as she turned away, she said, quietly, almost to herself: "For what it's worth — your father would have pushed on too."

Tomas's hands went still on the coil.

"And I'd have argued with him until dark," Mirabel finished, "same as I argued with everyone else who ever confused stubbornness with leadership. So don't measure yourself against his ghost. It's a bad map. I know. I've been navigating by one for twenty years."

She walked off toward the kitchen fly, and Tomas stood alone with the rope and the rising wind and the extraordinary, disorienting feeling of having been seen — accurately, unsentimentally, by the one person on this mountain with the best reasons to hate his name.

* * *

The storm arrived at midnight, three hours early, the way storms do when they've been insulted by a schedule.

Tomas was on second watch when the pressure dropped so fast his ears popped and the wind came off the western col like a door thrown open in hell. Snow didn't fall so much as travel horizontally, a white river moving at the speed of a galloping horse. He got the last mule blanket cinched down and made the rounds of the anchor lines with his lantern guttering, checking knots with fingers gone stupid with cold, and at the edge of the lantern light he found Mirabel already out there, hooded and roped, working the line in the dark.

They didn't speak. There was no speaking in that wind. She checked his knots; he checked hers. Then they stood together for a moment in the roaring white, backs to the gale, two small shapes on a shelf of stone at the top of the world, and Tomas thought: *this is the side I chose, and I chose it before tonight, and I will never tell my father, and I think I may already have told her.*

Behind them, the tents strained and held. Ahead, invisible, the col waited under its new load of snow — and beyond the col, three days hard climbing, the cache cairn, and everything his father had sent him up this mountain to bury or burn or steal.

In the morning, if the sky cleared, they would climb into it.

The lantern flame bent flat and died, and neither of them moved to relight it.
