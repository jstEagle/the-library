## 9. Snowlight

The storm found them an hour past treeline and gave them no choice about stopping.

It came down the western faces like something poured — not falling snow but a white flood, erasing the ridge they'd meant to cross, erasing the mules ten feet out on the rope line, erasing the world down to the circle of Mirabel's own breath. She had been watching the sky all day, reading it the way her father had taught her to read water: the lenticular cloud stacked over the peak like a lens cap, the wind swinging from the southwest to the north in a single sickening quarter hour. She had called for the cave. Odile had already been unrolling the tent fly when she said it.

The cave was a limestone throat halfway up the valley's north wall, wide enough at its mouth for the supply train, deep enough that the back of it stayed dry. They got eleven mules inside and lost only one pack to the wind — a crate of oats that went cartwheeling down the talus like a kicked drum. By the time the last animal was hobbled and blanketed, the entrance had become a wall of moving white, and the temperature outside had dropped through some threshold where snow stopped being weather and became a fact of geology.

"Two days," Odile said, crouched at the mouth with her mittened hand held into the wind like a man testing a current. "Maybe three."

"Maybe four," Mirabel said.

Odile glanced back at her. In twenty years of running lines together, they had developed a shorthand so compressed it was nearly telepathy, and what passed between them now was simple: *you always say the worst number first.* And: *and you are always right.*

They made camp the way they made camp everywhere, which was to say the way Mirabel had designed it to be made, years ago, in a notebook she still carried: fire ring of stacked stone against the back wall, reflector screen of tin to throw heat at the animals, watches rotated so no man sat the cold hours alone. The crew moved through it with the wordless economy of people who had done a hundred hard camps together, and even Vane's men — six of them, big-shouldered, quiet, used to their own foreman's way of doing things — fell into the rhythm within the hour, because rhythm is contagious and competence more so.

Tomas Vane worked like his father's men but watched like someone else entirely. Mirabel caught him twice studying her across the firelight — not the appraising stare of a rival's son measuring a competitor, but something more careful, more troubled. He had been carrying his father's sealed orders since the railhead, and he had read them somewhere along the gorge; she had seen the change in him afterward, a small deflection in his bearing, like a compass needle with a nail driven too near. She had not asked. There would be time. Or there would not be, and asking would have changed nothing anyway.

* * *

The storm did not slacken by morning. It did not slacken by noon. It piled itself against the mountainside with the patience of something that had all winter and knew it, and by late afternoon the cave had become a world entire: eleven mules steaming in the dark, nineteen people, a fire that turned the limestone ceiling amber, and beyond the mouth nothing but snowlight — that peculiar luminous grey that is neither day nor dark, in which distance dies and time goes strange.

It was Odile who started the stories. That was unlike her; Odile kept her history the way she kept her gear, packed tight and inventoried rarely. But there is a kind of confinement that loosens people, and a kind of firelight, and a kind of silence after a hard climb when everyone is too tired to be guarded. She told the story of the Yucatán line, the year a jaguar walked the length of their survey tape at dawn as if inspecting their work, and how young Ferro had stood so still he'd gotten sunburned waiting for it to leave.

Ferro protested the details. The jaguar had been a female. He had not been sunburned, he had been *composed*. The muleteers laughed. One of Vane's men — Halloran, a granite-faced Irishman who had not yet spoken forty words on the whole expedition — surprised everyone by offering that he'd once spent three days in a tree in British Columbia on account of a grizzly, and had eaten his own bootlaces, and had never since trusted anything advertised as waterproof.

More stories came. The time Odile had talked a customs officer out of impounding their theodolite by convincing him it was a church organ. The time Mirabel had forded a river in February to retrieve a dropped instrument case and come out on the far bank with ice in her eyelashes and the case held overhead like an offering, and had said only, "Zero two minutes lost," before walking on.

"That's her," Halloran said, nodding at Mirabel with something like awe. "That's exactly her."

"She's famous," one of the younger muleteers said, to nobody, the way people say things they've been thinking for days. "The Sterling line. Everyone knows the Sterling line. Straightest surveys in three continents. My uncle ran chain for a crew in Bolivia that used her methods." He paused, and then, because he was young and the fire was warm and the storm had made the whole world private: "Is it true what they say about her father?"

The cave went still in the particular way a room goes still when a question has landed in the middle of everything.

"Ferro," Odile said quietly. A warning.

But it was too late for warnings; the question was out, sitting in the firelight among them like a live coal. Everyone knew the outline of the story — that Aldous Sterling had died in the Karagol Range twenty years ago, that the expedition had broken apart afterward, that his daughter had walked out at seventeen leading two frostbitten survivors and had never gone back. The outline was famous. The interior of it, nobody had.

Mirabel fed a stick to the fire. She watched it catch, and she understood, with the strange clarity of exhaustion, that she was going to answer. Not because the boy deserved it, and not because the storm had earned it, but because she had been carrying this thing up the mountain toward the place it happened, and a carried thing grows heavier by the mile, and there was a limit approaching that she could feel in her chest the way you feel a storm coming behind your teeth.

She looked at Tomas. He was watching her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere — not curiosity, not pity. Something like bracing.

"My father," she said, "was the best surveyor I ever knew. Until me."

A ripple of uncomfortable laughter, quickly suppressed. She didn't smile.

"I mean that literally. I was twelve when he taught me to run a level. Fourteen when I reduced my first traverse. Sixteen when I found more sites in one season than he had in ten years, because he surveyed like a gentleman and I surveyed like a thief — I went where the ground told me instead of where the maps said. He was proud of me. I want that understood first, because everything after this will sound otherwise, and it isn't otherwise. He loved me. He was proud of me. And he destroyed me anyway, without meaning to, the way a man destroys a thing by standing on it."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark of the cave a mule shifted and sighed.

"The Karagol job. Twenty years ago. I was seventeen. We were mapping the high basins under contract — my father's contract, his name on the paper, his reputation riding on it. On the fourth week I cut away from the main camp to close a loop on the eastern cirque, and I found it. You've heard of it — everyone has, now. The terraced ruins above the ice lake. The old road. The whole complex that rewrote every book written about the range." She turned the stick in her hands. "I found it. I photographed it. I shot the bearings, sketched the approaches, logged everything in my field book in my own hand. I climbed back to camp with the site in my pocket like a coal, and I gave it to him, because he was my father and I was seventeen and that is what you do with a discovery — you bring it home."

Her voice had gone flat and even, the voice she used for reading instruments aloud. She knew it. She couldn't help it. Feeling was for afterward; the telling had to be done like work or it couldn't be done at all.

"He wrote it up that night. His name alone. I saw the draft by the lantern — I saw my own bearings copied into his tables, my photographs stacked under his elbow, and at the top of the page, in his handwriting, *discovered by Aldous Sterling.* I asked him about it. I remember exactly how I asked — I wasn't brave, I was seventeen and shaking, and I said, *Papa, the notes say you found it,* as if maybe I'd misread, as if there was some explanation. And he looked at me —" Her jaw tightened. "He looked at me the way a man looks when he has just realized the size of what he's done and cannot yet imagine undoing it. And he said, *The world will believe me. It won't believe you. This way the work gets done.* As if it were arithmetic. As if he were protecting the discovery from me."

"You were a child," Odile said softly.

"I was the better surveyor. That was the unbearable part — for both of us. He knew it. I didn't know it yet, but he knew it, and it had been eating him for two years, and finding that site through my eyes was the last course of the meal." She set the stick down. "We argued. Up on the moraine above the ice lake, the night after the survey was complete. I told him I would write my own report. He told me no one would print it. I told him I'd make them. And he —"

She stopped. Nineteen people breathed carefully around her.

"We argued on the moraine, and the edge of the moraine was rotten, and he stepped backward while he was talking, the way he always did when he was winning a point, arms open, certain the ground would hold him. It didn't. It was thirty feet onto the glacier tongue. It was quick. The doctor who examined us after said it must have been quick." She looked at the fire. "I was the one who found him. I was the one who led the survivors out. And I was the one who signed the accident report that said *arguing over route selection,* because if I had written the truth — that we were arguing over credit, over my own name — then either no one would have believed me, or worse, they would have, and he'd have been remembered as the man who stole from his daughter and died of it. I couldn't do that to him. So I did it to myself instead. Twenty years of it."

The snowlight at the cave mouth had gone blue with evening. Nobody moved. The youngest muleteer had his hat in his hands. Halloran stared into the fire as if it owed him money.

"So when Vane's letter came," Mirabel said, "offering a contract to recover a cache from that same range — a cache my father hid, that only I could find, because the map to it is drawn in a code he invented for me when I was twelve — I want you all to understand what you voted on in that tent. You voted to walk me back up the mountain where my father died, in service of a man who hated my father, to retrieve papers that will settle a quarrel twenty years cold. And I let you vote. Because the wages were real and your winters are real and I told myself it was business." She finally raised her eyes from the fire and let them move around the circle, each face in turn. "It was never business. I came because the truth is buried up here in a stone box, and I am done letting other men decide what it says."

Silence. The storm pressed against the mountain like a palm over a mouth.

Then Odile moved — not to her, that would have been too much, Odile understood the architecture of these things — but to the fire, adding wood, feeding the flames until they stood up bright, and said, gruffly, to the company at large: "Now you know why she counts miles instead of years. Miles you can close. Years just pile up."

And the crew, understanding that this was the signal, that the avalanche had finished falling and they had survived it, began very carefully to be ordinary again — Halloran asking after the coffee, Ferro checking the hobbles, voices rising in the low register of people being gentle with a room.

All except Tomas.

He had not moved through any of it. He sat at the edge of the firelight with his elbows on his knees, and when Mirabel's eyes found him he didn't look away, and what was in his face was not shock. That was the thing she turned over later, lying awake in her bedroll with the storm booming outside like surf. Not shock. Recognition. He had known something — some piece, some version — and her confession had not struck him as news but as confirmation, and the guilt on him was the specific guilt of a man holding a document he should not have, burning a hole in his coat pocket all the way up a frozen mountain.

"Tomas," she said, quietly, under the cover of the others' voices.

He came to her. Close enough that the firelight didn't reach, where the mules breathed their warm steam.

"You've known," she said. It wasn't a question. "Since the gorge. Since you read whatever your father sealed in those orders. You've been looking at me for days like a man watching weather he can't warn anyone about."

His jaw worked. For a moment she thought he would deny it, and she felt something in her go cold and ready for that. Then he exhaled, and the breath shook, just slightly, at the end.

"Not everything," he said. "I swear to you, not everything. But what I know — Mirabel, what my father sent me up here to do —" He glanced at the fire, at the sleeping crews, at the wall of white sealing the cave like a tomb. "You need to hear it from me before the pass. Before he gets here. Because he *is* coming, and he isn't coming for a cache."

Above the cave mouth, unseen through the storm, the wind swung hard to the north — and far below, on a route no reckoning allowed, men were already climbing.
