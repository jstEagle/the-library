## 10. Vane's Version

The storm held them for two days.

Two days of white roar at the cave mouth, of ice thickening on the guy-lines, of twenty-two people learning the particular intimacy of shared cold. Two days of Mirabel Sterling sitting across the fire from him with her father's story finally loose in the air between them, and Tomas unable to give her the rest of it because he did not have it himself.

He had half of it. He had his father's orders — *retrieve the cache, burn anything else, tell no one what the notes contain* — and he had the shape of a lie he'd grown up inside without ever seeing its walls. But when Mirabel asked him, in that level surveyor's voice that stripped a slope down to its angles, *what did your father do*, he had nothing but inference. And inference was not something she would carry up a mountain.

So on the second night, when the wind dropped to a snarl and the snow thinned enough to show stars like nail-heads punched through black canvas, Tomas did the thing he had been circling for three days the way a man circles a crevasse field looking for the solid line.

He wrote to his father.

* * *

There was no paper to spare worth the name, so he used the back of a supply manifest, writing small, in pencil, by firelight, hunched over his knees where the light wouldn't reach anyone sleeping. Odile saw him. He felt her eyes from across the cave — the old foreman slept badly, he'd learned, and woke at every shift of the wind like a ship's cat — but she said nothing. She had said nothing since the night Mirabel told her truth and the whole cave went quiet as a held breath. Odile was waiting, too. Everyone in this crew was waiting on him, and he hated it, and he understood it, and both at once.

The letter took him an hour, mostly crossed out.

*Father —*

*I've read your orders. All of them. I've carried them since the railhead and I want you to know that first, because what follows isn't the complaint of a son who didn't try to obey.*

*Mirabel Sterling told us tonight what happened on the Karagol expedition. Her version. She found the site first. Her father claimed it. They quarreled on the ridge and he fell. That's what she believes, and she believes it about herself more than about him, which is worse.*

*You were there. You were his rival and you were on that range the same season and you have never once, in my whole life, told me plainly what happened. You sent me up here with sealed orders and a lie about a cache and you hired the daughter of the man whose grave this mountain is, and you did not tell me why.*

*I'm not asking anymore. I'm telling you: I will not go one step past the pass carrying your version of events blind. If you want your cache recovered, if you want your name anywhere near what comes out of that cairn, you will send me the whole of it. What you did. What he did. What the notes say. Everything you ordered me to burn.*

*Tomas*

He read it twice. It was the hardest thing he had ever written, harder than any survey report, harder than the letter he'd written at nineteen withdrawing from his father's firm — a letter that had lasted four months before Elias Vane's gravity pulled him back into orbit, the way it always did. This one he folded along the manifest's crease and sealed with tallow from the cook's candle stub.

Then the problem of sending it.

The storm had slackened but not broken; the passes below were already closing; the valley was five days' hard travel even in good weather. No runner was going down through that. But Vane wasn't five days away anymore either — Mirabel had done the arithmetic aloud that morning, tapping the ice wall with one gloved finger: *if he left when the almanac said he should, he's behind us. If he left when I think he left, he's ahead.* Which meant the high route, the one no sane man took after mid-autumn, and which meant Vane's advance party might be within signaling distance by tomorrow or the day after.

Tomas went to Mirabel.

She was awake — she was always awake, it seemed, in the way of people who had trained themselves out of needing much sleep, though he'd caught her once at the riverbend asleep sitting up against a supply crate with her hand still curled around a pencil, and the memory of it sat in his chest like a coal. She looked up when his shadow crossed the fire.

"I need to get a letter to your father," he said.

Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise. She'd known this was coming since the night he gave her half the truth. "And you're telling me instead of just doing it."

"You'd find out anyway. Your people see everything." He crouched across the fire from her, hands open on his knees. "It's a demand, Mirabel. Not a report. I'm asking him for the whole story — what happened on the original expedition, what the notes contain, all of it. I'm asking him to put it in writing."

She was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the way it went careful when she was handling something that might cut her. "Why does it matter that it's in writing?"

"Because spoken things can be denied. Written things can be published."

That landed. He watched it land — watched her understand that he was offering her, however obliquely, whatever weapon his father's own hand might forge. Her chin came up a degree. "If he answers honestly, you'll give it to me."

"If he answers honestly," Tomas said, "I don't think I'll have a choice. And if he lies—" he turned his palms up "—then I'll know that too. I've spent thirty years reading the man. I know what his honesty looks like on a page. It looks cramped. He writes smaller when he's hiding something."

Almost, she smiled. It didn't survive, but it started. "All right. Take Ruiz. He's the only one light enough for the ice bridge below the notch, and he's been dying of boredom since the storm started. Go at first light, before the wind fills it in again."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." She fed a stick to the fire, and the light jumped, and for a moment her face was all planes and shadow, her mother's jaw and her father's eyes and none of their softness left anywhere visible. "Just come back with the truth, Tomas. Whatever shape it is. I've spent twenty years surviving a shapeless one."

* * *

Ruiz came back at dusk the next day, half-frozen, exultant, with a reply sewn into his shirt lining against the sweat.

Tomas read it alone.

His father had made camp in a hanging valley two ridges south — close, closer than any reckoning allowed, exactly as Mirabel had predicted — and the runner had reached him in under a day of brutal travel. The reply was written in the same pencil, on the same kind of manifest paper, and it was four pages long, and it was the most terrible thing Tomas had ever read, and he had watched a man lose fingers to frost two nights ago and heard Mirabel take them off with a bone saw by lantern light without one wasted motion.

His father wrote small. He wrote very small indeed.

*Tomas —*

*You ask for the whole of it, and you ask in a tone I don't recognize, which means either the mountain has changed you or the Sterling woman has, and I find I don't care for either possibility. But you are right about one thing: written things cannot be denied. So here is what cannot be denied, and once you have read it you will understand why I ordered what I ordered, and you will obey, because the alternative is a cruelty I am not willing to visit on that woman no matter what she costs us.*

*You grew up on the legend. Let me kill it.*

*Aldous Sterling and I were not rivals in the way the journals made it sound — two gentlemen explorers trading barbs over brandy. We were rivals the way two claims staked on the same ore body are rivals. Everything either of us touched, the other had touched first or wanted first. For eleven years we raced each other across three ranges, and I will tell you plainly: he was winning. Not because he was braver — I was never accused of cowardice — but because he was better. He read country the way other men read print. Every route I cut, he'd have found a cleaner one a week later and published it with some dry footnote that gutted me worse than an attack would have.*

*The Karagol season was to be the end of it. The high pass, the unclimbed face, the survey that would settle whose name went on the maps. I arrived to find he'd brought his daughter. Twelve years old, if you can believe it, hauling a theodolite half her size. I thought it was vanity. It wasn't. It was inheritance — he was training her the way a man trains a successor, and I understood later that he'd already begun letting her run lines herself.*

*Here is what I have never said aloud, and what I will deny to my grave if you repeat it as anything but my confession: the site — the ruins in the hanging valley above the icefall — was found by the girl. Not Aldous. She ran a compass line off the main traverse chasing a discrepancy in the scree, and she walked into the most significant discovery of the decade, at twelve years old, alone, and she marked it and flagged it and reported it to her father like a good soldier reports a position.*

*And Aldous took it.*

*Not quietly. He took it the way he took everything — thoroughly, with instruments and witnesses and a signed field report, and the girl's name appears nowhere in it. I knew. I was at base camp when the report went down the mountain and I recognized the coordinates' provenance because I'd seen her flagging tape on a reconnaissance two days prior. I said nothing. Worse than nothing.*

*I used it.*

*You have to understand what those years did to me, Tomas, though I know understanding is not forgiveness. I went to Aldous on the ridge above the icefall — the third day of the descent, the day the weather was turning — and I told him I knew the discovery was the girl's. I told him I would publish it. I told him I would make certain every society and every journal knew that the great Aldous Sterling had built his crowning claim on a child's work, stolen under his own roof. I had proof prepared. I had been preparing it for weeks. That was the sabotage — not ropes cut, not supplies spoiled, nothing so crude. I sabotaged him with the truth. I sharpened it and I waited and I drove it in on a narrow ridge in failing light, and I watched a careful man stop being careful.*

*They argued. You know how it ended. The ledger says a fall; the inquiry said misadventure. What I know is that I stood forty yards down-ridge and watched two Sterlings destroy each other over a question I had planted between them, and then I climbed down to the body, and I took his field notebook out of his pack before anyone else reached him.*

*That is what the cache holds, Tomas. His notes — including pages describing the discovery in his daughter's hand, transcribed in his own, with his acknowledgment in the margin. He wrote it down. God knows why — guilt, perhaps, or the surveyor's disease of recording everything truly regardless of consequence. The marginal note reads, and I have had twenty years to memorize it: "Found by M.S., aged 12. Reported as my own. Correct the record when I am beyond shame."*

*He meant to correct it himself. He died before he could. And I have spent twenty years making sure no one else did, because if that record is corrected, it is corrected alongside the question of who knew and who profited and who stood on a ridge and lit the fuse — and that question has my name on the answer.*

*Now the order, and you will follow it: the notes come down the mountain unopened. The letter in the cache — there is a letter addressed to the girl; I learned of it from his last dispatches — does not reach her. Burn anything that names me. Tell her nothing. She has built a life on the belief that her father stole from her and that she survived it; let her keep that life. The alternative gives her the theft and the theft's true author in the same breath, and makes her my enemy with a justification no court and no conscience would dispute, and puts you —*

*— and here his father's handwriting, cramped as it was, seemed to fail for a moment, the letters tightening until they were nearly illegible —*

*— puts you between us, which I will not allow. You are my son. You are the last thing I have made that I do not regret. Keep her from the notes, Tomas. By any means. Do this and everything I have is yours.*

*We make the pass in three days. Be there.*

*E.V.*

* * *

Tomas sat with the pages for a long time after the fire had burned down to coals, reading them until he could have recited them, until the words stopped being words and became a weight he carried in his chest like water.

So that was the architecture. Twenty years of it, load-bearing and invisible: a stolen discovery, a planted quarrel, a dead man's notebook lifted from his pack like a pickpocket's work, and a cache buried in ice to hold the evidence until the evidence's owner stopped mattering. His father had not merely hidden the truth. He had curated it. He had kept Aldous Sterling's confession frozen at altitude the way a man keeps a debt instrument — valuable precisely because unredeemed.

And now he had hired the daughter to fetch it for him. Hired her *because* of who she was — because her father's map lived in her jacket lining, because only a Sterling could read a Sterling's riddle. He had hired the key to open the box holding the knife.

*Burn anything that names me.*

Tomas looked at the pages in his hands. His father's handwriting, cramped and shrinking. *Everything I have is yours.* He thought about what that inheritance actually consisted of: a firm built on salvaged reputation, a name polished by omission, and now, delivered into his keeping by his father's own hand, a full written confession to the destruction of Aldous Sterling — addressed, in effect, to the one person on earth with the most right to it.

He had asked for the truth to arm himself. He had not understood that the truth would be ammunition, and that his father had just handed it to him with instructions to dispose of it, and that from this moment forward every choice he made was no longer filial duty or even betrayal. It was complicity, or it was rupture. There was no slope between them. There was a cornice, and he was standing on it.

Across the cave, Mirabel lay on her side facing the rock wall, asleep or performing sleep, her breathing slow and even, one hand tucked under her cheek like a girl's. Twelve years old, running a compass line into history. Twenty years old, burying a father. Forty-something, lying ten feet from the son of the man who killed him, trusting him with half-truths because the whole ones were all she had left to trade.

Three days. His father would make the pass in three days, and expected the cache cleaned, the paper burned, the silence intact.

Tomas folded the confession along its creases and did not burn it. He sewed it into his own shirt lining, against his own skin, where his father had taught him important documents belonged — the last lesson of that kind he intended to learn — and he lay down beside the banked fire and stared at the cave ceiling until the stars outside wheeled and the wind rose again, and somewhere below in the dark, men were climbing toward them through the cold with rope and iron and his father's will.

Three days. And Mirabel had already told him, once, in the river gorge, laughing with wet hair in her face: *the trouble with secrets on a survey line, Tomas, is that everything buried eventually gets found by somebody running a line across it.*

He just hadn't known he'd be the ground it surfaced from.
