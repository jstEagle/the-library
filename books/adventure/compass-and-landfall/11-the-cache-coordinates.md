## 11. The Cache Coordinates

The storm broke in the night the way storms do at altitude — not gradually, but all at once, as if someone had pulled a plug out of the sky. Mirabel woke in the cold camp's lee of boulders to a silence so complete she could hear her own blood moving, and when she crawled out of her tent the stars were out in such numbers that the snowfield below caught them and threw them back doubled.

Clear skies. Three days of them, if the barometer told the truth, and Odile's knees agreed with it, which between the two instruments was enough for Mirabel.

"Hard climbing today," she said to the assembled crews, breath steaming. "We make the col above the Serac Line by dark or we don't make it at all."

Vane's advance men — four of them, hard-faced, high-route veterans who had caught up two days ago and been fed and tolerated ever since — exchanged a look. Their leader, a broad man called Harker with a beard full of ice, said nothing. He'd been saying nothing for two days, watching Mirabel the way a man watches a lock he means eventually to pick. She let him watch. There was nothing on her face she wanted him to read, and there was going to be even less by nightfall.

They broke camp in the blue hour before dawn, rope teams forming in the dark by habit and lamplight, and started up.

* * *

The climbing was honest work, the kind Mirabel loved best: steep enough to demand everything, stable enough to give it back. The snow was wind-scoured and squeaking underfoot, cold enough to hold an edge. She led the first pitch herself, cutting steps in a rhythm older than her hands, and behind her the long train of the combined expedition strung out across the face like beads on a wire — mules below on the gentler ground, men above, the cache sledges abandoned now, everything they owned carried on their backs.

Tomas climbed second on her rope. He had stopped asking questions somewhere around the frostbite camp, and what had replaced the questions was something better: attention. He watched her work the way she imagined his father once watched her father work — cataloguing, measuring, deciding something.

"You've been quiet since the cave," he said, low, between swings of the axe.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're always *controlled*. It's different." He nodded upward, toward the col. "You know where you're going. You've known since the riverbend, maybe. Longer."

Mirabel set three more steps before she answered. "I know how to read terrain," she said. "It's my trade."

"It wasn't terrain I meant."

She looked back at him then. The sun had just cleared the eastern ridge and it lit him from the side, gilding the frost in his eyebrows, and she thought — not for the first time, but with new discomfort — that he deserved better than the half-truths she'd been rationing him. Also that she couldn't afford the whole truth yet. Not with Harker forty yards back and Vane himself a day behind, closing.

"When we reach the cache," she said, "you'll understand. That's the most I can give you this morning."

He held her eyes for a moment. Then he nodded and turned to the wall, and gave her his strength without further argument, which was its own kind of answer.

* * *

They made the col by mid-afternoon, hours ahead of any schedule Vane's timetable allowed, and Mirabel called a halt on the sheltered side where a rock wall cut the wind. While Odile organized water and the muleteers saw to the animals below, Mirabel sat alone against the stone with her jacket open and her fingers inside the lining.

The map came out stiff with cold. She'd sewn it into that jacket when she was twelve years old and had taken it out perhaps a dozen times since; each time, it seemed to have grown smaller, the way childhood things do, though the handwriting hadn't changed. Her father's hand — the same hand that filled the notes waiting somewhere ahead of her in a cairn, the same hand that had signed reports claiming discoveries that were hers. She knew every letter of it the way she knew her own pulse.

For twenty years she had read this map as a child reads a treasure: X marks the spot, follow the dotted line. A route into the Karagol high country, drawn in colored pencil, annotated in his quick slanting script. And at its heart, a small circled star beneath the north face of Mount Kesselring, with words beside it she had never thought to question:

*The cache lies here.*

But she had questioned everything else about her father for twenty years. Why not this? Because questioning it hurt too much, she supposed. Because it was the one thing he'd given her cleanly.

Today, with the actual mountain in front of her — the real north face of Kesselring rising white and enormous across the glacier, exactly where the circled star put it — she finally saw what she'd been refusing to see.

The star was wrong.

Not badly wrong. Wrong the way a surveyor is wrong when he wants to be — precisely wrong, wrong by intent. She laid the map flat on her knee and sighted across it to the peak, and her father's training rose up in her like water finding its level. The bearing lines on the map were drawn to true north. But the little compass rose in the corner — the one she'd traced with her finger a hundred times as a girl — was offset. Declination, she'd always assumed. Local magnetic variation, a surveyor's habit.

Except she'd run magnetic surveys through this very range for two decades. She knew the declination here to the minute of arc. And the offset on the rose didn't match it. Didn't match anything natural at all.

It matched a rotation of eleven degrees east.

Her breath went shallow. Eleven degrees. If you took every bearing on the map and corrected them through the false rose — if you treated the rose not as a key but as a lock, a deliberate error planted for anyone who read the map straight — then the route didn't end under the north face of Kesselring at all. The whole line swung east. The dotted trail her father drew crossed the glacier, climbed the shoulder of the secondary peak — unremarkable, unnamed on any map she owned — and terminated in a hanging valley that appeared on no government sheet because no survey party had ever bothered with it.

A valley her father had marked, in that same quick hand, with a single word she had always read as a joke, a private endearment, a nonsense name for his daughter:

*Mirabelle.*

The orchard valley. Because she'd been born in spring, he used to say, in a house with plum trees outside the window. She'd thought it sentiment. It was coordinates. It was a place. Somewhere on the shoulder of that gray, forgotten peak was a hollow her father had found and named for her, and that was where he had moved the cache — moved it after he wrote the location Vane believed in, moved it deliberately, leaving the old mark on the map like a coin left on a dresser for a thief who couldn't resist taking it.

Only she could solve it. Only someone who knew the declination tables of the Karagol by heart, who knew the story of the plum trees, who had carried the map against her skin for twenty years without once reading it as a riddle instead of a gift. Her father had built a lock whose key was his daughter, and he had done it — she understood this with a certainty that felt like grief and fury holding hands — *knowing* Elias Vane would come looking someday. Knowing Vane would get the map or force its secrets, find the cairn under Kesselring's north face, and dig in the wrong place until the snows took him.

Or knowing that Mirabel herself would come, someday, if she could forgive him enough to walk this far.

Both. He had planned for both. That was the thing about her father that twenty years of anger had never managed to erase: he thought in layers. He lied in layers. He loved in layers.

Mirabel folded the map along its old creases and sat for a long time watching the unnamed shoulder catch the afternoon light.

* * *

Harker found her there an hour later. She heard him coming — the man walked like a falling tree — and had the map back inside her jacket and her face arranged into professional blankness before he rounded the boulders.

"Miss Sterling." He touched his hat brim. "Mr. Vane's compliments. He asks your position on tomorrow's route. The north face approach, per the original plan." His eyes flicked, just once, toward her jacket. "He says the bearings are agreed upon. Under Kesselring, at the base of the ice cliff. Two days' travel."

"The bearings are agreed," Mirabel said pleasantly.

Harker waited. She let him wait. Below them the camp murmured — Odile's voice carrying over the muleteers, the clink of the cook's pots, Tomas laughing at something, actually laughing, up here at fourteen thousand feet with his father's orders folded somewhere against his chest.

"We'll take the glacier route," she said at last. "It's faster than the face approach in these conditions, and it puts us below the seracs before the sun softens them. Your Mr. Vane will appreciate the economy."

Something shifted in Harker's face — not suspicion exactly, but the wariness of a man handed a smooth stone that might be a knife. "The glacier route doesn't pass the cache site."

"No," Mirabel agreed. "But it passes within a mile of it, and the spur to the north face branches off the glacier's east bank. We'll camp at the branch. Everyone gets their bearings satisfied." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Tell him I'm eager to close this contract. Twenty years is a long time to leave a debt standing."

Harker studied her a moment longer, then touched his brim again and crunched away down the slope.

Mirabel exhaled slowly, watching her breath hang and fade in the gold light. A mile. She could put the whole expedition within a mile of the true site and let them dig at the decoy cairn while the real one waited in a valley named for her, and no one — not Vane, not Harker, not the ghost of her father's ambition — could take that from her unless she chose to give it.

Power, she thought, should feel heavier than this. It felt instead like a compass needle settling. For the first time in twenty years she was not running along a bearing someone else had drawn. She was drawing it.

Tomas came up the slope as the light went long, carrying two cups of tea, and handed her one without asking. He stood beside her looking at the unnamed peak, and she watched him look at it, and wondered what he saw.

"My father's runner came up an hour ago," he said quietly. "There's a letter. He's a day behind us now, maybe less. He wants to be at the cache when we open it."

"Then he'd best hurry," Mirabel said, and sipped her tea, and said nothing else at all.

Below them, the glacier ran down out of sight toward the east, toward the hanging valley with the plum-tree name, where her father had hidden the truth and left the key in the only hands he trusted.

Two days, she thought. One of them hers.
