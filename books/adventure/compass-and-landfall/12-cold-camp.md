## 12. Cold Camp

The storm that had spared them at the pass caught up two days later on the shoulder of the mountain they had no name for, and it did not spare them anything.

Tomas felt it first in the mules. They had been climbing since before dawn through a white world where sky and slope had traded places, and somewhere past midday the lead animal — a gray gelding called Proverb, because Odile said he quoted scripture with his teeth — stopped walking and would not be persuaded otherwise. He stood with his head low and his ears flat, and when Odile went to him and pulled off his pack frame, she looked at his hooves a long moment and then looked back down the line at Mirabel with an expression Tomas had learned to read as a sentence of its own.

*We are out of time.*

"Camp here," Mirabel said. No argument, no weighing. The word came out of her like a stone dropped into still water, and the whole expedition reorganized itself around the ripple.

They were at eleven thousand feet by the aneroid, on a shelf of wind-packed snow below a wall of black rock that gave them some shelter from the west wind but none from the cold itself, which came down now out of the north like something poured. By the time the tents stood, the temperature had fallen to the point where breath froze in the wool of a man's collar and stayed there, a rime of small white feathers. Tomas had wintered above ten thousand feet twice in his life, both times by choice and both times with a stove he trusted. This was different. This was the mountain deciding what it would allow them to keep.

He watched Mirabel work.

She moved through the camp the way water moves through cracks — everywhere at once, no wasted motion, no wasted word. She checked the mules herself, hand under each blanket, feeling for the damp that meant sweat freezing against hide. She redistributed loads with Odile in a shorthand of gestures that needed no speech. She put the injured climber, Halloran — whose frost-nipped fingers from the crevasse country were the reason they'd lost a day already — in the best tent, closest to the fire, without appearing to have made a decision about it at all.

And when Proverb's hooves told her what they told Odile, and when the second mule, a dun mare named after nobody knew what, showed the same gray-black deadening in her ears and muzzle, Mirabel knelt in the snow beside them and did not flinch.

Tomas had seen men flinch at less. He had seen his father flinch, once, at a broken arm in the Andes — not at the sight of it, but at the arithmetic of it, the days lost, the plan amended. His father's flinching was always arithmetic. Mirabel did no sums. She simply took the animal's face in her gloved hands and looked at the dying tissue the way she looked at a fault line: as a fact of the ground, to be worked with or worked around, never argued with.

"We'll lose them," Odile said quietly, beside him. "Both of them, if we push tomorrow."

"I know."

"You don't." Odile's eyes, dark and old and patient as tree rings, came around to him. "You know it in your head. She knows it in her hands. There's a difference, boy, and it's the whole difference between your father and her."

He didn't answer. There was nothing to answer with that wasn't true.

* * *

It was Halloran who nearly died.

The big Irishman had been favoring his right hand since the icefall, keeping it tucked inside his coat the way men do when they've decided a wound is a private matter. He was a proud man and a strong one, and pride and strength are the two qualities most likely to kill you above treeline. Sometime in the black hours before dawn, while the wind screamed over the rock wall and the temperature fell through numbers the thermometer couldn't print, Halloran's hand finished what the cold had started. And sometime after dawn, when they dug him out of his tent to check on him, they found him confused, slurring, shivering wrong — not the violent shiver of a body fighting, but the slow, drowsy tremor of a body surrendering.

"Hypothermia," Mirabel said, and the word came out flat and fast. "Get him into my tent. Strip him. Skin to skin, two of you, and heat everything you can find. Stones, water, anything. Move."

She said *skin to skin* and did not blush and did not pause, and neither did anyone else, because there was no room in her voice for embarrassment. Tomas stripped off his own outer layers and got into the sleeping bag with Halloran's shaking bulk, pressing his chest to the man's frozen side, and it was like embracing a corpse that hadn't gotten the news yet. Above him, Mirabel's voice ran the camp like a current through wire. Water heated. Stones wrapped in cloth against the groin and armpits, nowhere else, never burned skin. Sugar dissolved in warm water, fed by the spoonful when Halloran could swallow, wiped from his beard when he couldn't.

For three hours Tomas lay in the dark holding another man's life against his own ribs, listening to the wind try to peel the mountain open, and the whole time Mirabel's voice never changed pitch. Not once. It rose and fell with instructions, with questions, with answers, but underneath it there was a floor, a bedrock note of calm that he found himself clinging to harder than the sleeping bag. Once, when Halloran stopped shivering entirely and someone outside said, in a voice gone thin, *is he —*, Mirabel answered:

"He's not. Keep the water coming."

And that was all. And the water kept coming. And an hour later Halloran shivered again, hugely, gloriously, the way a drowning man coughs, and Tomas heard Odile outside say something in her mother's language that he suspected was a prayer or a curse and possibly both.

By noon Halloran was sitting up, stupid with weakness but alive, drinking broth and complaining about it, which everyone agreed was the surest sign of recovery a man could show.

That was when they saw his hand.

* * *

The four fingers of Halloran's right hand, from the second knuckle out, had gone the color of old candle wax. Waxy, walled-off, dead — the flesh beyond the line had died in the night and no heat on earth was going to call it back. Mirabel examined it in the weak light with a thoroughness that made Tomas look away and then make himself look back, because he understood, dimly, that looking away was a luxury the mountain didn't sell.

"Gangrene," she said. "Dry. If we leave it, it goes wet, and then it goes into the blood, and then we bury him under a cairn instead of carrying him home." She looked up. "Odile. My kit. The good scissors, the knife, the needle. Boil everything. All of it, twenty minutes, no less."

"You're going to cut," Odile said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Here? Now?" That was Ruiz, the muleteer, a small wiry man who had crossed the Cordillera twice and told stories about it that made grown listeners pale. "There's no doctor within a hundred miles. Señora —"

"There's no doctor within a hundred miles," Mirabel agreed. "Which is why it's me."

She said it without drama. That was the thing Tomas kept returning to, later, turning it over like a stone found in a riverbed: there was no drama in her anywhere. He had grown up in a house where his father turned every hardship into a story before the blood was dry, where suffering was raw material and every campfire was a stage. Mirabel Sterling knelt in the snow with a dying man's hand in hers and performed no narrative whatsoever. She simply began to prepare.

They rigged the largest tent as a surgery. Boiling water. The alcohol from the medical kit, hoarded against exactly this. A plank lashed between two crates for a table. Lanterns — four of them, borrowed from every tent in camp — hung from the ridge pole, because the day was cloud-dark and the light failing early, and this was not work to do half-blind.

Halloran insisted on watching. "I've seen worse done to better men," he said, which was either bravery or a lie, and Mirabel let him have it either way. They gave him the last of the medicinal brandy, enough to blur the edge if not to dull it, and Odile held his shoulders, and Ruiz held his arm above the elbow, and Tomas —

Tomas held the lanterns steady.

It was the only job left, and he took it the way he had begun to understand one took any job from Mirabel Sterling: completely, and without being asked twice. He stood at the head of the plank table with a lantern in each hand, arms locked, and he watched her work, because she had asked him — asked him with a look, in the moment before the first cut — *don't look away. If you look away, they'll feel it.*

So he didn't look away.

What he saw was this: a woman kneeling in a tent full of borrowed light, sleeves rolled, hands washed in boiled water until the skin was red, working with a steadiness that seemed almost inhuman until he understood it wasn't inhuman at all. It was human, entirely human — it was discipline worn so long it had become bone. She marked the line of demarcation where living flesh met dead, pressed to see the difference in color and give, tested with the needle whether Halloran could still feel, and where he could not, she cut. She cut cleanly, quickly, tied off what needed tying, cauterized what needed burning, dressed the stumps with carbolic and clean linen, and bound the hand in a way that Tomás could see was not merely bandaging but architecture — built to survive days of rough travel and come off the mountain intact.

Halloran made a sound during the worst of it. One sound, low, bitten off. Then silence. Then, unbelievably, a joke, slurred and terrible, about how he'd never liked the piano anyway.

Mirabel laughed. Just once, just a breath of it, and kept working.

When it was done — twenty minutes, maybe twenty-five — she sat back on her heels, and for the first time since dawn her hands shook. Only a little. Only enough to show that they were hands and not machinery. She pressed them flat against her thighs until they stopped, and then she stood, and looked down at Halloran, and said:

"You'll keep the thumb and the palm. You'll hold a rope with it by spring. You'll never play the piano, and I'm told you never could."

Halloran grinned up at her through the brandy and the pain, tears freezing in the creases of his eyes. "Marry me, señora."

"In spring," she said, deadpan, gathering her instruments, "you can ask me again."

The tent roared. Even Halloran laughed, and then groaned, and then laughed again, and Tomas stood in the lantern light with his arms aching from holding them still and understood, with the quiet totality of an avalanche starting somewhere far above, that he was in trouble.

Not the kind his father would have recognized. His father's troubles were rivals and ledgers and the long war of reputation, and love, when Elias Vane bothered to file it under anything, was filed under strategy. This was different. This was the specific, unarmored admiration of a man watching a woman refuse to be anything other than exactly what the moment required — watching her take up a knife over a screaming man and a dying hand and do the necessary thing without ceremony, without glory, without even wanting the story afterward. His father would have told this story for twenty years. Mirabel would never tell it at all.

*His father hired the wrong Sterling,* Tomas thought, standing in the swaying lantern light with the smell of carbolic and blood in his nose. *Or exactly the right one.* Because the woman Elias Vane wanted was a key — a name, a bloodline, a route into a cache and a claim. What he had hired, what he had dragged up onto this mountain, was something his ledgers had no column for: a person who, given a dying man and a dead hand and a night at eleven thousand feet, simply became the thing the situation needed and asked for nothing back. You could not spend a woman like that. You could only follow her.

He followed her out of the tent, into the blue dusk, where the wind had dropped at last and the clouds were tearing open along the western ridgeline to show a hard scatter of stars.

"The mules," she said. Her voice was level again, but he heard the cost in it now, heard it the way you hear the current under ice. "Proverb and the mare. I need to look at their feet before full dark."

"I'll hold the light," Tomas said.

She glanced at him — a quick, assessing glance, the same one she'd given him across the floodwater at Riverbend, the one that weighed and measured and, lately, lingered a half-second longer than measurement required.

"All right," she said.

* * *

They worked by lantern among the picketed animals while the camp behind them banked its fires and the temperature resumed its fall. Proverb's near forefoot was the worst — the frog of the hoof deadened, the coronary band graying. The mare's ears had gone stiff and dark at the tips. Mirabel handled each injury with the same unhurried gravity she'd brought to Halloran's hand, cleaning, wrapping, deciding.

"They can't carry loads tomorrow," she said finally. "Maybe not the day after. We split theirs across the rest and walk them slow, or we lose them entire."

"That costs us a day," Tomas said. "My father's advance party is—"

"I know where your father is." She straightened, blew out her breath in a long silver plume, and looked east, down the glacier, toward the hanging valley hidden below the next shoulder of the mountain. Toward the cache. Toward the letter she carried unopened in the bottom of her kit like a coal she was afraid to breathe on. "A day is what it costs. I don't trade lives for schedules. I learned that lesson," she added, very quietly, "from a man who didn't."

Tomas said nothing. There was the quarrel, hanging in the starlight between them — the one she'd confessed to in the cave, the fall, the argument over credit, the whole buried architecture of their fathers' war. Every day it surfaced a little higher. Every day she let him see a little more of it, and every day he carried a little more of what he hadn't told her: the order in his father's letter, *burn anything else*, and the newer letter, the runner's letter, and the word his father had used about her — *keep her from reading them* — sitting in his breast pocket like a stone he'd swallowed.

"Mirabel," he said.

"Don't." She said it without sharpness, almost gently, not looking at him. "Whatever it is you keep not telling me — don't tell it tonight. Tonight we've spent enough blood." She picked up the lantern. "Tomorrow we move. The day after, if the weather holds, we reach the valley."

"And the cache."

"And the cache." She turned to go, then paused, and in the lantern light her face was tired and young and utterly unbowed. "Tomas. When we open it — whatever's in there — I want you standing on my side of the cairn. Not his. Think about which side that is. You've got a day and a night to decide."

She walked back toward the fires, and he stood alone among the mules with the cold coming down out of the stars, and realized his hands were shaking — not from the cold, and not from the lanterns, and not from anything the mountain had done to him at all.

Below them, invisible in the dark, the hanging valley waited with its plum-tree name, and somewhere in it, under stone, his father's ledger and her father's letter lay side by side in the frozen dark — two documents, two versions of the truth, and one day left to choose between them.
