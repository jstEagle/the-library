## 13. The Pass Below the Snows

The morning came up colorless and hard, the kind of light that made everything look already remembered. Mirabel stood at the mouth of her tent with a tin cup cooling in her hands and watched the pass assemble itself out of the dark — a notch in the eastern wall of the range, still two ridges and a glacier's tongue away, wearing its snow like a man wearing a coat too heavy for him.

They were ahead of schedule. She had known it since yesterday's noon sight, when she'd shot the sun twice and gotten an answer she didn't trust and then a third time to prove herself wrong. She wasn't wrong. By her arithmetic they would stand under the cairn by tomorrow's midday, maybe sooner if the wind stayed down, and every hour of margin was a gift she did not know how to hold.

Twenty years of running lines across three continents had taught her one thing above all others: arrive early, because early is the only mercy a mountain ever offers. But this morning the arithmetic felt less like mercy and more like a door swinging open on a room she'd nailed shut when she was nineteen.

"Boss." Odile's voice behind her, low, gravel over gravel. "Mules are packed. Tomas wants to talk route before we move."

"Tomas always wants to talk route."

"He does." A pause that had weight in it. "It's not the worst habit a Vane ever had."

Mirabel drank the cold coffee and said nothing. Odile had been walking a narrow ledge for eleven days now — loyal to the crew, loyal to some older promise Mirabel still hadn't fully pried out of her, and honest enough to hate the space between the two. Last night at the fire she'd said, unprompted, *When we find it, you let me carry it down. Not you.* And Mirabel had understood that *it* meant more than survey brass and oilcloth packets, and had said nothing back, because there was nothing yet to say.

She broke camp.

* * *

The last approach ran up a shoulder of gray rock where the snow lay in wind-carved slabs, blue in their shadows, squeaking under boot like new leather. They roped in pairs. Mirabel led the first rope with Tomas on her heel, and behind them came Odile with the injured climber — Halloran, whose frost-nipped fingers were wrapped in clean linen and who climbed anyway, stubborn as a burr — and behind them the muleteers with the lightened train, and the rest strung out along the line like beads on a wire.

Tomas had stopped talking about his father somewhere below treeline. He talked instead about anchors and angles and the quality of the ice, and he was good — better than good, careful in a way that reminded her uncomfortably of someone else she had loved, someone who had also learned caution the expensive way. This morning he pointed with his axe at a slope above the trail.

"Cornice. See how it's curled? If the sun gets on it after ten, I'd rather be past the gully."

"So would I. We move fast through the shadow and slow through the open."

"That's backwards from what my father would say."

"Your father," Mirabel said, watching her footing, "has never been underneath the thing he wanted to hurry past."

Tomas was quiet for three steps. Then, almost smiling: "He'd argue that."

"He'd argue with the mountain, too, if it would hold still long enough to lose."

The laugh that got out of him was short and surprised, and it did something to the air between them that Mirabel chose not to examine. There had been a night in the cave — Snowlight, she thought of it now, though she'd never say it aloud — when she had told six near-strangers the worst true thing about herself, and the world had failed to end, and since then Tomas looked at her differently. Not softer, exactly. Steadier. Like a man who had finally been given the whole map and trusted himself to read it.

They moved fast through the shadow and slow through the open, and the cornice held, and the pass came up to meet them.

* * *

It was smaller than she remembered wanting it to be.

That was the thing no one told you about the places that break your life in half: they are ordinary. A notch of sky, a saddle of scree and old snow, wind combing through it from the west with a sound like someone slowly tearing cloth. The Karagol Range shouldered up around it, white and indifferent. Somewhere below and south, twenty years and half a world away, a nineteen-year-old girl had run a compass line into a hanging valley and found what no one had found, and had carried the news up to a father who took it from her the way men of that era took things from daughters — without even knowing it was theft.

And here, at the top of the world's indifference, stood the cairn.

It was not the neat monument of alpine tradition. It was a working cache: stones piled waist-high in the sheltered lee of a fin of rock, capped with a flat slab, weathered but sound. Her father's work. She knew it before she was close enough to see the details — the way he'd keyed the capstone into two smaller stones so the wind couldn't walk it off, the way the pile leaned its weight inward against the slope. She had learned cairn-building at his knee. She had unlearned everything else at his grave.

Nobody spoke. Even the muleteers, who had no stake in any of it beyond wages, went quiet, the way people go quiet in a church or a courtroom.

Odile reached her first. The foreman stood beside the cairn with her mittened hands loose at her sides and her face doing something complicated, and when she spoke her voice was rough. "I helped stack it. Second stone from the left, that one's mine. We were arguing about whether to make it taller." Her jaw worked. "He said tall cairns invite climbers. He said low ones keep secrets."

"You were here." Mirabel's voice came out flatter than she intended. "You've been here before."

"I've been trying to tell you since the river gorge, girl. You weren't ready to hear it." Odile looked at the pile of stones the way you look at a coffin. "Neither was I, maybe."

Tomas hung back, axe grounded, snow steaming faintly off his shoulders. He didn't come closer. Later she would understand that this was its own kind of courtesy — that he knew whose ground this was — but in the moment she only registered the space he kept, and was grateful, and hated being grateful.

"Open it," she said.

* * *

The capstone came free easier than it should have, twenty years of freeze and thaw loosening the keying, and beneath it the cache sat in its nest of stones like a heart in a chest cavity: a brass-bound tin box, oilcloth bundles tied with waxed cord, a canvas satchel gone stiff and gray with age. The tin had rusted at the seams but held. Her father had been thorough. Her father had always been thorough; it was the one virtue that had survived every other failure.

Mirabel knelt. The snow soaked through her knee immediately and she didn't feel it.

The oilcloth bundles she knew without opening: instruments, spare notebooks, the emergency rations of a man who planned for other people's disasters. The canvas satchel was heavier. Inside it, wrapped in a wool shirt she recognized — the gray one with the missing button, the one he'd worn the last summer she'd seen him alive — were notebooks. Dozens. Field notebooks in her father's small, upright hand, their spines numbered in sequence, their covers furred with mold at the corners but intact.

His notes. Twenty years of them, or however many he'd managed between the day he'd stolen her discovery and the day the mountain collected its fee. The record Elias Vane had sent them all this way to retrieve. The record Vane had ordered burned, if his son's conscience could be believed — and Mirabel believed it, because she had watched Tomas feed pages to a fire once already and seen what it cost him.

Beside the notebooks, bound separately in oilskin, lay a ledger with a name stamped on the cover in gold leaf gone dull: **E. VANE — KARAGOL SURVEY, FIRST SEASON**. Vane's own expedition book. His original. Which meant the copy Vane kept in his office, the one Mirabel had glimpsed once as a girl in a house that smelled of pipe smoke and rivalry, was not the whole truth. Which meant Vane had known, all these years, exactly what was written here, and had wanted it buried at altitude rather than read at sea level.

And beneath the ledger, flat and alone, an envelope.

Not field-paper. Good paper, cream-colored, the kind bought in stationers' shops in cities, carried up a mountain at considerable cost and trouble. Her name was written across it in her father's hand — *Mirabel*, just that, no surname, as if the surname were a thing he had no right to attach to her anymore — and the ink had faded to the brown of old tea, and the envelope bore no date, but she knew. She knew the way you know a voice through a wall. He had written it the week he died. Perhaps the day. The handwriting had the slight forward lean of a man writing quickly, or urgently, or both.

The wind tore the cloth off the ridge and slapped it down again. Someone behind her said her name — Odile, probably — and she didn't answer.

Her hands were shaking.

Not the fine tremor of cold, which she knew intimately and could suppress with technique. This was deeper, structural, the shaking of a building whose foundations have been questioned. She reached for the envelope and her fingers stopped a inch above it, hovering, refusing the order she gave them. Twenty years of discipline, of counting miles instead of birthdays, of running survey lines across three continents precisely so that every day had a shape that had nothing to do with that summer — and all of it, all of it, had been scaffolding around this moment, and the scaffolding was coming down, and her hands knew it before she did.

If she opened it, her father would speak. For the first time in twenty years he would say something to her that she had not reconstructed, imagined, argued with, or forgiven in absentia. Whatever was in that envelope would become the truest version of him she owned, and every version she'd built since — the monster, the martyr, the thief, the fool — would have to be weighed against it. She was not sure she could survive the weighing. She was not sure she wanted to survive anything else.

"Boss." Odile's hand settled on her shoulder, heavy and warm through the parka. "You don't have to do it standing on the mountain. Nothing in there can run away. Nothing in there can catch you either."

"I know what it is," Mirabel said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "I know what he wrote. I've known since I was nineteen. I just never had the proof."

"Then you know it doesn't change what's true. Only what's provable." Odile crouched beside her, close enough that Mirabel could see the frost in her eyebrows, the age in her eyes that hadn't been there on the first expedition. "Your father wrote that letter to a daughter he owed something to. Not to a surveyor on a contract. Read it when you're her. Not when you're this."

Mirabel looked down at the envelope. *Mirabel.* The lean of the letters. The urgency in the ink.

She drew a breath that hurt going in, and folded the envelope into the inner pocket of her jacket — the same jacket, patched twice, whose lining still held the map he'd sewn there for a twelve-year-old girl — and closed her hand flat over it, over her own heartbeat, over the postponed dead.

"One more ridge," she said. Nobody asked what she meant. Maybe everybody understood: grief could wait at the top of the next climb, the way it had waited at the top of every climb for twenty years. Grief was patient. Grief was the best surveyor of them all; it always knew where you were headed.

They repacked the cache. The notebooks and the ledger went into the strongbox lashed to the second mule, and Odile took the lead rope of that mule herself, as promised, and dared anyone with her face to object. The instruments they left — they had their own. The empty tin box Mirabel righted and re-capped with the flat slab, keying it the way she'd been taught, leaning its weight into the slope. A low cairn keeps secrets. She left it keeping one fewer.

They made camp that afternoon in the lee below the pass, earlier than any schedule required, because Mirabel called it — called it against Tomas's raised eyebrow and the good light still on the snow — and pitched the tents in the wind-shadow of the fin of rock, and set double watches, and told herself the early halt was prudence, storm sense, the respect owed to altitude. All true. None of it the whole truth. The whole truth was that her hands had not entirely stopped shaking, and that she wanted one more night of being a person who had not yet read her father's last words.

She ate little. Sat late by the stove with the envelope's corner pressing its small square weight against her ribs through two layers of cloth, a splinter of the past lodged against the present.

It was full dark when Halloran, on first watch, came to the tent flap and stood there a moment longer than watch-relief required.

"Boss," he said. "You'll want to come look at the south. Lights. Moving slow, but moving. High route, by the look of the line they're holding." He paused. "Counting torches, I make it six. Maybe seven."

Mirabel stepped out into the blue black cold and looked south along the high ridge, and there they were: a string of small flames crawling down out of the col like embers shaken from a fire, hours ahead of any reckoning Vane's party should have allowed themselves, coming on through the dark toward the pass, toward the cache, toward her.

Elias Vane had not waited for the weather. Elias Vane had never waited for anything in his life.

Behind her, in the tent, the strongbox sat with its notebooks and its ledger, and against her heart sat the letter, unread, and the first snowflakes of the storm everyone had feared began to fall without a sound.
