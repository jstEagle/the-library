## 14. What the Notes Say

Tomas saw them first because he had learned to read the mountain the way his father read ledgers — by what was missing.

The morning was hard and blue, the kind of cold that made the glacier creak like a ship's rigging. They had reached the cache cairn the evening before and Mirabel had ordered a fortified camp on the shelf of rock below it: walls of cut snow, the mules hobbled close, watches doubled. She had not opened anything from the cairn. He'd watched her stand over it for a long minute in the failing light, her breath smoking, her hands — those hands that could splice a rope in a whiteout without looking — trembling at her sides. Then she'd said, "Tomorrow," in a voice that meant *not tomorrow either*, and turned away.

So when Tomas scanned the high route at dawn out of habit rather than fear, he was looking for nothing. What he found was color where no color should be.

A fleck of red canvas, moving along the skyline ridge two miles east and nearly a thousand feet above them. Then another. Then the glint of an ice axe catching sun.

"Mirabel." His voice came out wrong, too loud in the still air. He tried again, lower. "Mirabel. We have company."

She came out of her tent with her hair full of frost and her boots half-laced, and she did not ask him what he meant. She looked where he pointed, and he watched something close down behind her eyes — not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman who has been running from a thing for twenty years and has just heard its footsteps stop behind her.

"That's impossible," Odile said, coming up beside them with the spyglass. "The high route's still buried. Vane would need wings."

"He wouldn't need wings," Mirabel said quietly. "He'd need to not care how many men he spent getting here." She took the glass, braced it against a boulder, and was silent for a long moment. "Six climbers. Light loads. Ropes only, no camp gear worth mentioning." A pause. "He's pushing them hard enough to kill one or two. He always did think distance was a moral question."

Tomas felt the words land somewhere below his ribs. *He.* Not *my father*. She had stopped saying it days ago, and he had noticed, and had told himself it didn't matter.

"How long?" Odile asked.

"At their pace? Two hours. Maybe less if the ridge holds." Mirabel lowered the glass and looked at Tomas, and there was no accusation in it, which was somehow worse than if there had been. "You knew he had an advance party."

"I knew he had *a* plan beyond us," Tomas said. "I didn't know this one."

"You didn't ask."

"No," he admitted. "I didn't ask."

She held his gaze a moment longer, then turned to the crew, and the woman who had been standing frozen over a cairn all night was gone. In her place was the surveyor who had pulled three muleteers out of the riverbend flood, who had taken a man's fingers off at the knuckle by lantern light while the man thanked her between screams.

"Odile — break camp priority on the cache contents. Everything into the sled, weighted and roped. Ruiz, Harkness, get the mules loaded, we may move fast. Everyone else, ice anchors on the downhill edge — I want this camp able to shed a rope team or a wind, whichever comes first." She paused. "And nobody does anything stupid. We are not fighters. We are the best survey crew on three continents, and we're going to act like it."

* * *

They worked, and the red flecks on the skyline grew into men.

Tomas carried the cache sled himself, twice, from cairn to camp — the oilskin-wrapped bundles his father had sent twenty years of letters chasing. He did not open them. He had burned one page of his father's orders in the gorge and had given Mirabel half the truth that night, and every day since he had carried the other half like a stone sewn into his coat lining: *tell no one what the notes contain*. And beneath it, unspoken but heavier: *keep her from reading them*.

His father's runner had come through four days ago, half-dead off the high route, with a letter Tomas had read alone in the dark with his mittens off so his bare fingers could feel every word. *I provoked the quarrel,* his father had written. *Rivalry is a tool like any other, and I sharpened mine until it broke. The notes prove it. Keep them from her.*

He had not shown Mirabel the letter. He had not shown anyone. And now his father was descending the ridge with five hired men and the whole weight of twenty years of guilt converted into forward motion, and Tomas understood that the hour of choosing had arrived whether he chose or not.

By midmorning the advance party stood at the top of the icefall below their shelf, six figures roped together, axes planted, faces raw with wind and altitude. And at the front of the rope, taller than Tomas remembered, straighter than any man of sixty had a right to be after that climb —

His father.

Elias Vane came up onto the shelf the way he came into rooms: as though it had been reserved. Snow crusted his beard. One of his men staggered and sat down hard and did not get up, and Vane did not look at him. His eyes went past his son as though Tomas were furniture, past Odile, past the shivering crew, and fixed on the sled with its oilskin bundles.

"Misabel Sterling," he said. "You found it."

"We found it," Mirabel said. She stood between him and the sled with her arms loose at her sides, and Tomas thought: *she isn't afraid of him. She's afraid of what's in the sled.* "Ahead of your schedule, I'm told."

"My schedule assumed caution." Vane pulled off a glove and flexed his fingers, studying the camp with a surveyor's eye despite everything — the anchors, the snow walls, the economy of it. "You've built this well. Your father built camps like this."

"I know," Mirabel said. "I learned from watching you both try to out-build each other."

Something flickered across Vane's face — the ghost of a smile, or its corpse. "Then you'll understand there's no need for theater. The cache is mine. I funded the original expedition. I hold the contract your crew signed. Hand it over and everyone walks down rich, and none of us has to discuss what's in these bundles in front of hired ears."

"The contract says recover the cache," Mirabel said. "It doesn't say surrender it unread. My crew recovered it. Under my name, on my line, by my father's route — which, incidentally, you needed me to find, because you never could." She stepped sideways, casually, and now her body fully blocked the sled. "I'll take my fee, Mr. Vane. I'll even take your confession, since you seem determined to deliver it in person. But I read these first. Alone."

"No."

The word cracked across the ice like a whip report. Vane's men shifted; Odile's hand moved to the ice hammer at her belt, and Tomas saw the whole scene tilt toward violence the way a slope tilts toward avalanche — slowly, then all at once.

"You forget yourself," Vane said, softer now, which was worse. "You are a contractor. I am the principal. And those notes—" his voice caught, and for one naked instant Tomas saw not the titan of the profession but an old man standing on a mountain where another old man had died, "—those notes are the last thing Aldous Sterling ever wrote. They belong to the man who survived him. That is the whole of the law up here."

"They belong to *me*," Mirabel said, and her voice did not rise at all, and that was the most frightening sound Tomas had ever heard her make. "Because half of what he wrote, he wrote about me. You know it. It's why you hired me. You didn't want a surveyor, Elias. You wanted the key to your own lock."

* * *

They might have stood there until the cold settled the matter itself. But Vane's men were exhausted, and exhausted men do not hold formation, and the second man from the left — young, frost-nipped, terrified — took one step forward on his own initiative, axe in hand, meaning perhaps only to help his employer, meaning perhaps nothing at all.

That was when the snowbridge let go.

It happened below the shelf, at the lip of the crevasse field they'd crossed the day before — a sound like a door closing in the belly of the mountain, and then the young climber dropped knee-deep, hip-deep, gone to the chest in a bloom of powder, his scream very small and very human. The rope snapped taut. Two of Vane's men were yanked off their feet and dragged toward the lip, and the whole rope team became a knot of flailing limbs sliding on blue ice with a hole opening beneath it.

Tomas did not decide anything. Later he would understand that whatever he decided had been decided years ago, in a boyhood spent listening to his father describe Aldous Sterling's failures in a voice that got quieter and more reverent every year, until Tomas realized his father had loved the man he ruined more than he had ever loved anyone he hadn't.

He moved.

Not toward his father. Toward the rope.

He hit the ice on his knees at the crevasse lip, drove his axe in one-handed, and took the drag of two falling men through his shoulder with a sound he felt more than heard. Odile was beside him — he never saw her arrive — her boot pinning the rope, her hands hauling. Behind them, Mirabel's voice cut through the chaos giving orders to *her* crew, anchors going in, a second line running out, and within ninety seconds the mathematics of rescue had overwhelmed the mathematics of collapse: six men anchored, one boy hauled back from the throat of the mountain, coughing snow, alive.

When it was done, Tomas knelt on the ice with his shoulder screaming and his father standing ten feet away, having moved not at all.

Elias Vane looked at his son kneeling in the rope-work between himself and Mirabel Sterling, and something in his face finally broke its formation.

"Tomas," he said. "Step aside."

"No," Tomas said.

"It is not your decision—"

"No," Tomas said again, and stood, slowly, his boots finding purchase, and put himself squarely in the space between them — a space about the width of a climbing rope, which was, he understood suddenly, exactly the width of everything that mattered. "You taught me that a man's word is his instrument, Father. So here is mine. Nobody touches that sled. Nobody burns anything. Nobody tells anybody what the notes contain — except the person they were written about." He turned his head, not taking his eyes off his father, and said: "Mirabel reads them. Today. With me standing next to her, if she'll allow it. And whatever they say — whatever *you* did — gets faced in daylight, or I publish it myself."

The silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere below, the rescued boy was being wrapped in spare parkas. The glacier ticked and groaned under the sun.

Vane stared at his son for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had lost its command and gained something else — something older and wearier, almost relief.

"You sound like him," he said. "Sterling. God help me, you sound exactly like him."

"Someone had to," Tomas said.

Mirabel came and stood at Tomas's shoulder. She did not touch him. She didn't need to; the standing was the touching. She looked at Elias Vane across the rope's width, and Tomas watched her weigh twenty years in the balance of a single breath.

"Your man needs a doctor and a warm tent," she said at last. "We have both, such as they are. Bring your crew in. We'll deal with the rest when no one is dying." Her eyes went to the sled, and Tomas saw her hands begin, very slightly, to shake. "And then I'm going to read my father's letter."

She turned toward camp. The clouds were building in the west, gray and low and patient, stacking themselves against the peaks like a debt come due — and every experienced eye on that shelf, including hers, including her father's rival's, already knew what they were looking at.

The storm was early. The storm was coming tonight.

And the letter was still unread.
