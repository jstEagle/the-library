## 15. Crevasse

The ice held its breath.

So did we — thirty feet of blue glacier between Elias Vane's advance party and my crew, with the cache cairn sitting between us like a coin on a table neither side would reach for first. Vane had come up the high route in four days when the maps said six. His men were frost-rimed and hollow-eyed, and he himself looked as if he'd been carved out of the same weather that had tried to kill us all week: sharp, gray, immovable.

"Mirabel." He said my name the way a man sets down something heavy. "The cache."

"The cache is ours," Odile said from my left. Her voice carried flat across the ice. "Contract's with Sterling. You know it."

"I wrote the contract," Vane said. "I can amend it."

"You can't amend the dead," I said.

His eyes came to mine then, and for the first time since the railhead I saw what twenty years had done to him. Not triumph. Not even guilt, exactly. Something more worn than either — the look of a man who had carried a stone so long his shoulder had grown around it, until removing it would hurt worse than keeping it.

"The notes are mine as much as his," Vane said. "I was there. Whatever your father wrote down—"

"He wrote everything down." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "That was his gift and his curse. He wrote down what happened on this glacier, Elias, and I think you know what that means, because you sent your son orders to burn anything else in that cairn."

Tomas flinched. I didn't look at him. I kept my eyes on Vane because looking away from a man on ice is how people die, and because some animal part of me wanted to see him break first.

What broke instead was the mountain.

* * *

It made no sound at first. That was the terrible thing about it — no crack, no groan, none of the warnings we'd been reading all week in the glacier's creaking language. Just a softness underfoot, a settling, the way snow goes quiet before it gives.

Vane took one step forward. The step he'd been taking toward the cairn, toward the notes, toward whatever version of the past he'd come four thousand miles to retrieve or destroy.

The snowbridge over the crevasse dropped him like a trapdoor.

He didn't even shout at first. There was a white collapse, a spray of crystals, and then his arms came up out of the hole, gloved hands slapping uselessly at the broken lip, and the sound he made wasn't a word at all. It was smaller than any word. One of his men lunged; the edge crumbled another foot and the man skidded back on his belly, screaming something in a language I didn't need translated.

Everything after that happened fast, but I remember it slow, the way the mind saves what matters.

Odile was already moving left, calling anchors. Two of her pickets went into the ice with the clean *thock-thock* of twenty years' practice. I heard myself giving orders in a voice that belonged to someone calmer than me — *rope team two, belay line, nobody crosses the bridge, nobody* — and I heard Tomas answer, "On it," without hesitation, without looking back at his father's men, without asking whose side the rope was on.

Because here is the thing I understood in that half-second while the coils paid out through my gloves: there was no question whose side the rope was on. Ropes don't take sides. Ropes just hold.

Vane's right hand found the lip again. His left hung wrong — I could see even at distance that the arm above it wouldn't obey him. He was sixty years old, half-frozen, dangling over a throat of blue ice deep enough to swallow every argument we'd ever have, and the last thing in the world he expected was for me to save him.

I knelt at the edge — close enough now to see his face, gray-lipped, the mustache crusted with his own breath — and threw him the loop.

"Take it," I said.

He stared at the rope as if it were written in a language he'd forgotten.

"Take it, Elias. I'm not asking twice. The ice isn't either."

His good hand closed on the line. And then it was work — nothing but work, the purest kind I knew. Odile on the primary belay, feeding smooth. Tomas and the injured climber, Ruiz, hauling on the second anchor, their breath tearing white in the cold. Me flat on the ice at the lip, boots braced against a picket, taking Vane's weight through my harness as they hauled and I guided his shoulders past the shattered overhang where the bridge had sheared.

"Left hand on the rock — no, the *rock*, not the snow—" His glove scrabbled. "Elias. Look at me. Look at my face. Now the rock."

He looked at me. That was the strange mercy of it: in the end he did exactly what I told him, the way my father used to do, the way every man on every rope team I'd ever led had done, because on the ice there is only one authority and it belongs to whoever knows the line.

His chest came over the lip. Then his hips. Then we had him — five of us dragging him backward across solid ice while the broken bridge shed the last of itself into the dark below, and the crevasse breathed out its cold breath, patient, unhurried, willing to wait for the next fool.

We got him forty feet clear before anyone spoke.

* * *

Afterward, they told me I'd worked the whole rescue without once saying my father's name. I don't remember that. What I remember is the rope running through my hands — the particular live weight of a man's life on a line, heavier than any cargo, lighter than any grudge — and thinking, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter:

*This is who I am. This was always who I am.*

Not my father's daughter. Not Vane's rival's daughter. Not the girl who found the site first, or the woman who burned a telegram, or the legend Odile had followed down out of the Karagol twenty years ago with a dead man's name in her mouth. All of those were true and none of them were the thing itself. The thing itself was this: when the ice opened, I tied in. When a man fell — *any* man, *this* man — I threw the loop.

My father had taught me knots before he taught me letters. He had never taught me this lesson in words. He hadn't needed to. It lived in my hands, and my hands had never once asked me whose life was on the other end.

Mercy, it turned out, was not a feeling. It was a climb. It was the hardest route on the mountain, and I had just led it, and my arms were shaking, and I would do it again tomorrow, and the day after, forever, because that was simply the surveyor I was. You run the line. You record what's true. You hold the rope.

Even for him.

* * *

Vane sat propped against a pack with brandy at his lips — Ruiz's flask, pressed on him without a word — while Odile splinted his left forearm with a tent stake and the calm brutality of long practice. Broken, not badly. Dislocated shoulder wrenched back into place by the fall, which was probably the only reason the arm hadn't torn off. He would climb down under his own power or be lowered, but he would climb down.

His men hovered at a distance, watching me with an expression I recognized because I'd seen it in mirrors for years: the look of people recalculating everything they believed about a person.

Tomas crouched beside his father, checking the splint, and the two of them looked at each other for a long moment with the rope still coiled between them like a sentence neither could finish.

"You gave the order," Vane said hoarsely. It wasn't accusation. It was almost wonder.

"You raised me on her family's stories," Tomas said. "You don't get to be surprised."

Vane closed his eyes. When he opened them, he was looking at me.

"Sterling." My name again, but different this time. Stripped. "Twenty years ago I—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. My fingers were still trembling; I let him see it. "Not here. Not on the ice. If you have something to say to me, Elias Vane, you'll say it in the valley, in daylight, in front of witnesses, where it counts. Down there—" I nodded toward the crevasse, breathing its cold breath behind us, patient as ever. "Down there doesn't count. Down there everyone tells the truth because they're afraid. I want yours sober."

Something moved through his face — relief, maybe, or shame wearing relief's coat. He nodded once.

I turned away and found Odile at my shoulder. She handed me tea without being asked, and we stood together looking north up the glacier, toward the pass, toward the cairn, toward the letter in my pack that I still had not opened.

"You knew," she said quietly. It wasn't a question. "When he stepped forward. You knew the bridge was rotten. You'd flagged it yesterday."

I had. A hollow note under the probe, a seam of darker ice, the telltale sag of a snowbridge going thin. I'd marked it in my book and meant to reroute the whole party around it in the morning.

"Yes," I said.

"And you let him walk at it anyway."

The tea steamed between my hands. Somewhere above us, wind combed the summit ridge, sifting powder into the blue.

"No," I said. "I called a warning. He didn't hear it — or he chose not to. There's a difference, and I've spent twenty years learning it." I looked at her. "But I'll tell you what I did know, Odile. I knew that if the mountain was going to take him, it would take him whether I hated him or loved him, and my hate wouldn't weigh one gram on the scale. So I set it down. Right there at the lip. Set it down and picked up the rope instead."

Odile studied me for a long moment. Twenty years ago she had carried my father's body down out of these mountains. Twenty years ago she had watched a friendship curdle into rivalry and rivalry into ruin, and she had said nothing, because the dead ask nothing and the living ask less.

"You're your own surveyor," she said at last. "Always were. Took you long enough to run the line."

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of the serac field, both crews mingled for the first time — Vane's exhausted men folded into our cook rotation without ceremony, because hunger doesn't care whose flag you fly. I posted double watch on the crevasse line and rerouted tomorrow's approach wide around the broken bridge, and I checked Vane's splint myself before turning in, because he was my patient now whether either of us liked it or not.

He caught my wrist as I tucked the blanket edge under his good arm. His grip was weak. His eyes were not.

"There's a page," he said. "In the ledger. Dated three days before the fall. Your father wrote it after our last argument — the real one, the one that started everything. I've spent twenty years telling myself it proved nothing." His breath fogged and tore away. "It proves everything. Mirabel. When you read it—"

"When I read it," I said, "it will be mine. Not yours. Not your orders. Not your version. Mine."

He let go of my wrist. In the lantern light his face looked suddenly very old, and very tired, and for one unguarded moment it looked — unbearably — like my father's might have looked, had my father lived to be old and tired and sorry.

"That's all I ever wanted," Vane whispered. "For it to finally be somebody's. Truly somebody's."

I doused the lantern and lay in my bag listening to the glacier grind its slow thoughts beneath us, and my hand found the oilcloth packet in my pack — the letter, my father's handwriting waiting inside it like a held breath — and I did not open it.

Tomorrow. Tomorrow we'd cross the high col ahead of the storm line, and there would be shelter, and light, and time enough at last.

Above the pass, the sky had begun to fill with clouds that moved like something summoned.
