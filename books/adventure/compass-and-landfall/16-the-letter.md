## 16. The Letter

The storm that had spared them at the pass found them anyway, two days later, in the shallow bowl below the eastern ridge where Vane's broken leg made retreat a slow, grinding arithmetic. They had no choice but to stop. The snow came down like a verdict — thick, wind-driven, erasing the world beyond arm's reach — and Mirabel ordered the tents pitched in a horseshoe against the rock wall and the mules hobbled close, and then there was nothing left to do but wait.

Waiting was the one discipline she had never mastered.

She sat in her tent with the canvas bag between her knees. Inside it: her father's field notes, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with twenty years of cold; Vane's original expedition ledger, its corners furred with frost; and the letter. The letter was thin. One page folded twice, maybe two. It had waited in a cairn at eleven thousand feet for longer than she had been running from it, and now it lay an inch from her hands, and she could not make herself pick it up.

Outside, the crews were doing what good crews did in a whiteout: melting snow, checking feet, telling lies about how much fuel remained. She could hear Odile's voice through the canvas, low and steady, walking the injured man — Halloran, whose fingers she had taken — through some story with a punchline. She could hear Tomas's boots crunch past her tent once, twice, pausing near the flap before moving on without a word. He had been doing that all day. Giving her the room she hadn't asked for and couldn't refuse.

The lantern hissed. The flame bent when the wind found a seam in the fabric.

She thought about the crevasse. Three days ago now, though it felt like a season. The snowbridge letting go under Vane with a sound like a held breath released, and the rope going taut, and every instinct she owned screaming to let him fall — let the mountain have him, let the ledger balance itself. Twenty years of carrying her father's death like a stone in her chest, and here was the man who'd sharpened the quarrel that caused it, dangling at the end of a line, and all she had to do was nothing.

She had done something. She had anchored in, run the line, called the counts, and hauled until her shoulders screamed, because that was who she was when everything else burned away — not Elias Vane's rival's daughter, not the girl who found the site first, just a surveyor with a rope and people depending on her. And somewhere in the third haul, watching Vane's frost-white face rise out of the blue dark of the ice, she had understood that mercy wasn't a feeling. It was work. You did it with your hands whether your heart agreed or not.

Vane had wept on the ice. She had pretended not to see.

And still the letter waited.

*He wrote it down so the record would someday correct itself.* That was what Tomas had told her, half of it, by the river — before he knew the whole of it himself. And Vane, half-frozen and stripped of everything he'd built his life on, had confirmed the rest at the cache cairn: *Your father knew what I did. He knew what I took from you. He put it in writing, Mirabel. He was a better man than me, and a better surveyor, and I have spent twenty years knowing it.*

But none of that was the letter. The letter was her father's voice. The last of it. Everything else — the notes, the ledger, the confession — was evidence. The letter was a man talking to his daughter across the only bridge left to him, and she had crossed three continents and two decades and one icefall to stand at this edge, and now her hands wouldn't move.

Because as long as she didn't read it, he was still alive somewhere ahead of her on the route. As long as she didn't read it, she was still twelve years old in a survey camp, tracing the lines he drew for her, believing he was the finest man who ever walked a ridge. Reading it would fix him. Dead men stopped changing. Dead men became exactly what they were, forever, and she would have to live inside that fixed thing instead of beside it.

The lantern guttered. She trimmed the wick.

*You've climbed worse,* she told herself. *You've climbed worse with less light.*

She opened the bag.

The oilcloth crackled. The notes beneath were a brick of pages in her father's small, precise hand — she set them aside without looking, the way you step over a body to reach the wounded. The letter came out last. Her name on the outside, in ink faded to the brown of old blood: *For Mirabel. If she comes.*

*If she comes.* Not *when*. He hadn't presumed. Even at the end, even asking forgiveness, he hadn't presumed on her.

She unfolded it. His handwriting filled the page top to bottom, cramped toward the end as though the words were crowding to get out before something stopped them. The date was nine days before he died. She did the arithmetic without wanting to — nine days. He had carried this up the mountain knowing. Knowing what? That the expedition was going wrong? That he was? She read.

---

*Mirabel —*

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead or I have failed to come down, and both amount to the same thing for you, which is that you will have spent years wondering, and I cannot bear that. So I will write it all down while my hand is still mine.*

*You found the site. I want that in ink where no argument can wear it away. On the fourteenth of August you brought me the readings — your own lines, your own reductions, done by a girl of sixteen with instruments older than she was — and you said, Papa, there is something up the east shoulder, and I looked at your figures and I knew you were right, and I was afraid.*

*I was not afraid of the mountain. I was afraid that you had outdone me, and that everyone would see it, and that I would become what fathers become when their children pass them: a preamble. And so when Vane arrived with his party and asked who had found the trace, I said I had. I said it in front of you. I watched your face do what faces do when the ground moves, and I said it anyway.*

*There is no version of this where that was anything but theft. I have told myself it was for your protection — the east shoulder was rotten, the approach untested, a girl of sixteen has no business on seracs — and all of that is true and all of it is a lie I told so I could sleep. I stole from you because I was small, and being small in front of your brilliance frightened me more than any icefall ever has.*

*Then the quarrel. Then the fall. You know the shape of that night; you do not know, I think, how much of it was Vane. He came to my tent beforehand and told me things — that I had squandered my reputation, that you were the better surveyor already, that history would remember me as the man whose daughter surpassed him unless I did something. He sharpened what was already dull in me. But Mirabel, hear this: he handed me the knife and I swung it. Do not give him the whole weight. It will be easy to hate him. Hate him if you must, but hate him accurately, or it will eat the wrong parts of you.*

*I argued with him on the shoulder because he wanted to push the summit in failing light to beat Vane's party to the record, and I refused, and the argument went where arguments go when both men are ashamed. The cornice did the rest. I was roped; he was not; that is the whole of the technical report and it changes nothing.*

*What I ask is strange, so I will say it plainly. I do not ask the mountain's forgiveness. The mountain does not keep accounts; that is why I loved it. I ask yours. Not tonight, and perhaps not for twenty years — I have watched you carry a grudge, my girl, you come by it honestly from your mother — but someday, when you are old enough to have been small yourself, I ask you to believe that the man who taught you to read a contour map loved you more than he loved his own name, even on the day he proved otherwise.*

*I have written the full notes so the record can correct itself. Publish them under your name alone if you wish. Under both, if you are generous. I leave it to you, which is the only fair thing I have done on this mountain.*

*Keep surveying. Keep counting miles. But count them forward, Mirabel. Not back.*

*Your father,*
*Aldous Sterling*

---

The lantern flame stood perfectly still, as if the wind had stopped out of respect, and Mirabel discovered she was crying — not the way she cried at nineteen, alone and furious, but the way water finds its level after a dam goes: quiet, inevitable, without drama. She let it happen. There was no one to perform steadiness for, and even if there had been, she found she didn't have the strength to build the performance.

Twenty years. She had counted them in miles — she saw that now, saw it the way you see the shape of a valley only from the summit. Every continent, every contract, every birthday refused and cake declined and distance logged instead of lived: all of it was her father's letter, unread, dictating the terms of her life from inside a cairn. She had thought she was running from the mountain. She had been running from a sentence she hadn't read yet.

*Hate him accurately, or it will eat the wrong parts of you.*

She laughed — a wet, cracked sound — because she had hated Vane inaccurately for two decades, had built her entire self on the foundation of a hatred that was load-bearing and misaligned, and the structure had finally, mercifully, come down. What was left underneath was smaller than the ruin and stronger. A woman who could run a rope team in a whiteout. A woman whose crew had voted to follow her into a job she never should have taken, because they trusted her more than she trusted herself. A woman someone's son kept pausing outside her tent flap, giving her silence like a gift he didn't know how to wrap.

She read the letter again. Then a third time, slower, the way she'd read a difficult bearing — fixing each point, triangulating. By the third pass the tears had stopped and something else had arrived in their place, something with weight and edges, sitting in her chest where the stone used to be. Not peace. Peace was too soft a word. This was more like ballast. Something that would hold her steady in weather.

*Count them forward.*

She folded the letter along its original creases and did not put it back in the bag. She put it inside her jacket, over her heart, where another map had once lived sewn into the lining. Her father had navigated her long enough. From here on, he rode as cargo, not compass.

The wind had dropped. Through the canvas she could hear the camp settling — Odile's laugh, low; the mules shifting; the tick of cooling metal. She crawled out into the night.

The storm had broken. The sky above the bowl was scoured clean, black and salted with stars, and the snowfield lay blue-white under them like a second sky turned upside down. Tomas was standing at the edge of the firelight, keeping his watch, and when he saw her face he didn't ask. He just looked at her the way you look at a route you're deciding to trust, and stepped aside to give her the high ground beside him.

"Storm's breaking," he said.

"No," Mirabel said. She looked south, where the stars came down to meet the ice, and felt the twenty years settle behind her like a pack set down at the end of a long day. "It broke."

Behind them, in Vane's tent, a candle still burned. And as they stood there, the flap pushed open and Vane himself leaned out into the cold, gaunt and gray, his splinted leg propped on a pack — and his voice, when it came, was hoarse with something that wasn't just the altitude.

"Sterling." He was looking past her, at the southern horizon, where the sky had begun to do a thing she didn't like at all. "How fast can you get these people moving at first light?"

Mirabel followed his gaze. Far to the south, above the pass they'd descended, the clear band of stars was narrowing — and beyond it, low and vast and moving, a wall of cloud was building with the patient appetite of weather that intended to stay the winter.

They had beaten the storm off the summit. It had only gone to gather more of itself.

And now it was coming down the mountain after them.
