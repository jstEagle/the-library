## 17. Whiteout

The storm did not arrive the way storms do in the lowlands, with a polite gray warning and a day of grumbling sky. It came down off the summit like something released from a cage.

Tomas felt it before he saw it — a pressure change in his ears, a stillness in the air that made the wind itself seem to hold its breath. He was coiling rope at the cache cairn when Odile's voice cut across the ice shelf, flat and hard as a piton: *"Sky's gone wrong."*

He turned. The western horizon had vanished. Not clouded — *vanished*, replaced by a wall of white so dense it looked solid, like a cliff face falling toward them in slow motion. Above it, the last blue of the sky was being eaten from the edges inward.

"Time to the pass?" Mirabel asked, and her voice was calm in a way Tomas knew cost her something.

"Two hours if we push," Odile said. "Three if the mules balk."

"And behind us?"

Odile didn't answer. She didn't have to. The route they'd climbed — the high route, Vane's route, the one his advance party had forced through against every objection — was four days of exposed traverse. In this, it would be a tomb.

"We go forward," Mirabel said. "Down."

* * *

They went down into the teeth of it.

The first hour was merely terrible. The second hour was worse than anything Tomas had known in twelve years of mountain work, including the Karakoram season that had taken two fingers from his left hand. The snow came not as flakes but as a horizontal river of ice crystals, moving at speeds that made the anemometer in his kit useless because the needle had sheared off against its stop. Visibility collapsed to the length of his own arm. The world shrank to a circle of lantern light and the backs of the people ahead of him, and even those kept dissolving, so that he learned to navigate by sound — the creak of harness leather, the mules' complaining breath, Odile's voice calling the line like a bell buoy in fog.

Twice the column stopped dead while someone probed for the edge of the ice shelf with an axe. Twice they found it closer than any map allowed. The glacier beneath them was alive and shifting, and the storm was shaking it awake.

Behind him, Elias Vane moved like a man made of broken timber. The crevasse fall had cracked three ribs and something less measurable besides; he climbed with his arm strapped to his chest and his jaw set in a way that Tomas recognized from childhood — the expression his father wore when pain was information he refused to share. But there was something else in his face now, something new since the ice had nearly swallowed him. He kept looking at Mirabel. Not the way a rival looks at a rival. The way a man looks at a door he'd spent twenty years nailing shut, only to find it open.

By the fourth hour the temperature had fallen past the point where numbers meant anything. Frost claimed every exposed surface. The mules were failing — one had already gone down twice, and each time it took four men and a quarter hour to get it up, and they did not have quarters or hours to spend.

Mirabel called a halt in the lee of a serac the size of a chapel.

"We can't make the valley floor tonight," she said. Her voice carried without effort, pitched under the wind rather than over it. "And we can't stop here. Anyone who stops here dies here." She turned to Tomas, and the lantern light caught her face — raw-lipped, frost-nipped, utterly steady. "Your father's map. The high route. Is there anything between here and the pass? Any shelter?"

"There's nothing on my father's survey," Tomas said. "It was a summer map. He never—"

"He never wintered here," Mirabel finished. "But I have a different map."

She pulled it from inside her jacket — the hand-drawn thing sewn into the lining, her father's work, ink faded to the color of old tea. She'd been studying it by firelight for days, and now she spread it against the serac and weighted the corners with stones, shielding it with her body while the storm tried to tear it from her hands.

"Here." Her finger found a mark below the pass, on the eastern flank where the glacier bent like an elbow. "A cave. He marked it with a symbol — not a campsite, not a cache. Something else. And look at the notation." She read aloud, and her voice changed slightly on the words, as though reading them in his handwriting put his ghost directly beside her ear: *"Wind-sheltered. Dry floor. Will hold six men and gear through a blow. Tested twice."*

"Tested," Odile repeated. "Twenty years ago."

"Tested twenty years ago," Mirabel said. "Which is either a promise or a lie, and we're going to find out which, because it's the only option that isn't a grave."

Vane leaned close to see the map. Tomas watched his father study the dead man's hand — the rival's hand, the hand he'd hated and envied and, perhaps, in some cellar of himself, loved — and watched something move through his face like weather.

"That route isn't surveyed," Vane said hoarsely. "The descent below the cave. It's guesswork. It's a dead man's guesswork."

"It's a dead man's *best* guesswork," Mirabel said, "drawn by the finest surveyor ever to work this range, and annotated for a child. My father did not write instructions he hadn't verified. Whatever else he was, whatever he took from me—" her voice caught, held, steadied — "he was careful. We follow his line."

* * *

They reached the cave ninety minutes later, in full darkness, navigating the final pitch by rope-team and prayer.

It was everything the note promised and nothing more. A hollow in the mountain's flank, floored with dry gravel, mouth angled away from the prevailing wind so that the storm screamed past it like water around a stone. It would hold all of them if they gave up every pretense of comfort. They stacked the mules' loads into a wall against the entrance, dragged the animals in last — two were done, truly done, and everyone knew what that meant for morning — and then eleven human beings packed themselves into a space built for six and began the slow business of not dying.

They got one stove lit, shielded behind packs. They got hot water into everyone by rotation. They got the worst frostbitten hands and feet unwrapped and assessed — Odile's foreman Pietri had blackening on two fingertips that no one wanted to name yet, and the youngest muleteer, a boy called Anselm, could not feel his left foot at all.

Tomas sat against the wall with his back to the storm and listened to the mountain try to kill them. The wind had risen past noise into something geological, a sustained roar with harmonics in it, and every few minutes the cave shuddered as some vast weight of snow came off the slopes above and thundered past the entrance. Nobody slept. You don't sleep inside an avalanche's throat.

Near midnight, his father moved across the cave and lowered himself onto the gravel beside him.

For a long time neither spoke. The stove hissed. Somewhere in the dark, Anselm was quietly crying and pretending not to be, and someone — Mirabel, Tomas thought — was talking to him in a low voice, not about the foot, not about fear, but about something else entirely, some story, some ordinary human nonsense, giving the boy somewhere else to be.

"Tomas." His father's voice was scraped thin. "I owe you an accounting."

"You've given me one. By letter. Through the runner." Tomas kept his eyes on the stove. "I read it. I wish I hadn't. That's not the same as wishing you hadn't written it."

"I provoked him." His father said it the way a man confesses to murder, which in every way that mattered, it was. "Your grandfather's expedition. Sterling's notes. I told myself it was rivalry, that rivalry was honest, that the mountain would sort out which of us deserved the range. And when the quarrel came — and I made certain it came, Tomas, I chose the night and the words — I told myself the fall was accident, was the mountain's doing, was anything but mine." He exhaled, and the breath shook. "Twenty years I let a dead man carry my guilt up there in the ice. And then I hired his daughter to go dig it out for me. There is a word for what I am. I've been avoiding learning it for two decades."

Tomas said nothing. What was there? He had spent the whole climb watching Mirabel carry her father's ghost like ballast, and here was his own father admitting he'd been the one who loaded it.

"The notes," his father said. "Her father's letter. When we get down — if we get down — she should have all of it. Everything. Unedited. I wrote orders telling you to keep it from her, and I want you to know I wrote those orders as a coward, and I'm asking you to disregard them."

"They were already disregarded," Tomas said. "In the gorge. Before the flood."

His father was quiet for a moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a small, cracked sound, half pain. "Of course they were. You always were your mother's son." He looked across the cave, to where Mirabel sat with the boy Anselm, her hands wrapped around the boy's frostbitten foot, warming it against her own belly with the patience of someone tending a small fire. "Look at her. Twenty years ago I thought Sterling was the better surveyor and I couldn't stand it. Now his daughter is the better everything, and do you know what I feel?" He shook his head slowly. "Relief. God help me. Relief that someone competent has the rope."

* * *

Morning brought no dawn — just a slow brightening of the white blindness outside — and with it, the reckoning.

Retreat was impossible; everyone knew it without saying it. The high route was buried under forty hours of storm-load, avalanche-sown, unfindable. Waiting was impossible too; their fuel would last two days, their food four, and the cold was eating the weak first. Anselm's foot had gone from numb to necrotic-gray at the toes. Pietri's fingers were worse. Every hour above the pass was a debt accruing interest in flesh.

That left the way down. Her father's route. The untested line through the icefall below the cave — drawn from memory and skill twenty years ago, never climbed since, annotated for a twelve-year-old girl who was now thirty-eight and standing in the cave mouth looking at it.

Mirabel stood there a long time. Tomas watched her read the mountain the way her father must have read it, two decades ago, tracing the line with her eyes: the ramp of wind-packed snow, the rock island, the series of icefalls stepping down toward the valley like a staircase built by something enormous and careless.

When she turned around, the whole cave was looking at her. Not just her crew — his crew too. Odile, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman who had made her decision weeks ago and was simply waiting for the rest of the world to catch up. The muleteers. Pietri, cradling his ruined hand. Anselm, gray-faced, leaning on a crate.

And his father.

Elias Vane pushed himself upright against the wall, one arm strapped to his chest, and Tomas braced for it — for the old reflex, the assertion, the need to be the one deciding. He'd seen it his whole life: his father unable to enter a room without taking command of it, unable to follow where he could not lead.

Instead, Elias Vane crossed the cave, stopped in front of Mirabel Sterling, and did the hardest thing Tomas had ever watched him do.

He deferred.

"Sterling." His voice was rough, and he did not dress it up. "This is your father's route and your father's daughter. My judgment got us onto this mountain early and my body got me into a hole in the ice. I am not fit to lead us down, and I know it, and I'd sooner say it out loud than die pretending otherwise." He paused, and something older and rawer moved under the words. "Your father was the better surveyor. I've known it for twenty years and been too proud to write it down. If anyone can read his line and bring us down it alive, it's the person he drew that map for." He straightened as much as his ribs allowed. "Give the orders. All of us will follow them. Myself included."

The cave was silent except for the wind.

Tomas watched Mirabel take it — watched it land on her not as triumph but as weight, exactly the way a burden lands when you accept it willingly, shoulders dropping an inch under it even as the spine straightens. This was not what she'd wanted. He understood that suddenly, completely. She had come up this mountain to settle accounts with the dead, not to inherit the living. Command wasn't a prize to her; it was a load, and she took it the way she took every load — because someone had to, and because she could.

"All right," she said quietly. Then louder, turning so her voice reached every corner: "We descend today. We rest until the light is full — no one moves on this mountain half-blind. We rope in teams of three, strongest with weakest. The mules that can walk walk; the ones that can't, we butcher for meat and leave the rest. I'm sorry," she added, to the muleteers, and meant it. "We eat, we rope up, and we follow the line on this map. My father wrote these instructions for a child to understand, which means they'll be clear, which means they'll hold." She looked down at the faded paper in her hands, and for one unguarded moment Tomas saw what it cost her — the ghost standing at her shoulder, the twelve-year-old girl still folded inside the woman, the twenty years of distance collapsing to nothing.

Then she folded the map away inside her jacket, over her heart, and lifted her chin.

"My father tested this shelter twice," she said. "He tested every anchor he ever placed. We're going to trust him now, all the way down. And when we reach the valley—" her eyes crossed Vane's, and held, and something passed between them that was not forgiveness, not yet, but was maybe its foundation stone — "we finish what he started. Together. Both names on the record. That's the price of following my line, Vane, and you can pay it now or argue with me about it in the icefall."

Vane bowed his head. "Both names," he said. "Done."

Mirabel nodded once, and turned to the entrance, and began to study the descent.

Tomas went to stand beside her. Below them the icefall fell away in blue steps, storm-scoured and gleaming, terrible and exact. Somewhere down there was the valley, and warmth, and the rest of their lives.

"Your father's anchors," he said quietly. "You really believe they'll hold?"

Mirabel was quiet a moment. When she answered, she was smiling — a small, fierce, grief-struck smile that he suspected no one but him would ever see.

"He placed them for me," she said. "Everything he did up here, in the end, he did for me. Hold on to something, Tomas. We're going home by the long way — the way he wrote it out, just for a little girl who never got to use it."

She stepped to the edge, checked the first anchor with her axe, and gave the order to rig ropes.

Below them, the icefall waited, holding its twenty-year-old secret: whether a dead man's care was stronger than a living storm.
