## 18. Her Father's Route

The storm broke at dawn the way storms do in high country — all at once, like a door slammed open. One hour the world was white noise and white light; the next, the sky was a hard clean blue and the Karagol Range stood around us glittering, terrible, indifferent to everything we had survived inside it.

Mirabel Sterling stood at the mouth of the cave with her father's map open in her mittened hands and felt something she had not felt in twenty years: that she was reading a letter.

Not the letter in the cache — she hadn't opened that yet, couldn't, had wrapped it in oilcloth and strapped it against her back where it rode like a second heartbeat. This was an earlier letter, one written in pencil on survey paper when she was twelve years old, sewn into the lining of a jacket she'd outgrown twice before she ever needed it. *If you ever come this way, little bird,* it began, in his looping hand, *and I pray you never have reason to —*

She had read it four times since the pass. She would read it forty more before they were down.

"Talk to me, Mirabel." Odile came up beside her, breath steaming, face raw from three days of wind. Behind her the crews were breaking camp in the cave with the grim economy of people who knew the mountain was not done with them. "Say it's possible."

"It's possible." Mirabel traced the line with one gloved finger. Down from the col, through the upper icefall, along the medial moraine, past the seracs her father had numbered like old friends — *the Three Sisters, mind their skirts* — then the long traverse under the east wall, and out onto the glacier tongue where the grade eased and a man could walk instead of climb. "It's possible because he surveyed it. Every anchor point. Every distance. He wrote it for a child to follow."

"A child," Odile said flatly.

"He trusted me with it." She heard how it sounded and didn't take it back. "That counts for something now."

Below them the icefall spilled down the mountainside like a river caught mid-fall and turned to glass — a thousand tons of blue fracture, towers and chasms, the whole chaotic architecture of moving ice frozen into a single held breath. It was the kind of ground that ate expeditions. It was the kind of ground no sane surveyor would descend with two wounded men, eleven mules' worth of remaining gear, and a rival party half-dead from crevasse and conscience.

It was also, as far as Mirabel could tell, the only way down that wasn't suicide.

Behind her, someone coughed — young Pell, whose fingers she had shortened by lantern light two nights ago, wrapped now and strapped across his chest like a broken wing. He'd insisted on walking. She'd let him, because the alternative was a litter and litters killed men in cold like this.

Tomas came out of the cave carrying the last of the rope coils over his shoulder. He stopped beside her and looked at the icefall for a long moment without saying anything, which she had learned was his way of saying quite a lot.

"You've read it again," he said finally.

"Twice this morning."

"And?"

"And he was better than I remembered." Her voice came out strange, thick. She cleared it. "The route is good, Tomas. It's not just good, it's — elegant. He found a line through that icefall that nobody else could have found, because he spent six weeks on that glacier learning how it moved. The notes say so. He measured the flow rates. He knew which seracs were leaning and which were seated, which crevasses were bridged with summer snow and which were bridged with winter. He mapped the thing alive."

Tomas was quiet. Then: "And you can follow a dead man's map through living ice."

"I can improve on it." She said it without pride, the way she'd state a bearing. "He wrote it for a twelve-year-old with a week of provisions and no rope team. I've got thirty years of survey work and the best crew I've ever run. He gave me the skeleton. I'll put the muscle on."

From the cave mouth, Elias Vane watched them with the hollow, chastened eyes of a man who had fallen into a mountain and been pulled out by the daughter of the man he'd ruined. He hadn't argued about the route. That frightened Mirabel more than any argument could have. A Vane who deferred was a Vane who understood exactly how close to nothing they all were.

"Rig up," she called, and the crews moved.

* * *

They entered the icefall at nine o'clock, in the blue shadow of the east wall, roped in teams of four with Mirabel leading the first string and Odile the second. The cold was absolute, a dry burning cold that made the ropes creak and the ice sing — a low, resonant hum beneath their boots, the sound of a glacier settling its weight, thinking its slow thoughts.

Her father's instructions ran along the margin of the map in his small, patient hand:

*Enter below the great leaning block on your left — the one like a tilted house. Do not go near it; go under its shoulder where the old avalanche has packed firm. Count forty paces. There will be a crevasse you cannot see. Step where I have marked the X.*

She counted. At forty paces she probed with the pole and found nothing, probed again, and on the third stroke the pole sank through six inches of snow into air. A crevasse, invisible, exactly where the X sat on paper drawn twenty years ago by a man who had known this ice the way she knew the lines of her own hands.

"X marks it," she said aloud, and heard Odile behind her exhale.

They crossed on the snow bridge, one at a time, spread wide. On the far side the route bent left, climbing slightly — counterintuitive, wrong-feeling, the kind of move that made green climbers mutter — and there her father had written: *Yes, it goes up. Trust me. The low line looks easier and will kill you; the ice below is rotten where the sun gets under it. Stay in my shadow, little bird.*

Stay in my shadow.

She climbed.

The morning became a rhythm: read, count, probe, cross, tie off, bring the teams through, move. Anchor after anchor. Her father had specified deadman anchors buried in the ice at each steep pitch — *cut a trench two feet deep, lay the stake horizontal, pack it hard, and it will hold a falling horse.* She cut them where he said, and where her own judgment said his placement could be improved by eight feet or a better angle, she improved it, and every single one of them held. Not because the mountain was kind. Because between the two of them — the dead surveyor and the living one — they had covered each other's blind spots across twenty years of silence.

At the base of the Three Sisters — three seracs like veiled women, leaning over the route with their frozen skirts spread — the map said: *Pass quickly and quietly in the early morning. By afternoon the sun loosens them. If you hear them groan, run, and do not look back to see what you are running from.*

It was nearly noon. The sun stood high and hot on the ice, and the Sisters towered above the traverse, and Mirabel made the call she knew he would have made: wait in the lee of a pressure ridge until the shadows lengthened, eat, rest the wounded, and cross in the cool of three o'clock.

"We're losing daylight," one of Vane's men protested — Harrow, a big silent climber who had followed his employer up the high route and looked like a man regretting every decision that had led him here.

"We're losing nothing," Mirabel said. "Daylight doesn't matter if we're under those towers when they let go. We wait."

Vane, sitting against the ridge with his frost-blackened hands wrapped in wool, said nothing at all. But he looked at her — a long, level look, the first honest one she could remember from him — and nodded once, and that nod carried more surrender than any words.

They waited. Pell slept. The mules stamped and steamed. And Mirabel sat with her back against the ice and read her father's margins until she had them by heart, and somewhere in the middle of the fourth reading she realized her eyes were wet and freezing on her lashes, and that she was not grieving.

She was talking to him. Arguing with him, actually — *you should have flagged this crossing harder, Papa, a twelve-year-old would have walked right off the edge here* — and hearing, in the neat pencil strokes, the answer: *which is why I drew the X twice, little bird. Which is why I never doubted you'd find it.*

* * *

They crossed beneath the Sisters at ten past three, fast and quiet, boots whispering on wind-packed snow. Halfway along the traverse, the middle Sister groaned — a deep, grinding moan, like a ship's hull in a storm — and every head on the rope turned toward the sound except Mirabel's.

"Eyes forward!" she barked. "Move!"

They moved. Thirty seconds later, with a crack like a rifle shot, a slab the size of a barn calved off the Sister's flank and fell two hundred feet to shatter across the route they had vacated, spraying ice shrapnel that stung their backs like sleet. The concussion rolled over them. Somewhere behind, a mule screamed and bolted.

"Hold the rope! Nobody chase it!"

But it was too late for the animal — a gray pack mule carrying half their remaining food and both of the tents — and it went over the lip of a hidden crevasse at the traverse's end with a shriek of harness and a sound like a door closing. The rope snapped taut, held, and then the snap-link on its breastplate, thank God and good rigging, tore free, and the mule was gone into the blue dark with everything it carried.

Silence on the ice. Breath and heartbeat.

Then Odile, practical as gravity: "Food inventory. Now."

They took stock in the shadow of the last serac. The loss was real but not fatal: they'd have half rations for four days, and the descent, if the map held true, was two days to the glacier tongue and sheltered ground beyond. They'd lost the tents. They'd keep the tarps, the stove, the rope, the instruments, the letters, and the people.

"Supplies, not lives," Mirabel said, and heard her own voice steady, and marveled at it. "That's the trade we accept. Any trade that costs lives is off the table. Everyone hear me?"

A ragged chorus of assent. Even Vane's men.

They moved out into the long afternoon light, and the icefall began, slowly, grudgingly, to release them.

* * *

They camped that night on the medial moraine, in a hollow her father had marked with a small, almost tender annotation: *Good camp. Wind-shadowed. The view of the west wall at sunset is worth the whole walk — show Mirabel.*

Show Mirabel.

She stood outside the tarp lean-to while the stove hissed and the crews fed each other half rations and bad jokes, and she looked west, and the wall was burning rose and gold above the ice, and she thought: *I'm showing myself, Papa. You showed me. Same thing, maybe, in the end.*

Tomas came to stand beside her. He did that now without asking permission, and she found she no longer minded, which was its own kind of map being redrawn.

"The anchors held," he said. "All of them."

"All but the ones I moved."

"That's not what I meant." He turned to look at her, and in the alpenglow his face was younger and older at once. "My father spent twenty years telling me Aldous Sterling was reckless. That the mountain killed him because he pushed too far, too fast, that his ambition wrote checks his skill couldn't cash. I believed it my whole life. And today I walked down a route that man drew for a child, and every inch of it was careful. Every inch of it was love, Mirabel. You don't measure crevasses for somebody you don't love."

She looked at the west wall so she wouldn't have to look at him. "He measured them for himself first. For the expedition. I was — insurance. A copy of the map sewn into a jacket, in case."

"No." Tomas shook his head slowly. "Insurance is a number in a ledger. This is a voice. *Count forty paces. Step where I marked the X. Show Mirabel the sunset.* That's not a man covering himself. That's a man talking to his daughter across twenty years because he couldn't say any of it out loud while he lived."

The words went into her like warmth into frozen hands — painful first, then something else.

"I hated him," she said quietly. "For twenty years I hated him. For taking the site, for taking the credit, for dying and leaving me the hating."

"And now?"

She was quiet a long time. Below them the glacier groaned and settled, and above them the first stars were coming out over the col they'd descended, and against her back the sealed letter rode like a second heart.

"Now I think he was a coward about the one thing that mattered and brave about everything else," she said. "And I think I've been the same, only inverted. Brave about everything, coward about the one thing." She touched the oilcloth bundle through her coat. "Tomorrow night, when we're on the tongue, I'll read it. Whatever it says."

Tomas didn't tell her it would be fine. He was learning her too well for that. He just stood beside her in the wind-shadow her father had chosen, and after a while his hand found hers inside the shared space of their mittens, clumsy and warm, and neither of them said anything about it, and neither of them let go.

* * *

The second day the icefall relented by degrees, towers giving way to ridges, ridges to rolling blue hills of ice, until late in the afternoon they came down off the last lateral moraine and onto the glacier tongue itself — a broad, gentle river of ice running valleyward, smooth enough that the mules picked up their ears and their pace together, and the whole line of them stretched out and simply walked, for the first time in days, without probing or anchoring or counting.

Pell laughed — actually laughed, a cracked, delighted sound — and Odile let him, and even Harrow smiled his big slow smile.

Mirabel walked at the front with the map furled under her arm and did not need to consult it anymore. The route ran plain ahead: down the tongue, off the ice at the snout, through the boulder fields, into the valley forest where the passes closed behind them like a book shutting.

Two days. Maybe less, if the weather held.

Behind her she heard Vane's voice, low, calling her name — the first time he'd used it rather than *Miss Sterling* — and she slowed without turning.

"When we reach the valley," he said, coming up beside her, breathing hard on the easy grade, "there are things I owe you. Things I intend to say properly, in front of witnesses, in writing if you'll allow it. About your father. About what I did."

Mirabel kept her eyes on the route ahead, on the long blue road of ice running down toward green.

"You can start by walking," she said. "You can start by getting everyone down alive. After that—" she glanced at him, at the ruined man who had hired her as bait and been undone by the bait, "—after that, we'll see what the record requires. My father taught me the record always requires something."

Ahead, the glacier bent around a shoulder of rock, and beyond the bend, faint in the evening haze, she could see the dark line of the treeline — the first trees, the first shelter, the first ground in weeks that did not move beneath them.

And moving along the slope above the bend, small with distance but unmistakable, figures. Men with packs, descending toward the tongue from the eastern spur, cutting their line to intercept theirs.

Odile saw them a heartbeat later. Her hand closed on Mirabel's shoulder.

"Miri," she said. "Who else is coming down?"
