## 20. Names on the Page

The printer's shop on Calloway Street smelled of ink and hot lead and the particular dust that lives in old paper — a smell Tomas had decided, somewhere between the third proof sheet and the fifth pot of coffee, was the smell of his father's ruin being set into type.

He stood at the window while the compositor worked, watching the lowland town go about its morning. Carts. A woman shaking a rug off a balcony. Two boys chasing a dog down the cobbles with the whole-hearted commitment of children who had never stood on ice at fourteen thousand feet and watched a man they loved fall into a crevasse. The Karagol Range was two hundred miles north and invisible from here, though on clear days you could see the blue suggestion of lesser peaks along the horizon, like teeth behind a closed lip.

"Mr. Vane." The compositor held up a galley. "Page one. If you'd care to check the names."

Tomas took it with both hands, which felt right, because it was heavy.

*THE KARAGOL SURVEY: A COMPLETE ACCOUNT OF THE RANGE, ITS PASSES, AND THE EXPEDITION OF TWENTY YEARS PAST.* Below that, in type large enough to be read across a room:

*M. STERLING AND E. VANE.*

And below that, smaller but not small enough to miss:

*WITH THE CONFESSION OF ELIAS VANE, APPENDED IN FULL.*

His father's name first in the subtitle, last in the confession. Mirabel had insisted on both arrangements, and when the printer's man had suggested — delicately, the way men suggest things to women with frost-scarred hands and surveyor's eyes — that perhaps the confession could be summarized, she had looked at him for four full seconds and said, "In full. Every word. Even the ones that cost him."

Tomas ran his thumb down the column of type. His father's errors were all there, set in neat rows: the falsified bearings, the credit taken, the letters written to Sterling's backers undermining his rival's competence, the final provocation on the mountain that had turned an argument into an accident into a death. Elias Vane had dictated it lying in a cot in the valley town's single hotel, wrapped in blankets, three fingers bandaged, his voice flat as a lake in winter. Tomas had sat in the corner and listened to every word and had not forgiven him yet, and did not know if he would, and had begun to suspect that not-knowing was itself a kind of answer.

"It reads true," the compositor said, mistaking silence for doubt. "I've set confessions before. Smugglers, mostly. This one reads like a man setting down something heavy."

"He is," Tomas said.

* * *

Mirabel arrived at noon, which meant she arrived at half past eleven and spent twenty minutes outside pretending to examine the town's water tower, because Mirabel Sterling was constitutionally incapable of arriving anywhere on time if arriving on time meant admitting she wanted to be there.

He watched her from the window. She looked different in the lowlands — or rather, she looked like what she actually was when the mountain wasn't demanding anything of her: a lean woman of forty-odd years with grey coming in at her temples and a walk that measured distance even on cobblestones. She wore the old jacket still, the one with the map sewn into the lining, though she'd had Odile reinforce the elbows. The jacket had been through fire and flood and icefall. It would outlast them all.

She came in stamping the autumn damp off her boots and stopped when she saw the galley in his hands.

"Is that—"

"Page one."

She crossed the room and took it the way he had taken it, with both hands, and read the names, and read them again, and then did something he had never seen her do in five months of knowing her: she sat down on the nearest stool without deciding to, the way a person sits when their legs have made the decision for them.

"Both names," she said quietly.

"You wrote the contract clause yourself."

"I know what I wrote. I'm surprised it survived contact with reality." She touched the type — lightly, the way you touch a thing you're afraid will vanish. "Printers have ways of losing inconvenient words. Whole lines go missing. It's practically a profession-wide disease."

"Not this line." He nodded at the compositor. "I paid for the type myself. It stays until the run is done."

She looked up at him then, and there was something in her face he couldn't name — not gratitude, which she dispensed rarely and precisely, and not softness exactly, but the loosening around the eyes of a person who has been braced against a blow for twenty years and has finally been told it isn't coming.

"Sterling and Vane," she said. "Alphabetical order would have put me second."

"Chronological," he said. "You found the site first. Everyone knows it now."

"Everyone." She laughed, short and dry. "Everyone being two hundred copies and whoever reads the alpine journals."

"And the newspapers. I sold the story to the *Meridian Courier*."

"You what?"

"The confession. Excerpts. Enough to make it impossible to bury." He shrugged, feeling suddenly like a boy caught raiding a larder. "Your father waited twenty years for the record to correct itself. I didn't trust twenty more to chance."

For a moment he thought she would be angry — the old Mirabel, the armored one, who counted miles instead of years and burned telegrams unopened. But she only looked at him for a long moment and said, "My father would have hated you."

Then, before he could respond: "He would also have done exactly the same thing. So." She folded the galley with exaggerated care and handed it back. "Set it. Print it. Let's be done with secrets."

* * *

They ate late lunch at the tavern across the street, because Mirabel refused to celebrate anything on an empty stomach and refused to admit this was a celebration. Odile joined them, then drifted away to argue freight rates with a mule dealer, leaving the two of them alone at a corner table with bread and stew and a bottle of wine that Mirabel pronounced "drinkable" and then drank most of.

The tavern was loud with ordinary life. A card game at the next table. A fiddler tuning up by the hearth. Through the window, the printer's shop glowed amber in the afternoon light, and Tomas kept glancing at it, the way you glance at a wound to confirm it's healing.

"My father asked about you," he said.

Mirabel's spoon paused. "Did he."

"He wants to know if you'll take him on next season. As a backer. He says—" Tomas heard himself quoting and made himself stop, made himself say it plainly instead. "He says he owes you a career's worth of support and he'd like to start paying it while he can still climb to base camp."

"And what did you tell him?"

"That I'd ask. That you'd probably say no."

"I probably would have said no a month ago." She turned her glass slowly on the wood. "But he led the rope out of that crevasse rescue without being asked. Badly — his technique is twenty years stale — but he went first. And he signed the confession without a lawyer reading it over, which any sensible man would have demanded." She looked up. "Sensible isn't the same as good. But it's a start."

"So you're considering it."

"I'm considering everything." She said it lightly, but he heard the weight under it, the same weight that had been in her voice on the ridge above town that morning in spring — no, that hadn't happened yet; that was the compass, that was later — he corrected himself, that morning three days ago, when she'd shown him the spot where she planned to bury her father's compass come thaw. *Not to forget him,* she'd said. *To stop navigating by him.*

"What about you?" she asked. "What does Tomas Vane do now that Tomas Vane has betrayed his father, saved his father, published his father, and learned to tie a butterfly coil properly? Does the son return to the family firm?"

"There is no family firm. There never was. There was just my father and whatever he was building toward, and I was staff." He turned his own glass. "I haven't decided."

"Haven't decided." She tested the phrase like new rope, checking for frays. "You? The man who carried sealed orders across an entire range without opening them? Who followed a timetable he disagreed with for six days before saying a word?"

"I've grown."

"You've grown," she agreed, and there was warmth in it, and something else, and the fiddler chose that moment to begin playing, badly, which gave them both somewhere to look.

* * *

The question came at dusk, walking back along the river road, the printer's windows lit gold behind them and the manuscript inside going to press with the dawn.

It was Mirabel who broke the comfortable silence, which was unlike her; usually she let silences stretch until they became structures you could live in.

"Next season," she said. "What will you do?"

"I asked you first."

"You didn't. You asked what I'd say to your father's money. Different question entirely." She walked a few steps, boots ringing on the frozen ruts. "But fine. I don't know."

He stopped walking. In five months he had never once heard Mirabel Sterling say *I don't know* about anything — not a route, not a bearing, not the load distribution on a mule train, not the correct response to a whiteout. She always knew, or she always knew how to appear to know, which on a mountain amounted to the same thing.

"You don't know," he repeated.

"I don't know." She faced him. The river was doing its dark work behind her, carrying ice south. "There are three contracts on my table. The Salt Desert survey, which pays well and bores me. A government mapping job in the eastern ranges, which pays poorly and interests me. And nothing at all — which I haven't ruled out." She smiled, crooked and real. "Twenty years I've known exactly what the next season held, because the next season was always running from the last one. Miles, not years. Keep moving and the past can't catch up." She spread her hands. "The past caught up. It turned out to be survivable. And now I'm standing in a lowland town in November with no idea what comes next, and instead of it feeling like falling—"

She stopped. Started again, plainer.

"It feels like standing on ground I chose."

Tomas found that his throat had gone tight. He thought of his own father, who had never once in Tomas's thirty-one years said *I don't know*, who had handed down certainty the way other fathers handed down watches, and whose certainty had killed a man on a mountain. He thought of the sealed orders, and of the moment above treeline when he had sided against them, openly, in front of everyone, and how the world had failed to end.

"Freedom," he said. "That's what that is. I've read about it."

She laughed — really laughed, head back, breath steaming — and the sound went through him like a warm current through cold water, and he understood that he was going to have to say it, because he had nearly lost the chance once already on the ice, and men who wait for perfect conditions die waiting.

"Mirabel."

"Yes."

"When the snowbridge went, and my father went down, and you started organizing the rescue before anyone else had finished screaming — I wasn't afraid for him. I mean, I was. But underneath that, underneath everything, I was watching you, and thinking: *there*. Whatever happens after this, however this ends — *there*." He took a breath. "I love you. I've been saying it sideways since the riverbend, in rope-work and route choices and paying for typesetting, and I'm tired of sideways. I love you. Plainly. On the record. Set it in type if you want."

The river ran. Somewhere behind them the fiddler had found a better tune.

Mirabel looked at him for a long time, and he watched her do the thing she did — measure, calculate, survey the ground — and then he watched her stop doing it.

"Well," she said at last, and her voice was not quite steady, and he loved her more for it. "I haven't decided what I'm doing next season."

"No?"

"But I've decided I'm not deciding it alone." She stepped closer, close enough that he could see the frost-scars on her knuckles and the grey at her temples and the exact color of her eyes in the last light. "If that's acceptable to the crew."

"It's acceptable to the crew," he managed.

"Good." She took his hand — the left one, the one that had held the rope over the crevasse — and laced her fingers through his with the efficiency of someone rigging a anchor she intended to trust her whole weight to. "Because I'm told the eastern mapping job needs two qualified surveyors. And I'm told one of them is tolerable in bad weather."

"Tolerable," he said.

"Don't fish for compliments. It's undignified." But she was smiling, and she did not let go of his hand, and together they walked back toward the lit windows of the town where, in the morning, two names would go to press side by side and stay there.

Behind them the river carried the ice south, unhurried, certain of its course.

Ahead, in the printer's dark shop, the type waited — *M. STERLING AND E. VANE* — and beneath it, in the smallest size in the case, the compositor had set the dedication page that Mirabel had delivered that afternoon without explanation, and that Tomas had not yet read:

*For the one who drew the map, and the one who walked it home.*
