## 21. The Crew That Carried Her

The house on Alder Street had been a surveyor's boarding house once, and it still smelled faintly of pipe tobacco and lamp oil, as if the walls had absorbed twenty years of men arguing over maps. Mirabel had rented it for the winter with money that was, for the first time in her career, not earmarked for anything — no mule feed, no rope, no permits. Just rent, coal, and food enough for eleven people.

Eleven. She had counted them twice, standing in the snow-muddied doorway while Odile directed the unloading of trunks like a general deploying artillery, and both times the number had come out the same. Eleven people under one roof, which was eight more than Mirabel Sterling had lived with at any point in her adult life, including the years she'd been married, briefly, to a man who had complained that she kept her boots by the door as though she might leave at any moment.

She had. She always had. That was rather the point of boots.

Now the boots lined the entry hall in a row of nine pairs, plus Tomas's and hers, and nobody's were pointed toward the door.

* * *

The injured climber was named Ferro, though everyone called him Nine Fingers now, and he answered to it with a pride Mirabel found both absurd and moving. He had lost the fingers to frost above the pass — she had taken them herself, by lantern light, with the blade Odile held steady and the whole crew watching in a silence broken only by the wind against the rock — and he had made a full recovery of everything except modesty. He showed the hand to visitors. He showed it to the butcher. He had, according to Odile, shown it to a woman at the church social, who had fainted, and Ferro had considered this a triumph.

"You're favoring it," Mirabel told him, catching him reaching for the bread with his left hand only.

"I'm *respecting* it."

"You're letting it stiffen. Flex them. All six."

"Seven," Ferro said, flexing grudgingly. "You left me seven."

"I left you what the frost did." She put the loaf in his right hand anyway, and watched him work the grip, wincing, working it again. "It'll ache all winter. Ache is the price of keeping it."

"That's what I keep telling the woman from the church," Ferro said, "but she won't come round to hear it."

From the corner where the muleteers had colonized the good chairs, Hark snorted into his tea. The three brothers had shared a tent for fourteen years and apparently saw no reason to change the arrangement indoors; they had pushed two settees together and treated the resulting island as sovereign territory, complete with a kettle, a tin of hardtack nobody ate, and a running argument about whether the third brother, Pell, had or had not abandoned a crate of dried apricots on the glacier.

"You left them," Pell said, for perhaps the hundredth time.

"I *saved* them," Hark said. "There's a difference between leaving and saving."

"They were in my pack."

"Which is why they were saved. If they'd been in yours they'd have been eaten by Tuesday."

Mirabel let it wash over her. She had learned, in the first weeks of the winter, that this argument was not about apricots. It was about the night on the icefall when Hark had gone back for Pell's dropped rope coil in whiteout conditions and come back with frostbitten cheeks and the coil and, eventually, Pell himself, and neither of them would ever say so directly, so they said it through fruit.

The fourth muleteer, Sefa, was the youngest and the quietest, and she had claimed the window seat on the second-floor landing, where she sat with a borrowed book and read the same page forty times because the view of the mountains took precedence. Mirabel passed her every morning and every evening and never once asked her to move.

* * *

Odile ran the household the way she ran a survey line: with a ledger, a whistle, and an absolute refusal to tolerate deviation. Meals at seven and six. Wood split before breakfast. Boots dried by the stove, rotated nightly so that no pair went two days damp. She had drawn up a chore chart in her block surveyor's capitals and pinned it inside the pantry door, and she enforced it with the same flat stare that had once made a mule back down a cliff path it had refused for an hour.

Tomas had lasted four days before he tried to trade his turn at the wash water for extra wood-splitting.

"No," Odile said.

"It's a fair trade. Wood's worth more than—"

"No."

"My father offered you double wages to—"

"Your father," Odile said, without heat, "is not the foreman of this kitchen."

Tomas had looked at Mirabel across the room with an expression of genuine betrayal, and Mirabel had looked back with an expression of profound satisfaction, and after that Tomas washed dishes like a man doing penance, which, Odile observed, was the correct spirit for it.

That had been November. Now it was deep December, the snow piled past the sills, the passes long since sealed, and the house had developed its own weather system: steam from the kitchen, smoke from the parlor stove, and a constant low pressure front of argument.

Tonight's storm concerned contracts.

* * *

"There's no copper work in the Atasha basin," Hark said, slapping a folded newspaper on the table. "I've got a cousin in the rail camps there. It's salt and sheep and nothing else."

"Your cousin also told you the Karagol job paid triple," Pell said, "and we know how that turned out."

"It turned out fine," Hark said. "We're rich."

"We are not rich."

"We are *comfortable*, which is rich for us."

Mirabel sat at the head of the table with a cup of coffee going cold and let the argument break around her like water around a stone. This was the third night of contract negotiations. Next season's season was four months off and already the crews of half the territory were being signed, and word had gotten out — word traveled faster than mail in the survey world, carried in the mouths of muleteers and barkeeps and the wives of outfitters — that the Sterling-Vane expedition had come down out of the Karagol Range in February, having entered in October, having lost supplies but no lives, having surveyed a route through the icefall that the government assessor had called, in his dry official prose, "novel and evidently sound," and having published a report with two names on the cover and a confession appended that had made Vane's old friends stop writing to him and Vane's old enemies start buying copies.

Every outfit in three territories wanted a piece of whatever that was.

The trouble was that "that" was not one thing anymore. It was two crews that had gone up the mountain as employer and hired hands and come down as something else, and nobody, least of all Mirabel, had worked out what the something else signed next spring.

"The Atasha's a fool's errand," Odile said. "The copper's in the western spur, everyone knows it, and the western spur means the Kessin River crossing, and the Kessin in June is a wall of water."

"So we cross in May."

"In May the Kessin is *ice*. You want to take mules over river ice?"

"I want to take mules over river ice *early*, before anyone else has thought of it, which is how you get the good rates."

"That's not surveying, that's gambling."

"Surveying is gambling with better instruments."

Mirabel drank her cold coffee. Across the table, Tomas caught her eye. He was sitting on her left, where he had quietly installed himself sometime in November without either of them remarking on it, and he wore the particular expression of a man watching a family argue and enjoying it more than he could decently admit.

"Say something," he murmured. "You're the principal."

"I'm enjoying the show."

"You're the *principal*."

"Hark's cousin is wrong about the copper," Mirabel said, and the table went quiet, because that was the other thing that had changed: when she spoke now, they listened first and argued second, and the order mattered. "But he's right that the Atasha basin is open. What he doesn't know is that the assessor's office has flagged the western spur for a resurvey in the spring. Officially. There'll be a posting in March."

Odile's eyebrows went up. "You've seen the posting?"

"I've written the posting. I consulted on it in November, before we came down for good. They wanted a name on the recommendation that would hold weight." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "They wanted mine."

Silence. Then Ferro, delighted: "So we take the contract before it's even posted."

"So we take the contract *at* the posted rate, honestly, like a legitimate firm," Odile said, but her eyes had gone bright, and Mirabel knew that look — it was the look Odile got at the top of a pitch, assessing the line, already climbing it in her head.

"A legitimate firm," Hark repeated, tasting the words. "With a name on the door."

"It hasn't got a name yet," Mirabel said. "That's rather what we're arguing about."

"It's not what we're arguing about," Pell said. "We're arguing about apricot logistics. The name's been settled for weeks. Ask anybody."

"Ask anybody what?" Sefa said from the stairs, where she had appeared with her book, drawn down by the smell of the stew Odile had set to simmering at four o'clock.

"The name of the firm," Tomas said.

"Oh," Sefa said. "Sterling. Obviously."

"Sterling-*what*," Hark said. "You can't just be Sterling. There's already Sterlings. There's a Sterling freight company in Corvane. There's Sterling & Daughters, dressmakers, and Ferro wrote them a letter—"

"I was lonely," Ferro said with dignity.

"—and there's the *other* thing," Hark finished, and stopped, and looked at Mirabel, and the table did that thing it had started doing in December, this quick collective glance, checking the weather before saying a name.

Mirabel set down her cup.

"Vane," she said. "You can say it. He eats dinner here twice a week."

"He eats dinner here when he's sober enough to walk from the hotel," Odile corrected. "Which is twice a week."

Elias Vane had taken rooms at the Terrapin Hotel, four streets over, in December, ostensibly to be near the printer during the final proofs. Ostensibly. In practice he came to Alder Street in the evenings, sat in the corner chair by the parlor stove with a whiskey he increasingly declined, and listened to the arguments about routes and rates with the expression of a man watching a fire in a house he used to own. He had confessed everything in print — the sabotage, the provoked quarrel, the twenty years of a story built on another man's fall — and the confession had cost him his reputation and, Mirabel suspected, very nearly his reason to keep walking around in the world. What had saved him, if anything had, was that Mirabel had not let him be a ghost in it. His errors were in the report because the record required them; his name was on the cover because the record required that too; and every evening he sat in the corner chair and watched the crew that should have hated him argue happily about apricots, and seemed to be learning, slowly, painfully, what a thing cost and what it bought.

He had voted, by letter, for the firm to be called Sterling alone. So had Odile, actually, and so had everyone, in the way people vote when they think they know what the answer should be before the question's asked.

Mirabel had not decided. Or rather — she had decided, and was waiting for the right hour to say it, which was a habit she was trying, this winter, to break.

* * *

The stew was ladled out, the bread was torn, the argument migrated from copper to the relative merits of two rope manufacturers, and Mirabel ate with her crew around her and felt the strange new weight of it, this table that held eleven, this noise that was not her noise but had become, somehow, hers to keep.

It was Odile who stood up.

It was Odile who went to the pantry, and it was Odile who came back carrying a cake.

Not a grand cake. A plain cake, flour and butter and the last of the sugar Odile had been hoarding since November, iced clumsily because Odile could read a contour map blindfolded but had never in her life held a spatula, with eleven candles jammed into it at angles that violated several principles of structural engineering. And on top, stuck upright in the icing, a single surveyor's flag, red cloth on a wire stem, the kind they planted at benchmarks.

Nobody sang. Survey crews do not sing. What happened instead was worse and better: the whole table went silent at once, eleven faces turning toward her, and Hark — sentimental, ridiculous Hark — cleared his throat and said, "Well? It's the twenty-first of December. We checked. Twice."

"We sent a runner to the parish records in Corvane," Sefa added, apologetic, as though confessing to a crime. "In October. Before the pass closed."

"You *what*," Mirabel said.

"You never say your birthday," Odile said. "Twenty-two years I've worked for you. Twenty-two years, and I have never once heard you say it. So we found it out ourselves." She set the cake down in front of Mirabel's plate with the care of a woman placing a benchmark. "And then we waited for a year when you couldn't run away from it. Which, as it happens, required sealing a mountain range."

The candles burned. The wax began its slow descent. Mirabel looked at the cake, and at the flag, and at the faces — Odile's stern and shining, Ferro holding up his seven-fingered hand as if in oath, the brothers shoulder to shoulder, Sefa smiling, Tomas watching her with an expression she had learned to read on a glacier and could not pretend to misread in a kitchen — and she understood, with the clarity she usually reserved for instrument readings, that this had been coming since Utah. Since the telegram she burned. Since the letter she almost didn't open. Every mile since had been leading here, to a plain cake with a flag in it, twenty years late and exactly on time.

"I don't count years," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "I count miles. It's how I—"

"We know how you are," Odile said. "Make a wish anyway."

"What if I don't have one?"

"Then wish for next season's rates. Everyone else at this table is."

Laughter broke the silence, and Mirabel laughed too, and then, before she could think better of it, she leaned forward and blew out the candles, all eleven, in one breath, the way her father had taught her when she was small — one breath, Mira, or the wish gets divided — and the kitchen went dark except for the stove glow and the snow-light at the windows, and in the dark someone, she thought Sefa, whispered "what did you wish," and someone else, definitely Hark, said "don't tell her, it divides the wish," and Mirabel sat in the sudden warm dark with cake smoke rising and thought:

*I wished for nothing. I have it.*

* * *

Later — the cake eaten, the plates washed by Tomas under Odile's supervision, the brothers asleep in their sovereign territory, Vane's knock come and gone with his proof pages and his two whiskeys and his careful, mended dignity — Mirabel stood at the pantry door in her stocking feet, looking at the chore chart, and Tomas found her there, the way he always found her, without seeming to look for her at all.

"You knew," he said. "About the cake. About the records runner. You've known since October."

"I suspected. Odile can't keep a secret, she just hides it behind competence."

"And you let them do it anyway."

"I let them do it because—" She stopped. Started again, which was also new. "For twenty years I made sure there was never a date anyone could point to. No birthday, no anniversary, no home address longer than a season. Nothing that could be taken away. Nothing that could *fail*." She touched the edge of the chart, Odile's block capitals, the rotation of boots and wood and wash water, the small domestic machinery of a life. "A crew is different from a family. A crew you can disband. I've disbanded five. It's clean. Nobody grieves a disbanded crew for more than a week."

"And this one?"

"This one I signed to a firm today," she said. "Papers are with the county clerk. Effective spring."

Tomas went very still. "You didn't say."

"I'm saying now." She turned to face him. The stove had burned low; the light was ember-colored, honest. "Sterling and Vane. Both names. Not for my father, and not for yours — for the eleven people in this house who carried both our names up a mountain and brought them home. Let the world sort out whose name means what. Under this roof it means *us*."

"The crew will vote on the name," Tomas managed. "Formally. There'll be a meeting. Hark will make a speech—"

"Hark will make three speeches and Odile will cut him off at two."

"And your father's compass," Tomas said. "You've been carrying it all winter. I've seen you check it some mornings like a man checking a wound. What happens to it now?"

Mirabel was quiet a moment. Through the window, above the snow-loaded roofs, the peaks of the Karagol foothills stood pale under the stars, sealed and silent, holding their dead and their secrets and, somewhere high on a ridge, a cairn of stones she had built with her own hands.

"In the spring," she said, "when the trail out of this valley thaws — I've been surveying a new one. My own line. First route I've ever run that wasn't inherited, or commissioned, or run away from." She looked up at him. "I know exactly where it goes. And I know exactly what I'm going to leave at the top of it."

Tomas nodded slowly, and did not ask, because he understood that some coordinates are not for sharing until the survey is done.

Outside, the snow began again, soft and unhurried, filling the tracks of the day. Inside, the house settled around its eleven sleepers, creaking like a ship at anchor, and Mirabel Sterling — daughter, rival, survivor, principal, partner, friend, thirty-nine years old as of one blown-out breath — climbed the stairs past Sefa's window seat and went to bed in a house full of her own name, and slept without counting a single mile.
