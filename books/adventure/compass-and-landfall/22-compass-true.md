## 22. Compass True

The snow went out of the valley the way a guest leaves a party — gradually, then all at once. One morning in late April the low fields were white to the fence lines; three days later they were brown and steaming, and the river below town had risen to a brown roar that you could feel through the soles of your boots on the bridge. Mirabel Sterling stood on that bridge with her hands in her coat pockets and watched the water carry the last of the winter downstream, and she thought: *that is what twenty years feels like when it finally lets go of you.*

She had not slept well. That was nothing new. What was new was why — not dread, not the old arithmetic of miles run against memory, but something closer to anticipation, the restless energy of a survey line about to be walked for the first time. She had been awake since four, checking her instruments by lamplight though she'd checked them twice the night before. The theodolite was clean and true. The notebooks were packed. And at the bottom of her rucksack, wrapped in oilcloth inside a tin inside a wool sock, sat her father's compass.

It was time.

* * *

The ridge above town had no name yet. That was the point of it. All winter, whenever the weather allowed — and in the valley it allowed more often than any of them expected — Mirabel had gone up alone or with one of the crew and run a line: from the spring at the head of Alder Creek, along the shoulder of the ridge where the pines thinned to larch and larch to bare granite, down the eastern face through a saddle that dropped into country no contract had ever sent them into. She had shot the bearings herself, chained the distances herself, written the numbers in her own hand in a notebook that had no client's name on the cover. It was the first route in twenty years of routes that belonged to nobody's commission. It was hers because she had made it, and it led south, toward a range that showed blue on the horizon like a promise someone else would have to keep.

This morning the whole crew came.

Odile arrived first, as Odile always did, standing at the trailhead with her arms crossed and her breath smoking, pretending she hadn't been there since before dawn. Then the muleteers, Ferro and young Callum, arguing cheerfully about whether the pack string could make the ridge or whether this was a walking party, an argument they would still be having at the top. Then Anselm, the climber whose fingers Mirabel had taken below the pass, his bandaged hand tucked in his coat, his grin already in place. He'd learned to rig anchors one-handed over the winter and refused, with a stubbornness Mirabel privately honored, to be anything less than fully useful.

Tomas came last, which was unlike him. He stopped beside her at the trailhead and looked up the line of the ridge, reading it the way she'd taught him to read it — fall lines, aspect, the places where the snow still held shade.

"You're quiet," he said.

"I'm working," she said. "There's a difference."

"There is." He glanced at the rucksack. "Is it with you?"

She didn't pretend not to understand. "Yes."

"Good," he said, and offered his arm, formally, absurdly, the way a man offers an arm at a wedding, and after a moment she took it, and they started up.

* * *

The climb took two hours because Mirabel let it take two hours. She stopped at each station and shot her bearings again, not because the winter's figures needed checking but because she wanted the crew to see her do it — wanted them to see that the line was real, that every number had been earned twice, once in snow and once in sunlight. Odile ranged ahead without being asked and set cairns at the turns. Callum named the mule after every landmark they passed and was overruled every time. By the time they reached the summit shoulder, the sun was high enough to warm the granite, and the southern range stood up out of the haze, sharp-edged, unsurveyed, waiting.

Mirabel found the place she had chosen months ago without admitting she'd chosen it: a flat shelf of stone just below the crest, facing south, where the wind couldn't reach and the whole of the next country lay open below. She set down her rucksack and knelt.

The crew gathered around without being told. Nobody spoke. Even Callum had gone quiet.

She unwrapped the compass from its three layers. The brass was dull with twenty years of tarnish, the glass clouded at one edge where moisture had gotten in and never left. Her father's initials were engraved on the case — E.S., in a hand she knew better than any handwriting on earth, because she had spent her childhood copying it, and half her adult life refusing to. The needle swung lazily under the glass, finding north, losing it, finding it again, patient as tide.

"I found this in his effects," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; she had expected it to break. "After. Vane sent it back with the rest of his kit, boxed up like evidence. I never opened the box until this winter." She turned the compass over in her palms. "I carried it on every job for twenty years. Not in my pack — in my head. Every line I ran, I ran it against him. Every decision, I asked what he would have done, and then I did the opposite, and called that independence. It wasn't. It was just navigation by a different bearing off the same fixed point."

Odile shifted her weight. "And now?"

"And now I'm done navigating by dead men." Mirabel looked up at her crew — her crew, hers, chosen and choosing. "He wrote me a letter. He told me he was wrong, and he asked my forgiveness, and I gave it. But forgiveness isn't the same thing as putting something down. This—" she lifted the compass, "—this is the putting down."

She dug. The granite shelf had a seam in it, a crack wide enough for a hand, filled with gravel and old snowmelt, and she worked it clear with her fingers and then with the small trowel from her kit while the crew watched. When the cavity was deep enough she wrapped the compass back in its oilcloth, and then — after a moment's thought — she added the notebook. The winter notebook, the one with the pass route in it, the descent her father had drawn for a twelve-year-old girl and Mirabel had finished herself. Let the mountain keep the record. Mountains kept records better than archives did.

She filled the seam back with gravel and set a small cairn on top, five stones, no more. Not a grave. A benchmark.

"There," she said quietly. "Not to forget him. To stop following him."

Anselm cleared his throat. "Should we say something?"

"Say the bearing," said Odile.

So Mirabel stood, opened her own instrument — her theodolite, her own name scratched into the tripod leg where her father's initials had been burned into the compass — and shot the line south, across the saddle, down toward the blue range on the horizon. She read the figure aloud, degrees and minutes, precise to the second, and Callum wrote it in the log in his careful schoolroom hand, and that was the ceremony. A bearing instead of a eulogy. A direction instead of a debt.

Tomas put his hand on the cairn briefly, lightly, the way you touch a shoulder. "He'd have hated this," he said.

"He'd have understood it," Mirabel said. "Eventually. He was slow, but he got there in the end. It's in the notes."

That got a laugh out of all of them, even Odile, and the sound of it went rolling off the ridge and out over the valley, and Mirabel thought: *there. That's the sound of a family. It argues about mules.*

* * *

They ate lunch on the summit shoulder, hard bread and hard cheese and Odile's miraculous sausage, and the talk turned — as it had turned every evening all winter — to the season ahead. There were contracts on the table in the boarding house downstairs: a boundary survey for the mining company, tedious and lucrative; a government road reconnaissance that Odile wanted and Mirabel didn't; and, folded separately in Mirabel's drawer, a letter that had come in March on thin foreign paper, requesting a survey party for the southern range, the blue one, the unnamed one, with terms generous enough to suggest the requester knew exactly how difficult the work would be.

She hadn't answered it yet.

"The road job pays through October," Ferro said, mouth full. "October money buys February bread."

"October money buys boredom," said Odile. "You've never met a fence line you liked, Ferro."

"I've never met a fence line that tried to kill me, either."

"Where's the fun in that?" said Anselm, wiggling his nine fingers.

Tomas said nothing. He was looking south, at the range, and Mirabel watched him look and knew he was doing the arithmetic she had already done: the passes open in June, closed in October, five months for work that the letter's own estimates suggested needed six. It was either impossible or it was the only job worth taking, depending entirely on who you asked, and she suspected that everyone at this lunch had already decided which it was and was simply waiting for her.

"We could do both," Callum said suddenly. Everyone looked at him. He flushed. "Well — split crews. Odile takes the road job with Ferro and the string, we finish by September, and—"

"And you'd trust me with your mules," said Odile dryly, "while you go chasing glaciers with a man whose father bankrupted two expeditions."

"My father," Tomas said mildly, "is currently re-roofing the church in repayment for past sins, and has asked me to tell you, Mirabel, that he will fund the southern expedition himself if the client falls through. His exact words were, and I quote, 'as restitution, and because she'll do it better than I ever did.'"

The silence that followed had a different texture than the silences of a year ago. Mirabel looked at Tomas, and Tomas looked back, steady, and she felt the old reflexive flinch — *Vane money, Vane name, Vane shadow* — and let it pass through her and out, like weather. Elias Vane had spent a lifetime trying to own the story. Last autumn she had taken the story away from him and given it back to the dead, and he had signed his confession to the printer with a shaking hand and meant it. People changed. She was living proof that people changed, if they were willing to bury the right things.

"I haven't decided," she said.

Everyone waited.

"That used to mean I was running," she went on, looking south again. "Undecided meant uncommitted, uncommitted meant gone by morning. Twenty years of leaving before anything could hold still long enough to know me." She picked up a pebble, weighed it, set it down precisely at the edge of the shelf, a marker for a station she might never occupy. "It doesn't mean that anymore. Undecided means the road's open. It means I get to choose instead of flee. I'm learning the difference. It's slower than I'd like."

"It's slower than most things," Odile agreed. "Most good things are."

* * *

They came down off the ridge in the long gold light of late afternoon, and at the saddle where the new trail branched — east toward town, south toward everything else — the crew stopped without discussing it, the way crews do, and looked at her.

"So," said Odile. "The letter in the drawer. Do we have a job?"

Mirabel took her time answering. Below them the valley was turning green at the edges; behind them the Karagol passes were closing their white doors for another year, keeping her father's compass and her finished map and all the weight she'd carried up there; ahead of them the southern range stood in the haze, unmapped, unnamed on any chart she trusted, its high country showing glacier ice even in April.

She thought about her father drawing a map for a twelve-year-old girl and sewing it into her jacket lining, believing — correctly, in the end — that she would need it someday. She thought about the note she had left unsigned in the margin of the manuscript proofs: *all bearings verified by M. Sterling, field season of the Karagol survey.* Her name. Just her name, standing alone on the page, needing no one else's beside it to be true.

"Yes," she said. "We have a job. We leave when the passes open."

Callum whooped. Ferro began negotiating immediately for a larger share of the mule budget. Anselm asked, with terrible brightness, whether the southern range was known for crevasses, and Odile told him that was the wrong question, and Tomas laughed and put his hand at the small of Mirabel's back, light as the touch he'd given the cairn.

She turned south. The whole crew turned with her, six shadows stretching long down the new-cut trail, and at the head of it, under five small stones, a brass needle settled at last and pointed north, holding its line, keeping its watch, needing nothing from anyone anymore.

Mirabel Sterling checked her own compass — the one in her chest, the one that had taken twenty years to calibrate — and found it true.

"South, then," she said. "Let's go see what's there."

And they went.
