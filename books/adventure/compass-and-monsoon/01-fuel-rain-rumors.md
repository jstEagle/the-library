## 1. Fuel, Rain, Rumors

The rain came in off the coast at four in the morning, the way it always did in the weeks before the monsoon decided to be honest about itself, and Gideon Zhou stood under the corrugated overhang of her hangar at Puerto Lobo airstrip and listened to it hammer the tin like a fist on a door she didn't want to answer.

She had learned to read rain the way other pilots read instruments. This was the soft kind, the warm-up act — a curtain of gray that would burn off by seven and leave the air steaming and the strip too wet for anything but patience. The real monsoon was still upriver somewhere, gathering its argument. She gave it three weeks. Maybe two.

"Three weeks," she said aloud, to no one, because talking to yourself was one of the occupational hazards of being the only pilot within two hundred kilometers who hadn't died, quit, or been arrested.

Her plane sat in the hangar with its cowling off, and she looked at it the way you look at an old friend who's been telling you small lies about their health. The Cessna 206 was older than her career, older than her exile, older than most of the government that kept trying to claim jurisdiction over it. Its registration papers were soft as cloth from handling. Its engine had 4,100 hours on it and opinions about all of them. She'd named it *Constance* eleven years ago, back when naming things still felt like something a person did without irony, and the name had outlasted everything else she'd once been sure of.

She wiped down the number-three cylinder with a rag, checked the compression by feel and memory rather than gauge, and told herself — as she told herself every month — that this was the year she'd do the top overhaul properly. And every month the money went instead into fuel, into landing fees paid in cash to men who didn't ask questions, into the quiet, unglamorous tax of staying airborne in a country where staying visible was the same thing as staying vulnerable.

Three years. That was the arithmetic she lived inside. Three years since she'd landed at Puerto Lobo with a duffel bag, a stack of cash wrapped in oilcloth, and a story about wanting to fly bush routes for the quiet of it. Three years of measured distance — not hiding, exactly, because hiding invited looking, but never quite arriving anywhere either. She flew mail and medicine and the occasional coffin into forty-one strips so short and muddy they existed mostly as rumors themselves, and she was known across the interior as a woman who could put a plane down on a river gravel bar in fog and take off again before the coffee cooled, and who would tell you nothing whatsoever about herself while doing it.

That reputation had cost her more than one friendship. It had also kept her alive, which she considered a fair trade, most days.

* * *

By seven the rain had thinned to a drizzle and the gossip had begun, because at Puerto Lobo the two arrived together the way thunder follows lightning.

The airstrip's social center was the fuel shed, a lean-to of rusted tin where old Emilio kept the drums of avgas and the only functioning radio outside Gideon's own cockpit, and where every morning between six and eight the town's information economy conducted its trading session. Pilots, if there had been any besides Gideon, would have been the brokers. In their absence the market ran on boatmen, mission nurses, police auxiliaries passing through, and Emilio himself, who heard everything and repeated most of it, carefully calibrated to whoever was buying.

Gideon bought, though she'd have denied it under oath. A pilot who didn't know what was moving on the rivers was a pilot who landed into trouble. So she carried her thermos down to the shed each morning, and she drank her coffee standing just outside the awning's drip line, and she let the talk wash over her the way she let the weather: noting what mattered, discarding what didn't.

This morning the talk was about water levels and a wedding in San Fermin and the price of diesel, and then, in the way such things always arrived — sideways, buried in the middle of something else — it was about a missing survey team.

"—up past the Serrania bend, government people, mapping the watershed or some such," said Raul Mendez, who ran supplies to the mining camps and therefore knew everything and trusted none of it. "Two weeks overdue at their check-in. My cousin flew supply for them last season. Says they were well equipped, satellite phone, the lot."

"Government loses things," said Emilio comfortably, cranking the pump handle. "Loses money, loses elections, loses survey teams. Then finds them again after the budget review."

"They're saying a helicopter went up Sunday."

"A helicopter." Emilio spat, with feeling. Government helicopters and Emilio had a history involving an unpaid fuel debt from a decade earlier, a history he raised at least monthly, liturgically.

Gideon sipped her coffee and filed it. A survey team, overdue. A helicopter already searching, which meant the government took it seriously, which meant it wasn't her problem. She flew medicine, not search-and-rescue; the distinction was load-bearing in her life and she maintained it carefully, the way she maintained the engine — not because it couldn't be pushed further, but because pushing it led to places she'd sworn off.

"You'd know the country up there," Raul said, turning to her with the mild, angled curiosity of a man who'd learned that direct questions got you nothing from Gideon Zhou. "Serrania headwaters. Bad flying?"

"Bad everything," she said. "Weather builds early this time of year. Cumulus by ten, storms by noon, and the valleys fill with cloud you can't climb out of because the passes are too high for a loaded 206." She shrugged. "If they're down in there, they're down in there until the pattern breaks."

"And if they're not down? If they walked out?"

"Then someone will find them walking, and this will all have been a very expensive misunderstanding." She drained her cup. "Any word on when the medical consignment lands?"

"Thursday, maybe Friday. The coastal boat's late."

Of course it was. She nodded and walked back through the drizzle toward the hangar, and behind her the conversation moved on to the wedding, and she noted — with the small, cold diligence she applied to everything — that she had dismissed the story in under ninety seconds, and that some part of her had been relieved to dismiss it.

That part she didn't examine. There was a whole province of her mind she didn't examine, fenced and posted, and she'd gotten very good at flying around it.

* * *

Thursday brought the consignment and the first real heat of the day together, and Gideon spent the morning loading *Constance* by hand in air thick enough to chew.

The cargo was the usual gospel of the interior: penicillin and antimalarials in foam boxes, deworming tablets, a crate of oral rehydration salts, dressings, sutures, a replacement oxygen regulator for the clinic at Alto Verde, and — packed last, wedged against the bulkhead where it wouldn't shift — a wooden box marked FRAGILE that held vaccines and dry ice and her professional conscience. Forty-one stops' worth of trust, weighed and balanced. She did the weight-and-balance twice, because the numbers were the one thing in her life that had never lied to her, and because doing them twice was a form of prayer she still believed in.

Her route ran counterclockwise into the interior: Alto Verde first, then the river villages along the lower Cobre — Santa Marta del Agua, Los Humildes, the fishing settlements whose names appeared on no map printed south of the capital — then a long leg northwest toward the foothills, skirting the Serrania headwaters, and home before the afternoon buildup ate the sky.

She took off at 0640 into air like warm milk, climbed on one magneto's grace and the other's mercy, and pointed the nose at the green wall of the interior, and for the next five hours she was, briefly and completely, unhaunted.

There was no other way to describe it. Flying was the only room in her life where the past didn't follow her in. Up here the world reduced itself to honest quantities: airspeed, altitude, fuel, weather, the weight of what you carried. The land below unrolled its slow argument of river and ridge and unbroken canopy, and every decision was hers alone and every consequence arrived immediately and proportionally, which was more than could be said for any decision she'd made in the life she'd left. Her hands knew what to do. Her hands had always known what to do. It was the rest of her that had once, catastrophically, failed to.

At Alto Verde the nurse flagged her down with the regulator and a complaint about the coastal boat, and at Santa Marta del Agua the children ran the length of the strip beside her rolling wake the way they always did, shrieking, and at Los Humildes she delivered the rehydration salts and drank sweet tea she didn't want with the headman, who talked about fish catches and said nothing about the mine upstream, in the elaborate, careful way people say nothing when saying something has been made dangerous.

She noticed that. She filed it too. The mine was three years old and state-sanctioned and nobody in the river villages ever mentioned it, and the silence had a texture she recognized from her previous life — the particular quality of quiet that isn't peace but instruction.

But the tea was sweet and the sky was holding, and she banked northwest on schedule with an hour of light and a tailwind, and it wasn't until she crossed the Serrania foothills at nine thousand feet, the headwaters glittering far below through a break in the cumulus, that the morning's rumor caught up with her.

She saw it before she understood she was seeing it: a scar of pale earth high in a valley of the headwaters, rectangular, deliberate, wrong. A clearing where no clearing should be, cut into forest that had been continuous since before maps. Even at this distance, even at speed, her eye — trained by thousands of hours of reading ground the way sailors read water — catalogued it automatically. Cleared vegetation stacked, not burned. Something pale at the center. Tents, maybe. Vehicles, maybe.

A survey camp. Theirs, presumably. Sitting right where it should be, visible, findable, two weeks after its owners had supposedly vanished from it.

Which meant either the team had come back to a camp everyone said they'd abandoned — or the helicopter searchers had found this already and the radio chatter simply hadn't reached Puerto Lobo — or the camp had been sitting there, empty and orderly, while the whole country above it argued about weddings and diesel prices.

Or, said the small cold voice she usually declined to seat at the table, it means somebody wants that camp found exactly as it is.

She held her heading. She was loaded, the weather was building over the passes ahead, and a government survey site was government business with teeth in it. She had a rule about things with teeth: she fed them nothing, especially not attention.

But her eyes went back to the clearing. Twice. Three times. She made herself look away on the fourth pass and put the ridgeline between herself and it, and she was aware — distantly, clinically, the way you're aware of a tooth starting to ache — that the dismissal she'd performed so smoothly in the fuel shed had been exactly that: a performance. You don't rehearse indifference that well unless the thing interests you.

* * *

She was on final into Puerto Lobo at 1530, low over the flooded grass with the afternoon storm shouldering up black behind the eastern hills, when the engine spoke to her.

Not dramatically. That was the thing about *Constance* — she never screamed, she conversed. A hesitation on the throttle advance, a roughness in the run-up that smoothed and returned, a vibration at cruise that lived just below the threshold of alarm. Gideon had spent three years listening to that conversation and translating it: *I am tired. I am owed an overhaul. I will keep carrying you, but we are running on credit.*

Today the message had a new clause. As she flared, there was a sound from forward of the firewall — metallic, brief, like a coin dropped into an empty drum — and the oil pressure needle flicked down and back up like a liar changing his story.

She set it down smooth anyway, because she was who she was, and taxied to the hangar with her jaw tight, and when she shut down and pulled the dipstick the oil came out glittering with metal.

She stood there a long moment in the smell of hot magnesium and rain, looking at the glitter the way you look at a diagnosis.

A rod bearing. Maybe worse. She could probably nurse it to a rebuild shop on the coast — *probably* doing a lot of work in that sentence, over water, in pre-monsoon weather, in an engine already speaking in coins. Or she could ground the plane and pay for the overhaul here, if she could get parts flown in, which cost money she had been carefully not having for three years.

Money. Which was how the thought arrived, fully formed and unwelcome, wearing the government's clothes: *a lucrative contract, a search contract, a reason to fly the Serrania headwaters daily with official sanction and official fuel allowances.* She hadn't heard the offer yet. She was already hearing it.

"No," she said to the engine, to the glittering oil, to herself. Firmly. The way you say no to a dog begging at a table.

The rain started again, harder now, drumming the hangar roof, and she covered the engine and wrote the discrepancy in the logbook in her small square hand — *metal particles in oil, suspect No. 3 rod bearing, aircraft grounded pending inspection* — and signed it, and stood back, and felt the strange vertigo of a woman whose wings had just been folded without her permission.

That night the fuel shed radio, tuned by habit to the government band, murmured through the static: routine traffic, a coastal forecast, and then a notice to airmen, dry as dust — *unauthorized aircraft are reminded that the Serrania headwaters survey area is closed to civilian overflight pending ongoing operations.*

Closed. After two weeks of open searching. Closed *now*, the same week a helicopter went up and came back down and said nothing on any frequency Gideon could hear.

Emilio looked at her across the fuel drums, and Raul looked at her, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing yet that could be said in the open. But the gossip economy of small planes runs on exactly this kind of silence, and everyone at the shed understood that a door had just been locked somewhere in the interior, and that locks, in this country, were built to keep people out of knowledge rather than out of places.

Gideon finished her coffee, thanked Emilio for nothing in particular, and walked home through the rain with her collar up and her mind — against every rule she'd written for herself — already flying the valley, already reading the wind up the Serrania drainage, already asking the question she'd spent three years training herself not to ask:

*What's in that camp that needed closing?*

Behind her, in the dark hangar, *Constance* ticked as she cooled, counting out the hours of her grounded days like a patient clock, and upriver, though she couldn't have known it, a man named Tomas Reyes was loading a boat he hadn't yet agreed to sail.
