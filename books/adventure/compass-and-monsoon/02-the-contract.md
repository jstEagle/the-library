## 2. The Contract

The rain came in the night and left by morning, as it always did that time of year, and Gideon Zhou woke to the smell of wet canvas and avgas and the particular silence of an airstrip before anyone else had claimed it. She lay in the hammock slung between two posts of her lean-to behind the fuel shed and listened to the drips coming off the roof — a slow, irregular percussion, like someone learning a rhythm they'd never quite master.

Three years she'd been sleeping here. Three years of this exact sound.

She got up, folded the hammock into its precise roll, and put the kettle on the camp stove. The routine was the point. Roll the hammock. Fill the kettle. Check the tie-downs on the Cessna even though she'd checked them at dusk. Wipe the dew off the windscreen with the same rag she'd been washing and rewashing since it stopped being white. A life was just a set of motions performed long enough, and if you performed them carefully enough, nobody — including yourself — could see what was underneath.

The morning mail run was to San Isidro and back, ninety minutes each way over green country that rolled like a frozen sea. She loaded the crates herself: penicillin, antimalarials, insulin in a foam cooler she'd rigged with a second battery, letters bundled in oilcloth. The village nurse, a wiry woman named Beatriz who'd once been a surgeon in the capital and never said why she wasn't anymore, met her on the strip and helped unload without either of them speaking more than ten words. That was another thing Gideon liked about this work. Out here, competence was conversation enough.

It was on the way back, at the fuel stop at Puerto Lobo, that the rumor found her.

* * *

Puerto Lobo was less a town than an argument between the river and the forest, a scatter of tin roofs and drying nets along a brown bend of water too wide to see across. The airstrip was a shelf of laterite cut into the bank, and the fuel depot was a corrugated shack where old Fermin kept the pumps and the gossip in equal measure, both dispensed slowly and neither free.

Gideon was checking the sump drains when Fermin came out wiping his hands on a rag that had once been a shirt.

"Zhou." He said her name the way he said everything, like he was testing whether it would hold weight. "You hear about the survey team?"

"What survey team?"

"Government people. Up past the Serrania. Geologists, map men, the whole circus." He leaned against the shed's doorframe, settling in. "Went quiet three weeks ago. Nothing on the radio. Capital's been asking questions."

Gideon straightened up, wiped her hands. "Asking questions of whom?"

"Of anybody with wings." He shrugged, elaborately casual, which from Fermin meant the opposite. "They want somebody to go look."

"And you thought of me because?"

"Because you're the only one fool enough to fly that far north this season, and because I figured you'd say no, and I wanted to watch your face while you did it."

She almost smiled. Almost. "I've got a schedule, Fermin. Medicine doesn't wait for government errands."

"No," he agreed. "But governments pay better than nurses do." He let that sit, then pushed off the doorframe and went back inside, and Gideon stood a moment longer looking north, where the ridgeline made its blue staircase toward country the maps shaded in that apologetic color cartographers use for places they're guessing about.

She dismissed it. That was the honest word for what she did: she took the rumor, turned it over once, and put it down. Government work meant government people, and government people asked questions with forms attached. She had spent three years building a life with no forms in it. Whatever was happening past the Serrania belonged to whoever had lost it.

She flew home. She rolled the hammock. She checked the tie-downs.

Two days later, Director Hale came to find her.

* * *

He arrived the way weather arrives in the interior — no announcement, just a change in pressure. One afternoon the strip was empty; the next there was a white government turboprop sitting on it like a knife laid across a table, and a man standing beside it in clothes too clean for the latitude.

Gideon saw him from the air on approach and knew immediately, with the animal certainty of three years of avoiding exactly this, that the plane was for her.

She considered, for the length of one full circuit, simply climbing out again and flying to San Isidro and pretending she hadn't seen him. But the turboprop would still be there tomorrow, and the day after, and men who sent planes didn't send them by accident. Better to know the shape of the thing.

She landed. Taxied in. Shut down. Climbed out.

"Ms. Zhou." He extended a hand. His grip was dry and brief, calibrated to be forgotten. "Director Elias Hale, Ministry of Interior Resources. Thank you for receiving me."

"You didn't give me much choice."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live in a man who owned one. "No. I suppose I didn't. May I?" He gestured toward the lean-to without waiting for permission, already walking, and Gideon fell into step beside him feeling like she was being escorted through her own home.

He looked at everything. That was the first thing she noticed about him, before the gray suit and the careful voice and the eyes the color of river water after a flood. He looked at the lean-to, at the tool bench, at the logbook lying open on the crate she used as a desk, and he looked at each thing the way an auditor looks at a ledger — not with curiosity but with inventory. When his gaze crossed the Cessna, it slowed. He read the tail number the way other men read a name.

"She's older than I expected," he said.

"She's older than most things that still fly."

"Sentimental about her?"

"I'm practical about her. Sentiment doesn't keep an engine turning."

"Mm." He turned, and now he gave her the full weight of those eyes, and Gideon understood that everything so far had been the man arranging his instruments. "Then let me be practical. Four weeks ago, a ministry survey team went missing in the upper watershed. Seven personnel. Geological assessment, hydrological sampling, standard resource mapping." He said *standard* the way a magician says *nothing up my sleeve*. "Their last confirmed position is here." From his jacket he produced a photograph, not a map — a photograph of a map, which told her something about how carefully this man controlled what left his office. He held it out. She didn't take it.

"I heard something about that," she said. "Fermin talks."

"Fermin talks," Hale agreed pleasantly. "Fermin also sells fuel to half the pilots in the territory, and every one of them has declined this contract, which tells me either the money is wrong or the fear is right. I'm hoping, Ms. Zhou, that in your case it's the money."

"And if it's the fear?"

"Then you're wiser than you look, and I'll take my plane elsewhere." He said it without heat, without threat, in the tone of a man discussing freight rates, and somehow that was worse than any threat could have been. He set the photograph down on her crate, next to the logbook, so that the two lay side by side — her three years of measured distance and his missing seven, touching at the edges.

She looked at the map. The marked position sat well north of her usual routes, in the fold of country beyond the Serrania where the rivers braided and unbraided and the charts thinned to guesswork. She knew it the way she knew all of it: from above, at altitude, in good weather, briefly. Country you looked at. Not country you landed in.

"The fee," Hale said, "is four times the going rate for aerial search. Plus fuel, plus a completion bonus that would cover a new engine for that aircraft of yours, cowling to crankshaft."

Her stomach did something small and traitorous.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. She'd known it was coming from the moment she'd seen the turboprop, and she'd told herself it wouldn't matter, and it mattered. Number four cylinder had been running hot for two months. The mags needed overhaul. The prop had a chip in it she'd been monitoring the way you monitor a tooth that aches only when you bite down wrong. The medicine runs paid in gratitude and occasionally in chickens, and gratitude didn't buy chrome. Every month the margin got thinner, and every month she did the arithmetic in the dark before sleep: how many more seasons, how many more landings on strips that were really suggestions, before the airplane — and the life built on the airplane — came apart not with a bang but with a bill she couldn't pay.

Hale watched her do the arithmetic. She could feel him watching, patient as sediment.

"That's a lot of money for a search," she said.

"It's a lot of country for a search."

"It's a lot of money for seven bureaucrats with radios."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger, nothing so legible. Recalculation. "You have a low opinion of the ministry."

"I have a high opinion of what things cost versus what they're worth. Somebody taught me that." She picked up the photograph finally, studied it, set it back down. "Why me? You said every pilot's refused. So go further. Charter out of the capital. Satellite pass. Drones, if you've got them."

"Drones don't judge terrain. Satellites see clouds. And capital charter pilots file flight plans." He paused, and the pause was a door opening onto a room she couldn't see. "You fly by eye, Ms. Zhou. Everyone says so. You read country from the air the way other people read print. If there's anything to find down there, you're the one who'll find it."

*If.* She caught it. Most people wouldn't have. Most people would have been counting the money. But the sentence had been built around that little word like a load-bearing wall, and she felt it the way she felt turbulence before the wing dropped — *if there's anything to find.*

Not *when we recover them*. Not *when you locate the team*. *If there's anything to find.*

"And if I find them?" she asked. "What then? I'm not equipped for rescue. I can carry four passengers on a good day, and these are not good days, and the monsoon's—"

"The monsoon will hold. The models say three weeks yet." He said it smoothly, but she noted that he answered a question she hadn't quite finished asking, and that his answer came a half-second fast, like a card palmed and produced. "Your task is location and verification. Visual confirmation of the party or their equipment. You report coordinates. Ground teams handle the rest."

"Ground teams." She looked at the turboprop gleaming on her strip. "You brought a long way to go for a man who's delegating."

"I wanted to meet you." For the first time, something almost human crossed his face — or something wearing the costume of human, tailored to fit. "Three years ago you walked away from a career most pilots would have killed for. No scandal, no debt, no explanation. You just... descended. Bought a tired Cessna and started hauling pills to villages that can't pay you. People who do that are either running from something or looking for something. Either way, they're serious. Serious people finish contracts." He straightened his cuffs, a small precise gesture. "I need someone serious, Ms. Zhou. The families of seven men need someone serious."

There it was. Families. He'd waited until now to play that card, which meant he'd been holding it, which meant he knew exactly what it weighed.

Gideon looked at the photograph again. At the blue-guesswork country beyond the Serrania. At the little cross inked where seven someones had last been somebody's husband, brother, son. She thought about number four cylinder running hot. She thought about the foam cooler and Beatriz's hands and the chickens and the thinning margin and the dark arithmetic. She thought about the word *if*, sitting in the middle of Hale's sentence like a stone in a shoe.

Every instinct she'd sharpened over three years of careful, formless living told her to say no. It was a clean signal, unambiguous, the kind pilots learn to trust before their instruments do: *this weather is lying to you, turn around.* And she had learned, at a cost she never named, that the times she'd ignored that signal were the times the world had burned.

But instinct didn't pay for chrome. And maybe — she heard the thought arrive and hated it — maybe that was the whole trick of a life like hers. You could call it freedom, living light, owing nothing, answering to no one. Or you could admit that a woman who avoided every obligation was just a woman with nowhere she was needed, and that the difference between the two was only visible on the days someone offered you four times the going rate to care about strangers.

"Fuel advance," she said. "Half, up front, wired before I take off. Contract in writing. And I report to you directly — no intermediaries, no ministry channels. My voice to your ear."

Hale inclined his head, and she had the distinct impression of a fisherman watching the line come tight. "Done."

"And if I find nothing?"

"Then you find nothing, you get paid, and you go back to your villages and your chickens." He smiled then, fully, and it was the worst thing she'd seen all day, because it was warm. "Everyone keeps what they came for."

He wrote the contract that evening at the fuel shed's single table, in longhand, in a hand so neat it looked printed. Gideon read every line twice. The document was clean — suspiciously clean, the kind of clean that takes effort — and she signed it anyway, because the alternative was number four cylinder and the slow arithmetic of decline, and because she told herself, signing, that she was only flying. Looking. Reporting coordinates. That the ground teams and the ministries and whatever lived under the word *if* were none of hers.

Hale shook her hand at the edge of the strip as the light went amber behind the ridge.

"One more thing," he said, conversational, already turning away. "The area you'll be searching — some of the older charts show names for it. Rivers, settlements, that sort of thing. Use the current edition. The current edition only." He glanced back at her, mild as milk. "The old ones contain errors."

He walked to his plane. The turboprop swallowed him. Ten minutes later the sound faded north, and Gideon stood alone on her strip in the cooling air, holding a contract worth more than her airplane, listening to the drips start up off the lean-to roof — that same broken rhythm, like someone learning a song they'd never quite master.

She went inside and pulled her chart case from under the bunk and found, as she'd known she would, the old survey sheets she'd bought years ago from a dead man's estate — the ones with the rivers named, the ones with the little pencil annotations in a hand that wasn't the cartographer's. *Errors,* Hale had said.

She spread them out under the lamp, all the way north, past the Serrania, into the blue guesswork, and began to plan a route.

Somewhere out there, seven men had stopped existing. In the morning, she'd go find out what the country had done with them — and what, if anything, it wanted in return.
