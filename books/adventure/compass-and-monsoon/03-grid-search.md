## 3. Grid Search

The first day she flew the grid like a woman mowing a field.

That was how Hale's liaison had drawn it up — a laminated sheet with numbered rectangles overlaid on the survey area, each one a morning's work, each one to be flown at two thousand feet on east-west legs a mile apart, eyes out for smoke, color, anything man-made that shouldn't be there. *Systematic,* the liaison had said, tapping the sheet with a pen capped in government gray. *No improvisation.* He'd said it the way men say things they've been told to say, and Gideon had signed the flight plan without comment because two hundred an hour plus fuel was two hundred an hour plus fuel, and the Cessna's engine had been eating oil all season like a man eating his last meal.

She flew Rectangle One and Rectangle Two and half of Three before the weather shut her down at noon, cloud building over the Serrania in towers the color of wet slate, and she put down at the mission strip outside San Rafael de Agua and drank warm beer with the priest and learned nothing except that everyone in the district had heard about the missing team and no one knew anything true about them.

"Seven men," the priest said, counting on thick fingers. "Or six. The radio operator was maybe not a man of the party proper." He shrugged with his whole body, a shrug that took in the ceiling beams, the rain starting on the tin roof, the general incompetence of governments. "They came through in March. Bought eggs. The young one was afraid of the chickens."

"That's your intelligence?"

"That is my intelligence." He smiled, unbothered. God, apparently, handled the rest.

* * *

The second day the sky cleared hard and blue and she flew until her eyes burned.

Rectangle Four held nothing but canopy — a million hectares of it, unbroken green going bronze where the creeks cut through, the whole thing breathing heat up at her like a living thing. Rectangle Five held a tapir trail and a slash-and-burn plot abandoned so long ago the secondary growth had healed it into something indistinguishable from wilderness. Rectangle Six held a river bend where the water ran the color of milk coffee and a fishing camp — three lean-tos, a drying rack, a dugout pulled up on the mud — whose occupants waved at her with the cheerful indifference of people who had watched planes for forty years and never once been interested in one.

By Rectangle Eight she had stopped expecting to find anything and started flying on muscle and arithmetic instead, which was its own kind of danger, and she caught herself doing it — catching herself thinking about the engine hours, the invoice from Puerto Lobo, whether the money from this contract would cover a cylinder overhaul or only postpone one — and hauled her attention back down to the green like a fisherman hauling in a line.

Because that was the thing about search flying nobody told you: the country did not want to be searched. It was built, specifically, over millions of years, to defeat exactly what she was doing. Canopy closed over a camp in a season. Rain erased tracks in an hour. A man could die twenty meters off a riverbank and lie there until he was soil, and the forest would wear him like a new ring inside the trunk. Seven men, six men, five — the number didn't matter. The country would take any of them and give back nothing, and it would do it without malice, the way a river takes a leaf.

So when she saw the clearing in Rectangle Nine, she almost didn't believe it, and then she almost flew past it, and then she made herself do the thing she'd trained herself to do three years ago in another life: circle once, wide, high, hands quiet on the wheel, and look.

It wasn't smoke. It wasn't color. It was geometry.

A straight line where nothing in a rainforest is straight. She throttled back, dropped a wing, and followed the line to its end, and the end was a camp — tents in a row, taut and pale, a fire pit swept clean, gear stacked in ranks beside the tents like soldiers' kit before inspection. Everything squared away. Nothing moving.

Her heart did something complicated that she chose not to examine.

She keyed the radio out of habit, then remembered there was no one to key it to — Hale's operation ran on scheduled calls twice daily, and this wasn't one — and instead she spent four minutes circling, reading the camp the way you read a page from altitude. No wreck. No signal mirror flashing. No trampled ground radiating outward, no game trail beaten into a path by men walking in and out. The airstrip — because there was one, a rough grass strip hacked along the ridge shoulder, grown soft at the edges but serviceable — showed no ruts, no recent tires.

Seven men had lived here. And then, apparently, seven men had stopped.

She found the gap in the trees where the strip began, set up wide, and brought the Cessna down through air that shivered with late-afternoon thermals, wheels touching grass with a sound like a hand smoothing a bedsheet.

* * *

She sat in the plane for a full minute after shutdown, prop still ticking, and listened.

Insects. A bird making a sound like a hinge. Wind working the canopy a quarter mile off. That was all. A camp should have sounded like this, she supposed, if everyone in it were asleep. But it was nearly five o'clock, and camps don't sleep at five o'clock. Camps cook. Camps talk. Camps run generators and argue over cards and curse at mosquitoes, and the noise of a camp carries further than you'd think through evening air.

This camp made no noise at all, and the silence had a texture to it, a weight, and every animal part of her brain was already screaming while the trained part said, calmly: *get out, walk the perimeter first, don't go in.*

She got out. She walked the perimeter first.

The tents were expedition-grade, state-issue olive drab, and all of them were zipped. She stood at the nearest one for a long moment, then reached out and worked the zipper down, and the smell that came out was canvas and insect repellent and cold ash and nothing else — no sweetness of death, no human staleness, just the smell of a place left tidy. Six sleeping bags rolled and stowed. Boots paired under the cot frames, laces tucked in. A shirt hung on a line. On the crate that served as a nightstand, someone had left a paperback novel open face-down, spine cracked, holding its place at page two hundred and eleven forever.

Men who walk out of a camp don't leave their boots. Men who are taken in a hurry don't roll their sleeping bags. And men who die here don't get buried so completely that even the ants have nothing to say about it.

She went tent to tent. Same story, six times. In the seventh — the largest, the command tent — she found the map table.

That was where she stopped being able to breathe normally.

The map was spread and weighted at the corners with stones, the way surveyors do it, and it was their own operational map, gridded in pencil with the survey lines, half the squares ticked off in a neat engineer's hand. A protractor lay square to the map edge. Pencils, sharpened, in a tin. A mug with a spoon in it, dry as bone. And in the center of the table, where a man working would keep it close to his hand, lay the compass.

She didn't touch it at first. She leaned over it the way you lean over a snake.

It was a good compass, liquid-damped, brass-cased, the kind that costs real money and keeps its truth for decades. The needle pointed south. Not wobbling south, not resting south — she picked it up, turned it, turned herself, walked a slow circle around the table with it flat on her palm, and the needle swung patiently, obediently, until it found south again and settled there, quivering slightly, like a dog that has been taught a trick it doesn't understand.

She checked it against her own. Her needle found north with the boring, absolute fidelity she'd trusted her life to for ten years. The brass compass on the table found south with the same fidelity, and the two instruments disagreed with each other the way two witnesses disagree when one of them is lying, except instruments don't lie, which meant one of them was broken, except it wasn't, she was almost sure it wasn't, and the sureness was worse than doubt.

Someone had done this. Someone had demagnetized it, or reversed it, or replaced the card, and laid it back on the map pointing the wrong way, and left it there — not dropped, not lost. *Placed.* Centered on the table like a signature at the bottom of a letter.

She set it down exactly where it had been, stone weights and all, and stepped back, and became aware that she was breathing fast and shallow in a tent that smelled of nothing, and made herself stop.

The radio log was clipped to a board hanging inside the tent flap. Standard format, state survey service, entries in different hands, times in Zulu, weather, position, routine. She read it standing in the doorway with the last light coming gold through the canvas behind her.

March 9th, 0800: position, conditions, all well.
March 9th, 2000: position, conditions, commencing Line 14 tomorrow.
March 10th, 0800: position, conditions, all well.
March 10th, 2017: *— and if the readings hold we'll want to flag the whole reach for re-survey before the ministry signs anything, tell the Director that the samples alone*

And nothing. No sign-off. No next entry. Not torn away, not blotted — simply ended, mid-sentence, mid-clause, the pen lifted between one word and the next, and the next word had never come.

*Tell the Director.*

Gideon stood there long enough for the light to change from gold to gray. Then she went out and walked the strip end to end in the failing light, looking at the grass, and found what she had known from the air she would find: nothing. No tracks leaving. No tracks arriving. The ground around the camp held the ghost-prints of the survey months themselves — boot scuffs, a stake hole, the shallow trench of a latrine long since collapsed in on itself — and then, after the second week of March, the ground simply stopped remembering anyone had ever been here at all.

Seven men had walked into this clearing carrying sixty kilos of kit apiece, and the country had folded shut behind them like water behind a swimmer.

She flew out at first light the next morning, low over the strip, and looked back once. From the air the camp was already nothing — a pale smudge in the green, geometry dissolving back into geology. By next month the canopy would have it. By next year, Rectangle Nine would hold nothing but canopy again, and some pilot on some future grid would fly over the place where seven men had stopped existing and see nothing at all.

* * *

She should have gone home. That was the procedure — site located, document, report, let the professionals come in with helicopters and dogs and forms in triplicate. She had photographs. She had coordinates. She had, if she wanted it, the compass itself wrapped in a chart in her flight bag, though she couldn't have said why she'd taken it, except that leaving it there had felt like leaving a message unread.

Instead, at ten thousand feet with the Serrania sliding past beneath her in its slow blue ridges, she found herself doing the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do for two days.

*Tell the Director.* Which director? Hale? Or whoever the radio operator had thought he was talking to, before the sentence stopped? Samples of what? Flag what reach for re-survey — and why would a survey team, whose job was to bless a mining concession, want to *re*-survey it, unless what they'd found in those samples was something the ministry would very much rather stay unsampled?

She thought about the village kids at Rio Claro, the ones with the rash that didn't respond to anything in her medicine crates, the ones the old women called *la enfermedad del agua*, the water sickness, and had called it that for two years now, casually, the way you name a season.

She thought about the reversed compass, patient and certain, pointing the wrong way with total confidence, and understood suddenly that it wasn't a joke or a malfunction or a madman's tic. It was a message. *Everything you know about direction is wrong. Everything you've been told points the wrong way.* It was the kind of message you leave when you can't leave words, and it was addressed to whoever came looking, and someone had expected someone to come looking, eventually, and had wanted them to understand: *we did not wander. We did not fall. Read the instrument.*

She flew home through the afternoon with the compass in her bag and the log entry copied into her notebook in her own careful hand, and she told herself she was doing it for the report, for completeness, for the extra fee Hale's office would grudgingly pay for recovered evidence.

She told herself that right up until she crossed the last ridge and saw the weather.

It stood over the eastern horizon like a wall being built in real time — a front of cloud stacked to the tropopause, purple-black at its base, lightning flickering inside it with the slow silent regularity of something enormous turning over in its sleep. The monsoon. Weeks early. Every old-timer on the river had been saying the rains felt wrong this year, and she'd nodded the way you nod at weather talk, and now the weather talk was thirty miles wide and coming to collect.

She put the Cessna down at Puerto Lobo with twenty minutes of daylight and the first fat drops already hitting the windscreen like thrown gravel, and she was still tying down the wings when the rain arrived in earnest — not falling so much as arriving, a gray cataract that erased the far side of the strip, the town, the world.

Under that roar, taxiing to a stop on a strip already going soft, the engine stumbled, coughed, and let go of something internal with a metallic crunch she felt through the airframe and in her teeth.

She sat in the shaking cabin, hands still on the wheel, and listened to the rods knock — once, twice, dying — and understood that the choice had just been made for her. North was a week's flying in clear weather. There was no clear weather coming. There was no flying at all, not for days, maybe weeks, not in this.

The road ended here. The river started here.

Through the windshield, blurred by rain, she could make out the lights of the waterfront coming on one by one, and among them, tied up along the muddy quay, the running lights of the trading boats — the small stubborn fleet that kept the upper river alive when everything else failed, the boats that went where roads didn't and maps didn't and, she was beginning to suspect, governments preferred that no one else did either.

She got her bag, wrapped the compass deeper in the charts, and stepped down into the rain.
