## 4. Grounded

The monsoon came three weeks early, and it came like an intruder who had been waiting a long time for the household to let down its guard.

Gideon felt it first in her teeth — a pressure change so sudden the fillings ached — then in the behavior of the birds, which went silent all at once over the airstrip at Puerto Lobo as if someone had thrown a switch. The sky to the west had turned the color of a bruise going bad, green-black at its heart, and the air that had been merely humid for days now lay on her skin like a wet cloth.

"Early," said old Mateo from under the wing of her Cessna, where he'd been helping her check the tie-downs. He said it the way other men said *war* or *flood* — a single word carrying everything that would follow.

"Three weeks early." Gideon tested the anchor stake with her boot. The ground was already soft, giving like day-old bread. "The government survey office still has their dry-season calendar posted."

"The government," Mateo said, "has never once been rained on."

She laughed, because it was true, and because laughing was cheaper than thinking about what three weeks of early rain meant for a woman whose livelihood was measured in flyable hours. Then the first drops came — fat, warm, deliberate, like the sky testing its aim — and within ten minutes the world dissolved into water.

---

She should have stayed at Puerto Lobo. Every instinct she'd spent three years sharpening told her so: *don't fly into weather you can't see out of, don't trust a strip that floods, don't be a hero for a crate of antibiotics that will keep.* But the medicine run to San Rafael had been scheduled, and the clinic there had a child waiting on those antibiotics, and Gideon Zhou had built her whole second life on being the person who showed up when the showing-up was hard. It was the one identity she trusted more than any name on any document.

So she flew, and the flying itself was fine — she was good enough to make fine look easy, threading the gaps in the squall line the way she'd thread mountain passes in another life, another country, another name she didn't say aloud even to herself anymore. The river below ran high and brown and angry, already swallowing sandbars that had been dry that morning. She noted it without noting it, the way pilots note things: *river up, channels moving, no margin left.*

San Rafael's strip was a grass shelf above the floodplain, and by the time she landed there it was less a runway than a rumor of one. Water stood in the wheel ruts. The rain hit the windshield in sheets that wipers were a joke against. She unloaded the crates with two men from the clinic holding a tarp over her head, shouted the dosage instructions through the roar, and got back in the plane soaked to the sternum and shivering.

Takeoff was ugly but survivable. The landing back at Puerto Lobo was where the day decided to collect its debt.

She came in low over the river because the ceiling allowed nothing else, and she saw the strip too late — saw it not as grass but as lake, the whole eastern third of it underwater, brown water sliding smooth and innocent over ground that had been solid at dawn. There was no go-around. There was barely a decision. There was only the arithmetic every pilot knows: *water or trees, water or trees,* and water at least gives you a chance to walk away.

She put it down long, on the wet half, flared soft, and for forty yards it was almost graceful. Then the left main wheel found the flooded rut, the nose swung, and the engine — her beautiful, cantankerous, rebuilt-twice engine — made a sound she had heard exactly once before in her life and hoped never to hear again. A crack like a rifle shot inside the cowling. A shriek of metal finding metal. The propeller stopped being a circle and became a sculpture.

The Cessna slewed to a halt thirty feet short of the waterline, steam hissing from the cowl seams, and Gideon sat with her hands locked on the yoke, listening to the rain hammer the roof like a fist on a door, and did the only honest thing available to her.

She swore. At length. In three languages.

---

By the time Mateo and half the town reached her, the rain had slackened to a steady gray pour, and Gideon had the cowling open and was staring at the truth of it. The connecting rod had let go and punched through the case. Oil and water mingled in the sump in a pale, fatal emulsion. She knew what she was looking at. Anyone who'd turned a wrench knew what they were looking at.

"Well?" Mateo asked, though his face said he knew.

"She's done. Not bent valves done. Done done." Gideon wiped her hands on her trousers and left darker streaks. "New crankshaft. New rod. Machine work. Parts flown in from the coast, if anything flies in from the coast during monsoon, which nothing does."

"How long?"

"If I had the parts and a hangar? Two weeks. If I have to wait for parts and beg hangar space?" She looked at the sky, which offered no comfort. "A month. Maybe more."

Mateo sucked air through his teeth, the universal sound of bad news confirmed. Around them the townspeople drifted back toward shelter; sympathy in river towns is real but brief, because everyone has their own leaking roof to attend. Gideon stood alone in the rain beside her dead airplane and felt the particular loneliness of the grounded — the earth pulling at her like gravity with an opinion.

Three years. Three years since she'd come down out of the mountains with a new name and a secondhand Cessna and a rule about never staying anywhere longer than the fuel lasted. The plane wasn't just a living. It was the exit. It was the thing that meant no door could ever close behind her, because she could always open the sky instead. And now the sky was shut, and the door was standing wide open behind her, and she could hear — or imagined she could hear — footsteps in it.

*Stop,* she told herself. *Nobody's looking for you. Nobody has looked for you in years. You're a bush pilot with a blown engine, not a fugitive.*

She almost believed it. Almost was a word she lived on.

---

Puerto Lobo in monsoon was a town turned inward. The market stalls had become awnings over awnings; the river, usually a working presence at the town's edge, had risen until it licked the pilings of the stilt houses and carried past them a parade of debris — branches, a drowned pig, half a canoe paddling itself in circles. Gideon took a room above the cantina because it was the only room, and spent two days doing what grounded pilots do: taking the engine apart further than necessary, drinking coffee that could strip paint, and listening.

Listening was the gossip economy of small planes, and Gideon was rich in it. Pilots passing through — the few fool enough to fly this weather — brought news the way boats brought cargo. A landslide on the coast road. Fuel prices doubling. And threaded through it all, growing louder each day like the river itself:

*The survey team. Still missing. Government won't say where they were. Won't say how many. Some say six, some say eight. Helicopters went out and came back empty. Now they're offering money.*

That last part arrived on the third evening, delivered by a cargo pilot named Ferro who drank like a man paying off a debt. "Search contract," he said, tapping the bar. "Big money. They want civilian pilots because their own people are stretched thin with the rains. You're the best stick on this river, Zhou. They'll want you."

"I've got a dead engine and a hangar full of nothing."

"So take the money and hire a mechanic. That's the point of money." Ferro shrugged. "Though between us — nobody comes back from these government searches saying much. Last man who flew for the survey office, what was his name — anyway. He doesn't fly anymore."

Gideon turned her glass on the bar, slow circles. The missing team was none of her business. Missing teams were weather to her — something that happened in the world, that she flew around. She had learned, at terrible cost, the price of making other people's secrets your business.

But the engine needed a crankshaft, and crankshafts needed money, and pride — she was honest enough with herself to name it — pride wanted her airborne again before anyone in Puerto Lobo got used to seeing her on foot.

"I'll hear them out," she said, and hated how eager it sounded.

---

The government liaison found her before she could find him, which told her something. He was waiting outside the cantina the next morning under an umbrella held by a driver, a lean man in a khaki shirt pressed sharp enough to cut, with the kind of face that had been arranged rather than lived in. His card said DIRECTOR HALE, SURVEY AND TERRITORIAL AFFAIRS, and the cardstock was heavier than anything else in Puerto Lobo including the church bell.

"Pilot Zhou." His handshake was brief and dry, a miracle in the rain. "I'm told you know the upper river better than my own charts do."

"Your charts have blank spaces where the river goes."

"Yes." He smiled as if she'd complimented him. "Which is why we're hiring, rather than mapping. One aircraft, grid pattern, three days' fuel. Photograph anything anomalous. Report only to me." He named a figure.

Gideon kept her face still, but inside her something sat up and growled. It was triple the going rate. It was quadruple. It was a new crankshaft, machine work, hangar fees, and six months of breathing room, handed over for three days of looking out a window.

"That's a lot of money for sightseeing," she said.

"It's a lot of rain for sightseeing. Hazard pay." Hale watched her with eyes that gave away nothing, eyes like the surface of deep water. "You have questions."

"Where were they last known?"

"A sector I'll show you on contract."

"How many men?"

"Personnel details are internal."

"What happened to the last civilian pilot who flew for the survey office?"

For the first time, something moved behind Hale's face — not a flinch, nothing so legible. More like a shutter adjusting. "He completed his contract and moved on. Rumor travels badly out here." He produced the contract folder from a waterproof case, set it on the rail between them, and stepped back into the rain-shadow of his umbrella. "You have until I leave town. Which is tomorrow, with or without you."

He left. Gideon stood in the drizzle holding the folder, feeling the weight of it wrong in her hand, the way you feel a fish that's been gutted and stuffed. Everything about the man was correct. That was the problem. Men who were correct to the decimal point were men who had decided things long ago and were simply executing them.

She read the contract twice that night. The liability clauses were strange — she'd be responsible for the aircraft, fine, standard — but also for "recovered materials," whatever those were, and there was a confidentiality section broad enough to cover her own thoughts. She read it a third time and signed it anyway, because the alternative was watching the monsoon eat her savings while her engine rusted into sculpture, and because she told herself — the oldest lie in her inventory — that she could always walk away later.

Walking away later. That was how she'd gotten here in the first place.

---

It was the mechanic who sent her to Tomas Reyes, though nobody called it sending. Mateo's cousin's wife's brother, that sort of chain, ending in: *"If you need to go upriver, there's only one boat that goes, and one man who'll take it."*

She found the boat first — a trading barge maybe fifty feet, steel-hulled, older than sin and maintained like a religion, tied up below the stilt houses with cargo tarped against the rain. Diesel smell and river smell and dried fish. A man was working on the deck winch in the rain, bare-chested under a slicker, hands moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who had done this ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more.

"Tomas Reyes?"

He looked up. He was perhaps fifty, built like the winch he'd been fixing — all functional angles — with a face browned and creased by decades of weather, and eyes that did the opposite of Director Hale's: they gave away everything, but slowly, like a lock opening one tumbler at a time. Right now what they gave away was assessment. He looked at her the way he'd look at a rope he was deciding whether to trust with weight.

"People say Reyes. Who says I'm Tomas?"

"Mateo. Through four cousins."

"Mateo talks more than my engine." But he wiped his hands and came to the rail, which was as good as an invitation. "You're the pilot. The one whose plane died on the strip."

"News travels fast."

"River's faster. What do you want?"

"Passage upriver. As far as the confluence at Yarí, maybe beyond. I can pay."

"No."

The word came flat and immediate, no negotiation in it, and Gideon blinked. "You haven't heard the offer."

"Don't need to. I don't carry passengers for money. Money makes passengers think they own the route." He turned back to the winch, conversation clearly over.

Gideon stood in the rain for a moment, pride rising in her throat like bile. She had negotiated with warlords, with customs officials, with a bishop, once, over contraband insulin. She was not accustomed to being dismissed by a man fixing machinery. She opened her mouth to try money again — bigger, blunter — and then some instinct, the same one that had told her the monsoon was coming, told her that this man's refusal wasn't about price. It was about something else entirely.

So she changed tactics, which for her was nearly a religious experience.

"It's about the survey team," she said.

Reyes's hands stopped on the winch handle. Just for a second. Just long enough.

"Government's offering pilots money to find them," she went on, watching him the way he'd watched her. "I took the contract. I need to get upriver to start the search grids, and my plane's a lawn ornament. So it's not passage I'm buying, really. It's the story. Everyone on this river has a version. I thought I'd ask the man with the fastest boat."

Silence, except the rain on the tarpaulins and the river worrying the pilings. When Reyes turned around, his face had rearranged itself into something unreadable, but the lock was opening — she could feel the tumblers moving.

"You signed a paper for those people," he said. Not a question.

"I signed a paper."

"And now you want to know what the paper's for."

"Now I want to know why a trader who won't take money cares so much whether I take theirs."

Reyes studied her for a long moment. Rain dripped off the end of his nose. Somewhere across the water a dog barked at the flood, hopeless and persistent.

"Come aboard tomorrow," he said finally. "Sunrise. Bring nothing you'd hate to lose." He paused, and something flickered across his face — grief, or guilt, or the two of them wearing each other's clothes. "And pilot. Ask me your questions on the water. Some stories don't like dry land."

He turned back to the winch, and Gideon walked away through the rain with the contract heavy in her pocket and a stranger's voice in her ears, telling herself she'd made a practical decision about transportation, knowing full well she'd just been handed the first page of something she wouldn't be able to put down.
