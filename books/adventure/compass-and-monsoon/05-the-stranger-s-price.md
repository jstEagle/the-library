## 5. The Stranger's Price

Tomas Reyes had learned to read men the way he read the river: by what floated on the surface and by what moved beneath it. The pilot woman — Zhou, she'd said, Gideon Zhou, as if the name were a receipt for something already paid — stood at the rail of the *Mariposa* with her arms crossed against the drizzle, watching the brown water slide past the pilings of Puerto Lobo, and everything about her floated competence. The way she'd assessed his boat in one sweep, hull to canopy. The way she named a price without flinching, then doubled it when he didn't answer fast enough. The way her boots sat on his deck like they belonged to someone accustomed to decks that moved.

But underneath, moving against the current of all that, something else. He'd seen it the moment she'd described what she found at the survey camp.

"The tents were still standing," she'd told him, there on the flooded airstrip with rain hammering the puddles flat. "Everything stowed. Cook kit clean and stacked. No tracks leaving — none. And on the map table, the compass." She had paused, and he had watched her decide whether to say the next part aloud. "The needle was reversed. Pointing south when north was right there on the map margin. I thought maybe it was broken."

Broken. He had kept his face still the way you keep your hands still when a snake crosses the path ahead of you. Not broken. Turned.

"Take me upstream," she said now, again, because he hadn't answered the first time either. "As far as the second gorge. I'll pay whatever the boat's worth."

"That's the problem," Tomas said. "It isn't worth anything. It's my whole life, and lives don't price well."

She looked at him then, really looked, the way pilots look at weather — measuring, unromantic, deciding whether to fly into it or around it. "You refused money."

"I refuse money from people who are about to do something stupid. It keeps my conscience clean and my ledger short."

"And am I doing something stupid?"

"You're asking to go upriver during an early monsoon toward a place where six government men vanished so completely that their own compass turned traitor." He shrugged. "I've carried worse cargo than stupid. But never stupid that knew its own name."

Rain came off the river in gray sheets beyond the awning, and beneath them the *Mariposa* rocked at her moorings, loaded with kerosene tins and salt and bolts of cloth, a quarter-ton of ordinary life bound for villages that would otherwise go without. Ten years he'd run this boat. Ten years since he'd walked out of the Survey Directorate with his resignation letter unsigned on Hale's desk and his hands shaking so badly he'd had to grip the doorframe. Free, he called it. He'd built a whole identity out of the word.

"So why are you even considering it?" Gideon asked. "If I'm stupid."

Because the compass is not broken, he did not say. Because I have seen this before. Because eight years ago I stood over another map table in another camp and watched a needle point south against all reason, and I reported it as equipment malfunction because that is what the form had a box for, and my brother signed the same form underneath my name, and three weeks later Mateo was gone along with five other men and every paper that said they'd ever existed.

"Because," he said instead, "you'll find some other fool to take you, and most fools can't handle the second gorge in flood water. People die up there. I'd rather it not be on someone else's boat."

It was a good lie. It had the shape of truth, which is the only kind worth telling. But she narrowed her eyes at him, and he understood that she was a person who had also built herself out of a word — distance, probably, or after — and recognized the architecture.

"You knew something," she said slowly. "When I mentioned the compass. Your face did something."

"My face does many things. It's a very expressive face."

"Tomas."

He looked out at the river. A barge light swung somewhere in the murk; the town smelled of diesel and wet wood and the green rot of things growing faster than they should. "I'll take you to the second gorge," he said. "Not for money. For a story."

"A story?"

"When we come back — if we come back — you tell me everything you saw at that camp. Every detail. You don't summarize, you don't soften it, and you don't leave out the parts that frightened you. Especially those." He held out his hand. "That's my price."

She took it. Her grip was dry despite the rain, callused across the palm in the pattern of yokes and throttles. "That's a strange price."

"It's the only currency I trust anymore."

* * *

He provisioned through the afternoon while she watched, and he let her watch, because a passenger who understands the boat argues less with the boat. Kerosene, rice, salt fish, a spare shear pin, the good bilge pump with the new diaphragm. He topped off the fuel drums and lashed them amidships where a wave could take them only by taking the whole boat first. Gideon turned out to be useful in the way of people who have worked alone a long time — quick, quiet, asking one question where other passengers asked ten.

"How long to the gorge?"

"Three days if the river behaves. Four if it doesn't. Five if it's angry."

"And after the gorge?"

"After the gorge," Tomas said, cinching a lashing, "the maps stop agreeing with each other. Some show tributaries that dried up a decade ago. Some show villages that were never there. One shows a mission station that burned before I was born." He straightened. "The river doesn't care what the maps say. That's the first thing to understand about it."

"What's the second thing?"

"The river remembers everything the maps are trying to forget."

She laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly folded away, as though laughter were another kind of weight she was careful about carrying. He found himself wanting to hear it again and put the wanting away with the rest of the cargo he did not load.

They cast off at first light, into a rain that fell with the patience of something that intended to keep falling until the world agreed to be different. Puerto Lobo slid away behind them — the flooded strip where her plane sat with a thrown rod and a drowned magneto, the cantina where the gossip economy traded in rumors of missing surveyors, the whole small human arrangement of the frontier dissolving into gray.

Tomas stood at the wheel and felt the old feeling come up through the deck plates, the one he always felt on the first hour upstream: that he was not traveling but returning, that the river ran backward through him toward something he had spent ten years pretending was behind him.

Mateo had loved this stretch of water. That was the thing nobody who wrote the reports could have known — that his brother used to stand at the bow of the survey launch with his shirt off in the rain, laughing, calling the rapids by pet names, *la vieja*, *el perro*, *the widow's teeth*. Mateo had believed in the work. That was the unbearable part, the part Tomas carried like ballast in the lowest hold of himself. His brother had believed the surveys meant something — mapping the watershed, testing the water, building the case for protecting the headwaters — and Tomas, older, wiser, more experienced in how institutions digest idealism, had told him gently, patronizingly, to play the game. Fill in the boxes. Report the anomaly, file it correctly, let the process carry it.

The compass needle pointing south. Mateo had radioed it in excited, not frightened — *we found something strange, hermano, the instruments all disagree, come see* — and Tomas had come, and seen, and written *equipment malfunction* in the box marked NATURE OF IRREGULARITY, because the alternative box had no label at all, and unlabeled boxes are where careers go to die.

Three weeks later the follow-up team went in. Six men. Mateo among them, promoted onto the roster two days before departure, which should have been an honor and was instead, Tomas understood now, a hole being quietly dug.

No bodies. No tracks. Gear stacked neat as a shop display. And when Tomas had gone looking — not then, he hadn't dared then, but years later, on his own runs, asking his own questions in river villages — the answers came back shaped like silence. Villages that lowered their voices. Old men who touched their hats and changed the subject. Once, a woman who pressed a dried fish into his hand and whispered, *ask about the water, not the men,* and then would not meet his eyes again.

Ask about the water. He hadn't known what she meant. He suspected the pilot's missing geologists had known.

"You're a long way from anywhere," Gideon said, coming forward with two cups of coffee that tasted of tin and chicory. She handed him one and leaned against the rail beside the wheel, watching the banks scroll past — kapok trees standing in their own reflections, a kingfisher stitching blue across the current. "Why does a trader know the second gorge so well? You sounded like you've run it in flood."

"I've run it in everything."

"That wasn't an answer."

"It was the shape of one." He sipped. "You flew supply runs out here for three years. Why does a pilot know the medicine crates by heart? You counted them at the strip. You knew which village gets the quinine and which gets the iron salts. Traders count crates too. It's how we stay honest."

Something moved behind her eyes — the same something he'd seen at the airstrip, moving under the surface. "Habit," she said.

"Habit," he agreed pleasantly, and let it lie there between them, two hooks in the water, neither biting.

* * *

The first night they tied up against a drowned fig tree above an oxbow gone the color of tea, and Tomas cooked rice with dried fish and chili while the rain played its one note on the canopy. Gideon cleaned the coffee pot without being asked and racked the cups, and he noticed she racked them the way a person racks cups on a pitching flight deck — wedged, secured, nothing left loose for turbulence to throw. A woman who trusted nothing to stay where she put it.

"Tell me about the man who hired you," he said. "Hale."

"Why?"

"Because names travel on this river faster than boats, and I've heard his before. I like to know which currents I'm swimming in."

She turned her cup slowly. "Director Hale. Liaison office. Very polished. He offered me triple the going rate for a grid search, and when I hesitated he didn't push — he just waited, like a man holding a door open for someone he already knows will walk through it." She looked up. "He knew about my plane. The engine. He knew exactly how much the repair would cost before I'd gotten the quote myself."

Tomas nodded slowly. Men like Hale did not offer money; they offered exits, and priced them later. "And you took it."

"I needed the plane airworthy."

"There it is," he said softly.

"There what is?"

"The reason everyone gives. Need. It's a good word. It covers a lot of ground." He scraped the pot. "I needed things once too. Needed to keep my position, my pension, my brother's respect. Need is how they get you to write the small lies. The big ones you write yourself, afterward, to justify the small ones."

She was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked on the canopy. Somewhere across the oxbow, a howler monkey complained about the weather on behalf of the entire forest.

"Is that what you did?" she asked. "Write the big one?"

He smiled and shook his head, and gave her the truth bent just enough to pass: "I worked for the government once. Survey service. I left because I learned that the forms matter more than the findings. If a finding doesn't fit a box, the finding disappears. Not covered up — that implies effort. Just... filed somewhere no one looks." He gestured upstream, into the dark. "Like men."

"You think that's what happened to the survey team."

"I think rivers swallow things," Tomas said. "And I think somebody taught this river how."

She studied him across the fire, and he let her, keeping his face open and empty as a beached canoe. This was the discipline of his freedom: to be asked anything and confess nothing. He had practiced it for ten years on dockhands and customs men and priests, and he was good at it, and it cost him nothing except every night, a little, in the dark, when the river spoke in Mateo's voice saying *come see, hermano, come see what we found.*

Because that was the arithmetic he could not escape, the sum that always came out the same: if he had written the truth in the box — *instruments reversed, cause unknown, request investigation* — his brother might have refused the follow-up assignment. Might have been reassigned. Might be alive, running a boat of his own somewhere, complaining about the price of fuel. Instead Tomas had chosen the labeled box, the safe career, the clean form, and the river had taken the difference out of his blood.

So when the pilot woman stood on the flooded strip describing a reversed compass like a signature, Tomas Reyes — free man, honest trader, keeper of a short ledger — had heard a door open that had been shut for eight years, and had walked through it before he remembered to ask himself whether he was steering or being steered.

Guilt, he thought, feeding the fire another stick. Guilt is a current. You can't see it. You can only feel it pulling, and pretend you chose the direction.

"We'll make the gorge by tomorrow evening," he said aloud. "Sleep while you can. After the gorge, we anchor early and travel by day only. Flood water moves logs around at night. A single strainer across the channel can take the boat and everything aboard."

"Strainer?"

"A tree the river has taken, holding position underwater. Water goes through. Boats don't."

She absorbed that with a pilot's economy, nodding once. "Understood."

He banked the fire and rolled into his hammock, and lay listening to the rain and the river working at its endless private labor, and across the dying embers he heard her breathing change into sleep, that deep unconscious surrender he envied and had not managed in eight years.

Tomorrow, the gorge. Beyond it, the country where the maps disagreed, where his questions had always died of politeness, where somewhere — he had begun, terribly, to believe — the answer to Mateo was waiting, patient as sediment.

He had told her his price was a story. It was true, as far as it went. What he had not told her was whose story he was really buying, or that he had been paying installments on it for eight years, or that the debt had just been called due by a stranger with a dead engine and a reversed compass and eyes that missed nothing except, thank God, the thing he most needed hidden.

The rain thickened. The river rose a hand's width against the pilings of the fig tree, patient, remembering.

Upstream, in the dark, something waited that the maps were trying to forget — and Tomas Reyes, who claimed to be free, slept at last with the current pulling him toward it.
