## 6. Leaking Hull

The river took them at first light, the way it took everything — without negotiation.

Gideon stood in the stern of Tomas Reyes's boat and watched Puerto Lobo shrink behind them into a gray smear of tin roofs and smoke, and she felt the particular vertigo of a pilot on the water: the world moving under her without her hands on it. She had spent three years trusting nothing but her own throttle, her own altimeter, her own judgment. Now she was cargo. The boat was forty feet of scarred hardwood and diesel stubbornness, stacked with trade goods — kerosene, salt, machetes, bolts of cloth — with just enough room carved out amidships for two people to sleep under a tarp if they didn't mind each other's breathing.

"Rules," Tomas said, not looking up from the tiller. He had a way of speaking that made even rules sound like observations about weather. "One: you don't touch the engine. Two: you bail when I tell you to bail. Three—" he glanced back at her, and there was something almost like humor in it, dry as riverbank clay, "—you don't ask me why I'm going."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to ask tomorrow." He turned forward again. "Everyone asks by tomorrow."

She let it go, because he was right, and because asking questions of a man who steered with his whole body — knees, hips, wrists, reading the current through the wood the way she read turbulence through the stick — seemed like a poor use of leverage. Instead she watched him work.

That was the thing she kept coming back to, all that first day. He worked.

Not the way traders worked, not the efficient economy of a man running goods upriver for margin. He worked the way she worked a preflight: total, unglamorous attention, repeated until it was ritual. Every hour he killed the engine and drifted while he checked something — the shaft seal, the fuel line, the bilge. He knew the river's moods by color. Where the water ran tea-dark he gave the banks a wide berth; where it ran the green of bottle glass he cut close, shaving minutes off the bends. Once, without slowing, he reached down and touched the surface with two fingers, held them up, looked at them, and altered course around a bend that looked identical to every other bend.

"What was that?" she asked.

"Silt," he said.

"That's all?"

"For now." And when she kept looking at him: "Silt tells you what the rain is doing in hills you can't see yet. Rain tells you what the river will be doing tonight. Tonight tells you whether we sleep on the boat or in a tree."

It was a good answer. It was also an answer that belonged to someone who had been reading rivers since before he could have learned it hauling cloth and kerosene. She filed it away with the other things — the way he'd named six tributaries in an hour without consulting any chart, including one that didn't appear on hers; the way he'd gone quiet, jaw tight, passing the mouth of a creek marked only as *Río Sin Nombre* on her survey map, as if the namelessness itself were an accusation.

A trader, she thought. Sure. And she was a mail carrier who happened to know how a compass needle felt when it was lying.

* * *

By the second day the rain found its rhythm and stopped pretending to be weather. It became climate. It came down in sheets that erased the far bank, then eased to a fine hissing veil, then hammered again, and between the bursts the forest exhaled steam so thick the river seemed to be smoking. The water rose. Gideon noticed it the way she noticed fuel burn — in increments, in the changing geometry of things. Sandbars she'd marked on the first day were gone by noon. Snags that had stood clear of the surface now showed only a fork of black wood, or nothing at all, which was worse.

"The river's a week early," Tomas said, squinting upstream. "Everything that drowned last year is drowning again, higher up."

"Is that bad?"

"It's honest," he said. "Bad is when it lies to you. A high river shows you its teeth." He nodded at the bilge. "Bail."

She bailed. That was the other rhythm they were falling into, and she was aware of it the way you're aware of a heartbeat — the handing back and forth of small tasks without discussion. He signaled, she scooped. She spotted a floating log ahead, he already had the tiller over. They ate squatting on the cargo crates, rice and dried fish and plantains gone soft, and passed the canteen without looking at each other, and somewhere in the repetition of it a kind of grammar developed between them, a language of gestures and glances that neither of them had agreed to speak.

The hull leaked. Not dangerously — a seep, a slow dark sweat along a seam near the bow that no amount of bailing quite beat, only managed. Tomas inspected it twice a day, pressing his thumb against the caulking, frowning at whatever he read there.

"How old is this boat?" Gideon asked.

"Older than my patience." He wiped his hands on his trousers. "She's like me. She holds together as long as nobody asks too much of her at once."

"And if the river asks?"

He looked at the seam, then at the rising water, then at her, and for a moment something moved behind his face — the same flicker she'd seen at the nameless creek, there and shuttered again.

"Then we find out what we're made of," he said. "Same as the boat."

* * *

The third night they tied up against a stand of bamboo on a bluff above a flooded oxbow, and the rain came down so hard the world reduced itself to sound. Gideon lay under the tarp listening to it drum the canvas and thought about airstrips — about how she'd give anything for the smell of hot asphalt and avgas, for the clean geometry of a runway against all this green chaos. Flying was lines drawn through emptiness. This was emptiness drawn through lines, and the lines kept moving.

Tomas sat up at the bow, awake, watching the water. She could see the shape of him against the gray, still as the moored boat itself.

"You always sleep sitting up?" she asked.

"I sleep when the river lets me."

"And it doesn't let you now?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "There's a set of rapids tomorrow. The map calls them nothing. The people here call them the Teeth. In low water you portage. In high water you run them, if your boat is strong and your luck is good." His voice was level, but she heard the weight underneath it, the way she heard a rough-running engine before it failed. "My boat is strong."

"And your luck?"

"My luck," he said, "is eight years overdue for an accounting."

She sat up too, tarp dripping, and studied the shape of him. "You've done this run before. Up past the Teeth. To where we're going."

"I've done every run," he said. "That's what a trader does."

"No." She kept her voice flat, factual, the voice she used for checklists. "Traders go where the money is. There's no money up there. No villages worth calling villages, no landing, nothing but the territory the maps gave up on. You refused my money and took a story instead, and you knew what a reversed compass meant before I finished describing it, and you flinched at a creek with no name." She let that sit in the drumming rain. "I'm not asking why. You made that rule. But I want you to know I've stopped pretending I don't see it."

For a long moment the only sound was the rain and the river working at the banks below, patient, chewing.

"You fly for a government contract," he said finally, "and you don't trust governments. You carry medicine to villages the state forgot, and you won't say why a woman with your hands ever settled for a bush strip at the end of nowhere. We're both carrying cargo we don't declare." He lay back, put his hat over his eyes. "Sleep, pilot. Tomorrow we run the Teeth, and after that, declarations get heavier."

* * *

They ran the rapids at midday, and it was the closest thing to flying she'd felt in weeks.

The river narrowed and dropped through a corridor of black rock, and the water went from brown to white to a churning violence that threw spray ten feet up the walls. Tomas became someone else at the tiller — or rather, became entirely himself, every part of him in conversation with the boat and the current at once, reading the holes and pillows, threading the channel that existed for maybe three seconds at a time. Gideon braced against the cargo and did what she did best in a cockpit: nothing, absolutely nothing, except watch and hold and trust the person whose hands were on the controls.

Twice the hull struck something deep and glancing, a shudder through the whole frame like a hard landing. The second time, the seep near the bow stopped being a seep.

"We're taking water!" she shouted over the roar.

"I know! Bail!"

She bailed like a woman possessed, the dented pot flashing, while the boat plunged and yawed and the jungle blurred past in streaks of green. Ahead the corridor opened, the water smoothed from violence into a fast brown hurry, and Tomas brought the boat alongside a gravel bar with a touch so gentle she would have missed it if her body hadn't been braced for impact.

They grounded. They breathed. The bilge sloshed three inches deep and rising.

Tomas was already stripping off his shirt, wading over the side into chest-deep water with a knife in his teeth, feeling along the hull. Gideon grabbed the spare tarp, the tar pot, anything. Together — his hands under the waterline finding the sprung seam, her hands above it driving oakum home with a wooden mallet while the current shoved at both of them — they patched it in twenty minutes that felt like a season. When it was done they collapsed on the gravel bar, soaked, shaking, and started laughing at almost exactly the same moment, helplessly, the laughter of people who have just been allowed to keep something.

"Not bad," Tomas said, wringing out his shirt, "for a mail carrier."

"Not bad," Gideon said, "for a trader."

They looked at each other, and the words hung there, polite and mutually understood lies, and neither of them took them back. That was the treaty, she realized. Not truth — truce. Two people with hidden holds, sailing together anyway, because the river didn't care what they were carrying and the work was real.

* * *

It was near dusk, poling through a flooded side channel to make camp on high ground, that she saw the thing she couldn't file away anymore.

They came around a fallen ceiba tree and Tomas, without breaking stride, without thinking, called out the channel by name — a soft word in a language she didn't know, half affectionate, the way you'd greet an old dog. And then he corrected himself, mid-word, and went silent, and poled harder.

Gideon said nothing. But she looked at the chart folded in her pocket, the government survey map with its blank spaces and its confident invented labels, and she understood suddenly that Tomas Reyes wasn't traveling through unnamed country.

He was traveling through named country that had been stripped of its names. And he remembered all of them.

That night, anchored in the lee of the bluff, she took out the survey map one more time and studied the blank space beyond the Teeth, the territory the maps refused, where a compass needle had been reversed like a signature. Three years she had kept her own history sealed in its hold, ballast she never mentioned. She knew what hidden cargo weighed. She knew what it did to a hull.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would start trading stories — real ones, fair ones, the kind of currency that keeps a partnership floating when the water rises.

Across the dark water, she heard Tomas turn over on his crate, once, twice, unable to settle — a man sleeping badly in sight of the place his river was taking him.
