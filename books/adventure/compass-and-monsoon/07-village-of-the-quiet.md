## 7. Village of the Quiet

The village announced itself by absence.

Gideon noticed it first as a gap in the river's texture — no dugouts pulled up on the mud banks, no nets drying on poles, no smoke rising thin and blue from cooking fires. Then the houses resolved out of the green: stilt-legged structures of palm and ironwood, roofs patched with plastic sheeting gone brittle in the sun, and not one person standing at the waterline to watch them come. On every other stretch of this river, children ran along the bank shouting at passing boats, waving with both arms, demanding to be seen. Here the bank was empty except for a dog that watched them from beneath a house without barking, its ribs moving like a bellows.

"They know we're coming," Tomas said quietly. He had cut the engine a hundred meters out and let the boat drift, which told her something — a trader who didn't want to look like he was arriving fast. "They've known since we passed the bend at dawn."

"Word travels?"

"Faster than boats." He let the current carry them the last distance, then swung the bow toward a gap in the reeds where a landing had been worn smooth by years of hulls. "But nobody sent word ahead for us. They just watch. That's different."

Gideon tied off at the post, testing the knot twice. The silence had weight here, like the air before a storm cell breaks. She'd flown over a hundred villages in three years of medicine runs, landed on strips carved from the forest within earshot of a dozen more, and she knew the sound of a place deciding whether to trust you. Usually it was loud — questions, bargaining, hands reaching up to touch the plane's wing struts like they were touching the sky itself. This was the other thing. The held breath.

A man emerged from between two houses as they stepped onto the mud. Old, but old the way river people got old — bent early by work, face creased into something like leather left out in weather. He wore a shirt that had once been a uniform, faded khaki with the buttons mismatched, and he stood with his feet planted wide, arms loose, a posture that could mean welcome or warning depending on what came next.

Tomas spoke to him in the river tongue, low and unhurried, and Gideon understood perhaps one word in five. She'd picked up trading phrases in three years of flying cargo — enough to buy fuel, enough to ask where a road went, not enough to follow whatever slow negotiation was happening now. The old man's eyes never left her while Tomas talked. When Tomas finished, there was a long pause, and then the old man said something short and turned, walking back between the houses without checking whether they followed.

"He says we can come," Tomas translated. "He says you're the pilot who brings the medicine crates. He says—" and here Tomas hesitated, which she had learned was rare enough to be information, "—he says the medicine stopped coming last year."

"That's not right. I flew the run in March. And November before that."

"I told him that. He says the crates came, but the medicine inside wasn't medicine anymore. Or it wasn't the right medicine." Tomas's jaw worked. "He says the clinic box was full of bandages and aspirin when what they needed was—"

He stopped again. Ahead of them, the old man had paused at the largest house, the one with a tin roof and a cross nailed above the door frame beside a bundle of dried herbs that suggested the cross shared its duties. Children were visible in the shadows behind him, clustered together, watching.

"What they needed was what?" Gideon asked.

"You should see for yourself," Tomas said. "I think that's why he's letting us in."

* * *

The interior of the longhouse was dim and smelled of smoke, drying fish, and something else underneath — a chemical sharpness she couldn't name at first, faint but wrong, like the ghost of a solvent. Her eyes adjusted. A dozen people sat or lay along the walls, and near the center, on woven mats, were the children.

There were six of them. The youngest looked four; the oldest maybe ten. Three slept. The others sat with the terrible stillness of children who have learned that movement hurts, and Gideon's stomach dropped before her mind caught up, because she knew that stillness. She'd seen it in a village two seasons ago, forty miles downriver, when she'd delivered a crate marked DERMATOLOGICAL SUPPLIES and stayed an extra hour because a mother asked her to look at her son's arms.

She knelt by the nearest child, a girl of six or seven with her legs drawn up, and waited until the girl's mother nodded permission. Then she took the small hand gently and turned it over.

The lesions started at the wrists. Dark patches of thickened skin, hyperpigmented in a pattern she recognized with a clarity that felt like falling — the same pattern from the medicine crate pamphlets, the ones bundled with the chelation agents she'd ferried to the downriver clinic. Raindrop-shaped spots of discoloration across the palms. The soles of the girl's feet, when the mother silently lifted her, showed the hard, cracked keratosis that made every step a negotiation with pain. On the girl's neck, the skin had begun to change in the way the pamphlet photographs called, with clinical blandness, *raindrop pigmentation*.

Arsenic. Or something wearing arsenic's clothes — some cousin of it, some industrial relative. Heavy metal poisoning, chronic, the kind that came from water. From drinking water, washing in water, eating fish from water, season after season after season.

"How long?" she asked the mother, though the answer was written in the progression. Early stage on the youngest, advanced on the oldest boy, whose palms were so thickened he held them curled like sleeping birds.

The mother answered in the river language, a stream of quiet words, and Tomas translated in a voice gone flat. "Two years since the first child. Three since the fish started tasting wrong. The elders say the river changed color below the falls after the rains, some years back. A shine on it, like oil, but not oil."

"The mine," Gideon said. It wasn't really a question. Every map she'd ever carried showed the concession upstream — the state-sanctioned operation, the one stamped with ministry approvals, the one whose tailings ponds appeared on satellite imagery as pale scars. She'd flown over it dozens of times. She'd thought about it exactly as much as any pilot thinks about anything she flies over: as terrain.

The effect of the word was immediate and strange. The room did not gasp or react — it *closed*. Conversations that hadn't been happening stopped happening harder. The old man in the khaki shirt, who had followed them in, looked at the floor. The mothers drew their children fractionally closer. Even the dog outside seemed to press itself flatter under the house.

Nobody said the word back to her. Nobody would say it. She watched the silence move through the room like cold water through a net, and understood that she had just done something careless — spoken aloud the name everyone here kept locked behind their teeth.

"They won't say it," Tomas murmured. "Don't push."

"Why not? It's not a secret. It's on government maps. There are signs on the haul road, I've seen them from the air—"

"And who printed those signs? Who approved the concession? Who pays the men who drive the trucks?" He shook his head slowly. "In these villages, saying the name isn't talking about a place. It's calling something down on yourself. You say the name, and then men come. I've seen it happen. Not here. Downriver. A man complained about his fishing grounds to a district officer, and six weeks later the complaint came back to him wearing uniforms."

Gideon sat back on her heels. The girl whose hand she still held had not pulled away, and Gideon realized she was stroking the small calloused palm absently, the way you'd calm an animal, and stopped herself. The girl looked at her with an expression far too old for her face — not fear, exactly. Assessment. Deciding what Gideon was worth trusting.

"I can treat symptoms," Gideon said, mostly to herself. "I've got dimercaprol in the emergency kit on the boat — not enough, nowhere near enough, it's meant for acute exposure, one pilot, one accident. What they need is a proper course. Chelation therapy, months of it. Clean water. Testing of the wells and the fish stocks. And above all—" she gestured vaguely upstream, toward everything, "—it has to stop at the source, or treatment is bailing a boat with a hole in it."

"Yes," Tomas said simply.

"This is what the medicine crates were supposed to be. Last year, the downriver clinic requested heavy-metal protocols for three villages. I remember because the requisition was flagged — delayed twice at the regional office. I flew what finally came through." She closed her eyes, trying to reconstruct the manifest from memory. Bandages. Antibiotics. Aspirin. Everything useful for everything except the thing itself. "I thought it was bureaucracy. Paperwork rot. Somebody lost the file."

"Do you still think that?"

She opened her eyes. The room was watching her now, all of them, the mothers and the old man and even the half-asleep children seeming to track her voice. She felt the question land somewhere deeper than her professional pride. Because she *had* thought it was paperwork rot, had logged the discrepancy in her delivery notes and moved on, because three years ago she had taught herself to move on, to see discrepancies and log them rather than chase them, to keep her nose pointed forward and her history pointed behind her. Measured distance. That was how she'd survived. That was the whole architecture of her life.

"No," she said. "I don't."

The old man spoke then, addressing her directly for the first time, his voice dry as seed husks. Tomas listened, then translated, and his translation came slower than usual, as if he were handling each sentence carefully before passing it to her.

"He says: the survey team came through here in the dry season. Six men and a woman. Government people, with instruments and a generator. They tested the water at the falls and at the wells. They took blood from the children — little glass tubes. They were kind. The woman gave the children sweets and wrote everything in a book." He paused. "They said they would come back with help. They went upstream, toward the second falls, and they did not come back down. Later, a helicopter came and took their camp away, piece by piece. Nobody from the government has asked these villages a single question since."

Gideon's pulse ticked up. "Did they leave anything? Papers? A copy of their findings?"

The old man exchanged a look with one of the mothers, and something passed between them — a decision being weighed, Gideon could feel it, the whole room leaning on the scale. Finally the old man rose, joints cracking like green wood, and beckoned them to follow.

* * *

He led them behind the longhouse to a storage hut raised on posts against the damp, unlocked it with a key on a cord around his neck, and inside, among baskets and coils of net, lifted down a dented aluminum case of the kind that carried field instruments. It was empty except for one thing: a single glass vial, wrapped in banana leaf and cloth, and a page torn from a notebook, folded into eighths, soft as cloth itself from handling.

"He says the woman geologist gave this to the headman's wife the night before they went upstream," Tomas translated. "She said: if we don't come back, keep this hidden. Don't show soldiers. Show a doctor, or a priest, or someone who is not afraid." Tomas's voice had changed — rougher. "He has shown no one for eight months. He was waiting to find out if anyone who came asking was afraid."

"Are you?" the old man asked — or asked something, looking straight at Gideon, and though she didn't speak the words she understood the shape of the question perfectly.

She unwrapped the vial. Inside was water — clear, unremarkable, labeled in fine handwriting: *Site 4, seepage channel below tailings weir, 14:20.* The notebook page she unfolded with the care owed to a relic. It was a table in the same hand, columns of numbers, sample sites and readings, and at the bottom, underlined twice, a single line in English:

**Concentrations exceed safe limits by factors of 40–200. Source confirmed. Recommend immediate suspension pending investigation. Copies filed with Regional Office & Ministry.**

Filed with the Regional Office. Filed with the Ministry. And then a helicopter came and took the camp away, and no one ever asked a question again.

Gideon stood very still in the dim hut, holding a dead woman's — or a disappeared woman's — handwriting, and felt the mission turn over inside her like a boat in current. Three days ago this had been a contract. Find the team, log the site, collect the fee, fix the engine, go home. Even after the compass needle lying reversed on the map table, even after the radio log cut mid-word, some part of her had kept it professional, contained, a puzzle with a per-diem attached.

But the puzzle had been sitting in villages like this one the whole time. The missing team wasn't the story. The missing team was the *witness*. Something upstream was poisoning children so slowly and so thoroughly that entire communities had learned to lower their voices about it, and the government's response to the people who documented it had been erasure — and its response to the sick had been bandages and aspirin.

"Gideon." Tomas's hand was on her arm. His face, in the hut's slatted light, was unreadable in a way that was itself readable — a man working very hard to keep something off his face. "What are you thinking?"

She looked down at the vial. Site 4. Below the tailings weir. Upstream, past the second falls, past the maps' polite blank spaces, the source was running day and night.

"I'm thinking," she said, "that I've been delivering the wrong medicine for a year, and someone made sure of it. And I'm thinking the survey team found the same thing we just found, and wrote it down, and were made to disappear for it." She folded the page along its soft creases and handed it back to the old man with both hands, the way you return something sacred. "Ask him to keep hiding it. But tell him — tell him we're going upstream, and if there's anyone left from that team, we'll find them. And when we come back down this river, we'll come back loud."

Tomas translated. The old man listened, and for the first time something moved through the ruined landscape of his face that might have been hope, quickly suppressed, the way you'd suppress a fire you couldn't afford to feed.

Then, as they turned to go, he caught Gideon's sleeve and said one more thing, rapid and urgent, and this time Tomas went very still before translating.

"He says the geologist is alive."

Gideon's heart slammed once, hard. "Alive? How does he know?"

"Because," Tomas said slowly, "twice since the autumn, someone has left fish at the shrine above the second falls. Someone who moves at night. Someone the hunters have seen only as a shape — thin, quick, alone." He met her eyes. "The old man thinks the river people know better than to hunt a ghost. But he also thinks a ghost doesn't take only what it needs and leave thanks."

Above the second falls. Two days upstream, through water rising with the early monsoon, toward the mine, toward whatever Hale's machinery had buried there — toward a survivor who had been living wild for eight months on stolen fish and caution.

Gideon looked up through the hut's slats at the sky, where the clouds had begun stacking themselves in earnest, gray on gray, the monsoon gathering its weight. Every instinct that had kept her alive for three years said turn around, log your fee, fly home.

She'd spent three years learning to stop listening to that instinct. It occurred to her, standing in the quiet village with a vanished woman's water sample burning a hole in her memory, that maybe that was the problem — not that she'd fled something once, but that she'd made fleeing into a discipline.

"Pack the boat," she said. "We leave within the hour."
