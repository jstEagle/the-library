## 8. What the River Keeps

The fire was small on purpose. Small enough to cup in two hands, small enough that a man standing on the far bank would see only darkness and the river's shine and nothing else. Tomas had built fires like this for eleven years — since the last time he'd built one too big, too confident, too certain that no one was watching, and learned what watching cost.

He fed it a strip of dried palm frond and watched the flame lie down flat under the wind coming off the water. Upstream, the river spoke its long sentence against the hull of the *Esperanza*, that low continuous syllable of water finding wood, wood giving way. He'd bailed her twice today. The seep was getting worse, or his patience was getting thinner; with boats, as with men, it was hard to tell which thing failed first.

Gideon Zhou sat across the coals with her boots off and her feet bare on the wet planks of the dock they'd tied to, turning a tin cup slowly in her hands as if the coffee inside were an instrument she was calibrating. She hadn't spoken in twenty minutes. Most people filled silence the way a leaking hull fills with water — steadily, inevitably, from every seam at once. She didn't. She let it sit between them like a third passenger, and Tomas found, to his considerable irritation, that he liked her better for it.

"You're staring at my boat," he said.

"I'm staring at your bail bucket." She nodded toward the dented galvanized pail hooked over the rail. "It's got more holes than the hull."

"The hull has exactly four holes. The bucket has five. We are ahead."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its tools. She drank her coffee. The rain had stopped an hour before dark, and now the whole world dripped in a thousand irregular rhythms, leaves and canvas and the great dripping canopy overhead, so that the silence wasn't really silent at all. It was the jungle breathing around them, patient as debt.

Tomas turned his own cup in his hands. He had been carrying something all day, all week, all the slow miles since she'd shown him the photograph of that map table with the compass lying reversed upon it, needle pointing south when every honest needle points north. He had carried it up through the shallows where the channel braided, through the narrows where he'd killed the engine and poled them through by feel because the charts lied there, through the afternoon when she'd stood in the bow with her binoculars sweeping the banks and he had watched her and thought: *she doesn't know what she's looking for, and I do, and God forgive me, I haven't told her.*

He almost told her now. The words came up his throat like floodwater, and he swallowed them back down, and what remained in his mouth instead was another truth — smaller, older, worn smooth by telling himself versions of it for years.

"I worked for the government," he said.

Gideon's cup stopped turning.

"Cartography office. Puerto Lobo annex." He kept his eyes on the fire. "Eleven years ago. Before the boat, before the trading, before any of this." He gestured with his chin at the river, the dark, the whole ungridded world. "I drew maps."

"You don't seem like a man who sits at desks."

"I didn't. That was the problem." He laughed, and it came out wrong, too short. "Field surveyor. I walked rivers for a living. Every tributary from the escarpment down to the delta, every sandbar, every oxbow. I knew this water before I loved it, which is the wrong order to do things in, if you're asking."

"I'm not asking," Gideon said. But she leaned forward slightly, elbows on knees, and the firelight found the planes of her face and made them honest-looking, which he suspected was a trick of the light, because he had already decided she was a woman made mostly of things she didn't say.

"Why did you leave?" she asked anyway.

*Because of Rafael,* said the truth, from the bottom of him, in his brother's voice. He set his cup down on the planks with exaggerated care, as though the answer might spill out of him the way coffee spills, irretrievably, staining everything.

"Because I mapped a village that isn't on any map anymore," he said.

The river said its sentence. Somewhere upstream a bird called twice and quit.

"There was a place called San Isidro de las Aguas. Two hundred and some people. Good ground, good water, a church with a bell the priest claimed was older than the country." He heard himself speaking in the flat surveyor's cadence, coordinates and elevations, and hated it and couldn't stop. "My job was to chart the floodplain for a road project. Simple work. Walk the ground, shoot the levels, draw the lines. And while I was doing it I noticed the water table dropping. Wells going brackish. Fish floating belly-up in a creek that had run clean for — well, forever, as far as anyone knew. So I wrote a report. Hydrological anomaly, probable contamination, recommend investigation before construction proceeds."

"And?"

"And my report went into a file, and the file went into a drawer, and the road went through anyway, and eighteen months later the mining concession upstream got its final permits." He turned his hands over, palms up, as if the evidence might be written there. "I went back once. There was no village. There was a settling pond where the church had been, and the pond was the color of old teeth. The people had been relocated, the paperwork said. Compensated. Resettled to improved conditions." His voice did something he didn't authorize, cracked along one seam, and he pressed it flat again. "I looked for them. For three years I looked. You know how many I found? Eleven. Eleven people out of two hundred and some. The rest are — somewhere. In the numbers. In the arithmetic nobody shows you."

Gideon hadn't moved. But the quality of her stillness had changed; he could feel it across the fire the way you feel weather change before you can name it. She was listening not just with her ears but with whatever it was she carried, that measured distance of hers, those three years she never talked about and everyone in Puerto Lobo gossiped about anyway.

"So now I trade," he said. "Rice, fuel, salt, medicine, news. I go where the maps stop, because the maps stop where the government stops looking, and the government stops looking wherever it's already gotten what it wanted." He picked up a stick and stirred the coals, and sparks rose and died like short-lived stars. "That's why I left. Not principle. Principle is what you tell the priest. I left because I helped draw the line that erased them, and I couldn't stand to hold the pencil anymore."

The silence after that was different from the other silences. He had given her something true, or true enough — everything except the name at the center of it, Rafael, Rafael who had served on a survey team, Rafael whose letters had grown strange and then stopped, Rafael whom Tomas had reported for the seditious things he wrote in those letters because Tomas had been a good employee, a loyal man, a man who believed the state was clumsy but not cruel — and Rafael had gone into the field one more time and never come out, and the file on his disappearance had been signed by a director named Hale, and Tomas had spent eleven years learning the river the way a penitent learns prayers, going over the same ground again and again, looking for what the water keeps.

*Almost.* He had almost said the name. It sat behind his teeth like a stone.

Gideon set her cup down. When she spoke, her voice was lower than before, stripped of the pilot's briskness.

"When I was younger," she said, "I flew for someone. Not bush work. Real flying. Uniform, orders, a chain of command." She looked at the river, not at him. "There was a day I was given coordinates and told to fly a route and not ask questions, and I didn't ask questions, because I was twenty-six and good at my job and being good at your job feels like being a good person, right up until it doesn't." Her thumb traced the rim of her cup, around and around. "What was at those coordinates — I found out later. Everyone finds out later. Later is where they keep all the information."

Tomas held very still. He understood suddenly that he was being paid in kind, that she had weighed his confession against her own and matched it almost coin for coin, holding back exactly as much as he was holding back. Two merchants at the same stall, each showing honest goods and palming the real ones.

"What happened?" he asked quietly.

"I resigned. Effective immediately. Left everything — the pension, the record, the references. Took a job hauling mail in a plane older than I am, to places that don't appear on the maps you used to draw." She smiled then, briefly, and it was the first real smile he'd seen from her, and it hurt to look at, the way a wound that's healing hurts. "Three years ago. People in Puerto Lobo probably have theories."

"They do. None of them are kind."

"None of them are right, either." She pulled her feet up onto the dock, wrapped her arms around her knees. "Here's the thing I've figured out, Reyes, and it took me three years and a lot of river miles: running doesn't cost what you think it will. You think you're paying with your career, your name, your old life. That's the invoice they show you. But the real bill comes later, and it's smaller items. It's that you can't hear a certain engine note without flinching. It's that you check the mirror in every room. It's that when a stranger asks where you're from, you have to decide, every single time, whether to lie — and you get so good at deciding fast that you forget which version is true."

"Yes," Tomas said. Because there was nothing else to say. Because she had described his own house so precisely he half expected her to name the street.

They sat with that, the two of them, while the fire burned down to a red memory of itself and the moths came in to investigate the dying light. A fish jumped somewhere in the black water, a slap like a hand on wood. Tomas found that his chest felt strange — lighter and heavier at once, the way it did after he confessed at Easter, back when he still went, back when he still believed the ritual moved anything but air.

"Gideon." He said her name carefully, testing the weight of what he was about to do and then, at the last moment, setting it back down. "The camp you found. The compass on the table, reversed."

"What about it?"

"If we find another site — an older one — and it has the same signature..." He chose the next words the way he chose channels in shallow water, feeling for the deep line. "Don't assume it means what Hale wants it to mean. Don't assume it's a message meant for the searchers. Some messages are left for the dead. Or for the families." *For the brothers,* he did not say. "The river keeps things. That's not a figure of speech. Things sink, things drift, things wash up years later on bars fifty miles downstream. Whatever happened to those surveyors, the river was there. The river saw."

She studied him across the embers, and he felt her attention like a lamp beam — thorough, unhurried, missing nothing. He had the uncomfortable certainty that she saw the shape of the thing he wasn't saying, the Rafael-shaped hole in his story, the way you can see the outline of a boat under a tarp without seeing the boat.

"You knew one of them," she said. Not a question. She was a pilot; she read instruments; she had read him.

"I knew the pattern," he said. "That's all I'll say tonight."

"All right." To his surprise, she let it go — gathered it up gently, the way you gather a bird with a broken wing, and set it aside rather than crushing it. "Tonight," she agreed. Then, after a moment: "Your report about San Isidro. Did anything ever come of it?"

"One man in the ministry read it. Wrote a marginal note in red ink. *Insufficient basis for delay.*" Tomas looked at the river. "I've wondered for eleven years whether that man ever came out here. Whether he ever saw the settling pond. Whether he sleeps well."

"And have you decided what you'd say to him if he did?"

"Yes." He fed the last of the palm frond to the coals. "Nothing. I'd show him the water. Water says it better than I do."

Gideon huffed — almost a laugh, almost grief. She reached into her jacket and brought out the folded page she'd taken from the survey camp, the radio log with its last entry cut mid-word, and smoothed it on her knee, though in the dark neither of them could read it. "Tomorrow we make the confluence by midday," she said. "Past that, your charts end and your memory begins. So tell me honestly, Reyes — what's up there?"

Tomas looked upstream, into the dark where the river bent away toward country that had no names on paper, only the names people gave it in whispers, and beyond that the high green wall of the interior, and somewhere inside all of that, a second camp growing over with vines, keeping its secret the way the river kept its drowned things, patiently, in the dark, waiting for someone foolish enough to come looking.

"Up there," he said, "is where the government stopped drawing the map. Which means it's where they started hiding something worth the ink."

* * *

He woke before dawn, as he always did, to the sound of the river and the smaller sound of Gideon already moving, checking the tie-lines, running her hands along the hull seams with those precise pilot's fingers. He lay in the boat's narrow shelter and watched the gray light come up and thought about the rusted tin tag he had seen once, years ago, washed up on a gravel bar near the second bend — a survey marker, standard issue, stamped with a serial number he had never been able to forget and had never dared to trace, because tracing it would mean asking the ministry for a file, and asking the ministry for a file would mean writing his brother's name on government paper again, and the last time he had done that, the paper had eaten him alive.

But tomorrow — or the day after, past the confluence, past the end of the charts — they might find a camp with a compass lying reversed on its table. And camps like that kept records. Tags. Names.

Tomas got up, took the bail bucket, and began, in the gray half-light, to mend its fifth hole.
