## 9. The Second Camp

The river told them where to look before either of them said it aloud.

They had been running the channel for two days since the village of the quiet — two days of brown water climbing the banks, of Tomas reading the current like a man reading a face he loved and feared in equal measure. It was on the third morning that he cut the engine without warning and let the boat drift, and when Gideon looked up from the chart she'd been pretending to study, she saw why.

A tributary opened on the left bank, narrow as a throat, half-hidden by curtain vines. On no map she owned did it exist. But someone had marked it — not with paint or ribbon, but with absence: a gap in the riverside growth too straight to be natural, a corridor of younger trees where old ones should have been, as if something had come through years ago and the forest had only recently finished forgiving it.

"Old logging spur," Gideon said, because saying nothing felt worse.

Tomas didn't answer right away. He stood at the tiller with his head tilted, listening to the water slap the hull the way other men listened to radio. "Logging spurs don't go uphill," he said finally. "That one climbs."

She looked again. He was right. The corridor rose away from the river, angling back toward the ridgeline where, three days' flight south and a lifetime ago, she had found the survey team's camp arranged like an argument no one had stayed to finish.

"We're going up there," she said. Not a question. She'd stopped asking questions somewhere past the second set of rapids, and Tomas had stopped requiring them.

He nosed the bow into the tributary mouth and killed the engine entirely, shipping it to pole them through the shallows. The silence that fell was total in the way only rainforest silence can be — not empty but crowded, layered, every bird and insect holding its note. The boat slid forward under his push, and Gideon sat low in the bow with her hands around the compass she'd taken from the first camp, the one whose needle still swung wrong, still pointed its red tip stubbornly southeast when every honest law of magnetism said north.

She had stopped trusting it. She hadn't stopped carrying it.

* * *

The climb took most of the morning. They hauled the supplies in dry bags and left the boat tied under netting at the waterline — Tomas's insistence, done with a thoroughness that told her he'd hidden things before, more than once, from people who looked hard. The trail, if trail it was, had been cut deliberately: not wide, but level, switchbacked where the slope steepened, drainage channels still faintly visible in the earth like scars on a palm. Whoever built it had expected to use it for years.

"Survey teams don't build roads," Gideon said, breathing hard, sweat stinging the mosquito bites on her forearms.

"No," Tomas agreed, somewhere behind her. His voice had been flat all morning, economical, and she'd filed that away without deciding what to do about it. Since the village he had changed by small degrees — quieter, quicker to move, slower to joke. A man walking toward something he already knew was waiting.

The forest thickened, then thinned strangely, and then they came over a shoulder of ground and into a clearing so overgrown it took her a full minute to understand what she was looking at.

It was a camp. Or it had been. The tents were gone — rotted, collapsed, swallowed — but their footprints remained in the architecture of the vegetation: rectangular shadows in the undergrowth where nothing grew quite as tall, frames of rusted aluminum pushing up through ferns like ribs through skin. A cooking area, its stones furred with moss. A latrine trench long since become a garden bed for some pale-flowered vine. And in the center of it all, exactly where it had no business surviving, a table.

Not wood. Steel, angle-iron legs bolted to a plate top, and though the rust had climbed it in orange tides, it still stood level, because someone had leveled it into poured footings. Someone had meant this table to outlast everything else.

Gideon approached it the way you approach a sleeping animal.

The map was still on it. That was the first impossible thing. Laminated, yes, sealed against weather, but eleven years — she judged eleven years from the growth, from the way a sapling had grown up through the guy-line anchor beside the table and now stood thicker than her wrist — eleven years of monsoons should have taken even laminated paper. This map had been protected. Covered, maybe, weighted, kept deliberately. Someone had wanted whoever came next to find it.

And on the map, drawn in grease pencil gone ghost-pale but legible, were contour lines of a watershed. Sampling points, dozens of them, numbered. And beside several of those numbers, in a smaller, tighter hand, annotations. She leaned close, wiping green rot from the laminate with her sleeve.

*Anomalous. Re-test.* Beside another: *Downstream villages affected? Confirm.* And beside a point almost directly beneath the ridge where, if you drew a line far enough, the mine would sit in its fenced floodlit future: *Source confirmed. Report immediately.*

*Report immediately.* The words sat there in their dead man's handwriting, urgent across eleven years, addressed to superiors who had certainly received them.

"Tomas," she said. Her voice came out strange. "Come look at this."

He was already looking. He had come up beside her without her hearing him, which frightened her more than the camp did, and he was staring down at the map with an expression she couldn't read — until she could, and then she wished she hadn't.

Recognition wasn't the word. Recognition is a flash. What moved across his face was slow, like water finding a crack.

"You knew," she said. "You knew this was here."

"I knew something was here." His jaw worked. "I didn't know what survived."

* * *

They searched the camp the way grief searches a house, touching everything, taking nothing in at first. Gideon found the mess kit, nested cups fused by corrosion into a single sculpture. A boot, one boot, upright and full of soil and rooted through with white fungal thread, as if the forest had planted it. A tin of pencils dissolved to a stain. And then, in the collapsed ruin of what must have been the command tent, she found the second thing that could not have survived unless it had been made to.

A compass. Brass-cased, military pattern, lanyard still attached, lying on a flat stone as if placed rather than dropped. She knelt and picked it up and felt the weight of it, the cold of the metal even in the heat, and she did not want to open the lid and she opened it anyway.

The needle pointed southeast.

Not wobbling. Not damaged. Pointed southeast with the calm certainty of a thing doing exactly what it had been told to do, and Gideon held it in her palm and felt the hair rise along her arms despite the wet heat, because she had seen this before, three days' flight south, on a map table in a camp where men had vanished mid-word, and coincidences did not repeat themselves across a decade with this kind of precision. This was a signature. This was someone signing their work twice.

"Tomas." She turned to show him and stopped.

He was standing at the edge of the clearing where the vegetation began again, and he was holding something small between finger and thumb, and he had gone white. Not pale — white, the blood leaving his face in a single tide, so fast and so complete that for one irrational moment she thought he'd been struck. His lips had color and nothing else did.

"Tomas."

He didn't hear her. He was reading the thing in his fingers, turning it over, and his hand was shaking, and Gideon crossed the clearing in six strides and took his wrist gently and looked.

It was a tag. Equipment tag, aluminum, the cheap stamped kind that government issue had used for a decade before plastic — she'd seen hundreds, logged hundreds, in another life. Rusted nearly through, the stamping shallow but intact, because stamping goes deep enough to survive almost anything except indifference.

*E. REYES. SURVEY TEAM 4. HYDRO.*

The name sat in the rusted metal and Gideon felt the whole shape of the last week rotate around it, every odd thing reorienting like iron filings near a lifted magnet. The way Tomas had known the reversed compass detail mattered before she'd described it fully. The way he'd refused money but taken the story. The way he'd gone quiet on the climb, quiet at the village, quiet in a hundred small ways that she had filed and not opened.

"Elias," she said quietly. "Your brother."

His eyes came up to hers and there was nothing guarded left in them, which was somehow worse than any lie would have been. Guardedness was a wall; this was rubble.

"He was hydrology," Tomas said. His voice sounded scraped. "Third team sent into this watershed in six years. I told myself — for eleven years I told myself he got out, went overland, went native, got a woman pregnant in some border town and was too ashamed to write home. You can live a long time on a story like that. You can build a whole life on it." He looked down at the tag. "I built mine on it."

Gideon's mouth was dry. "Tomas—"

"I reported him." The words came out of him like something being extracted. "Did you know that? No. Of course you didn't know that. Nobody knows that. He was writing letters. Circulating them. Saying the ministry's surveys were fraudulent, saying the water data was being doctored before it reached the capital, saying people upstream of the new concessions were going to die. And I was twenty-four years old and ambitious and I believed in institutions, Gideon, I believed in them the way you believe in instruments — you trust the needle, you fly the heading — and so I reported him. To my superior. Who thanked me. Who shook my hand." He laughed, one dry note with no humor anywhere in it. "Six weeks later Elias's team went missing. And the file closed. And I resigned, and bought a boat, and spent eleven years telling myself I'd left because I distrusted the government, when the truth is I left because I couldn't stand the sound of my own name."

The clearing held its breath around them. Somewhere overhead a bird gave three notes and stopped.

"The reversed compass," Gideon said slowly, working it through. "Both camps. Eleven years apart. Same signature."

"Same hand," Tomas said. "Or same order. Either way—" He stopped. He looked at the table, at the map with its dying urgency, at the sapling grown up through the anchor line, and when he spoke again his voice had changed, had gone from broken to something harder, tempered, the way steel changes and doesn't look different afterward. "This isn't one missing team. This is a practice. They've been erasing teams that find the truth for longer than I've been alive, maybe. And every time, they leave just enough to warn the next ones off. The compass pointing the wrong way. That's not a message to the families. That's a message to whoever comes looking from inside. *We can make north mean whatever we want it to mean.*"

Gideon looked down at the brass compass in her own hand, needle steady on its false bearing, and understood that she had been carrying the threat itself for days, fondling it like a keepsake.

* * *

They worked the rest of the site in silence, but it was a different silence now, shared, load-bearing. Gideon photographed everything — the map, the annotations, the tag, the compass, the tent footprints, the sapling through the guy-anchor, timestamps rolling. She worked with the methodical calm of a woman who had once processed crash sites and crime scenes and had thought that part of her life composted years ago. Her hands remembered. Her hands had always remembered; that was the trouble with hands.

It was Tomas who found the last thing, in the latrine garden, of all places, buried under the pale-flowered vine. A waterproof document tube, military surplus, capped and waxed. Inside, rolled tight in oilcloth, a sheaf of papers — water assays, chain-of-custody forms, and a letter, handwritten, its ink faded to the brown of old tea but every word legible.

Elias Reyes's final report. Addressed not to the ministry but *to whoever finds this*, because he had known, Gideon realized, reading it with her heart slamming, he had known no ministry would ever receive it. He wrote about the anomalous readings. He wrote about the source confirmation. He wrote, in a paragraph that seemed to press harder than the others, as if the pen had been gripped tighter: *If we are recalled early, or reassigned without explanation, or if our equipment is found and we are not, the reader should understand that this watershed has been poisoned with the full knowledge of the Directorate of Concessions, and that Director Hale personally signed the order classifying our preliminary findings.*

Hale.

The name landed in Gideon's chest like ballast dropped from height. Hale, whose contract money was too clean. Hale, whose handshake had been calibrated to the gram. Hale, who had asked her, casually, over the fuel manifest, whether she planned to return via Puerto Lobo — and she had answered honestly, because she had had no reason yet to lie.

"Tomas." She held the letter out. "Read the signature block."

He read it. And Gideon watched something happen in his face that she would carry for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be: the exact moment a man stops grieving a mystery and starts owing a debt. His eyes didn't fill. His whole body simply settled, the way a boat settles when cargo is properly stowed, every part of him arriving at once into purpose.

"Eleven years," he said, "I've been running this river like a man serving out a sentence. Hiding cargo. Dodging customs launches. Telling myself it was freedom." He folded the letter along its original creases with a care that undid her. "It was preparation. All of it. Every year of it. I just didn't know what for."

Gideon looked east, where the light was beginning to fail through the canopy, where the ridge line ran dark above them, where the mine sat in its floodlit impunity, and where — she understood now with total clarity — the first camp's message had been leading all along. Two camps. Two signatures. One direction. The pattern didn't span years randomly. It was a trail, laid breadcrumb by breadcrumb by men who had known they might not survive to walk it themselves, pointing steadily upstream toward the thing that had to be witnessed.

And downstream, in the capital, a man named Hale had just learned that his search pilot had not come home.

"We need to be on the water before first light," she said. "And Tomas — we're being followed. I'm sure of it. I've been sure since Puerto Lobo and I let the money talk me out of my own instincts."

He slung the dry bag over his shoulder and looked at her, and for the first time since she'd met him, he smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man handing someone a loaded weapon.

"Then let's give them a wake worth following."

Behind them, as they started down through the trees toward the river, the brass compass lay open on its stone, needle steady, pointing faithfully southeast — toward the mine, toward the truth, toward everything the state had spent a decade making north mean anything but.
