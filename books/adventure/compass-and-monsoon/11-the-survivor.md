## 11. The Survivor

The waterfall announced itself an hour before they saw it — a low thunder rolling up the gorge like a drum being carried toward them through the trees. Tomas cut the engine and let the boat drift, and in that sudden quiet Gideon could hear the water before she could see it: a white seam splitting the cliff face ahead, falling maybe forty meters into a pool the color of hammered tin.

"You knew this was here," she said.

"I knew there was a fall on this stretch." Tomas shipped the oars he'd been using to steer them through the flooded channels. His hands were blistered from three days of poling where the current ran too strong for the motor, and he flexed them now without looking at them, the way a man does when he doesn't want to admit they hurt. "I didn't know what we'd find behind it."

They had been following the pattern for two days now — the reversed compass needle drawn again on a strip of bark lashed to a tree at the mouth of the gorge, the same deliberate inversion Gideon had found on the map table of the first camp. Not a sign of panic. A signature. Someone had walked out of that camp alive, someone who understood that the only people who would ever read the message were people who had already seen the first one.

Gideon stood in the bow as the boat slid under the overhang of wet rock. Spray came off the falls in a fine cold mist that soaked through her shirt in seconds and made her teeth ache. The pool below the falls was deep and green-black, ringed by boulders furred with moss, and the noise was enormous — a sound that filled the skull and left no room for anything else.

"Amara Osei," she said aloud, testing the name. The geologist. The only name on the survey roster that Hale's file had flagged with a single typed annotation: *unaccounted for.*

Tomas looked back at her. In the shadow of the cliff his face was all planes and darkness. "If she's here," he said, "she's been here a long time."

"Then we knock politely."

He brought the boat against the rocks beneath the falls' eastern lip, where a shelf of stone ran back into a cleft behind the curtain of water. Gideon stepped off onto rock slick as glass, caught herself, and stood a moment letting the roar wash over her, feeling it in her sternum. Then she ducked under the falling water.

The passage behind the falls was narrow and dripping, lit by nothing but the greenish glow that filtered through the cascade, and it opened after twenty meters into a chamber the size of her cabin at Puerto Lobo. Her eyes needed a full minute to adjust. When they did, she stopped walking.

Someone lived here.

Not survived here — *lived*. There was order in the cave that starvation and fear should have erased long ago. A sleeping roll of woven palm fronds laid on a raised stone shelf above the seep line. Firewood stacked in a dry niche, sorted by size. A row of river stones arranged along one wall, each one painted with a mark — she counted fourteen of them, then understood with a cold turn of her stomach that they were a calendar. Fourteen days. No — she looked closer at the older marks, faded, half worn away by damp. More than sixty. This woman had been counting days behind a waterfall since before the monsoon was even a rumor.

And against the far wall, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with vine cord, sat a metal case. On top of the case, placed where no one entering could miss it, lay a compass. Needle pointing south.

Pointing the wrong way.

"Tomas," Gideon said quietly. "She knows we're coming. She's known since we started up the gorge."

"Then why hasn't she—"

The firewood moved.

It took Gideon a full second to understand that the stack of wood beside the niche had shifted, that behind it was a gap in the rock, and that something human was unfolding out of that gap — slowly, painfully, a shape resolving from shadow into a woman so thin that her joints looked like knots in dry wood. She held a machete in both hands, and even though her arms shook with the weight of holding it up, she held it anyway, blade level, pointed at Gideon's chest.

"Stop there." Her voice was a ruin, scraped down to almost nothing, but the words were precise. Educated. "One more step and I will open you, and I want you to know I've thought about exactly how."

Gideon stopped. She kept her hands wide and empty at her sides, palms out, the way you'd approach a spooked animal on a strip at dawn. "Dr. Osei," she said. "My name is Gideon Zhou. I'm a pilot. I flew the search contract for your team."

The machete did not waver. The woman's eyes — enormous in a face gone hollow, fever-bright — moved from Gideon to Tomas and back. "Search contract," she repeated. "Whose?"

"Director Hale's office. Lands and Resources."

Something happened in the woman's face then that Gideon would remember for the rest of her life. It wasn't fear. It was the look of a person watching a door close — a small, controlled grief, almost administrative, as if she were filing the information in a drawer already too full. "Hale sent you," she said. "Of course he did. He always sends someone eventually. It took the last ones eleven weeks."

"We're not—" Gideon began.

"The pilot." The machete dipped a centimeter. The woman swayed, caught herself against the rock. "There was gossip on the radio traffic I used to monitor, before I stopped trusting any of it. Zhou. The bush pilot who does the medicine runs. You landed at San Rafael twice while we were staging." Her eyes narrowed, and behind the fever Gideon saw a mind still working, still sharp, running calculations in the dark like a machine that refused to stop. "If Hale sent you, you're here to finish it. If you're not, you're here because you found the camps. Which means you found the compasses."

"Reversed," Gideon said. "Both of them. Deliberate."

"Deliberate." Amara Osei laughed — a terrible sound, two dry clicks in her throat. "Sixty-eight days I've wondered if anyone would read it. Sixty-eight days wondering if I scratched my whole message into the dark for no one." The machete lowered another degree. "Say the last entry in the radio log. The one that cut off."

Gideon frowned. She'd read the log four times; it was burned into her. "*Position confirmed at grid nine-four. Samples show concentration levels beyond—*" She stopped. "Beyond threshold. It just ended. Mid-word, mid-sentence."

"*Threshold*," Amara repeated softly. "Yes. That was me. I was transmitting when their boat came around the bend." Her gaze went past Gideon, to the case against the wall, and stayed there. "You can put the knife story away, pilot. If you'd come to kill me you'd have done it at the mouth of the cave. Nobody walks behind a waterfall in monsoon season for murder." She let the machete drop. It clattered against stone and she staggered, and Tomas moved forward without thinking and caught her elbow, and she didn't pull away — didn't have the strength to pull away, Gideon thought, and hated herself for noticing it clinically, the way she'd once noticed fuel numbers.

* * *

They got a fire going in the dry niche — small, screened, the smoke threading out through a fissure in the ceiling that Amara had clearly spent days finding. Gideon dug through the boat's stores and came up with the last of the rice, a tin of fish, the dregs of the sugar. Watching Amara eat was its own kind of ordeal. The woman ate with terrible discipline, small measured mouthfuls, forcing herself not to bolt the food she so obviously dreamed about, and between mouthfuls she talked.

"I hid the night the boat came," she said. "Eight men. Not soldiers — contractors, the kind the ministry hires when it wants work done that never appears in a budget line. They took the others upriver. All six of them. Alive." She said *alive* the way other people say *dead*, and Gideon felt the floor of the conversation tilt. "I watched from the treeline. I had a radio in my hand. I could have called it in. Do you know why I didn't?"

Gideon shook her head.

"Because every channel I had led back to him." Amara set down the tin with exaggerated care, as if her hands might betray her. "I'm a geologist, Ms. Zhou. Twelve years. Water tables, sediment cores, contamination mapping. When the ministry contracted our survey, the brief was routine — baseline hydrology for the new concession upstream. Standard. We sampled the river at eleven points over three weeks." She closed her eyes. "At point seven, downstream of the mine's tailings outfall, the arsenic reading was forty times the safe limit. Mercury past anything I've seen outside a textbook. Cadmium. Lead. I ran the assays three times because I was certain I'd contaminated the samples myself. Three times."

"The villages," Gideon said. Her voice came out flat. She was thinking of San Rafael, of the skin lesions on the children there, of the crates of antiseptic cream she'd been delivering for two years to treat symptoms whose cause she'd never once questioned. The medicine run. The good work. Three years of flying supplies to people the state was poisoning from a facility it had licensed itself.

"You've seen it," Amara said, reading her face. "Of course you've seen it. You fly those runs." She nodded slowly, and there was no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse. "So you know. And you know what happens when a village elder writes to the regional health office. The regional health office reports to the ministry. The ministry issued the concession. The concession is held by a company whose board includes two men who appointed Director Hale." She spread her ruined hands. "Every road leads home. I learned that in the capital years ago, when I was younger and believed in memos. So when I got the readings, I didn't report them. I copied them. Every page. Every assay, every chain-of-custody form, every field note. And then I kept surveying, and I waited, and I watched what the ministry did about the anomaly I'd logged through official channels."

"And what did they do?"

"They sent a second team to re-verify." Amara's mouth twisted. "A team that never arrived. And then a third, the year before yours. And then ours." She reached over and rested her hand on the metal case, and Gideon saw that her fingers trembled — not from weakness alone. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? Your team was the fourth. Four teams, eleven years. Everyone who has ever taken readings downstream of that mine has been collected. Because the readings are the one thing the ministry cannot argue with. Paper burns. Assays replicate."

Tomas had gone very still across the fire. Gideon glanced at him and saw something in his face she couldn't name — not surprise. Recognition, maybe. A man hearing a story he'd half-known and dreaded confirming.

"The case," Gideon said. "That's the copies."

"That's the proof." Amara drew the case into her lap and rested both hands on it, and for a moment she looked less like a starving woman in a cave than a priestess at an altar. "Water samples sealed in glass, labeled, dated, with duplicate sets hidden in three locations I will die before revealing. Field notebooks. Photographs. The concession documents showing state authorization. Correspondence — including a directive, signed, instructing our team lead to restrict sampling to points upstream of the outfall. Signed by Hale himself, eight days before we disappeared." She looked up, and her fever-bright eyes fixed on Gideon with a force that had nothing to do with hunger. "This case ends him. It ends the mine. It might end the minister. It is the heaviest object I have ever carried, and I have carried it sixty-eight days up a river valley with a broken rib and nothing to eat but palm hearts and river snails."

"Why keep it?" Gideon asked quietly. "Why not destroy it and walk out? You'd live."

The question hung in the roar-filtered silence. Amara looked at her for a long moment, and then, incredibly, she smiled — a cracked, exhausted, utterly unbroken thing.

"Because I buried a colleague at the second camp," she said. "Elias Voss. He stayed behind with me when the rest were taken — he was older, his knee was bad, we thought they'd only take the ones who could identify the sample sites. He lasted nine days. Sepsis, from a cut that should have killed no one." Her voice frayed and she pulled it tight again. "His last hours, he kept asking me if I'd gotten everything. Everything, he said. Not *get me out.* Get *everything.*" She pressed her palm flat against the case. "I am the last person alive who knows what's in these pages, Ms. Zhou. If I die, it dies with me, and Elias died for paper. I don't intend to let that be true."

* * *

Later, when Amara finally slept — collapsing mid-sentence into her palm-frond roll with the case still hooked under one arm like a child clutching a toy — Gideon sat by the small fire and listened to the waterfall and tried to do arithmetic.

The numbers were bad. The boat was leaking and low on fuel; the monsoon had weeks left in it; the coast was five days' hard travel downstream even in good conditions, and these were not good conditions. Amara weighed perhaps thirty-five kilos and could not walk more than a few hundred meters without resting. And somewhere behind them, or ahead of them, or both, Hale's machinery was turning. Gideon had signed a contract. She had filed a float plan. She had reported finding the first camp over a channel she now understood was monitored. Hale knew where she'd gone. It was only a question of when the net came down.

"You're doing the math," Tomas said from the mouth of the cave. He'd taken the watch at the entrance without being asked, sitting with his back against the wet stone, looking out at the green curtain of falling water.

"I'm always doing the math."

"What's it say?"

Gideon fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "It says carrying her out is nearly impossible. It says leaving her is unthinkable. It says the case gets us all killed." She looked up. "It also says the children at San Rafael have been drinking poison for two years while I flew them antiseptic cream and called it help."

Tomas was quiet for a moment. The falls filled the silence, patient as a heartbeat.

"My brother used to say," he said at last, "that the truth is like ballast. You don't carry it because it's light. You carry it because without it, you capsize." He didn't explain, and Gideon, watching his profile against the falling water, sensed that there was a whole submerged country beneath that sentence — his brother, his government years, all the things he circled and never named. She let it be. There would be time, or there wouldn't.

Behind them, in her sleep, Amara Osei murmured something — a number, a grid coordinate, some fragment of the ledger reciting itself even in dreams — and tightened her arm around the case.

Gideon looked at the fire, at the sleeping geologist, at the compass lying on the stone with its needle stubbornly pointing the wrong way, and understood that the choice she'd flown three years to avoid had finally landed in front of her, dripping, starving, and impossible to refuse.

"We leave at first light," she said. "Downstream. All of us."

Tomas nodded once. But he was still looking out at the water, and after a moment he said, very quietly, "Then we'd better hope the river's faster than whatever he sends."
