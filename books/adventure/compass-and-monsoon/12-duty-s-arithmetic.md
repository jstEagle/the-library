## 12. Duty's Arithmetic

The cave behind the waterfall was a throat of stone and cold water, and for eleven days it had kept Amara Osei alive the way a miser keeps coins — grudgingly, and only because she had learned to count everything.

She counted now, sitting with her back against the rock while the falls thundered beyond the curtain of vines: two tins of sardines, half a bag of cassava flour gone hard as plaster, three water-purification tablets, one ledger wrapped in oilcloth beneath her shirt against her ribs like a second heart, and forty-one sample vials sealed in wax and hidden in a crevice only she could reach at low water. One body, running on perhaps nine hundred calories a day. One mind, still sharp enough to frighten her with what it knew.

And now, two visitors.

The pilot — Zhou, Gideon Zhou; Amara had memorized the names of every bush pilot licensed on this river, back when names were things that mattered — crouched near the fire they'd dared to build small, feeding it dry kindling from Tomas Reyes's boat stores. The firelight moved across her face and showed a woman doing arithmetic. Amara recognized the expression. She'd worn it herself once, in an office with a view of the capital's administrative quarter, the night she first understood what the numbers added up to.

"You're Dr. Osei." The pilot said it quietly, not a question. "The survey team's geologist."

"Was." Her voice came out rusted. She hadn't spoken aloud in six days except to herself, counting. "The team is dead. Or scattered. I don't know which, and I've stopped pretending I'll find out."

"The camp we found—"

"Was staged to be found." Amara pulled the oilcloth bundle from inside her shirt and set it on her knees, not opening it. "Tents upright, gear stacked, no blood. You noticed the compass."

Gideon Zhou went very still. "Reversed needle. On the map table."

"Like a signature," Amara said. "Like someone leaving a note under a door and hoping the right person reads it. The survey teams didn't leave those. I did. This time." She looked past them, toward the grey-green shimmer of the falls. "I hoped whoever found it would be stubborn enough to keep looking instead of filing a report."

* * *

Eight months earlier, in a government laboratory that smelled of floor polish and formaldehyde, Amara had run the same assay four times because she did not want to believe it.

Mercury. Arsenic. Cadmium, in concentrations that made her recalibrate the machine twice. The samples came from tributary stations downstream of the Cerro Blanco concession — the state-sanctioned mining operation whose environmental compliance report she herself had signed off on, in her capacity as consulting geologist, eighteen months before. Her signature. Her seal. Her assurance to the ministry that tailings containment met code.

The numbers said otherwise. The numbers said the containment cells had been failing since the first wet season, leaching into the watershed, into the fish, into the villages along the lower river where children swam in water that glittered with something finer than light.

She ran it a fifth time. Same result.

That night she sat in her office with the printouts fanned across her desk like a losing hand, and she did what careful people do: she mapped the reporting chain. Her findings would go first to the regional environmental directorate. She looked up who held that post. A man named Okonjo, who had been deputy director of concessions before his promotion — concessions including Cerro Blanco. Fine, she thought. Above him, the ministry's inspection board. Its chair had given the keynote address at Cerro Blanco's opening ceremony; there was a photograph of him shaking hands with the operation's director on the ministry website. Above the board, the office of oversight itself, and above that—

Above all of it, in every channel, at every level, like a watermark visible only when you held the paper to the light: Director Hale. Deputy Director Hale. Adviser Hale. Consultant Hale. The same name recurring through the org chart the way mercury recurred through her samples — not everywhere, but everywhere that mattered.

Amara understood then that there was no such thing as reporting. There was only delivering the evidence to the man it condemned.

She was not naive. She had known governments were porous, that institutions bent. But she had believed — she could admit it now, in the dark behind the waterfall, admitting it to herself if no one else — that the system was corrupt at its edges and sound at its core. That there was a door somewhere that opened onto honesty.

There wasn't. There was only Hale, wearing different titles, and the door he held open led down.

So she did the thing careful people do when the map has no honest road: she went off the map. She copied everything — the assays, the chain-of-custody logs, the internal memos she had no clearance to read and read anyway, the shipping manifests showing Cerro Blanco's chemical imports dwarfing anything its containment design could hold. She hid copies in three places. And when the survey team was assembled — a routine hydrological survey of the upper watershed, she was told, nothing sensitive — she volunteered, because the survey's route passed directly through the villages, and she wanted to see with her own eyes what her signature had helped do.

What she saw was skin. Lesions on the arms of children, the particular pattern of chronic heavy-metal exposure she'd only ever seen in textbook photographs. Mothers who lowered their voices when they mentioned the mine, glancing at the river as though it might be listening. A clinic nurse who showed Amara her stock of chelation agents, donated by a church mission, and said, "We use them up in weeks. We order more. They never ask why a village of four hundred needs so much."

Amara took her samples quietly, labeled them in code, and told no one on the team what they meant. Not yet. She wanted the full dataset first. She wanted to be unanswerable.

She was almost finished when the radio call came.

* * *

"They were recalled," Amara told the pilot and the trader, in the present, in the cave. "Day nineteen of the survey. A message from the regional office: survey concluded early, return to base by air, equipment to follow. Except no plane came. Instead, men came upriver by boat. Six of them, armed, with paperwork authorizing them to 'secure' our findings." She smiled, and knew it was a bad smile. "Secure. That was the word. Like we were cargo."

The trader, Reyes, sat with his forearms on his knees, motionless in a way that suggested he was holding himself motionless on purpose. "You ran."

"I ran." The words tasted like ash even now. "I told myself I'd come back with proof, with journalists, with lawyers. But the truth is simpler and uglier: I ran because they were going to kill me, and staying would have been suicide dressed up as principle. The others—" Her voice caught. She let it. "The others trusted the paperwork. They gathered their instruments and waited to be escorted out. I watched from the tree line. I heard the shots. Two, then silence, then more later, farther off. I don't know how many survived. I have spent eight months not knowing, because knowing would require going back, and going back requires being alive."

The fire popped. Outside, the monsoon rain hammered the jungle with the patience of something that intended to continue forever.

"And the compass?" Zhou asked. "At the camp?"

"Afterward. I went back — close enough, at night, while they were burning documents. I couldn't save anyone. So I left a message instead. Reversed the needle on the map table, arranged the gear so it looked deliberate rather than looted. A reversed compass points south when you think it points north. It says: *everything you're reading here is backwards. Look again.*" She shrugged. "It was a small thing. It felt obscene, how small it was, next to what had happened. But it was the only thing I could carry out of there besides myself."

"And the ledger." Zhou nodded at the oilcloth bundle. "That's why you're still here. Why you haven't tried to walk out."

"Walk out where?" Amara laughed, short and hard. "Every road leads to Hale. Every official, every garrison, every port authority. If I surface anywhere within his reach, I disappear the way the survey team disappeared, and the evidence disappears with me. No. My plan was to wait out the rains and move downriver to the coast, slowly, trading passage, and find—" She stopped. Found what? A journalist? An embassy? She'd had eight months to construct an exit and had produced nothing but survival. "Find someone. Anyone outside the chain."

Her eyes went to the pilot. To the hands that had been feeding the fire — steady, capable, a mechanic's hands, a flyer's hands.

"But you," Amara said, "have a plane."

* * *

Later, when Reyes had gone out into the rain to check the boat's mooring lines — or, Amara suspected, to give them privacy, though she couldn't yet read what passed between these two — Gideon Zhou sat down across the fire from her, and Amara asked the question directly, because she had learned that directness was the only currency that held its value out here.

"How much fuel does your plane have?"

Zhou didn't pretend to misunderstand. "Enough to reach the coast if the weather breaks and holds. Maybe. The engine threw a rod at Puerto Lobo — I patched it, but a patched engine over mountains in monsoon season is a prayer, not a plan."

"So it's possible."

"It's possible." The pilot's jaw worked. "You're asking me to fly evidence out. Samples, documents. To the coast, to someone who can use them."

"To anyone outside Hale's chain. Your mentor, if you have one. A newspaper. A foreign consulate. I don't care who, as long as they're real and they're far." Amara leaned forward. The firelight made her shadow enormous on the cave wall, a hunched giant behind her starved actual body. "Listen to me, Pilot Zhou. Forty-one vials and one ledger. The vials prove contamination. The ledger proves intent — procurement records, internal correspondence, a directive in Hale's own hand ordering the suppression of the previous surveys. Yes. Plural. Yours isn't the first team to find a reversed compass. There was one five years ago. Seven years ago. Hale has been erasing this for a decade, and each time, the mine expanded, and each time, more villages went quiet."

She saw it land. She saw the pilot's face do the thing faces did when private dread became confirmed fact — the slight settling, like a house taking damage.

"The villages I supply," Zhou said quietly. "Downriver. The lesions. I've been flying medicine to them for three years. Antibiotics, antifungals, painkillers. Treating symptoms of—" She stopped.

"Of a mine the state licensed and a man named Hale protects," Amara finished. "Yes. You've been bailing a boat with a hole in the hull, and the man who cut the hole has been watching you bail and calling it progress."

Silence, except for the falls. Amara let it sit. She had learned, in eight months of solitude, not to rush people toward decisions. Decisions arrived at their own speed or not at all.

But she watched the pilot calculate. She could nearly see the columns of figures: the risk of the flight itself, weather and engine and Hale's reach; the cost of involvement, because carrying this evidence meant becoming the kind of person Hale erased; and somewhere in the arithmetic, surely, the exit — the option of walking away, of saying *this is too big for me*, of returning to medicine runs and mail drops and the manageable griefs of ordinary work.

Amara knew that column existed because she had run the same calculation herself, eight months ago, in a laboratory that smelled of floor polish. She had chosen self-preservation, and she had been right to, and she had paid for it anyway — paid in the shots she'd heard from the tree line, paid in six days of counting sardines alone in the dark, paid in the knowledge that her survival had been purchased with other people's deaths. Self-preservation was not free. It just billed you later.

"Say I take it," Zhou said at last. "Fly it out. What happens to you?"

"If the flight succeeds, I testify. Publicly, loudly, with my name attached. That's the whole point — the evidence is worthless without a witness willing to stand behind it." Amara touched the bundle at her ribs. "If the flight fails, I'm already dead; I just haven't been informed. Either way, the samples matter more than I do. Promise me you understand that. If it comes down to saving the evidence or saving me, the evidence goes."

"That's not a promise I'll make."

"It's the only one worth asking for."

"Then ask for something else." The pilot's voice was flat, tired, certain. "Because I've done the arithmetic you're doing. I know what it costs. I flew for the government once." Something moved behind her eyes, old and locked. "I know what it means to carry orders you shouldn't have carried. I'm not going to sit in a cave and negotiate whether your life weighs less than your ledger. Both come out, or neither does."

Amara stared at her. In eight months, no one had offered her a condition so absurd, so human, so entirely beside the point — and no one had made her feel, in the same breath, less alone.

"You don't know what you're agreeing to," she said. "Hale doesn't send letters. He sends men."

"Then we'd better be gone before they arrive." Gideon Zhou stood, brushing ash from her knees, and looked toward the curtain of the falls, where the rain fell silver and endless. "How long until the weather breaks?"

"Two days. Three. The barometer's been climbing since dawn."

"Then we spend tomorrow preparing. Waterproofing the samples, planning the route, figuring out how we get to my plane without being seen." The pilot paused, and for a moment the competence in her face cracked into something younger and rawer. "And Dr. Osei — when we fly out, if we fly out — I need you to understand that I'm not doing this because I'm brave. I'm doing it because I did the math once before and chose wrong, and I can't do it twice."

Amara Osei pressed the oilcloth bundle back against her ribs, against the second heartbeat it had become, and nodded.

Outside, the monsoon roared on. And far downriver, though none of them could know it yet, a launch with no lights aboard was moving upstream through the dark, carrying men with paperwork and orders, closing the distance at twelve kilometers an hour.
