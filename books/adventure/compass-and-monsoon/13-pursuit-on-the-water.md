## 13. Pursuit on the Water

Tomas heard the launch before he saw it — or rather, he heard the absence of it.

The river at night had a vocabulary he'd spent eleven years learning: the slap of water against hull, the cough of outboards from villages that ran their engines late to charge batteries, the argument of monkeys, the wet percussion of rain on broad leaves. Every sound had a place in the grammar. What didn't belong was silence with weight behind it — an engine running low and slow and dark, throttled back to a murmur, moving without lights against a current it should have been fighting.

He was on watch because he'd stopped pretending he could sleep. Three days upriver from Amara's cave, three days of flooded channels and a hull drinking more than Tomas liked to admit aloud, and every instinct he owned — instincts older than his government service, older than his grief — had been whispering the same word since dusk.

*They know.*

He stood at the stern with one hand on the tiller and watched the black water, and there, where the channel bent around a drowned island, he saw it: not a light, but a shape darker than the dark, a long low line displacing the river's surface wrong. Too straight. Too purposeful. A log drifted; this thing *drove*.

"Tomas?" Gideon's voice from under the tarp amidships, sleep-roughened, instantly awake. She was like that now — had been since the cave, since the ledger, since the moment she'd said yes to a dead woman's evidence. Sleep sat shallow on her and broke at a breath.

"Boat," he said quietly. "No lights. Coming up the main channel."

A pause. Then she was beside him, barefoot on the wet planks, her hair plastered flat by drizzle, and he felt rather than saw her go still the way pilots went still — total attention, nothing wasted.

"How far?"

"Two kilometers. Maybe less. They're not hurrying." He turned the fact over as he said it, testing its edges. "They don't need to hurry."

"Because they think we're ahead of them or behind them?"

"Because they think it doesn't matter."

Amara emerged from the shelter of the cargo tarp, the oilcloth bundle strapped against her chest beneath her jacket, her face a pale smudge. She didn't ask what was happening. She'd been a survey geologist for nine years; she knew what a boat with no lights meant on a river where the government owned every official vessel and most of the unofficial ones.

"The side channels," Gideon said.

"Flooded," Tomas answered. "That cuts both ways. The water's high enough to take us through the old logging cuts north of Isla Serena — channels that aren't on any chart they'll be carrying. But—" He hesitated, hating the next part, hating that the next part was true. "The channels braid. In the dark, at flood stage, with this hull taking water the way she is—"

"You know them," Gideon said. Not a question. She'd stopped asking questions about how well he knew things weeks ago, and he'd stopped inventing answers.

"I know them." His brother had known them better. Mateo had run these cuts at sixteen with a borrowed peke-peke and a lantern wired to a motorcycle battery, laughing into the rain, calling the turns before they came. *Left at the strangler fig, right where the bank fell in, trust the smell of the water.* Eleven years dead now, or disappeared, which was the river country's way of saying dead without giving anyone a body to bury. "I can do it. But we can't run the engine loud. If they hear us working hard, they'll know exactly which water we're in."

"So we drift."

"We drift and steer. And pray the bilge keeps up."

Gideon looked down at the deck between her feet, where the water seeping through the old seams had begun its nightly shine. Then she did something he hadn't seen her do yet in all their weeks together: she reached out and gripped his forearm, once, hard, pilot's fingers finding the bone.

"Then let's be ghosts," she said, and went forward to wake the engine to idle and kill the running lamp.

* * *

For two hours they moved through the flooded forest like something dreamed rather than driven.

It was work Tomas loved despite everything — the kind of work that emptied a man of everything except the next ten meters of water. The channels here were corridors cut through standing rainforest, the trees rising black on both sides, their lower trunks submerged so that the canopy reflected off water that had swallowed the understory whole. Driftwood hung in the branches at eye level. Orchids floated past on rafts of broken root mat. Twice a snake crossed the bow, riding some invisible current of its own, and neither time did anyone speak.

Tomas steered by memory and by the smell of the water — mud and iron meaning the deep cut, green rot meaning the shallows where the fig roots waited just under the surface to peel a hull open. Gideon crouched at the bow with a paddle, silent, reading the water's texture the way she read weather, calling hazards back to him in a whisper pitched below the engine's idle: *Root ball left. Sandbar shifting right. Log — dead ahead, port side, now.*

She was good at this. That kept surprising him. A woman who lived in the sky, who thought in three dimensions and fuel weights and crosswinds, learning the river's grammar in six weeks the way she seemed to learn everything — completely, hungrily, as if the sky had only ever been another way of paying attention.

The launch behind them held its course up the main channel. Once, distantly, they heard a voice carry across the water — amplified, official, bored-sounding, the voice of a man reading a proclamation to a river that wasn't listening. An announcement. A warning about unregistered vessels. Hale's machinery, doing what it always did: making erasure sound like procedure.

Then the channel braided, and Tomas took them left at the strangler fig, and the world closed in.

* * *

The trouble came at the narrows past Isla Serena, where the flood had done something new.

Tomas felt it before he understood it — a change in the current's pull, the tiller going light in his hand, the water's voice dropping from its monsoon roar to a strange sucking hush. He brought the boat around the last bend and the moon, briefly unshuttered through a tear in the cloud, showed him the problem: a raft of logs, dozens of them, a whole hillside's worth of timber the flood had stolen from some upstream concession, jammed nose-first across the channel in a tangle that groaned and shifted under its own pressure.

"Back," he breathed, and Gideon was already reversing their momentum, the engine burbling, the bow swinging — and then the engine caught, stumbled, caught again, and died.

The silence afterward was enormous.

"No," Gideon said, to the engine, to the night, to the arithmetic of it. She got the cover off in seconds, hands moving by feel in the dark, and Tomas heard her diagnosis arrive in the set of her shoulders before she said it. "Fuel line's starved. Either the pickup's clogged or—" She lifted the tank cap, tilted it, swore softly in the flat tone of someone confirming what they already knew. "Water in the fuel. The hull's been shipping it for days; it's gotten into the tank. There's maybe a liter of clean gas above the muck, and I can't get at it without draining the whole system."

"How long?"

"To drain, dry, refill from the spare cans? Twenty minutes. Here?" She looked up at the logjam, which chose that moment to shift with a sound like a door opening in the earth. "We're sitting in a funnel. Everything the river takes comes through this gap eventually. Including us."

Behind them, far back down the braid of channels, a searchlight swept the junction where their route met the main stem. It lingered. Swept again. The men in the launch were being methodical. Hale's men were always methodical; that was the horror of them, that they believed in process the way other men believed in God.

"There's a portage," Tomas said slowly, feeling his way through the map in his head. "Old logging skid, maybe four hundred meters, around the jam to the cut on the far side. My father dragged dugouts over it when I was a boy."

"With the boat? With everything?"

"With the boat. With everything."

Gideon was quiet a moment. Rain ticked on the tarp. Somewhere in the flooded forest a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"Or," she said, "we drain the fuel system here, fast, and run the last kilometer of channel quiet before they finish sweeping the junction."

"And if the engine won't restart after?"

"Then we portage anyway, having lost twenty minutes instead of gaining them."

Tomas weighed it — the two gambles, the two directions failure could come from — and found he couldn't separate them. So he did what he'd learned to do in government offices full of men who never touched water: he asked the person whose hands would do the work.

"Which one can you guarantee?"

"Neither," Gideon said. "But I can almost guarantee the engine. Nobody can guarantee that logjam stays put for a portage." She was already pulling the drain plug's housing loose. "Help me get the spare cans forward. We do it here."

* * *

It went wrong the way things went wrong on rivers: quickly, sideways, and involving water.

They got the system drained — Gideon flat on her back in the bilge with a flashlight clenched in her teeth, Tomas passing tools and bailing the fouled gasoline over the side in careful, reeking arcs — and they got the first spare can open and the line primed, and the engine caught on the third pull and ran rough and then ran true, and for ten seconds Tomas allowed himself the dangerous luxury of relief.

Then the logjam moved.

Not all of it. One section — a trunk the size of a church pillar, undercut, buoyant, patient — lifted free of the tangle with a groan and swung downstream on the flood current like a battering ram, directly across their path, and the current that carried it carried the boat too, and there was no room and no time and no choice. Tomas threw the tiller hard over. The bow came halfway around. The log's root mass, a claw of torn earth and stone, caught the stern quarter and rolled the boat onto its beam ends with a violence that turned the world into noise.

Cold. Total cold, the river's true temperature revealed at last, the monsoon's warmth a lie told only at the surface. Tomas went under with the deck above him becoming the ceiling becoming the sky, and his body did what eleven years of rivers had trained it to do: shed panic, find the current, swim not against it but *with* it, angling for the eddy behind the jam where the water curled back on itself and gave a drowning man a place to stand.

He surfaced gasping in the eddy's slack and turned in a circle, counting. Amara — there, clinging to the tarp bundle, which was floating, which was designed to float, thank God and whoever had sewn it. Himself. And Gideon—

"Gideon!"

The river answered with rain.

He found her thirty seconds later, or a year later — time gone strange in the cold — snagged against a submerged sapling, her head barely clearing the churn, one arm hooked over a branch that flexed and cracked each time the current hauled at her. Her legs trailed downstream, useless, caught in something he couldn't see. As he swam for her she went under once, came up, and he heard her say his name in a voice stripped of everything except the fact of it.

"Don't fight it," he shouted. "Let it push you to me—"

The branch broke.

She came at him like a thrown parcel, and he caught her by the collar and the hair and the sheer animal refusal to let go, and the current took them both, tumbling, the world alternating between air and a brown roaring dark, until the eddy's curl caught them and slammed them against the flank of the great stranded log itself, and Tomas got an arm around the trunk's barnacled shoulder and held on, Gideon crushed against him, both of them breathing like bellows.

For a while there was nothing in the universe but rain and heartbeat and bark.

"Amara," Gideon rasped finally.

"Alive. On the bundle. I see her." He did — a pale shape in the eddy fifty meters back, hauling herself and the oilcloth package hand over hand along the logjam's edge toward them, stubborn as erosion. "You?"

"I've been drowned before," she said, which was not an answer, and which he filed away among the many things she'd say that weren't answers, to be opened someday. "The boat?"

He looked. The boat was there — miracle of miracles, wedged against the jam's downstream shoulder, half-swamped but upright, the tarp still lashed, the engine cowling just breaking the surface. And lashed to the stern rail, bobbing, the second spare fuel can, its cap holding.

The first can — the one they'd opened, the one that had been feeding the engine when the world rolled over — was gone. He scanned the flood downstream and saw it once, a small yellow drum riding the crest of the current, growing smaller, going south with everything else the river decided to keep.

He did the arithmetic without wanting to. Two cans had bought them a margin — enough to reach the coast strip with a reserve, enough to turn back if turning back had ever truly been on the table. One can bought them the coast strip with nothing over. No return. No second attempt. No version of this story anymore except the one where they went all the way through.

"We're committed now," he said aloud, because saying it made it real, and making it real was a kind of courage you could hand to another person.

Gideon, still pressed against the log, shivering so hard her words came out in pieces, managed something that was almost a laugh. "You say that like it's new information."

"It's new information," Tomas said, "when the fuel says it too."

* * *

They salvaged what the river permitted.

The boat bailed, the engine — miraculously, or because Gideon's hands remembered things her shaking body forgot — drained, dried, and coaxed back to life on the surviving can's fuel. Amara's bundle dry inside its oilcloth, checked twice, checked a third time by its owner with the tenderness of a woman handling a sleeping child. Their supplies scattered but mostly gathered: a lost crate of trade goods, a lost tarp, a lost box of Gideon's charts, gone spinning into the flood like leaves.

And the fuel. One can. Tomas lashed it amidships where nothing could take it without taking the boat, and stood a moment looking at it, and felt the strange lightness that came after terror — the specific clarity of a man who has run out of exits and discovered that the walls of the trap, viewed from inside, look exactly like a road.

"They'll have heard the engine die," Gideon said. She was wrapped in the one dry blanket, steering again, gray-faced and steady-eyed, her hands reliable even as her jaw trembled. "They'll figure the flood did their work for them. Or they'll come looking at first light."

"Then we're through the far cut before first light."

"And after the far cut?"

"After the far cut," Tomas said, "there's the dead lake, and the mission station on its shore, and after that nothing between us and the coast but river." He paused. "And whatever Hale sends next."

Ahead, the channel narrowed toward the old logging cut, black water sliding between black trees, and somewhere behind them the searchlight swept its patient arc across the junction, methodical as a clock. The rain thickened. The engine, fed by its single remaining heart of fuel, beat on.

Tomas put the tiller over and took them into the dark, committed, going forward, because forward was now the only direction the story allowed — and because, though he would not say it, not yet, perhaps not ever, watching Gideon Zhou refuse to drown had settled something in him that eleven years of drifting had left unsettled.

The river rose. The night held them. And one yellow drum rode amidships like a promise with a hole burned through its middle.
