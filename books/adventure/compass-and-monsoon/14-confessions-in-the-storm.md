## 14. Confessions in the Storm

The squall came down the river like a wall being pushed.

Gideon heard it before she saw it — a sound like the whole forest inhaling — and then the light went from green to gray to nothing, and the rain arrived not as water but as weight. Tomas killed the engine and steered them by feel toward the black slash of shoreline he'd been watching for an hour, muttering coordinates under his breath like prayers, and then they were inside a flooded oxbow choked with fallen trees, and the boat was tied off to a trunk thick as a church pillar, and the world had narrowed to the sound of rain hammering the canopy above them.

They rigged the tarp between two branches with hands that shook from cold, not fear. Gideon's fingers wouldn't close properly anymore; three days of hauling line and bailing water had stripped her palms raw. She sat on the boat's bench seat with her knees drawn up, watching the river rise around them in visible increments, eating dried fish Tomas had produced from somewhere in his endless cargo hold, and thought: this is what it feels like when there is nowhere left to run.

The storm made the decision for them. No fuel to turn back — Tomas had lost the spare drum in the chase, and the main tank held maybe enough to reach the next trading post if the current cooperated, which it would not. They were committed. Committed people, she was learning, talked differently than people still pretending they might leave.

Tomas sat across from her, one boot braced against the gunwale, a tin cup of something hot and terrible in his hands. He offered it. She took it. It tasted like bark and burnt sugar and it was the best thing she'd ever drunk.

"Where did you learn to read water like that?" she asked. "Back at the split channel. You called the current wrong-footed before I even saw it move."

He looked at her for a long moment. Rain drummed the tarp overhead like applause from an audience that never stopped.

"I worked for the government," he said. "Hydrographic service. Eleven years."

* * *

Tomas watched her absorb it. He'd expected more surprise, maybe. But Gideon Zhou only nodded slowly, the way people nod when a suspicion finally gets its paperwork.

"The maps," she said. "You knew the channels the charts don't show."

"The charts are lies," he said. "Most of them. The government redraws the interior every few years, and every redrawing erases something. A village. A tributary. A name." He turned the cup in his hands. "I helped draw those lines. That was my job — making the country legible so it could be managed. Managed means taxed. Taxed means controlled. And where control failed—" He shrugged. "You've seen the second camp."

She had. The overgrown tents. The rusted tag with *R. REYES* stamped into corroded aluminum, and the way his face had gone the color of the river mud.

"Your brother," she said quietly.

"My brother." Rain filled the silence for him, gave him somewhere to hide while he spoke. "Mateo. Older than me by four years, smarter than me by ten. He joined the survey corps because he believed the work meant something — mapping the interior so roads could reach it, clinics, schools. All the words we used back then." His mouth twisted. "I joined two years later. Partly to follow him. Partly because our mother cried less when we were both employed."

"What happened?"

"He started noticing things. Discrepancies. Survey teams going out and not coming back, and their files closing without bodies attached. Water readings from the mine districts that got 'corrected' in transit. Mateo kept copies. Of course he did — he was the kind of man who kept copies." Tomas's voice flattened out, went procedural, the way voices do when feeling would otherwise drown them. "And I was the kind of man who reported him."

Gideon didn't move. The tarp snapped once in a gust and settled.

"They didn't arrest me," Tomas continued. "That's the part people don't understand about how it works. There was no interrogation room, no threats. A supervisor bought me lunch. Asked how things were at home. Asked if Mateo seemed stressed lately, if the isolation upriver was getting to him, if maybe family should intervene. And I — " He laughed, a short ugly sound. "I told him my brother was under strain. I told him I'd keep an eye on him. I thought I was protecting Mateo. Giving him cover. Making him look supervised instead of suspicious."

"And then?"

"Then Mateo's team went upstream and didn't come back, and his file closed without a body, and I understood what my lunch had purchased." He set the cup down on the bench with exaggerated care, as though his hands could no longer be trusted with objects. "I resigned within the month. Took the trading boat because it was the only thing I knew that moved through the country he disappeared into. Eleven years now, running this river, asking every village about survey teams, about men who came through and didn't return. Everyone thinks I'm eccentric. Collecting stories." He looked up at her. "I'm collecting evidence. Or I was, until you landed at Puerto Lobo with a reversed compass drawn on your map and I understood the pattern was bigger than Mateo. That it had teeth all the way to the top."

"Hale," Gideon said.

"You know the name."

"I flew for him." She said it before she could stop herself, before the eleven years of practiced distance could catch the words and drag them back. "Or for what he represents. Director Hale was already senior when I was in service. You don't fly orders past his desk without learning his signature."

The rain roared. Somewhere across the flooded oxbow, a tree groaned and went down, and the water shivered against the hull.

* * *

Gideon had never told anyone the whole of it. Not her mentor, who'd signed off on her discharge papers with a look she still couldn't parse. Not the mechanics and missionaries and traders of her three years of measured miles. She'd built a life the way you build a levee — one load at a time, never looking at the water behind it.

But the storm had taken the levee apart piece by piece. The plane grounded. The contract revealed as bait. Amara Osei in her cave with her ledger full of names, and among them villages Gideon had flown medicine to, villages whose children wore the mine's poison on their skin. There was no dry ground left to stand on. So she stood in the water and told it.

"There was a district called San Rafael," she began. "Eastern slope. I was twenty-six, flying transport for the Interior Ministry — mail, personnel, occasionally cargo I wasn't cleared to ask about. One October we got tasking for a relocation support operation. Resettlement assistance. The paperwork was beautiful. It always is." She wrapped both hands around the tin cup because they wanted to shake. "We flew in census teams first. Then agricultural assessors. Then, in November, we flew fuel drums marked as generator supply, and men in unmarked fatigues, and I noticed the drums were heavier than generators and smelled wrong, and I asked my crew chief, and he looked at me with such pity that I stopped asking."

Tomas said nothing. He had the stillness of a man who knows that moving might end the telling.

"In December the radio traffic from San Rafael changed. Then thinned. Then stopped. And in January we got tasking to overfly the district for cartographic update, and I saw it from six thousand feet. What was left of it. The fields burned in patterns — deliberate patterns, the same geometry as your compasses, the same signature thinking. And I flew the grid, and I took the photographs, and I delivered them to the ministry, and I signed the flight log, and three weeks later the cartographic section published a map that showed San Rafael as uninhabited forest." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "Uninhabited. Like nobody had ever been there. Like forty years of a village was a rounding error."

"You reported it," Tomas said. Not a question.

"I tried. Every channel. My squadron commander sent me to the inspector general. The inspector general sent me to legal. Legal sent me to a counselor, for my stress. And somewhere in the middle of that circuit I realized every office I walked into had the same photograph on someone's desk, or the same academy ring, or the same way of saying *let me look into it* while already reaching for the phone to warn whoever needed warning. The system wasn't leaking. The system was the pipe." She stared into the cup. "So I did what cowards do. I finished my tour. I kept my head down. I flew the last eight months of tasking knowing what some of it was, and then I mustered out and came here and learned to love small planes and small strips and small distances, and I told myself the story that I'd escaped."

"Did you?"

"No." It came out of her flat and true. "I relocated. There's a difference. I carried it with me every mile — that's why I measure everything in miles, Tomas. Miles are honest. A mile is a mile no matter what you're fleeing. People aren't. Places aren't. But a mile never lies to you about what it is."

He was quiet a long time. The storm had settled into its long middle age, the initial violence spent, rain falling steady and enormous, the kind of rain that could go on for days.

"Mateo believed in the state," Tomas said at last. "Right up until the end, probably. He thought if he documented carefully enough, followed procedure precisely enough, the truth would protect itself. He trusted the machinery." His jaw worked. "And I trusted it too — trusted it enough to hand my own brother to it, believing I was oiling the gears rather than feeding them. So between us, we have the whole lesson, don't we? Believers get disappeared. Doubters get recruited. Nobody gets out clean."

"No," Gideon agreed. "Nobody gets out clean."

"But you flew home after San Rafael. You could have stayed in service. Promoted, even — they reward the ones who swallow."

"I couldn't stay and I couldn't speak, so I left. It's not courage. Don't make it courage."

"I'm not." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and in the gray light his face looked older and younger at once. "I'm saying we both ran. And I'm saying running isn't the sin I made it. The sin was the lunch. The sin was signing the log. Running was just the body refusing to keep doing what the mind had agreed to." He paused. "Amara didn't run. She hid. She stayed inside the wound with the proof of it, starving, for months. She's braver than either of us."

"She's also half-dead," Gideon said. "Bravery has a cost curve too."

"Yes. Everything does." He gestured vaguely at the rising water, the drowned forest, the whole sodden indifferent world. "That's the arithmetic nobody teaches you. Every choice costs. Even the choice not to choose — that one just bills you later, with interest."

* * *

The rain eased near dawn, or what they guessed was dawn, the darkness turning merely deep instead of absolute. Neither of them had slept much. They'd talked until the talking wore itself smooth, and then sat in the shared silence of two people who have shown each other their worst rooms and found the other still standing in the doorway.

Gideon broke the quiet first.

"When we find Hale's men — when, not if — you could still turn back. Take the boat downstream at the next fork. No one would call it desertion. You've carried Mateo alone for eleven years. No one could ask more."

"I'm asking more," Tomas said simply. "Of myself." He looked at her across the wet space between them. "You reported nothing and fled, and you've hated yourself for it for three years. I reported everything and stayed, and I've hated myself longer. Do you know what I've figured out? Guilt doesn't care about the direction you ran. It only cares that you ran." He tapped the deck of the boat, twice, like a man knocking on a door he intended to open. "This time I'm not running. Whatever's upstream — Hale's men, the mine, whatever happened to Mateo at the end — I'm going toward it. Not for redemption. I don't believe in redemption. For accuracy. I want the record corrected. That's all any of us can do now. Correct the record."

Gideon thought about her plane sitting dead on the flooded strip at Puerto Lobo, her whole life measured in miles sitting dead with it, and found — to her surprise — that the thought had edges but no blood.

"Tomas. When this is done, if we're alive — I need you to know I won't apologize. For San Rafael, for the log, for any of it. Apology is a currency and I don't have enough to pay what it costs. All I have is what I do next."

"Good," he said. "Apologies are for people who plan to keep transacting. We're past transactions." He almost smiled; it was mostly weather-weariness, but underneath it something real moved. "What we do next is enough. It has to be. It's all there is."

She passed him the cup. He drank, grimaced at the dregs, and passed it back, and that small exchange felt like a treaty signed between two bankrupt nations: no reparations, mutual recognition, open borders.

Then the radio crackled.

It was the little battery set they'd salvaged from Amara's cave cache, tuned to the regional band, kept on low for weather bulletins. Static washed and washed — and then, threading through it, a voice. Official cadence. Measured, reasonable, terribly calm.

"—advisory to all river traffic. Armed fugitives are moving upstream through the flood zone, transporting stolen government property. They may be traveling under the guise of relief delivery. Any vessel providing them aid will be considered complicit. Report sightings to—"

Tomas reached over and clicked it off. In the sudden silence the rain sounded louder than ever.

"That was fast," Gideon said.

"He's not searching anymore," Tomas said. "He's announcing. There's a difference. Searching means he doesn't know where we are." He looked up at her, and the gray light caught the lines of his face like contour marks on one of his old maps. "Which means somebody told him exactly where we are. And that narrows our list of friends considerably."

Gideon looked upstream, where the drowned forest opened onto water no chart named, and felt the last comfortable lie of her life dissolve into the rain: that distance could keep anything safe.

"We move at first light," she said. "Whatever's waiting."
