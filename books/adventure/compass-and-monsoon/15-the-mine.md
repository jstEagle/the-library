## 15. The Mine

They smelled it before they saw it.

Gideon noticed it first as a wrongness in the air — a thin, mineral tang riding above the river's usual breath of rot and green. She had spent three years learning the interior's vocabulary of smells: the sweet mushroom heaviness after rain, the smoke-and-woodsmoke signature of a village cooking dinner, the iron bite of a storm still hours downstream. This was none of them. This was something manufactured, something that did not belong to water or earth at all.

"You smell that?" she said.

Tomas didn't answer. He was standing at the tiller with his head slightly tilted, the way he did when he was listening to the river instead of looking at it. His jaw worked once. "Bend coming up," he said finally. "Stay low when I say."

Amara Osei rose from the bottom of the boat where she'd been sleeping — if you could call it sleep, that twitching half-consciousness she fell into for twenty minutes at a time, one hand always on the waterproof case strapped to her chest like a child. Three weeks in a cave behind a waterfall had left her with a way of moving that reminded Gideon of birds: quick, economical, head turning before her body followed. She'd filled out some since they'd found her, on Tomas's rice and dried fish and Gideon's emergency rations, but her wrists still looked like knots in rope.

She lifted her face into the wind and breathed it in, and Gideon watched her expression do something terrible and quiet.

"That's sulfuric acid drainage," Amara said. "Diluted. That's what diluted looks like." Her voice was flat, clinical, the voice she used to keep herself from screaming. "We're close."

* * *

The river bent north around a shoulder of black rock, and the current slowed as the channel widened, and there it was.

Gideon had seen photographs of mines. She had flown over open pits in her government years, great terraced wounds in the earth that from altitude looked almost orderly, almost beautiful, like amphitheaters carved by patient giants. Nothing had prepared her for this from water level, at boat speed, close enough to hear it.

The operation sat on the eastern bank where two ridges converged, a sprawl of corrugated steel and sodium light in the last gray hour before full dark. Floodlights on steel towers threw cones of white across the worksite, and inside those cones everything moved with the mechanical patience of an organism going about its digestion: haul trucks crawling the switchback roads, a conveyor belt lifting ore into the mouth of a processing plant, steam rising from settling ponds in slow silver columns. A chain-link fence topped with razor wire ran along the whole perimeter, and beyond the fence the forest simply stopped — not cleared so much as erased, a margin of raw orange earth fifty meters wide where nothing grew and nothing would grow again for a long time.

But it was the water below the mine that made Gideon's hands go cold on the gunwale.

A creek came down out of the worksite through a culvert under the haul road and joined the main river in a plume she could see from two hundred meters away — a fan of pale, milky blue-gray spreading into the brown current like ink dropped in milk, except inverted, the poison lighter than the living water, floating on top of it, spreading. Where the plume met the river there was a line as clean as if it had been drawn with a ruler. On one side, water the color of tea and life. On the other, water the color of old porcelain.

Dead water. She thought of the children at the village of the Quiet, their forearms mapped with gray lesions, the mothers who would not meet her eyes, the way no one would say the mine's name aloud — *upstream*, they said only, *it comes from upstream*, as if naming it might call it down upon them. And here it was, upstream, with its lights and its fences and its trucks, pouring itself into the river all night, every night, patient as rain.

"Kill the running lights," Tomas said quietly. "Now."

Gideon reached back and thumbed off the small red lamp they'd been using to navigate the flooded channels after dark. Darkness folded over the boat. The only light now came from the mine itself, a mile upstream and growing, and from the fitful lightning walking the horizon behind them, silent, still too far to thunder.

Tomas eased the boat toward the western bank, into the shadow of the overhanging forest, and let the current carry them. The engine idled so low it was more vibration than sound.

"Amara," he said. "How much time do you need?"

Amara was already moving. She unzipped the canvas satchel she'd carried out of the cave, out of the waterfall, down three days of river, and began laying out her instruments with the unhurried precision of a surgeon: sample vials in foam slots, a pH kit, a hand lens, a battered field notebook whose cover had gone soft with damp. "I need to be opposite the confluence," she said. "Close enough to see which channel the discharge follows. And I need—" she checked the sky, the lightning, the black bulk of the clouds rolling down from the northwest— "I need about forty minutes before that squall hits, because after it hits nobody can document anything."

"Forty minutes," Tomas repeated. He looked at the mine, at the fence, at the dark shapes of men moving between the floodlight towers. Then he looked at Gideon, and in the dimness his eyes were unreadable, but his voice wasn't. "This is the part where we can still turn around."

It wasn't. They both knew it wasn't. There was no fuel to turn around — that had been decided for them two nights ago, in the chase through the flooded channels, when the spare drum went into the water and sank and Tomas went in after Gideon instead. Every liter they had left was committed to the coast, to the derelict missionary strip, to the rendezvous with her old mentor that might well be a trap. But Tomas said it anyway, the way a man holds a door open he knows is closing, because the holding matters even if the door doesn't stop.

"No," Gideon said. "Take us in."

* * *

They drifted.

It took all of Tomas's skill and most of Gideon's nerve. He worked the tiller in small, patient corrections, using the eddies along the western bank to hold them nearly stationary against the current while keeping them in the tree shadow, and Gideon realized — not for the first time, but more completely than before — that what she had taken for a trader's competence was something older and stranger. He read the water the way she read weather. He knew where the river wanted to go before it got there.

The mine grew in the windshield of darkness. Details resolved out of the glare: the company name stenciled on the processing building in letters tall as a man — Gideon didn't need to read them; she already knew; everyone in the territory knew the name and spoke it carefully, the way you speak the name of a dangerous animal — the flag flying over the administration block, the rows of white barracks housing the workers, the fuel depot with its ring of sandbags. And along the near fence line, at intervals, the small dark shapes of guard posts.

Amara worked. She filled vials from the dead side of the confluence and the living side, labeling each in tiny handwriting, narrating in a whisper that was half for the notebook and half for herself: *"Discharge point, east bank, culvert beneath haul road. Visible plume approximately four hundred meters into main channel. Water clarity nil. No visible aquatic life within plume margin. No insects on surface. Note absence of fish activity at confluence despite evening rise—"*

Her voice caught on that. Just barely. Gideon heard it.

"Evening rise," Amara said again, steadier. "There should be fish feeding here. There are no fish here. Write that down. Somebody has to write that down."

"I've got it," Gideon said.

She had the camera — Hale's own irony, a government-issue survey camera from the equipment cache in the second camp, rusted but functional, loaded with film Amara had vacuum-sealed in her cave for three weeks against exactly this night. Gideon braced her elbows on the gunwale, found her breathing the way she'd learned to find it behind the stick of a bouncing plane — slow, deliberate, the body told to wait — and began to shoot.

She photographed the plume with the floodlights behind it, so the boundary between living water and dead was unmistakable. She photographed the culvert, the discharge, the stain on the rocks below it. She photographed the fence and the flag and the sign at the gate, framing each shot with the river in the foreground so no one could later claim the contamination came from anywhere else. Her hands were steady. Some cold, professional part of her that she had believed dead for three years — the part that had once worn a uniform and flown orders — sat up inside her and did its work, and Gideon let it, because tonight its work and her conscience pointed the same direction for the first time in longer than she could count.

Through the viewfinder, the world was small and framed and true.

Then a figure walked the fence line, and Gideon stopped breathing.

Not a worker. Workers moved with the tired shuffle of men ending a shift. This figure moved with purpose, flashlight held low, and it stopped at the near corner post and stood facing the river, and after a moment a second figure joined it, and the two of them stood together looking out across the water at the darkness where the forest came down to the bank.

"Down," Tomas breathed.

Gideon lowered the camera slowly. In the bottom of the boat, Amara froze with a vial half-filled, and for a moment the only sounds were the river against the hull and the distant industrial hum of the mine and the thunder, closer now, beginning to find its voice.

The two figures at the fence talked. One raised an arm and pointed — not at the boat, Gideon thought, not at the boat, at the river downstream, gesturing the way men gesture at weather. Then a third shape loped up from the direction of the barracks, and there was a brief conference, and one of them turned and jogged back toward the administration block, and the other two remained at the fence, and one of them lit a cigarette, and the match flame was very small and very bright in the enormous dark.

"They're not looking for us," Amara whispered. "They're watching the storm."

"They're watching the river," Tomas corrected softly. "Storms move logs. Storms move boats. Nobody runs a patrol on a night like this unless they're worried about what the water might bring them." He was quiet a moment. "They know someone's coming. Maybe not us specifically. But they know."

The lightning walked closer. Thunder arrived seconds behind it now, a long rolling pressure Gideon felt in her chest, and the first fat drops began to strike the leaves overhead with a sound like slow applause.

"Thirty seconds," Amara whispered, filling the last vial, capping it, wrapping the notebook in oilcloth. "That's all I needed. We have it. We have all of it — discharge, plume, dead zone, the works, timestamped, georeferenced. It's real now. It exists outside my head."

Gideon wound the film forward with her thumb and felt the spool take up its final frames, and the small mechanical resistance of it felt like the most important thing she had ever held.

Then the searchlight came on.

It bloomed from the top of the nearest tower — not one of the work floods but a separate lamp, a proper searchlight, sweeping the river in a slow white arc, and Gideon understood with a lurching drop of the stomach that the men at the fence hadn't been pointing at weather at all. The beam crawled along the western bank toward them, indifferent, methodical, turning the rain to drifting silver, and there was nowhere to go — the bank behind them was a wall of flooded brush, the current ahead ran straight past the mine's front door, and the engine, if she started it now, would sound like a gunshot in all that listening dark.

"Tomas," Gideon said.

"I see it." His hands were already moving on the tiller, reading the water one last time. "There's a channel marker buoy fifty meters down — navigation light, battery-powered, red. When the beam passes it, it'll flare. For maybe two seconds, every eye on that bank will be on the buoy and not on us." He glanced back at her, and rain was running down his face, and he was almost smiling. "When I say *now*, you get flat, and you don't look up, and you don't stop rowing until I tell you, no matter what you hear."

"Rowing?"

"We're killing the engine. From here to the bend, we go the old way." He pulled the starter cord loose and let the motor die, and the silence that rushed in was total and enormous, broken only by rain and thunder and the sweep of the light coming around again, closer, whiter.

The beam touched the buoy.

"*Now.*"

Gideon came off the camera, grabbed the spare paddle, and dug it into the black water, and the boat slid out of the tree shadow into the open current with the searchlight blazing behind them and the mine burning its hundred lights into the storm, and somewhere upstream a whistle began to blow — short, sharp, urgent, answered by another — and the clock that had been ticking since the day a survey team vanished into the pattern of a reversed compass began, at last, to run.
