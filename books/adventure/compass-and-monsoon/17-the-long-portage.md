## 17. The Long Portage

The rapids announced themselves a full kilometer before they arrived — a low, continuous thunder that Tomas felt in the soles of his feet through the deck planks, the way he had felt it since he was six years old standing on his father's boat. *La Boca del Diablo.* The Devil's Mouth, where the river dropped forty meters through a channel of black granite no wider than a church aisle, and where every trader on this water had a story about someone who hadn't made it.

He cut the engine and let the current carry them into the eddy below the point, and Gideon read his face before he said a word.

"That's a no," she said.

"That's a no."

She looked at the water the way she looked at weather — professionally, without flinching, cataloguing what could not be argued with. White water stood up in the throat of the gorge like the backs of animals. Somewhere in that churn were rocks the size of houses, and somewhere downstream of the rocks, if they survived the rocks, were Hale's men, who would not need to survive anything because they'd simply wait at the bottom for whatever the river gave them.

"So we don't run it," she said.

"So we don't run it." He was already moving toward the bow, pulling back the tarp. "There's a portage trail on the left bank. Old logging track from before the concession days. It goes around the gorge and puts us back on the water below the falls. Four kilometers, maybe five."

"Five kilometers carrying—" She stopped. Looked at the boat. At the samples wrapped in oilcloth and sealed in Amara's ammunition can, lashed under the thwart since the cave. At the ledger, which rode against Tomas's own chest in a waterproof bag because he did not trust any part of the boat to hold it. "Five kilometers carrying all of it."

"The boat comes apart. I've done it before." He handed her the machete. "You'll take the front half."

* * *

Taking a boat apart, it turned out, was easier than either of them pretended. The hull came off its ribs in sections; the outboard went into a pack frame Tomas had carried since Puerto Lobo and never explained; the fuel drum, half empty now after the chase, became two loads instead of one. By the time the gray light thickened into actual morning they had the whole enterprise distributed across four backs' worth of burden, except there were only two backs, and the rain had started again, the fine vertical rain of the deep interior that didn't fall so much as accumulate, as though the forest itself were sweating.

Gideon took her first load and made it thirty meters before Tomas understood what he was watching.

It wasn't weakness. That was the thing that surprised him. It was that everything she knew was wrong here. Her hands — those hands he'd watched rebuild a carburetor by feel in the dark, hands that had landed a plane on a strip barely wider than the boat — took hold of the jungle like it was an instrument she'd never been trained on. She reached for branches the way you reach for something solid, and liana is not solid; it gives, it swings, it delivers your weight sideways into a sapling that snaps and drops you hip-deep into a fern brake full of water. She stepped where a pilot steps, testing surfaces, and the forest floor does not test; it deceives. Within the first hour she had fallen twice, gashed her forearm on a thorn palm, and lost one of the hull sections down a slope when her footing went and she had to choose between the load and her collarbone.

She chose the load. He'd give her that. She rode the slope down on top of the hull section like a toboggan, cursing in three languages, and came up at the bottom with mud to her jaw and the section intact.

"Don't say it," she called up to him.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say it with your face."

He climbed down and took the section from her, and together they hauled it back up the switchback, and neither of them spoke about how much time the fall had cost, or what time cost now, with men behind them who moved through this country the way he did.

Because that was the other arithmetic, the one ticking underneath: the patrol that had taken Amara had found their old campsite. Found the cold fire, the hidden canoe, the trail. Hale's men were not trackers by trade but they had radios and numbers and no need to sleep well, and the portage trail was the only way around the gorge unless you swam, and nobody swam the Devil's Mouth.

By midday the trail narrowed along a ridge above the roar of the falls, and Tomas's own body began to betray him.

He had known the river for forty years. He had known hunger, and fever, and the particular exhaustion of poling a loaded barge against current for twelve hours. But he was fifty-three years old, and the portage was not the river. It was stairs built by no one, mud greased over clay, humidity that turned each breath to drinking. His knees had started talking to him around the second kilometer, and by the third they were shouting, and he found himself stopping more often than he admitted, finding reasons — checking a lash point, listening for pursuit — that were really just his legs asking for ten seconds of mercy.

Gideon saw through every single one of them.

She didn't call it mercy. She just started taking things. When he set the outboard down to rest it, she picked it up before he straightened. When he paused at a fork, she was already twenty meters along the correct branch, because she had learned the trail's logic faster than he'd expected — not the jungle's logic, never that, but the logic of a route, which is a kind of navigation, which is the one language she and the forest shared. She began walking first, breaking trail, choosing lines through the deadfall with a pilot's eye for terrain, and it worked until it didn't, and then it worked again, and slowly, without either of them announcing it, they stopped being two people carrying separate loads and became one animal moving through the green dark with a single purpose distributed between them.

At the ridge's high point, where the falls showed themselves whole for the only time on the portage — a white column of violence dropping into mist, loud enough to swallow thought — Gideon stopped and looked back the way they'd come.

"Tomas." Her voice was flat. "Smoke."

He followed her finger. Far downslope, maybe two kilometers back along their own trail, a gray thread rose through the canopy. Not cooking smoke. Cooking smoke rises lazy. This rose in a column, disciplined, from a fire built fast by men who wanted heat and light and didn't care who saw.

"How far ahead are they?"

"If they're moving our pace? An hour. Less, if they're fresh." He shifted the ledger bag against his chest. "They'll have caught the hull drag marks where we crossed the creek. After that it's just reading."

"Can we get off this trail?"

"Not without losing a day. And the day after that they're still behind us and we're still slower." He looked at the falls, at the mist, at the impossible white noise of the place, and an idea arrived that he hated immediately, which was usually how his best ideas arrived. "But we can make them think we did."

* * *

What he needed was fire, and what they had was the last of the kerosene, a quarter liter, meant for the stove. What he needed was time, and what they had was the fact that the trail along the ridge ran close to the lip of the gorge for two hundred meters, close enough that the spray came through the trees like rain that had changed its mind about falling downward.

He soaked his spare shirt in kerosene, wired it to a dead sapling at the point where the trail bent away from the gorge, and waited for the light to fail. When it failed, he lit it. From below, from upslope, from anywhere a man might be climbing with tired eyes, it would look exactly like a campfire burning careless and bright on the lip of the falls — and the obvious conclusion, the conclusion any reasonable man would draw, was that the fugitives had decided the fire was worth the risk, had settled in for the night within sight of their pursuers' smoke, exhausted past caution.

Then they walked away from it, off the trail, down a game path Gideon found — found, herself, following the water logic of the slope, because drainage runs downhill and so do deer — and they walked it in the dark, in the rain, for three hours, Gideon ahead with the flashlight hooded to a coin of light on the ground and Tomas behind her navigating by the sound of the river growing on their left, and when they finally came down through the trees to the water below the falls, the river lay wide and brown and gentle as a road.

They reassembled the boat by touch. Neither of them suggested sleeping. The fire behind them and above them burned on its sapling, visible, they hoped, until dawn, and sometime in the black hours they heard — faint, swallowed by distance and water — a burst of voices, a whistle, the crackle of something that might have been shots fired at a shirt on a stick.

"They bought it," Gideon whispered.

"For tonight." He pushed the boat out into the current. "Get in."

* * *

They ran the river through the last of the night and the first of the morning, trading the tiller without discussion every two hours because exhaustion had become a third passenger and had to be managed like cargo. Somewhere in the gray middle of that running, Tomas fell asleep at the tiller.

Not long. Ninety seconds, maybe two minutes. Enough for the boat to wander toward the cutbank where a fallen tree reached out over the water like a beckoning arm, its trunk swept clean by floods, its rootball a wall of black spikes waiting just under the surface on the upstream side.

Gideon saw it before he did. Later he would understand that she had seen it the way she saw everything — ahead, in time rather than space, the way pilots see weather coming — and she didn't shout, because shouting wakes a man into panic and panic at the tiller kills everyone. She put her hand over his hand on the tiller grip and turned it, smooth and slow, easing the boat's nose away from the snag in a curve so gentle that his body never even jolted awake. He surfaced from the dream he couldn't remember to find the tree sliding past an oar's length off the beam, the rootball's spikes combing the current beside him, and Gideon's hand still resting on his, steering.

"I had it," he said, though his voice betrayed him.

"You had nothing. You were gone." But she didn't move her hand yet. "I've got the river for a while. You've got the ledger. Sleep."

And here was the thing he discovered, lying in the bilge with the evidence against his heart while she steered them down the flooded morning river: he did sleep. Instantly, completely, the way you can only sleep inside trust. He had not slept like that since his brother was alive. Maybe not even then.

* * *

The debt came due that afternoon.

The channel narrowed into a maze of flooded forest where the river, swollen weeks beyond its banks, ran among the trunks of trees that should have been dry land, and the current in such places is a liar — it looks like slack water and moves like a freight train, and it hides snags that wait just beneath the surface with their patience of stone.

Tomas was awake by then, back at the tiller, reading the water the way he read faces, and he was reading it well, and it didn't matter. The log came out of the drowned forest at them sideways, a whole tree traveling on the flood, invisible until it was ten meters away and unavoidable, and it hit them amidships with a boom he felt in his teeth.

The boat rolled. Not capsized — rolled, shipped a ton of water, and threw them both into the brown flood.

The river took him the way it takes everything, tumbling, and his first coherent thought was not of himself but of the ledger, the bag against his chest, the strap — the strap held, he felt it hold, and then his second thought arrived, which was Gideon, and he came up out of the water spinning, searching the brown chaos for her.

She was downstream of him, tangled. A submerged branch had hooked the strap of the sample can across her body, and the flood was pressing her against it, and her head was above water but barely, tilted back, and he could see — even at that distance, even in that chaos — that she was fighting the way a pilot fights, methodically, working the problem, and the problem was not workable. The river does not negotiate.

He swam. Forty years on this water and he had never once had to swim for his life, and now he swam the flood-current of a river in spate toward a drowning woman, angling across the current the way you angle a ferry, letting the river carry him to her rather than fighting toward her, using everything the river had ever taught him against the river itself. The branch scraped his shoulder. His hand found her wrist, then her collar, then her hair, and he got his arm across her chest from behind and kicked, and the branch tore loose — whether the flood pulled it free or his weight broke the hook he never knew — and suddenly they were both loose in the current, together, spinning downstream.

The boat found them before they found the bank. It had righted itself, half-swamped, riding low but riding, and Gideon, coughing the river out of her lungs, caught the painter as it drifted past and held on with both hands while Tomas kicked them alongside it, and somehow, absurdly, they climbed aboard a boat that had tried to kill them an hour earlier and lay in the bilge together gasping like landed fish.

The sample can was still strapped to her. The ledger was still against his chest. Everything that mattered had stayed above the waterline, and everything that mattered, he realized, looking at her gray face and shaking hands, now included her.

Neither of them said it.

She wrung out her hair and checked the can's seal with fingers that wouldn't quite obey, and he bailed, and the boat went on down the river, and the word that had arrived in him during the swimming — during the moment his hand closed on her wrist and he understood that losing her was not an acceptable outcome of any calculation — that word sat in his chest unspoken, heavy as the evidence, waterproof as the evidence, carried the same way.

He thought she might have heard it anyway. In the way she leaned her shoulder against his when the shivering took them both at dusk. In the way she divided the last of the food and put the larger half in front of him without comment. In the way that, when he woke at dawn on the third day to find her already at the tiller, she had rigged the tarp as a shelter over the stern seat so the rain wouldn't find him, and had done it badly, with piloting hands, and he had lain still under it a long time before getting up, not because he was tired but because he wanted to keep the feeling of being sheltered by her a few minutes longer.

* * *

On the fourth day the river brought them to the missionary station.

It rose out of the flooded forest like a memory — a chapel with its roof caved in, a bell tower leaning, a compound of buildings being slowly digested by vines, all of it abandoned for decades and all of it, crucially, built on the one piece of high ground for fifty kilometers. They grounded the boat on the old landing steps and stood in the ruins dripping, and Gideon's eyes went immediately to the tower, and he watched her look at it the way she'd looked at the reversed compass in the survey camp: seeing not what it was but what it could be.

"There'll be wire in there," she said. "Antenna wire. And if the lightning ground is still connected—"

"Tomas." She touched his arm. "If I can raise anyone — my mentor, the coastal strips, anyone — Hale will hear it too. You know that."

He looked at the tower. At her. At the ledger against his chest, and the can in her arms, and the four hundred kilometers of river still between them and the sea.

"Yes," he said. "But it's the only door out. And doors are for walking through."

She smiled — the first real smile since the branch, since the water — and slung the can onto her shoulder, and started up the hill toward the tower with the rain streaming off her and the storm-light gathering in the west like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Behind them, far downriver, very faint, an engine.
