## 18. Radio Silence Broken

The missionary station announced itself the way old grief does — gradually, then all at once. First a rusted tin roof glinting through the canopy, then a bell tower leaning like a drunk against the sky, and finally the whole settlement: three buildings gone soft with rot, a chapel whose windows had been punched out by decades of storms, and a graveyard where the names on the stones had been eaten down to first initials by the wet.

Gideon brought the boat in slow, Tomas standing at the bow with the pole, reading the water the way she read weather. The channel here was braided and treacherous, split by a sandbar that had grown up around the bones of some long-ago flood. Amara lay in the stern under the tarp with the samples, too weak to sit upright but tracking everything with eyes that refused to stop working even when the rest of her had surrendered.

"Left of the bar," Tomas said. "There's a cut. Old landing."

"You've been here."

"Everyone's been everywhere once." He said it lightly, but his jaw was set in that way she'd learned meant the lightness was load-bearing. "The mission closed when I was a boy. Father Anselmo used to trade downriver past our village. He had a radio you could hear two provinces away."

The cut was there, half-choked with water hyacinth, and they pushed through it into a pool of brown stillness where the station sat like something waiting to be remembered. Mosquitoes rose off the water in a sound like low singing.

Gideon tied off at the rotted pilings and stood a moment looking up at the main building. Her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself now — she checked, the way Amara checked her samples, the way Tomas checked the sky. Three people who had learned not to trust their own instruments without verification.

"All right," she said, mostly to herself. "Let's see what's left."

* * *

What was left was more than she'd dared hope. The chapel had been stripped — pews gone, altar gone, even the bell taken from the tower — but the residence behind it had been simply abandoned rather than emptied. Dust lay over everything like a second skin, and in the back room, behind a desk swollen with damp, she found it: a transmitter. Valve-driven, pre-war maybe, or close enough to make no difference, its casing furred green with mold, its dial glass cracked but whole.

She carried it out into the better light of the main room and set it on a table that still had three legs and one dignity left.

"That's a museum piece," Tomas said from the doorway. He had Amara settled on a cot they'd dragged from another room, propped on folded vestments that smelled of mildew and incense remembered.

"It's a transmitter." Gideon was already opening the casing. "Museums don't have to work."

"And this one does?"

She didn't answer. She was reading the machine the way she read an engine — not as an object but as a system, a chain of intentions, each part asking something of the next. The power supply was the first problem: no grid out here, and the generator that must once have fed it was a skeleton of seized pistons in the shed. But there was a bank of lead-acid cells in the corner behind the desk, and when she tipped the first one, it gave her a sluggish gurgle. Not dead. Sleeping.

"Tomas. There'll be tools somewhere. A mission like this repairs its own roof, its own boat, its own soul. Look for a workshop."

He went. Amara watched Gideon work from the cot, and after a while she said, quietly, "You've done this before. Repaired things in bad places."

"I've done everything in bad places." Gideon scraped corrosion from a terminal with the edge of a knife. "That's most of what flying is. Fixing things far from anywhere, because nobody else is coming."

"And the radio? You know radios."

"My mentor made me learn. Elena Vasquez. She ran the mail route before me, thirty years of it. She said a pilot who can't fix a radio is just a passenger with a license." Gideon smiled despite herself, despite everything. "She also said never trust a frequency someone else gives you until you've listened to it yourself. She's probably the only person in the capital who still monitors the old bands. Everyone else has moved to satellite, encrypted, clean. But Elena keeps the old frequencies alive like stray animals. Feeds them. Won't let them die."

"You trust her?"

"With my life I already did, once. That's how I ended up here." Gideon seated a valve that had survived the decades in its foam padding, tested its filament with a battery and a bulb scavenged from a headlamp. It glowed. Something in her chest unclenched slightly. "Which is either the best argument for trusting her or the worst. I've stopped trying to decide."

Amara was quiet a moment. Then: "When you transmit, they may hear you. Hale's people. The mine has monitoring equipment — I saw the arrays when we passed it. They listen to everything on these bands. It's how they caught the others."

Gideon's hands didn't stop moving. "I know."

"Then you're transmitting anyway."

"I'm transmitting anyway." She looked up. "Amara. There is no way out of this forest that doesn't pass through someone who wants us dead. The river is watched. The trails are watched. The only thing Hale doesn't control is the sky, and the sky is closing for another week of rain. So the question isn't whether to risk being heard. The question is who hears us first."

Amara held her gaze for a long moment, and then nodded slowly, the nod of a scientist confirming a hypothesis she'd hoped was wrong.

* * *

Tomas found the workshop — a lean-to behind the residence, its tool rack still hung with wrenches furred orange with rust, and, miracle of miracles, a hand-crank charger bolted to the bench, built for exactly this kind of dead-end posting where fuel was rumor and sunlight undependable. They took turns cranking it through the afternoon while Gideon rebuilt the transmitter's guts: cleaning contacts, re-soldering joints with a soldering iron heated over a spirit lamp, coaxing the antenna wire up the bell tower's leaning frame with a fishing line and a stone.

By dusk the thing was assembled on the table like a patient after surgery, wires running out the window and up into the grey sky, batteries connected in series, the crank rig rigged to top them off between transmissions.

Tomas stood wiping his hands, watching her check the dial. "You understand what happens next," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Tell me what you think happens."

"He hears it. Or one of his people does. And whatever you arrange with your Elena, he'll be there too, or his men will be, and you'll be walking into a place where every exit belongs to him." Tomas's voice was flat, careful. "You've seen what he does to survey teams. To brothers." His voice caught almost invisibly on the word, and Gideon let it pass, though she marked it, the way she marked a change in engine note — filed away, to be addressed later, when there was time.

"Yes," she said. "And I've also seen what he does when things stay hidden. He wins by default. Every day we don't transmit is a day he wins for free." She looked at him. "Tomas, I spent three years building a life designed so that nothing would ever be asked of me again. Small runs. Small risks. Deliver the medicine, mind my own history, keep flying under the radar — literally. And you know what I learned? That kind of safety is just a slower way of disappearing. I was already gone. I just hadn't told myself yet."

"So this is you telling yourself."

"This is me telling everyone." She turned back to the radio. "Get Amara's ledger. We do this at midnight. If anyone's listening on schedule, they'll be listening to the routine traffic then — fishing cooperatives, the weather nets. We'll be one more voice in the dark."

* * *

Midnight came down on the mission like a held breath. Rain had started again, a fine insistent drizzle that ticked against the tin roof and made the antenna hiss faintly with static. Gideon sat at the table with the key under her fingers, headphones on, the valves glowing their warm orange in the black room. Tomas stood at the window watching the river. Amara sat wrapped in a blanket beside her, the ledger open on her lap, reading frequencies and call signs from a page she'd torn out and burned after memorizing.

Gideon swept the band first, the way Elena had taught her. Fishing boats. A weather report from a coast station, bored and automatic. Somewhere far off, a hymn service drifting in from another mission, another world. Nothing that sounded like ears.

She keyed the mic. "CQ, CQ, this is Kilo-Seven-Wren calling Albatross. Albatross, this is Kilo-Seven-Wren. Do you copy? Over."

Static. The rain. Her own heartbeat, loud in the phones.

She tried again. And again. On the fourth attempt, between the second call and the third, something shifted in the static — a presence, the way you feel a person enter a room behind you.

Then a voice, dry and cracked and unmistakable, forty years of cigarettes and stubbornness packed into four words:

"Wren. That you, girl?"

Gideon's eyes stung. She keyed the mic. "It's me, Elena."

"Took you long enough. Three years, and you call me on a stolen church radio in the middle of a monsoon." A pause, and beneath the dryness, something rawer. "You're alive. Good. I'd begun writing the letter I'd hate to send."

"Elena, listen carefully. I don't have much time and I have to assume this channel is being monitored." Beside her, Amara had gone very still. At the window, Tomas turned. "I need a pickup. Coastal strip — the old one at Punta Salina, the grass strip north of the lighthouse. Five days from now, dawn. Can you be there?"

Another pause, longer. When Elena spoke again, her voice had changed — flatter, more careful, a pilot's voice doing arithmetic. "Punta Salina. Dawn, five days. I can be there. Wren — who else knows you're calling?"

"That's the problem. Maybe everyone."

"Then hear me. If anything feels wrong at that strip — if there are vehicles that shouldn't be, if the wind sock is new, if there's fresh fuel drums where there shouldn't be — you do not land, you do not approach, you turn around and you vanish. Promise me."

"I promise." Gideon closed her eyes. "Elena. There's something else. What I'm carrying — it's going to come out regardless of what happens to me. I want you to know that. Whatever you hear in the next few days, whatever they say about me —"

"Wren." The old voice gentled. "I flew those rivers for thirty years. I buried friends the government called statistics. Whatever you've got in your hands, bring it home or make sure it lands somewhere it can't be buried. That's all I ever asked of any pilot I trained." A crackle, and then, quieter: "Five days. Dawn. Don't be late, girl. I'm too old to wait in the rain."

The channel clicked off, and the static rushed back in like water filling a hole.

* * *

They sat in the dark a long time afterward, none of them moving, the transmitter ticking softly as it cooled.

"He heard it," Tomas said at last. No question. "Or someone did."

"Almost certainly." Gideon pulled off the headphones. "Hale has monitoring on the regional bands — Amara saw the arrays. A transmission from an unregistered station, in code, arranging a coastal rendezvous? He'd have to be dead not to notice."

"Then why do it?"

"Because of what Elena said. Because the evidence has to reach daylight or it dies in the dark with us." Gideon stood, and her body protested — days of river, of portage, of fear, all presenting their invoices at once. "But also because of what she didn't say. Listen. *If the wind sock is new. If there are fuel drums.* She wasn't giving me safety advice. She was telling me how to recognize the trap, because she assumes there will be one. She's known me since I was nineteen. She knew I'd already done the same math."

Amara spoke from the cot, her voice thin but precise. "You're saying you want him to come."

Gideon walked to the window beside Tomas. Down the black slope, the river moved with a sound like slow breathing. Somewhere out in that darkness, men were probably already moving toward Punta Salina, or toward them, or both. Somewhere in the capital, a file was burning and a plan was forming, calm and well-funded.

"I'm saying Hale thinks in terms of capture," she said. "Recover the situation. Contain the evidence. Silence the witnesses. Every move he makes assumes the story stays inside the room he controls." She turned. "But a trap is a stage, Tomas. Everyone comes to the same place at the same time. His men. Elena. Us. And stages have audiences."

Tomas frowned. "What audience?"

"The region runs on radio. Every boat, every village, every pilot — they all listen, because radio is the only news the interior has ever had. Elena taught me that too." Gideon felt it settling in her chest as she said it, the shape of the thing she'd been building since the cave, since the waterfall, since the moment she'd said yes. "So we go to Punta Salina. We walk into whatever he's built for us. And while he's busy springing it — while every gun he has is pointed at the stage — the story goes out on the open band, to every radio in the basin, in plain language, with names. His name first."

Silence. The rain ticked. Amara had sat up, the blanket fallen from her shoulders, her eyes bright with something that was not quite hope and not quite fear.

"They'll jam you," Tomas said. "Cut the power. Shoot the transmitter."

"They'll try. That's why it can't be me alone, and it can't be here. It has to happen at the strip, at the moment of maximum confusion, from equipment Elena brings — she'll understand when she sees us arrive with company. And it has to be worth interrupting a man like Hale mid-sentence." Gideon looked at Amara. "The ledger. The samples. The names of the villages. Everything. One broadcast. Unstoppable, because it's already everywhere."

Amara was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Every channel led back to him. That's why I ran. I thought there was no way to speak that he couldn't silence." Her hands tightened on the ledger. "You're proposing to use his own ambush as the microphone."

"I'm proposing," Gideon said, "that we accept the invitation. He's set a trap. Fine. Traps only work on animals that run."

Tomas stared at her, and then something moved across his face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live in a man who had forgotten the address. "My brother used to say something like that. Before." He didn't finish it. He didn't need to.

They worked through what remained of the night. Amara copied the ledger's critical pages in her cramped, exact hand, splitting them — one set sewn into the lining of Gideon's jacket, one hidden in the boat's bilge planking, the originals and the samples sealed in the waterproof case that would go wherever Gideon went. Redundancy. Amara insisted. *A result isn't real until it replicates.*

At dawn, Gideon climbed the leaning bell tower one last time and looked east, where the storm front was massing over the coast like a wall being built between her and everything she'd loved. Five days. One grass strip. One old woman in a plane she couldn't afford to lose, and one man in a capital who thought he owned the silence.

She put her hand on the cold iron of the bell mount, where a bell had hung for sixty years before someone took it away, and she made it a promise instead of a prayer.

*Come what may at that strip,* she thought, *the silence ends there.*

Far downstream, unseen, a patrol boat's wake drew a long white line across the brown water, heading for the coast — and, though she could not have known it, for a rendezvous five days early.
