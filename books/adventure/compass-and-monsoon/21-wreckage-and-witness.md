## 21. Wreckage and Witness

The inquiry was held in the provincial capital, in a courthouse that smelled of mildew and old varnish, and Tomas Reyes learned within the first hour that the truth, once it finally arrived, moved slower than a river in drought.

He sat on a bench behind Amara Osei while she testified. She had gained back some of the weight the cave had taken from her; her hands no longer shook unless she held them out flat, which she did now, deliberately, when the presiding magistrate asked her to describe the water samples. She held them out as if presenting evidence of her own body, and perhaps she was. The tremor ran through her fingers like a current through wire.

"Chronic exposure," she said. "Mercury and arsenic compounds in concentrations that would be illegal in any city water supply. I tested eleven villages downstream of the operation. Eleven. Children under ten show the highest tissue burden." She paused, and Tomas watched her choose her next words the way a pilot chooses a landing line through weather. "I reported this internally fourteen months ago. My report was never filed. Three weeks later my team was ordered to survey territory we had already surveyed, and then we were not heard from again."

The courtroom made a sound — not a gasp, nothing so dramatic, but a collective shifting of weight, the sound of two hundred people deciding at once to stop pretending they hadn't known something was wrong. Tomas had heard that sound before, years ago, in a different room, when he had stood up in a government office and said the word *irregular* about a supply manifest, and had been told, gently, by men who would later prove anything but gentle, that irregularities were the price of development.

His brother had said a stronger word than *irregular*. His brother had said it too loudly, and Tomas had signed the report that put the word into official channels, believing — God help him, he had believed it even then, even after everything — that channels were where truth went to be acted upon.

The channels had swallowed Emilio whole.

Now he sat in the humid heat of the courtroom and listened to Amara Osei lay it all out: the ledger, the samples, the chain of custody Gideon had insisted on documenting with a bush pilot's obsessive precision — dates, locations, seal numbers, photographs of the mine's tailings ponds glowing their chemical green against the rainforest like wounds that wouldn't close. Hale's name came up on the second day, and stayed up, and would not come down. Men in good suits began to sit very still. A minister resigned before the week was out, which everyone understood to mean the minister had been offered the dignity of leaving before being shown the door.

Tomas testified on the fourth day. He told them about the reversed compass needle, about the second camp overgrown with strangler fig, about the rusted tag with his brother's name that he had carried in his shirt pocket for three weeks before he could bear to look at it again. He told them about the night chase on the flooded river, the fuel they'd lost, the portage through leech-hung jungle with a boat on their shoulders and Hale's men crashing through the understory behind them. He kept his voice level. He had practiced being level for so many years that it cost him almost nothing now, and that realization frightened him more than anything else he said that day.

When they asked him why he had gone upstream with a stranger whose plane was broken, he looked at Gideon, sitting in the gallery with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and he lied by telling the truth.

"Because she asked," he said. "And because I had already lost one person to that river's silence. I found I couldn't stand to lose another."

* * *

The mine closed on a Tuesday.

There was no ceremony. A government notice appeared on the operation's gate, printed on cheap paper that the first hard rain would dissolve, and soldiers who had spent months guarding the perimeter now guarded its emptiness instead. The floodlights went dark. The dredges sat in their dead green ponds like the skeletons of enormous animals that had drunk the river dry.

Tomas took his boat past the site a month later, running supplies upriver to the villages — the same crates of medicine Gideon had flown in for three years, only now the labels were different, the contents heavier on chelation agents and antibiotics, lighter on the palliative things you send when you've given up on cure. The villages were not healed. Mercury does not leave a body the way a flood leaves a field. But the children's lesions were being treated now, properly, by doctors who came on the government's money rather than despite it, and the elders had begun saying the mine's name aloud, testing it in their mouths like a fruit they'd been forbidden for too long.

At San Rafael, the worst-hit village, an old woman named Doña Inés stopped him on the bank as he unloaded. She pressed both her hands around his forearm, her grip surprisingly strong.

"You're the one who carried it out," she said. Not a question. The river towns had their own inquiry, faster and more thorough than the capital's, and everyone knew the story by now: the trader, the pilot, the doctor from the cave behind the waterfall.

"The pilot carried it," Tomas said. "I just kept the boat off the rocks."

Doña Inés smiled without much humor. "That is what the river says too. But the river also says you knew this water was poisoned for six years and kept trading on it."

The words went into him clean, no deflection possible. He had known. Not the full shape of it — not the ledgers, not the disappeared teams — but he had known the fish were wrong, known the skin diseases moving downstream year by year, known and traded anyway because a man has to eat and the government's silence had seemed like weather, like monsoon, like something no single man could argue with.

"Yes," he said. "I knew some of it."

She nodded slowly, and her grip on his arm tightened once, not in anger but in something older than anger. "Then you will keep coming back," she said. "That is how a man pays. Not with words. With returns."

He kept coming back.

* * *

Gideon's plane never flew again.

It sat on the apron at Puerto Lobo for five weeks while the inquiry ground through its procedures, roped off with official tape that faded from red to pink in the sun, and then one morning a transport barge came and men with clipboards winched it aboard — the Cessna that had carried medicine and mail across three years of rainforest sky, that had thrown a rod at the exact moment the monsoon arrived, that had grounded her precisely long enough to change everything. Confiscated as material evidence, the paperwork said. Subject to disposition following conclusion of proceedings.

Everyone in Puerto Lobo knew what *disposition* meant. The plane would sit in a government yard until the seals on its logbooks yellowed, and then it would be sold at auction to someone with connections, or stripped for parts, or simply left to the birds and the rain. Bureaucracies do not return things. They file them.

Tomas found Gideon on the dock the morning the barge pulled away. She stood with her hands in her pockets watching the aircraft shrink against the horizon, and her face wore the expression of a woman watching a funeral from across a street she isn't allowed to cross.

"They didn't even let me take the compass out of it," she said when he drew alongside. Her voice was light, which is how he had learned to hear the heaviness in her. "My father's compass. It's in the panel mount. Probably in an evidence bag somewhere with a number on it."

"You could petition," he said. "Amara knows people now. People who owe her."

"I know." She was quiet a moment. The river slapped the pilings beneath them. "You know what the strange thing is? For three years that plane was the only thing I owned outright. Everything else — the house in the old life, the commission, the record — I'd already burned or been stripped of. But the plane was mine. Every dent in the struts was mine. And when they took it, I waited to feel gutted." She turned to look at him, and there was something in her face he had only seen once before, on the night in the storm shelter when she had finally told him what she had flown, and for whom, and what village had paid for her obedience. "And what I felt instead was lighter. Like losing ballast."

"Ballast keeps you steady," Tomas said.

"Ballast keeps you down." She almost smiled. "I spent three years measuring my life in miles. Fuel range, leg distances, hours between strips. Miles are a way of counting that never asks what you're carrying. Turns out I was carrying a lot of things I called necessities that were just weights."

He thought about his own boat, moored below them, riding high and empty in the afternoon heat. He thought about Emilio's tag in his pocket, which he still carried, though the inquiry had its copy now, photographed and catalogued. He thought about the report he had signed, seven years ago, that had converted his brother's anger into a case number, and how every mile he had run since — every free river mile he'd bragged about to himself — had really been a long slow flight from that signature.

"I sold my cargo concession last week," he heard himself say. It surprised him; he hadn't decided to say it. "The government freight contract. The one that pays well and requires me to pretend the manifests are honest."

Gideon raised an eyebrow. "Tomas Reyes, giving up revenue. Should I check you for fever?"

"I'm keeping the boat." He looked downriver, toward the villages, toward the long brown reach of water that had taken his brother and given him back a name on a tag and, impossibly, a purpose. "But only for the runs that matter. Medicine. Mail, if anyone still writes letters. The stuff nobody pays well for." He shrugged, feeling the old guilt shift inside him like cargo settling — not gone, never gone, but redistributed, balanced. "Doña Inés says I pay with returns. I've decided to believe her."

* * *

They walked to the wreck of the airstrip that evening.

Puerto Lobo's strip had flooded during the early monsoon and never fully recovered; the grass had come up through the laterite in green whorls, and the windsock hung from its pole in ribbons, faded to the color of weak tea. Gideon walked the length of it with her hands behind her back, reading the surface the way she must once have read it a hundred times — checking drainage, judging the soft patches, her feet finding the old centerline out of pure habit.

"It'll fly again someday," Tomas said. "Strips heal. Someone with a blade and a roller, one dry season—"

"No." She said it without bitterness, which was how he knew she meant it. "Someone else's plane will fly from here. That's fine. That was always going to be fine." She stopped at the midpoint, where the floodwater had scoured a channel across the runway, and stood looking north, toward the interior, toward the drowned country where a survey camp still sat with its tents stacked and its compass needle pointing the wrong way, waiting now for investigators instead of vultures. "You want to know the last thing I felt flying? Before the rod let go?"

"Tell me."

"Relief." She laughed softly, shaking her head. "The engine started hammering and I was fighting to hold altitude with the strip flooding under me, and underneath the fear there was this — quiet. This tiny voice saying *you don't have to go back up*. I didn't understand it then. I thought it was shock." She turned to face him fully, and the evening light came low across the ruined strip and caught the gray starting at her temples, the lines the last months had carved deep around her mouth. "It wasn't shock. It was the truth arriving ahead of schedule. The miles were never the point. I built a whole life out of staying in motion because motion doesn't ask you who you are. Then the motion stopped, and everything I actually chose got exposed, like a riverbed in drought."

"And what did you find?" he asked. "In the riverbed."

She considered him for a long moment. Around them the frogs were tuning up for the night chorus, and the first bats stitched the darkening sky above the hangar roof, and somewhere down in the town a radio played the news — the inquiry, the resignations, the word *criminal referral* spoken aloud on the open band for anyone with ears to hear it.

"A pilot," she said finally. "Not the flying kind. The kind who chooses a heading and holds it. Even through weather. Especially through weather." She looked away, out at the water standing silver in the strip's old ruts, and her voice dropped to something almost private. "I grieved for the plane, Tomás. Don't mistake me. I'll grieve for it a long time — it was my father's wings I learned on, in a way. But grief turns out to be lighter than avoidance. Nobody tells you that. You spend years thinking the weight is the loss, and it's not. The weight is the pretending the loss hasn't happened yet."

He reached out and took her hand. She let him. Her palm was calloused in the places a yoke builds calluses, and those calluses would soften now, or become something else, and she would carry that too.

"Doña Inés told me a man pays with returns," he said.

"She's right."

"So we return." He nodded downriver, where the boat waited and the crates waited and the villages waited, and beyond them the coast, where Amara Osei was preparing to testify again next month, and the month after, however long the machinery of accountability took to grind its slow way toward whatever justice it could stomach. "Season after season. As long as it takes. As long as we're asked."

Gideon squeezed his hand once, hard, and let go, and turned to walk back toward the town lights with the ruined strip at her back and the river ahead of her, and Tomás followed, understanding that neither of them was leaving anything behind anymore — that everything they had lost had been folded into who they were becoming, the way a river folds its dead into its delta, not erased, only changed into ground that something new can grow from.

Behind them, the windsock stirred in the evening breeze, pointing downriver.

They walked the way it pointed.
