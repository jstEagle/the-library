## 22. Miles, Then Years

The river had a smell in the dry season that Gideon had never learned from the air — woodsmoke and mud and the green rot of the banks baking in the sun, all of it layered over the mineral tang of water that was slowly remembering how to be clean. From three thousand feet the watershed had been a brown ribbon stitched through green. Down here at water level it was a country, with moods and neighbors and grudges, and she was still learning its grammar eleven months after she'd last held a yoke.

"Port side," Tomas said without looking up from the engine.

She saw it: a fisherman's net strung across a back channel, half-submerged, its floats bleached bone-white. She eased the throttle down and swung wide, and the *Mariposa* — his old boat, now their boat, though neither of them said the word *their* out loud very often — slid past into the main current with only the softest complaint from her hull.

"You're getting good at this," Tomas said.

"I'm getting adequate at this."

"Adequate is what keeps you alive on a river." He wiped his hands on a rag that had been gray since before Gideon met him and stood, rolling the stiffness out of his shoulders. "Flying, you could be perfect and die. Out here, adequate and patient, you live forever."

"That's not wisdom, that's just pessimism with a nice rhythm."

"It's both." He grinned, and went forward to check the lashings on the cargo.

The cargo was medicine. That was the part that still caught in her chest sometimes, when she stacked the crates in the morning light: crates like the ones she'd flown for three years, same stenciled labels, same weight in her hands — but these were going where they were needed most, to villages whose names appeared on no government manifest because the government had spent years pretending those villages weren't being poisoned. Amara's ledger had changed that. The inquiry had changed more. The mine was closed, its fences cut and sold for scrap by the same communities it had nearly killed, and the water below the old site ran turbid and ordinary instead of chemically dead. Ordinary was a miracle. Gideon had learned to count ordinary among the miracles.

They were two days out of Puerto Lobo, running the supply loop they'd built through the dry season: medicine upriver, dried fish and rubber and stories downriver. The loop took nine days if the river cooperated and twelve if it didn't, and Gideon had stopped measuring her life in miles somewhere around the time she'd watched her plane — her *Cessna*, her wings, the thing she'd traded for a truth — get hauled away under tarp as evidence and never come back. She measured in seasons now. Dry season runs. The rains. The long weeks when the channels shifted and the maps everyone else trusted became suggestions. It was a smaller unit of measurement than miles, and somehow it held more.

* * *

The village of San Rafael de Agua came to meet them before they'd finished tying off.

This was new. Eleven months ago, when she and Tomas had first stopped here — the village of the quiet, she still thought of it that way, the place where children had shown her lesions she recognized from her own medicine crates and no adult would say the word *mine* above a whisper — the dock had been empty except for one old man who watched them with eyes like a man watching weather he couldn't control. Now there were twenty people on the bank, and the children didn't hang back, and a woman named Rosa Ferreira who had appointed herself the village's voice shook Gideon's hand with both of hers and said, "The doctor sent word you were coming."

"The doctor" was Amara Osei, who had refused every offer to leave the interior and instead established a clinic that moved by boat, three villages a month, treating heavy-metal poisoning with chelation therapy and stubbornness in roughly equal measure. She and Gideon spoke by radio twice a week, short transmissions, business first and then the other thing underneath the business, which was friendship conducted in the language of two women who had each survived something by refusing to be silent about it.

Unloading took two hours. Gideon worked the crates while Tomas negotiated — he was better at negotiation, having spent thirty years learning exactly how much a man could ask for before the asking became theft — and by mid-afternoon the clinic shelf in the schoolhouse was stocked, Rosa had extracted a promise that they'd carry her nephew's carvings down to Puerto Lobo market, and the children had adopted Gideon for the length of an afternoon in the way children do, trailing her, asking questions, demanding she look at things.

It was one of them, a boy of maybe ten named Elio, who asked the question.

He'd been watching her hands while she restocked the shelf. Children noticed hands; they hadn't yet learned to watch faces instead. "You hold the bottles like my uncle holds tools," Elio said. "Careful, like they're expensive. But you're not a nurse."

"I'm not."

"My cousin says you used to fly. That you had a plane and flew medicine everywhere, way up high, faster than the river." He said it with the flat delivery of a child testing whether an adult will lie. "Rosa says you gave it up. Nobody gives up flying. My cousin says once you've flown you can't stop wanting it."

Gideon set the last bottle in its crate slot and looked at him. Behind him, through the schoolhouse window, she could see the river, brown and patient, going where it went.

"Your cousin's right," she said. "I still want it. Every day. Some mornings I wake up and my hands already know the preflight checks, and I lie there and run through them — fuel, controls, instruments — before I'm even properly awake."

"Then why did you give it up?"

And here was the moment, she thought, where the old Gideon would have reached for the measured distance. Three years of it, before all this — the practiced vagueness, the smile that redirected, the art of being liked without being known. She had been so good at it. She had built a whole life out of almost-answers, and it had taken a reversed compass needle on a map table and a woman starving in a cave behind a waterfall to show her what the almost-answers cost.

So she knelt down so they were eye to eye, which is what you did with a ten-year-old, and told the truth.

"Because I found out that the medicine I was carrying was only half the job," she said. "I flew supplies to your village for three years. I treated the symptoms with my little crates and I felt good about it, and I never once asked why the sickness kept coming back. Someone upstream was poisoning the water, and the government knew, and I didn't know because I never made myself look. Flying let me be everywhere and see nothing. You're up high, and everything's beautiful, and nothing's your fault." She tapped the shelf of bottles. "I gave up the plane because keeping it meant staying the person who didn't look. And I'd rather be someone who looks."

Elio considered this with the gravity of a judge. "Did it hurt? Giving it up?"

"Every day," Gideon said again. "But some things are supposed to hurt. That's how you know they mattered."

He nodded slowly, accepting this the way children accept hard truths — completely, and then immediately distracted by a beetle on the windowsill — and ran outside, and Gideon stayed kneeling a moment longer by the shelf, in the dust-smelling dimness of the schoolhouse, feeling the truth sit in her chest where the measured distance used to be. It fit differently. It fit better.

* * *

They camped that night on a sandbar downstream, the way Tomas always insisted, because "a dock is where other people's decisions find you." He cooked rice and dried fish over a driftwood fire with the economy of a man who had fed himself on rivers for three decades, and Gideon cleaned the day's grit off her hands and watched the stars come out over the water.

Her hands still knew the preflight checks. She doubted they ever wouldn't. Some nights, in the hammock, she'd catch herself running them — fuel, controls, instruments, free and clear — like a prayer in a language she no longer spoke but hadn't forgotten. And some nights the wanting rose up in her like weather, huge and impersonal, grief for the Cessna and the altitude and the clean cold silence at eight thousand feet. She had learned not to fight the weather. You read it. You waited it out. You flew around the worst of it if you could, and if you couldn't, you went through and came out the other side wet and alive.

Tomas sat down beside her with two cups of coffee boiled thick enough to stand a spoon in, and handed her one.

"Elio asked me today if we were married," he said.

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him boats take longer to commit to than marriages, and I wasn't ready." He sipped. "He seemed satisfied."

"He would be. He asked me if giving up flying hurt, like it was a medical question."

"And what did you prescribe?"

"Honesty. Side effects include everything."

Tomas laughed, low, and for a while they drank their coffee and listened to the river work. Somewhere across the water a night bird called, three notes, repeated, patient as a lighthouse. The fire cracked. This was the shape the days had settled into, and Gideon had stopped waiting for it to feel temporary. For three years after she fled the capital — after the orders she'd flown, the village that paid for them, the record she'd run from — she had treated every arrangement of her life as provisional, a campsite, a place to wait out whatever came next. Even Puerto Lobo, even the medicine runs, even the careful friendships: all of it provisional, all of it held at the arm's length that altitude makes easy. It had taken losing the plane to learn that the distance was never in the miles. It was in the choices. She had chosen distance for three years, daily, the way you choose a heading, and she could choose otherwise now, daily, the same way.

"Tomas," she said. "Do you ever think about your brother?"

He didn't flinch anymore. That had taken months too — the name had been a wound between them for a long while, the brother he'd reported for dissent, the brother who'd vanished with a survey team and a reversed compass, the guilt that had steered him upriver with a stranger before he'd ever admitted why. "Every day," he said. "Less than yesterday. In a few years maybe less than that. That's how it works, I think. Not gone. Just lighter."

"He'd have liked this," Gideon said, gesturing at the river, the fire, the boat riding at anchor in the dark. "The supply run. What it turned into."

"He'd have hated the coffee." Tomas looked into his cup. "But yes. He'd have liked that the villages know our names now. That they send word ahead. That when Rosa's nephew needed chelation transport in March, we were the ones they called." He paused. "Miguel wanted to be useful. That was his crime, really. He saw what the surveys were covering up and he thought being useful meant telling the truth. They taught him it meant disappearing." He set the cup down. "So we do it for him. Quietly. Without asking anyone's permission."

"Without asking anyone's permission," Gideon agreed. It had become something between a toast and a creed.

The fire burned down. Tomas banked it, checked the anchor line with a tug born of thirty years' habit, and turned in. Gideon stayed up a while longer, as she often did, sitting with the dark and the water sound and the compass.

The compass lived in a wooden box under the helm seat now — the boat's compass, brass-cased, older than either of them. But inside the box, wrapped in oilcloth, she kept another one: the survey compass from the second camp, the one with the needle that pointed backward, the signature of every team the state had disappeared. Amara had given it to her after the inquiry, when the evidence was entered and the hearings began and the thing had no further power to hurt anyone who didn't want it to. *Keep it,* Amara had said. *Not as a wound. As an instrument. It reminds you what happens when the people who should look away are rewarded for it.*

Gideon took it out now, as she did most nights, and held it in the starlight. The needle swung, settled, swung again — pointing south, faithfully, toward the mine that no longer operated, toward the capital that had fallen into scandal and inquiry, toward everything they had rowed against. A broken instrument. Except that it wasn't broken; it was honest. It pointed where it pointed, and it had cost everything to make it do so, and that was worth more than accuracy.

She wrapped it away and looked upriver, into the black.

Tomorrow they'd run the Narrows at first light — the channel that shifted every season, where the charts were fiction and Tomas's memory was the only reliable instrument aboard. After San Rafael they'd push on to the upper villages, the ones the recovery effort still hadn't fully reached, where a teacher had radioed that the clinic roof had gone in the storms and there were children with fevers nobody could name. There was always a next thing. That was the discovery of this year of her life: that purpose wasn't a destination you flew to, once, gloriously. It was a loop you ran, season after season, crates loaded and unloaded, names learned and remembered, the work done again because it needed doing again. Smaller than flying. Slower. Closer to the ground, close enough to see faces, close enough to be seen.

She thought about the boy's question. *Nobody gives up flying.* He was right; she hadn't, not really. She had given up the cockpit, the machine, the altitude — but flying was never the point. The point had always been the carrying. Getting the thing that saves a life to the place where the life is. She was still doing that. She would always be doing that. She'd simply traded the fastest route for the truest one.

Gideon banked the fire, took one last look at the stars doing their slow wheeling over the water, and turned in beside Tomas in the dark of the boat, her hands finally, for tonight, empty of checks to run.

In the morning the river would be ahead of them, brown and patient and going where it went, and the compass in its box would point its stubborn backward truth, and they would go with both — upriver, into the country the maps were only beginning to learn how to name.
