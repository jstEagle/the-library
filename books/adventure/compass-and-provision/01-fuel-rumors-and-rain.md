## 1. Fuel, Rumors, and Rain

The rain came sideways off the lake at dawn, the way it always did in October, and Hazel Marchetti stood in the open door of the hangar with a tin cup of coffee going cold in her hand, watching it hammer the water into a gray, boiling sheet.

Kettle Creek woke slowly on mornings like this. Down the strip, the lamp in Junie's bunkhouse flickered on — she'd been up since five, same as Hazel, though neither of them had said so. The town itself was eleven buildings and a gravel runway, strung along the shoreline like beads on a wire that somebody had forgotten to reel in. The trading post. The church that doubled as the school. Doc Alderman's clinic with its sagging porch and its hand-painted sign that had lost its E years ago, so it read CLINIC, which Hazel had always thought was honest enough.

Her plane sat in the hangar behind her, nose to the door, waiting. A 1936 Fairchild, high-winged and patient, her father's old bird. The paint was the color of weak tea and the fabric on the left aileron had been patched so many times it looked like a quilt, but her engine turned over clean and her airframe was true, and in the interior country that was worth more than pretty.

"Fuel's in," Junie said behind her, materializing the way she did, wiping her hands on a rag that had been black since before Hazel met her. Junie was sixty if she was a day and built like a fence post, with forearms that could turn a propeller by hand and a face that gave away nothing she didn't want it to. "Pumps are slow in this cold. Took an extra twenty minutes."

"You could've let me do it."

"You were doing the manifest." Junie nodded at the clipboard in Hazel's other hand. "Which you were doing wrong, because you always put the penicillin on the wrong line, and Doc Alderman counts."

Hazel looked down. The penicillin was on the wrong line. She crossed it out and rewrote it, and Junie watched her do it with the particular satisfaction of a woman who had been right so many times she no longer needed to say so.

The manifest was short. Forty pounds of medical freight — the penicillin, sulfa, a crate of plasma in wax-sealed cans, a bag of mail that the floatplane from the coast had brought in yesterday. One passenger, if you could call her that: Mrs. Ellery, the midwife, going up to the Kowalski place on the Tazlina fork where a baby was due two weeks early and coming sideways, from the sound of it. Mrs. Ellery was seventy-three years old and had delivered half the population of the interior, including, in a manner of speaking, Hazel herself — she'd been the one who'd put Hazel's mother back together after, and the one who'd sat with Hazel's father the night before his last flight, though Hazel had never asked what they'd talked about. Some things you didn't ask.

"Heard from the Kowalskis?" Hazel asked.

"Radioed last night. Baby's turned, mostly. Mrs. Ellery says mostly is a word that means hurry."

"Mostly means hurry," Hazel agreed.

* * *

They loaded in the rain. Hazel had done it ten thousand times — the freight slung up through the big cargo door, strapped to the floor rings, weight and balance worked out in her head the way her father had taught her before she was tall enough to see over the panel. Mrs. Ellery climbed aboard with the help of Theo, who was sixteen and all elbows and had been hanging around the strip since spring, sweeping the hangar floor and hauling fuel drums for the privilege of being near the airplanes. He was the son of the Kettle Creek schoolteacher, and he wanted to fly the way some boys wanted to breathe, and Hazel pretended not to notice because noticing would mean admitting she had something to teach, and teaching meant staying, and staying meant the debt had another year on it.

"Weight's fine," Theo said, climbing out of the cabin. "I did the arithmetic twice."

"Twice?"

"Three times." He pushed his wet hair off his forehead. "The third time was just to be sure the first two agreed."

Hazel almost smiled. "Get out of my rain."

He grinned and stepped back, and she swung up into the pilot's seat, and the Fairchild came alive around her with the smell of avgas and old leather and the particular cold-oil smell of an engine that had been loved by the same two people for twenty years.

"Radio check," she said.

"Loud and clear," Theo said from the hangar door, holding the handheld. "Wind's down to eight, gusting twelve, out of the southeast. Ceiling's — " he squinted at the gray — "ceiling's an opinion."

"Ceiling's always an opinion." She ran up the engine, watched the gauges settle into their familiar agreement, and rolled out onto the strip. The rain streaked the windscreen and the wiper — a hand-cranked thing, bolted on by Junie, operated by a cable that ran to a wooden knob by her left knee — squeaked its way across the glass. She gave Theo a two-fingered salute off her cap, and he saluted back with the radio, and then Kettle Creek fell away beneath her and there was only the lake and the gray and the long valley running north into the country where nobody lived who didn't have a reason.

* * *

It was an hour and a half to the Tazlina fork, most of it up a river valley that the clouds had claimed for their own. Hazel flew it the way she'd flown it a hundred times, half by compass and half by memory — the bend where the river doubled back, the rock slide that looked like a spilled sack of flour, the dead spruce standing alone on the ridge that her father had called the Sentinel, though it had no name on any map. She flew low when she had to and high when she could, and she kept her hands light on the wheel and her mind on the numbers, because the numbers were the one part of this life that never lied to her.

The debt was eleven hundred and forty dollars. She knew it to the dollar because she made herself know it every morning, the way a man with a bad tooth makes himself touch it. It wasn't the money that was the weight of it. It was the name on the paper. *Marchetti Air Transport, indebted to the Kettle Creek Mercantile Company and sundry partners, per the agreement of 1938.* Her father had signed it two years before he died, borrowing against the airplane and the hangar and the strip itself to keep his last expedition in the air, and when the expedition went into the Sawtooths and came back without him, the debt had come back to the only Marchetti left. The partners who held the paper — men named Voss and Renard and a silent third whose name nobody would say out loud — were patient the way wolves are patient. They didn't foreclose. They didn't have to. They just let the interest walk beside her, year after year, and let her carry the load one med run at a time, and every year the load was a little heavier and the strip was a little more theirs than hers.

Eleven hundred and forty dollars. Fourteen months, at her best, if nothing broke. Something was always breaking.

Mrs. Ellery dozed against the window, her carpetbag on her knees, and Hazel let the plane fly itself for a minute and watched the Sawtooths come up out of the murk ahead — white shoulders under the cloud deck, sharp as their name. Her father was up there somewhere. Not him, exactly. The airplane. A Fairchild just like this one, or near enough, gone into the white on a day in November six years ago with her father and two other men aboard, and they'd never found enough of it to bury properly. The mountain kept what the mountain took. Hazel had made her peace with that, or she'd built something that looked like peace from a distance, which amounted to the same thing most days.

She flew on. The rain turned to sleet at four thousand feet and back to rain as she dropped into the fork, and the Kowalskis' meadow came up out of the gray exactly where it lived in her memory, and she put the Fairchild down on the grass in a long, flat, gentle arrival that Mrs. Ellery slept through entirely.

* * *

The delivery took twenty minutes. The baby, as it turned out, was in no mood to be hurried, and Mrs. Ellery elected to stay — "I'll ride back with the mail plane Thursday, girl, don't you fuss" — so Hazel flew out light and alone, and the plane climbed like it was glad of it.

She stopped at the trading post at Miller's Landing on the way home, because it was on the way and because the mail there was three days fresher than Kettle Creek's, and because the Landing was where the interior's rumor mill did its grinding. The trading post smelled of woodsmoke and canvas and the paraffin they sold by the case, and it was full of the usual congregation: trappers drying out, a pair of surveyors from the government party working the upper river, old Marta behind the counter who could tell you the price of beaver pelts in Anchorage and the price of a man's reputation in the same breath.

Hazel bought coffee, a bolt of canvas, and a pound of the licorice Theo pretended he didn't like, and she was counting out her coins when the surveyors started talking.

"—saw it twice through the glass," the younger one was saying. He had a government-issue parka and the excited voice of a man who'd found something he didn't yet know the price of. "Up in the Sawtooths, past the high col. Aluminum skin, both engines sheared off. It's been there years — the tail's half under the ice. But it's there, and it's big enough to be a transport."

"Nobody's missing a transport," the older one said, not looking up from his map.

"The airlines are missing one. Twenty years back there was that freight outfit that went in — the one with the pilot who — " He stopped, because Marta had set a tin cup down on the counter with slightly more force than the cup required, and because half the room had gone quiet in the way rooms go quiet when a name is about to be said or carefully not said.

Hazel kept counting coins. Her hands were steady. She'd had practice.

"Old wreck in the Sawtooths," Marta said, to nobody in particular, in the tone of a woman closing a door. "Country's full of them."

But the younger surveyor wasn't finished. "There's a man in Anchorage asking after it," he said. "Came through here Tuesday, chartered the mail boat up the lake. Big man, silver hair, expensive boots. Asking about the high col and the icefield and whether anybody knew the old Marchetti routes." He said it casually, the way you'd say a place name, and Hazel heard it the way you hear your own name called across a crowded room.

She finished counting. She picked up her canvas and her licorice and her coffee, and she said her thank-yous to Marta, and she walked out into the rain with her heart going hard and even, like a well-tuned engine.

Silver hair. Expensive boots. The old Marchetti routes.

She stood on the Landing's one dock and looked north, where the Sawtooths had already swallowed themselves back into the cloud, and she thought: *not yet. Please. Not this year.*

But the mail boat had already come and gone, and the man in the expensive boots was somewhere up the lake ahead of the weather, and Hazel Marchetti had been in the interior long enough to know that when a rumor like that took wing, it was already too late to shoot it down.

She walked back to her plane through the rain, and she did not run, and she did not look back.
