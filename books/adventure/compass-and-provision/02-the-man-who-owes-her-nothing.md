## 2. The Man Who Owes Her Nothing

The rain came in off the lake at dusk, the way it always did in October, and Hazel Marchetti was under her father's old Cessna with a trouble light and a wrench when she heard the truck.

She knew every engine in Kettle Creek by its cough. Doc Ellery's Subaru rattled like a coffee can full of bolts. Ruthie Amos's pickup idled smooth but died on hills. This one was new to her — a heavy diesel, city-plate, rolling up the gravel spur from the highway with its headlights cutting cones through the wet. It stopped at the edge of the apron and sat there, engine running, wipers going, like it wasn't sure it wanted to be here.

Hazel slid out from under the wing and stood up slow, wiping her hands on a rag that had stopped being white sometime around the Carter administration. The rain smelled like iron and coming winter. Behind her, the hangar door stood open on Junie's workbench, the radio murmuring a hockey game out of Vancouver that nobody was listening to.

The truck's door opened. A man got out who did not belong to the rain.

He wore a canvas coat that had never seen honest weather — the kind of coat you bought to look like you worked outdoors, not the kind that worked. Under it, a wool sweater, pressed trousers tucked into boots too clean for the mud of the apron. He was tall, silver at the temples, with a face built for boardrooms that had learned to smile at hunting camps. He stood there a moment, letting the rain hit him, as if he were taking a photograph of himself standing in it.

Hazel knew him before he said a word. You didn't fly cargo into the interior for eleven years without knowing Silas Crane's name the way you knew a storm front — from a long way off, and with your hand already checking the tie-downs.

"Miss Marchetti." His voice carried across the apron without effort. "Silas Crane."

"I know who you are." She didn't move from the shadow of the wing. "You're a long way from your kind of runway."

"My kind of runway." He smiled like she'd paid him a compliment. "I've been told you're the best bush pilot between here and the Yukon. I've also been told you don't much care for me."

"Whoever told you that was being generous."

He laughed — a real laugh, warm, easy, the kind that made strangers trust him. Hazel had watched men like that all her life. Her father had been one of them, right up until the day his luck ran out on the far side of the Sawtooths.

Crane came closer, unhurried, rain beading on his shoulders. Up close he was older than his photographs, or maybe just better lit in them. There was a scar through one eyebrow she hadn't expected, thin and old.

"I'll come to the point," he said. "I have a supply aircraft down in the high country. Sawtooth Range, east side of the divide. Went in three weeks ago. Both souls walked away, but the airframe's intact, and what's in it matters to me."

"Matters how?"

"Enough that I'm prepared to pay triple your standard charter rate. Triple, plus a completion bonus that would cover your fuel bill for a year." He said it plainly, no flourish, watching her the way a man watches water rise against a bridge piling. "I need it recovered before the snows seal the pass. That gives us perhaps two weeks."

Hazel folded the rag and put it in her back pocket, mostly so her hands would have something to do besides shake.

"The Sawtooths," she said. "East of the divide."

"That's right."

"You know what's east of the divide."

"I know what happened in 1961, if that's what you're asking." Something moved behind his eyes — not quite sympathy, something more patient than that. "I also know your father was the finest mountain pilot this province ever produced, and that whatever took him wasn't the mountains."

For a moment the rain was very loud.

"You've done your homework," Hazel said.

"I always do." He glanced past her at the Cessna, at the patchwork of fabric and paint, at the engine cowl she'd been nursing along since spring. "That's a 180, isn't it? His? I'd heard you kept it flying."

"It keeps itself flying. I just stay out of its way." She stepped out into the rain now, because she wanted him to see her face clearly when she said the rest. "The answer's no, Mr. Crane. No to the job, no to the money, no to whatever version of my father you're about to offer me next. Find yourself another pilot."

"I don't want another pilot. Another pilot would take the wrong things out of that wreck." He let that sit, then tipped his head toward the hangar. "May I make my case to your crew? I understand decisions like this are shared."

"They're not."

"No?" He smiled again, and this time there was steel under it. "Then I'll leave my card, and my number, and I'll say again: triple rate, two weeks, before the snows. Offers like mine don't keep, Miss Marchetti. Neither does the weather."

He went back to his truck. She watched it turn around on the apron, spray throwing off the tires, and grind away down the spur until the taillights drowned in the dark and the rain closed over the road like water over a stone.

* * *

Junie Okafor was waiting inside the hangar with her arms crossed and a socket wrench in one hand like she'd been considering using it.

She was fifty-one, built like a fence post, and she'd been keeping airplanes alive in Kettle Creek longer than Hazel had been alive anywhere. She'd come north twenty years ago with a failed marriage and a toolbox and never found a reason to leave. Theo called her the only adult either of them knew. Junie usually responded by handing him something heavy.

"Well," Junie said.

"Well nothing. The answer's no."

"I heard the answer. I'm asking about the math." She set the wrench down on the bench and picked up the grease pencil instead, writing on the dented tin sheet they used for accounts. "Fuel bill's four hundred past due. Landing gear overhaul before freeze-up, if we want the 180 legal in January. Theo's mother needs the dental work done in Whitehorse before the roads close, and Theo won't ask you for it, which means I have to. And the propane tank behind your house is empty enough that you've been heating with the wood stove and pretending you like it."

"Junie—"

"Triple rate." She wrote the number. It looked obscene sitting there among the small desperate arithmetic of their lives. "One trip. One trip pays all of it and leaves margin."

"One trip east of the divide." Hazel pulled the rag back out and wrung it though it wasn't wet anymore. "You know what I swore."

"I know what you swore. I was standing there when you swore it, at the funeral, in the rain, same as this rain." Junie put the pencil down and looked at her straight, the way she did when an engine was making a sound she didn't like. "Hazel. Your daddy flew east of the divide because Crane's outfit sent him in with bad charts and a heavier load than the manifest said. That's the story you've told yourself for eleven years, and maybe it's even true. But Crane didn't kill him. The mountains didn't even do it alone. Debt did it. Debt and pride, and a man who couldn't say no to one more run because the creditors were circling the airstrip."

"That's not fair."

"No," Junie agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about it is fair. But you're doing exactly what he did — turning down good money out of pride while the bills eat the airplane out from under you. And when the airplane goes, everything goes. The med runs. The mail. All of it. Then you'll be flying Crane's jobs anyway, except you'll be doing it scared and broke instead of fed and angry."

Hazel turned away and looked at the Cessna. In the trouble light her father's old plane hung in the half-dark like something sleeping, the compass rose he'd painted on the cowl faded to a ghost of blue. He'd bought that plane the year she was born. He'd named it *Provision*, because he said a pilot's whole job was making sure everybody ate.

"He offered triple," she said quietly. "Nobody offers triple unless they're afraid of what happens if they're late."

"Or unless what's in that wreck is worth ten times triple." Junie shrugged. "Either way, it's not our problem what he wants out of there. Our problem is the gear overhaul and Theo's mother's teeth and whether we eat in February."

"And if it is our problem? If whatever he's after east of the divide has his name on it somewhere?"

"Then we'll know it when we see it, and we'll decide then." Junie came and stood beside her, shoulder to shoulder, both of them looking at the sleeping plane. "You're the pilot, Hazel. Nobody flies that girl anywhere you don't point her. But the crew eats first. That's the rule you made, and it's a good rule, and I'm telling you as crew that we vote yes."

Hazel was quiet a long time. Outside, the rain kept working at the roof, patient as interest accruing.

"If we go," she said finally, "we go on my terms. Base camp only. I fly you and the gear to the glacier strip, and I sit on that strip until you walk back out. Nobody crosses the pass. Not me, not you, not Crane, not in my airplane, not in this weather window. And if the front comes in early, we're gone, deal or no deal, wreck or no wreck. I don't care what he's paying."

"Base camp only," Junie repeated. "And the terms go in the contract, in ink, before his wheels ever touch our gravel."

"In ink."

Junie nodded once, satisfied, and reached for the grease pencil. But Hazel caught her wrist.

"Junie. Why didn't you wait for me? You accepted before I even came inside. Theo said you told Crane's man on the phone this morning — before he ever drove up. Before I'd said a word."

Junie didn't flinch. She'd survived worse conversations than this one, in worse places, with worse people.

"Because I've watched you carry this place on your back for three years," she said, "and I've watched you get smaller every time the bills come due, and I decided that once — just once — somebody was going to choose survival for you before pride could get its boots on." She gently freed her wrist and picked up the pencil. "You can fire me in the spring. Right now I've got a landing gear to rebuild."

She went back to the bench. Hazel stood alone in the hangar with the rain and the radio and the sleeping plane, and felt something shift in her chest that she didn't have a name for yet — the first hairline crack in a wall she'd been building since she was nineteen years old, standing at a graveside, swearing she'd never fly east of the divide.

From the apron, footsteps. Theo came in out of the wet, shaking rain off his cap, cheeks bright with cold and something else.

"Hazel," he said. "Truck's been sitting at the end of the spur for ten minutes. Engine off. Somebody's still in it."

Hazel looked out through the open hangar doors into the dark. Far down the gravel, barely visible, two points of reflected light where headlights should have been dark — a man sitting in a parked truck in the rain, watching the hangar, waiting to see what the lights inside would tell him.

Watching to see if she'd say yes.

Hazel reached over and killed the trouble light.
