## 3. Sworn Off

The rain hadn't stopped in three days, and neither had Junie.

Hazel found her under the wing of the *Kestrel*, the old de Havilland Otter that had been her father's before it was hers, elbow-deep in the number two engine with a flashlight clamped between her teeth and a string of curses trailing out around the metal like smoke.

"He's not going to say yes," Hazel said.

Junie spat the flashlight into her palm. "He hasn't said no either."

"He will."

"Then let him say it to my face." Junie wiped her hands on a rag that had been white during the McKinley administration and stood, working the kink out of her back. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a fence post, and she had been turning wrenches on this strip since before Hazel could reach the fuel caps. "Triple rates, Hazel. You know what winter costs. Propane's up again. Theo's boots have holes in them he swears are ventilation. And the *Kestrel* needs a top overhaul I've been putting off since June because we can't afford the cylinders."

"I know what things cost, Junie."

"Do you?" It wasn't cruel, the way she said it. That was almost worse. "Because I do the books, honey. I know exactly what your father left you besides the airplane and the compass. I know to the penny."

Hazel looked at the rain drumming on the wing above them, running in silver ropes off the trailing edge, pooling in the mud that passed for Kettle Creek's main street this time of year. Somewhere down the strip, Theo was arguing with the mail pilot about a crate of oranges, his voice carrying the way nineteen-year-old voices carry when they think they're being quiet.

"Crane killed my father," she said. "Not with his hands. With his schedule. With his 'the pass'll hold another day, Marchetti, don't be a coward.' He pushed and pushed and my father pushed back one time too many, and the mountain—" Her voice did something she didn't authorize. She shut it down. "The mountain settled the argument."

Junie was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked against the aluminum.

"Silas Crane didn't kill Emilio Marchetti," she said finally. "The Sawtooths did what the Sawtooths do. But I'll tell you what Silas Crane is doing right now, tonight, in the lodge with the good whiskey: he's counting on the fact that you hate him more than you love this crew. Because a woman who flies angry makes mistakes, and a woman who won't fly at all—" she shrugged— "well. Then he wins too, doesn't he? Either way he gets to watch you lose."

"That's a hell of a thing to say to your boss."

"I'm not saying it to your boss. I'm saying it to the girl I taught to gap plugs." Junie picked up her flashlight and turned back toward the engine. "Sleep on it. The offer keeps till morning. Weather doesn't, but Crane does — men like him always do."

* * *

That night Hazel dreamed about the sound.

She always dreamed about the sound. Not the crash itself — she'd never heard the crash, hadn't been there, had been fourteen years old and safe in Kettle Creek, which was its own kind of wound — but the radio recording. The Mayday call from the last flight of Cessna 206, November-Charlie-Marchetti, cut short by static. Her father's voice, calm as a man reading a grocery list: *Sawtooth Base, this is NCM, we're in the turn at the notch, getting some — * and then nothing, eleven seconds of carrier wave hiss that the coroner's office played twice in a courtroom while her mother held Hazel's hand so hard the knuckles went white.

Eleven seconds. She'd listened to it so many times afterward — smuggled the cassette from the evidence box, played it on the floor of her closet with the door shut — that she could hear it with her eyes open now, whenever the wind came off the range at the right angle.

In the dream, the eleven seconds stretched. Her father kept talking. In the dream he said, *Turn back, mija. Turn back,* and she never knew whether he was talking to himself or to her, and she woke with the word *mija* sitting on her chest like a stone.

She got up. Made coffee in the dark. Sat at the kitchen table with the oilcloth worn through in the shape of her elbows and looked at the compass on the shelf above the stove.

Brass case, glass gone cloudy, needle painted red once by her father's own hand because he said factory needles were the color of other people's decisions. *North is whatever you decide it is, Hazel. Everything else is just weather.* He'd bought it in Anchorage the year she was born, or so the story went, and it had hung in every cockpit he'd ever flown, and after the crash it had come home to her in a manila envelope along with his logbooks and a bill from the estate lawyer that read like an obituary for their finances.

The debts weren't dramatic. That was the insult of them. No gambling, no scandal — just a charter outfit run on margin through three bad winters, loans signed with a handshake and then, when the handshake wasn't enough, with paper, and paper held by men whose names her father had trusted. Partners, the papers said. Hazel had learned to say the word *partners* the way other people said *cancer*.

And now Silas Crane wanted to hire her to fly back into the teeth of the place that had taken him.

* * *

He came to the hangar at first light, when the rain had thinned to a gray mist and the whole valley smelled like wet spruce and avgas. He came alone, which she noted, and he came hatless, which she noted harder — a man who wouldn't shield himself from the weather was a man who wanted her to see his face while he talked.

"Hazel." He said her name like they were old friends. They were, in the way a dog and a porcupine are old friends. "You look like your mother."

"My mother looked tired?"

"She looked like someone who knew her own mind." He smiled. He had a good smile; she'd give him that. It was the smile of a man who'd sold ice to a river. "Have you thought about my offer?"

"Theo tells me you've already talked terms with my crew."

Something flickered behind his eyes — surprise, quickly filed away. "Boys get ahead of themselves."

"My crew isn't boys. And you going around me to them tells me everything I need to know about how you'd treat me above ten thousand feet." She crossed her arms. Behind her, the *Kestrel*'s cowling was off and Junie was pointedly not listening, which meant she was listening to every word. "So here's my answer, and it's the only one you're getting. I'll fly you in. Once. To base camp on the glacier, below the pass. We land, we unload, I stay with the airplane, and when your people are done with whatever it is you're actually doing up there — because it isn't salvage, Silas, nobody hires triple rates to pick up drill cores — I fly out. Same day. If the window closes, you wait it out on the ice with the rest of your gear until it opens. But I do not cross the pass. Not for triple rates. Not for ten times rates. Not if you put a gun in my hand and a map in front of my face. The notch stays closed to me. Sworn off. Understood?"

Crane studied her for a long moment. The mist beaded on his shoulders. Somewhere up-valley, invisible behind the cloud deck, the Sawtooths were waiting the way they always waited — patient, indifferent, keeping her father.

"You know what's up there," he said quietly. "Don't you. You know what the wreck holds."

"I know what you told Theo. A supply plane, mechanical failure, cargo recovery. I also know you lie the way other men breathe."

"And your father's logs — "

"Are none of your business." The words came out faster than she meant them to, sharper, and she saw him register the flinch for what it was. Damn him. Damn him for knowing where the soft places were. "Those are my terms, Crane. Take them or walk back down the strip and find yourself a pilot who doesn't know your history."

For a moment she thought he would walk. She found, to her own disgust, that part of her hoped he would — hoped hard enough that she could stop having this conversation and stop standing close enough to smell the good whiskey still on him from the night before.

Instead he nodded slowly, like a man conceding a chess piece he intended to win back later.

"To base camp," he said. "Below the pass. Your airplane stays yours." He extended his hand. She looked at it. She took it anyway, because a contract was a contract and his grip was warm and dry and she hated herself for noticing. "Three days' prep. My geologist says the high pressure ridge holds through Thursday."

"Then Thursday we fly. And Silas — " she held his hand one beat longer than friendly — "if I find out you've lied to me about what's in that wreck, all bets are off, terms included. I'll leave you on the glacier with a radio and my regards."

"Spoken like your father's daughter."

"Don't," she said. "Don't you ever say his name like you knew him."

But he was already turning, already walking away down the strip through the thinning mist, unhurried, a man with nowhere to be because everywhere was already arranged. And Hazel stood in the wet gravel watching him go, her hand still remembering the shape of his, and felt the old guilt rise in her throat dressed in its Sunday clothes, wearing its most reasonable face: *This is just caution. This is just prudence. Flying only to base camp has nothing to do with the notch, nothing to do with the turn at eleven thousand feet where the radio went to static, nothing to do with the fact that you have never once in nine years flown past the place your father died.*

Behind her, Junie let the wrench in her hand clank against the cowling, deliberate as a church bell.

"Well," she said. "I guess we're going."

Hazel turned. "We?"

"Triple rates, honey. Theo's already packing." Junie's face was unreadable, which from Junie was a full paragraph. "Besides. Somebody's got to keep an eye on you up there. You swear off a pass, you start thinking about it every waking hour. Trust me. I've watched you do it with smaller mountains."

She bent back into the engine. Hazel stood a moment longer in the mist, looking north up the valley to where the clouds sat low and gray over the shoulders of the range, hiding everything above eight thousand feet.

Thursday, she thought. Three days.

Above the pass, in the ice, something of her father's was waiting — and Silas Crane knew exactly what it was.
