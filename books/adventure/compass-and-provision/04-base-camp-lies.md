## 4. Base Camp Lies

The glacier camp sat in a bowl of blue ice like a bruise on the mountain's knuckle, and by the time Hazel stepped off the Otter's ski gear onto the packed snow, she had already counted four things wrong with it.

One: the tents. Crane had told her salvage operation, three weeks of hauling scrap aluminum down to the strip at Talkeetna Junction in his chartered Cat. But salvage crews lived big — wall tents, wood stoves, crates of canned peaches and cigarettes to keep morale from freezing solid. These were mountain tents, light nylon, pitched low against the wind. Camps built for people who expected to leave fast.

Two: the cache. A salvage job needed room for parts, and there was none. What Hazel saw instead was a single aluminum trunk, padlocked, sitting dead center in the largest tent like an altar.

Three: the men. There were six of them besides Theo, and not one of them looked like a rigging hand. They looked like they'd been hired out of a card room in Anchorage — heavy through the shoulders, quiet in the wrong way, watching her the way wolves watch a sled dog that has wandered too close to the kill.

Four: Silas Crane himself, standing at the edge of the camp with his hands jammed in the pockets of a parka worth more than her father's whole airplane, smiling at her like they were old friends instead of old wounds.

"Marchetti." He said it warm, easy, a man greeting a neighbor across a fence. "You made good time."

"Tailwind." She pulled off her goggles and let him see her face, let him see that she wasn't going to be charmed. "Where's the wreck?"

"Two miles up-glacier, in the cirque below the shoulder." He gestured with his chin, casual. "Cessna 207, went down in September. Cargo contract out of Fairbanks. My client wants the airframe assessed before the insurance adjusters get up here in spring."

"Your client," Hazel said. "You never said who that was."

"You never asked hard enough to make me answer." The smile didn't move but something behind it did, some small recalibration, the way a man shifts his weight before he decides whether to push or pull. "Come have coffee. You look half-frozen."

"I'm fine." She wasn't, quite. The flight up from base had been two hours of white glare and crosswind, and her hands still ached around the yoke. But she stayed where she stood, boots planted on the ice, because she'd learned a long time ago that the first thing you gave away in a negotiation was your comfort. "I'll take the coffee after I've seen the manifest."

Something flickered across Crane's face — quick, gone, like a fish turning under ice. "Manifest's in my tent."

"Then let's look at it in your tent."

* * *

The tent smelled of kerosene and pipe tobacco and, underneath it, paper. Old paper, the dry vanilla smell of documents kept too long in the dark. Hazel noticed it before she noticed anything else, and the noticing put a cold wire down her spine that had nothing to do with the glacier.

Crane's trunk sat open on the camp table while he dug for the manifest, and Hazel did not mean to look, but looking was what she was built for — twenty years of reading weather and freight lists and the faces of men who owed her money. Inside the trunk: oilskin wrapping. A document case, leather, brass corners, the kind lawyers used. And beneath it, stacked flat and bound with twine, logbooks.

She knew those logbooks the way you know your own handwriting.

Blue covers, corners worn round, the topmost one stamped in faded block letters: *MARCHETTI AIR SERVICE — FLIGHT LOG, 1958–1961.*

Her father's logs. Every hour he ever flew, every load he ever carried into the interior, every strip he ever touched down on in thirty years of bush flying — including, she understood with a lurch that felt like freefall, the strips nobody else knew about. The hidden ones. The cache strips a man charts when he's carrying things he doesn't want found, or hiding things he can't afford to lose.

"Hazel." Crane's voice came soft. "Manifest."

She took the clipboard without letting her eyes leave the trunk a second longer than she had to. Then she read.

And there it was, item by item, the lie laid out in Crane's own careful handwriting. No airframe assessment. No cargo manifest for a Cessna 207. Instead: *retrieval — personal effects, priority.* *Retrieval — documents, sealed, priority.* *Do not open in field.* And at the bottom, in a different ink, added later: *client authorization — Hollis & Vane.*

Hollis and Vane. Her father's partners. The men whose signatures had been all over the debts that swallowed Marchetti Air Service after the crash, the men who had testified so sorrowfully at the inquiry about Emilio Marchetti's shortcuts, his overloaded planes, his reckless heart. The men who now, if the rumor mill of Kettle Creek ran true, bankrolled half of Silas Crane's operations.

Salvage crews don't carry document cases. Insurance assessors don't fly in mountain tents with hired muscle. And no client on earth sends a man two hundred miles to retrieve *personal effects* unless what he really means is evidence.

"You're not salvaging a plane," Hazel said. She set the clipboard down on the table, gently, the way you set down something you might need to throw. "You're salvaging paper."

Crane closed the trunk. He did it slowly, deliberately, letting her watch him do it. "Paper's salvage too. Sometimes the most valuable thing on a mountain."

"Whose paper?"

"Client's paper."

"Your client," Hazel said, "put my father in the ground twice. Once in the fire, once in the inquiry. So you'll understand why I'm asking carefully: what is Hollis and Vane's paper doing in a trunk at the bottom of a glacier, and why does it smell like it came out of a safe?"

For a long moment Crane just looked at her, and she watched him decide — she could see the decision happening behind his eyes, options being weighed and discarded like cards. Finally he sighed, and the sigh was almost honest. "You always were your father's daughter."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Here's what is: the wreck up-glacier is a Piper that went down in '61. Same storm your father flew into. Different mountain, same weather. It's been sitting under the ice pack for fourteen years, and this winter — this exact winter — the glacier spat it out." He tapped the trunk lid once, lightly, like knocking on a door. "What's inside that wreck belongs to my clients. That's all you need to fly me to the base of the cirque and back."

"The logs say otherwise." She nodded at the trunk. "Those are my father's flight logs, Silas. Stolen property. I could have the troopers on you before the snow melts."

"You could." He poured coffee from a dented percolator, unhurried, and handed her a tin cup she didn't take. "But then you'd have to explain to them why you're up here at all, working for the man you swore off fourteen years ago. And Junie would have to explain why she signed the crew agreement without telling you. And Theo—" he smiled again, smaller this time, "—well. Theo's young. Young men say things."

The cold wire in her spine turned to a blade.

* * *

She found Theo at the cook tent an hour later, chopping kindling with the furious concentration of a boy trying very hard not to think. He was nineteen, all elbows and earnestness, and he'd come out to Kettle Creek in the spring with a duffel bag and a story about wanting to learn engines, and Junie had taken him in the way Junie took in everything broken and interesting. He looked up when Hazel's shadow fell across the chopping block, and his face did exactly what Crane had promised it would do.

"Hazel. Hey. Didn't know you were—"

"Theo." She sat down on the woodpile, unhurried, and took the hatchet gently out of his hands. "Tell me about the logs."

"What logs?"

"The blue ones. In the trunk."

His ears went red. Nineteen years old and transparent as a window. "I didn't— Mr. Crane said—"

"Mr. Crane said what?"

He sat down across from her, elbows on knees, and stared at the snow between his boots. When he spoke it came out in a rush, like pulling a splinter. "Back in September. Before we even signed on. He had me catalog boxes at his office in Anchorage — night work, cash, no questions. And there were these logbooks, and he had me copy out pages. Strip names, coordinates, dates. He said it was research for an insurance claim." Theo's jaw worked. "One of the books had a name written inside the cover. I only saw it once. It said *E. Marchetti.*"

The wind moved through the pass above them with a sound like a long exhale, and Hazel sat very still on the woodpile and let the truth settle over her the way snow settles — silently, completely, changing the shape of everything underneath.

Her father's logs. In Crane's hands since September. Copied out page by page by a boy who hadn't known what he was holding. Which meant Crane hadn't stumbled onto the wreck this fall. He'd been hunting it for months, using her father's own coordinates, following Emilio Marchetti's secret strips across the range like a bloodhound running a fourteen-year-old scent. And when the hunt led him somewhere he couldn't go alone — somewhere that needed a pilot who knew the Sawtooths, a plane rated for the pass, a crew —

—he'd come to Kettle Creek. To her. Because she was the best pilot in the valley, and because she was desperate, and because he knew that desperation was a leash.

"Did he tell you why?" she asked quietly. "Why the coordinates mattered?"

"He said the pilot who owned those books hid cargo all over the interior. Said some of it was never recovered." Theo finally looked up, and there was misery in his face, young and unguarded. "Hazel, I didn't know. I swear on my mother I didn't connect it until you landed today and I heard your name. Junie doesn't know either. I checked the crew agreement last night and the rates, the triple rates — they're coming from Hollis and Vane's account. I saw the deposit slip. I thought maybe it was just business, but—"

"It's not just business, Theo."

"No," he said. "I figured that out around midnight."

Hazel looked up-glacier, toward the cirque where the ice had given back its dead. Somewhere up there, under fourteen years of blue compression, sat a Piper full of paper that could clear her father's name or hang what was left of it — and Silas Crane meant to carry it out himself, and the men paying him meant to make sure no one else ever read it.

She thought about Junie back at Kettle Creek, wrenching on the Cessna in the cold, trusting Hazel to bring everyone home. She thought about the debt ledger pinned above her own desk, every line a stone around her father's memory. She thought about the compass in her jacket pocket, its needle trembling toward true north no matter how the world lied.

"Theo," she said. "How many of those six men would you trust in a whiteout?"

He blinked. "None of them."

"Good." She stood, and handed him back the hatchet, and her voice came out level and cold and certain, the voice her father had used on the radio in bad weather, the voice that meant the plan had already started. "Then starting tonight, you keep your ears open and your mouth shut, and you stay within ten feet of me. Because whatever Crane thinks he's retrieving off that mountain—"

Above the cirque, far to the north, the sky had begun to build — high gray cloud stacking itself against the peaks like a man stacking sandbags before a flood.

"—he's running out of time to do it. And so are we."
