## 5. The Pass Closes Early

The storm announced itself the way storms always did in the Sawtooths — not with thunder but with a smell. Hazel caught it at dawn while she stood at the edge of base camp with a tin cup of coffee going cold in her hands: ozone and cold iron on the wind out of the north, the scent of a sky loading itself with weight.

By midmorning the high cirrus had thickened into a gray lid. By noon the peaks to the west had vanished entirely, swallowed whole, and the wind had begun to sing through the guy-wires of the tents in a thin, rising note that made her think of her father tuning a radio between stations.

Crane's men worked faster after that. She watched them haul crates up the glacier tongue toward the wreck site, watched Crane himself stand at the icefall with binoculars and that same patient hunger he wore like a coat, and she felt the clock ticking behind her ribs.

*Two days,* she thought. *Maybe three.*

The barometer on the Cessna's panel agreed with her gut. It had fallen a tenth of an inch since breakfast and was still falling, steady as a man walking downhill.

* * *

The prospector came down off the ridge at four in the afternoon, half-carried between two of Crane's deckhands, and everything changed.

His name was Aldous Wren — Theo dug it out of him between fits of shivering — and he'd been trapping the high country above the pass since before Hazel was born. He'd come to camp three days ago to trade salt and cartridges and stayed because his chest had begun to sound like wet gravel shifting in a sack. Now it sounded worse. Much worse.

Hazel knelt beside him in the medical tent she'd insisted on rigging her first night there, the one Crane had sneered at as a waste of cargo space. She pressed her stethoscope against the old man's back and heard the fluid moving with each breath, a soft ugly sloshing, lungs drowning from the inside.

"Pneumonia," she said. "Maybe worse. He needs antibiotics, real heat, and a doctor. In that order."

"So get him to one," Crane said from the tent flap. He didn't come inside. He never came inside; the sick offended him, or perhaps just reminded him of something.

"With what?" Hazel stood and wiped her hands on her thighs. "You want me to fly him out through that?" She jerked her chin at the tent wall, where the wind had risen to a steady moan and the light had gone the color of dishwater.

"I want you to fly him out *before* that," Crane said. "Tonight. The front won't break for two days yet. There's a window."

"There is no window." She kept her voice flat. "There's a gap in the cloud over the pass right now, and gaps close. You know what happens to a plane that gets caught closing a gap in the Sawtooths?"

Something moved behind Crane's eyes — not sympathy, calculation. "I know what happened to your father," he said quietly.

The words landed like a slap. Behind her, Junie went still over the camp stove, and Theo stopped pretending to organize the supply crates.

"That's not what this is," Hazel said.

"No? A dying man, bad weather, a pass you've sworn off." Crane shrugged, unhurried, a man laying cards face-up one at a time. "Seems exactly what this is. But you're the pilot. Your call." He turned and left, and the tent flap snapped shut behind him like a period at the end of a sentence.

* * *

Night came down early and hard. The temperature dropped twenty degrees in an hour, and Wren's fever climbed to meet it.

Hazel sat by his cot and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding all evening. The nearest doctor was at Kettle Creek, ninety miles south. Over the pass. The pass she had sworn off in front of God and Junie and her own dead father's memory, sworn off with the particular ferocity of a woman who had buried enough people in those mountains and refused to add herself to the list.

Wren coughed, long and wet, and when it passed he lay gray-faced against the rolled jacket someone had put under his head and looked at her with eyes gone bright with fever.

"You're Marchetti's girl," he rasped.

"I am."

"Flew with him once. 'Sixty-one. Bad year for weather too." He smiled, cracked lips splitting. "He went through the pass in weather like this to bring a trapper's wife down off the mountain. Baby coming. Flew through a hole no bigger than a barn door, he told me after. Said the trick wasn't being brave." He coughed again. "Said the trick was knowing your airplane better than the mountain does."

Hazel said nothing. Her hands were shaking, and she folded them in her lap so no one would see.

Outside, the wind rose another notch.

* * *

She found Junie at the fire, feeding it slats of crate wood, her broad shoulders hunched against the cold, grease still under her nails from an afternoon spent babying the Cessna's temperamental carburetor.

"Say it," Junie said without looking up. "Whatever it is, say it, because you've got the face you get."

"The face I get."

"Like you're doing math you hate."

Hazel sat down across from her. The fire popped and spat. Above the trees, the clouds had closed ranks; there were no stars tonight, none at all, and the darkness over the cirque was total and moving.

"Wren dies if he stays here," she said. "Not maybe. Not probably. He dies tomorrow, day after at best. And if we wait for the front to clear, we're grounded a week anyway, and Crane's window closes with it."

Junie fed another slat to the fire. "And if you fly tonight?"

"If I fly tonight, I cross the pass in weather my father died in."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the dark, one of Crane's men laughed at something, the sound carrying wrong and thin across the camp.

"You swore off," Junie said at last. It wasn't an accusation. It was an invitation to take it back.

"I know what I swore."

"And I know why." Junie finally looked up, firelight catching the deep lines around her eyes. "Hazel. I've known you eleven years. I rebuilt that engine with you piece by piece. So I'm going to tell you what I see, and you can throw me in the fire after." She leaned forward. "That vow isn't keeping anybody alive. It's a gravestone you visit. And Wren's not dead yet, and neither are you, and that plane is the best thing either of us ever built."

"He could die in the air," Hazel said. "Bumping him around in that turbulence—"

"He *will* die on the ground." Junie stood, brushed off her knees, and looked down at her with the terrible gentleness of a woman who had buried a husband and kept working the next morning. "You taught me that. First aid course, four years ago. You said: the hardest thing about medicine up here is knowing which risks are the kind ones." She stuck out her hand. "So. Which risk is the kind one?"

Hazel looked at the offered hand. Then past it, at the black wall of the storm, where the pass lay hidden somewhere behind all that moving dark.

*Knowing your airplane better than the mountain does.*

She took Junie's hand and stood.

* * *

They loaded Wren at midnight, wrapped in every blanket in camp, lashed into the Cessna's rear bench with Theo holding his head steady. Crane appeared out of the dark to watch, hands in his pockets, saying nothing, which was somehow worse than anything he could have said. At the last moment he stepped close to the cockpit door.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your father would have flown it."

"That's the first true thing you've said to me," Hazel answered, "and I hate that you know it."

She cranked the engine. It caught on the third try, coughed, steadied. The propeller washed snow-dust sideways across the strip they'd hacked out of the glacier moraine, and the world beyond the spinner was a howling gray nothing.

"Hazel," Theo said from behind her, small-voiced. "There's nothing out there."

"There's a valley out there," she said. "It hasn't moved. Neither have I." She ran the checklist with fingers that had gone perfectly steady somewhere in the last hour, the way they always did once the decision was made — fear lived in the choosing, never in the doing. "Junie. Carb heat as we climb. If she ices, we turn south and run the low country to Millhaven, and Wren takes a bumpy road the rest of the way. Everybody breathe."

She pushed the throttle forward.

The Cessna lurched, rolled thirty feet, lifted. The ground fell away into darkness, and the wind took hold of the wings and shook them like a dog shaking a rat, and Hazel flew into the storm with her teeth set and her father's voice running under the engine noise like a bass line: *trim her nose-down, girl, let the mountain come to you.*

The pass was fifteen miles north. The cloud deck started at eight thousand feet and gave no sign of ending. She climbed anyway, instruments flickering, watching the ice creep along the wing leading edges like frost forming on a windowpane, and at nine thousand five hundred feet she found it — the gap, narrow as a barn door, glowing faintly gray-green with moonlight leaking through from above.

Or maybe it wasn't moonlight. Maybe it was just the eye of the storm, the calm rotten center where the pressure balanced itself for an hour before slamming shut.

"Gap's open," she said aloud, to nobody, to everybody. "Going through."

She banked into the gray.

* * *

Kettle Creek's lights appeared forty minutes later like a handful of coins spilled on black velvet, and Hazel brought the Cessna down through gusts that shoved the tail sideways twice on final, both times catching it, both times hearing Junie exhale behind her. The airstrip truck was already rolling out to meet them, Doc Ellery's headlights swinging wild, because Theo had keyed the radio from base camp an hour before they left and old Marge at the trading post had done what Marge always did: called everyone who mattered.

They carried Wren into the clinic on a door pried off its hinges. Doc Ellery took one listen to his chest and said, "Christ, Marchetti, another hour—" and then stopped, because Hazel was already walking back out into the wind.

She stood at the edge of the strip with the storm roaring down off the mountains behind her and her whole body trembling now that it was allowed to, hands, jaw, the backs of her knees, everything shaking at once. She had crossed the pass. She had crossed the pass in weather that killed her father, and she was standing on the ground, alive, and somewhere above the white chaos the gap was already sealing shut behind her like water closing over a stone.

Junie came and stood beside her. Neither of them spoke for a long time.

Then Hazel looked up at the churning dark over the Sawtooths and understood two things at once, both of them arriving with the clean certainty of instrument readings.

The first was that she was capable of more than she had ever let herself believe — and that Crane knew it now too, and would use it.

The second was that the storm was going to seal the pass for days. Days in which Crane's crew would be alone up there with the wreck, the ledgers, and whatever else the mountain had kept for eleven years.

Days in which no one — not her, not Junie, not anyone — could stop him from taking all of it.

"We have to get back," she said. "Before the pass closes for good."
