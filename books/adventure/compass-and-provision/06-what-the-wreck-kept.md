## 6. What the Wreck Kept

The storm broke at dawn the way storms do up there — all at once, like a hand letting go. Hazel stood outside her tent with a tin cup of coffee going cold in her fingers and watched the Sawtooths come back into the world, peak by peak, white and indifferent. The air had that scoured, ringing quality it got after three days of wind, and somewhere above the pass, a glacier creaked like an old floor.

Crane was already awake. Of course he was. He stood at the edge of camp with binoculars raised toward the saddle where the wreck sat wedged in a couloir two thousand feet up, a splinter of aluminum visible only when the light hit right. He'd been looking at it for three days through weather, and now that the sky had cleared he looked at it the way other men looked at women or money or God.

"Today," he said, without turning around.

"Your climbers say the ice is rotten after a storm." Hazel blew on her coffee. "Avalanche country."

"My climbers say a lot of things." He lowered the binoculars. His face was raw from wind, fifty years old and carved down to essentials. "You could come along. You know these mountains better than any man I've got."

"I swore off the pass."

"You swore off a lot of things, Marchetti. Your father swore off plenty too, right up until—" He stopped himself, which was its own kind of cruelty, letting the sentence die where everyone could see what it had been reaching for.

Hazel drank her coffee. It tasted like burnt pine and altitude. "Take your rope team. I'll keep the engines warm."

He smiled then, thin and satisfied, and went to wake his men.

* * *

They were gone four hours. Hazel spent them the way she spent most hours — working. She pulled the cowling off the Otter's number two cylinder and found what she'd expected to find, a fuel line chafing against a bracket, and fixed it with a length of safety wire and the kind of patience that was really just anger wearing work clothes. Theo hovered nearby handing her tools without being asked, which he'd gotten good at, and which she pretended not to notice.

"The prospector's stable," Theo said. "Fever's down. Junie's got him drinking willow bark tea like it's whiskey."

"Good."

"Hazel." He crouched beside her, keeping his voice under the wind. "I checked Crane's sled last night. Before you say anything — I wasn't snooping. Well. I was snooping. But there's a strongbox lashed under the tarp, and it's got stenciling on it. *Marchetti Air Transport.*"

Her hands went still on the wrench.

"It's his," Theo said quickly. "It came up with him from Kettle Creek. I watched him load it."

She sat back on her heels. Her father's company stencil, faded blue paint on steel, riding up a mountain strapped to Silas Crane's sled like luggage. Like something he'd owned all along.

"Anything else?"

"He's got papers in his coat. Lots of them. And he told his lead climber if they found anything paper up there, it goes to him first, before anyone else sees it. Not salvage tags. Not inventory." Theo swallowed. "Paper first."

Hazel looked up at the couloir, at the tiny glint of metal in the sun. Sixteen years ago a plane had gone into that mountainside carrying her father's name and three other men's futures, and everyone in the valley had agreed on what it meant, because agreeing was easier than asking. She'd built her whole life on top of that agreement the way you build a cabin on permafrost — knowing, some nights, that the ground underneath was moving.

"Junie," she called.

Junie emerged from the prospector's tent wiping her hands on a rag, gray-streaked braid coming loose, eyes sharp as awls. She took one look at Hazel's face and sent Theo to check the tie-downs, which didn't need checking.

"You knew," Hazel said.

"I suspected." Junie didn't flinch. "Man doesn't pay triple rates for scrap aluminum. Scrap's worth nothing since the war surplus flooded the market. Whatever's in that wreck, it's worth more than the wreck." She paused. "And whatever it's worth, he means to keep it from us."

"He hired us."

"He hired an airplane, Hazel. There's a difference, and we're standing on it."

* * *

Crane's team came down at midafternoon, roped single file across the glacier, hauling a sled heavier than it had gone up. Hazel watched from the Otter's wing, arms crossed, and counted the way Crane moved — quick, lit, a man walking on the far side of some border he'd crossed alone. The climbers looked tired and uneasy. One of them kept glancing back up at the couloir like he'd left something there he shouldn't have.

Crane came straight to her. He was breathing hard from the descent and didn't try to hide it, and there was snow in his beard, and for a moment he looked less like a creditor and more like a man who'd seen a ghost and liked it.

"We found her," he said. "Tail section intact. Fuselage crushed, but the aft compartment held. Cold and dry all these years." He unslung a canvas map case from his shoulder and held it against his chest like a rifle. "You'll want to see this, Hazel. Believe me. You especially."

"I don't want anything from you."

"No." He almost laughed. "You want it anyway, though. That's the hell of it." He glanced around — at the tents, at Theo pretending to coil rope thirty feet away, at Junie in the Otter's doorway — then jerked his head toward the lake shore, and because not knowing was worse than anything he could say, Hazel followed.

They stopped where the wind off the ice couldn't carry their voices. Crane opened the map case with fingers that shook, and she told herself it was the cold.

Inside were ledgers. Three of them, bound in oilcloth, water-swollen at the corners but whole. And beneath them, wrapped separately in waxed paper, a bundle of correspondence tied with the kind of string her father had used on everything — twine, always twine, he'd said knots were honest.

Hazel reached out and stopped herself before she touched them. "Where—"

"Aft compartment. In a document case bolted under the floor panels. Your father bolted it there himself, I'd wager. Man planned for fire, for water, for customs inspections." Crane's voice dropped. "He didn't plan for me."

"What are they?"

"They're the books, Hazel. The real ones. Marchetti Air Transport, 1931 through '34. Every shipment, every payment, every name." He watched her face while he said it, watching the way you'd watch a fuse burn. "And every entry showing that the gold shipments your father supposedly skimmed were diverted on the ground, in Kettle Creek, by the men who signed for them. Aldous Vann. Peter Rourke. Your father's partners. The men who stood at his funeral and called him a thief."

The wind moved across the glacier with a sound like a long exhale. Hazel heard her own heartbeat in her ears, slow and enormous.

"That's not possible," she said, and heard how flat her voice came out, how much it sounded like a door closing rather than opening.

"Isn't it? Vann owns half the freight contracts in the valley now. Rourke sits on the aviation board that sets your landing fees and signs off on your medical permits. They've been collecting interest on your father's ruin for sixteen years — and so have I, because when I went looking for backing for this expedition, guess whose money was behind mine?" He tapped the map case. "Vann financed this flight, Hazel. He thinks I'm up here recovering salvage to bury. Instead I'm holding the shovel that digs up everything."

"Why tell me?"

"Because I need you." He said it plainly, without shame, which was somehow worse. "A story like this needs a witness people believe. A daughter with clean hands and a good reputation and no stake anyone can prove. If I come down off this mountain alone waving these books, I'm a crank, or worse, a blackmailer, and Vann's lawyers will eat me alive. But if *you* stand in front of the valley and say *these are my father's ledgers and here is my father's handwriting*—" he spread his hands, "—then it isn't a story anymore. It's history."

"And what do I get?"

"Half." He let the word sit. "The ledgers are worth more than money. Vann will pay anything to make them disappear quietly — or the newspapers will pay anything to print them loudly. Either way, you come home rich, your father comes home innocent, and the men who broke him spend their last years doing what he did. Dying owing everybody."

Hazel looked at the ledgers. Oilcloth and string and her father's careful stencil, and inside them, maybe, the truth that had hung over her whole life like weather. She thought about saying yes. She let herself think it, honestly, all the way through — the debt gone, the airstrip free and clear, her father's name cleaned in front of the whole valley, the look on Rourke's face, the look on *everyone's* face. It would be so easy. It would be justice, even. That was the trap of it. It would actually be justice, sold retail, with Silas Crane taking his cut off the top.

"And the crew?" she asked. "Theo. Junie. The wages you promised."

"Paid in full, either way. I'm not a monster, Hazel."

"No," she said. "You're a partner. That's what my father used to say about Vann, too."

Something flickered across Crane's face — the first honest thing she'd seen him do. Then he wrapped the ledgers back in their oilcloth with those careful, shaking hands, and she understood suddenly that he hadn't been trembling from cold at all. He'd been trembling the way a man trembles holding something he'd kill to keep.

"Think it over tonight," he said. "We fly out as soon as weather holds. Give me your answer at breakfast."

He walked back toward his tent with the map case slung against his spine, and Hazel stayed at the lake shore a long time, watching the light go copper on the ice, feeling sixteen years of grief turn slowly over in her chest like a stone showing a new face.

Her father hadn't stolen anything. Her father had been *right*, all along, and dead for it, and the proof had been sitting in the ice two thousand feet above her head the entire time she'd been paying off his debts.

She should have felt triumphant. What she felt instead was the sourness rising under the vindication, sharp as battery acid: the knowledge that the truth had come down off that mountain in Silas Crane's pack, priced and parceled, and that every road it could travel from here ran through a deal with him.

Behind her, boots crunched on gravel. She turned, expecting Theo.

It was Junie, and Junie's face was wrong — tight and pale in the failing light, her eyes fixed past Hazel's shoulder, on Crane's retreating back.

"Hazel," she said quietly. "Come look at the radio. Somebody's been transmitting from this camp, and it isn't us."
