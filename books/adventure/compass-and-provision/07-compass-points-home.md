## 7. Compass Points Home

The radio sat in the corner of the mess tent like a guilty thing, its canvas cover thrown back, the dial still warm. Junie crouched beside it, her wrench hand curled loose at her side — the way she held it when she was thinking about hitting something.

"Frequency's been logged twice tonight," she said. "Once at nineteen hundred, once an hour ago. Short bursts. Somebody with a good set of lungs and a bad set of manners."

Hazel leaned over the set. The logbook lay open on the crate beneath it, and the handwriting in it wasn't Theo's careful schoolroom print or Crane's clipped capitals. It was slanted, hurried, written by someone who didn't expect to be audited. She read the last entry twice because her eyes refused to take it in the first time.

*Package secured. Subject cooperative for now. Request instructions re: disposal after transfer.*

Subject. Not *the pilot*. Not *Marchetti*. *Subject*.

"When you say it isn't us," Hazel said, and her voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's, "who do you mean by us?"

"I mean everybody standing in this camp breathing our own air." Junie looked up at her. "I checked the call sign against the registry I keep in my head. It belongs to a charter outfit out of Fairbanks that went belly-up four years ago. Or it belongs to whoever bought their license off a drunk administrator, which is the same thing."

Outside, the wind had begun to talk to itself again, low and restless along the glacier's edge. Somewhere across camp, Crane's tent glowed yellow, two shadows moving inside it. The ledgers were in there. Her father's name was in there, folded into oilcloth, priced and promised.

"Where's Theo?"

"Asleep, last I saw. Which is why I'm telling you before I tell him, because that boy will want to march over there and knock on canvas, and I'd like us all to live through the night."

Hazel straightened up. Her hands were steady. That surprised her; she'd have bet anything they wouldn't be. There was a particular kind of cold that settled in when a person finally understood the shape of the trap they were standing in, and it wasn't panic. It was clarity. Her father had flown into weather like this once, she thought — not cloud, but human weather, the kind you couldn't instrument your way through.

"He needs me alive until the transfer," she said. "Whatever he's selling — the ledgers, the story, my testimony if he can get it — it has to come from me willing. A dead pilot doesn't sign affidavits. So I've got until the deal closes."

"And after?"

"After, I'm cargo."

Junie was quiet a moment. Then she reached over and snapped the radio's cover back down, gentle as tucking in a child. "Then we don't let him close any deals," she said. "We fly out tomorrow, first light, weather be damned—"

"We can't. You know we can't. That front's still stacked up over the pass like a freight train."

"Then we wait, and we watch, and nobody goes anywhere alone. Not to the latrine, not to the fuel drums, not to shake hands." Junie's jaw worked. "I brought you into this job, Hazel. I signed for the crew when you wouldn't. Whatever's coming down, that's mine to carry too."

Hazel looked at her — at the grease under her fingernails, the gray coming in at her temples that she refused to dye, the woman who had kept Kettle Creek's one working engine turning through three lean winters. Something in her chest that had been clenched since the radio log loosened, just a fraction.

"Go wake Theo," she said. "Quietly. We need to know everything he heard, and I need to look him in the eye when I say what happens next."

* * *

Theo took it worse than either of them expected, and better than Hazel feared.

He sat on his bunk with his knees drawn up, twenty-two years old and trying very hard to be a man about it, and when she finished telling him — the log, the call sign, the word *disposal* sitting in the air between them — he didn't argue and he didn't bluster. He rubbed his face with both hands and said, "There was a second plane. On the manifest. I told you about the extra crates, but there was a second aircraft listed on the fuel requisition. A Stinson. I thought it was a typo."

"A Stinson could reach Kettle Creek and back inside a day," Junie said. "Could be waiting anywhere."

"Or already gone, with instructions." Hazel paced the length of the small tent, three steps each way. "Crane plays this long because he plays it careful. He won't do anything crude while I'm useful. But the moment those ledgers change hands—" She stopped pacing. "We're leaving. Weather or no weather. We find a window or we make one."

"With what? The Goose is parked on the glacier shelf, and the front's supposed to stall another thirty-six hours. I've watched that sky all day, Hazel. It's not a window. It's a wall."

"Then we hold here, together, and we get smart." She crouched so she was level with Theo. "You found the manifest discrepancy. That was good work. Better than good. Now I need one more thing from you, and then you sleep."

"Anything."

"Tomorrow you stay visible. Chopping wood, hauling ice, being the same eager deckhand Crane thinks he hired. He watches me because he knows what I am to him. He doesn't watch you because he thinks you're nobody yet." She put her hand on the boy's shoulder. "Being underestimated is the best camouflage there is. Trust me. I've been flying under it for years."

Theo managed something that was almost a smile. "Yes, ma'am."

"And Theo — if anything happens to me, you take Junie and you go east over the moraine to the cache strip my father marked on the old charts. Don't negotiate. Don't explain. Go."

"Hazel—"

"That's not a request. That's a heading."

* * *

They shouldn't have built the fire. Every instinct of field discipline said keep the light down, keep the smoke low, don't advertise. But the cold had teeth that night, the kind that got into joints and judgments alike, and Hazel made the call anyway: one fire, in the lee of the supply crates, the three of them huddled around it with their boots nearly touching the coals.

It started as tactics. Junie sketched the camp layout in the dirt with a stick — Crane's tent, the crew tents, the fuel cache, the sight lines. Theo added what he'd seen of the hired men's routines. They talked windows and wind shifts and who slept light. And then, somewhere between the second pot of coffee and the third, the talking changed.

It was Theo who broke first, though Hazel would remember afterward that it might have been the firelight making his eyes wet.

"My brother flew," he said, to the flames more than to them. "Everybody thinks I signed on with Crane because I needed wages. I did. But also — Danny flew bush runs out of Talkeetna, and when he went in, nobody recovered him. Too high, too far, too expensive. They just... closed the file." He turned a stick in the fire. "I figured if I learned how this business works, someday I could go get him myself. Bring him home."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The glacier groaned out on the icefield, that deep shifting sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

"First body I ever pulled out of a wreck," Junie said finally, "was my husband's. Nobody asked me to. I hitched a ride with a survey team and climbed down to him with a rope and a prayer, and I closed his eyes, and I flew home with him in the back of a mail plane." She fed the fire a piece of driftwood from the old supply crates. "People think grief gets smaller. It doesn't. You just build a bigger life around it, is all. Room enough that it can ride along without driving."

Hazel stared into the coals. She hadn't meant to say anything. She had a whole architecture of silence built around her father, load-bearing walls she'd maintained for eleven years. But the fire was warm and the night was enormous and these two people had, without being asked, walked into the weather with her.

"The last thing he said to me," she heard herself begin, and then stopped, and then made herself go on, "was on the phone, the night before the Crane expedition. I was mad at him. About money, about the debts, about him always saying yes to men like Silas Crane. I told him he cared more about his reputation than his own daughter." Her voice caught on the wire of it. "He laughed. He said, 'Hazey, reputation's just what people write down. You know where to find me.' And then he flew into that pass, and I've spent eleven years trying to figure out whether that was forgiveness or a door closing."

"It was a compass bearing," Junie said simply. "Men like your father don't say things plain. They give you a heading and trust you to fly it."

Theo looked up. "And the compass itself. The brass one. You never let it out of your sight."

"It was his. It was in the wreck with him — they shipped his effects back, and this was in the box, needle bent almost double." Hazel drew it from her jacket, held it in the firelight. The case was scarred, the glass crazed on one side, but the needle had been trued years ago and swung steady now, faithful as a heartbeat. "I used to think fixing it meant letting go. Then I realized it was the opposite. He gave me a heading. I'm still flying it."

The fire popped. Out past the light, the storm wall over the pass flickered with heat lightning, silent and vast, like God flipping through a ledger of His own.

"You know what I keep coming back to?" Hazel said. "Those ledgers. Three days ago they were the whole point. My father's name, clean. Proof. I could taste it." She turned the compass over in her hands. "And now some clerk writes the word *disposal* about me in a radio log, and I'm sitting here thinking — the paper doesn't matter. Even the truth doesn't matter, not by itself. What matters is who's standing next to you when the weather comes down. He knew that. That's why he flew supply runs for half what he was worth. He wasn't paying a debt. He was building a crew."

Junie reached over and squeezed her wrist, hard, once — Junie's version of an embrace. "Took you long enough."

"I'm a slow learner. Bad eyes."

Theo laughed — a real laugh, cracked and young — and for a while none of them said anything at all, and it was the best conversation Hazel had been part of in eleven years.

She was half asleep against the crate, the compass warm in her palm, when Theo stiffened.

"What is it?"

"Voices." He tilted his head. "By Crane's tent. Two men, arguing low."

They all listened. Beneath the wind, yes — the murmur of voices, rising and falling, and then a third, sharper, unmistakably Crane's, cutting through like a blade through canvas. A single phrase carried across the camp on a gust:

"—not before the transfer, you idiot. After. After."

Then silence, except for the wind.

Hazel rose slowly, sliding the compass back into her jacket. Across the dark camp, a lantern moved toward the fuel cache — one of the hired men, walking the perimeter, checking the airplanes.

Checking the Goose.

"Junie," she said quietly. "How fast can you pull a spark plug?"
