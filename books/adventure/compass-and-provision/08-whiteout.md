## 8. Whiteout

The gun was small and black and looked absurd in Silas Crane's gloved hand, like a toy he'd bought for the occasion. He held it low, casual, pointed at the space between Hazel's chest and the stove.

"Set it down," he said.

Hazel stood with her father's compass in her palm, brass worn to gold at the edges from forty years of thumbs. The wind screamed against the tent walls like something trying to get in. Behind her, Junie had gone very still by the gear crates, and Theo's breath came fast and shallow, a boy's panic barely leashed.

"Silas." She kept her voice level. "You're standing in a tent on a glacier in a storm that's going to bury us all by morning. You want to think about this."

"I've thought about nothing else for six years." His eyes were wrong — not angry, not even greedy anymore, just emptied out, the way a man looks when he's spent everything he has on one bet. "The ledgers. And that."

He nodded at the compass.

"The compass isn't yours."

"It was Elias Marchetti's, and everything Elias Marchetti touched is collateral now." He stepped closer, boots crunching the frozen floor of the tent. "The ledgers first."

Hazel looked at him for a long moment. Then she set the canvas satchel down between them, slow, deliberate, letting the weight of it thump against the ice. Inside were the ledgers — her father's name written through every page like a spine, proof that he'd been framed, proof that the men who'd ruined him had paid Crane to keep it buried.

"Good girl," Crane said, and the words landed like a slap.

She held up the compass. "This stays with me."

"No." He raised the gun an inch. "It doesn't."

* * *

They took everything. That was the thing she couldn't stop circling afterward, huddled in the wrecked supply plane's fuselage where Crane had ordered them confined — not just the ledgers but the radio's antenna lead, the spare fuel drums' caps, the tent stakes from the secondary camp. He hadn't panicked. He'd planned it. Every item removed with the tidy efficiency of a man closing accounts.

"He left us the primus," Junie said flatly, crouched over the little stove in the fuselage's tail section. Her hands were steady, which meant she was furious; Hazel had learned years ago that Junie Okafor shook only when calm. "And half a tin of tea. Generous man."

"He left us alive," Theo said from the cockpit doorway. His voice cracked on *alive*. "That's more than I expected."

"That's because you're young." Junie didn't look up. "Being left alive is just being killed slower."

Hazel said nothing. She sat in the co-pilot's seat with her hands empty in her lap and felt the absence of the compass like a missing tooth — her tongue kept going to the place where it should be. Sixteen years it had ridden her jacket pocket or hung around her neck. Her father had pressed it into her hands the last morning she ever saw him, there on the Kettle Creek strip, fog burning off the trees.

*"It doesn't point north, Haze. It points home. Learn the difference."*

She'd thought she understood. She hadn't.

Outside, the storm had arrived in earnest. It came off the icefield in long white detonations, snow moving horizontally, the world beyond the fuselage windows erased so completely that looking out felt like looking at the inside of your own skull. Wind hammered the airframe and the old plane groaned and shifted on its frozen gear like a ship working loose from its moorings.

"We can't stay here," Hazel said finally.

Junie snorted. "We can't *leave*. That's rather the problem."

"The cache strip." Hazel turned in the seat. "My father's cache strip."

Junie went still. Theo frowned. "What cache strip?"

"There's a bench of flat ground on the east shoulder of the range — my father used it for staging runs into the interior before the war. He kept fuel cached there, supplies. If the drum seals held, if the snowpack didn't shift the markers—" She stopped herself. Too many ifs. She made herself say the rest anyway. "It's nine miles northeast across the icefield. Crane will assume we're trapped here until someone comes looking in spring. He'll fly south the moment this front lifts. But if we move tonight—"

"Tonight." Junie gestured at the window, at the white annihilation beyond it. "In *that*."

"In that," Hazel agreed. "Because in three days, when the storm breaks and we haven't moved, we'll be too weak to move. And because Crane took the compass thinking it was the only way anyone could find their way anywhere." She felt something kindle in her chest, unfamiliar and hot, and recognized it after a moment as clarity. "He thinks navigation is a tool. My father taught me it's a memory."

Theo stared at her. "You know the way? In this?"

"I know the way," Hazel said, and discovered as she said it that it was true. "Dad flew that crossing two hundred times. I've flown it thirty. There's a crevasse field shaped like a fishhook below the second moraine. There's a serac that leans left like a drunk leaning on a wall. There's wind scoured blue ice along the shoulder where the drifts won't hold. I have all of it up here." She tapped her temple. "Crane stole a needle in a brass case. He didn't steal this."

Junie looked at her for a long moment. Then she stood, rolled her sleeves, and started pulling rope from the survival kit. "Then we'd better eat everything we have and go while we can still feel our toes. Theo — harnesses. Hazel — how much rope do you trust me to tie around your waist?"

"All of it," Hazel said. "I trust you with all of it."

Something passed across Junie's face then, quick as a bird — surprise, maybe, or the beginning of something else. They had worked together four years and Hazel could count on one hand the times she'd said anything that plain.

* * *

They went out into it at what Theo swore was midnight and Hazel guessed was closer to two, roped together at fifteen feet, Hazel leading. The storm hit them like a wall of hands. Within twenty steps the fuselage was gone behind them — not dimmed, not distant, *gone*, swallowed whole — and they existed in a sphere of white perhaps eight feet across, lit by Theo's headlamp bouncing chaos off the snow.

Hazel closed her eyes.

That was the trick, the thing her father had drilled into her over a decade of summers: *when the whiteout takes your eyes, give it your other senses and let the body remember.* She tipped her face into the wind and read it. The main flow came off the northwest col, which meant the pressure gradient was running true to the forecast pattern, which meant — she turned slowly, feeling the wind swing across her left cheek, her nose, her right cheekbone — northeast lay ninety degrees off her right shoulder.

"This way," she shouted, and pulled the rope.

The icefield tried to kill them methodically, the way big country does. A hidden hollow swallowed Theo to his chest in the first hour and it took both women ten minutes of hauling and cursing to lever him out. Drifts rose like dunes and collapsed under them. Twice the wind died entirely — the dead calms inside a storm's structure — and in those holes the silence was worse than the noise, vast and total, and once Hazel heard Theo sobbing quietly into his balaclava and pretended she hadn't, because some mercies are privacy.

But the landmarks came. That was the miracle she would carry the rest of her life: they *came*. The fishhook crevasse field opened on her left exactly where memory said, a black lattice under the snow bridges, and she skirted its hook on a bearing she held by counting steps — four hundred and twelve paces on a heading fixed against the wind. The leaning serac loomed out of the murk like a whale surfacing, tilted exactly as her father had described it in a story he'd told her when she was eleven, laughing, drinking coffee in the Kettle Creek galley. She put her glove against its blue ice for one second as she passed. *Thanks, Dad.*

Somewhere past the third hour, terror burned off. She noticed it happening — noticed the animal fear draining away and something else flooding in behind it, fierce and clean. Her body knew this route. Not her mind — her body, the way it knew her own name. Every stride landed on ground her father had crossed and she had crossed, and the storm could take the compass and the ledgers and the money and the name, but it could not take the fact that Elias Marchetti's daughter was walking northeast across the Sawtooth icefield in a whiteout without losing a step.

"Left cheek!" she called back over her shoulder. "Wind on the left cheek means we're holding true!"

"You're insane!" Junie shouted back, delighted.

"I'm *oriented!*"

Theo laughed then — a wild, cracking, alive sound — and the three of them went on into the white, roped together, which was, Hazel thought, the truest picture of them there had ever been.

* * *

The wind scoured blue ice appeared at dawn, or at what the graying light suggested was dawn — a long exposed rib of glacier running up the shoulder, polished hard, wind-stripped, exactly as promised. And above it, when the storm thinned for ninety seconds in one of its breathing spells, Hazel saw the bench.

Flat ground. A dark line of rock. And at its edge, half-buried, a cairn with a pole — her father's marker, the paint long gone but the pole unmistakable, leaning like an old man waiting.

Hazel stopped walking. For a moment she couldn't make her legs work.

"Haze?" Junie's hand found her shoulder.

"He's here," Hazel said. Her voice came out strange. "He's been here the whole time."

They dug the cache out together as the light strengthened — Theo swinging the shovel like a man possessed, Junie prying the drum lids, Hazel clearing snow from the small aluminum box bolted beneath the fuel drums, a box she had known about since childhood and never told a living soul. Inside: a flare pistol with three cartridges, a coil of signal wire, a tin of chocolate gone white with age, and a folded chart in her father's handwriting, the ink faded brown.

She smoothed it on her knee. At the top, in block letters: *CACHE STRIP — 1,900 FT, LAND INTO THE WIND OR NOT AT ALL.* And below it, a single line that stopped her heart:

*If you're reading this and I'm not with you — you already know the way. Go home.*

The storm was breaking apart to the southeast. Through the tearing clouds, miles down-valley, she caught it: a glint of sun on metal. An engine sound, thin and high, climbing away south.

Crane, airborne, running for the border with her father's ledgers in his baggage and her compass in his pocket.

Theo saw it too. "He's getting away."

Hazel stood, folded the chart into her jacket, and looked north along the bench where the snow lay smooth and unbroken and the wind, shifting, came straight up the valley into their faces. Nineteen hundred feet of her father's strip, waiting under the white.

"He's getting away," she agreed. She pulled the flare pistol from the box and checked the cartridges, one, two, three. "Unless somebody gets to a plane first."

Junie followed her gaze up the strip, and understanding bloomed across her frostbitten face. "Hazel. What's buried under the snow at the far end of this bench?"

Hazel smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile at all.

"Dad's backup Cub," she said. "Under a tarp, under six winters of snow. Let's dig."
