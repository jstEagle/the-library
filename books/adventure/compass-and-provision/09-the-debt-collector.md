## 9. The Debt Collector

The cache strip appeared out of the murk like a rumor made solid — a shelf of gravel and frost-browned grass wedged between two shoulders of granite, exactly where her father's memory said it would be. Hazel brought the Cub in low, crabbed against the crosswind, and felt the tailwheel kiss stone at the precise moment her fuel gauge told her she had no business being choosy about landing surfaces.

She killed the engine and sat for a moment in the sudden quiet, hands shaking on the yoke. Behind her, Junie exhaled a word she wouldn't repeat in church. Theo didn't say anything at all. He just put his hand briefly on her shoulder, the way you'd steady something you were afraid might break.

They'd flown through the whiteout on starlight and stubbornness, three hours of Hazel reading wind through the fabric of her seat and ice formations she half-remembered from a childhood spent in this cockpit with her father's voice narrating the range. *The mountain tells you what it wants, Haze. You just have to shut up long enough to hear it.*

She'd shut up. She'd heard it.

And now, down the length of the strip, half-hidden behind an outcrop, there was Crane's plane — engine cowling open, running lights burning, a man-shaped shadow moving around it with the frantic energy of someone loading gold before the tide came in.

"He beat us here," Theo whispered.

"No," Hazel said, and something cold and certain settled into her chest. "We beat him. He's been waiting for weather to lift. We're what the weather sent."

* * *

Crane heard them before he saw them — the crunch of boots on frozen gravel — and turned with a pistol already drawn, because Silas Crane had always been a man who led with his worst assumptions about other people.

"Hazel." Her name in his mouth sounded like an invoice. His eyes went past her to Junie and Theo, and she watched him do the arithmetic: three against one, but one of his held a gun, and two of theirs held nothing but loyalty, which weighed less in a crisis and more in everything else. "You're a hard woman to strand."

"You managed it," she said. She kept walking. Not fast, not slow. The gun tracked her and she let it, because she'd learned something crossing the icefield tonight: fear was just another kind of weather, and weather passed. "Where are they?"

He nodded toward his plane. Through the open door she could see the leather satchel — her father's satchel, she realized, the one from the photographs, the one that had ridden shotgun on every flight he ever took until the last one — sitting fat and smug on the passenger seat. And beside it, small and brass-bright, her compass. His compass. The one with the needle her father had painted red so she could find north even when everything else was spinning.

"Ledgers, logs, the deed," Crane said. "Everything Marchetti left in that wreck. Twenty years I've been paying men to look for this, and your father hid it under a dead prospector's claim marker like he knew someone would come digging." He almost smiled. "Sentimental fool. He could've bought his way out of the frame and he chose a hiding spot instead."

"He couldn't buy his way out," Hazel said. "That was the point. The frame only worked if he looked guilty. The ledgers were never for him. They were for whoever found them after." She stopped ten feet from him, close enough to see the storm-light flicker across the pistol barrel. "You're holding them for the same reason his partners wanted them gone. Because paper is the only thing in this world that can't be intimidated."

"Paper can be burned," Crane said.

"So can men. Doesn't mean either of us will."

The wind gusted, flinging grit against the fuselage, and Crane glanced at the sky with the naked hunger of a gambler watching the river card come. The front had a seam in it — she'd seen it from the air, a ragged corridor of broken cloud running south toward the low country. Two hours, maybe three, and it would be gone until spring.

"I'm going to make you an offer," Hazel said. "One offer. Then I'm done offering."

"Girl, you're standing in my weather window with no airplane and no—"

"Testimony." The word came out flat and clean, a blade laid on a table. "Not the ledgers. Me. I flew those mountains with my father for eleven years. I know which loads were light and which were heavy and why. I know who signed for the crates that never got opened. I know the name of the man who paid for the last Crane expedition, and it wasn't you, Silas — it was Aldous Wren, and Wren doesn't spend money without expecting a body back. Give me the ledgers and walk away, and I'll stand in any courtroom in this territory and say all of it. Your backers go down. You don't. You get to be a small, broke, honest man somewhere warm, instead of a rich one waiting for Wren to decide you're a loose end."

Something moved behind Crane's eyes. For one unguarded second she saw it land — saw him actually consider a life he'd never once imagined wanting.

Then greed did what greed does. It closed like a hand.

"Wren made me rich," Crane said. "You're asking me to trade a fortune for a conscience I've never missed."

"I'm asking you to trade a fortune for a pulse. Wren burned the last expedition, Silas. My father wasn't the only man on that mountain who died for somebody else's books. Ask yourself why you're the only one still alive to collect."

His jaw tightened. She'd gotten close enough to touch the nerve, and they both knew it. But then he shook his head, slow, almost sorrowful, and raised the pistol a few degrees.

"Here's my counteroffer," he said. "You turn around, walk back up the strip, and wait for spring. When the story breaks — and it'll break the way I write it — you'll be the loyal daughter who helped recover her father's effects. There's a check in it for you. A real one."

"And if I don't turn around?"

"Then you're a witness to an accident. Storms kill people up here all the time. Ask your father."

The words landed like a slap, and for a heartbeat Hazel felt the old grief rise up hot — but it kept going, kept rising, and came out the other side as something harder. She thought of her father's hands on this same kind of gravel. Of the debt collectors who'd hounded her mother's door. Of twenty years of carrying a name that was never hers to carry, because it had never been dirty in the first place.

"No," she said quietly. "I don't think I'll ask him anything. I think he's finally done talking. And so am I."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way storms do.

Crane shifted his weight to free his left hand for the satchel — she saw it, the tell of a man who'd never trusted anyone to carry his own money — and in that half-second of divided attention, Theo threw the wrench.

It caught Crane across the wrist, not hard enough to break bone but plenty hard enough to matter, and the pistol spun off into the dark grass. Junie was already moving, sixty years old and built like a stove, and took Crane at the knees while he was still reaching for the gun. They went down together in a tangle of expensive wool and cheap gravel, and Hazel stepped over both of them, picked up the pistol, and held it loosely at her side like something she'd found rather than something she wanted.

"Get off my strip," she said.

Crane lay in the frost, breathing hard, blood at his hairline, staring up at her with an expression she'd seen once before — on the face of a man watching his winnings blow off a card table in the wind.

"You'll freeze out here," he rasped. "All of you. That front closes in an hour and there's no—"

"There's a cache cabin," Hazel said. "My father built it. Fuel, food, a stove, blankets enough for four. He provisioned it for people he'd never meet, because that's who he was. You could have known that, Silas. You could have asked me about him sometime instead of buying his enemies." She crouched, set the pistol on the gravel between them, within his reach and deliberately so. "But you never asked anybody anything. You only ever collected."

She stood and walked to his plane, and Junie and Theo fell in behind her, and none of them looked back.

* * *

The cabin was exactly as advertised: low, tight-built, smelling of cedar and decades of dry storage. Hazel fed the stove while Theo bolted the door, and when the fire caught she held her hands over it and let herself shake for the first time since the pass.

Junie put a tin cup of coffee in her grip. "You okay?"

"No," Hazel said. "Yes. I don't know yet." She looked at the satchel, sitting on the bunk where she'd dropped it. Her father's initials tooled into the leather, worn nearly smooth. "I used to think getting these back would feel like winning."

"And now?"

"Now it feels like holding a snake by the tail. Everything in here ruins somebody. Half of it ruins people who deserve it. The other half—" She stopped. Through the little window she could see Crane's plane on the strip, lights still burning, and beyond it the wall of the storm coming down the valley like a verdict.

Her father's partners had built their fortunes on these pages. But so had Wren's lawyers, and Wren's bankers, and a dozen smaller men who'd signed things they shouldn't have, some of them desperate, some of them dead now anyway. Burning the ledgers wouldn't bring her father back. It wouldn't even bring him justice — not the courtroom kind. What it would do was take the weapon off the table. All the tables. Forever.

"They'll fight over the ashes," Junie said quietly, following her gaze. "Wren and the rest. Each of them sure the others kept copies. Each of them afraid of the ones who did. Men like that eat themselves, Hazel. Always have."

Hazel knelt at the stove. She opened the satchel and took out the ledgers — her father's neat, upright hand filling page after page, dates and weights and names, the whole ugly architecture of the frame rendered in pencil. She read one line. Just one. A delivery logged to a mine that hadn't existed yet, signed by a partner who'd sworn under oath he'd never met her father.

Then she fed the pages to the fire, one at a time, and watched twenty years of other people's sins curl and blacken and rise as heat.

It didn't feel like power. It felt like setting down something heavy after a very long walk.

The compass she kept.

* * *

Near dawn the storm hit the cache strip full force, and Silas Crane — grounded, snowbound, his fortune reduced to a satchel of ash and a plane he couldn't fly until June — hammered on the cabin door until Theo let him in out of simple human decency, which was more than Crane had ever shown anyone.

He sat in the corner with his coffee and his bruised wrist and said nothing for a long time. Then, watching Hazel turn the compass over in her fingers, red needle swinging true:

"That thing's worth nothing, you know. Brass and glass."

Hazel smiled, small and tired, and looked out the frosted window toward Kettle Creek, somewhere south under all that white, where a town full of people who owed her nothing had given her everything.

"It's worth exactly one thing," she said. "It points home."

Outside, the storm began, at last, to spend itself — and far below, in the valleys, the first thaw of a very different spring was already gathering.
