## 10. Provision

The ice went out of Kettle Creek on a Tuesday, with a sound like a rifle shot that rolled down the valley and set every dog in town barking at once. Hazel heard it from the hangar floor, where she was flat on her back under the Cessna with a torque wrench in one hand and a smear of grease across her jaw, and she lay there a moment longer than she needed to, listening to the river remember how to move.

Spring had come late and then all at once, the way it always did in the interior — three weeks of gray slush compressed into four days of green. The willows along the strip were budding yellow. The mountains still wore their white, but softer now, like they'd forgiven the winter for what it had asked of them.

She slid out from under the fuselage and sat up, and Junie's boots appeared in her field of vision, planted wide on the concrete.

"You're supposed to be resting," Junie said.

"I am resting."

"You're holding a wrench."

"Resting *with* a wrench." Hazel stood, joints complaining, and looked past her mechanic to the open hangar doors, where the morning light came down the strip gold and clean. "Besides, if I rest any longer I'll start thinking about things."

"That'd be new."

Hazel laughed — actually laughed, out loud, which was still novel enough that Theo poked his head around the wing to check the source. He'd grown an inch over the winter, or maybe she'd just stopped seeing him as the kid who'd spilled Crane's secret by accident and started seeing him as the man who'd flown into a whiteout against orders because he refused to leave her behind. Both were true. She'd stopped trying to sort them.

"Mail plane's coming in," he said. "Ranger station called ahead. There's a crate."

"A crate."

"For you." He grinned. "Big one."

* * *

The crate sat on the dock beside the fuel drums, stenciled with a Seattle address and her father's old company name — Marchetti Air Provision, the letters faded but legible, someone in some warehouse having honored a shipping label twenty years stale. Inside, packed in wood shavings gone soft with age, was the engine.

Not just any engine. The radial off the *Provision*, her father's first bird, the one that had gone into the trees on the far side of the pass with Hazel's whole childhood strapped inside it. Crane's recovery crew had pulled it from the wreck before the ledgers burned, before everything, and after the storm took its accounting of all of them, the engine had sat in evidence storage in Fairbanks while lawyers and rangers argued about what belonged to whom.

The letter on top was from the ranger herself, typed on department letterhead. *No claimant filed within the statutory period. Property released to next of kin.* Below the signature, handwritten: *Thought you should have it back. What your father did for the people up here — we haven't forgotten.*

Hazel read it twice. Then she folded it carefully and put it in her breast pocket, over her heart, where she kept the compass.

The compass itself hung there now on a leather cord instead of a chain, the crystal scratched, the needle freshly painted. That had been a January project, one long night when the wind screamed down off the ridge and none of them could sleep: she'd taken the instrument apart on the kitchen table while Junie drank coffee and Theo did his correspondence-course arithmetic, and she'd painted the north needle red — not the dull factory red it had been, but a bright, defiant vermilion, the color of a flare, the color of the paint her father had used to mark his cache strips so he could find them again from the air.

*So you can always find home,* he'd told her once, turning the compass in his big cracked hands. *Everything else is navigation.*

* * *

By April the word had traveled, the way words do in the interior — faster than mail, slower than rumor, but arriving eventually and mostly intact. The partners who had bankrolled Silas Crane, the men whose names had lived in those ledgers, found their credit drying up in Anchorage boardrooms and their leases quietly not renewed. Nobody proved anything. There was nothing left to prove; the ledgers were ash scattered somewhere along the divide, and Hazel Marchetti would not say under oath what she knew, only that she knew it, and that she'd chosen mercy over leverage, and that men who built their fortunes on a dead pilot's name might want to consider what else was built on sand.

It was enough. It turned out it had always been enough — the truth didn't need paper to walk. It walked on its own legs, and it walked into every office and roadhouse and supply depot between Kettle Creek and the coast, and everywhere it arrived, doors that had been closed to the Marchetti name for twenty years began, cautiously, to open.

The bank in Whitehorse sent a letter in May. Hazel read it standing in the doorway of the hangar, and then she read it aloud, because some things shouldn't be held alone.

"They're releasing the lien," she said. "On the strip. On the hangar. Paid in full." She looked up. "There's no debt."

Junie wiped her hands on a rag with great deliberation, giving herself time. "There never was," she said finally. "Not the kind they were collecting."

Theo said nothing at all. He just came over and put his hand on the Cessna's wing, on the fresh paint — the new name, lettered in red two weekends ago, three names long:

**MARCHETTI & CREW — CHARTER AND PROVISION**

"It needs a comma," Junie had grumbled, painting. "Grammatically."

"It needs nothing," Hazel had said. "It's perfect."

* * *

They signed the partnership papers at the kitchen table in June, because Hazel refused to do it anywhere with a lawyer's carpet. Junie got a third share for the engines she kept alive through sheer stubbornness and blasphemy. Theo got a third for the flying he was learning fast and the radio work that had saved all their lives more than once that winter. Hazel kept the last third and the debt of gratitude that came with it, which she intended never to pay off.

"To Marchetti and Crew," Theo said, raising his glass of Junie's terrible spruce beer.

"To provision," Hazel corrected, and clinked. "That was the whole point of the name. My dad used to say a plane's just metal until it's carrying something somebody needs. Medicine, mail, a prospector with a busted leg, a letter from somebody's daughter." She looked around the table — the scarred wood, the stove that had burned a ledger's worth of lies, the window with its view of the strip and the mountains beyond, patient and white and no longer anything to fear. "We're not in the airplane business. We're in the provision business. Everything else is navigation."

Junie snorted, but her eyes were wet, and she didn't hide it, which from Junie was a eulogy and a christening both.

* * *

Summer came on strong. The strip grew busy — med runs to the interior villages, freight for the mining camps that paid honest rates now that the old money had loosened its grip, and once, memorably, a wedding party of six ferried to a lake lodge, the bride riding up front with her bouquet wedged between her knees and laughing the whole way through the turbulence.

Hazel flew them all. Her hands knew the yoke the way they'd always known it, but something behind her hands had changed — some knot worked loose, some weight set down at eight thousand feet over the pass and never picked back up. When she crossed the Sawtooths now, she looked at the glacier where the wreck had been and felt only weather. The mountain had taken what it wanted. It had given back more than she'd expected. That was the trade, maybe. That had always been the trade.

In July she flew a supply run up the valley she'd avoided for years — the one that passed over the cache strip her father had cut into the timber, hidden, overgrown, hers now by blood and by memory. She circled it once. The meadow was green, dotted with wildflowers, the old markers rotted to stubs. Someday, maybe, they'd clear it again, add it to the chart. A place to land when the weather turned. That was all her father had ever meant to build, she thought. Not an empire. Just places to land, and people waiting at them.

She flew home low along the creek, windows open, the smell of spruce and river water coming through the struts, and lined up on the strip with the sun full on the windsock and the whole long evening ahead.

Junie stood by the fuel truck. Theo waved from the hangar roof, where he was fixing the antenna. Down at the end of the strip, a stranger's bush plane was parked — a woman in a canvas jacket leaning against it, watching Hazel land with the particular patience of someone who has come a very long way to ask a favor.

Hazel shut down, climbed out, and walked toward her through the prop wash, the compass hanging warm against her chest, its red needle steady on north.

"You're Marchetti," the woman said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm Marchetti and Crew," Hazel said. "What do you need carried?"

The woman smiled, reached into her jacket, and drew out a folded map covered in pencil lines — routes, elevations, a range Hazel didn't recognize, far north of anywhere she'd ever flown. "Something nobody else will take," she said. "And I'm told you're the only outfit crazy enough to say yes."

Hazel looked at the map. Then at the strip, the hangar, her people. The debt was paid. The name was clean. And the sky, as her father used to say, was the one creditor who never stopped offering credit.

She held out her hand.

"When do you need us ready?"

* * *

They were ready by Friday.
