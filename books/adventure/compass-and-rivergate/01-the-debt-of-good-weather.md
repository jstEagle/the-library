## 1. The Debt of Good Weather

The storm had a name before it had a shape—*Kestrel*, the weather bureau called it, as if naming it would tame it—and Silas Nakamura was three kilometers ahead of it in a chase rig doing ninety on a levee road that wasn't built for ninety.

"Left fork," Juno said from the nav seat, not looking up from her screens. "The bridge is rated for four tonnes and we're pulling six with the mast down."

"We're not taking the bridge."

"We're not taking the fork either if you don't decide in the next ten seconds."

Silas decided. The wheel kicked left and the *Vireo* leaned into the turn like a animal changing its mind mid-stride, all six wheels finding the packed clay of the fork road, spray coming off the ditches in gray sheets. Behind them, Kestrel's leading edge came off the delta flats like a wall being pushed over—wind first, then the rain arriving sideways, then the sound, which was less like thunder than like a door slamming somewhere enormous.

Ahead, on the ridge above the old ferry crossing, their client's sensor array stood naked against the sky. That was the job. That was always the job: get there before the weather did, bolt the thing down, get out. The Rivergate Delta paid its living to people who could stand between a storm and something fragile, and Silas's crew had made a name doing exactly that, cheap and fast and never late.

"Two minutes," Mateo called up from the engine bay, his voice riding the roar through the deck plates. "If you want the anchor bolts pre-torqued I need to know now."

"Now," Silas said.

"They're already done. I just wanted you to say it out loud so you'd feel involved."

Juno snorted. Silas let the ghost of a smile cross her face and kept her eyes on the ridge, where the rain had begun to arrive in visible curtains, marching toward the sensor array like soldiers who hadn't been told the war was over.

This was the part she loved. She held onto that thought the way other people held onto railings. This was the part she loved—the speed, the race, the clean arithmetic of a storm you could outrun. Everything else was ballast.

* * *

They beat it by forty seconds.

That was enough. It was always enough or it was nothing at all; the delta didn't grade on curves. Silas and Juno worked the array in wind that tried to peel them off the platform, shouting numbers neither of them needed to shout, hands moving on muscle memory older than their partnership. The last anchor seated with a clang Silas felt in her teeth, and then they were back in the cab, soaked through, watching Kestrel arrive at the ridge and find nothing loose to take.

"Client's pinging," Juno said, wringing out her braid over the footwell. "Confirmation, payment release, and a tip. A good one."

"How good?"

"Enough for fuel, food, and dock fees at Rivergate proper."

"Not enough for anything else?"

Juno looked at her then, and Silas watched her navigator do the math she was trying not to do herself. Fuel, food, dock fees. And behind those, patient as water rising, the number that lived in Silas's chest pocket like a second heart: what they owed, and what was left, and the distance between.

"It's enough for tonight," Juno said carefully. "It's enough for tonight, cap."

"I heard you."

"You didn't answer me."

"There wasn't a question."

"There's always a question." Juno turned back to her screens, but her voice stayed soft. "You just keep pretending it's about weather."

* * *

The calls started at dusk, because they always did.

The first came while Mateo cooked—real food, actual fish traded off a net boat, because good runs deserved good meals and bad nights were easier to survive full. Silas's wrist unit lit up with a prefix she knew by heart now, the Meridian Syndicate's routing code, polite as a letterhead. She turned the unit face-down on the chart table and passed the salt.

Second call, an hour later, while she was supposedly reviewing tomorrow's contracts. She watched it ring itself out against the underside of her palm.

Third call came at midnight, when the others were asleep and the *Vireo* rocked gently at her mooring among thirty other boats in the Rivergate harbor, lanterns strung along the boardwalk like a festival nobody was celebrating. This time Silas answered—not to talk, but because the unit had begun to feel like a creature she was starving, and starved things got creative.

She didn't say anything. Neither did the voice on the other end, at first. Just breathing, and the faint sound of a fan somewhere, and then:

"Nakamura." A woman's voice, unhurried, almost kind. "You picked up. Progress."

"I picked up by accident."

"Mm. Your balance has entered review. I'm sure you know what that means."

Silas looked out the porthole at the harbor lights smearing across black water. "I know what it means."

"Then we understand each other. Meridian is flexible, Silas. Flexibility is our whole business. But even flexible things have limits, and yours arrived this morning with your quarterly statement." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Come see us when you make port. Properly. In person. Consider it a courtesy—we prefer faces to frequencies."

"I'll come when I come."

"Yes," the voice agreed pleasantly. "You will."

The line went dead. Silas sat in the dark with the unit warm against her ear long after it stopped mattering, listening to the hull tick and the water slap, feeling the particular exhaustion that no amount of sleep touched—the exhaustion of a body that never stops moving and a ledger that never stops counting.

Perpetual motion, her father used to call it, back when he was alive to name things. Some folks run toward, some run away, and the unlucky ones can't tell the difference anymore.

She'd laughed at that once. She was eleven, and the delta was a map spread on the galley table, and every place on it was still a place she might love.

* * *

Morning came gray and busy, and Silas met it the way she met everything: already moving.

There were contracts to sign—a week of survey work upstream, unglamorous, steady money—and gear to inventory, and a coolant line Mateo had been threatening to replace since the spring floods. She threw herself into all of it with the focus of someone outrunning something, which was accurate, though the something had no shape and couldn't be outrun, only outpaced, day by day, contract by contract.

"You slept four hours," Mateo observed around midmorning, handing her a wrench she hadn't asked for.

"I slept fine."

"The hammock in the aft compartment isn't sleeping, it's napping with ambition."

"Then I napped ambitiously." She torqued the fitting he pointed at. "What's your point?"

"No point." He watched her work for a moment, and Mateo watching you work was its own kind of audit—he missed nothing, filed everything, and spent his observations only when the interest compounded. "New contract's good money. Survey work, low risk. You took it without haggling."

"It was fair."

"It was fair," he agreed. "You never take fair. Fair means you're not negotiating from strength, and you're always negotiating from strength, it's your whole personality."

"Careful."

"I'm careful. That's my whole personality." He took the wrench back, gently, the way you'd disarm someone. "Cap. If the Syndicate's leaning on you—"

"The Syndicate," Silas said, "is a lender, and lenders lean on everyone. It's weather, Mateo. You don't take it personally, you just read the sky and adjust the sails."

"And if the sky doesn't adjust?"

"Then you outrun it." She smiled at him, the smile she used for clients and customs officers and anyone standing too close to the truth, and she saw him receive it, weigh it, and file it away unsatisfied. "Go check the coolant line. If it fails upstream we lose two days and I've promised these people punctuality."

He went. He didn't believe her, and she knew he didn't, and some small honest part of her was grateful for that—for crew who saw through her and stayed anyway, which was either loyalty or foolishness, and on the delta the two were often the same trade at different prices.

* * *

By afternoon the harbor had filled with the particular restlessness that preceded a front. Gulls rode wind that smelled of iron and far-off rain. Down the boardwalk, a chart-seller was pulling in his awnings, and a pair of dredge crews were doubling their lines, and everyone moved with the shorthand urgency of people who had grown up reading water the way city folk read faces.

Silas walked the deck of the *Vireo* and felt the old rhythm settle over her like a coat: check, tighten, stow, move. There was comfort in it, real comfort, and she clung to that too. Motion was the one debt she could always service. As long as the crew was running—chasing storms, beating fronts, delivering on time—there was no room for the other arithmetic, the one with the Syndicate's routing code and her father's last transmission and eleven years of distance traveled that had never once been distance escaped.

Her wrist unit buzzed. Not the Syndicate code—she'd have known it by now, the way you know a knock. Just a port authority notice: berth fees due, standard language, standard threat. She approved the debit and watched their balance drop by a number that mattered, and told herself the survey contract would cover it, and knew she was lying, and kept walking.

At the bow rail she stopped. Below, the harbor water moved in slow brown coils, carrying leaves and a child's lost toy boat and the reflected lights of a town that trusted its charts, its levees, its luck. Somewhere upstream, Kestrel was dying over the flats, spending itself against country that had seen ten thousand storms and remembered none of them.

Her father had loved that about the delta. *The river forgives everything except the maps,* he used to say, laughing, and she'd never asked what he meant, and now there was no one to ask, and the sentence sat in her memory like a stone in a shoe she'd worn so long she'd forgotten it was there.

The wrist unit buzzed again. Syndicate code this time. She watched it ring, and ring, and ring, and did not pick up, and hated how much effort not picking up cost.

"Cap." Juno's voice from the cabin hatch, careful again, too careful. "Message came through the broker channel. Not a contract."

"Then it can wait."

"It's a meeting request. Private berth, Rivergate docks, tomorrow evening." Juno paused. "It's signed Voss."

The name landed the way cold water lands—no impact, just sudden everywhere. Silas kept her eyes on the harbor, on the toy boat spinning slowly out with the current, going wherever the water took it because it had no engine and no crew and no debts at all.

"Odile Voss," Juno said, when Silas didn't answer, "wants to meet with you. Personally. Tomorrow."

"I heard you the first time."

"You're doing the thing where you hear me and pretend the river carried it away."

Silas turned from the rail. The exhaustion was there under her ribs, the perpetual-motion ache, the sense that she had been running for eleven years along a track laid down the day Halcyon Reach went under—and now the track had bent back toward the one person alive who knew exactly where it began.

"Tell her yes," Silas heard herself say. "Tomorrow evening. Tell her I'll be there."

Juno searched her face for a long moment, and whatever she found there, she had the grace not to name. "Aye, cap," she said, and went below, and Silas stood alone at the bow of the *Vireo* while the first drops of the evening's rain began to spot the deck around her feet, thinking: *whatever she wants, it will cost more than money.*

She was right. She just didn't yet know how much more.
