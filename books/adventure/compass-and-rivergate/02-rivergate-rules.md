## 2. Rivergate Rules

The rain in Rivergate didn't fall so much as negotiate—sideways, in grudging installments, always holding something back. Silas Nakamura had learned to read it the way other people read faces. This morning it promised nothing worse than inconvenience, which was the best weather the delta offered anyone who owed money.

She stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Errand* and watched the harbor wake up around her: crane arms swinging like patient herons, barges shouldering through the brown water, the fish-market gulls conducting their eternal, vicious parliament over the cannery roofs. Somewhere behind her, inside the boat, Mateo Reyes was arguing with a bilge pump that had developed opinions. She could hear him through the hull—a low, rhythmic cursing that meant he'd win eventually. Mateo always won eventually. It was pumps and engines he couldn't forgive, not people.

Her comm unit sat on the rail beside her, screen dark.

It hadn't been dark an hour ago. At 0600 exactly—the Syndicate kept banker's hours even for threats—it had lit up with a number she knew by heart and had never once saved to memory, because saving it would mean admitting it belonged in her life. She'd watched it ring itself out. Then it had rung again. Then a message had arrived, three words long:

*We should talk.*

Four years of dodging the Meridian Syndicate had taught her their grammar. *We should talk* was what they said when *talk* had stopped being optional. When she'd first defaulted—when the storm took the *Marrow's Promise* and half her father's old crew with its cargo, and the loan on the replacement hull went from manageable to mythological—they'd sent letters. Polite ones, on heavy paper. Then calls. Then a man named Ferro had come to sit in her galley drinking her coffee and explaining, with genuine warmth, how much the Syndicate valued its relationship with her. The escalation ladder of optimism, Juno called it. Every rung friendlier and more final than the last.

*We should talk* was maybe the fourth rung. There were perhaps two more above it, and Silas had seen what stood at the top of that ladder in the case of a dredger captain named Okonkwo, who now worked the Syndicate's own boats under terms no one asked about.

"Captain." Juno Park came up the companionway with two cups of tea and the particular walk she used when she wanted to seem casual and never quite managed it. Juno did everything at a slight lean, like a person permanently braced against wind that hadn't arrived yet. "You're up early."

"I'm always up early."

"You're up early *and standing very still*, which is different." She handed over a cup. Her eyes flicked to the dead comm unit, then away, quick as a card sharp. "Bad news?"

"No news."

"That's usually the worst kind." Juno leaned on the rail beside her and looked out at the harbor with the expression of a woman cataloguing exits. She'd been Silas's navigator for six years, ever since Silas had caught her cheating at cards in a dockside bar in Port Meridian—which was itself a feat, because everyone cheated at cards in Port Meridian, but nobody else cheated *beautifully*. Silas had hired her on the spot. A person that good at deception ought to be pointed somewhere useful.

"The tide turns at ten," Juno said. "If we're taking the survey contract upriver, we want to be through the lock before the current argues."

"We're not taking the survey contract."

Juno turned her head slowly. "We discussed this."

"We discussed it before I checked the rates." Silas drank her tea. It was too hot and slightly bitter, the way Juno made it, the way Juno insisted real navigators needed it. "Hollis is paying forty percent under market because he knows every chase crew in the delta is broke this season. Forty percent under market doesn't cover fuel and it definitely doesn't cover your cut."

"My cut is fine. My cut has always been fine. You know what isn't fine? Sitting still." Juno's voice stayed light, but there was steel under it, the way there was steel under everything with her. "Silas. We've been tied up at Rivergate for eleven days. Eleven. Mateo's started naming the barnacles. I heard him call one Gerald."

"Gerald stays."

"Eleven days, and you keep turning down contracts that are beneath us, which is a phrase I've noticed you use for anything that pays less than what we owe." She let that sit in the air between them like smoke. "I do the books sometimes. Late at night. When I can't sleep."

"You're supposed to navigate."

"I'm supposed to do whatever keeps us floating." Juno set down her cup. "Who called you?"

The gulls screamed over the cannery. A barge horn moaned somewhere upstream, long and mournful as a foghorn with regrets. Silas looked at her navigator—at the lean of her, the watchfulness, the loyalty she wore like a coat she'd never been properly fitted for—and felt the old exhaustion settle over her shoulders, familiar as a wet canvas jacket.

"Nobody called me," she said.

Juno held her gaze for three full seconds. Then she nodded once, slow, and picked up both cups. "Lock opens at ten," she said. "Whatever we're doing, we should decide by nine."

She went below. She didn't push. That was the thing about Juno Park that Silas trusted most and liked least: she never pushed. She just made sure you knew she'd seen the shape of the thing you were hiding, and then she waited for you to grow into the admission.

*

The negotiation was scheduled for noon at the Saltcellar, a dockside house that served food of aggressive mediocrity to people whose business required neutral ground. Neutral ground was one of Rivergate's chief exports. The town sat at the hinge of the delta where five channels braided together, governed by no one, tolling everyone, and its taverns had witnessed more betrayals per square meter than any courthouse in the territory.

Silas arrived twenty minutes early, which was standard. You scouted the room, found the back door, noted who was armed and who was merely dressed like they wished they were. The Saltcellar's back door opened onto a fish-gutting alley; its front onto the boardwalk; its windows were too narrow to climb through but wide enough to shoot out of, which she'd seen done once during a dispute over oyster beds.

Her counterpart was already there when she walked in, which was not standard, and put a cold finger against the back of her neck.

He sat at the corner table with his hands folded, a broad, gray-templed man in a coat too good for the Saltcellar, eating nothing. Two younger men occupied the table behind him—one reading a paper he wasn't reading, one doing nothing at all except existing with a density that suggested muscle. Not enforcers, exactly. Enforcers were for when talking failed. These were the furniture of talking. The things in the room that reminded you what talking was *for*.

"Captain Nakamura." The gray-templed man smiled. He had the smile of a man who practiced in mirrors and had gotten good enough to fool mirrors. "Thank you for coming. I'm Ansel Rook. We've spoken on the comm."

"We've spoken at each other," Silas said, and sat down across from him, back to the wall, sightline to both doors. "Mostly me at you. Voicemail's a wonderful listener."

Rook laughed, genuinely delighted, and she hated how much she wanted him to keep being delighted. That was the Syndicate's real weapon—not the muscle, not the paper, but the way they made you want their approval like a debt all its own. "I'll be direct, since I understand that's your preference."

"It's my preference the way a knife is a preference."

"Then here's the knife." He folded his hands again. "Your principal balance stands at 214,000 marks. Accrued interest brings the true figure closer to 300,000. You've made eleven payments in four years, totaling—" he consulted a leather folio, though Silas would have bet her boat he knew the numbers cold, "—9,400 marks. Captain, at your current rate, you will clear this debt in the year 2061."

"I'm a fast worker when motivated."

"You are a *fast runner* when motivated. It's not the same skill, though I admire both." He closed the folio. "The Syndicate is restructuring its delta portfolio. Bad seasons, bad harvests, bad politics upstream. We are calling in positions that have underperformed. Yours is among them."

The word landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. "Calling in."

"Full balance. Due within thirty days."

The Saltcellar went very quiet around her—or rather, she went quiet inside it, the noise of the room receding like water pulling back off a reef before the wave comes. Thirty days. Three hundred thousand marks. She earned, in a good season with a good crew and merciful weather, maybe sixty. In the arithmetic of her life, thirty days might as well have been thirty minutes.

"That's not a negotiation," she said. "That's an obituary."

"It's an opening position." Rook spread his hands, magnanimous, reasonable, a man offering her a rope and calling it a hand. "The Syndicate prefers partners to corpses. Corpses don't pay interest. So—we have alternatives. Asset seizure, obviously. Your vessel. Your equipment. Possibly future earnings, assigned to us at a rate we'd determine. Or—" and here his voice softened into something almost kind, which was the most frightening register he'd used yet, "—work. Special work. Recovery work. There are jobs in this delta that pay what you owe in a single contract, and the Syndicate knows about them before anyone else does. We could point you at one."

Every instinct she owned stood up and screamed at once, and Silas told them all to sit down, because the alternative was showing him they existed. "What kind of job pays three hundred thousand?"

"The kind that requires someone desperate enough to take it." He smiled again. "Someone who can't afford to ask why the pay is so generous. Someone like you, Captain. No offense. Desperation is a qualification. We hire for it."

Behind him, the man with the newspaper turned a page he hadn't read. The dense one shifted his weight, and the floorboard under him creaked like a ship's knee.

"I'll need to see the contract," Silas said.

"Of course. When the time comes." Rook rose, buttoned his excellent coat, and extended his hand. She looked at it until he withdrew it, still smiling, unbothered. "Thirty days, Captain. And do consider the special work. The delta's getting interesting this season. Storms coming in early, they say. Big ones. Weather like that creates opportunities—and obligations." He paused at the table's edge. "Oh, and one more thing. A courtesy. We understand you've had inquiries recently. From outside parties. Regarding certain sites in the lower delta."

The cold finger against her neck became a whole cold hand.

"If those inquiries turn into employment," Rook said pleasantly, "the Syndicate would expect to hear about it from you first. Consider it a professional courtesy we'd hate to see wasted." He tipped an invisible hat. "Enjoy Rivergate while it's dry."

He left. His furniture left. Silas sat alone at the corner table with her heart going like a bilge pump with opinions, and ordered a drink she didn't want, because her hands needed something to hold that wasn't shaking.

They knew. They knew someone had approached her—though nothing had approached her yet, unless you counted the feeling of being watched that had dogged her for a week, the sense of doors closing ahead of her down every street. Which meant either Rook was fishing, or—

Or someone had talked to them about her before they'd talked to her. Someone had named her to the Syndicate before she'd even heard the offer herself. And there was only one category of person who did that: the people making the offer, clearing the field, ensuring their salvage crew came pre-leashed.

She paid for the drink she hadn't touched and walked out the front door into the flat gray light, and the fear in her chest did what it always did: it put on its bravado like a borrowed coat, squared its shoulders, and swaggered.

*

Juno found her an hour later on the boardwalk outside the chandler's, where Silas was pretending to price rope.

She didn't ask questions. She just fell into step beside her, matching her pace, and after half a block said, "Rook. Right? Gray coat, mirror smile, two gorillas?"

Silas stopped walking. "How—"

"Because he talked to me first." Juno was looking straight ahead, jaw tight. "Yesterday. Came aboard while you were at the harbormaster's office. Very polite. Asked a lot of questions about our schedule, our debts, our—loyalties. I told him I was just the navigator and didn't know anything, which is the oldest lie in my repertoire and he bought it like a tourist buys a map." She finally turned. "So before you decide how much to tell me, Captain, understand that I already lied to a Syndicate man for you. Unprompted. On spec. I'd like to know what I bought."

The boardwalk traffic flowed around them—dockhands, fishwives, a pair of children racing a hoop along the planks. Ordinary life, going about its ordinary morning, oblivious to the fact that Silas Nakamura's world had just acquired a countdown.

"He's calling the debt," Silas said. "Full balance. Thirty days."

Juno absorbed that without flinching, which was its own kind of gift. "And the special work?"

"He offered it. Didn't name it. But he knew someone was coming to me with a job. Knew before I did." Silas watched a barge slide past, loaded with timber, riding low and patient. "Which means whoever's bringing the job already cut the Syndicate in. Already told them who they were hiring. I'm not being recruited, Juno. I'm being *delivered*."

"To do what?"

"I don't know yet."

"But you're going to find out." It wasn't a question. Juno studied her face the way she studied charts—for hazards, for depth, for the truth underneath the surface features. "You've got the look. The one you get right before you do something the rest of us have to survive."

"The look keeps us fed."

"The look nearly got us killed twice last year, and once the year before, and I've started keeping a list." But her voice had gentled, and she stepped closer, and lowered it. "Silas. Thirty days. What's the actual play? Because if you tell me there isn't one, I'll believe you, and then I'll start packing, and Mateo will start crying, and none of us wants that."

Silas looked out at the harbor—at the *Kestrel's Errand* riding at her mooring, patched and stubborn, the only thing she'd built since her father died that hadn't washed away. Thirty days. Three hundred thousand marks. A leash disguised as a lifeline, and whoever held the other end of the job already knew her name, her debts, her weaknesses, and her schedule.

"There's a play," she said. "I just haven't seen the whole board yet."

"When do you?"

As if the delta had been waiting for its cue, a figure detached itself from the crowd at the far end of the boardwalk and began walking toward them—tall, unhurried, dressed in river-gray silk that cost more than Silas's engine, with a smile arriving several steps ahead of her like a lantern carried into a dark room.

Juno saw her too. She felt Silas go rigid beside her, felt the breath catch, and turned.

"Who," Juno said quietly, "is that?"

Silas watched Odile Voss approach across fourteen years and one unhealed grave, and heard herself answer from somewhere very far away:

"Trouble," she said. "Old trouble. Coming to make me an offer."
