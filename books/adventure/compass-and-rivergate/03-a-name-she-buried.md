## 3. A Name She Buried

The message came in on a Tuesday, which Silas would remember later because Tuesdays were the days the Syndicate's collectors took their rest, and so for one morning out of seven her hands didn't shake when she poured the coffee.

It was a single line, no signature, routed through three relays to scrub its origin: *The Meridian Room. Dusk. Come alone or don't come at all.*

Silas read it twice, then a third time with her thumb hovering over the delete key. Nobody used the phrase *come alone* anymore except people who wanted you to feel the weight of it. But the address was real—she knew the Meridian Room by reputation, a private dining floor above the old customs house where deals got made that never appeared in any ledger—and whoever had paid for three relays of anonymity had money and patience, which made them either dangerous or desperate.

In her experience the two qualities traveled together.

She should have ignored it. Every instinct honed across eleven years of storm-chasing said ignore it, let the mystery rot, keep moving. But the debt sat behind her sternum like ballast she couldn't jettison, and debts made cowards curious. Curiosity was just fear wearing a better coat.

So at dusk she climbed the customs house stairs alone, past a doorman who checked her name against nothing at all, into a room where the windows overlooked the Rivergate waterfront and the light came down amber through glass older than the city.

Odile Voss was already seated.

Silas felt it land in her chest before her brain finished processing—the specific vertigo of seeing a ghost who'd had the decency to stay buried. Eleven years since Halcyon Reach. Eleven years since the funeral where Odile had stood at the back in a gray coat and left before the last prayer, and Silas had been grateful, because if Odile had spoken to her that day Silas might have done something the whole delta would have remembered.

"Silas." Odile said her name like she was testing the weight of a coin. "You look well."

"I look tired," Silas said. "There's a difference."

"There always is with you." Odile gestured to the chair across from her. White tablecloth, two settings, a bottle of wine that cost more than the crew's monthly fuel budget. "Sit. I ordered for both of us. You still hate being surprised, but you've learned to tolerate it."

"You don't know what I've learned."

"No," Odile agreed, and something flickered behind her eyes—not warmth, exactly, but the memory of it, like light off a surface that had long since gone cold. "I don't. That's rather the point of tonight."

* * *

They had met at nineteen, in the certification program for delta expeditioners, two scholarship kids from opposite ends of the same poverty, competing for the same handful of licenses. Odile had been all precision—flawless charts, flawless diction, a family name she mentioned only when it won her something. Silas had been all instinct, reading water the way other people read faces, and the instructors had said, more than once, that between the two of them they made one complete expeditioner.

For six years they'd been rivals, then partners, then something adjacent to friends that neither of them had ever named. Odile's father had sat on the Hydrographic Commission. Silas's father had captained survey runs. They'd grown up around each other's ambitions the way trees grow around fences.

Then came Halcyon Reach, and the archive, and the storm that wasn't supposed to be there, and her father's last transmission cutting to static mid-sentence, and eleven divers sent into flooded corridors after survivors who were past needing rescue. Odile had walked away from the wreckage with her reputation polished and her family's name unblemished. Silas had walked away with an oath of silence sworn over her father's empty coffin and a hatred so clean it had taken years to dull into something she could carry.

Now here Odile sat, pouring wine she knew Silas wouldn't drink, smiling the smile that had opened every door in the delta since they were girls.

"You're wondering how I found you," Odile said.

"Your collectors found me first. You just read their reports." Silas turned her glass without drinking. "Meridian Syndicate. Classy clientele, Voss. I thought your name opened doors, not hired the men who kick them in."

"My name opens doors," Odile said. "Yours kicks them in. That's why I'm here." She set down the bottle. "I need a salvage crew. The best one working the delta. Everyone I asked gave me the same answer, usually while complaining about you."

"The answer's no."

"I haven't told you the job yet."

"Don't need to." Silas stood. "Whatever it is, whatever the pay is, the answer's no. Enjoy the wine."

"Halcyon Reach."

Silas stopped with her hand on the chair back. The room went very quiet, or maybe that was just her own blood. Outside the window, a cargo barge groaned against its moorings, and somewhere below a gull screamed once and gave up.

"You swore that site off," Silas said. "Everyone did. The whole community signed the moratorium. You signed it, Odile. I watched you sign it."

"I watched my career die signing it," Odile said. "And I've spent eleven years deciding whether that was grief or cowardice. I've concluded it was both, which is the honest answer nobody gives at funerals." She looked up at Silas, and for a moment the smile was gone and something rawer showed underneath. "The archive survived. Most of it. The lower vaults held. There's a sealed inheritance inside—documents, records, materials belonging to a family that will pay a fortune to see them recovered before the next monsoon season takes the vaults for good. Your father helped build those vaults, Silas. You know the layout better than any diver alive, because he taught it to you over dinner tables and chart desks your whole childhood."

"That's not a reason. That's a wound."

"It's both. It's usually both." Odile rose too, and they stood across the table from each other like two fencers waiting for the call. "I'm offering triple your standard rate. Expenses covered. And I'm offering it knowing you'll say no, because you're Silas Nakamura and saying no to me is the one pleasure you've never given up."

"Then why ask?"

"Because in two weeks I ask someone else, and they'll go in blind, and they'll either die or succeed, and either way the vaults get opened by strangers." Odile's voice dropped. "I'd rather it be you. God help me, after everything, I'd rather it be you."

Silas laughed—a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. "You want absolution with your salvage. You want me to walk back into the place that killed my father so you can watch me do it and call it closure. That's not a contract, Odile. That's therapy with a diving schedule."

"And if it were?" Odile didn't flinch. "Would that be so terrible? You've been running from that site for eleven years. You chase storms, Silas. Do you know how that reads to anyone who's paying attention? You put yourself in the path of the worst weather on the continent and dare it to finish the job. Halcyon didn't kill you. It just convinced you that drowning is a matter of time. So you spend every day trying to choose the hour."

The words went in under her ribs, precise as a diver's knife. Silas hated her for it—hated her for still knowing exactly where the soft places were, hated herself for standing there letting her find them.

"The answer's no," Silas said again, quieter now.

"Then say it properly," Odile said, "and walk out, and we'll both pretend this evening never happened."

Silas said it properly. She walked out.

* * *

The rain caught her four blocks from the customs house, a warm fat delta rain that turned the streetlights into smears. She didn't hurry. Getting wet was a problem with solutions; everything else in her life lately was a problem with interest accruing.

Juno was waiting under the awning of the chandlery when she got back to the berth, arms crossed, watching the water with the expression she wore when she'd already read the situation and was deciding how much of it to say aloud.

"Well?" Juno said.

"Well what?"

"A car brought you back. Private car. I saw it drop you." Juno turned. "You hate private cars. You say they smell like other people's decisions."

Silas managed half a laugh. "Odile Voss wants to hire us. Salvage job at Halcyon Reach."

Juno was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked on the awning above them. Down the dock, Mateo's welding torch flickered blue through the boat's open hatch, and the smell of hot metal drifted out over the water.

"She actually said the name," Juno said finally. "Out loud. To your face."

"Said it twice."

"And you told her—"

"No."

Juno nodded slowly, and Silas braced for relief, for agreement, for the easy solidarity of *good, we don't touch that place.* Instead Juno said: "What's the pay?"

"Triple rate. Doesn't matter. We're not taking it."

"Right." Juno pushed off the post and headed for the gangway, then paused. "For what it's worth—I'm glad you said no. Not because of the money. Because some doors you don't reopen just because someone rich is holding them open and calling it kindness." She glanced back. "But Silas—if the reason you said no is the one I think it is, you should know the crew can tell. We've known for years there's a site you won't sail near. We never asked. That was courtesy. Courtesy has a shelf life."

She went inside. Silas stayed on the dock until the rain stopped, watching the black water move, thinking about doors, and shelves, and how many weeks were left before the Syndicate's patience ran out and courtesy stopped mattering entirely.

She told herself the evening was closed. A refusal given, a ghost reburied, a name put back in the ground where it belonged.

She almost believed it, right up until the morning the Syndicate's tow crew arrived at the berth with paperwork for the boat—and Odile Voss's card was tucked into the enforcement officer's file, printed neat as a watermark, as if the offer had never really been an offer at all.
