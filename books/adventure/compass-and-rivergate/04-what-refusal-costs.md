## 4. What Refusal Costs

Juno Park had learned to read Silas Nakamura the way other navigators read weather. There were the obvious tells—the way her jaw set when a client lowballed them, the half-second delay before she answered anything about money—but the real signals lived in the small things. The angle of her shoulders at the helm. Whether she ate standing up or sitting down. How many times she checked the fuel gauges on a run that didn't need checking.

Tonight, sitting in the galley of the *Kestrel's Wake* with the harbor lights of Rivergate smearing gold across the porthole, Silas was checking the gauges on a boat that was moored.

That was how Juno knew the Voss woman's offer hadn't died in the dockside argument. It was still in the cabin, breathing.

"You're going to take it," Juno said.

Silas didn't look up from the chart she'd been pretending to study for twenty minutes. "I'm going to sleep."

"You've redrawn the same approach line to Halcyon Reach three times since we tied off." Juno crossed the galley and put her palm flat on the chart, pinning it. "I count things, Silas. It's my job. I counted you doing that."

For a moment the only sound was the hull ticking against its mooring lines, the water nudging the boat like something patient asking to be let in. Then Silas exhaled through her nose—not quite a laugh, not quite surrender.

"Even if I wanted to," she said, "it doesn't matter. We're not taking any contract. Voss can find herself another crew of masochists."

"Good." Juno pulled her hand back. "Because I have a bad feeling about—"

The knock came at the companionway, three measured raps, and every good feeling Juno had about the evening died on the spot.

* * *

There were two kinds of people who knocked on a moored boat after midnight in Rivergate. The first kind called out your name and waited. The second kind knocked like they already owned the door.

The man on the dock was the second kind. He was broad without being heavy, dressed in a gray coat too clean for the harbor air, and he stood with his hands visible at his sides in a way that was meant to be reassuring and instead made Juno think of holsters. Behind him, at the head of the pier, two more figures leaned against the rail of the fish market's loading ramp, lit by a single sodium lamp. They weren't hiding. That was the message. The Meridian Syndicate had never once needed to hide in this city.

"Ms. Park," the man said. He had a voice like a filing cabinet closing. "Juno Park, navigator. Correct?"

"Depends who's navigating," Juno said.

Something that might have been amusement moved behind his eyes and decided against surfacing. "My name is Ansel Roque. I represent the Meridian Syndicate's interests in the Rivergate Delta." He produced a slate-thin tablet from inside his coat and held it up so the screen caught the dock lights. On it: a photograph of the *Kestrel's Wake*, taken from the water, her storm-rigging silhouetted against a gray sky. Below the photo, a string of numbers Juno recognized with a lurch in her stomach as the vessel's registration.

"We're calling in an audit," Roque said. "Your captain's account is ninety-one days past terms. Standard remedy under clause fourteen is seizure of collateral. The boat qualifies."

"The boat isn't hers alone," Juno said. "It's crew-held. Four names on the title."

"I know all four names." He said it gently, the way a doctor delivers a diagnosis he's delivered a hundred times. "Which is why I'm here at midnight instead of sending a wrecker at dawn. Captain Nakamura has until sunrise to sign the Voss contract. If she does, the Syndicate converts the debt to a performance bond—the boat stays yours, the arrears roll into the new agreement, everyone sails away happy. If she doesn't—" he spread his hands, apologetic, terrible at it, or very good at pretending to be— "then at dawn the harbormaster receives notice of lien, and by noon the *Wake* is on a crane barge headed for the impound yards at Corben's Landing. You're welcome to buy her back at auction. Market rate plus penalties. I'd estimate you couldn't afford her."

Behind Juno, at the companionway, she heard the hatch open. She didn't need to turn around to know Silas had come up. She could feel her there, the way you feel a front moving in.

"How long have you been waiting to use me as leverage?" Silas said.

Roque's attention slid past Juno like she'd become furniture. "Since the day you signed the original note, Captain. Leverage is just a word for what you put up before you know what you'll owe." He tapped the tablet once, and the screen went dark. "Sunrise. The contract will be delivered to your wheelhouse within the hour. Ms. Voss sends her regards, and her apologies for the method. She argued for more time. She lost."

He turned and walked back up the pier without hurry, and the two figures at the lamp peeled off after him, and the sodium light buzzed over the empty dock like nothing had happened at all.

* * *

They sat in the wheelhouse because neither of them could stand the galley anymore. Silas took the helm chair and didn't touch the wheel, which for her was a kind of collapse. Juno leaned against the chart table and waited, because pushing Silas Nakamura was like pushing a river—you chose where to stand and let the current do the work.

"Ninety-one days," Juno said finally.

"Fifty-eight."

"What?"

"You said ninety-one. It's fifty-eight past terms. I paid thirty-three days' worth in the spring, off the Kestrel Point salvage." Silas's thumb moved along the wheel's rim, back and forth, a metronome with no music. "Roque rounds up. It's a tactic. Makes the debt look older, more settled, more hopeless. Everything the Syndicate does is arithmetic wearing a suit."

"That's not the part that matters, Silas."

"No," Silas agreed quietly. "It isn't."

Juno watched her for a while. Outside, a tug groaned somewhere upriver, hauling some late cargo against the current, and the sound came across the water flattened and lonely.

"Four years," Juno said. "Four years I've run nav for you. I've eaten freeze-dried noodles in a hailstorm with you. I've held your hair back—metaphorically, you'd never allow it literally—in three different ports after three different funerals. And in four years you never once told me the Syndicate held paper on this boat. On *us*. We found out tonight from a man in a gray coat, at midnight, like strangers."

Silas's thumb stopped moving.

"I know."

"You *know*?" Juno pushed off the chart table. "That's it? You know? Silas, if they seize the *Wake*, Mateo loses his workshop, Tam loses her rigging certification posting—it's tied to the vessel, you know that—and I lose the only navigation table in the delta I trust. This isn't your debt. It stopped being yours the day you put our names on that title. Which, by the way, was the one decent thing you did, and now it's the hook they're using to gut us."

"I put your names on the title so the Syndicate couldn't take it," Silas said, and her voice had an edge now, thin and bright. "Joint title means joint consent required for seizure. It was armor."

"It was bait. Because joint consent means they only need *you*, and they own *you*." Juno heard her own voice rising and didn't care. "How much, Silas? What's the number? Because I've been doing the math in my head since Roque walked away, and every figure I come up with is bigger than anything a storm-chase crew earns in a season. So either you've been skimming from us—which I don't believe—or you borrowed against us for something, and you've never said what."

The wheelhouse went very still. Even the river seemed to hold its breath.

Silas looked at her then, fully, and Juno saw something she'd only seen twice before: once over the radio the night the Halcyon dike failed, and once in a hospital corridor in Corben's Landing when a client's son hadn't survived the rescue. Grief with nowhere to go, packed down so hard it had turned into something like stone.

"It's not a number," Silas said. "Not anymore. It compounds. It was a number eight years ago, and I've been paying interest ever since, and the principal never moves, because that's how Meridian builds their books. They don't want repayment, Juno. Repayment ends the relationship. They want the relationship." She laughed, short and awful. "They call it optimism. Did you know that? Their internal term. A client in good standing is an *optimistic account*. They lend against your future because they're optimistic you'll always have one worth taking."

"So what was the original loan for?" Juno asked. "Eight years ago. What did you buy that cost this much?"

And here it was, Juno thought later. Here was the moment she would replay a hundred times, the moment the betrayal curdled and changed state and became something else, something harder and more durable. Because Silas opened her mouth. Juno watched her do it. Watched the answer rise in her like water in a lock chamber—and watched her close her mouth again and let it sink.

"I can't tell you that," Silas said.

"You can't—"

"Not won't. Can't. There are—" Silas pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a second, and when she lowered them she looked ten years older. "There are promises involved. Oaths. From before you, before the crew, before the *Wake*. Things I swore to people who are dead now, and the swearing-out-loud of it has consequences that aren't mine alone to risk. I know how that sounds. I know exactly how that sounds. But if I tell you it's because I'm protecting you, you'll call it a lie, and if I tell you it's because I'm protecting myself, you'll walk off this boat tonight, and both of those would be wrong, and I don't have time to prove it."

"Prove what? You haven't told me anything!"

"I've told you the shape of it!" Silas was on her feet now, and the wheelhouse felt too small for both of them and the truth all at once. "The shape is: I owe the Syndicate for something I did right after Halcyon. Something connected to Halcyon. Something my father—" Her voice snagged on the word and she hauled it back like a fouled line. "Something that happened because of what my father found there. And Odile Voss knows it, which is why she's holding the leash, and why the job she's offering isn't salvage. It's a key turning in a lock I built eight years ago and welded shut."

Juno stood there, breathing hard, the words landing in her one at a time like stones dropped into deep water. *Halcyon.* Everyone in the expedition community knew the word and flinched from it. Eleven dead. The archive drowned. The oath the survivors swore—she'd been at the memorial, she'd heard the recitation, everyone had. Silas had stood in the second row with her father's compass in her fist and her face like a closed door.

"And Voss," Juno said slowly. "You two have history."

"Voss and I grew up racing each other to every wreck in the delta. Voss and I were friends before Halcyon, and we buried whatever we were after it, together, badly." Silas sank back into the helm chair. All the fire went out of her at once, the way it did, leaving behind that terrible economy of motion, that person-shaped absence. "She didn't come to me because I'm the best salvage navigator left. She came to me because I'm the only one who'll recognize what's down there. And she knew—I'd bet everything—that refusal wouldn't be the end of it. She knew the Syndicate would finish the argument for her."

"So she sicced your creditors on you."

"She *is* my creditor. Or she's married to them, financially speaking. The Voss family name is all over Meridian's founding capital. I should have seen it the moment she stepped onto the dock." Silas stared out the wheelhouse glass at the black water. "I did see it. That's the worst part. I saw it and I still yelled at her on the pier because yelling felt better than admitting she'd already won."

Juno looked at her captain—at this woman she'd followed into hail cells and flood seasons and the mouths of rivers no chart recommended—and felt the anger in her chest do something complicated. It didn't leave. Anger like that didn't leave; it had earned its tenancy. But it shifted, settled, made room for something else alongside it. Not forgiveness. Not yet. Maybe not ever, entirely. More like recognition. The recognition that Silas Nakamura had been carrying a weight the size of a drowned station for eight years, alone, in plain sight, and had done it badly and proudly and at enormous cost to everyone who loved her, and that none of those facts canceled each other out.

"If you sign her contract," Juno said, "we go to Halcyon Reach."

"Yes."

"Where your father died."

"Yes."

"And you'll tell us the rest. Before we get there. Not after. Not when it's convenient. Before." Juno held up a hand before Silas could speak. "I'm not asking for the oath itself. I'm asking that when we're inside that place, nobody on this crew learns anything about our captain from a stranger in a gray coat ever again. Whatever you can give us, you give us up front. That's the price of my consent. Joint title, remember? Joint consent. Turns out you built that armor pretty well."

Silas looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the sky over the eastern breakwater had gone the color of dirty pewter—the false dawn, hours early, the monsoon season pressing its thumb against the horizon.

"You should hate me," Silas said. "It would be simpler."

"I do hate you. A normal amount. It coexists with the other thing." Juno pushed off the bulkhead and headed for the companionway, then stopped, hand on the hatch. "Sunrise is in three hours. Mateo and Tam are going to wake up and find out their boat was collateral this whole time, and they're going to be angrier than I am, because they've known you less time and loved you just as much. You get to have that conversation. I'm done having it for you."

"Juno."

She paused.

"Why aren't you walking off the boat?" Silas asked, and there was no bravado in it at all, just the raw question underneath, the one she'd probably been carrying since Roque's footsteps faded up the pier. "By every rational measure, staying is insane. I'd understand. I'd even write you a reference."

Juno considered the honest answers. *Because the Syndicate doesn't get to decide who my people are. Because someone has to navigate and you steer like a drunk. Because leaving you alone with Odile Voss and a syndicate of optimists is how you die.* All true. None of them the root.

"Because you checked the fuel gauges on a moored boat," she said finally. "Three times. You only do that when you're deciding whether to run. And I've decided I'd rather see what you're like when you stop running than keep navigating for a woman who never will." She pulled the hatch open. "Get some sleep, Captain. Tomorrow we sail for the one place in the delta you swore you'd never go again."

She was halfway down the companionway stairs when Silas spoke again, quiet enough that Juno almost missed it under the hull-tick and the water.

"There's a logbook," Silas said. "My father's. If it's still down there—if it survived—it changes what this job is. For everyone. Not just me."

Juno stood in the dark stairwell, one hand on the cold rail, and understood that she'd just been handed the first piece of the truth, deliberately, like a rope thrown across a gap.

"Then I guess," she said, "we'd better make sure we're the ones who find it."

Above her, through the wheelhouse glass, the false dawn kept climbing, and somewhere out past the breakwater, the river was already beginning to rise.
