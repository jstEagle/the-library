## 5. Sworn Off Water

The pen was heavier than it looked.

Silas Nakamura sat across from Odile Voss in the back room of the Saltglass Tavern and stared at the contract as if it might blink first. Twelve pages. Voss's lawyers had a gift for making ruin sound like opportunity—*the undersigned agrees to conduct salvage operations at the site designated Halcyon Reach*—and every clause was written in that smooth, bloodless language that existed specifically so that people like Silas could not say no cleanly.

She didn't touch the pen. Not yet.

"You said you'd give me until morning," Silas said.

"I lied." Voss turned her glass on the table by its base, a slow orbit, amber catching lamplight. "The Syndicate seized your boat this afternoon. You know that. Every hour you sit here pretending you have options is an hour Corrigan's people spend deciding how much of your crew's equipment they want to strip for parts."

"Then take the boat. Let them." Silas leaned back. "We've run leaner."

"On what? Juno's savings? Mateo's broken wrist?" Voss smiled, and it was the same smile she'd worn at nineteen, at twenty-five, at every age where being beautiful and ruthless were the same skill. "You're not a woman who lets her people bleed for her pride, Silas. I've watched you for fifteen years. You always pay. The only question is who sets the price."

The tavern noise washed against the door—laughter, a fiddle badly played, someone arguing about tide tables—and Silas let it fill her ears like water filling a hull. She thought about the boat. *The Kestrel Line*, twenty-two feet of welded aluminum and stubbornness, the only thing she owned outright in the world, and even that wasn't true anymore, not since Meridian's lien had wrapped around it like kelp around a propeller.

She thought about her father instead. That was the trap of this table. Voss knew it. That was why Voss had chosen this table.

*Halcyon Reach.*

Say the name and the delta went quiet inside her head, the way a forest goes quiet when something large moves through it.

* * *

Eleven years ago, the storm season came early, and Halcyon Reach was still a research station then—a crown of steel and solar glass rising off a shoal at the delta's ragged edge, where the river gave up its argument with the sea. Her father, Kenji Nakamura, ran the hydrographic survey out of there. Flood mapping. Bathymetry. The unglamorous arithmetic that kept three hundred thousand people in the lowland towns alive and dry, or was supposed to.

Silas had been twenty-three, running her first real contracts, chasing squalls upriver for insurance adjusters and grain merchants. They argued constantly, she and her father—about risk, about money, about the way he looked at charts the way other men looked at scripture—but they argued on the same side of everything that mattered. He'd taught her to read water before he taught her to read words. *The river never lies,* he used to say. *People lie about the river.*

The last transmission came at 0347 on the ninth of Stormmonth.

She'd been asleep in the cab of the chase vehicle outside Marsh Hollow when the radio crackled her callsign—her private channel, the one only two people in the world had. His voice was calm in the way that frightened her more than shouting would have. Calm the way a man is calm when he has already decided something.

*"Sila. If you're hearing this, I need you to listen and not come looking. Not yet. There's a discrepancy in the Reach survey data. It isn't small. Someone has been adjusting the baseline charts—the flood projections for the whole lower delta. If the monsoon tracks east this year, the official maps will be wrong by as much as four meters in the low towns. I've pulled the original sensor logs. I'm taking them down into the archive vault tonight, along with my personal logbook. If anything happens to me, the vault seals itself. Pressure-locked. It can only be opened from inside, during a survey window, by someone with my access codes—"*

Static. Then his voice again, tighter now, wind audible behind him, the station groaning in the background like a ship changing its mind about staying afloat.

*"—they know I found it. I don't know who 'they' is yet, but the resupply skiff turned back without orders and there's a boat holding offshore that shouldn't be there. I'm not going to make this dramatic. I did the math on my own survival a long time ago; it's just numbers. What matters is the data survives me. It's in the vault. Lower level, behind the core sample library. Sila—"*

And here his voice changed. Here, eleven years later, she could still hear it change—the professional calm cracking just enough to show the father underneath.

*"You'll want to come after me. Don't. Promise me you'll finish what I started instead. Get the data out. Make them print the truth. That's the rescue I actually need."*

The transmission ended at 0352.

At 0419, Halcyon Reach's emergency beacon began to scream.

* * *

She broke the promise in under six hours. Of course she did. She was twenty-three and her father was drowning and the promise had been made to a dead frequency.

By noon she was standing on the deck of a borrowed salvage tug with nine other volunteers—expeditioners, mostly, people who'd worked the Reach over the years—and the station was already half-gone. The storm surge had come through the night like a debt collector, and Halcyon's legs had folded one after another, and now the upper labs lay tilted into the gray water at an angle that made the whole structure look like it was bowing toward the sea, apologizing for something.

They pulled eleven survivors out over two days. Silas remembered each one the way you remember stitches—individually, painfully, by location.

She did not pull out her father.

On the third day the water inside the station stabilized and the dive teams went down into the flooded corridors, and Silas went with them, against every regulation, against the explicit order of the expedition coordinator, a granite-faced woman named Anselm who would later become the closest thing Silas had to a mentor. Silas swam the length of the residential deck in zero visibility with a hand light and a dive knife and found the door to her father's quarters wedged open by a floating chair.

His bunk was stripped. His go-bag was gone. His waterproof field case—the one he'd carried on every survey since she was a child, green canvas, brass buckles—was missing from its hook.

He'd tried to get out. That was the thing she hadn't been able to stop turning over for eleven years. He hadn't stayed in the vault. He'd taken the data down, sealed it, and then he'd tried to reach the surface, and somewhere between the fourth deck and the second, the water had decided otherwise.

Or someone had helped it decide. She'd never known. She would never know. That was the shape the not-knowing had carved in her: a hollow exactly the size of a question.

What the divers did find, on the fifth day, in the flooded vault antechamber, was the pressure seal—intact, fused, exactly as designed. The archive below was unreachable without opening the station's internal survey systems, which required power, which required weeks of restoration work the expedition community could not fund and, increasingly, did not want to attempt.

Because by the seventh day, the questions had started arriving from outside. Insurance investigators. A very polite man from the Delta Cartographic Authority who asked the survivors, twice, whether Kenji Nakamura had seemed "under strain" in his final weeks. And a lawyer whose card simply read MERIDIAN SYNDICATE — ASSET RECOVERY, who asked nothing at all, which was worse.

On the ninth day, the survivors gathered in the marsh-side hall at Rivergate proper—eleven rescued, plus the dive teams, plus Anselm, plus Silas with salt still crusted in her hair—and Anselm stood at the front of the room and said the words that ended the golden age of delta exploration:

"Halcyon Reach is sworn off. No expedition returns. No survey touches it. We do not sell its coordinates, we do not answer questions about it, and we do not speak of what Kenji Nakamura told us in his final transmission. Not because the truth doesn't matter. Because right now, the truth has teeth, and everyone who goes near it gets bitten. We wait until the biting stops. We swear it on the drowned."

And thirty-one people—Silas among them, her hand raised, her voice steady, her whole chest a closed fist—had sworn it.

*Sworn off water.* The oath had a rhythm to it, liturgical, that she could still recite in the dark: *No return. No recovery. No word to the powers that drowned him. Until the water gives up its dead honestly, we leave the water its secrets.*

She'd kept that oath for eleven years. She'd built a career in its shadow—storm-chasing, salvage runs, everything fast and shallow and far from the deep places. She'd told herself the oath was protection. Of the survivors. Of the families. Of the truth itself, kept safe in its vault like a heart on ice.

It was only recently—only since Voss had walked into the Saltglass with that smile—that Silas had allowed herself to consider the other possibility. That the oath wasn't a shield.

That it was a lid.

* * *

Back in the tavern, Voss set down her glass.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said. "Where you go somewhere else and stare at me like I'm a wall you're planning to climb. I've seen it across negotiation tables for half our lives. It doesn't work on me, Sila. I invented it."

"Did you?" Silas said. "Because as I remember, at the Meridian Open, I invented it first, and you copied it badly."

Something flickered across Voss's face—not quite warmth, but the fossil record of warmth. They had been rivals for fifteen years and friends for three of those, and the friendship had died at Halcyon too, though neither of them ever said so directly. Odile had been on the survey team that season. Odile had been ashore when the surge hit, running logistics out of Rivergate, and Odile had spent the aftermath doing what Odile always did: positioning. Making calls. Cultivating the authorities. Being seen as competent, composed, essential—while Silas was underwater looking for her father's body.

Odile had signed the oath with everyone else. And then, Silas suspected, Odile had spent eleven years quietly making sure the oath held, because the oath served her. Whatever her family's connection to the Cartographic Authority—and the Voss name was threaded through that institution like wire through cable—quiet was good for business.

"Why now?" Silas asked. "Eleven years, Odile. Why does the sealed inheritance matter *now*?"

"A client wants it recovered. The details are privileged."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting." Voss leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for a moment the polish dropped and Silas saw the calculation underneath, bare and quick as a fish flashing in shallows. "Here's what I can tell you. The survey window opens in twelve days. The vault can only be accessed during a pressure equalization cycle that happens once a season, and if we miss it, we miss it for another year—and your boat stays in a Syndicate impound yard for another year, accruing fees your grandchildren will inherit. Those are the facts. Everything else is grief, and grief makes terrible arithmetic."

"Grief made me the only person in this delta who knows those waters," Silas said quietly. "I dove that station for five days while the rest of the expedition community stood on the shore and swore oaths. So don't lecture me about arithmetic, Odile. I did mine a long time ago. I know exactly what you're buying when you buy me."

"And what's that?"

"My father's access codes live in exactly one place." Silas tapped her own temple. "He seeded them into me when I was a teenager, the way he seeded everything into me—half lessons, half paranoia. The vault won't open for your client's technicians. It won't open for Corrigan's divers, if that's who's really behind this. It opens for a Nakamura, or it doesn't open. Which means you don't need a salvager, Odile. You need a key. And you've spent eleven years waiting for the key to get desperate enough to fit herself into the lock."

The silence between them had texture. The fiddle outside hit a wrong note and recovered.

Voss didn't deny it. That was the thing about Odile—she respected Silas enough to skip the lying, most of the time, when it counted. "I need the inheritance recovered intact," she said. "Whatever it is. Sealed containers, documents, whatever your father locked down there. Delivered to my client, unopened, and the site left clean. That's the job. What's in the containers is not your concern."

"Not my concern." Silas laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "My father died sealing those containers. Eleven years ago I swore on his grave that no one would ever drag them up and sell them to whoever paid enough. And now you're asking me to do it myself. For a debt I owe to the people who probably killed him."

"I'm asking you to save your crew," Voss said. "Let's not dress it in martyrdom. Juno Park has been covering your debts for eight months. Mateo took that contract in the Sedge Flats knowing the payout was garbage because you needed the fee. These people have tied their livelihoods to a woman who is sinking, Sila, and they keep bailing because they love you, and you keep letting them because it's easier than admitting the water's over the gunwales." She slid the contract across the table. One page. The signature line waited at the bottom like an open mouth. "Sign, and tomorrow morning the *Kestrel Line* is released, your crew is paid a retainer, and in twelve days we go to Halcyon Reach together and end this. All of it. Whatever 'all of it' turns out to mean."

Silas looked at the signature line.

Guilt signing its own name, she thought. That's all this is. That's all it's ever been since 0352 on the ninth of Stormmonth—the moment his voice cracked and said *finish what I started*, and she had spent eleven years finishing nothing, running everywhere, chasing weather across the delta like motion was a form of obedience. The oath had given her a shape to live in. Sworn off water. Sworn off the deep places. Sworn off the question that ate her alive: *what did he find, and who made sure he didn't come back up with it?*

But oaths, her father had told her once, kneeling beside a chart table, his finger on the contour lines of a coastline she'd later inherit in nightmares—oaths are tools, Sila. Like knives, like anchors. A tool that can't be put down stops being a tool and becomes a wound.

The oath had protected the guilty for eleven years. It had kept the truth in the dark, and the dark was where the Syndicate liked it.

Maybe the way to honor a promise to a drowned man was to break the promise made by the living.

Silas picked up the pen.

"One condition," she said. "Anything we bring up, I see first. Before your client. Before anyone. You want a key, Odile, the key gets to read what it unlocks."

Voss's eyes narrowed—a flicker of the old rivalry, the old chess. "That's not in the terms."

"Then the terms can drown."

A long moment. Outside, the fiddle gave up entirely and someone applauded it anyway. Somewhere across town, in a Syndicate impound yard, her boat sat chained and silent, and Juno was presumably sitting on its deck keeping watch out of pure stubbornness, and Mateo was taping his wrist, and none of them knew yet what she was about to sign them into.

"Fine," Voss said. "Seen first. Witnessed. Now sign, before I remember why I stopped liking you."

Silas signed.

Her handwriting came out steadier than she expected—her father's handwriting, really, the tight engineering script he'd drilled into her alongside Morse code and knot work. *Silas Nakamura,* offered up to the ghost of Halcyon Reach like a coin thrown into black water.

The pen clicked shut. Somewhere far downstream, twelve days out, a drowned station waited in the green dark with its vault sealed and its secret patient, and her father's last words hung in the static between frequencies: *finish what I started.*

"Pack light," Odile Voss said, rising, gathering the contract. "We sail with the morning tide."

Silas stared at the empty doorway long after Voss had gone through it, and in the tavern's smoky lamplight she could almost see the water rising—slow, patient, inevitable—coming for all of them at last.
