## 6. Downstream of Everything

The *Kestrel's Debt* had been a good name for the boat once, back when it was a joke instead of a diagnosis. Mateo Reyes had painted it on the hull himself, three years ago, in letters that were already starting to peel at the K. Now he stood in the slip at Corder's Landing with a torque wrench in one hand and a list in his head that kept getting longer the more he looked at the boat, and he thought: we are about to take this vessel somewhere the river itself has been trying to forget for eleven years.

"Tell me she's not as bad as she looks," Juno said, dropping onto the dock beside him with two cups of tar-black coffee and the particular walk of someone who'd slept four hours and wanted credit for it.

"She's worse." He took the cup. "But bad is honest. Bad I can fix."

"And what can't you fix?"

Mateo nodded across the slip, to where Silas stood at the bow with her back to all of them, turning something small over in her hands. "That."

Juno followed his gaze and said nothing for a moment. She was good at that—Juno Park could hold silence like ballast, let it sit low in the hull where it did actual work. Then she drained half her coffee and said, "I'll do the fuel lines. You do whatever it is you do when you talk to engines like they're wounded animals."

"They respond better than people."

"That's because you don't lie to them."

He watched her go, then climbed aboard, and the deck took his weight with the familiar complaint of old welds and older compromises. The *Kestrel* was forty feet of salvage-grade stubbornness: a shallow-draft river runner with a reinforced keel, twin diesels that had been rebuilt so many times they were mostly metaphor, and a cargo bay that smelled permanently of chain oil and the ghost of every fish market they'd ever fled. He loved her the way you love anything that has never once made your life easy but has also never once left you stranded.

Which was, he supposed, how Silas loved her crew.

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the work was the conversation.

"You hear about the Meridian man asking around the fish docks?" Priya called down from the mast step, where she was re-seating the antenna mount with the grim focus of someone who'd decided the weather was a personal enemy.

"I heard," Mateo said, not looking up from the port engine's fuel filter. "I also heard he asked about us by name."

"He asked about Silas by name. We're just the punctuation."

"Punctuation gets deleted first."

Priya laughed, which was her way of not answering. Up top, Juno fed him tools when he held out his hand without looking, which after four years together was less trust than reflex—the way your hand knows where the light switch is in a house you grew up in. The banter ran along its usual rails: Priya's theory that the Syndicate's enforcers all bought their jackets from the same catalog, Juno's counter-theory that the catalog was a front, Mateo's contribution that anyone who dressed like that deserved what happened to their dry cleaning in a monsoon.

Nobody said *Halcyon Reach*. Nobody said *your father*, or *the disaster*, or any of the words that sat under the floorboards of the crew like water under ice. They'd signed on to chase storms for insurance surveyors and run emergency cargo ahead of flood season, and that was the job, and the job was fine, and if the job now pointed downstream toward the one place on the delta map drawn in hatching lines and old grief, then fine was going to have to stretch.

Around noon, Silas finally came below.

She moved through the engine compartment the way she moved through everything—like the space owed her passage and knew it—and crouched beside him at the starboard block without being asked. For a while she just watched him work, handing him things, and Mateo understood that this was her version of an apology for whatever was coming. Silas Nakamura apologized in labor.

"Bilge pump's new," he said. "Third time I've replaced it this year. Either the river hates us specifically or someone's been running her hard."

"The river doesn't hate us. It doesn't know we exist." She passed him a gasket. "That's the whole problem with rivers. Total indifference. Very humbling."

"Says the woman who races them for money."

"For money," she agreed, "and because indifference is the only opponent who never gets tired."

He snorted and torqued the fitting, and for a few minutes there was only the sound of the compartment: drip of condensation, tick of cooling metal, the far-off argument of gulls over the fish docks. Then Silas reached into her jacket—not for the contract, he noticed, though the contract was surely folded in there like a splinter—and brought out the compass.

Mateo had seen it a hundred times. Brass-cased, dented at the bezel, older than both of them put together. Elias Nakamura's compass, the one piece of the man that had come home when the man hadn't. Silas carried it the way other people carried cigarettes: something to hold when the hands needed a job.

Except today she wasn't holding it. Today she was watching it.

"It's wrong," she said.

"It's old."

"It's wrong *here*." She turned it so he could see. The needle swung, settled, swung again—a slow, sick oscillation, like a dog circling before lying down and never quite lying down. "It does this within thirty miles of Halcyon. Did it back then too. My father used to joke that the place had too much iron in its bones." Her voice did something small and controlled on the last word. "It stopped doing it the day after the flood. Out on open water, fifty miles away, needle steady as scripture. Came back into range a week later."

Mateo set down the wrench. "You think it's the station. The structure. All that steel in the water."

"I think it's *something*." She closed her fist around the compass, and he saw her knuckles whiten, and then deliberately relax, one finger at a time, the way she did when she was putting herself back in order. "Or nothing. Probably nothing. Old brass, old magnetism, old stories."

"Silas." He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last season. "You don't study a broken compass for twenty minutes unless part of you believes it's telling you something."

For a second—one second—she looked at him the way people look out of high windows. Then the shutters came down, neat as a hatch closing, and she smiled the smile she used on clients and customs officers.

"What it's telling me is that my engineer worries too much. Is the boat ready?"

"The boat will be ready. Boats are simple." He held her eyes, because someone on this crew had to, occasionally, and Juno got tired. "The question is whether the crew is."

"We're always ready. That's the whole trick of us." She pocketed the compass and stood. "Fuel?"

"Topped by evening. Water by dark. If the weather window holds, we run tomorrow on the morning tide."

"Good." And she was gone up the ladder, and the compartment felt smaller without her in it, which was its own kind of data.

* * *

He found Juno on the aft deck at dusk, splicing line with the automatic grace of someone whose hands had learned knots before sentences. The sky over Rivergate was doing its pre-monsoon performance—bruised gold at the horizon, stacks of cloud building inland like slow applause. Mateo handed her the last of the coffee, cold now, and she drank it anyway.

"She showed you the compass," Juno said. Not a question.

"So you've seen it too."

"Seen her watch it, yeah. Three nights running, standing at the rail like it owes her money." Juno tied off the splice and bit the tag end clean. "Here's what I know, Mateo, since apparently knowing things is my job now. Voss didn't pick us out of a directory. She picked *Silas*. There's a reason, and the reason is buried at Halcyon, and our captain would rather eat the anchor chain than say it out loud."

"You could ask her."

"I could ask her the same way you could ask the river to run uphill." Juno leaned back against the rail and looked at him sideways. "Four years, I've followed that woman into weather that had no business having a name. She's earned the benefit of the doubt. But doubt compounds, Mateo. Like interest." A thin smile. "And you know who else understands compounding interest."

He did know. Everyone at Corder's Landing knew; the Syndicate's logo was on the harbor office door and on the loan papers and, lately, in the way dockhands went quiet when certain men walked past. The debt wasn't Silas's alone anymore—that was the thing nobody said directly. Collateral had a way of spreading. A boat, a crew, a reputation. You co-signed with your whole life when you sailed with someone.

"Tomorrow we point downstream," Mateo said. "Past the last charted marker, into water the guides won't take. And I keep thinking about the bilge pump."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning I've replaced it three times and each time I tell myself it's just wear. But boats talk, Juno. This one's been trying to tell us something all season and we haven't been listening." He looked out at the darkening water, at the far bend where the Rivergate narrowed and began its long patient slide toward the delta country. "Maybe it's nothing. Maybe it's iron in the bones."

Juno was quiet a moment. Then: "You believe in omens now?"

"I believe in maintenance schedules. Same thing, if you're honest."

That got a real laugh out of her, short and surprised, and for a moment the dread thinned and it was just the two of them on a working deck at the end of a working day, and the boat was sound beneath their feet, and the crew was whole, and tomorrow was a problem shaped exactly like every yesterday.

Then Priya came down the dock at a jog, tablet in hand, and the evening changed temperature.

"Satellite feed updated," she said, slightly breathless. "Seasonal forecast model revised overnight. The monsoon trough—" she pulled up the image, a spiral of red and violet crawling across the sea like a bruise spreading under skin, "—it's tracking west-northwest. Earlier than consensus. Days earlier. If it holds this course..."

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Every one of them could read a trackline; it was the trade they shared. The line ran straight as a thrown spear toward the Rivergate Delta.

Toward the coast. Toward the river mouths.

Toward Halcyon Reach.

Below decks, faint through the hull, Mateo heard the small sound of Silas moving—checking stores, checking charts, checking the thousand small systems of departure—and above it all, in his mind, the needle of a brass compass swinging and swinging, unable to settle, pointing at nothing and therefore, maybe, at everything.

"Get some sleep," Juno said quietly. "All of us. Tomorrow we find out what's downstream of everything."

The tide would turn at 5:40. None of them slept much anyway.
