## 7. The Drowned Archive

The river changed its mind about Halcyon Reach three miles before we could see it.

Silas felt it through the deck first—a tremor in the hull, a stutter in the current, like the water itself was hesitating. She'd spent eleven years reading rivers the way other people read faces, and this one was telling her to turn around.

"Current's wrong," Mateo called from the engine housing, echoing her own thought. He had his head half inside the access panel, grease to his elbows, voice pitched over the thrum of the modified outboards. "Sluggish on the starboard side. Like something's pulling against us."

"Sandbar," Juno said, without looking up from the chart table.

"It's not a sandbar."

"It's always a sandbar until it isn't." Juno marked their position with a grease pencil, a small X on a chart that had more X's than honest soundings. "We're four klicks out. You want to turn back, say so now, because after the bend there's no clean water for a retreat."

Nobody said turn back. Nobody ever did. That was the trouble with a crew built out of people who'd each been thrown off somewhere else—retreat sounded too much like the thing that had already happened to them once.

Silas stood at the bow with her father's compass open in her palm, and watched the needle do its slow, sickening drift.

It had started an hour ago. A degree of wobble she'd blamed on the engine's vibration, then two degrees she'd blamed on iron in the hull plating, and now the needle was describing a lazy arc, east to south and back again, like a man pacing a room he couldn't leave. Near Halcyon, Mateo had said last night, turning the compass over in his careful engineer's hands. It points wrong near Halcyon. She hadn't asked how he knew that. She hadn't asked because the answer lived in expedition folklore, in the stories the old crews told with their voices lowered—the compasses that spun at the Reach, the radios that whispered, the instruments that failed in ways no fault-tree could explain. The stories were why the delta community had sworn the place off. The stories were also, if you believed the less charitable version, why her father's boat had gone down with him aboard.

She closed the lid. The needle kept moving anyway; she could feel it, some superstitious pressure against her thumb, which was nonsense, which was exhaustion, which was the third night of bad sleep in a bunk that smelled of bilge and river mold.

"Silas." Juno's voice, flat as the chart table. "Come look at this."

* * *

The chart table was buried under Juno's gear: the depth sounder's readout, a salvaged tablet running hydrographic software older than any of them, and the paper charts Voss had provided—official reproductions, stamped with the Delta Survey Authority's seal, showing the channels around Halcyon Reach as they'd been mapped twenty-two years ago.

Juno laid the tablet beside the paper. On the tablet, the live sonar return painted the riverbed in amber and black. On the paper, the channel ran deep and wide, a clean blue vein leading straight to the station's dock.

They didn't match. They didn't come close to matching.

"The channel's silted," Juno said. "Badly. What the chart calls six meters is reading two-point-one. There's a shoal across the whole approach where the map says open water." She tapped the paper chart with one finger, twice, the way she tapped things when she was keeping her temper on a leash. "This isn't age. Silt doesn't move like this in twenty years, not with the training dikes upstream doing their job. This looks like—" She stopped.

"Like someone redrew it," Silas said.

"Or never surveyed it honestly in the first place."

The words sat between them. Silas looked at the Authority's seal, the crisp blue stamp of official truth, and thought about how many boats had trusted that seal. How many crews had run aground on channels that weren't where the maps promised, and cursed their own seamanship, and paid for salvage out of pocket, and never once thought to blame the paper.

Her father had thought to blame the paper. That was the whole of it, maybe. That was what had killed him—or whatever version of it she could bear to carry.

"We go in shallow," she said. "Cut the engines at the shoal line and pole the last stretch. Mateo, can the hull take contact?"

"The hull can take anything. It's my nerves I'm worried about." Mateo wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag years ago. "I'll rig the skiff. We take her in light—dive gear, cutting tools, comms. Leave the heavy salvage rig aboard."

"And the boat stays ready to run," Juno said. It wasn't a question. It was the rule they'd all agreed to before signing Voss's contract: wheels up, engines warm, nobody sleeps past the point where they couldn't be moving in ninety seconds. The rule existed because of the Syndicate. Silas let herself believe that was the only reason it existed.

* * *

Halcyon Reach came out of the mist the way drowned things do—not all at once but in pieces, as if the river were deciding how much to show them.

First the mast array, a skeleton of rusted lattice rising forty meters above the waterline, guy-wires trailing into the current like hair. Then the roofline of the main block, a long low wedge of concrete and dead solar paneling, its white cladding gone the color of old teeth. Then, as they poled the skiff through water that should have been a channel and wasn't, the whole of it: a research station built on stilts and pilings above the floodplain, designed to ride out a hundred-year flood, now sitting hip-deep in the river's permanent embrace. Half architecture, half reef. Birdlime streaked the upper windows. Below the waterline, Silas knew from the old photographs, the lower vaults went down three levels into the earth—archive vaults, climate-sealed, built to hold data the way a tomb holds kings.

Twenty-two years ago, two hundred people had worked here. Twenty-two years ago, in one night, most of them had stopped.

The skiff bumped against the loading dock, concrete scarred with old mooring rings, and Silas stepped off onto solid ground for the first time since she'd signed Odile Voss's name on a contract, and the ground felt wrong under her boots. Not unstable—wrong. Like standing in a house where someone had recently been.

"Awe," Mateo said quietly, beside her. He was looking up at the mast array. "That's the word you're not saying. I can hear you not saying it."

"I'm saying dread."

"You're saying both. They braid." He shrugged, shouldering the dive bag. "My grandmother used to say that's what holy places feel like. Same wiring, different god."

Silas didn't answer, because the feeling in her chest wasn't fit for words yet. Her father had walked on this dock. She was almost certain of it—certain enough that her boot seemed to find the exact hollows his had worn. He'd stood here with his compass and his notebooks and his terrible, incorruptible habit of checking everything twice, and he'd looked up at that same mast, and whatever he'd found here had gotten him killed, and she had spent half her life pretending the finding of it meant nothing to her.

The station's main entrance gaped at the end of the dock: double doors, glass long since gone, framing a corridor of standing shadow. Water lapped at the threshold, black and patient.

"Comms check," Juno said. "Before anyone goes romantic."

Mateo powered the field radio, thumbed the transmit key. Static. He adjusted the frequency, tried the relay band they'd agreed on with the boat. Static, but strange static—there was a rhythm under it, a pulse, like a carrier wave trying to be born.

"That's interference," he said. "Local source. Something on-site is broadcasting, or something on-site doesn't want us broadcasting." He tried a third band, and this time the radio produced a sound that made every one of them go still: a voice, shredded almost past recognition, repeating a single syllable pattern. Not words. Numbers, maybe. Or a name.

Then it cut out, cleanly, mid-syllable, the way a machine cuts out when a hand finds a switch.

"Tell me that was ghost noise," Juno said.

"Ghosts don't find switches," Silas said.

* * *

Inside, the station breathed.

That was the only word for it. The corridors ran back into the dark with the regularity of lungs, and the air moving through them carried a smell Silas had hoped never to encounter again: standing water over electronics, mineral and sweet-rotten, the perfume of drowned machines. Their headlamps carved cones through the gloom. Water marks climbed the walls in tide-lines, year over year, a geological record of neglect—high-water lines like tree rings, each one a monsoon the station had endured alone.

"Archive wing's this way," Silas said. She'd memorized the floor plan Voss provided, and it matched what she saw, which bothered her more than a mismatch would have. The plan was accurate. Voss had been here, or someone Voss trusted had. "Lower vaults are accessed through the stairwell past the server hall. If the seals held even partially, the paper records might—"

The lights came on.

Not all of them. A strip of emergency fluorescents down the corridor ahead, flickering up in sequence, humming to life with the buzz of old ballards shaking off decades—and dying again just as fast, section by section, as whoever or whatever had woken them thought better of it. In the half-second of illumination, Silas saw the corridor stretch away toward the server hall, and saw, at the far end, a shape that might have been a person step backward out of sight.

Might have. Shadows lied. Her heart didn't care; it slammed like a fist on a door.

"Everyone stop breathing," Juno whispered, which was Juno for *freeze*, and they froze.

Silence. The drip of water. The hum of the dying fluorescents fading to nothing.

Then, from somewhere below—down, beneath their feet, in the flooded levels where the archive vaults lay—a sound rolled up through the structure. Metallic. Deliberate. A hatch closing, or a bulkhead dogging shut, the heavy round *clunk-clunk-clunk* of a wheel lock being turned by someone who knew exactly how it worked.

Mateo's lamp swung to Silas. Juno's hand had found the knife at her belt without her seeming to notice it move. And Silas stood in her father's graveyard, on ground the whole delta had sworn off, listening to the sound of a lock turning in a place that was supposed to be empty, and understood with a clarity that felt like cold water in her lungs:

Voss hadn't sent them to recover an inheritance.

Voss had sent them into a room where someone was already waiting—and whoever it was had known they were coming long enough to prepare a welcome.

"Back to the skiff," Silas said softly. "Now. Slow. Nobody runs until we know what's between us and the dock."

They moved. Behind them, in the dark of the server hall, something began—very faintly, very patiently—to hum a radio back to life.
