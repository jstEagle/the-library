## 8. Company in the Deep

Juno Park had a rule about dark rooms: you counted the exits before you counted anything else. It was a rule she'd learned young, in the border towns where her mother ran cargo and favors, and it had kept her alive through eleven years of navigating for Silas Nakamura, which was eleven years longer than most navigators lasted.

So when the radio in Halcyon Reach's server hall began to hum, Juno's first instinct wasn't fear. It was cartography.

*Exits.* The main corridor behind them—flooded to the knee now, black water carrying the smell of twenty years of drowned drywall. The maintenance shaft Mateo had spotted on the schematics, two meters up and left, its grate rusted but intact. And the far door, the one at the end of the server hall, standing open just wide enough to be an invitation or a threat.

Three exits. Except none of them were exits anymore, because whoever owned that radio owned the room.

"Juno." Silas's voice came low from beside her, barely more than breath. "Light discipline. Kill it."

Juno thumbed off her headlamp. Darkness dropped over them like a lid, total and wet, broken only by the green ghost-glow of the emergency strips along the floor—the same strips that had been freshly powered, freshly maintained, freshly *wrong*. In the dark those strips looked like the spine of some enormous animal sleeping under the water.

The humming resolved into static, then into music. Tinny, distorted by corrosion and cheap speakers, but unmistakable: an old delta folk song, the kind dockworkers sang when the season turned. Someone was playing it deliberately. Someone wanted them to know they'd been heard.

"That's a greeting," Mateo whispered somewhere to her right. His voice had gone tight, which meant his hands were shaking, which meant he was scared, which meant Juno should be too. Mateo Reyes didn't scare easy; he scared *accurately*.

"It's a taunt," Silas said.

"Could be both."

"Move toward it," Silas decided. "If they wanted us dead, we'd already be floating. They want us to walk in with our hands where they can see them. So we walk in."

"And if we're wrong?" That was Wren, their diver, who had said perhaps forty words since they'd entered the Reach and made each one count like ballast.

"Then we're wrong underwater," Silas said, "and it won't matter."

Juno switched her lamp back on, low beam, pointed at the floor. She took point because that was her job—not just charts and currents, but being the first one into every room so that Silas could watch the room instead of watching Juno. Eleven years of trust compressed into a single habit of walking.

They waded down the server hall. The water rose from knee to thigh, cold enough to make her teeth ache. Racks of dead servers stood on either side like headstones, their cables trailing into the flood like kelp. And ahead, through the last doorway, light—warm, electric, *generous*—spilled across the water.

The hall beyond had been the station's operations center, back when Halcyon Reach did science instead of rotting. Now it was something else. Work lights on stands. A folding table with a camp stove. Tarps rigged into dry quarters. A generator purring somewhere behind a bulkhead, fed by a fuel line that someone had run with real competence. Twenty years of drowning, and here was a room that refused to drown—a room that had been *kept*.

Four people stood in it. Five counting the man at the table, who didn't stand at all.

He sat with his boots up on a supply crate, a tin cup in one hand, and he was older than Juno expected—fifty maybe, gray going white at the temples, with the build of a man who'd been heavy once and then spent hard years shaving himself down to function. His face was unremarkable in the way that expensive faces learn to be. He wore no insignia, no colors, nothing that would photograph badly.

But Juno knew the type. Everyone in the Rivergate knew the type. You didn't need a badge when your reputation walked in ahead of you wearing your face.

"Corrigan," Silas said.

Not a question. Which told Juno that Silas had known this name before tonight, and hadn't shared that either. Juno filed the hurt away where she kept all the others. There was a growing drawer of them lately.

"Nakamura." Corrigan set his cup down without spilling a drop, which Juno noted the way she noted current speeds: automatically, professionally. Steady hands. "You're late. We expected you Tuesday."

"We weren't hired by you."

"No," Corrigan agreed pleasantly. "You were hired by Odile Voss, who was hired by us, who was hired by—" he waved the sentence away like smoke "—well. The org chart gets complicated above a certain altitude. Point is, you're here, we're here, and the archive isn't going anywhere. So why don't you all come out of the water and have some coffee before somebody does something everyone regrets?"

Juno glanced back. Silas stood hip-deep in the flooded doorway, and her face was doing the thing it did—the flat, unreadable calm that Juno had learned to read anyway, the way you learn to read a river by what *isn't* moving. Underneath the calm: calculation. Underneath the calculation: fury.

"You said we were alone on site," Silas said, and her voice was aimed past Corrigan, at the air itself, at a woman fifty miles upriver who wasn't there to hear it. "Voss said we were alone."

"Voss says a lot of things." Corrigan spread his hands. "In her defense—and I'm feeling generous tonight—in *our* defense, you were never supposed to find us until we wanted finding. Your equipment failing on approach? That was us. Jamming, mostly. Cheap trick, but effective." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "You came in anyway. I respect that. Stubborn crews are reliable crews."

One of the four standing figures shifted—a big woman with a shaved scalp and a shock baton on her belt, moving half a step to cover the doorway Juno's crew had entered through. Not aggressive. Just *positioned*. Juno marked her as the professional. The other three were muscle with radios, younger, nervous in the way of men who'd been told the job was simple and were beginning to suspect otherwise.

Silas waded forward. Juno followed, because following Silas into bad rooms was also the job.

"Here's my problem, Corrigan," Silas said, stopping at the edge of the work-light glow, water streaming off her gear. "I don't know what you want yet. And in my experience, Syndicate men who want coffee want something worth more than coffee."

"What I want is simple." He poured a second cup from the stove's dented pot and set it on the crate beside him—an offering, or a test. "Cooperation. The archive has a vault level, below the waterline, sealed since the disaster. My team can't crack it. Pressure locks, custom keys, the whole paranoid package. But I've read the personnel files, Nakamura. Your father designed half those locks. And if anyone alive knows how his mind worked—" he tapped the side of his nose "—it's the daughter who grew up listening to him talk shop over dinner."

The name landed in the room like a stone in still water. Juno saw Wren go rigid. Saw Mateo's jaw tighten. Nobody on the crew said *Nakamura's father* out loud, ever, not after the oath, not after the funerals that had no bodies. Hearing it from a stranger's mouth felt like trespass.

"My father's dead," Silas said evenly.

"His work isn't." Corrigan shrugged. "Dead men leave combinations behind. So: you help us open the vault, we split the salvage per a schedule I'll draw up, everybody gets paid, everybody goes home rich, and nobody has to spend another night in this beautiful aquarium. That's the offer."

"And if we decline?"

Corrigan picked his cup back up. Sipped. Looked at her over the rim with mild, patient eyes.

"Then you decline," he said. "I'm not a monster, Nakamura. But I'd think about the tide tables. This building's got maybe four days of structural honesty left in it, and the monsoon's coming early this year—you've seen the satellite feeds, same as we have. Four days, five if the river's kind. You can spend them fighting me, or working with me. Either way, we're all leaving together or not at all."

*We were never hired alone.*

The thought arrived in Juno's chest with the weight of certainty, and it rearranged everything. Voss's contract, Voss's urgency, Voss's insistence that they move fast and travel light and tell no one—all of it had been theater staged for an audience of one crew. They hadn't been sent to recover an inheritance. They'd been sent to be *labor*. Divers and lockbreakers, deniable and disposable, working alongside—or beneath—a Syndicate team that had been on site long enough to install a generator.

And Silas had signed them onto it. Had signed them onto it knowing—no. Juno watched her captain's face and revised. Silas hadn't known about Corrigan. But Silas had known *something*, had known enough that the contract's shape hadn't surprised her the way it should have, and the difference between those two things was a door Juno intended to walk through later, at volume.

For now: exits. Still three. Still all bad.

"Give us the night," Silas said.

Corrigan raised his eyebrows, delighted. "The night?"

"To talk it over. Crew decision. That's how we work."

"That's charming." He considered, then nodded at the big woman with the baton. "Kestrel will show you to the east wing. Dry bunks, working air scrubbers—we keep the generator honest. Consider it hospitality." His smile thinned by a single degree, and suddenly Juno could see the enforcement officer underneath the reasonable man, the way you see the rebar in concrete when the surface cracks. "Also consider it logistics. The east wing's the only dry space left, and the only way out of the building passes through this room. So whatever you decide by morning, you'll decide it nearby."

* * *

The east wing had been the researchers' quarters, once. Bunks bolted to walls, lockers rusted shut, a window that showed nothing but black water pressing against the glass like a palm. Kestrel installed herself outside their door without being asked, and her shadow under the doorframe was as good as a wall.

Mateo checked their own gear with the grim thoroughness of a man counting his remaining friends. "Comms are dead. Jammed, probably, same as on approach. Boat's tied at the dock, and the dock's visible from their ops room. We're not sneaking anywhere."

"So we don't sneak," Wren said. "We dive the lower levels ourselves, tonight, while they sleep."

"They're not sleeping," Juno said. "Corrigan doesn't strike me as a man who sleeps first."

Wren shot her a look. "You've met his type before."

"I've *navigated* around his type before. Different skill."

Silas hadn't spoken since the ops hall. She sat on a bunk with her father's compass open in her palm, turning it over, and Juno watched her do it and understood, finally, that the compass wasn't a keepsake. It was a question she kept asking and it kept answering wrong, and she couldn't stop asking.

"Silas." Juno crossed the room and crouched so they were eye to eye. "Talk to me. Not the captain voice. The voice you use when it's three a.m. and the chart's wrong and we're both tired enough to be honest."

Silas looked up. For a moment something moved behind her eyes—something old and exhausted, a woman carrying a weight she'd been pretending was luggage.

"He knew Dad's name," Silas said quietly. "Corrigan. He didn't read it off a personnel file today. He said it like a man who'd studied it. Which means the Syndicate's been interested in Halcyon for a long time, and interested in *my family* for longer, and Voss—" Her hand closed over the compass. "Voss sold us the moment she wrote that contract. Maybe she sold us before that."

"Then we leave. We swim out, we take our losses, we let the Syndicate have their vault."

"With what boat? With what comms?" Silas shook her head. "No. We stay. We play cooperative. And tomorrow I get inside that vault before Corrigan does, because whatever's down there, he wants it bad enough to fake a truce for, and I want to know why."

"Why?" Juno held her gaze. "Or *whether*? Because those aren't the same mission, Captain, and you've been running them both since Voss walked into our lives, and I'm done pretending I haven't noticed."

The silence stretched. Outside the door, Kestrel's shadow didn't move. Water ticked against the window. Somewhere deep in the building's bones, the river groaned through a joint the way a ship groans in weather.

"I'll tell you everything," Silas said at last. "I swear it, Juno. But not tonight. Tonight I need you to trust me one more day on credit."

Juno stood. Her knees ached. Everything ached.

"One day," she said. "Then I stop being your navigator and start being your ex-navigator, and believe me, I know channels out of this delta that don't appear on any chart you've ever seen."

She turned to check the door seal, and that was when Wren, who had drifted to the window, said in a voice gone very quiet:

"Someone's diving."

They crowded to the glass. Below, in the floodlit murk beyond the dock, lights were moving—two, three points of pale green, descending along the sunken superstructure toward the lower levels. Toward the vault. Corrigan's people, working the night shift.

Except one of the lights wasn't moving like the others. It had separated from the group, drifting low and slow along the seabed, heading not for the vault but for the collapsed research wing on the structure's far side—the wing that, according to every schematic Juno had memorized, contained nothing but wreckage.

Nothing but wreckage, and a sealed personal locker that a dead man had once told his daughter about, in the way fathers tell daughters things, in code, over dinner, years before anyone knew the words would matter.

Juno looked at Silas. Silas was already pulling on her fins.

"That's not Corrigan's diver," Silas said.

"How do you know?"

"Because I know where they're going." She cinched the strap tight, and her face in the dim light was the calmest Juno had ever seen it, which was how Juno knew they were all in terrible danger. "And there's only one person alive who knows to look there. Get Wren. Tell her we're diving in ten minutes—and Juno? Whatever happens down there, Corrigan can't see us leave."

Juno was already moving. Behind them, faint through the hull, the river kept rising, patient as a debt.
