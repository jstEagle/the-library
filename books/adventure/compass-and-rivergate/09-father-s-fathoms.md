## 9. Father's Fathoms

The lower vault was where the river kept its secrets, and the river had been keeping this one for eleven years.

Silas hung in the green murk above the flooded stairwell, her rebreather hissing its slow metronome against her ears, and looked down into a darkness that had once swallowed her father whole. The water here was different from the surface channels—colder, heavier, tasting of iron and old concrete when it seeped past her mask's edge. Below her, the emergency lights of Halcyon Reach still glowed on their backup cells, dim amber rectangles sunk in black like the windows of a drowned house where someone might still be home.

*Someone is home,* she thought. *We've known that since Mateo's gear started failing. The question is whether they're watching us right now.*

She pushed the thought down with the rest of them and kicked for the stairs.

* * *

They'd found the vault entrance on the second day, behind what had been the station's hydrology lab—a door marked LOWER ARCHIVE: AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY, its seal broken long ago by hands that hadn't bothered to hide the fact. Corrigan's team had already been through the upper levels. That much was obvious from the wreckage: filing cabinets gutted and overturned, data cores pried from their cradles, the particular chaos of people searching for something specific and not finding it.

But the lower vault was another matter. The stairwell had flooded to within four meters of the ceiling, and the water down there moved like something with intentions. Juno had mapped the currents from the surface using sonar pings, her face grim over the display.

"There's a flow-through," she said. "Water's moving through the vault at maybe half a knot, coming from somewhere below the foundation. Which means there's an opening we can't see, which means—" she tapped the screen where a smudge of return echo refused to resolve, "—there could be a second way in that isn't ours."

"Or a way out," Mateo said.

"Or a throat," Silas said, and nobody laughed, because they'd all been thinking it.

The plan they'd settled on was simple and bad: Silas would dive alone. It wasn't bravado, whatever Juno's expression suggested. It was arithmetic. One diver meant one set of bubbles, one light signature, one person to lose if the vault decided to keep what it caught. And more than that—it was the only part of this job that had ever truly been hers.

Because Silas Nakamura knew Halcyon Reach the way you know a house you grew up in. Not the station as it stood now, drowned and rotting, but the station as it had been drawn in her father's blueprints, spread across their kitchen table when she was nine years old while he explained load tolerances with crayon marks on the margins. Kenji Nakamura had helped design the archive's lower levels. He'd walked her through every corridor in his careful, patient voice, drawing cross-sections on napkins, on scrap paper, on the fogged window of their truck during rain delays.

*If anything ever happens to me at the Reach,* he'd told her once, only once, in a voice she'd never heard him use before or since, *you look where I showed you. Nowhere else. Promise me.*

She'd promised. She'd been twelve years old, and she'd promised, and then he'd died there anyway, and the promise had sat inside her for eleven years like a splinter working its way toward the heart.

* * *

The stairwell swallowed her by degrees.

First her light lost the surface—looking up, she could see it only as a pale wound of gray far above, the entry hole shrinking to a coin, then a pinprick. Then the temperature dropped so sharply her suit's thermal readout chimed a warning she silenced. Then the dark changed quality entirely: not empty dark but *occupied* dark, the kind that pressed against her lamp beam like something curious.

She descended along the handrail, counting landings. Her father's blueprints said seven flights to the vault floor. At the fourth landing, the corridor mouth yawned to her left, choked with silt and a drift of paper that had gone to pulp—decades of hydrological surveys dissolving back into the water they'd described. She didn't let herself look long. Looking long was how divers died in places like this; the mind reached for the rooms, and the body followed.

At the sixth landing, her compass did something wrong.

She'd felt it coming before she saw it—the faint wrongness in her inner ear, the sense of being slightly turned even though she hung motionless. When she checked the instrument on her wrist, the needle had swung forty degrees off true north and was trembling there, quivering like a trapped insect. Near Halcyon, her father's old compass had always done this. She carried his compass now, the brass one with the dented case, and near the Reach it lied to her the way it had always lied, the way it had lied the day of the disaster when search teams' instruments spun and stuttered and no one could say why.

*There's something under the station,* her father had written in one of the letters she'd memorized and burned. *Something in the geology. The charts don't account for it because the charts weren't made to account for anything. Remember that phrasing, Silas. I chose it carefully.*

She forced herself past the sixth landing without stopping, though every instinct screamed at her to turn around, and dropped into the vault.

* * *

The lower archive opened beneath her like a cathedral that had committed a crime.

Her lamp swept across row after row of shelving, industrial steel rated for two hundred years of service, now furred with silt and draped in the white filaments of bacteria that bloomed in cold, still water. Between the rows, sediment lay in smooth dunes, sculpted by the slow flow-through Juno had mapped. Data cores sat in their wall cradles like reliquaries, most of them shattered or stripped, but some intact—sealed against exactly this fate, designed by people who had honestly believed the knowledge they preserved deserved to outlast the building.

And everywhere, the amber emergency lights, casting everything in the color of old photographs.

Silas swam the main aisle slowly, checking the damage pattern. Corrigan's team had been here too—but differently. They hadn't ransacked the lower vault the way they'd torn apart the upper floors. They'd searched it *selectively*, she realized. Certain shelves were disturbed; others wore their silt undisturbed, a decade-thick blanket of gray that no one had brushed. They'd been looking for something specific, following some index or inventory, and whatever they wanted, they hadn't found it—or hadn't found all of it.

Her father's name was on three of the shelf labels. NAKAMURA, K. — SURVEY SERIES 4 through 6. The folders were there, waterlogged but legible, full of bathymetric readings and channel-flow measurements in his small, precise handwriting. She took Series 5, the monsoon-season surveys, and tucked it into her dive bag with hands that stayed steady through sheer discipline. This was salvageable evidence. This was the shape of the thing Voss had sent them to find.

But it wasn't what her father had meant.

*You look where I showed you. Nowhere else.*

She hung in the aisle, breathing, and made herself think instead of feel. Where had he shown her? Not told—shown. She'd been twelve, and he'd been drawing on the kitchen table, and she could see the napkin in her memory as clearly as if it floated in front of her lamp: a cross-section of the lower vault, seven rows of shelving, and in the margin, in red crayon, a small circle drawn at the base of the fourth row. She'd asked him what it was. He'd said, *That's where the building keeps its conscience.*

She'd thought it was a joke. She'd laughed. He'd watched her laugh with an expression she hadn't understood until eleven years too late.

Row four.

She swam to it. The shelving here was intact, undisturbed, wearing its full coat of silt—and at the base, where the uprights met the floor anchors, there was a maintenance panel. Standard issue, except that standard panels opened with a quarter-turn latch, and this one had been welded shut. Welded recently? No. The weld was old, corroded, done in haste with portable equipment—done, she understood with a lurch, by someone working late at night who needed the panel never to open again, or never to open until the right person came looking.

Her father had been many things, but sloppy with tools was not one of them. A weld like this was a message. A weld like this said: *forced entry required.* A weld like this said: *I want whoever opens this to know it was sealed deliberately, and to wonder why.*

She pulled the cutting torch from her thigh mount, and her hands, steady through all of it, finally shook.

* * *

The panel gave way in a bloom of orange sparks and brown cloud. Behind it: a cavity between the structural members, dry-ish, lined with plastic sheeting that had yellowed but held. And inside the sheeting, wrapped in a second layer, in a waterproof document tube of the kind surveyors used—

A logbook.

Not an official one. Official logs lived in the archives, catalogued and indexed. This was a personal book, cheap-bound, the cover cracked, and on the flyleaf in her father's handwriting: *K. Nakamura — personal — if found outside my hands, contact S.N. Only S.N.*

Only S.N. Only her.

Silas held the tube against her chest plate and felt her breath go ragged on the rebreather, fogging the mask's lower edge, and for a moment she was not a diver in a drowned vault but a twelve-year-old girl at a kitchen table, and the dead man was speaking through paper directly into her hands.

She stowed it and began the ascent, and it was only at the fifth landing, in the black water with her father's compass spinning gently on her wrist, that she allowed herself to open the tube's end cap and slide the logbook free enough to read by lamplight.

The early entries were ordinary. Supply inventories. Equipment notes. A complaint about the coffee. Then, dated six weeks before the disaster, the handwriting changed—tighter, faster, pressing hard enough to emboss the next page:

*Cross-checked the Reach soundings against the published delta charts today. They don't match. They don't come close. The official flood projections show the eastern channels carrying capacity they simply do not have. Someone has revised the baselines. If the monsoon comes in heavy, the towns downstream of the Reach—Rivergate proper, the ferry settlements, all of them—are going to flood to depths the evacuation maps say are impossible.*

*I took it upstairs. I was told the discrepancy is a rounding artifact. Eleven meters is not a rounding artifact.*

*I'm making copies. I don't know yet who I can trust, so I'm trusting paper.*

Silas's vision blurred behind her mask. She blinked it clear and turned pages with numb fingers, following his handwriting down the weeks toward the disaster date, each entry shorter, darker, more hurried:

*Copy hidden. Second copy hidden elsewhere. Third copy—*

The page ended mid-sentence. She turned it.

The next page was gone. Torn out at the spine, cleanly, deliberately. So was the one after. She flipped forward, faster now, lamp beam shaking—the entries resumed three pages later, fragmentary, coded, references to "the third location" and "what M. agreed to carry," and then the final entry, dated the day before the disaster:

*If I'm right about why the schedule moved up, then someone knows what I know. Keep your head down tomorrow, Kenji. Watch the weather line. Trust the girl.*

*Trust the girl.*

He'd died the next day. The official finding was equipment failure during a routine sounding run. Eleven years of expedition-community silence had buried the question alongside him, wrapped in the oath everyone surviving had sworn: *the Reach is closed, the Reach is grief, we do not go back, we do not dig.*

She'd sworn it too. She was down here breaking it, holding proof that the oath had been built on a lie—or worse, on a truth someone had paid to bury.

And the missing pages—the ones that named the third hiding place, the ones that named who M. was, the ones that might have named who moved the disaster schedule—

Were gone. Torn out. By her father? To protect them, scattered separately? Or by someone else, sometime in the eleven years the vault had belonged to the dark?

Or by whoever else was on this site right now, searching selectively, following an index, tearing out precisely what they needed removed.

Silas closed the logbook, sealed the tube, and hung alone in the black water of the sixth landing, listening to her own breathing and the distant groan of a drowning building, and understood with total clarity that she was not the first person to find her father's words.

But she intended to be the last person to let them be taken.

Above her, far up the stairwell, her tether line twitched twice—Juno's signal. Trouble on the surface. Trouble, or company.

She started swimming.
