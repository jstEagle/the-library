## 10. Voss's Version

Odile Voss had learned long ago that the most dangerous lies were the ones you told yourself first.

She stood at the window of her suite aboard the *Meridian Grace*, watching the Rivergate Delta unspool beneath her in braided silver and green. The airship was a Syndicate courtesy—loaned, like everything else in her life, against future payment. Three hundred meters below, fishing boats stitched their patient lines across the channels, and the towns sat where the official charts said the water could never reach.

Her grandfather had drawn those charts.

Not personally, of course. Aldous Voss had been a cartographer of reputation, a man whose name still appeared in the prefaces of hydrographic surveys across three nations. But it had been his firm—Voss Hydrographic, his seal, his signature on the certification page—that had taken the Syndicate's money in the year Odile was six years old and quietly redrawn the flood baselines of the entire lower delta. Two meters here. A meter and a half there. Enough to move the hundred-year floodplain off the land where the Syndicate wanted towns built, levees sold, insurance underwritten. Enough to make worthless marshland into bankable ground.

Enough, eventually, to kill people. Though that part took decades to arrive, arriving as it did one drowned village at a time, each loss attributed to weather, to chance, to the capricious cruelty of rivers.

Odile turned from the window and looked at the contract spread across her writing desk. Silas Nakamura's signature was still fresh enough that she could smell the ink.

*Recover sealed inheritance from Halcyon Reach.* That was what the document said. What it always said, in every version she'd shown, because a lie repeated with sufficient confidence became indistinguishable from fact—even to the liar. Especially to the liar.

She poured herself two fingers of plum brandy and did not drink it. The glass was for her hands, something to hold while the truth moved through her the way cold water moves through a hull: slowly, then all at once.

The truth was this: she didn't want the archive recovered. She wanted it erased.

* * *

Her father had told her everything eleven years ago, the night before he died. Emeric Voss had been a gentler man than his father—a surveyor who loved the mathematics of water, who kept tide tables by his bed the way other men kept prayer books. He had discovered the falsification himself at forty-two, cross-referencing old gauge readings against the published charts, and he had done nothing. That was the wound he'd carried to his grave: not the discovery, but the silence after it.

"You'll understand when you're older," he'd said, though she'd been twenty-six and understood perfectly well even then. "The name is everything. The name is the only thing that survives us. If I tear it down, I tear down your mother, your brother, the institute, the scholarships, all of it. And the charts don't change back. The water doesn't care about our shame."

"The water cares," Odile had said. "That's rather the point."

He'd looked at her then with an expression she had spent eleven years trying to forget—not anger, not disappointment, but recognition. He'd seen himself in her, the same hunger wearing a different face, and he'd known exactly what she would become.

"Then be careful," he'd said. "Because if you ever try to fix it, you will have to choose which version of yourself survives. And I promise you, Odile, they are not the same person."

He'd died the next morning of a heart that simply stopped, and Odile had spent the following decade building herself into someone who never had to make that choice—or so she'd believed. She'd rebuilt the family name into something shinier than cartography: expedition financing, salvage brokerage, the glamorous architecture of other people's discoveries. She'd made herself indispensable to the Meridian Syndicate, the way her grandfather had before her, trading access for protection. The Syndicate liked having a Voss on retainer. It made their history look respectable.

And then, four months ago, a data clerk in the Syndicate's own archives had found Aldous Voss's working papers—the drafts, the corrections, the original gauge readings with the falsified figures written over them in her grandfather's unmistakable hand—and the whole careful edifice had begun to tremble.

The clerk had been reassigned. The papers had been marked for destruction. But destruction required certainty, and certainty required knowing whether copies existed elsewhere. Which meant finding out what Elias Nakamura had actually pulled from the Halcyon Reach archive on the last day of his life.

Which meant, eventually, finding Silas Nakamura.

* * *

Odile finally drank the brandy and let it burn.

She thought about Silas the way she thought about all her oldest mistakes: with a tenderness that was almost indistinguishable from contempt. They had been friends once—real friends, the kind forged in the specific fire of being young and brilliant and broke together, sharing a rented room above a chandlery in Port Meridian, splitting a single ration of dried fish and arguing about tide mechanics until dawn. Odile had been the theorist; Silas had been the instinct. Between them they'd mapped three uncharted channels before either turned twenty-five, and Odile had genuinely believed they would spend their lives doing it.

Then Halcyon Reach. Then the storm. Then Elias Nakamura's last transmission cutting off mid-sentence, and the oath, and the funerals, and the slow poison of the inquiry that followed—the official finding that the station's structural survey had been signed off by Voss Hydrographic, that the flood modeling used to site the archive had come from Voss charts, that the disaster was, in the driest possible language, *an act of nature compounded by certified engineering.*

Silas had looked at her across the memorial hall with eyes full of a grief so total it had no room left for anything else, and Odile had understood that some doors close permanently. She'd let them close. It had seemed merciful at the time—for both of them.

Now she needed that door open, and she hated herself for the precision of her own timing.

* * *

There was a knock, and Corrigan entered without waiting.

He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with the unhurried manner of someone who had never once needed to raise his voice because other people raised theirs for him. He wore the Syndicate's gray the way clergy wore vestments. Odile had brokered contracts alongside him for six years and trusted him exactly as far as the water line outside the window.

"The Nakamura woman signed," he said, settling into the chair across from her desk without invitation. "Your instinct was right. She'd have refused anything else. Pride is useful that way—you just have to know which lever it's attached to."

"She's not motivated by pride," Odile said. "She's motivated by debt. Pride is just how she carries it."

Corrigan smiled, which he did rarely and never warmly. "Same thing, downstream." He produced a slate and set it on the desk between them. On it glowed a copy of the recovery manifest—the list of items specified in the contract Odile had drafted. Sealed inheritance. Personal effects. Archival materials. All of it deliberately vague, all of it funneling toward a single clause buried on the fourth page: *all recovered documents to be surrendered intact to the contracting party for private verification prior to any public disposition.*

Private verification. That was the phrase doing all the work. Once the logbook—Elias Nakamura's logbook, if it existed, and Odile's researchers were now ninety percent certain it did—once it passed into her hands, it would pass into a furnace, and the last physical proof of what her family had done would go up as smoke over the delta while the monsoon season came and went and the charts stayed wrong and the towns stayed put.

"And if she reads it first?" Odile asked. She hadn't meant to ask aloud. The question had been sitting in her chest for days, pressing.

Corrigan looked at her with mild interest, the way a man looks at weather. "Reads what?"

"The logbook. If it exists. If she finds it. She's not stupid, Corrigan. She grew up on her father's knee reading gauge tables. If there are falsified numbers in that vault, she'll recognize them faster than anyone alive."

"Then we're fortunate," Corrigan said, "that the contract specifies surrender before public disposition. And more fortunate still that the Syndicate has already placed its own team inside the Reach." He tapped the slate, and a second file opened: personnel rosters, dive certifications, a supply manifest for a salvage vessel called the *Iron Meridian*. "Corrigan's team deploys tomorrow. We'll be on site before she is. Whatever's down there, we inventory it first. Anything sensitive gets handled before the Nakamura woman ever sees it."

*Handled.* Odile held the word up to the light and examined it. It had a great many meanings, and none of them were gentle.

"You said your team would provide security," she said. "Discreet support. That was the arrangement."

"The arrangement evolved." Corrigan rose, smoothing his coat. "The Syndicate's position has refined since the clerk incident. We prefer redundancy. You should too, Voss. Your name is on those charts. Ours is only on the money. When the water rises, paper drowns slower than ink—but it does drown." At the door he paused. "One more thing. The weather service revised the monsoon track this morning. Consensus models still show landfall south of the delta. But there's a minority projection—" he glanced back at her, and for a moment something almost human crossed his face, something that might have been unease, "—a minority projection that puts it directly over the lower basin. Early. Days early."

"That's absurd. The charts—"

"Yes," Corrigan said softly. "The charts."

He left. Odile stood alone with her empty glass and felt the floor of her certainty tilt by a single degree, the way a ship tilts when it takes on water somewhere below the waterline—invisible, deniable, and absolutely fatal if ignored.

* * *

She worked until midnight, because work was the only prayer she knew.

The suite's desk lamp carved a yellow island out of the dark, and on it she arranged the artifacts of her double life like a surgeon laying out instruments: the contract, the manifest, her grandfather's certification seal in its velvet case, and beneath everything, folded into quarters, a photograph taken thirty-one years ago at the Voss Hydrographic offices. Her grandfather stood at the center, hand resting proudly on a drafting table, flanked by Syndicate directors whose names still appeared on charitable foundations. In the corner, half out of frame, a young surveyor held a rolled chart against his chest like a shield. Emeric. Her father, already complicit, already silent, already dying by inches.

She had built her life on the theory that inheritance was a debt like any other—one could service it, restructure it, pay it down over time without ever looking at the principal. She had financed expeditions to prove that a Voss could put good things into the world. Scholarships. Restoration projects. A reef survey that had gotten a lagoon protected. Small goodnesses, purchased at interest.

But the principal remained. Two meters of redrawn baseline. Every town sitting inside the true floodplain, insured against a risk that had been edited out of existence. And somewhere in the drowned dark of Halcyon Reach, possibly, the arithmetic that proved it all, written in her grandfather's hand.

Destroy it, and the name survived. The name was everything. Her father had said so, and her grandfather had bet lives on it, and the Syndicate had built a fortune on the assumption that no one would ever choose truth over legacy.

Expose it, and—

And what? She tried to construct the sentence and found she couldn't finish it. Expose it, and the name burned. Expose it, and the towns had a chance. Expose it, and Silas Nakamura would look at her across whatever remained between them and see, finally, the whole of what she was.

Odile pressed her palms flat against the photograph until her grandfather's face disappeared under her hands.

There was a third option, of course. There was always a third option, and it was the one she'd already chosen, already paid for, already set in motion: let others do the destroying. Let Corrigan handle it. Keep her own hands clean enough to swear innocence later, if innocence was ever demanded. Ambition had carried her this far on exactly that logic—delegate the rot, retain the fruit.

She told herself it would work. She was very good at telling herself things.

Outside, far to the south, lightning flickered along the horizon in silent, rhythmic pulses, like a pulse being counted by something patient. The monsoon was still supposed to be a week away and a hundred kilometers east. The lightning disagreed.

Odile watched it for a long time, and for the first time in eleven years, she allowed herself to think the thought she'd forbidden: *my grandfather drew the maps these people are trusting tonight.*

She closed the curtains on it.

In the morning she would board a tender downriver to Halcyon Reach, ostensibly to oversee the recovery she had contracted, actually to supervise its erasure. She would smile at Silas Nakamura across a deck rail and call her an old friend, and every word would be both a lie and, in some rotten chamber of her heart, a wish.

The lightning flickered again behind the curtains, closer now, and Odile Voss slept badly, dreaming of water rising over charts that refused to change.
