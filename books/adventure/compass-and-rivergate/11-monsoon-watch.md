## 11. Monsoon Watch

Mateo Reyes had a superstition about weather satellites: he never trusted them until they told him something he wanted to hear. It wasn't rational. Satellites didn't care what he wanted. But twenty years of rigging engines and reading skies over the Rivergate Delta had taught him that the machines that watched from orbit were like the Syndicate's accountants—precise, indifferent, and always delivering bad news with perfect calm.

So when the uplink finished its crawl at 0400 and the composite image resolved on the boat's salt-fogged screen, he sat very still for a long moment, coffee going cold in his hand, and let himself hope he was misreading it.

He wasn't.

The monsoon cell—the one every forecast in the delta had pinned for landfall nine days out, swinging wide east of Rivergate like it always did—had turned.

Not drifted. Turned. The storm's track bent southwest across three consecutive satellite passes, a clean elbow of cloud aimed straight down the delta's throat, and the pressure readings underneath it were wrong in the way that made Mateo's back teeth ache. He'd ridden out eleven named systems in this boat. He knew what a monsoon looked like when it was being polite. This was not that. This was the sky rolling up its sleeves.

"Jun," he said quietly, because Juno Park slept lightest and woke angriest, and he needed her angry right now. "Jun. Get up."

From the aft bunk came a sound like a knife being drawn. "If this is about the bilge pump again—"

"It's about the end of the world. Slightly bigger."

* * *

By 0430 all four of them were crammed into the wheelhouse, which was built for two people and a chart table and currently smelled of wet rope, engine grease, and Silas's black coffee, which she drank like it was a debt she intended to collect. Rain ticked against the glass in fat, patient drops. Outside, Halcyon Reach rose out of the dark water half a mile off their bow—a drowned research station, its upper floors jutting from the floodplain like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die and changed its mind halfway.

Nobody was looking at it. Everybody was looking at the screen.

Mateo walked them through it the way he always did, finger tracing the storm's track, because if he treated it as data instead of dread his hands stayed steady. "Three passes, six hours apart. Consistent turn. Central pressure's dropped fourteen millibars since yesterday's fix, and the feeder bands are wrapping tighter than anything I've seen since—" He stopped. There was no point finishing that sentence. Everyone in the delta knew which storm he meant. "It's coming here. Not east of here. Here. And it's early."

"How early?" Silas asked.

"That's the ugly part." He pulled up the model runs, four thin lines fanning across the map like spilled thread. "Official forecast says nine days. These tracks say five. Maybe four, if the steering current keeps doing what it's doing. The charts everyone downriver is using—the ones off the government feed—they've got the landfall window so far east it might as well be another country. People are going to hear 'nine days' and 'east' and go to bed."

Silence, except for the rain.

Juno spoke first, arms crossed, her voice flat in the way Mateo had learned meant she'd already done the math twice and hated both answers. "Five days. We haven't even found the vault yet. We're diving a flooded archive with a rival crew upstairs and sabotaged comms, and you're telling me the river's going to come up around our ears before we finish the job."

"I'm telling you the river doesn't care about the job."

"Then we turn back." Juno said it fast, like pulling a tooth. "We take whatever Voss's contract penalty is, we run upstream ahead of the surge, we live to argue about money another day. That's the play. That's the only play."

"We can't outrun it anyway," Mateo said. Not cruelly. Just honestly, because someone had to be honest or the argument would rot. "Look at the track. If we leave now, we make Rivergate in maybe three days with the current against us and the wind turning on our nose by tomorrow night. If we stay, we're sitting inside a concrete structure that's survived one flood already. I'm not saying staying is smart. I'm saying running isn't the escape hatch everybody thinks it is."

"So we're choosing between drowning in the open and drowning in a filing cabinet," Juno said. "Fantastic. Great expedition. Five stars."

"Nobody's drowning," Silas said.

Mateo looked at her. She hadn't moved from the doorway—she rarely stood in the middle of rooms, he'd noticed; she stood where she could see exits—and her face had that quality it got sometimes, the stillness of deep water. Behind her, half-asleep in the corner with a wrench still in one hand, Mateo Reyes's other problem stirred: young Theo, the deckhand they'd picked up in Rivergate, nineteen years old and all elbows and questions, blinking awake now with the particular terror of someone who has just understood that the adults are frightened.

"Theo," Mateo said gently, "why don't you check the anchor line."

Theo checked the anchor line. He moved like a man escaping a burning building at a polite walking pace.

* * *

The argument lasted an hour, and Mateo let it happen, because he'd learned long ago that a crew that doesn't argue is a crew keeping secrets, and secrets on a boat are ballast you can't dump.

Juno laid out the case for retreat with navigator's precision: fuel margins, tide windows, the sabotage Corrigan's people had done to their comms array, the fact that Voss's contract paid on delivery of archive contents, not on heroism. "We're not obligated to die for her inheritance," she said. "Whatever's down there has been underwater for fifteen years. It'll survive five more days. We won't, necessarily."

"And if it doesn't?" Silas asked.

That stopped everyone. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

"If the surge takes the lower vault," Silas went on, and her voice was careful, each word set down like a foot on ice, "whatever my father sealed in there goes into the river for good. Paper doesn't survive that. Data cores don't survive that. Fifteen years it waited, and we're the first ones who could reach it, and if we walk away because the timing got inconvenient—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Everyone aboard knew what lived under that sentence: a transmission from a dying man, an oath sworn over his absence, a daughter who had spent half her life pretending the wound was a scar.

Mateo watched Juno watch Silas, and saw the navigator do the thing she did—weigh loyalty against survival on scales only she could see, her jaw working, her eyes narrow. Juno had signed on to chase storms for hire, not to dig through a dead man's secrets. But she'd also signed on for Silas, in the wordless way crews sign on for each other, and Mateo could see the exact moment the second weight tipped the pan.

"You know something," Juno said quietly. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse than that. It was an observation. "You know what's down there. Or you know enough. And you've known since Voss walked onto the dock."

Silas said nothing.

"Silas."

"I know my father thought it was worth dying for," Silas said. "That's what I know. And I know that if we leave now, we'll never know whether he was right."

The wheelhouse hummed. The generator cycled. Out on the black water, lightning flickered inside the distant cloud mass—not a strike, just the storm clearing its throat, testing its voice.

Mateo cleared his own throat. "For what it's worth," he said, "the structure will hold. Halcyon was built to ride out a hundred-year flood. The lower levels are the risk—if the surge tops the old containment bulkheads, the vault floods, and there's no diving that once the current gets into the corridors. So it's not five days we have. It's however long it takes the river to rise past the third sill. Call it seventy-two hours after landfall starts, best case. Worst case—"

"Worst case, we're inside it when it happens," Juno said.

"Worst case," Mateo agreed, "we're inside it when it happens."

He expected Juno to fight. Instead she laughed—one short, sharp syllable with no humor in it at all—and dragged both hands down her face. "I left a good job cartography firm for this," she told the ceiling. "Stable salary. Climate control. Nobody's dead father."

"You love it," Mateo said.

"I hate how much I love it," she said, and that, Mateo thought, was as close to a yes as anyone was getting tonight.

* * *

They voted, because Silas insisted on votes even when everyone knew how she'd vote herself—another thing Mateo had catalogued about her over the years, the way she manufactured consent like a woman building a raft out of whatever agreement she could find. Three to one. Theo, who technically shouldn't have had a vote and who looked like he wanted to be anywhere else, raised his hand for staying with the desperate earnestness of youth, and then went pale when he realized what he'd voted for.

Juno cast the one against, then started plotting dive schedules before the vote finished, because that was Juno: she argued with everything and abandoned nothing.

Mateo went below to the engine room, because bad news always made him want to put his hands on something honest, and the engine was the most honest thing he owned. He checked the mounts, the intake screens, the bilge alarms. He double-lashed the spare fuel drums. He pulled the emergency pack from behind the tool locker and inventoried it by feel in the dark: flares, water bricks, the hand-crank radio they'd been using since Corrigan's people gutted the main array. The crank handle was cold and worn smooth. He turned it a few times, listening to the little dynamo whir, thinking about signals.

One radio. One antenna. A storm coming five days early, aimed at a delta full of towns whose flood maps were—according to a dead man's logbook nobody had fully read yet—lies.

The thought arrived the way the worst thoughts do, quietly, dressed as a question: *if the charts are false, and the storm turns early, who tells them?*

He climbed back up into the grey pre-dawn and found Silas alone at the bow, her father's compass open in her cupped hands. Mateo had seen the thing a dozen times—brass, dented, older than any of them—but he'd never seen it behave the way it behaved out here. Near Halcyon the needle didn't settle. It shivered, swung wide, hunted for a north that wasn't there, like an animal smelling smoke.

"Still lying to you?" Mateo asked, settling beside her against the rail.

"Or telling me something truer than north." Silas closed the lid with a click. Beyond her shoulder, the drowned station stood against the bruised horizon, windows dark, water lapping at the third-floor sills. "My father carried this on every expedition. He used to say a compass doesn't point home. It points away from everything that isn't home, and you do the rest yourself."

"He sounds like he talked a lot."

"He never shut up." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. Then it faded, and she looked at Mateo with the directness that always made him feel like a chart being read. "You think I'm making a mistake. Taking us forward."

Mateo considered lying. Decided against it; she'd smell it. "I think the storm doesn't care what we choose, and I think you know something you're not saying, and I think those two facts together are more dangerous than the weather." He held up a hand before she could answer. "But I rigged this boat, and I'll keep it floating as long as physics allows, and physics allows more than most people think. So. Forward. Fine. But Silas—when we're inside that vault, and you find whatever it is you're looking for—you tell us. All of it. No more standing in doorways."

For a long moment she said nothing. The rain thickened, soft and steady, the leading edge of something enormous still far off but no longer far enough.

"Deal," she said finally.

And that was when the hand-crank radio in Mateo's pocket crackled to life on its own—no, not on its own; someone was transmitting, weak and stuttering through the interference, a signal bouncing off the storm itself. Mateo fumbled it out, thumbed the volume up, and the wheelhouse filled with a voice he didn't recognize, clipped and professional and wrong in every way a voice could be wrong:

"—all stations, all stations, hydrological advisory, repeat, hydrological advisory—monsoon track revised, landfall window accelerated, all delta communities advised—"

Then static ate it, and the needle of Silas's compass, in her open hand, swung hard toward Halcyon Reach and stopped trembling—as if whatever it had been waiting for had finally begun.
