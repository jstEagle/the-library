## 12. The Missing Pages

The logbook smelled of river silt and thirty years of patience.

Silas sat cross-legged on the deck of the *Vesperine* with a headlamp clamped over her brow and her father's last words spread across an upturned crate, each page pinned flat under brass diving weights. The lower vault had given them this much before the water temperature dropped and Mateo's dive computer started throwing errors—forty pages of dense, cramped handwriting in a wax-sealed case wedged behind the pressure gauge panel of Station Nine's old bathymetry lab. Behind the panel her father had installed himself, back when the station was new and nobody thought to question where a chief hydrographer kept his spare tools.

Only she would have known to look there. He'd taught her gauges the way other fathers taught their daughters to fish—hands over hands, *trust the needle, not the dial*. When she'd found the case, her breath had fogged the inside of her mask and she'd had to hold still for a full minute before her hands stopped shaking enough to work the seal.

Now, by lamplight, with the delta breathing around her—the creak of the hull, the far-off mutter of thunder stacking up over the western ranges—she read what her father had died carrying.

The first pages were routine. Depth soundings, sediment cores, salinity gradients. His handwriting was beautiful in that engineer's way, every numeral identical to its brothers, columns ruled straight without a guide. She could hear his voice in it. *Measure twice, publish once.* He used to say that at the dinner table like a joke, but it hadn't been a joke. It had been a creed.

Page eleven was where the creed broke.

*Soundings at Reach Channel do not match published chart 44-C. Discrepancy is not error. The 44-C series shows a channel depth of 9.2 meters at benchmark stations where we are measuring 4.1. Someone has redrawn the floodplain. If these charts are what the towns use—*

The sentence stopped. Not faded. Stopped, mid-thought, the ink trailing off as though he'd been interrupted, or as though he'd looked up from the page and understood, all at once, what finishing the sentence would cost him.

Silas turned the page with fingers gone clumsy.

He'd come back to it, in fragments, across the next twenty pages. Never the whole accusation at once—always pieces, tucked between legitimate survey data like contraband sewn into a coat lining. A note that the chart office at Meridian House had "requested revisions" before publication. A sketch comparing the official floodplain boundary against his own measured one, the difference shaded in pencil: a wide gray tongue of land along the Rivergate's eastern bank where perhaps forty thousand people lived in towns whose names she knew like her own heartbeat—Calloway Bend, Ferrow's Landing, the market villages strung along the levee road like beads on a wire.

His measured line put all of it underwater in a hundred-year monsoon.

The published line drew the water neatly around every one of those towns, as if the river had been bribed.

*They have asked me to sign off,* he wrote on page nineteen. *I have asked who asked them. Nobody asks questions here anymore. The Syndicate holds the chart office's debt the way they hold everything else.*

Silas sat very still.

She had spent fifteen years believing her father died because Halcyon Reach was dangerous—because the delta was dangerous, because storms were dangerous, because some risks you take knowing the arithmetic might not favor you. That was the story the expedition community told itself afterward, the story that let people keep going out onto the water. An accident. A tragedy. Weather.

But page nineteen said otherwise. Page nineteen said he had found something, and that the something had teeth, and that he had known it.

She flipped forward, hunting. If he'd documented the falsification—if he'd compiled the comparison charts, the raw soundings, the correspondence—then somewhere in these pages was the complete case. Proof that would crack the chart office open like a rotten hull. Proof that would have cleared the Meridian Syndicate's name off every door in the delta and nailed it to a courtroom wall instead.

She reached page thirty-one and the handwriting changed.

Not dramatically. Her father was too disciplined for dramatics. But the letters tightened, the spacing compressed, the way his hand went when he was writing fast in bad light—or writing afraid.

*Have hidden the primary evidence set. They searched my quarters yesterday. Corrigan's people—I recognize the man from the Meridian offices; he watches me at meals now. I will not carry the whole thing on my body. If anything happens to me, the key is where I taught Silas to find it.*

Silas's throat closed.

*Where I taught Silas to find it.*

He had written her name into his last testament the way he'd done everything else—precisely, without flourish—and then he'd walked out onto the Reach channel in weather no sane hydrographer would boat in, and the delta had swallowed him, and the official report had said *operator error*, and the expedition community had sworn off the site, and everyone had agreed so quickly, so gratefully, to agree.

She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until she saw sparks.

When she could see again, she made herself do the work. That was the inheritance that mattered, maybe—the discipline, the refusal to drown in feeling when there were numbers to be run. She laid the pages out in sequence across two crates and the deck itself, weighting corners, building the argument the way he'd built it: discrepancy, cover-up, pressure, concealment, hiding place.

Then she counted.

Forty pages recovered. His final entry referenced a numbered appendix—*Appendix C, comparative plates, seven sheets*—and the raw sounding ledgers, and the correspondence file. None of it was in the case. The case held the narrative, the skeleton of the proof, the confession of a dead man naming names. But the muscle and bone of it—the physical documents that would stand up against lawyers and syndicate accountants and fifteen years of institutional lying—were elsewhere.

Or with someone else.

Silas went back through the early pages with new eyes, and there it was, small enough she'd missed it the first time, on page fourteen, in a margin note beside a list of equipment inventory:

*Duplicate plates pulled 3 Sept. Vault copy retained. Working copies logged out under M.S. authorization.*

M.S. Meridian Syndicate.

Her father hadn't just found the falsification. He'd reported it through channels—through the only channels that existed, which belonged to the people doing the falsifying. And the Syndicate hadn't destroyed his findings. They'd *logged them out*. Filed them. Kept them.

Because proof of a falsified chart wasn't only a liability. In the right hands, it was leverage. Every town on the true floodplain was a town that could be bought cheap before the truth came out, insured through Syndicate brokers, indebted through Syndicate lenders, dependent on Syndicate goodwill when the water came. You didn't sell a secret like that. You *farmed* it.

And when her father refused to stop asking questions—they'd let the river do the rest.

Silas stood up so fast the weights slid and a page skated toward the gunwale. She caught it two inches from the water, heart slamming, and stood there holding her father's handwriting above the black current while thunder rolled closer over the ranges, and she understood—with the clean, cold clarity of a depth reading that comes back wrong—that she was not holding a treasure.

She was holding half a map to a trap.

* * *

Juno found her like that, an hour later, still standing over the spread of pages, not moving except to turn them.

"You haven't eaten," Juno said. It wasn't a question. Juno Park navigated people the way she navigated channels—by reading the surface tension and knowing what lay underneath.

"Sit down," Silas said. "I need your eyes."

Juno sat. She read fast, lips moving slightly, and Silas watched her face go through the same sequence hers had: interest, confusion, the slow arrival of horror, and then—on page nineteen—a stillness that had nothing calm in it.

"They killed him." Juno's voice came out flat. "This says they killed him."

"It says he was scared of them and then he drowned in perfect weather. You can draw the line yourself."

"And the rest of it? The actual evidence?"

"Gone. Logged out by the Syndicate fifteen years ago." Silas tapped the margin note. "Which means it never left their possession. Which means—" She stopped. Made herself say it. "Which means Voss didn't hire us to find her family's secrets. Voss hired us to find whatever my father hid, because the Syndicate wants to know if any of it survived outside their vault. We're not salvagers, Juno. We're a test. We're the metal detector they sweep over the site before they build on it."

Juno was quiet for a long moment. Out on the water, lightning stitched the western sky, silent at this distance, a rumor of what was coming.

"Corrigan," Juno said finally. "The rival team. They weren't rivals."

"No."

"They're the retrieval crew. We're the—what, the bloodhounds? They let us go first because anything we find tells them where to look?"

"Or because anything we find, they take." Silas thought of the sabotaged equipment on their approach, the failures that felt deliberate. Not sabotage, maybe. *Triage.* Corrigan's people disabling what they intended to confiscate. "My father wrote that Corrigan watched him at meals. Fifteen years ago. The man's been minding this site longer than my crew's been alive."

"So we take what we have and we run."

"With half a confession?" Silas shook her head. "A dead man's diary saying the charts are wrong, with no plates, no ledgers, nothing a court could hold? The Syndicate's lawyers would eat it in an afternoon. Worse—they'd know exactly how much we know, which is exactly how much they can afford for us to know. No, Juno. If we run, we run having told them their secret's safe."

"Then what?"

Silas looked down at the pages, at her father's tight, frightened hand, and felt the trap close around her with a click she could almost hear. Every path ran through Corrigan now. Fight him and lose the evidence. Flee him and leave the evidence buried in Syndicate vaults forever. Trade with him—

Trade with him.

"He'll offer," she said slowly. "That's why we're still breathing. That's why our boat still floats. Corrigan doesn't want us dead—he wants us *negotiable*. My father's logbook is worth nothing to him unless we bring it to the table ourselves."

"That's insane. He could take it whenever he wanted."

"Could he?" Silas met Juno's eyes. "Think about it. He's had fifteen years and a salvage team and he hasn't found the vault. He needed us to find it. Which means he needs us alive, cooperative, and reasonably stupid—all three of which expire the moment we hand it over." She began gathering the pages, squaring them, returning them to the wax-sealed case with the tenderness she'd show a wound. "So we don't hand it over. And we don't run. We make him think we're about to do one or the other, and we watch which way he moves."

Juno stared at her. "You want to bait a Syndicate retriever with his own operation."

"I want to finish what my father started." Silas sealed the case. "He hid the evidence where only I would look. That wasn't sentiment, Juno. That was a plan. He knew someday someone would come back to Halcyon Reach, and he wanted that someone to be me, and he wanted me armed." She stood, slinging the case across her body, the strap biting her shoulder like a hand. "Half-armed. So we get the other half."

Thunder cracked overhead now, close enough to feel in the deck plates. Mateo's voice floated up from below, tight with weather-report static: the monsoon track had shifted again, west and south, days ahead of schedule, aimed at the delta like a thrown spear.

Silas looked east, toward the dark line of Halcyon Reach rising out of the flooded station, toward the corridors where Corrigan's lamps moved behind salt-fogged glass like eels under ice.

The river was coming to collect. And somewhere in those drowning halls, a man who had watched her father eat his last meals was waiting to learn what she'd found—and what she'd pay to keep it.

Let him wait, she thought. Let him learn what a Nakamura measures twice.
