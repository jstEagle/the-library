## 13. Rising Water, Falling Trust

Juno Park had learned to read a river the way other people read faces. The Rivergate Delta spoke in moods—silt-brown sulks after a dry week, green glass calm that lied about the current underneath, and then this: a low gray mutter, the water climbing its banks by inches and pretending it wasn't.

She stood on the deck of the *Kestrel's Wake* at first light and watched the river lie to her.

"Six centimeters since midnight," Mateo said beside her, coffee steaming in his hands despite the humidity. He'd run the numbers three times off the mooring line's waterline marks. "That's not seasonal. That's not even rude. That's hostile."

"Satellites still down?"

"Every uplink I try bounces off nothing. It's like shouting into a pillow." He sipped his coffee. "And before you ask—no, it's not our gear. Our gear is beautiful. Our gear is the only honest thing left in this delta."

Juno didn't answer. She was looking at the comms mast, at the antenna coupler she'd personally inspected two days ago, and at the hairline score of bright metal visible along its weatherproofing seam where no weather had ever touched.

Someone had opened it. Someone had opened it and closed it again with care, with patience, with the particular neatness of a person who wanted the failure to look like rot.

She climbed down without a word and went to find Silas.

* * *

Silas was in the chart room, which was what they called the aft cabin because calling it a shrine would have been too honest. She sat with her father's compass open on the table before her, the brass case catching the single working lamp, and she wasn't looking at it so much as through it, the way you look through a window at something you'd rather not see.

"The antenna's been cut," Juno said from the doorway. "Not broken. Cut. Clean job, resealed after. Corrigan's people did it while we were all underground playing nice with their truce."

Silas didn't flinch. That was the thing about Silas that Juno both loved and had come, over these last weeks, to quietly resent: nothing landed on her surface. Everything went somewhere deep and stayed there, radiating cold.

"I know," Silas said.

"You *know*?"

"I checked it yesterday. After Mateo's first failed uplink."

Juno stepped into the room and shut the door behind her, which was a thing she almost never did, because doors meant conversations with weight and weight meant Silas retreating further into whatever vault she kept behind her eyes. "You checked it yesterday. And you didn't tell us."

"There was nothing to tell yet. No signal out either way. Telling you just meant everyone standing around being scared together."

"That is *not* your call." Juno heard her own voice rise and let it. "We are moored below a drowned research station where eleven people died, with a rival crew full of Syndicate muscle upstream of us, and now we have no radio, no satellite, no way to call for help if anything goes wrong—and you knew, and you let Mateo spend half the night blaming himself and rebuilding a transmitter that someone gutted."

"He fixed the transmitter. The antenna's the problem. We can rig a replacement if we cannibalize the storm chaser's array—"

"That's not the point, Silas."

"Then what is the point?" Silas looked up finally, and there it was—not coldness, Juno realized. Not anymore. Something rawer, held in place with both hands. "The point is we're stuck here until the river decides otherwise, and Corrigan knows it, and every hour I spend explaining things is an hour he spends getting comfortable."

"The point," Juno said, "is that you keep deciding what we're allowed to be scared of."

The lamp hummed. Outside, the river muttered against the hull, a little higher than it had been when she came aboard.

Silas closed the compass. Slowly, like closing a casket.

"Okay," she said.

Juno blinked. She had rehearsed this fight on the climb down—the accusations, the counterattacks, the long siege of Silas's defenses. She had not rehearsed surrender.

"Okay?"

"You want the whole truth. Fine. Sit down." Silas pushed the logbook across the table—her father's logbook, water-swollen and rebound in waxed canvas, the thing she'd pulled from the lower vault two days ago and hadn't let out of arm's reach since. "All of it. Because you're right. I've been rationing it, and rationing got my father killed, so maybe it's time I stopped."

* * *

She told it badly at first, the way people tell things they've carried too long—out of order, skipping the parts that hurt, circling back. Juno let her. Mateo arrived at some point, drawn by the raised voices, and Silas waved him into the third chair without breaking stride, and he sat with his coffee going cold and his face going pale.

Halcyon Reach. Nineteen years ago. Her father, Kenji Nakamura, hydrographer, the best in the delta, leading the survey expedition that would produce the flood charts every town from Rivergate proper down to the salt marshes still trusted with their lives. Eleven people. A monsoon season that came early, the way monsoons do when the climate forgets its manners. A station flooded faster than any model predicted.

Except—that was the story. That was the official finding, signed and sealed: unprecedented surge, tragic anomaly, the charts were sound, the station was simply unlucky.

"My father transmitted for six hours as the water rose," Silas said. Her voice had gone flat, recitation-flat, the voice of someone who has said these words only inside her own skull for nineteen years. "The last transmission wasn't a distress call. It was data. Gauge readings, flow rates, historical comparisons. He was documenting that the flood levels he was recording didn't match the charts he himself had helped publish. The station didn't fail because the surge was unprecedented. It failed because the charts were wrong. Deliberately wrong. Baseline elevations falsified, flood projections shaved down—whole neighborhoods mapped safe that were never safe."

Mateo set his cup down very carefully, as though it might detonate. "Who falsifies flood charts? Who *benefits*?"

"Land value. Insurance. Development permits along the 'safe' corridors." Silas's mouth twisted. "Take your pick. My father found the discrepancy in his own final days and started pulling the original survey records, and Halcyon flooded, and the finding was written before his body was recovered. The surviving expeditioners took an oath. Silence. Sworn off water, sworn off Halcyon, sworn off the whole question. I was seventeen and I swore it too, because the adults said it protected the families. Protected *us*."

"And now?" Juno asked quietly.

"Now Voss hired us to recover a sealed inheritance from the archive. Which turns out to be—" Silas tapped the logbook. "This. Or part of it. Pages missing. And Corrigan's crew was here before we ever signed, which means the Syndicate knew exactly what was buried at Halcyon, which means the Syndicate has known about the falsified charts for nineteen years, and they didn't hire us to find the evidence." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and terribly clear. "They hired us to help them control it. To put hands on it that could plausibly be blamed if it surfaced. My debt was never really money. It was leverage. It was always leverage."

The cabin felt smaller. The river sounded louder.

"So the towns downstream," Mateo said slowly. "Rivergate. Marsh Landing. All the little places that trust the official charts."

"Are mapped to survive a hundred-year flood that will actually drown them," Silas finished. "And the monsoon is coming early. Mateo, you told me yourself the pressure gradients are wrong. You thought it was instrument error."

"It *was* instrument error," Mateo whispered. Then: "It was never instrument error."

"No one outside this room knows," Juno said. It wasn't a question; it was her mind doing what it did, mapping the current under the surface. "No authorities, no towns, nobody. Just us, Corrigan, Voss, and a dead man's logbook."

"Just us," Silas agreed.

Juno stood up. She needed to stand. She walked to the porthole and looked out at the brown water climbing the pilings of the old dock, at the drowned station looming above them on its ridge, windows dark, like a skull wearing a research facility as a hat.

Nineteen years. Nineteen years of Silas running—every contract taken fast and far, every port left before the anchor cooled, every friendship kept at oar's length. Juno had thought it was the debt. The Syndicate, the interest, the chase-vehicle payments. She'd built her whole understanding of her captain around the arithmetic of money owed.

It was never the money. The money was just the shape the fear took.

"You could have told me," Juno said, without turning around. "Years ago. Any of it."

"Could I have?" Silas's voice cracked, just slightly, on the second word. "Tell you what? That my father died proving the ground under half the delta was a lie, and that I helped bury the proof, and that the oath I swore keeps me quiet while thousands of people live their lives on top of it? You'd have looked at me the way you're looking at me now."

"I'm not looking at you any particular way."

"You've been looking at me that way since I said the word *falsified*."

Juno turned. It was true. She made herself stop, made her face do something else, something older and better. "I'm looking at you like someone who finally understands why you never sleep in the same bed twice," she said. "You weren't running from the Syndicate, Silas. You were running from that room. From this conversation. From telling anyone, because saying it out loud makes it real, and real means you have to *do* something."

Silas said nothing. Which was, from Silas, a full confession.

* * *

They convened on deck because the walls had gotten too small for the truth. Rain had started—not monsoon rain, not yet, but a fine gray needling that beaded on the deck plates and made the drowned station above them gleam like wet bone.

"We rig a new antenna," Mateo was saying, pacing, hands moving the way they did when his brain outran his mouth. "Cannibalize the chase vehicle's array, splice it to the mast, and I can get us maybe short-range. Enough to reach Rivergate relay if the atmosphere cooperates. Two hours' work, maybe three."

"Corrigan will see us doing it," Juno said. "He cut the antenna once. He'll cut it again. Or worse."

"Then we don't ask permission."

"With what, exactly, are we fighting Corrigan's crew? They outnumber us two to one and they're carrying more than survey tools, I promise you."

"Nobody's fighting anybody," Silas said.

They all turned. She stood at the rail with the logbook under her arm and the rain starting to slick her hair flat, and she looked, Juno thought, like a woman standing at the edge of a decision she'd been walking toward for nineteen years.

"Nobody fights," Silas said again. "Because the moment we fight, we confirm we're thieves sneaking off with something, and Corrigan gets to write the story. We do this in the open. We fix the antenna in daylight, on the main deck, where his people can watch. And when it works, we don't transmit to Rivergate relay." She paused. "We transmit everything. The logbook scans. The gauge data. The chart discrepancies. All of it, to every station that'll listen."

Mateo stopped pacing. "That's—Silas, that's the oath. That's the thing you swore."

"The oath protects the men who falsified the charts," Silas said. "It doesn't protect my father. It doesn't protect the towns. I've been hiding inside a promise that was designed to bury him twice." Her jaw tightened. "I know what it costs. I know what the Syndicate does to people who cost them money. But there are ten thousand people downstream sleeping tonight under charts that are going to kill them, and I am done being quiet."

The rain thickened. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled—a long, low sound, less like weather than like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Juno looked at her captain—at this woman she'd followed through storms and debts and a decade of perpetual motion, this woman who had just, for the first time since Juno had known her, chosen a direction and planted her feet.

"Three hours for the antenna," Juno said. "I'll take first watch on the deck. If Corrigan wants to object, he can object to my face."

Mateo exhaled, nodded once, and headed for the equipment locker, already listing splices and connectors under his breath.

Silas stayed at the rail. Juno moved to stand beside her, shoulder to shoulder, watching the river rise.

"For what it's worth," Juno said quietly, "the truth fits you better than the secrets did."

"Don't get sentimental. We're not out of this yet."

"No," Juno agreed. "Look."

Silas followed her gaze. Downriver, where the channel bent toward the delta towns, the water had begun to move differently—faster, dirtier, carrying branches and foam in long ragged lines. The river was no longer rising politely. It was gathering itself.

And above them, on the ridge, a light flickered on in the drowned station's upper level. One window, briefly lit, then dark.

Corrigan's people had seen the equipment locker open. They knew what was coming.

"They're moving," Juno said.

"So are we," said Silas, and turned toward the mast.
