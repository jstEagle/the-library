## 14. What the Charts Hid

The water had reached the second landing by the time Silas finished rigging the lanterns.

She hung them in a triangle around the archive's upper gallery—three work lights salvaged from the boat's dead comms array, their cables spliced and taped by Mateo's hands, throwing amber circles against the mildewed walls of what had once been the Halcyon Reach reading room. Beyond the shattered windows, the river moved like something with intent. It had swallowed the lower vault hours ago. It was patient about the rest.

The crew gathered without being asked. That was the thing about them, Silas thought as she watched them settle onto overturned shelving and upturned crates—after thirteen days in each other's pockets, after Corrigan's sabotage and the drowned skiff and the radio that spoke only static, they had stopped needing instructions. They read her face instead. And right now her face was telling them something they didn't want to hear.

Juno sat closest, arms crossed, one boot braced on a shelf bracket. Mateo perched on a crate with his tool roll across his knees, fingers working a wrench he wasn't using. Odile Voss stood apart by the window, arms folded tight, watching the water with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

Silas set her father's logbook on the table between them. The waterproof case came off with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a lock turning.

"You've all been asking," she said. "And I've been giving you pieces. Juno got more than most. Mateo, you've earned better than what I gave you." She looked at Voss last. "You already know most of it. You just know it wrong."

"Silas." Juno's voice was flat. "Don't do the speech. Just say it."

So she said it.

* * *

"The flood charts for the Rivergate Delta are falsified."

She let the sentence sit there. Outside, rain began to tick against the remaining glass—not the monsoon yet, not the real thing, but its scouts.

"Not badly falsified. Not sloppy. Deliberately, precisely, over thirty years, by the Cartographic Bureau in partnership with the Meridian Syndicate and—" she glanced at Voss, who did not flinch, which was its own kind of confirmation "—the Voss family's survey trust. The elevation data for the eastern delta is wrong by an average of two point three meters. The levee ratings are wrong. The evacuation zones are wrong. Every town from Rivergate proper down to the salt marshes is sitting inside a flood plain that official charts say doesn't exist."

Mateo's wrench went still. "Two point three meters," he repeated. "That's not a rounding error. That's—"

"That's the difference between wet ankles and drowning." Silas opened the logbook to the pages she'd spent the night cross-referencing, her father's cramped hand beside the original survey figures he'd recovered from this very archive eleven years ago. "My father found the originals. The pre-falsification surveys, done before the Syndicate bought the Bureau. He compared them against everything published since. He documented the whole scheme."

"And then?" Juno asked, though Silas could see in her eyes that Juno had already assembled the shape of the answer. She'd been assembling it since the compass, since the oath, since every deflection Silas had ever offered about Halcyon Reach.

"And then the expedition that came back down was one man short."

* * *

She told it plainly, because dressing it up would have been worse.

Eleven years ago, the Halcyon Reach expedition—nine people, her father among them, Odile Voss among them—had come to recover the historical survey records for what they'd been told was an academic preservation contract. Her father had recognized what the records showed within two days. He'd transmitted a message to the expedition's sponsor, a Bureau liaison, asking why the published charts diverged from the originals.

Three days later, during a routine dive of the lower vault, a structural collapse killed him. Official finding: deteriorated supports, tragic accident, the site was condemned, the community swore off Halcyon Reach forever. The surviving expeditioners signed a joint statement calling for the archive to be left sealed. They also signed something else—an oath of silence, brokered quietly through lawyers, about what the survey records contained.

"I always thought the oath was grief," Silas said. "People closing ranks around a wound. It took me ten years to understand it was fear. The Syndicate didn't need to threaten nine people. They needed to threaten one—the sponsor—and let the rest figure out the arithmetic themselves. My father asked a question out loud. The answer came back as a collapsed vault."

"The collapse wasn't an accident." Mateo said it quietly, not as a question.

"The supports were cut. My father's own dive notes say so—he logged unusual scoring on the steel two days before he died. He thought it was salvage damage. He was going to report it." She turned a page. The paper had gone soft as cloth, but the ink held, her father's handwriting slanting downhill the way it always did when he wrote fast, when he was excited, when he was afraid. "He knew. At the end, he knew."

Voss spoke from the window for the first time. "I didn't know about the cut supports." Her voice was careful, stripped of its usual lacquer. "I want that understood. I knew the charts were wrong—I grew up knowing, it's my family's rot—but I never knew they killed anyone for it."

"You knew enough to hire a crew to bury the evidence," Juno said.

"I knew enough to survive," Voss answered, and for once there was no performance in it. "There's a difference. Some days it's a very small difference."

Silas held up a hand before Juno could respond. "Fighting about Odile is a luxury. Listen to the rest."

* * *

She spread the copies on the table—her father's transcriptions of the original elevations, overlaid against the current published charts, the divergence marked in red where she'd traced it by lantern light. The eastern delta bloomed red like a bruise.

"This is what the monsoon will find when it makes landfall. Not the storm the forecasts are tracking—the forecasts use the same corrupted baseline, so even they underestimate the surge. When it comes ashore, the surge will push up channels the official maps call high ground. Rivergate's eastern districts. Marsh Crossing. Ternholt. The ferry towns." She tapped each name. "Tens of thousands of people who will look at the chart on their wall, see they're outside the flood zone, and stay in their homes."

Mateo had gone gray. "How long?"

"If the storm holds its current track? Four days. Maybe five. The satellites are blind to us now—Corrigan saw to that—but I got a fix off the backup receiver before he clipped it. It's coming east. It's coming early."

"So we leave." Mateo stood, the wrench finally dropping to the crate. "Tonight, while the water's still below the third landing. We run upstream, we get ahead of it, we warn people ourselves. Town by town if we have to."

"With what credibility?" Juno's voice cracked like a whip. "A chase crew with a seized vehicle and a dead radio walks into Marsh Crossing and says the government charts are lies? They'll laugh us off the dock. Or worse—they'll call the Bureau, the Bureau calls the Syndicate, and Corrigan's people find us standing still for the first time in years."

"Then we find another way—"

"There isn't another way." Silas kept her voice level. "There's exactly one way, and it's the relay tower on the roof of this station. It's built to broadcast to every delta station on the emergency band—it was designed for exactly this, for the day someone needed to tell the whole delta the truth at once. The transmitter's flooded, but the antenna array is intact, and Mateo could rebuild the power coupling if we strip the boat's cells."

Mateo stared at her. "You want to go back down. Into the flooding station. To fix a forty-year-old transmitter. During a monsoon."

"I want to tell the delta the truth while there's still time to act on it. Yes."

"And the debt?" Mateo's voice rose. "Silas, the Syndicate owns your life. If you broadcast this—if you burn the whole scheme in public—they don't forgive that. There's no negotiating past it. You'll be running until they catch you, and they will catch you, because you'll be famous. Everyone will know your face."

"I know what it costs."

"Do you?" He gestured at the logbook, at the red-inked maps, at all of it. "Because it sounds like you're deciding to die in this building, and you're dressing it up as arithmetic!"

The words hung in the lamplight. Silas let them. She'd been asking herself the same question since midnight, alone with the logbook and the sound of rising water, and she hadn't found a clean answer. Only a true one.

"My father died so this information would survive him," she said finally. "Not so it would sit in a vault. Not so it could be traded or buried or sold. He believed charts were promises—that when a person looks at a map, they're trusting everyone who made it. Someone broke that promise, and thousands of people are going to die believing it unless somebody breaks the silence first." She closed the logbook. "He couldn't. I can. That's the whole of it."

* * *

It was Juno who broke the silence that followed, and she broke it by standing up and walking to the window.

Rain streaked the glass behind her. Below, the river had claimed the boathouse roof; the skiff's mast jutted from the water at a drunken angle, their escape route reduced to driftwood. Juno watched it all with the stillness she brought to navigation—the same stillness she used when reading currents, when plotting a course through water that wanted to kill them.

"When you hired me," she said, without turning, "in Port Calloway, four years ago. You told me the crew rule. One rule. No secrets that get people killed."

"I remember."

"I've watched you dodge Syndicate calls for four years. I've covered for you in ports. I've lied to enforcers with your name in my mouth, and I never asked why, because the rule worked both ways—you never put me in a position where the secret could kill me." Now she turned, and her eyes in the lamplight were hard and bright. "This secret has been killing people for thirty years. It killed your father. It's aimed at every town on that map. And you carried it alone because of an oath signed by frightened people protecting themselves."

"Yes."

Juno nodded slowly. Then she walked back to the table, picked up one of the red-marked chart overlays, and studied it the way she studied tide tables—thoroughly, without flinching.

"Ternholt has maybe nine hundred people," she said. "Marsh Crossing, twelve hundred. The ferry towns, another thousand between them. Plus the eastern districts of Rivergate itself." She set the overlay down. "Twenty-some thousand people, give or take. Against one crew of four and whatever's left of our lives afterward."

"That's the arithmetic," Silas said.

"Then here's mine." Juno pulled out a crate and sat, elbows on knees, facing Silas directly. "I'm staying. Not because I believe in oaths broken properly, or dead men's logbooks, or any of the noble architecture you've built around this. I'm staying because twenty thousand people is a number I can navigate by. Everything else is weather."

Mateo looked from her to Silas and back, and something in his shoulders surrendered. He sat back down heavily and picked up his wrench again—purposefully this time, turning it over, already planning. "The power coupling needs a day's work," he muttered. "Maybe less if the antenna couplings aren't corroded through. Which they will be. Because everything in this building hates us."

"Odile?" Silas asked.

Voss hadn't moved from the window. For a long moment she stood silhouetted against the rain, and Silas watched her wrestle with whatever lived behind that composed face—the family name, the survey trust, thirty years of complicity she'd inherited like a birthright. When she finally spoke, her voice was small in a way Silas had never heard it.

"My grandfather drew the first false line," Voss said. "Did you know that? On paper, in ink, in his own study. One line on one chart, to protect a levee investment. And now it's a monsoon aimed at twenty thousand people, and every drop of it runs back through my family's hands." She turned. Her eyes were dry, but barely. "I hired you to destroy this evidence, Silas. I won't pretend otherwise. But I'm looking at that map, and I'm looking at that water, and I find that I can't be my grandfather anymore. So yes. I'm staying. God help me, I'm staying."

Silas exhaled—a long, shaking breath she hadn't known she was holding. Four people in a drowned library, surrounded by rising water, bound now not by contract or debt or oath but by choice. It was more than her father had. It was more than she'd ever had.

"All right," she said. "Then we work. Mateo, the transmitter. Juno, plot us a route from the gallery stairs to the tower access that stays above the waterline as long as possible. Odile—" she hesitated, then handed her the logbook. "You know the old survey conventions better than any of us. Help me finish reconstructing the missing pages' contents from what my father transcribed. If we're broadcasting this, it has to be complete. It has to be undeniable."

They scattered to it, and the gallery filled with the sounds of purpose—Mateo's tools, Juno's murmured calculations, pages turning. Silas stood a moment at the table, her hand flat on her father's logbook, feeling the weight of what he'd handed her across eleven years and a locked vault.

Outside, the rain thickened. Far to the west, under cloud cover no satellite could now confirm, the monsoon leaned into its turn toward the delta—early, hungry, and aimed straight at everything the charts swore was safe.

And down in the flooded dark of the lower levels, faint through the structure's bones, came a sound none of them had heard before: metal, striking metal. Rhythmic. Deliberate.

Someone else was still in Halcyon Reach—and they were climbing.
