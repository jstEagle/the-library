## 15. Corrigan's Bargain

Corrigan had negotiated with drowning men before, and he knew the trick of it: you didn't threaten them. You offered them air.

He stood at the window of Halcyon Reach's second-floor common room—what had once been a researcher's lounge, all rotted couches and a water-stained corkboard still pinned with a decade-old tide schedule—and watched the river eat the delta road below. The monsoon was two days out, maybe three. The barometer on his wrist unit had been falling all afternoon like something with a grudge against arithmetic. Behind him, his men had rigged lanterns and a camp stove in the corner, and the room smelled of kerosene and wet plaster and, faintly, of old paper—the smell of an archive slowly digesting itself.

Silas Nakamura sat across the room with her crew, close enough to watch him and far enough to pretend she wasn't. She'd dried off since the standoff in the flooded stacks, but her hair still held the river's memory of her, dark and flat against her skull. Her engineer was tinkering with a radio that would never transmit again—not after Corrigan's man Voss-cut the antenna array that afternoon, clean as surgery. Her navigator kept one hand near a pry bar and her eyes on everything. And Nakamura herself kept looking at the canvas satchel slung across Corrigan's shoulder like it might confess to her.

The logbook wasn't in the satchel. He'd made sure she saw him carry it, though. Let her do the math. Let her lie awake doing it.

"Reyes," he said pleasantly, not turning from the window. "Your bilge pump is going to lose that fight by morning. I'd move the generator up another flight if I were you."

"I know how my own boat works," Mateo Reyes said from the doorway, arms crossed, grease to his elbows.

"No," Corrigan said. "You know how boats work. Nobody knows how this one works anymore. It's been taking on water since before you got here." He let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to, then turned and smiled at all of them, the smile he'd practiced in a hundred conference rooms above a hundred flood lines. "Which brings me to why we're all still awake."

* * *

He'd done his research on Silas Nakamura the way he did everything: thoroughly, patiently, with an accountant's patience for other people's ruin. He knew about the debt. Everyone in the Syndicate's ledger-rooms knew about the debt—two hundred and forty thousand, compounding, three years of weather-chasing contracts skimmed thin by interest that grew like mold in a wet wall. He knew she'd taken Voss's contract because refusing meant the chase vehicle gone, then the boat, then the crew scattered into the delta economy like seed corn eaten in a famine. He knew about her father, too. The file on Elias Nakamura was thin but sufficient: lead hydrographer, Halcyon Reach expedition, drowned or crushed or simply erased when the station's eastern wing went into the river eleven years ago. Cause listed as structural failure. Cause believed, by anyone who'd ever read the actual survey data, as nothing of the kind.

And he knew—this was the part that mattered, the part he'd built the whole evening around—that she had found the logbook that morning in the lower vault, in the dry box welded behind the pressure door where only a daughter who'd grown up watching her father hide things from auditors would think to look.

She thought she'd found it secretly. That was the charming thing about people like Silas Nakamura. They believed the world was full of hiding places.

"The way I see it," Corrigan said, lowering himself into the one intact armchair with the ease of a man settling in for a long story, "we have a problem of accounting. Not money. Accounting." He held up fingers. "Item one: you have a logbook containing pages that certain parties consider proprietary. Item two: those same pages are missing from your copy, which means they exist somewhere I control. Item three: you have a debt that will follow you to every port in this delta until it kills you or you die of old age running from it. Item four—" he gestured at the window, at the brown water climbing the pilings outside, at the sky the color of a bruise going septic, "—item four is coming whether we talk or not."

"You want the book," Juno Park said flatly. "We know what you want. Say the number."

"I'm not buying it," Corrigan said, and enjoyed the flicker of confusion that crossed her face. "Buying implies it has value to me. It doesn't. It has *cost*. There's a difference, and the difference is worth more than your boat, your truck, and your captain's freedom combined." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and made his voice the voice he used for closing—warm, reasonable, the voice of a man who had already decided to be generous. "Here's my offer. You give me the logbook—all of it, including whatever copies exist—and you forget you ever read it. In exchange, the Meridian Syndicate forgives your debt. Not restructures. Not defers. Forgives. Zero balance. Clean ledger. You walk out of here owing nothing to anyone, which, Ms. Nakamura, given your history, may be the closest thing to grace you'll ever be offered."

* * *

He watched her take it apart while he talked. That was the pleasure of negotiating with someone competent—you could see them working, the way you could see current working under a river's skin. She looked at his hands, checking for the tell of a liar. She looked at her navigator, reading permission or prohibition. She looked at the satchel again, doing the geometry of distance and bodies between herself and it, and arrived, correctly, at the answer that any attempt ended with someone bleeding.

But underneath all of it, he watched her look at the arithmetic. Two hundred and forty thousand. Three years of running. Every storm chased not for the love of weather but because standing still cost money she didn't have. He'd read her file enough times to know the shape of her exhaustion better than she did. People always thought their debts were private. Nothing was private. Debt was the most public thing in the world; it just wore different faces.

"Say I believe you," she said finally. "Say I hand it over. What happens to the towns?"

Ah. There it was. He'd wondered which version of her would surface—the runner or the daughter. Turns out both lived in the same body and took turns driving.

"What towns?" he said mildly.

"Don't." Her voice didn't rise. That was the dangerous thing about her; he'd noted it in the file annotations. *Subject does not escalate. Subject goes quiet.* "You've read the same data I have. The charts say the levee districts hold at nine meters. Your employers helped write those charts. The real surveys say seven. When the monsoon makes landfall, everyone downstream of Rivergate who trusts the official numbers is going to be standing in a house that floods to the roofline. You're not asking me to sell a book. You're asking me to sell the warning."

"I'm asking you to sell a book," Corrigan said, "and I'm telling you what the book is worth. The warning—" he spread his hands, regretful, a man constrained by reality, "—the warning isn't yours to give. It never was. Those charts were falsified twenty years ago by people with titles and seals, and the delta has been living on them ever since. One logbook doesn't change that. One broadcast doesn't change that. All it changes is who goes to prison, and I promise you, Ms. Nakamura, the people who go to prison will not be the people who signed the maps. They'll be whoever's holding the evidence when it surfaces. Which is you. Which is your crew. Which is a very short list ending in a very deep hole."

"That's not an argument," Park said. "That's a threat wearing an argument's clothes."

"All arguments are," Corrigan said, and meant it, and let them hear him mean it, because honesty was the most disarming tool he owned. "I'm not pretending to be your friend. I'm offering you a trade that is, objectively, the best trade available to you tonight. Take it, and tomorrow you're free. Refuse, and tomorrow you're free too—free, broke, hunted, and standing in rising water with a dead man's notebook that won't keep anyone dry. I've done this a long time. I can count on one hand the number of people who chose the second option and were glad of it afterward."

* * *

They broke for the night, because that was part of the design. Never let them answer in the room where you asked. Let them carry the offer into the dark, into the cold, into the small hours where every person negotiates alone with themselves and loses.

Corrigan posted Rennick at the stairwell and took the window watch himself, mostly because he wanted to see the river. It had come up half a meter since dusk. Out past the drowned road, the channel markers were going under one by one, their lights blinking shorter and shorter intervals, like a pulse accelerating toward something. The monsoon's leading edge was visible now even to the naked eye—a wall of cloud over the western basin, lit from inside by heat lightning, silent at this distance, patient as paperwork.

His wrist unit chimed. Voss.

"Well?" Odile Voss's voice came through thin and taut, relayed from her ship anchored well downstream, where the water was still behaving itself.

"He hasn't answered yet. I gave him the night."

"And if he says no?"

"Then we're past the point where his answer matters much." Corrigan watched the lightning stitch the horizon. "Though I'll tell you something, Ms. Voss, professional to professional. I don't think he says no. I think he wants to. Wanting to say no is the strongest predictor of saying yes there is. People who genuinely intend to refuse negotiate like it's a formality. People who intend to accept negotiate like it's a war crime trial. She's been cross-examining my soul for three hours."

Voss was quiet a moment. "Her father died for those pages."

"Her father died for a lot of reasons," Corrigan said. "Mostly bad engineering and worse management. The pages are incidental. But yes—if you're asking whether she'll feel like a collaborator, she will. For a while. Then the debt clears, and the crew eats, and the feeling gets smaller every year, the way feelings do. Grief keeps. Guilt doesn't. That's the whole architecture of the modern world, Ms. Voss. We built it that way on purpose."

"You say that like it comforts you."

"It does comfort me," Corrigan said. "It's the only reliable thing I've found." He paused. "How's your end?"

Another pause, longer. Through the relay he could hear wind starting to arrive at her position, a low moan under the signal. "The river's up," she said. "At my anchorage. Which shouldn't happen for another day at least."

"Then the models are wrong again," Corrigan said. "Imagine that."

He cut the call before she could say anything else, because he'd heard something in her voice he recognized and preferred not to think about: the sound of a woman beginning, very quietly, to look at rising water through honest eyes. That was a problem for later. Everything was a problem for later. Later was the only country Corrigan had ever lived in.

* * *

Past midnight, he heard footsteps on the flooded stairwell—the slow, deliberate splash of someone who wanted to be heard coming.

Silas Nakamura stood in the doorway with a lantern, alone, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The rain had started outside, the first fat, indifferent drops of the real thing, ticking against the broken windows like a clock winding down.

"Couldn't sleep?" Corrigan asked.

"I slept fine. Twenty minutes. That's fine for me." She came into the room and stood across from him, and he noticed she hadn't brought the pry bar or the navigator or anyone else. Alone. That was either surrender or strategy, and he'd learned long ago that the two looked identical right up until they moved.

"Ask me anything," he said. "Before you decide. Whatever's eating you."

"Why do you care about the book? Really. Not the Syndicate line. Yours."

He considered giving her the line. Then, because honesty disarmed better than lies, he gave her a piece of the truth instead. "Because I've seen what happens when this kind of paper gets loose. Not the towns. The towns are a rounding error to the people who employ me—I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. I mean what happens to the holder. Eleven years ago a whole expedition swore silence around whatever your father found, and the ones who kept that oath are still alive, still working, still welcome in every port on this coast. The one man who tried to take it public is dead, and his daughter has spent her entire adult life outrunning a number. That's the pattern, Ms. Nakamura. Truth doesn't drown the guilty. It drowns whoever carries it farthest from shore." He gestured at the window, at the rain thickening into sheets. "I'm offering to take it off your hands. That's all this is. A rescue."

For a long moment she looked at him, and he watched something move behind her eyes that he'd seen a thousand times in a thousand negotiations: the exact moment when the reasonable face of temptation became almost indistinguishable from wisdom. She was tired. She was grieving. She was standing in a building her father had died in, holding a debt like a stone in her chest, and a man was offering to make it all stop with a signature. Every argument she had for refusing was abstract—towns she'd never met, a truth that wouldn't save anyone, a broadcast that might not even clear the storm static. Every argument for accepting was concrete. Warm food. A paid crew. Silence, and sleep, and an end to running.

"Two hundred and forty thousand," she said quietly. "That's the number, isn't it. You made sure I'd know it."

"I make sure everyone knows their number," Corrigan said. "It's respectful."

She laughed—one short, ugly sound with no humor in it, grief transmuting itself in real time. "My father used to say the delta doesn't forgive debts. It just moves them downstream." She looked at the satchel. Looked at the window, where the rain was now a solid gray curtain and the river below had swallowed the last channel marker entirely. Looked back at him.

"One condition," she said.

Here it was. Corrigan kept his face pleasant and his pulse level. "Name it."

"The missing pages. I read them first. Before I hand over anything, I read what he wrote, all of it, and then I decide. You get the book and my silence—but I get to know what I'm selling."

Every instinct Corrigan had said *no*. Every protocol said the pages left his possession for nothing, not even an hour. But he'd built this night on reasonableness, and refusing a mother's-last-request clause now would crack the whole façade at the worst possible moment. Besides—he told himself, in the smooth internal voice he used for signing off on things—he could control what she read. He'd choose the pages. He'd stand in the room.

"Ten minutes," he said. "In front of me. Then it's mine, and the debt dies at dawn."

She put out her hand, and he shook it, and her grip was steady, and he thought: *done.*

It was only later—only when she pulled her hand back and he caught the look that passed, quick as a river bird, between her and the dark of the stairwell where Juno Park had been standing the entire time, close enough to hear every word, every number, every term—that Corrigan understood he had spent the whole night negotiating with the wrong member of the crew.

Outside, the monsoon made landfall six hours early, and the lights of Halcyon Reach went out floor by floor, top to bottom, like a building closing its eyes.
