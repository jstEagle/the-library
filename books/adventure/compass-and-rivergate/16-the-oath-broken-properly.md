## 16. The Oath, Broken Properly

Dawn came to Halcyon Reach the color of old tin.

Silas had not slept. She sat in the flooded gallery of the lower vault with her back against a cabinet that had once held sediment cores, her father's logbook open across her knees inside its waterproof sleeve, and she watched the water rise by inches against the wall and thought about the word *yes*.

Corrigan's offer was still in the room. It didn't need him here to occupy it. Debt forgiveness—every credit of it, the whole rotting weight of what she owed the Meridian Syndicate, dissolved like salt in warm water. All she had to do was hand over the logbook and the pages Corrigan already half-held, and then spend the rest of her life being a woman who had seen the falsified charts and said nothing. A comfortable silence. A purchased one.

She had almost taken it. That was the part that shamed her—not the temptation itself, but how reasonable it had felt at two in the morning, when the river outside groaned against the station's bones and Mateo's voice over the shortwave had cracked with exhaustion and Juno hadn't spoken to her in six hours, and forgiveness had seemed less like surrender than like mercy. Mercy for her crew, who'd never signed anything, never sworn anything, never drowned anyone. Mercy for people who deserved it more than she did.

The water lapped at the base of the doorframe. Above her, through three decks of corroded stairwells, she could hear the wind beginning to organize itself into something with intent.

Her father's handwriting swam in the gray light. *Day 41. The charts are wrong. Not mistaken—wrong. Someone drew the hundred-year floodplain forty kilometers east of where the model puts it, and they did it after the peer review, and every town west of the Reach is sitting inside the real line.* And then, on the next page, in a hand gone suddenly careful: *I've told the others we hold this until monsoon season passes. Voss agrees. Safer that way. Nobody acts on bad data in a panic.*

*Safer that way.* She'd read those words a dozen times since pulling the book from the niche behind the vault panel—the niche he'd built for her, keyed to a compass bearing only she would know—and each time she heard them in his voice, warm and certain, and each time she wanted to scream at them. He had found the truth and then he had *held* it. Held it the way the surviving expeditioners held their oath afterward: *What we found at Halcyon stays at Halcyon, until the water tells us it's time.* Eleven voices on a dock at Rivergate, hands stacked, grief still wet on all their faces. Silas had been twenty-three. She had pressed her palm against her father's oldest friend's shoulder and meant it with everything in her.

The oath had kept her crew fed for nine years. Storm-chasing paid badly but honestly, and nobody asked questions about why the Nakamura girl wouldn't take delta survey work, wouldn't touch chart contracts, went strange and silent whenever Halcyon came up over drinks. The oath had been a shape she could live inside.

And the oath had been written by guilty men.

She closed the logbook. That was the sentence she'd been circling all night, the one she couldn't get past: the oath wasn't protecting her father. Her father was dead, and had been dead for nine years, and no silence she kept would bring him up off the riverbed. The oath protected the people who moved the floodplain line. It protected the Meridian Syndicate, whose founding capital had come from delta land speculation—buy low on ground everyone believed was safe, sell high when the "safe" designation drove prices up. It protected Odile Voss's family name, stitched as it was into the survey consortium that had signed off on the falsified charts. It protected eleven exhausted survivors from having to look at what their silence had purchased: nine more years of towns trusting maps that would kill them.

*Until the water tells us it's time.*

The water was telling her now. She could hear it coming up the estuary, a sound below sound, the deep tidal intake of a river making room for a storm.

Footsteps on the gallery above—Juno's uneven tread, favoring the knee she'd banged during the sabotage scramble. Then Mateo's heavier stride beside it. They stopped at the head of the stairs. Silas watched their silhouettes against the failing emergency lighting, two people deciding whether to come down to her.

"Come down," she said. "Both of you. I'm done keeping things in rooms."

They descended. Juno lowered herself onto an overturned sample crate, arms crossed, face set in the expression she wore when she'd decided to listen before deciding to forgive. Mateo stayed standing, which for Mateo was a statement; the man sat through engine fires.

"I told you about the charts," Silas said. "Two nights ago I told you everything—the falsified floodplain, my father's logbook, the pages missing. What I didn't tell you is what I did last night."

"You met Corrigan alone," Juno said. Flat. Not a question.

"I met him alone. He offered me debt forgiveness. Full amount. In exchange for the logbook and my silence." She let that sit. "And I want you both to understand that I stood there in that corridor and I *considered* it. Not for a second. For minutes. Long enough that he smiled, because he could see it working. I stood in front of a man whose people sabotaged our boat and left us stranded above a rising river, and I priced your lives against my ledger, and the ledger got closer than I will ever admit out loud again."

Mateo exhaled slowly through his nose. "Okay," he said. "That's the worst thing anyone's ever told me, and I once watched a client try to pay us in live eels."

"Why are you telling us this?" Juno asked. "Why now?"

"Because last night I understood something, and it changes what happens next." Silas stood. Her legs were stiff from the cold and the hours, and she let them be stiff; she wanted to feel the cost of standing up. "I've spent nine years thinking the oath was mine to keep or break, like it belonged to me. Like honoring it was loyalty to my father and the others who died. But that's not what the oath is. The oath is a lock, and I finally looked at who holds the key. The Syndicate. Voss's family. The survey consortium. Every person who profits from the delta believing a lie. My silence isn't loyalty—it's inventory. It's an asset they've been holding through me, interest accruing, waiting for a buyer." She tapped the logbook sleeve. "Corrigan wasn't offering to buy the evidence. He was offering to close the account. To make sure the truth dies with the last person holding it, quietly, the way it was always meant to."

"And if you refuse?" Mateo asked, though his voice said he already knew.

"If I refuse, they take it. Or they take us. The river may do their work for them." She looked at Juno. "You asked me for the whole truth and I gave it, and you stayed. I don't have the right to ask what I'm about to ask. So I'm not asking. I'm telling you what I'm going to do, and you have until the water reaches the second deck to decide if you're on that boat without me."

Juno uncrossed her arms. "Say it."

"The oath ends today. Not broken the way Corrigan would break it, selling pieces in the dark. Broken properly—in the open, on the record, with names attached. When we reach the relay tower, I transmit everything. The logbook scans, the reconstructed chart overlays, the consortium signatures, the Syndicate's land holdings inside the true floodplain. Every delta station. Every town west of the Reach. Before the monsoon lands, so the warnings mean something while they can still act." Her voice didn't shake, and she was grateful for that, because inside she was shaking like a wire in wind. "My father found this and held it, because he believed patience was protection. His patience killed him—maybe literally, maybe just eventually, it doesn't matter anymore. I am done being patient with the dead's mistakes. They were good men and they were wrong, and the towns downstream have been paying the interest on that wrong for nine years, and the principal comes due this week."

The wind found the station then, a long low note through the broken skylights, like the building clearing its throat.

Mateo spoke first. "You understand the Syndicate doesn't forgive debts. They convert them. If you broadcast, you're not a debtor anymore—you're a problem. Problems get solved differently."

"I know."

"And Voss," Juno said. "Voss's family name is on those charts. You burn the consortium, you burn her. She hired us. She has resources we don't, and she knows exactly where we are."

"I know that too."

"So you're choosing to make enemies of the only two institutions in this delta with the reach to ruin you, during a Category 4 landfall, from a tower that might not survive the night." Juno's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but the ancestor of one. "Good. I was worried you'd do something reckless."

Silas laughed—a short, cracked sound, surprised out of her. "Is that a yes?"

"That's a yes with conditions." Juno stood, and even with the bad knee she managed to loom. "One: we don't transmit until Mateo says the tower's array can hold a signal, because a dead broadcast helps no one. Two: we warn the towns first, data second—people before proof, always. Three—" she paused, and something in her face shifted, older and sadder, "—when this is done, whatever's left of it, you tell me the rest. Not the mission rest. The *you* rest. Why you really kept the oath. All nine years of it. I've earned that, and you owe it, and I'm calling the debt due."

It was, Silas thought, the cruelest possible phrasing, and perfectly chosen, and she loved her navigator with her whole chest for it. "Deal," she said.

Mateo clapped his hands once, the sound swallowed by the growing wind. "Then let's go wake up a relay tower. Fair warning—I checked the schematics this morning and the array's azimuth drive seized sometime around the disaster. We'll be hand-cranking that dish into position like it's 1890."

"Can you do it?"

"Mateo Reyes," he said, heading for the stairs, "has never met a machine he couldn't argue with. Whether the machine agrees is a separate question."

* * *

They climbed.

The station's central shaft rose through five flooded levels toward the roof, and the water followed them up, patient as arithmetic. By the third deck the stairwell lights were dead and they moved by headlamp, three beams swinging through corridors full of drifting paper and the pale shapes of instruments that had been state-of-the-art when her father died. Silas kept one hand on the wall as she climbed and felt the building tremble under her palm—not yet fear, not yet panic, just the deep structural hum of a body bracing.

On the fourth deck they passed the old survey office. Silas stopped.

She hadn't planned to. But the door stood open, and inside, under a skin of silt and mold, she could see the long table where the expedition team had spread their charts that final season, and the wall pins where the master flood map had hung, and she stepped in without deciding to, her light finding the ghost-rectangle on the corkboard where the map had been nailed before someone took it down and carried it away to be redrawn.

Her father had stood right here. Right on this tile, in boots like hers, looking at the real numbers, and he had chosen patience, and patience had chosen the river.

"Silas." Juno, from the stairwell. Not impatient. Just present.

"I'm coming." But she took one more second. She put her palm flat on the table, on the silt, the way she'd once pressed her hand into wet concrete as a kid because her father told her marks lasted longer than memories. "I'm sorry," she said—to the room, to him, to the eleven voices on the dock. "You swore to protect each other, and I know that. I know the oath was love before it was anything else. But love that only runs one direction isn't an oath. It's a cage. And I'm opening it."

The words didn't feel like betrayal. That was the thing she'd feared most—that breaking the vow would feel like the night of the disaster all over again, like losing him twice. Instead it felt like setting down a load she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy. Grief didn't leave her; it changed states, solid to liquid, and started moving, and moving things have direction.

She turned and climbed.

The roof access hatch fought them—corroded shut, then surrendered to Mateo's pry bar with a shriek that the wind immediately stole. They came up onto the top of Halcyon Reach into a world already ending.

The sky to the southeast was a bruise the size of a country. The monsoon's leading edge had eaten the horizon whole, a wall of cloud lit from within by stuttering internal lightning, and beneath it the estuary had gone the color of hammered lead, whitecaps running upstream against the tide. The relay tower stood at the roof's far corner, thirty meters of rusted lattice crowned with the dish array, guy wires singing three different notes in the strengthening wind.

"There's our cathedral," Mateo shouted, and led the run across the roof, hunched against gusts that arrived like shoulders checking them.

The equipment shelter at the tower's base still had power—trickle current from the station's dying solar bank, enough to wake the transmitter's standby systems. Mateo disappeared inside with his tool roll and began, immediately, to talk to the machinery in the low coaxing dialect he reserved for machines he respected. Juno climbed the lattice to inspect the dish mounts, testing bolts with a wrench and her whole weight.

Silas stood at the tower's base with the logbook sleeve strapped across her chest and looked south, toward Rivergate, toward the string of towns—Marrow Bend, Canebreak, Little Ferry—sitting fat and unbothered inside the lie of the official charts. Somewhere down there, people were boarding windows against a storm they believed would pass them by, because a map drawn by liars said they were safe.

Her radio crackled. Corrigan's frequency—he'd never changed it, some arrogance about owning the airwaves. His voice came through flattened by static and distance.

"Nakamura. My spotters say you're on the roof. That's high ground. Smart. Here's my last offer, and it's better than the first: walk away from the tower, keep the logbook, keep your silence, and I'll have my boats pull your crew off this rock before landfall. Everyone lives. Everyone's rich. The delta keeps its maps."

She keyed the transmit. "Corrigan. Do you know what my father wrote on the last page of his log? Not coordinates. Not names. One sentence." She read it aloud into the wind, into the radio, into the gathering dark: *'A chart is a promise. Break it and the water keeps the difference.'*

Static. Then, quieter: "You're going to do it. You're actually going to burn it all down."

"I'm going to tell the truth," Silas said. "You should try it sometime. There's room."

She switched off the radio before he could answer, and turned to the tower, and put her hands on the first rung.

Above her, Juno was already climbing, wrench between her teeth, and the first rain hit the roof of Halcyon Reach like applause—hard, cold, and far too early, the opening note of a storm aimed straight at the heart of everything she loved. From somewhere below, faint through the wind, came the sound of engines on the water.

More than one engine. Moving fast.

Silas started to climb.
