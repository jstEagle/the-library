## 17. Voss Turns

The Meridian Syndicate did not send cars to meetings like this. They sent a boat, and the boat was always the same: a matte-grey river cruiser with no name on the stern and a wake so clean it looked like it had been ironed. Odile Voss watched it come up the channel toward her mooring at Tallow Bend and thought, with the detached clarity that had served her all her life, *so this is what the end of an ambition sounds like.*

She had dressed for the meeting the way she dressed for everything—as armor. Charcoal suit, pressed within an inch of its life. Her mother's pearl at her throat, the one that had survived two floods and one divorce. If they were going to take something from her today, they would take it from a woman who looked like she had never been taken from in her life.

Corrigan stood on the cruiser's deck with his hands folded, patient as a man waiting for a ferry. That was the worst part of him. He never hurried. Violence, in his economy, was simply a slower form of bookkeeping.

"Ms. Voss." He extended a hand to help her aboard, and she took it, because refusing would have been a message, and messages were what she could no longer afford to send.

"Mr. Corrigan. You're a long way from Halcyon."

"And you're a long way from the woman who hired a storm crew and told them it was about family honor." He gestured her toward the cabin, where a table had been cleared and a single folder sat in the center of it, squared to the table's edge. Corrigan was a man who squared things to edges. It was, she suspected, the only tenderness he had.

She sat. He sat across from her. The folder stayed closed between them like a third guest.

"Let's not perform," Corrigan said. "You know why I'm here."

"I imagine it's the same reason you're always somewhere. The Syndicate's money went somewhere it wasn't supposed to, and now you're collecting."

"The Syndicate's money went into your archive expedition. Fourteen years of it, in increments small enough to escape notice and large enough to matter. Your family's foundation. Your family's name." He opened the folder and turned it toward her. Photographs. Ledger pages. Signatures—her father's, her grandfather's, her own, from three years ago, authorizing a survey she had been told was routine. "The flood charts, Ms. Voss. The delta's flood charts. Somebody paid to have them drawn the way they were drawn. Somebody paid to keep the corrections from ever reaching the hydrographic office. And every one of those payments traces back to a Voss."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere downstream a barge sounded its horn, long and indifferent.

"I didn't know," Odile said.

"I know," Corrigan said, and that was somehow worse. "You didn't know. That's what makes you useful. A guilty woman runs. An innocent woman negotiates."

She looked at the signatures. Her own handwriting, younger and more confident, looped across an authorization she had never read closely because reading closely had never been necessary. That was the inheritance, she understood suddenly—not the name, not the pearls, not the mooring at Tallow Bend. The inheritance was the luxury of not reading closely. Her whole life had been built on the assumption that someone else had already checked the arithmetic.

"What do you want?"

"The same thing you wanted when you hired Nakamura. The logbook. The original survey data. Anything that proves the charts were falsified." He laced his fingers. "You wanted it to destroy it. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise. I watched you negotiate her contract. You buried a clause about 'site remediation' on page eleven. You intended to burn it all."

"And if I did?"

"Then the Syndicate's exposure dies with it, and your family's exposure dies with it, and everyone goes on believing the river will stay where we've told it to stay." He slid a second document from the folder. "But Nakamura found something. And now the Syndicate has a problem, and problems at this scale get solved one of two ways. You're going to tell me where she is, what she has, and how close she is to the vault. In exchange—"

"In exchange for what? My family's already ruined. You have the ledgers."

"In exchange for the ledgers never leaving this boat." He tapped the folder. "There are copies, Ms. Voss. There are always copies. But copies require someone willing to spend a year in court making them mean something, and courts require the original signatories to still be respectable enough to testify against. You can be the woman who saved the Syndicate from a thief, or you can be the woman who bankrupted the delta's most patient lender. I've watched you your whole career. I know which one you'd rather be."

The cabin was very quiet. Through the porthole she could see the grey water of the channel, flat and brown and patient, the way it always was this time of year. The way it had been, she realized, for fourteen years of falsified charts. The river hadn't gone anywhere. It had just been waiting for someone to stop lying about it.

"They're at Halcyon," she heard herself say. "Nakamura's crew. They reached the Reach two days ago. Corrigan—your Corrigan, the one you put on the site—they know about him now. There was a standoff. The boat's comms were sabotaged. They're stranded until the weather breaks."

"And the logbook?"

"Her father's logbook. She has most of it. Your man has the missing pages." She swallowed. "She knows what the charts hid. She's worked it out. If she gets to the relay tower—"

"She won't get to the relay tower." Corrigan closed the folder and squared it to the table's edge. "Thank you, Ms. Voss. You've been very helpful."

"Will you kill her?"

He looked at her with something that was almost curiosity. "The Syndicate doesn't kill people. The Syndicate makes people unnecessary. There's a monsoon coming, Ms. Voss. Rivers do the rest." He stood, and opened the cabin door, and the daylight came in hard and honest and made her feel filthy in her pressed suit. "You're free to go. Consider your family's debts settled."

She walked off the boat on legs that felt borrowed. Her own mooring, thirty meters of grey water between her and the shore, had never looked so much like a cell.

* * *

She did not go home. She told herself it was prudence—Corrigan would have the house watched, would want to know if she ran—and prudence was a language she had spoken fluently her whole life. But the truth was that she pointed her skiff downstream, toward the Reach, and she could not have told you why, and the not-knowing frightened her more than the Syndicate did.

The delta changed as she went downriver. That was the thing about the low country: you could watch the weather arrive like watching a rumor become a mob. The sky over Tallow Bend had been the bruised grey of a normal monsoon season. By the time she passed the drowned orchards at Kessel's Landing, the clouds had organized. They had weight. They moved with intent, a slow rotation in the south that the old delta hands called a *bowl* and never said loudly, as if the weather might take offense.

She stopped at a fuel dock at Halfway Slough, and while the attendant pumped diesel he kept glancing at the radio mounted above his register. It was murmuring the official forecast, the one from the hydrographic office, the one built on the Voss charts: *storm surge of one to one and a half meters expected in the lower delta, well within levee tolerances, no evacuations advised at this time.*

The attendant caught her looking. "You believe that?" he asked, not unkindly.

"It's the official forecast," she said.

"Sure." He wiped his hands on a rag. "My grandmother lived through the flood of '61. She used to say the river doesn't read the reports." He nodded at the sky. "That bowl's been sitting there two days. Water's up a foot at the slough since this morning, and the official gauge at Marsh Point says eight inches. Somebody's reading a different river than the one I'm looking at."

Odile paid for the diesel and did not trust her voice to answer.

She got back on the water and the words followed her. *Somebody's reading a different river.* She had spent her career reading a different river. She had signed the authorizations, buried the clause on page eleven, hired Silas Nakamura with a story about family honor that had been—she made herself look at it squarely, the way she had never looked at the ledgers—a lie. Not entirely a lie. Her family's name *was* tied to Halcyon. Her grandfather had built the station. Her father had helped bury what it found. And Odile had grown up eating off the table that money bought, and had told herself, in the fluent language of prudence, that inheritance was not complicity.

But the towns. She had never let herself think about the towns. Rivergate, Marsh Point, the string of low settlements down the eastern channel—forty thousand people, maybe more, living their lives on charts her family had paid to falsify, trusting levees built to a waterline that was a fiction. And now she had sold the location of the one person trying to tell them, to a lender whose optimism was enforced with violence, and the enforcement mechanism was a monsoon.

The bowl in the south had a face now. She could see the wall of it from the open water past Kessel's Landing, a blue-black rampart of cloud with lightning walking along its base like sentries. The air had gone strange and green. The river, which had been flat all day, had begun to move with a current she could feel through the hull, running *backward*, upstream, pushed by the first breath of the surge.

Her radio crackled. She almost didn't answer it. Then the voice resolved and her stomach dropped, because it was Corrigan's channel, Corrigan's clipped cadence, and he was not talking to her.

"—position confirmed at the Reach, tower access from the north stair. Move on the site at first rain. Nakamura and three crew, plus our man inside has the pages. Nothing transmits. Nothing leaves that station. Understood?"

A pause. Static. Then, faintly, from the other end, an acknowledgment, and Corrigan signed off, and Odile sat in her skiff in the green light with her hand still on the radio and understood exactly what she had done. Not sold a location. Sold a funeral. *Nothing transmits.* He was going to make sure the truth died at Halcyon the way her father's survey team had died there, and he was going to use the storm as the weapon, and the storm was coming because the charts had lied, and the charts had lied because her family had paid them to.

*The river doesn't read the reports.* But Odile Voss had just read one, and it was written in lightning.

She looked downstream, toward the Reach, toward the half-drowned station where Silas Nakamura was right now—she was certain of it, with the terrible certainty of a woman who had finally read the arithmetic—climbing toward a relay tower with a dead man's logbook, toward the truth, toward Corrigan's men coming up the north stair.

She looked at the fuel gauge. Half a tank. Enough to run. Enough to reach the high ground at Tallow Bend, lock her door, and be the woman who saved the Syndicate from a thief. Prudence had a route map, and it had never once failed her.

Then the surge came—not the real one, just the first shove of it, a swell that lifted her skiff and set it down hard, and out of the eastern channel came a family in a flat-bottomed boat, a man poling, a woman holding a child, moving fast and low toward the levee at Marsh Point. Moving the way people move when they trust the water to stay where the reports say it will stay. The child was waving at her. Just waving, the way children wave at any boat, at anything that moves, at a world that has never once lied to them.

Odile Voss turned the wheel downstream.

It was not courage. She wanted that on the record, wherever records were kept, in whatever court eventually asked her to explain herself. It was not courage and it was not redemption and it was certainly not the family honor she had invoked to buy Silas Nakamura's loyalty. It was simpler and more damning than any of that: she had looked at the rising river with honest eyes for the first time in her life, and she could not unsee it, and there was no version of the woman in the charcoal suit who could sit on high ground and listen to the water take forty thousand people who had trusted a chart with her name on it.

The rain hit as she passed the last marker buoy—fat, warm, horizontal, the sky emptying itself all at once. Visibility dropped to a boat-length. The radio spat static and fragments, and then, cutting through the noise, a voice she knew: Silas Nakamura, live on an open channel, breathless, shouting over wind, *"—any station, this is Silas Nakamura aboard the survey vessel out of Rivergate, I have evidence the delta flood charts were falsified, repeat, falsified, the surge models are wrong, the eastern levees will overtop, you need to move to high ground now—"*

Odile's hands went white on the wheel. Corrigan's men would be at the tower already. She could hear it in the transmission's ragged edges, in the way the signal stuttered—someone was cutting it, or trying to. The broadcast was dying by degrees, and Silas was still talking, spending her voice like it was the last money she had.

The Reach rose out of the rain ahead of her, the old station's skeletal superstructure half-swallowed by the flood, and Odile did the arithmetic she should have done fourteen years ago, three years ago, at the fuel dock an hour ago. Half a tank. One skiff. A north stair full of Syndicate men, and one woman at the top of a tower with the truth in her hands.

She opened the throttle and drove her family's name straight into the storm.
