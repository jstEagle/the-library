## 18. Landfall

The sky went green before it went black.

Silas had seen that color once before—over Halcyon Reach, eleven years ago, the afternoon the river took her father. Green like old bottle glass, like the underside of a wave about to close over you. She stood on the station's flooded loading dock and watched the monsoon come down the delta in a wall of gray that erased the horizon, the far banks, the memory of dry land.

"Silas!" Juno's voice cut through the rising wind. "We need to move *now*."

The evidence was strapped against Silas's chest in a waterproof case—her father's logbook, the recovered pages they'd taken back from Corrigan's strongbox, the data drives Mateo had pulled from the vault's sealed core. Everything the Syndicate wanted drowned. Everything the delta needed to hear.

The relay tower rose from the center of Halcyon Reach like a mast from a sinking ship—a hundred meters of rusted lattice, its antenna array still standing because the engineers who built this place had built it to outlast everything except human greed. The only way up was through the station itself: corridors already ankle-deep, stairwells that climbed through the drowned floors toward the tower's base.

And between them and those stairs: Corrigan's men.

"Three of them at the junction," Mateo reported, pressed against a bulkhead, rain hammering the broken skylight above him. "Maybe four. They've got lights."

"They've got orders," Silas said. "That's worse."

The first gust hit then—not wind so much as the atmosphere changing its mind—and the whole structure groaned around them like something waking up angry. Water sloshed across the dock in a sudden surge, cold as a grave. Somewhere below, the river found a new way into the building, and Silas heard it happening: a deep, hydraulic roar, the sound of the delta reclaiming what the charts said it could never have.

*The charts lied,* she thought. *This is what they hid.*

She ran for the junction.

* * *

Juno Park had been in firefights before—small ones, ugly ones, the kind that happened over cargo in ports where the law was whatever the last man off the boat was holding. None of it prepared her for fighting through a building that was actively trying to die.

The corridor ahead of Silas was a shooting gallery of collapsing infrastructure. A pipe burst somewhere overhead and turned the passageway into a waterfall. Corrigan's men fired anyway, muzzle flashes strobing through the spray, and Juno hauled Silas down behind an overturned server rack as rounds chewed through the casing above their heads.

"You're supposed to be the navigator," Silas shouted over the din, "not the shield!"

"I'm multitasking!" Juno fired twice down the corridor, not aiming to hit—aiming to make the other side keep their heads down while she counted exits. There were none. Just the flooded main hall, the stairs beyond it, and four men who knew exactly where those stairs led.

Through the curtain of water she saw Corrigan himself, wading forward with his pistol held low and steady, his face lit by his own headlamp in a way that made him look like something dredged up rather than something alive. He wasn't shouting. That was the worst part. Even now, even here, he moved like a man conducting business.

"Nakamura!" His voice carried flat across the water. "You can't outrun a flood carrying a dead man's paperwork!"

"He wasn't paperwork," Silas shouted back. "He was right!"

The river answered for her. It came down the main hall all at once—a brown wall waist-high and gaining, carrying a door, a desk chair, half of somebody's office in its teeth. One of Corrigan's men went under screaming. The others scrambled for higher ground, and Corrigan—for one unguarded second—looked genuinely afraid.

Juno grabbed Silas's arm. "Stairs. *Now.*"

They ran with the flood at their backs, climbing two steps at a time through water that chased them upward like it had opinions. Behind them, Corrigan's voice rose over the roar, no longer calm:

"After them! Don't let them reach the tower!"

* * *

Mateo Reyes had spent his whole life believing that machines failed for reasons—that every breakdown had a cause you could find, name, and fix. The last three days at Halcyon Reach had tested that faith. This morning it broke entirely.

The tower's service elevator was dead. The emergency generator—the one he'd personally coaxed back to life two days ago, swearing at it in two languages—was flooding in its basement vault. Which meant the relay antenna would be running on whatever residual charge the battery bank still held, which meant Silas might get ninety seconds of transmission time instead of ten minutes.

Ninety seconds. For everything.

"Tell me you have good news," Juno said, hauling herself onto the third-floor landing beside him, soaked to the bone, Silas a step behind.

"I have news," Mateo said. "The battery bank will hold maybe ninety seconds of broadcast. After that, the tower goes dark until the storm passes or the world ends, whichever comes first."

"Then we make the ninety seconds count." Silas was already moving, taking the maintenance ladder toward the fourth floor, the case strapped tight against her chest. "Mateo—when we hit the top, I need you on the transmitter. I'll feed you the files. You keep the signal alive however you have to."

"And if the signal dies mid-broadcast?"

"Then the towns downstream get half the truth," Silas said, "which is more than they've ever gotten from the official charts."

Above them, the tower began to sing. Wind through the antenna lattice, a hundred meters of steel moaning a note that Mateo felt in his molars. He'd rigged boats through hurricanes. He'd never heard anything sound this much like a warning.

He climbed.

* * *

Odile Voss watched the monsoon come ashore from the window of the Syndicate's river launch, and for the first time in her adult life, she understood that she had been reading maps her entire life without ever once looking at the water.

The falsified charts had been her family's masterwork. Her grandfather had signed the survey that moved the hundred-year flood line two kilometers east of where any honest measurement placed it. Her father had defended the forgery in hearings. Odile herself had built a career on the quiet authority of the Voss name—*the family that mapped the delta*—and she had hired Silas Nakamura to retrieve the proof of the lie not to destroy it, she'd told herself, but to *manage* it. To control the story. There was always a way to control the story.

There was no controlling this.

The launch bucked through swells that no chart said should exist this far inland. Ahead, Halcyon Reach was vanishing into the rain, its lower floors already gone, the relay tower leaning into the wind like a man shouting into a gale. And somewhere inside it, Silas Nakamura was climbing toward that tower with the truth in her arms, pursued by men whose employer had just realized that a flood doesn't honor contracts.

Her comm crackled. Corrigan, his voice stripped of all its usual patience: *"Voss. If Nakamura transmits, the Syndicate is finished. Every claim, every lien, every quiet arrangement—we are finished. Get to the tower. Stop her. That is not a request."*

Odile looked at the water swallowing the delta. Towns downriver—Rivergate proper, the ferry villages, the stilt settlements the charts promised were safe—full of people who trusted the Voss name the way they trusted the sunrise.

*"Corrigan,"* she said, and was surprised by how steady her own voice sounded, *"I'm coming to the tower."*

She didn't tell him why.

* * *

The last thirty meters of ladder were the longest climb of Silas's life.

Wind tore at her from every direction the moment she cleared the tower's base platform. Rain came sideways, hard enough to bruise, and the whole structure swayed beneath her in slow, sickening arcs—ten degrees this way, ten degrees that, the antenna array howling overhead. Below her, through gaps in the grating, she could see the flood churning through the station's upper windows. She could see lights moving in the corridors below. Corrigan's men, still climbing.

"Mateo! Talk to me!"

His voice came through her earpiece, thin and ragged: *"Transmitter's hot! Battery's at eighty percent and falling—I've got your window when you're ready!"*

She reached the antenna housing and braced herself against it, fumbling the case open with fingers that wouldn't obey. The logbook fell open in her hands—her father's handwriting, cramped and urgent, the pages she'd carried through drowning corridors and gunfire and eleven years of silence.

*Dad,* she thought. *You tried to send this. They killed you for it. Let's finish the job.*

"Mateo. Broadcasting everything. Go."

* * *

Juno felt the transmission begin as a change in the air—or imagined she did, some electric prickle as the tower threw the truth out into the storm. Down in the stairwell, wedged behind a collapsed door with Mateo's spare pistol in her hand, she heard boots on the landing below and made her choice about who she was going to be.

Not the woman who covered for Silas's secrets. Not anymore. Those days were over, washed away with everything else.

The first of Corrigan's men came around the corner and found himself looking down the barrel of a very determined navigator.

"Turn around," Juno said.

He didn't. Of course he didn't. But the scuffle that followed bought forty seconds, and forty seconds was most of a lifetime, tonight. She took a boot to the ribs, put the man's head into the railing, and kept climbing, because Silas was above her and the truth was above Silas and the flood was below everyone.

On the next landing she met Corrigan himself, and stopped.

He was alone. His men were scattered somewhere below, beaten by water and by her. He stood in the rain-lashed doorway with his pistol raised, and for a long moment neither of them fired, because the tower chose that instant to lean hard into the wind and the whole world tilted fifteen degrees.

"You're done, Park," he said. "It's over. The signal can't reach far enough through this storm. You've thrown away everything for nothing."

Juno spat rainwater and blood. "You don't know her at all, do you?"

Above them, faintly, impossibly, through the howl: Mateo's voice, counting down. And beneath it, if you knew how to listen, the almost-inaudible song of a carrier wave punching through the storm, station by station by station, down the whole length of the delta.

* * *

Silas fed the files into the transmitter with hands that shook and a heart that didn't.

Flood maps. Original surveys. Her father's field notes, page after scanned page. The comparison overlays Mateo had built showing exactly which towns the real hundred-year line would drown—Rivergate's eastern districts, the ferry villages, the stilt towns. All of it going out raw and unedited into the storm, to every relay that could still receive.

*"This is Silas Nakamura,"* she said into the transmitter, because her father had believed the person mattered as much as the data. *"The official flood charts are false. The water is coming. Move east, move high, move now. I'm sorry it took us this long to tell you."*

Ninety seconds. She used every one.

At second seventy, boots on the platform—Corrigan, soaked and wild-eyed, pistol up, closing the distance through the horizontal rain. At second eighty, another figure behind him, and Silas's stomach dropped: Odile Voss, of all people, climbing into the wind with her hair plastered to her face.

Corrigan leveled the gun at Silas. "Shut it down."

"It's already done," Silas said. "Every station in the delta has it. Killing me just makes it a story."

"The battery dies in ten seconds—"

"Ten seconds was enough."

Corrigan's finger tightened. And Odile Voss—soaked, shivering, her family's name dissolving in the rain along with everything it had ever been worth—stepped directly into the line of fire.

"That's enough, Corrigan."

He stared at her. "Voss. Your name is *on* those documents. You of all people—"

"I know exactly what's on them," Odile said, and her voice cracked, finally, beautifully, like ice giving way. "I've known my whole life. Get off this tower."

Below them, the river rose. Above them, the antenna sang. And Silas Nakamura stood in the middle of the storm with an empty case and a full heart, watching the last seconds of the broadcast drain out of the battery bank, watching the signal die not in defeat but in completion—

—watching Corrigan raise the pistol anyway.
