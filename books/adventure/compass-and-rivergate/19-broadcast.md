## 19. Broadcast

The relay tower rose out of the flood like a drowned man's last gesture—one arm up, fingers spread against a sky the color of a bruise going bad.

Silas climbed.

The maintenance ladder was bolted to the tower's seaward leg, and every rung hummed under her hands with the vibration of wind that had stopped being weather and started being intent. Below her, Halcyon Reach had become an archipelago: the research station's rooftops breaking the brown water in ragged lines, the old crane arm jutting like a rib, and somewhere beneath all of it—the vaults, the archive, her father's last workplace, breathing silt.

She didn't look down. Looking down was how you died on ladders. Her father had told her that when she was nine, on a rig outside Port Meridian, his hand steady over hers on the rail. *Eyes on where you're going, Silas. The water will still be there when you need it.*

He'd been wrong about that too. The water had come for him first.

*Keep climbing.*

Wind hit her sideways at the forty-meter mark and she flattened against the lattice, cheek pressed to cold steel, waiting out the gust while the whole structure groaned like a ship taking on sea. Rain came through the gaps in the cross-bracing not as drops but as driven sheets, each one stinging like thrown gravel. Her pack—her father's logbook sealed inside three layers of waterproofing, plus the data slate with the chart comparisons Juno had burned onto it—pressed against her spine like a second heart.

Below, faint through the roar, she heard Mateo's voice crackle in her earpiece: *"Signal chain is live from our end. You've got maybe twenty minutes before the atmospheric interference eats everything above emergency bands. Maybe ten before Corrigan's boat reaches the tower base."*

"Then I'll be quick," she said.

*"You're never quick."*

"I'm motivated."

Juno cut in, her voice stripped down to its bones by the transmission: *"West stairwell's flooded past the third landing. If you have to come down fast, you come down the ladder. Same way you went up. Don't get clever."*

"When have I ever gotten clever?"

The silence on the line was its own answer, and Silas almost smiled as she climbed on.

* * *

The platform at the top of the relay tower was four meters of rusted grating wrapped in a knee-high railing, crowned by the antenna array—a skeletal crown of rods and dishes that the expedition community had abandoned along with everything else at Halcyon. It had been built to push data inland during the delta survey years, back when the charts were honest. Back when her father had stood on this same platform, probably, checking signal strength, believing the numbers he sent downstream would protect people.

The equipment shed at the platform's center had a door that fought her, hinges screaming, then gave. Inside: a transmitter older than she was, its casing furred with salt bloom, but Mateo had spent two sleepless days prepping it remotely through the station's hardlines, and when Silas threw the breaker the console woke with a green blink that looked, absurdly, like welcome.

She shrugged off the pack. Pulled the logbook free. Set it on the console beside the data slate, side by side, paper and glass—two generations of the same truth.

Outside, the monsoon made landfall.

She felt it before she heard it—a pressure change so vast it seemed to press the rain flat, a wall of sound rolling up the delta from the coast like something enormous turning over in its sleep. The wind shifted a full quarter-turn in the space of three breaths and came back twice as hard, and somewhere below, deep in the flooded corridors of the station, metal tore loose with a shriek that she felt in her teeth.

*"Landfall confirmed,"* Juno said, flat as a surgeon reading vitals. *"Eastern eyewall's coming ashore right on schedule. Silas—towns downstream are going to start losing comms in the next hour. Whatever you're going to do, do it now."*

Silas keyed the transmitter. The console chirped, thought about it, and found its voice—a low carrier hum that rose through the grating under her boots.

"Testing," she said. "Rivergate Delta, this is Halcyon Reach relay. Anyone receiving, confirm."

Static. Then, faintly, a voice from Port Andelin: *"—copy, Halcyon. Weak but copy. Who is this?"*

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them shaking and made herself look at them, made herself be interested in the fact, because if she let herself feel what she actually felt she would stand here doing nothing until the water took the tower.

"This is Silas Nakamura," she said. "Daughter of Kenji Nakamura. Some of you knew him. Some of you signed the oath with him." She swallowed. "I'm about to break that oath. Listen anyway."

* * *

She transmitted everything.

The logbook pages, photographed and enhanced by Juno's careful light work. The original survey figures from 2041, in her father's handwriting, next to the official published values—and the gap between them, wide enough to drown in. The internal correspondence from the Rivergate Cartographic Authority, the memos with Odile Voss's family name threaded through them like wire through wax, adjusting the projected floodplain downward year after year because accurate maps would have collapsed land values across half the delta, and collapsing land values would have collapsed the financing, and the financing had names, and the names had enforcers.

The Meridian Syndicate hadn't just lent money against optimistic futures. It had manufactured the optimism itself. It had redrawn the water.

"The official charts say the hundred-year floodline runs south of Tarn Flats," Silas said, voice level, eyes on the waveform meter climbing as stations across the delta began answering, one after another after another. "That's a lie. My father measured the truth and they killed him for carrying it out of this building. The real floodline runs through Tarn Flats. Through Lower Sable. Through the eastern half of Rivergate itself. The monsoon making landfall right now is tracking eight percent east of forecast, and if you live in those towns, you need to move to high ground tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight."

*"This is Port Andelin harbor control,"* a voice cut in, tight. *"Nakamura, those are serious allegations. Do you have verification?"*

"I have the primary survey data. I have the falsification trail. Everything I'm broadcasting is mirrored on six independent relays as we speak—you can't unsend it, and neither can anyone else." A breath. "Verify it yourselves. Pull your own gauge readings from the last twelve hours and compare them to the official stage projections. The river already knows the truth. It's been trying to tell you all week."

The channel lit up like a struck match. Andelin. Graywater. The fishing cooperatives at Tarn Flats themselves, voices overlapping, someone crying, someone swearing, someone—this was the one that undid her a little—an old woman's voice, calm as Sunday, saying simply: *"We heard you, Kenji's girl. We're moving."*

Silas pressed her palm flat against the console and let herself have five seconds. Five seconds of the thing underneath the fear, which was not triumph, nothing so clean—it was closer to the feeling of setting a bone. Something broken for eleven years, finally being forced straight, and it hurt exactly as much as it should.

*"Silas."* Juno again, and the flatness in her voice had cracked into something urgent. *"Boat at the tower base. Two men on the ladder. Corrigan's coming up."*

* * *

Corrigan climbed the way he did everything—deliberately, unhurried, as though the storm were a formality he intended to overlook.

Silas heard him before she saw him: the measured clang of boot on rung, patient as a metronome. She stepped out of the shed and stood at the platform rail with the rain hammering her shoulders, and watched his head rise past the grating edge, then his shoulders, then the rest of him—big, gray-bearded, water sheeting off his oilskin, one hand never far from the pry bar slung across his back.

"Nakamura." He stepped onto the platform and stood there, taking the wind without bracing against it, which was somehow worse than bracing. "You know what I admire about you? Most people who owe the Syndicate as much as you do spend their whole lives looking over their shoulder. You climbed a ladder in a hurricane instead. That's focus."

"You could have taken the logbook at the vault," Silas said. "You had me dead to rights in the lower corridor. Why wait?"

"Because a dead courier carries nothing." He moved closer, unhurried, and the wind chose that moment to lull, as if the storm itself wanted to hear the offer. "Here's the arithmetic, and I'll only do it once. You kill that transmission, hand me the originals, and walk away. Debt's gone. Not deferred—gone. Voss's contract pays out, the Syndicate writes you clean, and in five years you're chasing storms for hire with no leash. Your crew keeps their vehicle, their licenses, their livelihoods. Everyone walks away dry."

"And the towns downstream?"

"Are not in my column." He said it without cruelty, which was the terrible part. Corrigan wasn't a sadist; he was an accountant with forearms. "Nakamura. Look at me. In ninety minutes that surge comes through here and nobody—nobody—will care what was in a dead man's notebook. The Syndicate's leverage dies with exposure, sure, but so does yours. You think the Cartographic Authority forgives? You think Voss does? You burn those charts, you're burning your own name onto them. There's no port in the delta that'll berth you. Is that worth it? For people who trusted the liars in the first place?"

Silas thought about her father's compass, sitting in her jacket pocket, its needle swinging wrong near Halcyon for eleven years like a thing grieving. She thought about the oath—*what we found stays below the waterline*—and how she'd finally understood, standing in the vault with the logbook open in her gloved hands, that the oath had never protected the dead. It had protected the living who profited.

"Yes," she said.

Corrigan sighed, genuinely regretful, and reached for the pry bar.

And that was when Odile Voss came over the platform rail.

* * *

Later, Silas would replay it a hundred times and never quite resolve it into sense. Voss should not have been there. Her escape barge had been sighted heading downstream hours ago; Juno had confirmed it. But here she was, hauling herself over the railing with bleeding palms and mascara running in black rivers down her face, her expensive coat plastered to her like a second skin, and the first thing she said—not to Silas, to Corrigan—was:

"Touch her and the secondary mirror goes into the sea."

She had the backup data drive in one raised hand, held out over the water beyond the railing, her arm trembling with cold or effort or both. "I copied everything. Before we ever left Rivergate. Insurance—you understand insurance, Corrigan, it's the family business. You take that drive from her, I drop this one. You throw me off this tower, my solicitor releases a third copy at dawn regardless. There is no version of the next five minutes where the charts stay buried. There is only a version where you're standing on a drowning tower when they surface."

Corrigan went very still. Rain ran off his beard. "Your name is on those memos, Voss. Every one of them. You sink the charts, you sink with them."

"I know." Something moved across her face that Silas had never seen there in fifteen years of rivalry—something raw and unarmored, terror's honest cousin. "I've known since the vault. Since I read my grandfather's handwriting explaining why Tarn Flats was 'acceptable variance.' I spent my whole life thinking I was climbing toward something. Turns out I was just descending politely." Her voice cracked and she hated it, Silas could see her hating it. "I hired you to destroy the evidence. I'm standing here telling you I won't let you. Make whatever you want of that. I don't understand it myself."

The wind came back like a verdict, slamming the platform, driving rain sideways in silver ropes. Corrigan looked from Voss to Silas to the drive hanging over the black water, and Silas watched the accountant run the numbers one final time—watched him arrive, visibly, at the sum.

"You're both insane," he said. "Both of you. This costs you everything."

"It cost my father everything too," Silas said. "At least mine buys something."

For a long moment the only sounds were the storm and the transmitter humming behind her, still pushing truth downstream into the dark. Then Corrigan unslung the pry bar—and set it down on the grating, deliberately, where everyone could see it was a choice and not a defeat.

"The Syndicate doesn't forgive this," he said. "Understand that. Whatever happens when the water goes down, there are people upstream who don't forget ledgers."

"Good," Silas said. "Let them remember it in daylight."

He went down the ladder into the rain, and the storm took him.

* * *

Voss lowered the drive with both hands, like setting down something breakable, and leaned against the railing, suddenly unable to hold herself upright. "My barge didn't make it past the narrows," she said, not looking at Silas. "If you're about to say something cutting, I'd ask you to wait until I've stopped shaking."

"I wasn't going to say anything cutting."

"That's worse, somehow."

Silas turned back to the console. The waveform meter was stuttering now, the signal fraying at its edges as the atmosphere thickened into soup—but the channel was still alive, still crowded with voices, and the last window was closing.

"One more pass," she said. "Full packet, one more time, for every station that just tuned in. Then we go down and we get off this tower."

She keyed the mic. "All stations, all stations, Halcyon relay, final transmission. The data stands. The floodline is a lie. Move to high ground. This is Silas Nakamura, signing off from Halcyon Reach—" her voice caught, just barely, on the name of the place that had taken her father and given her back the truth, "—and I'm glad somebody finally heard him."

She killed the link. The carrier hum died. In the sudden quiet between gusts, Voss spoke from the railing, very quietly:

"He'd have been proud of you. For what it's worth. From someone whose pride isn't worth much anymore."

"It's worth more than you think," Silas said, and slung the pack. "Come on. The ladder's the only way down, and the surge is coming."

They started down together into the roaring dark—and thirty meters above the floodwater, Silas saw it rising to meet them: a wall of brown water moving up the drowned streets of Halcyon Reach, swallowing the station roofs one by one, faster than anything she had ever seen water move.

"JUNO!" she screamed into the mic. "SURGE! IT'S EARLY—IT'S HERE—"
