## 20. High Water

The surge came like a door slamming shut on the world.

Juno Park heard it before she saw it—a low, grinding roar rolling up the delta channel, the sound of a river remembering it was an ocean. She was waist-deep in the flooded lower gallery of Halcyon Reach, one hand locked around a cable stanchion, the other still gripping the waterproof case with the broadcast drive inside, when the water in the corridor simply *stood up*.

"Surge!" Mateo's voice cracked through the dead air like a whip. The comms were fried—Corrigan's sabotage had seen to that—but sound carried strangely in the drowned station, bouncing off tile and steel until every shout arrived twice, once from its source and once from its ghost.

Juno braced. The wall of water hit the gallery and turned it into a churning brown throat. She went under, swallowed whole, the case torn from her grip—and then a hand closed on her collar. Silas. Of course Silas. Her captain hauled her up by sheer fury and muscle memory, both of them gasping against the stanchion as the flood roared past, tearing doors off hinges, peeling cabinets from walls, carrying a decade of Halcyon's drowned secrets out into the open channel.

"The case—" Juno choked.

Silas pointed. The drive case had wedged itself into the frame of a collapsed bulkhead three meters away, bobbing like a stubborn cork. Between them and it: nothing but moving water and a floating filing cabinet spinning slowly, patiently, like something waiting its turn.

They moved together. Juno had chased storms with this woman for six years and she knew without being told when to step, when to hold, when to trust the count of seconds between waves. Three steps. Grab. Turn. The cabinet clipped Silas's shoulder and spun away into the dark. They got the case, got the cable, got each other, and climbed.

* * *

The upper level of Halcyon Reach was a rooftop garden gone feral—research greenhouses shattered years ago, their frames now strung with delta vines, the whole terrace tilted five degrees toward the river like a ship listing at anchor. The crew's boat, the *Perihelion*, was tied off against the old loading crane, riding swells that shouldn't have existed this far inland.

Juno hauled herself over the rail and lay flat on the wet deck, breathing like a bellows. Around her: Mateo, soaked to the bone, rigging a jury-rigged line from the crane to the greenhouse superstructure; Odile Voss, of all people, hauling a rescue canister across the tilting tiles with the grim efficiency of someone who had decided, somewhere in the last hour, that she would not die looking useless; and Corrigan's men, what remained of them, scattered—two fled downstream in their own skiff before the surge crested, one clinging to a light mast and screaming for help that only they could give.

The monsoon had made landfall forty minutes ago. The sky was a single gray organ note, rain falling so hard and so flat it felt less like weather than like pressure. And below them, spreading across the delta like spilled ink, the flood was doing exactly what the falsified charts said it never could.

Exactly what Silas's broadcast had warned them it would.

"Look." Mateo pointed downstream, toward the scatter of lights along the Rivergate channel—Ashford Bend, Tern Hollow, the string of small towns the official maps had promised were safe behind levees rated for a hundred-year event. Except now those lights were *moving*. Headlamps. Vehicle beams. Whole towns emptying themselves uphill and inland in orderly, urgent streams, because forty minutes ago every radio tower in the delta had carried a dead man's data and a living woman's voice.

"They're getting out," Juno said, and heard her own voice crack on the second word.

"They're getting out," Silas confirmed. She stood at the rail with rain hammering her shoulders, watching the evacuation lights crawl toward high ground, and for a moment—just a moment—the exhaustion in her face broke open into something else. Not triumph. Nothing so clean. Something closer to a debt being paid in a currency no lender had ever asked for.

Then the light mast groaned, and the screaming started again.

* * *

Getting the Syndicate man off the mast took twenty minutes and nearly killed all of them.

He was young—younger than Juno had expected, younger than anyone the Syndicate sent to do its quiet work. His name, when he finally stopped screaming long enough to give it, was Pell. He'd been Corrigan's comms tech, which meant he'd helped cut the *Perihelion*'s lines, which meant Juno should have hated him, and she tried to, she genuinely tried, but hate was heavy and she was so tired, and in the end she just threw him a rope.

Voss caught his other arm as they dragged him over the rail. For a heartbeat the two of them stared at each other—Voss, whose family name was stitched through the falsified charts like a signature; Pell, who had taken Syndicate money to keep that stitching hidden. Then Voss looked away first, and wrapped him in a thermal blanket, and said nothing, and Juno understood that silence was its own kind of confession.

Corrigan himself was gone. In the chaos of the surge he'd vanished into the flooding station with whatever he could carry, and whether he'd drowned or escaped or simply ceased to matter, nobody had the time or the heart to find out. The Syndicate's leverage, though—that had died in the broadcast tower, publicly and permanently. You couldn't quietly ruin people anymore when your quiet power had just been read aloud to every station in the delta. Half the Syndicate's creditors were delta townsfolk. Half its enforcers had families in Tern Hollow. The machine hadn't been destroyed, but its engine had flooded, and everyone aboard the *Perihelion* could hear it sputtering.

"He'll surface," Silas said quietly, beside Juno at the rail. "Corrigan. Men like him always do. But he'll surface smaller."

"And us?"

Silas watched the evacuation lights. "Us too. Just in the other direction."

* * *

Night fell black and howling, and they rode it out lashed to the crane superstructure, the *Perihelion* groaning beneath them like a living thing in pain. There was no sleep to be had, only watches—Mateo keeping the bilge pumps alive with prayer and profanity, Voss sitting rigidly awake with Pell's blanket around her own shoulders now, Silas staring at her father's compass in the cabin light.

Near Halcyon, the needle had always wandered wrong. Juno had seen it herself, back in the early days, back when Silas still talked about her father in the present tense. But tonight, Juno noticed, Silas wasn't looking at the needle. She was looking at the case. The drive. The logbook pages, recovered from Corrigan's abandoned gear when the surge scattered his team like dropped cards.

"You did it," Juno said. It wasn't a question, but it wanted answering anyway.

Silas didn't look up. "We haven't done anything yet. The broadcast bought them hours. The truth has to hold after the water drops. Investigations, hearings, the whole slow machinery." She finally raised her eyes, and Juno saw the exhaustion there, deep as the flooded archive, but underneath it something steadier than she'd ever seen in six years of storms. "But yeah. Tonight, we did it."

Juno sat down next to her, shoulder to shoulder, and let the rain say the rest.

* * *

Dawn came the color of pewter, and with it, the survivors.

They came out of the delta in everything that floated—flat-bottomed skiffs, roof-riding families, a floating market stall with an old woman poling it like a gondola, her produce lashed under tarps and her chin set like a figurehead. The broadcast had given them hours, and hours had been enough. Ashford Bend had emptied with four injuries and no deaths. Tern Hollow, worse hit, had lost buildings but not people. The numbers came in fragments over Mateo's half-repaired receiver, and each fragment landed on the deck of the *Perihelion* like a small, improbable gift.

By midmorning they were hauling people aboard. A family of five from the Tern Hollow fishery. Two levee workers who'd stayed to open the emergency sluices and very nearly paid for it. An ancient ferryman named Odom who shook every hand on deck—including, after a long pause, Voss's.

"I know your face," Odom said. "Your grandmother's face, anyhow. She signed the chart I used my whole life."

Juno watched Voss take that blow standing. Watched her not flinch, not deflect, not reach for the polished deflections that had armored her since the archive. "Then you know better than most what my family owes," Voss said. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking for work. Whatever rebuilding needs doing."

Odom studied her for a long moment, rain dripping off his beard. "Charts need redrawing," he said finally. "Honest ones. That's work enough for a lifetime." He turned away, then paused. "Start with ours."

It wasn't absolution. Juno doubted Voss would ever get absolution, and suspected she'd finally stopped wanting it. But it was a door, cracked open, and Voss stood before it like a woman seeing daylight after a long dive.

* * *

The debt died quietly, the way drowning things do.

Two days later, sheltering in the high harbor at Ashford Bend while the delta drained itself raw, Juno sat in a dockside café that had become the town's unofficial recovery office, and listened to the news come in. The Meridian Syndicate's local offices had shuttered overnight. Its senior partners were "assisting investigations"—a phrase that, in the delta, now meant running. The debt ledger, the one with Silas Nakamura's name inked into it like a brand, had surfaced in the document dump that followed the broadcast, and the delta's magistrates had frozen it pending review. Pending, pending, pending. For the first time in Juno's memory, the machinery of delay was working *for* them.

Silas read the notice three times, folded it, and put it in her jacket pocket over her heart, which was either sentiment or evidence storage, and knowing Silas, both.

"So," Mateo said, sliding a cup of terrible coffee in front of her. "You're rich again. Or at least, you're not hunted. Same thing, right?"

Silas laughed—a real laugh, rusty, startled out of her like a bird. "I'm none of those things. I'm unemployed, publicly infamous, and sleeping on a boat in a flooded delta." She looked around the table: Mateo grinning, Juno leaning back with her boots on a crate, even Voss sitting at the edge of things with her hands wrapped around a cup like it was the first warm thing she'd held in years. "I've never been better."

Juno believed her. That was the strange part. Six years of chasing storms with a woman who treated stillness like a trapdoor, and here she was, sitting in one place, letting the morning happen to her, and she looked more anchored than Juno had ever seen her.

Outside, the river ran high and brown and honest, carrying the last of the flood seaward. Somewhere down that channel, towns were counting their dead—which was to say, coming up short, coming up *grateful*, coming up with names for the voice on the broadcast. Somewhere upstream, investigators were opening the falsified charts like a wound to see how deep it went. And somewhere in Silas's pocket, over her heart, a dead man's data was turning into a living town's future.

Pell, the young Syndicate tech, had given a statement to the magistrates that morning. All of it. Corrigan's orders, the sabotage, the plan to bury the logbook pages in a private vault. He'd cried through half of it, Juno heard, and nobody in Ashford Bend had blamed him.

The quiet power was broken. What grew in the crack was still anyone's guess.

* * *

On the third evening, with the water dropping and the sky rinsed clean for the first time in a week, Juno found Silas at the *Perihelion*'s bow, doing nothing. Just standing. Watching the delta breathe.

"You're not packed," Juno observed.

"Nope."

"No contingency route. No fuel cache staged downriver. No exit."

"Nope."

Juno leaned on the rail beside her. Below, Odom's ferry was making its first run back to Tern Hollow, loaded with lumber and lantern oil and hope. On its deck, unmistakable even at distance, Odile Voss worked alongside the levee crews, sleeves rolled, taking directions from people she'd spent a lifetime looking down on—or being looked down on by; the distinction had gotten pleasantly blurry.

"There's talk," Juno said. "Odom and the fishery council. They want new charts. Real ones. Surveyed honestly, every channel, every levee. Delta-wide." She paused. "They asked for us. By name. Even hers."

Silas was quiet for a long moment. The compass sat on the chart table behind them, lid open, and its needle—Juno checked, because she couldn't help checking—pointed steady and true at north, as if Halcyon had finally forgiven it.

"A contract," Silas said at last. "To stay."

"To stay," Juno agreed. "First one we've ever taken where the job is the opposite of running."

Silas turned the idea over the way she turned a storm cell over—reading its rotation, hunting for the trick, the hidden shear, the reason it would tear apart. She kept finding none. Finally she looked at Juno, and the last of the perpetual motion drained out of her face, leaving something Juno had almost never seen there and recognized only because she'd been waiting years to see it.

Home, choosing itself.

"Well," Silas said, and picked up the pen. "Somebody has to draw the honest map."

She signed her name—and for the first time in longer than Juno could measure, it wasn't guilt signing it.
