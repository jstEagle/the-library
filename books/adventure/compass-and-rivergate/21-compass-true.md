## 21. Compass True

The questions came in the third week, when the floodwater had finally let go of the lower streets and the delta smelled of mud and drying reeds instead of diesel and dread.

They came politely at first. A woman from the Hydrographic Commission with a recorder and a folder of charts that Silas had spent her whole life learning to distrust. A man from the Rivergate Courier who wanted to know what it felt like to broadcast a lie's autopsy while a monsoon chewed the antenna above her head. Then less polite: two investigators with laminated credentials and the particular patience of people paid to find the crack in any story.

Silas gave them all the same thing—the logbook, the recovered pages, Corrigan's transmission records, the testimony of a crew that had no reason left to lie. She gave it sitting still, which was the strange part. She kept waiting for the itch under her skin, the old compass-needle twitch that said *move, move now, they're close.* It never came.

"You understand," said the senior investigator, a gray woman named Adeyemi whose eyes never left Silas's hands, "that the Syndicate will want you dead. Quietly, if possible. Loudly if not."

"The Syndicate is being investigated by four governments and three insurers," Silas said. "Their enforcers are busy hiring lawyers."

"Enforcers don't need lawyers."

"No," Silas agreed. "But they need money, and Meridian's accounts are frozen. Turns out even violence runs on credit." She turned her father's compass over on the table between them, an unconscious habit she'd stopped apologizing for. "You want to know why I didn't sell it. Everyone wants to know that."

Adeyemi waited. She was good at waiting.

"Because I watched what a lie does when it has twenty years to put down roots." Silas set the compass down flat, its brass face catching the window light. "My father died carrying the truth because he thought no one would believe him against the official charts. I wasn't going to let the truth die the same way twice."

The investigator wrote something. Outside, a barge horn sounded long and low across the water, and Silas felt the sound settle into her chest like a hand on a shoulder—*stay,* it seemed to say, *stay, stay*—and for the first time in her adult life, she didn't argue with it.

* * *

Rivergate had changed its shape around her.

The town that had been a waypoint her whole life—a place to refuel, resupply, keep moving—had become something with texture. She knew which café opened before dawn and which one watered its coffee. She knew the harbormaster's daughter by name, and the name of the dog that slept on the fuel dock, and the particular way the morning fog came off the river in October, slow as a held breath being released.

People looked at her differently now. That took adjusting.

Before Halcyon, she'd been invisible by design—one more storm-chaser among dozens, one more debt-ridden drifter the Syndicate could squeeze when it pleased. Now dockhands nodded to her like she'd earned something. An old woman at the fish market pressed a bag of smoked catfish into her hands and refused payment, saying only, "My sister lives in Lowmarsh. She got out because of you." A boy of maybe ten asked her to sign his arm, which she did, feeling ridiculous and strangely moved, and then he ran off shouting to his friends that the Storm Widow had signed him, and Silas stood there in the market noise realizing she had a name in this place—not a reputation, not a bounty, a *name*—and that it fit better than she expected.

The Storm Widow. The expedition community had coined it years ago, after Halcyon, after her father. She'd hated it. It had sounded like a diagnosis.

Now it just sounded like hers.

Juno found her at the market, weaving through the stalls with the navigator's economy of motion she brought to everything, a folded paper in her fist.

"Contract inquiry," Juno said, without preamble. "Official channel. They want a crew with delta experience and—" she glanced at the paper, and something flickered across her face that Silas had learned to read as Juno trying not to smile, "—'demonstrated commitment to hydrographic accuracy.'"

"They saw the broadcast."

"Everyone saw the broadcast. There are children in the interior doing school projects about it." Juno handed her the paper. "It's charting work. Full survey of the lower delta. Rebuilding every map the falsified data touched."

Silas read it twice. The numbers were honest numbers—no Syndicate backing, no impossible payout, no escape hatch built into the terms. Just work. Months of it. Years, maybe.

"It doesn't pay enough to clear anything," Silas said slowly. "Not that there's much left to clear."

"That's what I told them." Juno leaned against a stall post, arms crossed, watching Silas with that level dark gaze that had pulled her out of more trouble than either of them counted anymore. "They said that wasn't the point. They said—" and here Juno's voice went carefully flat, which meant the words underneath mattered enormously, "—they said they wanted the crew that stayed."

The market noise filled the silence between them. Somewhere a gull argued with a fishmonger. The river moved past the docks, brown and patient, going where it went.

"We'd have to base here," Silas said. "Actually base. Not 'port of convenience.' Not 'winter anchorage.' Here."

"I know."

"Voss will come sniffing around eventually. She always does."

"She already sent a message. Three paragraphs, very elegant, offering her family's private survey archives as a goodwill gesture." Juno's mouth twitched. "Mateo wants to frame it. I want to burn it. We're negotiating."

Silas laughed—an actual laugh, unguarded, and it startled her how easily it came now, how little it cost. She folded the contract inquiry and put it inside her jacket, over her heart, which was melodramatic, and she knew it, and she did it anyway.

"Tell them yes," she said.

* * *

She flew the last leg to Halcyon Reach alone, in a chartered skiff, on a morning so clear the horizon looked drawn rather than real.

The Commission had lifted the exclusion zone a week earlier—new surveys, new maps, the whole drowned archive documented and sealed as evidence—and the site sat in the delta light looking smaller than it had in her nightmares. The research station still rose from the shallows on its concrete legs, half-submerged, rust bleeding down its flanks in long orange tears. But scaffolding wrapped its upper levels now. Survey buoys bobbed in neat lines across the flooded courtyard. There was a crane barge anchored off the north wing, and men in hard hats moved across the station's spine with the unhurried confidence of people dismantling a tomb to build something over it.

Silas cut the engine a hundred meters out and let the skiff drift.

This was where her father died. This was where the oath was sworn, in a rain-lashed muster hall with eleven survivors and one empty chair, everyone swearing on their livelihoods and their children's names that Halcyon was closed, finished, cursed, done. She had been twenty-three. She had sworn loudest.

And this was where she'd nearly died herself, weeks ago, transmitting truth through a storm that wanted the tower and everything on it. Where Corrigan's men had chased them through flooding corridors. Where Voss had stood aside at the last moment and let the signal fly—cowardice or conscience, Silas still wasn't sure, and maybe Voss wasn't either yet.

She took out the compass.

Her father's compass. Brass case worn smooth at the edges where his thumb had worried it through thirty years of fieldwork. It had been wrong near Halcyon her whole life—or she'd believed it was wrong, the needle dragging, wavering, pointing at nothing true whenever the station came into view. Mateo had theories: local iron deposits, the station's steel skeleton, magnetic anomalies in the flooded vaults. Silas had her own theory, one she'd never said aloud. She'd thought the compass was grieving. Pointing at the thing that broke it.

She held it level now, over the gunwale, the station dead ahead.

The needle swung once—a lazy half-circle, like something waking—and settled.

North. True, steady north, quivering only with the skiff's gentle motion, holding its line the way a held breath holds until it's finally allowed out. No drag. No waver. No grief.

"Oh," Silas said quietly, to nobody, to the water, to a man thirty years dead whose last transmission she could still recite word for word. "There you are."

She hadn't realized, until that moment, how much of her restlessness had been borrowed. How much of the perpetual motion—the storm-chasing, the debt-dodging, the never sleeping two nights in the same port—had been her body's answer to a question her father's death had planted in her: *if you stop moving, you'll have to feel it.* And underneath that, quieter, crueler: *if you stop moving, you'll have to admit the maps were lies and do something about it.*

She'd done the something. The needle knew it. Maybe needles always knew; maybe it had been pointing steady for weeks and she'd been too afraid to look.

The skiff drifted another few meters. A survey worker on the scaffolding waved—casual, friendly, a stranger greeting a stranger in a place that used to be forbidden. Silas raised a hand back.

Then she started the engine and turned the bow toward Rivergate, and the compass rode open in her palm the whole way home, pointing north, pointing true, pointing nowhere she needed to run.

* * *

Evening found her on the roof of the crew house—which was now, officially, the crew house, lease signed, keys cut, Mateo already talking about a workshop extension and Juno pretending she hadn't already measured the walls for chart racks.

Rivergate spread below them in the blue hour: lamplight coming on along the levee road, boats knocking gently at their moorings, the smell of someone's dinner drifting up from three streets over. The monsoon season was ending. The new charts—honest ones, provisional, stamped DRAFT in red—were pinned up in the harbor office, and towns downstream were using them, arguing with them, correcting them, making them truer.

"You're quiet," Mateo said. He was sprawled beside her on the warm tar paper, engineering the last light out of a beer. "Quiet's suspicious. You're usually packing when you're quiet."

"Not packing."

"Huh." He considered this. "Weird. Good weird?"

"Good weird."

Below, in the yard, Juno was teaching the harbormaster's daughter knotwork by lantern light, patient and precise, and the girl was laughing at her own failures, and Juno—who had once confronted Silas on a swaying deck and demanded the whole truth as the price of staying—was laughing too. Down the street, Voss's rented rooms showed lit windows; Odile had extended her stay twice now, ostensibly to coordinate archive access, actually because leaving had become harder than staying, though Silas doubted she'd admitted that to anyone, including herself.

Silas lay back and looked at the first stars.

Her father had taught her navigation on a night like this, on the deck of a boat she couldn't clearly remember anymore, his voice saying *the sky doesn't lie, little bird, but the charts might—you always check the charts.* She'd spent her life checking charts and finding them wanting, and running from what she found. Now she was going to spend whatever came next drawing them straight.

The debt was dead. The oath was broken properly, on her own terms, in service of the living. Her father's compass pointed true over the grave of the thing that killed him.

And tomorrow there would be investigators, and hearings, and Corrigan's trial creeping through the courts, and Voss's name dragged through both, and hard days ahead that Silas couldn't chart her way around. But tonight—

Tonight the river ran quiet. Tonight she was home.

She was almost asleep when Juno climbed up onto the roof, moving fast, and the rhythm of her footsteps was wrong—urgent, not casual—and Silas's eyes opened before her mind caught up, twenty years of instinct firing all at once.

"Silas." Juno crouched beside her, lantern light catching the paper in her hand. "Harbor office just relayed a message. Priority flag." She paused, and Silas watched her choose the words carefully, which Juno only did when the words were dangerous. "It's from the Commission survey team at Halcyon. They've opened the lower vault for final documentation."

"And?"

"And there's a compartment your father sealed. Behind the logbook cache. It's intact—it was never found." Juno held out the message, but her eyes stayed on Silas's face. "It's addressed to you. By name. He wrote it before the flood took the station."

Silas sat up. Below them, the river went on moving in the dark, and her father's compass sat in her jacket pocket, needle steady, pointing at nothing—waiting, like her, to see which way the truth would turn next.
