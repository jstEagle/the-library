## 22. The Crew That Stayed

**JUNO**

The contract arrived on paper, which was the first strange thing about it.

Nobody in the Rivergate Delta used paper anymore except people who wanted a thing to be real—wanted it heavy in the hand, wanted it to survive water damage long enough to be read by lamplight when the servers drowned again. Juno Park turned the pages over twice before she believed them. The letterhead read *Delta Cartographic Authority, Provisional*, and beneath it, in ink that had not yet learned to be bureaucratic, a single line of scope:

*Survey and re-charting of the Rivergate Delta flood basins, sectors one through nine. Full hydrographic honesty required.*

*Full hydrographic honesty.* As if honesty were a depth measurement. As if you could sound it with a lead line and mark it on a chart.

She carried the pages down to the dock where the *Kestrel's Wake* sat on new pilings, her hull patched, her comms array rebuilt from three different wrecks, her name freshly painted over the scars Corrigan's sabotage had left in her plating. Mateo was underneath her somewhere; she could hear him arguing with a bilge pump in the particular tone he reserved for machinery that had disappointed him personally.

"Contract's here," she called down.

A clatter. Then Mateo's head appeared through the engine hatch, grease-streaked, grinning like a man who'd won an argument even if the pump hadn't conceded yet. "The honest-charts job? The one that pays half what storm-chasing pays?"

"The same."

"And we're taking it anyway." He hauled himself up onto the dock, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last month. "I want to hear you say it out loud, Juno. I want to watch your face while you say it."

Juno looked at the contract. Six months of work. Shallow-water runs through channels that hadn't been properly sounded in twenty years. Town after town where people would come down to the banks to watch them pass, because the broadcast had reached them in time—the broadcast Silas had sent from the tower at Halcyon Reach while the monsoon ate the sky—and because gratitude in the delta was a thing you could feel coming off the water like heat.

"We're taking it," she said, and found that her face did something she didn't expect. It softened. "Because for once we'd be running toward the work instead of away from it."

Mateo nodded slowly, as if she'd passed some test he hadn't told her about. "That's what I figured. Just wanted it on record."

Everything was on record now. That was the other strange thing about the world they'd inherited from the flood: the Syndicate's quiet power had died in the open air of Silas's transmission, but its corpse had been dragged into hearings, into provisional courts, into the kind of scrutiny that made men like Corrigan give depositions instead of orders. Juno had testified twice. She'd expected to feel afraid. Instead she'd felt something closer to relief—the specific relief of setting down a weight so old you'd forgotten it wasn't part of your body.

Her own secret had come out in those rooms too, eventually. The forged transit logs she'd filed for Silas over three years, the false flight plans, the little cartographies of evasion she'd drawn to keep the *Kestrel's Wake* one step ahead of debt collectors. She'd confessed it the way Silas had confessed everything in the flooded archive: plainly, without asking forgiveness first. The provisional authority had fined them both and then, in the same breath, hired them. Apparently honesty, once you started practicing it, compounded.

Footsteps on the dock behind her. She didn't need to turn; she knew the uneven rhythm, the slight favoring of the left leg that the flood surge had left in Silas Nakamura like a signature.

"Well?" Silas said.

"It's real," Juno said, and handed it back. "Six months. Nine sectors. Voss co-signs."

Silas took the pages and read them standing in the sun, and Juno watched her do it, and thought—not for the first time since the tower—that she was watching a woman learn to stand still. It looked harder than any storm run they'd ever done.

**MATEO**

Mateo Reyes had a theory about compasses, which he shared with anyone who stood still long enough and several people who didn't.

"A compass doesn't point north," he'd say, tapping the housing. "It points *home*. North is just where home happens to be for most people. You get a needle that's been dropped, or demagnetized, or haunted—" he'd shrug—"and it stops lying to you about where home is. That's all a bad reading is. A lie about home."

He was thinking about this on the morning they signed, because Silas brought her father's compass aboard for the first time since Halcyon.

It sat on the chart table while Odile Voss—actually *aboard*, actually signing, her expensive coat smelling faintly of river mud because she'd insisted on walking the last stretch of dock rather than arriving by skiff like a person with something to hide—read through the scope clauses aloud. Mateo pretended to check the depth sounder calibration. Really he was watching the needle.

It held steady. Steady as a struck bell. Near Halcyon Reach it had spun like a drunk, had pointed at nothing and everywhere, and Silas had told him once, quietly, in the engine room at three in the morning, that her father's last transmission had come through the same interference—that the compass had always known something about that place that none of them had wanted to hear. Now it pointed north-northwest, calm, certain, toward the delta towns and the survey lines they were about to draw.

"—provisional authority retains publication rights," Voss was saying, "but attribution goes to the survey crew. All of you. By name." She paused over the clause, and Mateo saw her jaw tighten around something. "I insisted on that language."

"You insisted," Juno said flatly, "or your lawyers did, after the hearings made your family name a synonym for fraud?"

"Both things can be true," Voss said, and there it was—the old rival's smile, but worn thin now, translucent, the bone showing through. She signed. Her pen scratched loud in the quiet cabin. When she finished she set the pen down with exaggerated care, as if it might go off. "I want to be clear about what I am to this operation."

"Uneasy partnership," Silas said. "You wrote it into the cover letter yourself."

"I did." Voss straightened. For a moment the ambition came back into her face like weather, the old driving storm of her, and Mateo braced for the speech. But what came out was quieter. "My grandfather drew those charts. My mother defended them. I spent my whole life building on top of a lie because it was the only foundation anyone offered me. I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to let me dig. I'm better at digging than anyone you'll ever meet, and I intend to spend that talent unearthing every falsified document my family touched, until there's nothing left of the lie but the paperwork proving it was a lie." She looked at Silas. "Your father started that excavation. I intend to finish it under his daughter's flag."

The cabin went still. Even the bilge pump, Mateo thought, seemed to hold its opinion.

Silas picked up the compass, weighed it in her palm, and set it down facing Voss across the table.

"My father would have hated working with you," she said. "He'd have done it anyway. He believed charts should tell the truth more than he believed in holding grudges." She slid the contract across. "Co-sign. And Voss—if you ever let ambition outrun honesty on my boat again, I will personally introduce you to the delta's shallows at speed."

"Noted," said Odile Voss, and signed her name a second time, and Mateo could have sworn the needle on the compass didn't waver at all.

**SILAS**

They spent the first week of the survey in Sector One, the lowlands below Rivergate proper, where the channels braided and re-braided like something alive trying to remember its own shape.

Silas ran the bow line herself. Old habit, old grief, old inheritance—all the same muscle now. She'd take the sounding, call the depth, and Juno would log it, and Mateo would cross-check against the satellite telemetry Voss had procured through channels that were probably legal now, probably, and by evening they'd have another strip of honest water mapped where a lie used to live.

The towns came out to watch. That was the part she hadn't prepared for.

At Corder's Landing, the whole village lined the levee—fishermen, grandmothers, kids racing their dogs along the grass—and when the *Kestrel's Wake* passed, they didn't cheer, exactly. They raised their hands. Open palms, lifted toward the boat, held up until she was past. Silas asked an old woman at the fuel dock what it meant.

"It means *we heard you*," the woman said. "Storm night. Your voice came through on the emergency band and my husband got the shantytown moved to high ground ninety minutes before the surge. Ninety minutes." She'd taken Silas's hand in both of hers, hands like tree roots. "Ninety minutes is forty-one people. You count them every day or you stop counting, and I don't stop counting."

Silas had gone back to the boat and sat in the stern through two full soundings without calling a number, and Juno had covered for her without being asked, and that was the crew she had. That was the crew that stayed.

Her father had chased storms his whole life. She understood him differently now. It wasn't restlessness—it was service wearing the costume of restlessness, because a man who mapped floods honestly couldn't afford to be anywhere but where the water was rising. He'd died carrying that service into a place that had been built to kill it, and the expedition community had sworn silence afterward, and Silas had spent eleven years believing the oath protected the living.

It had protected the guilty. She knew that now. Breaking it had been the only way to honor it.

On the eighth evening, anchored off a shoal they'd just proven was forty centimeters shallower than the official chart claimed—forty centimeters between a safe channel and a swamped ferry—she sat on the foredeck with the compass open on her knee and let the stillness settle over her like ballast.

Juno climbed up and sat beside her without speaking. That was their language now. They'd earned the silences.

"Debt's officially dead," Juno said eventually. "Provisional court voided the Syndicate's claims. Fraudulent instrument, they ruled. All of it." She turned the words over like stones. "You're free, Nakamura. You could go anywhere. Any coast, any chase. Nobody hunting you."

"I know."

"That's not an answer."

Silas watched the light die over the water. Somewhere downstream a generator coughed awake in one of the towns they'd re-charted, and she thought: *that light is burning because someone trusted a number we gave them.* There was no chase in the world that paid like that. There never had been. She'd just needed eleven years and a drowned archive to see it.

"My father's compass pointed wrong near Halcyon," she said. "Spun wild. I used to think the place was broken." She closed the lid. The click was small and final. "It wasn't the place. It was the lie. The lie bent every needle that came near it. And now—" she opened it again, showed Juno the steady needle, north-northwest, unwavering—"now the lie's dead, and the needle knows where home is."

Juno studied her for a long moment. Then she laughed, short and real, and knocked her shoulder against Silas's. "Mateo's theory. He'll be insufferable."

"He already is."

Below them, lights were coming on along the shore—one by one, town by town, the delta lighting itself against the dark with numbers that told the truth. Silas watched them and felt the perpetual motion finally, finally wind down inside her, replaced by something heavier and better: the weight of staying.

**ODILE**

Odile Voss kept her own cabin on the *Kestrel's Wake* now—a converted storage locker, narrow as a coffin, and she had stopped minding. Humility, she'd discovered, was mostly a matter of square footage.

She worked nights. That was when the archives spoke most clearly: the microfiche from the old Hydrological Bureau, her grandfather's marginalia in his own hand, the ledger trails that led from falsified charts to construction contracts to the Meridian Syndicate's founding capital. Every night she dug up another root of the lie. Every night she burned a little more of her inheritance to keep the light on.

Tonight she found something that made her sit very still for a long time.

A requisition order, dated nineteen years back. Signed by her grandfather. Authorizing the physical removal of documents from the Halcyon Reach research station—*for secure disposal*—eleven days before the disaster. Eleven days before the flood that killed the survey team. Eleven days before Elias Nakamura's last transmission.

The documents had never been destroyed. The disposal contractor had defaulted; the order bore a cancellation stamp, and a note in a clerk's hand: *materials returned to private holding — M.S.*

Meridian Syndicate. The missing pages of Elias Nakamura's logbook—the ones Corrigan had carried out of the vault, the ones the hearings had catalogued as evidence and then sealed pending trial—were only half of it. There was a second cache. Her grandfather's cache. And it was sitting, right now, in a Syndicate asset warehouse in Rivergate's old industrial quarter, listed in a probate inventory that no one had read because everyone assumed the Syndicate's paper empire had died with its power.

Odile sat in her coffin-cabin with the page trembling slightly in her fingers, and she thought about the woman sleeping ten feet aft, whose father had died chasing exactly this paper.

She could bury it. One page, quietly removed, and the Whitlock-Nakamura account of history would stay tidy and complete, and no one would ever know what else had been hidden, or how deep her family's hand went. Self-preservation wore the same face it always had. It fit her like her grandfather's coat.

Instead she copied the page. Twice. One copy for the provisional authority. One copy she would deliver herself, at dawn, to Silas Nakamura—because if the next excavation was going to happen, it would happen in the open, on the record, with the whole delta watching.

That was the difference between her and her grandfather, she decided. He had dug in the dark. She would dig in the light, and let the light judge what it found.

Up on deck, she could hear the anchor chain shift as the tide turned, and beneath it, faint, the voices of the crew—Juno logging depths, Mateo arguing with the pump again, Silas laughing at something, actually laughing. The sound of people who had chosen to stay in the hardest place on the map.

Odile folded her copies into a waterproof case, checked the chart for dawn's route, and allowed herself, briefly, the unfamiliar luxury of looking forward to morning.

Outside, the river ran true toward the sea, carrying the honest soundings of a new chart on its way to the towns that would build their futures on it—and for the first time in a hundred years, the numbers would hold.
