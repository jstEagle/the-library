## 1. The Wreck They Won't Name

The navy called it Site K-7. Wendell Lockridge called it the *Merrow*, because that was her name, welded into a brass plate he'd pried off her wheelhouse three years ago and sold to a collector in Portsmouth who didn't ask questions. Forty-one meters of coastal freighter, sunk in '52 under circumstances nobody official would discuss, resting on gray sand in ninety feet of green Atlantic water that the charts marked with a dotted line and a single word: RESTRICTED.

Wendell had been diving restricted sites since he was nineteen, when an old salvage man named Coyle told him the secret of the whole business: restrictions weren't walls, they were price tags. Somebody had decided what was down there was worth more than what you'd pay to take it. Your job was to find out who, and how much.

He was forty-six now, and his hands knew the arithmetic better than his head did.

The morning of the dive came in flat and silver, no wind, the kind of day the coast gave you maybe six times a year, as if apologizing for the rest. Wendell ran his boat — a converted fishing trawler named *Half Interest*, because that's what every salvage man actually owns, half of whatever he finds and all of the trouble — out past the breakwater at Grayharbor with the sun still low enough to lay a gold road across the water. He worked alone. That was the first rule after Coyle drowned. Partners split the money; they also split the blame, and the navy had long memories and longer paperwork.

At the site he killed the engine and let the boat swing. Below him, invisible from the surface, the *Merrow* lay on her port side like a woman asleep, cargo hold open, holds full of whatever she'd been carrying the night she went down. The manifest said machine parts. The manifest said a lot of things.

Wendell suited up in silence, checked his gauges twice, and rolled backward off the gunwale into water cold enough to feel like a decision being made.

* * *

Ninety feet down, the world narrowed to the circle of his torchlight.

The wreck had been down seventy years, and the sea had done what the sea does: softened her edges, dressed her in plumage, turned steel into reef. Anemones waved from the railings like pale spectators. A cod the size of a dog watched Wendell work with one flat eye and no opinion whatsoever.

He wasn't here for scrap. Scrap paid the fuel bills and nothing more. He was here because two weeks ago, a man from the naval records office in the capital — a civilian contractor, which meant deniable — had bought him dinner and shown him a photograph of a strongbox recovered from a sister ship, and asked, very casually, whether Wendell had ever worked the K-7 grid. And Wendell, who had survived this long by knowing when a question was bait, had said no, sir, never heard of it.

Then he'd gone home and studied his tide tables and his old charts, and waited for a day with no wind.

The cargo hold was silted to the knees. Wendell moved through it the way he moved through all wrecks — slow, economical, breathing like a man asleep — sweeping his light along the deck plating until it caught something that wasn't rust. Brass doesn't rust. Brass waits.

It was wedged under a collapsed shelf, a case about the size of a bread loaf, its leather long since gone to black slime. He worked it free with a pry bar, taking his time, letting the silt settle between pulls. Inside, packed in wool that had fused into a solid mat, were instruments. A sextant, eaten but recognizable. A box of signal flares fused into one red brick. And on top, in a fitted wooden sleeve, a compass.

Brass cased, gimbal-mounted, heavy in the palm even through his gloves. Wendell tilted it into his light and read the engraving on the back, four lines in a jeweler's script that seventy years of salt hadn't managed to blur:

**E. LOCKRIDGE**
**EXPEDITION OF 1948**
**THE MOUNTAIN PROVIDES**

He stopped breathing for a moment. Not the diver's trained stillness — something older than training. His grandfather's name. His own name, one letter different, the name they'd put on his father's headstone and would someday put on his.

Elias Lockridge had walked up a mountain in 1948 with five men and come down alone, or hadn't come down at all — the family versions differed, and Elias himself, in the years Wendell had known him as a small fierce old man who smelled of pipe smoke and iodine, had never once spoken of it. He'd died when Wendell was eleven, leaving a house full of locked drawers and a legend the family kept the way some families keep a debt: quietly, resentfully, paying interest.

The compass sat in Wendell's glove, needle swinging, finding north, losing it, finding it again. The mountain provides. Elias used to say that. Wendell had thought it was just an old man's saying, the way old men collect sayings the way other men collect stamps.

His gauge showed fourteen minutes of bottom time left. He looked at the open hold, at the dark beyond his light where the rest of the case's contents might be, at everything the navy had written off so thoroughly that they'd hired a stranger to check whether anyone else had written it off too.

Then he did what he'd been doing his whole working life. He took the valuable thing, and he left.

* * *

The liaison's name was Trench, and he arrived at Grayharbor two days later wearing a civilian jacket that fit him like a uniform he'd outgrown. He had the kind of face that had been handsome once and had since been promoted into administration. He sat in Wendell's kitchen drinking coffee he hadn't asked for and laid a photograph on the table: the sister ship's strongbox, opened, empty except for packing wool.

"Salvage rights on this grid are suspended," Trench said. "Has been for decades. But we get rumors. Fishermen, mostly. Divers." He smiled without engaging any muscles above his cheekbones. "You work these waters, Lockridge. You hear things."

"I hear fish prices are criminal."

"You hear anything else, you'll call this number." He set a card down. "There's compensation for useful information. Discreetly handled."

"What kind of information is useful?"

Trench looked at him then, really looked, the way a man checks a lock he already believes is sound. "Anything concerning the 1948 expedition. Any artifacts. Documents. Instruments."

"Instruments," Wendell repeated.

"Navigational equipment. Survey gear." Trench stood, buttoned his jacket. "The expedition was a joint civilian-naval operation. Its records were lost in a fire. We're simply trying to close the file."

"A fire," Wendell said. "Seventy years ago."

"These things take time." At the door he paused. "You have licenses, Mr. Lockridge. Commercial, salvage, explosives handling. They come up for renewal in the spring. Bureaucracy being what it is, I'd hate to see complications."

After his truck pulled away, Wendell sat at his kitchen table with the compass in front of him and the card beside it, and understood that he'd just been threatened by a man who'd never raised his voice, over a wreck the navy officially didn't care about, containing cargo the manifest said was machine parts.

He turned the compass over. **E. LOCKRIDGE.** His grandfather had been a surveyor — that much the family admitted, the way you admit a relative served a prison term. In '48 he'd led six men up the mountain behind Grayharbor, the one the locals called the Old Man, the one with the summit nobody local climbed because the weather owned it. Five hadn't come back. The official report, what little of it survived, blamed weather. The unofficial version, the one in taverns after the third pint, blamed the navy, which had supposedly promised a pickup at a rendezvous point and never came.

And now the navy was buying drinks and asking about instruments.

Wendell carried the compass to the window, where the afternoon light could reach it. Through the glass, forty miles north, the Old Man stood against the sky with its head in cloud, exactly as it had stood when Elias looked at it last.

A man makes his living off other people's losses. That was the honest ledger of salvage: every brass fitting Wendell had ever sold had belonged to someone's last morning. He'd made peace with it long ago, or told himself he had — the dead don't need sextants, and the living need diesel. But holding his grandfather's compass, feeling the needle swing and settle, swing and settle, like something deciding, Wendell understood that the ledger had a page he'd never read. Somewhere out there was a loss with his name on it, and he'd been diving over it his whole life without knowing.

When Trench called three days later — a voice message, brisk, professional — asking whether Wendell had heard anything, seen anything, found anything, Wendell listened to it twice and then deleted it and typed his reply:

*Nothing on my grounds. Will call if that changes.*

It was the first lie he'd ever told the navy, and it fit his mouth so well it frightened him.

That night he wrapped the compass in oilcloth and hid it inside the hollow keel step of *Half Interest*, and lay awake listening to the tide come in, thinking about a word Trench had used. *Instruments.* Plural.

Whatever Elias had carried up the mountain in 1948, there had been more than one of it. And somewhere between the wreck on the seabed and the summit in the clouds, the rest of it was waiting — along with whatever it was that made grown men threaten divers' licenses seventy years after the fact.

Two mornings later, the tide went out farther than it had gone in living memory, and the sea gave something back.
