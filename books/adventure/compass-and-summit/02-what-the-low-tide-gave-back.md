## 2. What the Low Tide Gave Back

The tide went out like a held breath finally released.

Wendell had lived on this coast his whole life, and he'd never seen the water pull back that far. The harbor channel ran thin as a creek. Kelp beds lay exposed and glistening, steaming faintly in the cold morning air, and the rocks below the headland — rocks he'd only ever known as dark shapes under swell — stood up out of the sea like the bones of something enormous.

He was on the water before six, running his skiff along the cliff line with the engine at idle, just looking. Word had come through on the marine band around midnight: a perigean spring tide, lowest in seventy years, maybe ever recorded here. By dawn half the town was down on the flats with buckets and headlamps, prying mussels off stones their grandfathers had never touched.

But nobody was below the cliffs. Nobody wanted to be. That stretch of coast belonged to the navy's exclusion zone, same as the wreck Wendell had been diving two weeks ago, same as everything else the charts marked with a small printed lie: *obstruction, uncharted.*

He should have gone home. He told himself he was going home. Instead he idled into the shadow of the headland where the swell couldn't reach and killed the engine, and there it was.

A cave mouth, black and dripping, forty feet above the exposed seabed.

It hadn't been a cave mouth an hour ago. It had been a notch in the rock, maybe, a seam — the kind of thing you'd paddle past ten thousand times without a second look. Now the low tide had peeled the ocean back from it like skin from a wound, and Wendell could see the arch of it, tall as a barn door, rimmed with old barnacle lines that said the sea came and went from that throat on its own schedule, not anyone's.

His grandfather's voice, thirty years dead, rose up in him unbidden: *There's a door in the mountain, boy. Your great-grandfather saw it at a tide like this one. Never saw it again, and neither will you.*

Family legend. The kind of story told over cards and cheap whiskey, the kind that got bigger every retelling until nobody believed it, including the teller. Wendell had filed it away with the mermaids and the Spanish gold, salvage-diver's folklore.

Except here was the door.

* * *

He anchored in the lee, pulled on his drysuit out of habit more than need, and dropped onto the seabed with a splash that echoed off the cliffs. The bottom was strange country — a forest of kelp lying flat and dying in the air, pools stranded between rocks full of trapped fish turning silver in the light. His boots crunched over shell hash that had never been walked on by anything with lungs.

The cave floor sloped up gently, still wet, still weeping seawater from cracks in the ceiling. He switched on his headlamp and the beam fell into darkness that swallowed it whole.

Forty feet in, the smell changed. Salt gave way to something older — stone dust, dry rot, and beneath it a faint sweetness he couldn't name and didn't want to.

Fifty feet in, he found the camp.

Or what was left of it. A canvas tent collapsed into itself, rotted to lace. A rusted Primus stove. A coil of hemp rope fused into a single stiff mass. And scattered through it all, the small archaeology of men who had stopped expecting rescue: a tin cup, a boot sole, a fountain pen grown a coral of green crystals.

Wendell stood very still, his own breathing loud in the mask, and felt the vertigo come on — not from height or depth but from time. This wasn't a wreck. Wrecks were his trade; wrecks were other people's losses, catalogued and invoiced. This was something else. This was a room where someone had waited to die, and the someone had a name Wendell had grown up hearing spoken like a prayer and a curse in the same breath.

Elias Lockridge. Lost with all hands, 1948. The expedition that walked up the mountain and never walked down.

Except Elias hadn't walked up. He'd climbed. Through *this*. From the sea.

"No," Wendell said aloud, and the cave took the word and gave nothing back.

He found the skeleton twenty feet deeper, past a shoulder of rock where the lamp's reach ended and began again. It sat against the wall with terrible dignity, legs extended, one arm resting across its lap — and in the grip of that yellowed hand, held the way a man holds a thing he means to keep hold of past the end, was a brass compass.

Wendell knew it before his light touched it. He knew the weight of it, the worn compass rose, the hinged lid with its worn thumb-stamp. He had one exactly like it in a drawer in his kitchen, recovered two weeks ago from a wreck the navy wouldn't name, stamped with his own family name in letters he'd lied about to a man with a clipboard.

*Twin.* The word arrived in his mind with the force of a physical blow. Not similar. Not matching pattern. Twin — one for each brother of the expedition, or one for the man who went up and the man who stayed, or one for—

He crouched beside the bones and did not touch them. Some part of him, the professional part, noted details: no boots, boots placed neatly beside the body. No wounds visible on what remained. A man who had arranged himself, then. Who had put his boots aside and sat down facing the cave mouth and the sea beyond it, and waited.

In the lap of the other hand, folded under the compass arm, was a leather case. What remained of it. Green with rot, slumped like a dead thing, but stenciled on the flap in paint that had survived where the leather hadn't:

**SURVEY — 1948 — LOCKRIDGE EXPEDITION**

The letters seemed to move when he looked at them. He blinked and they held still.

*Lockridge Expedition.* His name. His family's name, written on government paper, sitting in a dead man's lap in a hole in the mountain that officially did not exist, on land the navy had fenced off with paperwork instead of wire.

Wendell sat back on his heels inside the drysuit and let the grief hit him, because it was going to anyway. It came sideways, the way it does — not for the skeleton, which was a stranger and wasn't, but for his grandmother, who had waited sixty years for a body that never came and died still setting a place at the table some nights without knowing she did it. For his father, who'd drunk himself quiet over a grandfather he'd built into a giant and then a fool and then, near the end, just a question mark. For himself, a boy on a dock being told stories by men with missing fingers, believing none of them.

Here was the answer. Here it had been the whole time, forty feet above the low-tide line, holding a compass in its hand, waiting for a tide that came once in seventy years.

And the navy knew. Had to know. You don't write off a wreck, seal a headland, stamp a chart with lies unless there's something under all that salt worth burying. The compass in his drawer wasn't salvage anymore. It was evidence. And so was this.

Which meant the survey case was either worthless paper or the most dangerous object on this coast, and Wendell Lockridge had made his living long enough to know that things were never worthless just because they looked like rot.

* * *

He worked fast after that, hands steady in the way they got when the decision was already made and the rest was just labor. He photographed the skeleton with the little waterproof camera in his kit — not for anyone else, for himself, so he could prove later he hadn't dreamed it. He took the compass from the yellowed fingers gently, murmuring something that wasn't quite an apology, and wrapped it in a dive glove.

The case he almost left. Then he didn't.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job across three-quarters of a century, were maps. Hand-drawn, annotated in a cramped engineer's script he recognized from the one surviving letter in the family Bible — his grandfather's hand, unmistakable, the same hard downstrokes. Contour lines. Grid references. Numbers in columns. And on the top sheet, in the margin, a single underlined sentence:

*If Corrick gets this down, none of the rest matters.*

Corrick. The name snagged in him like a hook. He didn't know it. He was almost sure he didn't know it. But it went in deep and stayed there, the way certain words do, waiting.

He rolled the maps into the oilcloth, zipped them inside his drysuit against his chest, and stood a moment longer over the bones of Elias Lockridge.

"I'll come back for you," he said. "Properly. With witnesses."

The cave said nothing. Caves are honest that way.

* * *

The tide was already turning when he came out. The sea had begun its slow crawl back across the seabed, filling the pools first, then the channels, patient as paperwork. In an hour the cave mouth would be a notch again. In two, a rumor. In three, nothing at all — the mountain swallowing its own secret, the way it had since 1948.

Wendell ran the skiff out past the point before he let himself stop shaking. He opened the drysuit at the collar and put his hand flat against the oilcloth bundle over his heart, feeling the crisp edges of the maps through the wrapping, and understood with total clarity that he had just crossed a line he couldn't see the far side of.

Salvage was taking what the sea gave back. This was different. This was theft — from the navy, from history, from a dead man whose last written thought had been another man's name.

He told himself it was leverage. He told himself knowledge was the only inheritance his family had ever actually received, and he'd be damned if he'd hand it to a clipboard and a fence.

Then he ran his skiff into the harbor, tied off, and looked up to see a black SUV parked on the quay with government plates, and a woman leaning against it in a coat too good for this town, watching him with the pleasant, patient attention of someone who has been waiting for exactly this tide.

"You must be Lockridge," she called, smiling. "We've been hoping the water would cooperate."
