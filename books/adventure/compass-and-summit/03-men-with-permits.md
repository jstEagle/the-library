## 3. Men With Permits

They came on a Thursday, three days after the tide gave back the cave, and they came by boat — which told Wendell something before anyone said a word. People who arrived by road wanted to be seen. People who came by water wanted to choose their moment.

He was hosing silt off the deck of the *Maren K* when the cabin cruiser rounded the breakwater, white hull, no name on the transom, riding low in a way that suggested money rather than cargo. It eased into the harbor slip like it had been there before. Maybe it had.

Three people stepped onto the dock. A woman first, mid-forties, in a field jacket that cost more than his winch, shaking out her hair from the wind. Behind her a big man with a shaved head and the particular stillness of someone who'd been paid to stand still in worse places than this. And last, a slight woman with a canvas roll of instruments under one arm, already looking past all of them at the headland cliffs like she could see through them.

"Wendell Lockridge." The first woman said it as a statement, not a question, and offered her hand. "Imogen Vale. We spoke on the phone."

"We didn't."

"Your harbormaster did. On your behalf. He's an efficient man." Her smile was quick and warm and entirely unbothered by the correction. "We're here about the headland. An environmental survey — coastal geology, sediment stability, that sort of thing. Our permits expire at the end of the season, so we're moving fast. We need a local guide who knows the water and the rock. Your name came up in every conversation we've had."

"Who've you been having conversations with?"

"The harbormaster. The dive shop in Port Ellen. The fisherman who watched you pull a compressor off the *Corvina* wreck without bending a fitting." She ticked them off like items on a manifest. "You come well recommended for a man who doesn't advertise."

Wendell set down the hose. The compass was in his wheelhouse safe, wrapped in an oily rag, and he thought about it sitting there the way you think about a lit stove when someone knocks at the door.

"Environmental survey," he said. "Of what, exactly?"

"Sediment cores along the shelf. Cliff-face sampling where access allows. Standard stuff." She said *standard* the way some people said *trust me*.

The big man hadn't moved since stepping ashore. He stood with his weight even on both feet, hands loose, eyes doing a slow circuit of the harbor — the navy buoy marking the restricted wreck site, the coast guard shed, Wendell's truck. When his gaze crossed Wendell's, it stayed a half-second longer than courtesy required. Cataloguing, not greeting.

"And him?"

"Dray," Imogen Vale said. "Site security. Some of our equipment is expensive and some of our permits are delicate. He keeps both intact."

"Permits don't usually need security."

"These do." She smiled again, and this time it didn't quite reach anywhere. "Restricted waters make people curious. Curiosity costs time. Time is the one thing our concession doesn't have."

The third woman stepped forward then, holding out a hand with ink stains on the fingers. "Mara Okonkwo. Geologist." Her grip was brief and dry. She was looking at the headland again, at the cliff line below the old light station, and something in her face was doing arithmetic. "That east face — is there a route up from the water? Below the caves?"

"There's always a route," Wendell said. "Question is whether you come back down it."

"What about the sea caves? At low tide?"

"Low tide like this week's doesn't happen every year. Sometimes not every decade."

"But it happened this week." Mara's eyes came back to him, sharp and dark and patient. "I read the tide tables. That was a fifty-year minimum. Anything exposed in a window like that tends to stay interesting."

Wendell kept his face still. Somewhere behind his ribs, the survey case marked 1948 sat in its hiding place under the floorboards of his mother's old house, and he felt it the way you feel a splinter — small, specific, impossible to ignore.

"Interesting how?" he asked.

"Mineralization. Coastal basalt can host some unusual chemistry if the conditions were right." She shrugged, as if the answer were casual. "What did you find down there?"

"I dive wrecks. I find rust."

Mara held his gaze a beat longer than the question deserved, then let it go — or appeared to. Imogen Vale clapped her hands together once, briskly, like a woman closing one tab and opening another.

"Here's the shape of it," she said. "Six weeks of work, weather permitting. Boat support, shore access, route-finding into the high country above the headland. Triple your standard rate, plus hazard pay above the treeline, plus a completion bonus that would buy you a new boat outright. We cover all equipment, insurance, everything."

"That's a lot of money for sediment."

"It's a lot of money because the window is short and the terrain is real." She said it smoothly, but Wendell had spent twenty years reading people underwater, where nobody bothers to perform. Underneath the charm she was watching him the way Dray was — measuring what he knew against what he'd admit.

Triple rate. The *Maren K*'s hydraulic ram was dying; he'd priced the replacement twice and flinched both times. His diving licenses were due for renewal in the spring, and renewal meant fees, inspections, a medical he wasn't sure his ears would pass anymore. The numbers she was quoting weren't an offer. They were a solution.

And that was exactly why he didn't trust them.

People paid triple rate for two reasons: urgency or secrecy. Usually both. Nobody funded a fifty-year tide window and a security chief and a nameless boat to look at sediment.

"I'll want to see the permits," he said.

Imogen produced a tablet from her jacket without breaking stride, thumbed through to a document, and turned the screen toward him. Official letterhead. Concession boundaries drawn in red along the coast — and Wendell's stomach tightened, because the eastern boundary of the concession ran directly beneath the headland. Directly beneath the cave the tide had opened and closed like a mouth.

"Everything in order," she said. "Countersigned, filed, expiring October thirty-first. After that, the region reverts to open bidding, and frankly, whoever holds the data wins the next round. So we work fast, and we work quietly."

"Quietly."

"Discreetly," she amended, with the grace of a woman who'd made that swap before. "Coastal communities get nervous about surveys. Rumors depress property values, fishermen get superstitious, everyone suffers. We'd prefer the work speak for itself when it's ready."

Wendell looked at the red boundary line a long moment. Then at Dray, who was now examining the *Maren K*'s winch with the expression of a man assessing a vehicle for a long drive. Then at Mara Okonkwo, whose instrument case bore a manufacturer's sticker he recognized — the kind of magnetometer that didn't care about sediment at all.

Knowledge is leverage, he told himself. Whatever they're really hunting, the man who guides them sees every step first. Better inside the tent than outside reading the tide tables.

It sounded reasonable. It sounded exactly like the kind of thing a man tells himself right before he stops being able to tell the difference between using them and being used.

"Triple rate," Wendell said. "Half up front. And I choose the routes."

"Done and done." Imogen Vale's handshake was warm and firm and final. "We start Monday. Wear boots you trust."

* * *

They left in the late afternoon, the white cruiser sliding out past the breakwater into long gold light. Wendell stood on the dock and watched it go, and only when it was hull-down did he let himself breathe out.

Mara Okonkwo had paused at the gangway, just for a second, and looked back at him. Not a smile. Something more like a warning delivered without words — or a request for one. He couldn't tell which, and that bothered him more than Dray's cataloguing stare.

He drove home the long way, past the light station, and parked where the headland filled the windshield, black rock going purple in the dusk. Somewhere up there, above the treeline, was whatever his grandfather had climbed toward in 1948 and never climbed down from. Somewhere under those cliffs, the sea had shown him a skeleton holding a compass with his own name stamped on the case, and a survey case older than he was.

He went inside, pried up the floorboard, and took out the rotted leather case. Inside, the folded pages had survived better than the leather — waxed paper, military surplus, the kind of thing the 1948 expedition would have carried without thinking. He hadn't dared open them fully yet. Tonight, with the consortium's money sitting fresh in his account and their red boundary line burned into his memory, he unfolded the top sheet by lamplight.

Hand-drawn contour lines. Elevations in Elias's careful draftsman's hand. And across the upper margin, in pencil gone brown with age:

*Summit cave — DO NOT let Corrick's people have the full set.*

Wendell sat very still.

His grandfather had known. Seventy-five years before Imogen Vale stepped onto that dock with her warm handshake and her expiring permits, Elias Lockridge had looked at the man he trusted most in the world and written a warning into the margins of his own map.

Outside, the tide was coming back in, covering the cave mouth inch by inch, patient as anything with seventy-five years to wait.

Monday, Wendell thought, folding the page closed. He'd guide them up. He'd watch everything, say little, and keep the one map that mattered folded against his chest.

Leverage, he told himself again.

The word didn't sound as convincing the second time.
