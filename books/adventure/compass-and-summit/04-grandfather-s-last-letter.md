## 4. Grandfather's Last Letter

The envelope had been waiting in the same drawer for thirty years, and it took the compass to make me open it.

I'd gone home that night with salt still drying in my beard, the brass compass wrapped in a chamois cloth in my jacket pocket like something stolen, which in a way it was. My house sits above the harbor on a street that floods twice a year now, a narrow cedar-shingled box my grandfather built before I was born, and inside it there is a room that has been closed since my mother died — her sewing room, which became her storage room, which became the room where everything from Elias Lockridge went to wait.

I turned on the light and stood in the doorway a long time.

Grief does strange arithmetic. For thirty years I had made my living off other people's losses — pulling engines off drowned yachts, cutting free the anchors of boats whose owners had stopped paying, recovering bodies when the coast guard asked and sometimes when they didn't. I told myself it was honest work, salvage, that things belonged with the living. But standing in that room, looking at the boxes labeled in my mother's careful hand — ELIAS, LETTERS; ELIAS, PAPERS; ELIAS, MISC. — I understood that I had never once applied that logic to my own family. Some things belonged with the dead. I had just never wanted to know why.

The letter was on top of the first box, as if she'd left it there for me. One envelope, soft with age, addressed in a slanted engineer's hand: *For Wendell, if he ever asks.*

I was eleven when he disappeared officially, though he'd been gone since 1948, before I was born. My mother never talked about him except once, drunk on Christmas wine, when she said: *He wrote us letters from that mountain and then he stopped writing, and the navy sent a man to tell us he was dead, and that man cried in our kitchen.* She never said the man's name. I hadn't thought about it in decades.

I sat down on the floor among the boxes and opened the envelope with a dive knife, because my hands weren't steady enough for anything finer.

* * *

*August 3rd, 1948*

*Dear Ruthie,*

*We made base camp today and I am writing by lantern light with my fingers too cold to feel the pen, so forgive the hand. The mountain is everything they said and worse. Corrick says worse is what we came for.*

*You remember Lieutenant Corrick — the naval officer who arranged our permits, the one I told you about, young as spring water but with an old man's eyes. He is up here with us as far as the tree line, running the supply line from the boat. I will tell you plainly what I have not told the others: of every man on this expedition, he is the only one I trust without reservation. The surveyors are good men but they are paid men. Corrick asked for nothing. He said the mountain owed him a look and he intended to collect.*

*Tell Wendell's mother the air here tastes like iron. Tell her I'll bring some down for her in a jar if she wants it.*

*Your father,*

*Elias*

* * *

I read it twice. Then I got up, found the whiskey I keep for funerals, and read the rest.

There were nine letters. They spanned six weeks, August into September 1948, and they charted the expedition's climb the way soundings chart a seafloor — each one a little deeper, a little darker, the handwriting degrading as the altitude did its work. The early ones were full of my grandfather's voice, that dry humor my mother must have inherited somewhere else: complaints about the cook, jokes about the mules, careful descriptions of rock strata that meant nothing to me then and would mean everything soon.

By the fourth letter the tone had changed.

* * *

*September 2nd, 1948*

*Ruthie,*

*I'm going to write you something true and then I'm going to ask you to burn it, and we both know you won't, because you're your mother's daughter.*

*We found it. Not at the summit — below it, in a cave the old maps mark as nothing. Harrow (the senior surveyor) has been measuring for three days and he has stopped sleeping. He walks around camp at night with his instruments like a man carrying a baby he's afraid to put down.*

*It's ore. Not the copper we were licensed to look for. Something rarer — Harrow keeps saying words I won't write down, words that belong to governments. He says what's under this mountain could remake the whole coast. He also said, and these are nearly his exact words, that men have killed each other over less than a tenth of it.*

*I want to be clear: I am a diver and a climber and a mapmaker, and none of those trades prepared me for being afraid of a rock. But I am afraid of this one. Not of it — of what it does to the men looking at it. I watched Harrow's assistant try to copy the survey pages last night when he thought everyone slept. Harrow caught him. There was no fight. That was the worst part. They just looked at each other for a long time, and the assistant put the pages back, and nobody said a word, and this morning the assistant volunteered to carry loads across the glacier alone.*

*Corrick came up to high camp yesterday against orders. He looked at the cave entrance for a long time and then he looked at me and said, "Elias, whatever happens, get it written down and get it witnessed." I believe him. God help me, I believe him, and I've been wrong about men before, but not about him.*

*Burn this.*

*Elias*

* * *

I didn't burn it. Neither had my mother.

The letters after that came faster, shorter, the sentences tightening like rope under load. The fifth mentioned that two porters had quit and walked out through the storm. The sixth mentioned that Harrow had sealed the survey case with wax and given it to Elias personally — "because," Harrow said, "you're the only one who doesn't want it." The seventh was mostly instructions: where the case should go if Elias didn't come down, who could be trusted in the harbor town, how to reach a lawyer in the capital whose name I wrote on my wrist in pen like a schoolboy.

The eighth letter was dated September 21st, 1948. It was four lines long.

*They're going up tomorrow without me. Corrick warned me tonight — quietly, at the edge of camp, the way he does everything. Orders came down. I don't know from whom. He's crying, Ruthie. A naval officer, crying into his collar where the men can't see.*

*If I don't come down, I went up anyway. Remember that. Nobody left me. I climbed.*

*Elias*

* * *

The ninth letter was undated. It was in the envelope separately, folded smaller than the others, as if it had been carried a long distance in a pocket.

*To whoever finds this:*

*My name is Elias Lockridge. If you are reading this, I did not come down from the summit cave, and neither did the survey I was carrying, and I need you to understand that both of those facts were choices.*

*The deposit in this cave is real and it is enormous and it is not ours. Not mine, not the consortium's, not the government's that ordered me abandoned rather than delayed. Mountains do not belong to the men who climb them. What comes out of them belongs to everyone who lives in their shadow, and no one living in this mountain's shadow was ever asked.*

*I have hidden the survey where only someone who loved this coast enough to look would find it. I trusted one man to help me do it, and I was right to trust him, and I hope to God he was able to live with what he was ordered to do, because none of it was his fault.*

*Take care of each other. Leave the mountain its own.*

*Elias Lockridge*

* * *

I sat on the floor of my mother's sewing room until the light through the window went from gold to gray to blue, and the whole time my mind kept circling one name like a fish under ice.

Corrick.

A young naval officer named Corrick, who ran the supply line, who climbed past orders to warn Elias, who cried where the men couldn't see him. Who was ordered — by someone above him, someone whose name Elias either didn't know or wouldn't write — to abandon the expedition on the mountain. And who, according to the ninth letter, helped hide the survey afterward. Helped my grandfather make sure the thing worth killing for stayed buried.

I knew that name. I'd been turning it over in my head all evening without knowing why, the way you worry a loose tooth, and now the tooth came out.

I keep my business papers in the kitchen, in a drawer under the phone charger, because I am not a complicated man. Three days ago Imogen Vale had pressed a folder into my hands — contract, insurance riders, liability waivers, and behind all of it, the funding documentation her lawyers insisted I review "for transparency." I'd skimmed it the way you skim anything written by people who can afford lawyers, signing where the tabs pointed, telling myself knowledge was leverage.

Now I pulled the folder out and laid it on the kitchen table under the overhead light.

The consortium had a structure like a ship with false bottoms — a project company inside a holding company inside a trust, names nested inside names. I worked through them slowly, reading aloud in the empty kitchen like a man sounding a channel, and on the fourth page, in the section listing the beneficial interests behind the lead investment vehicle, I found it.

Not the name Corrick.

Worse. Better. Both.

*Admiral (Ret.) T. H. Corrick.*

Not a young officer. An old one. Forty years older, a career between, a rank like a wall built around a single bad night in September 1948. The man Elias trusted above all others had spent his life climbing the same institutional ladder that had ordered the abandonment — and had ended up, at the top of it, funding an expedition back to the summit cave to retrieve what he had helped bury.

Or to retrieve what he had helped hide. I didn't know yet which. Sitting there in my cold kitchen with my grandfather's letters spread out beside a contract I'd already signed, I understood that the difference between those two things might be the difference between a guilty man trying to finish something and a guilty man trying to erase it — and that either way, I had just agreed to guide them up the mountain.

Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one, and somewhere down at the marina the consortium's charter boat rode at anchor, riding easy, patient as a hook.

In the morning, Imogen Vale would expect me at the dock at seven. In the morning, I would hand over my grandfather's compass to no one, keep the survey case hidden where I'd stashed it, and walk aboard that boat wearing the face I wear for clients — the face that says *everything's fine, the water's fine, trust me.*

And somewhere above the tree line, in a summit cave my grandfather had died choosing, the answer to whether Thomas Corrick was finishing his debt or collecting on it was waiting in the dark, charging admission.

I put the letters back in their envelope. I put the envelope in the waterproof bag I use for charts.

Then I set two alarms, because tomorrow I would need to be a liar twice before breakfast — once to the consortium, and once to myself.
