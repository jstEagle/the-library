## 5. The Approach

The trail left the treeline at dawn, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge, Wendell had stopped counting switchbacks.

Twelve of them, plus him. Imogen Vale set a pace that looked casual and wasn't — she'd done this before, somewhere, and her boots found the rhythm of the mountain like she'd been briefed on it. Dray walked sweep, always twenty meters back, always where he could see everyone's hands. The four porters moved in their own quiet economy ahead, loads balanced with the indifference of men who had carried worse for less. And Mara Okonkwo ranged the flanks, stopping every few hundred meters to hammer a hand lens against rock faces or kneel over scree like a woman reading letters addressed to someone else.

Wendell carried the survey case against his spine, inside his pack, wrapped in a dry bag that had been around the world twice. He'd told himself on the drive up that knowledge was leverage. He'd told himself it again at the trailhead. By the third hour he'd stopped telling himself anything and just felt the weight of it, square and patient, riding between his shoulder blades like a second lung.

"You climb like a diver," Imogen said, falling into step beside him. "All shoulders. Like you're waiting for the water."

"Old habit. You hold your breath long enough, it follows you uphill."

She smiled without looking at him. "Dray says you've been quiet about the headland caves."

"Dray asks a lot of questions for a security chief."

"He's thorough." A pause, measured out like rope. "So are we. That's why we pay triple rate, Wendell. For thorough people."

Above them, the weather was doing something it shouldn't. The forecast window Imogen kept on her phone showed three clear days, then a wall of gray marching in off the Pacific like it had an appointment. But the lenticular cloud forming over the summit already wore the smooth, ugly shape of wind nobody had paid for.

* * *

Mara had known from the first briefing that the numbers didn't add up.

Environmental surveys didn't fly in ex-military security. They didn't need helicopter contingency budgets or satellite uplink schedules or a clause in the contract about "discretion regarding subsurface findings." She'd signed anyway — the money was real, the fieldwork was real, and after eleven years of grant committees telling her to narrow her scope, a consortium offering her the whole headland to herself was a temptation shaped exactly like her weaknesses.

But the core samples they wanted weren't environmental. She'd run the assay requests through her own lab contacts before leaving the coast, quietly, the way she did everything. The target elements were rare earths. Neodymium. Dysprosium. The metals that lived inside every motor, every turbine, every weapon system the century was building. Someone wasn't asking whether the mountain was healthy.

Someone was asking whether it could be owned.

She watched Wendell now across the campsite as they made cold camp below the col — no fires above the treeline, Dray's rule, though Mara suspected the rule served surveillance more than fire danger. Wendell moved like a man conserving something. She'd seen it before in divers and miners: people who worked in places that killed you slowly learned to spend nothing they didn't have to.

She crossed the camp and crouched beside him while he checked his crampons.

"Your grandfather," she said, keeping her voice under the wind. "Elias Lockridge. 1948 expedition. That's why Vale hired you. Not the guiding."

Wendell's hands didn't stop moving. "You've been reading my file."

"I wrote half of it. The geology section." She glanced toward Dray, a dark shape at the perimeter doing his slow arithmetic of sightlines. "The original survey your grandfather ran — the one the navy classified — I've seen fragments. Assay values from the upper cirque. If those numbers are real, there's a deposit under this mountain worth more than everything on the coast combined."

"And that's bad?"

"That depends who holds it." She stood, brushed grit from her knees, and let her voice drop further. "Rare earths aren't dug politely, Wendell. Open pits, acid leaching, tailings ponds the size of towns. If that survey surfaces into the wrong hands, this coastline becomes a hole in the ground with a harbor attached."

Wind came off the glacier and flattened the grass. Somewhere above, loose stone ticked down the moraine like a clock.

"Why tell me?" Wendell said.

"Because you're the only one here who isn't being paid enough to lie." She held his eyes a moment longer than was comfortable, then walked back to her tent, and Wendell sat with the case against his spine feeling heavier than it had that morning.

* * *

Dray found him alone at first light, which was how Dray did everything — alone, at the edges, when no one else was watching.

They were an hour below the col, the team strung out along a ledge with a drop on the right side that went down far enough to make conversation unnecessary. Dray fell into step beside Wendell and matched his pace, hands loose at his sides, eyes forward.

"Nice morning," Dray said.

"It'll snow by noon."

"It always does eventually." He let a few steps pass. "You know what happens to dive licenses these days? Coast Guard audits, insurance reviews. One complaint from a federal contractor and a man finds himself dry for a year. Two, maybe. Long enough to lose the boat."

Wendell kept walking. "Is there a question in there?"

"Just context." Dray smiled the way a closed door smiles. "Vale likes you. Thorough people stick together. But thorough people also notice things, and when they notice things, they report them. Up the chain. Cleanly. Nothing gets handled sideways — not compasses, not cases, not family heirlooms found in restricted wrecks."

The word *compasses* landed with the precision of a tool chosen for the job. Wendell felt his pulse go flat and slow, the way it did at forty meters with the bottom still invisible below him.

"I don't handle anything sideways," he said.

"No," Dray agreed pleasantly. "I didn't think you did."

He dropped back to sweep, and Wendell climbed the rest of the ledge listening to his own breathing and understanding that the threat hadn't been a threat. It had been a receipt. Proof of purchase, filed away, to be produced later at a price.

* * *

By noon it was snowing, fine and dry, the kind that came sideways and erased the trail behind them. The col appeared out of the white like a rumor — a saddle of black rock between two shoulders of the mountain, wind screaming through it from the far side. The porters got the tents up in twenty minutes with the competence of men who wanted this over, and then everyone crowded into the cook tent while the storm tested the guy lines.

Imogen spread the route map on a crate. "Window closes tomorrow night. We move at first light, hit the upper cirque by midday, sample, and descend before the front lands. One day up top. That's all we need."

"One day," Dray said, "if nobody wanders."

His eyes went to Wendell when he said it. Nobody else seemed to notice. Mara noticed.

Later, in the blue hour before sleep, Wendell sat in his tent with the dry bag open and Elias's last letter in his gloved hands, reading it again by headlamp because the words hadn't changed and he needed them not to.

*I have given the survey to no one. Corrick knows why. Whatever they offer me on the way down, the mountain can keep it better than any man alive.*

Outside, the wind rose another register, and Wendell thought about the lenticular cloud, and the wall of gray coming off the Pacific, and how every window on this mountain was already closing.

Tomorrow they would reach the cave. He could feel it pulling at him through the rock and the snow, patient as tide, and somewhere above it, whatever waited there had been waiting since 1948 for exactly this kind of fool to come knocking.
