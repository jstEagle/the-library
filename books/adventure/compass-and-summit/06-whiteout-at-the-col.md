## 6. Whiteout at the Col

The weather window closed the way Mara had seen windows close in the Karakoram: not with a wall of cloud but with a slow dimming, as if someone were turning down the sun a lumen at a time. By noon the light on the glacier had gone pewter. By one the wind had a taste to it — iron and cold stone — and by two the porters were moving with the particular economy of men who had grown up under this sky and knew what it was about to do.

Mara counted them twice. Six porters, four clients, herself. Eleven souls on a ridge that was about to stop existing.

"Col by four," she told Imogen over the radio, shouting against the wind even though the wind wasn't the problem yet. "We sleep at the col or we don't sleep at all."

"Copy," Imogen said. Then, after a pause that was a half-second too long: "Dray wants to know about the samples."

"The samples are in my pack. The samples are fine. Tell Dray the mountain doesn't care about his schedule."

Another pause. Mara had spent three weeks with these people and had learned to read their silences the way she read ice — by what was absent. Imogen's silences said *I agree with you and can't say so.* Dray's silences said nothing at all, which was worse.

She clipped into the fixed line and kept moving.

The col itself was a saddle of wind-scoured ice between two shoulders of black rock, a notch in the headland's high country that the old maps called, in faded pencil, *the Pass of the Two Winds*. Whoever had named it had not been poetic. They had been accurate. The wind came through from the seaward side in gusts that arrived like freight, and from the landward side in a constant grinding hiss, and where the two met above the saddle they made a turbulence that could pick a man off his feet and hand him to the void with the courtesy of a customs official.

They reached it at 3:40, twenty minutes ahead of her own estimate, and Mara allowed herself one breath of something like relief. Then she looked west and stopped allowing herself anything.

The cloud was not coming. It was *here* — a gray-white ocean pouring over the far shoulder, silent, total, eating the horizon one ridge at a time.

"Tents," she said. "Now. All four, anchored to the snow, guy lines doubled. Nobody wanders. Nobody *anything*."

The porters moved fast and well. These were local men, hired in the valley town, led by a broad, quiet man named Sefu who had climbed this headland since he was a boy carrying supplies for scientific parties that never said what they were studying. Sefu didn't talk much, but when he anchored a tent, the tent stayed anchored. Mara had quietly reorganized the whole rope order on day two so that Sefu's team carried the heaviest loads, because his team was the only one she never had to think about.

It was the other rope team that failed.

* * *

It happened at dusk, in the first hour of the whiteout, when the world had been reduced to a sphere of gray about four meters across and the wind was doing a hundred kilometers an hour and still building. They had all four tents up. They had the stove going in the cook tent. Mara was inside her own tent logging the day's GPS fixes when she heard it — not a scream, which would have been almost better, but a sound like a heavy rope snapping taut, and then a voice, Sefu's voice, raw with a register she'd never heard from him:

"Rope! Rope! *Rope!*"

She was out of the tent before she'd finished deciding to be.

The whiteout was total. She could see nothing — nothing — except the faint orange smear of the cook tent's fabric and, below the saddle on the seaward side, a dark shape that should not have been there. The second rope team had gone out to secure an extra cache of fuel drums against the wind, a routine chore, and something had gone wrong with it. Later she would reconstruct it: a buried anchor that wasn't buried deep enough, a gust that came in the gap between gusts, a porter named Tomas who had unclipped for thirty seconds to reposition a drum. Thirty seconds. On a mountain, thirty seconds is a geological era.

Tomas was on the slope. The slope was forty degrees of wind-hammered ice running four hundred meters down into a crevasse field that the storm had erased from existence. His rope was anchored — barely — to a deadman that was already creeping, the snow around it pluming outward in little exhalations with each of Tomas's involuntary swings.

And Wendell Lockridge was already moving toward the edge.

She would think about this later, in the way you think about a photograph: frozen, detailed, unfair. Wendell on his belly at the lip of the slope, boots kicked into the ice, one hand locked on his own anchor, the other paying out rope from the team's spare coil with the unhurried precision of a man who had spent thirty years hanging off things that wanted him dead. A salvage diver's hands. Hands that knew, in their tendons, that panic is just a faster way of falling.

"Talk to me!" Wendell shouted down into the white.

Tomas's voice came back shredded: "I can't — my boots — I can't find—"

"Stop looking. Stop moving. I'm coming to you."

"No—" Sefu, grabbing Wendell's harness from behind. "No, I go, I am lighter—"

"You're anchored where I need *you*," Wendell said, and there was something in his voice Mara hadn't heard before. Not the careful, watchful, half-distrustful tone he'd used with Dray since the trailhead. Something older. "Sefu. Anchor me. Both lines. Now."

And Sefu, who did not take orders from clients, took this one.

Mara dropped into the anchor chain behind them, third in the system, her ice screw already going in. The wind hit them in waves now, freight after freight, each one trying to peel the whole human arrangement off the mountain like a label. She could hear Dray shouting something from the tents — *my men, my equipment, get my equipment* — and she made a decision she would not regret: she stopped listening to him entirely.

Wendell went over the lip on two lines, face-down, in the manner of a man descending a ladder that wasn't there. The whiteout swallowed him whole. For eleven seconds — she counted, because counting was the only thing her hands could do usefully — there was nothing below the lip but gray.

Then: "Tomas. I've got you. Look at me. Look at my face. There."

The rescue took forty minutes and was, in Mara's professional judgment, a small masterpiece. Wendell reached Tomas, clipped him to a second system, got a boot wedge into a hidden ledge that only a man who read ice the way Mara read strata could have found, and talked the whole time — low, constant, a river of ordinary words about nothing, about the taste of cold coffee, about how his grandfather used to say the mountain only borrows what it means to give back. Tomas stopped shaking somewhere in the middle of it. When they came up over the lip, one after the other, the porters made a sound Mara had only heard once before, at a mine collapse in the Andes: not cheering. Something quieter. The sound of men putting a name back on a grave they'd dug in advance.

Tomas's left boot was gone. Taken by the ice somewhere in the fall. His ankle beneath it was broken, cleanly, the way bones break when the mountain is being almost merciful.

* * *

They operated by headlamp in the cook tent, Mara reducing the fracture while Sefu held the boy's shoulders and Wendell — who turned out to have splinted worse things on worse nights, on decks, in black water — cut and taped a walking boot around the splint. The stove hissed. The storm leaned on the fabric with the weight of a standing ocean.

"He cannot walk down," Sefu said. Not a question.

"Not tomorrow," Mara said. "Day after, on the fixed lines, if the wind drops. I'll carry the pack. Wendell takes the rope behind him."

"You'll lose two days," said Dray from the tent door.

It was not a question either, but it was not a statement about Tomas. Mara set the tape down and looked at him. Snow had gotten inside his hood and was melting down the collar of a jacket that cost more than a porter's yearly wage. His face was doing nothing at all, which was how it did everything.

"We'll lose two days," she agreed. "The mountain keeps the receipt."

"The fuel cache is gone. Half the food with it. That changes the math."

"The math," Mara said, "is that a man almost died tonight and your first inventory is tonnage."

Dray looked at her for a long moment. Then at Wendell, who was still taping, unhurried, not looking up. Then at Tomas, who was gray-faced and trying to apologize and being told by Sefu, in their own language, to stop being a fool.

"Rations change at dawn," Dray said. "Everyone's. Including mine." And he left, which was either discipline or a message, and Mara suspected it was both.

Wendell smoothed the last strip of tape. "He's right about the math," he said quietly.

"He's right about the arithmetic," Mara said. "That's not the same math."

Wendell almost smiled. Outside, the storm hit the col with a gust that flattened the tents and held them down, and somewhere below in the erased dark was the crevasse field, and below that the sea, and somewhere in the sea, Mara thought, watching Wendell's hands, was a wreck this man wouldn't name and a compass she'd seen him touch at night like a man touching a scar.

"You knew that ledge was there," she said. "On the slope. You read it in a whiteout."

"I've been falling off things since I was nineteen." He tied off the tape. "You learn where the mountain hides its handholds. Same as you learn where the rock hides its ore."

It was the closest he had come, in three weeks, to the thing he was actually here for. She let it sit between them, unclaimed, the way you leave a coin on a table to see who picks it up.

He didn't pick it up. He banked the stove, checked Tomas's toes for circulation, and went out into the storm to re-anchor the tents.

* * *

Mara did not sleep. At two in the morning, wedged in her tent with the wind trying to read her field notes over her shoulder, she took out the fragment — one page of the 1948 survey, a photocopy of a photocopy, provenance the consortium had never explained to her and she had never explained to them. Rare-earth assays. Concentrations that made her sit down the first time she'd read them, alone, in a hotel room, with the curtains drawn.

A deposit like that didn't make a region rich. It made a region *strategic*. It made it a thing that governments wrote memoranda about and navies wrote off wrecks near. It made the word *environmental survey* a legal fiction wearing a very expensive coat.

She thought about Tomas's boot, somewhere down in the ice now, a small dark loss the mountain had taken as its tax. She thought about Dray's arithmetic. She thought about the name on the funding page she probably wasn't supposed to have seen — Corrick — and the fact that a naval officer's name does not usually appear on mining consortium paper unless the navy wants plausible deniability more than it wants credit.

The storm screamed. The tent held.

At dawn the wind dropped as if a hand had been lifted, and the whiteout tore open in rags, and Mara stood outside in the blue, brutal cold and looked up at the summit ridge — one day's climb now, maybe two with Tomas — and saw, just below the summit, a black seam in the rock. A cave mouth. Facing the sea.

Through her binoculars, at that distance, in that light, she could see something else: a cairn. Old. Man-made. Standing at the cave's threshold like a doorpost.

Someone had been there before them. Someone had marked the door.

And beside her, following her gaze, Wendell Lockridge went very still, in the way of a man who has just recognized his own name written on a mountain.
