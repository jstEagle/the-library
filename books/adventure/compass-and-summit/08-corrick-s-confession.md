## 8. Corrick's Confession

Dray had been in rooms where men decided other men would die. Briefing rooms in Kandahar, a shipping office in Lagos, a hospital corridor in Baltimore where a general held his wife's hand and gave the order with his eyes. He knew the texture of those rooms. This one — a canvas shelter lashed to the summit ridge, wind screaming through the guy lines like something trying to get in — was the same room wearing different clothes.

The helicopter came up out of the cloud deck at 0700, exactly on schedule, because old money did not arrive late. It set down on the shelf of scree below the cave with the delicacy of a heron landing, and Dray went down to meet it alone, as instructed.

Corrick climbed out unassisted. That surprised Dray. Ninety-one years old, according to the file, and he came down the skid like a man stepping off a ferry. Tall still, straight-backed, dressed not in expedition gear but in a wool overcoat that belonged to another century. His face was a map of fine creases, and his eyes were pale and clear and did not blink enough.

"Mr. Dray." The voice was dry paper. "You have the look of a man who does his job."

"I do my job."

"Yes. I chose you for it." Corrick looked past him, up at the black mouth of the cave. "Is it true? You found Elias Lockridge's camp?"

"We found a camp. A journal. And the survey."

Corrick closed his eyes. For a moment — just a moment — the ninety-one years arrived all at once, and Dray watched an old man stand very still in the wind as if waiting for it to knock him down.

"Show me," Corrick said.

Wendell heard the helicopter before he saw it, and by the time it lifted off again he was standing at the cave mouth watching three figures climb toward him through the spindrift: Dray, Mara, and an old man in a wool coat moving with a strange, deliberate grace, like a man walking to a gallows he'd built himself.

He knew the face from the photographs in his grandmother's attic. Younger by seventy years, but the same long jaw, the same pale eyes. The man who stood third from the left in the 1948 expedition photo, one hand on Elias Lockridge's shoulder.

"You're Corrick," Wendell said.

"I am." The old man stopped an arm's length away. Up close his coat smelled of cedar and jet fuel. "You have his chin. And his stubbornness, I'm told."

"You knew my grandfather."

"I carried him on my shoulders for two days of that climb." Corrick said it simply, without sentiment. "And then I left him here to die. Would you like to know why?"

The wind filled the silence. Wendell found he couldn't answer.

"Sit," Corrick said. "This will take as long as it takes, and we are all of us short on time."

They sat inside the cave, around the cold stove, in the same hollow where Elias had made his last camp. Corrick lowered himself onto a pack with the care of a man whose bones were a hundred years of paperwork, and began.

"The navy sent us up in '48 to find out what the mountain was sitting on. Cold War arithmetic — everyone wanted rare earths, and this coast geology said maybe. Elias was our surveyor. Brilliant, half-feral, trusted nobody except—" he paused "—except me. I was twenty-three. He taught me to read ice. He called me the son he didn't have yet."

Wendell's hands were fists in his lap. He made them open.

"We found the deposit. All of it. Elias mapped every seam. And then the radio call came down from the fleet — orders signed above my rank and his. The deposit was to be classified. Not developed. *Buried.* Because if the consortiums and the Soviets both learned what was here, the coast would be fought over for fifty years. So the survey was to come down with us or not exist. And the men who knew about it—"

Corrick's voice caught, the first crack in the dry paper.

"—the men who knew were to come down too. Or not. My orders were explicit: if the weather closed and the survey could not be secured, I was to descend with what I carried and report the team lost. Elias gave me the case himself. He thought I was going for help. He shook my hand and told me to tell his wife he'd see her by Christmas."

Dray, standing by the entrance, looked away.

"I walked down that mountain carrying his life's work," Corrick said, "and I let them write him off as a storm casualty, and I spent seventy-seven years telling myself it was duty. It wasn't. It was obedience. There is no dirtier word in any language." He turned the pale eyes on Wendell. "Now you've found what I buried twice. And history has come back around — the permits expire in nine days, the consortium wants the deposit, and there are men in Washington who remember what this mountain cost the first time. So I am going to offer you a trade, Mr. Lockridge, and I want you to hear all of it before you refuse."

"I haven't refused yet."

"You will. But listen anyway." Corrick leaned forward. "The survey becomes property of the trust I control. Your grandfather is officially recovered — a hero, a naval funeral, his name on a memorial wall. Every debt on your boat paid. New boat, if you want it. Your diving licenses restored — yes, I know about the suspension; I sign the letters that make those things happen. And you walk away clean. No record, no questions, no consortium, no Dray. You keep the compasses and the letter and your grandfather's good name, and the mountain keeps everything else. Wealth and erasure, Mr. Lockridge. It is the only honest product I have ever sold."

"And the deposit?"

"Stays buried. That is the entire point." For the first time something moved behind the pale eyes — something that might have been grief, or its fossil. "I did not survive seventy-seven years to watch this coast get strip-mined in my grandson's lifetime. But I cannot hold the line alone anymore. I need the survey in trusted hands, and yours are the only ones with a claim to it. Give it to me, and I give you your grandfather back — the version of him the world can live with."

Wendell sat very still. It was a good offer. That was the obscenity of it — it was a genuinely good offer, generous even, wrapped around a truth Wendell wanted more than air: Elias Lockridge, hero, recovered, named. His grandmother's attic photograph captioned at last.

He looked at the old man's hands, folded like a dead bird's, and thought: *those hands shook my grandfather's hand and then walked away.*

"No," Wendell said.

Corrick didn't flinch. "May I ask why?"

"Because you don't get to decide what my grandfather's name is worth. And neither do they." He nodded at Dray. "The survey doesn't go to your trust, or their consortium, or anyone. It goes into that stove, and then it goes home with me as ash, and my grandfather stays where he chose to stay. On the mountain. With his work."

"That is not your decision to make," Dray said quietly.

"It's the only part that is."

Corrick sighed, and it was the sound of a door closing after seventy-seven years. "Mr. Dray," he said, not looking at Wendell, "the contingency."

Something in Wendell's chest went cold. "You said wealth and erasure."

"I offered it," Corrick said. "He declined. I am too old to fight, Mr. Lockridge, and too tired to lie about what happens next. The survey comes off this mountain today. How much of you comes with it is a variable I have asked Mr. Dray to minimize." He rose, slowly, gripping Dray's offered arm, and would not meet Wendell's eyes. "Your grandfather forgave me once. I don't deserve it twice."

They walked him out to the helicopter pad. Wendell stood in the cave mouth and watched the old man climb aboard without looking back, and understood, finally, the shape of the thing he'd inherited: not a discovery. A debt. Seventy-seven years of interest, come due on a summit in a storm.

Dray stayed behind. He didn't reach for a weapon. He just rolled his sleeves, unhurried, a man clocking in, and nodded at the survey case under Wendell's arm.

"We can do this the way where you walk down on your own feet," Dray said. "Or the way where the mountain keeps another Lockridge. Either way, I fly out of here holding that case."

Wendell backed toward the stove, and put his hand on the last of Elias's kerosene.
