## 9. Descent in the Storm

The stove was a squat iron thing Elias had carried up in 1948, and it still held a smell of kerosene and old smoke, as if the mountain had been keeping his fire warm for him.

Wendell knelt beside it with the survey case open on his knees.

Dray stood between him and the cave mouth, one hand resting easy on the flare pistol at his belt, the way a man rests a hand on a dog he hasn't decided to feed. Corrick sat against the far wall in Elias's camp chair, a blanket over his knees, seventy-six years of altitude in his face. Mara stood near the entrance with a headlamp beam full on Wendell's hands, saying nothing, watching.

"Think about what you're holding," Dray said. "That's not paper. That's a hundred years of jobs. Schools. A port. You'd burn a region's future because your grandfather had a grudge?"

"My grandfather had a compass," Wendell said. "And orders nobody followed through on."

He fed the first page to the stove.

The paper caught with a soft, almost apologetic sound. The ink flared green for a heartbeat — the contour lines of a deposit that could remake the coast — and then it was just fire. Wendell fed it the second page, then the third, working steadily, the way he worked a line through a block. Dray took one step and stopped, because Mara had shifted her lamp to his eyes and because Corrick had begun, very quietly, to laugh.

It was not a happy sound. It was the sound of a man hearing a door close on a room he'd been locked in since 1948.

"I told them it would come to this," Corrick said. "I told the consortium. That boy's blood doesn't sign things."

"Shut up, sir." Dray's voice had gone thin.

Page by page. The deposit map. The assay summaries. Elias's careful pencil notations in the margins, *rich beyond sense, God forgive us what this will do to the coast*. Wendell read each one for a half second as it burned — not to memorize it, he told himself, but because it seemed like a debt of attention owed. The last sheet was the cover page, stamped 1948, and he held it a moment before letting it go, the way you hold a body at the surface a moment before letting it sink.

Then it was ash, and the stove was just a stove, and the mountain outside was screaming.

Dray moved fast. Faster than a man that size should. He didn't go for Wendell — he went for the case, snatching it, finding it empty, and the sound he made was almost a growl. "You have no idea what you've cost. There are copies. There's always—"

"There aren't," Corrick said. "I made sure of that in '48. One survey. One case. I was supposed to bring it down myself." He looked at his own hands. "I left it with Elias instead. I told myself it was safer with him. I told myself a great many things."

Dray turned on him. For one moment Wendell thought the man might strike a seventy-nine-year-old in front of witnesses, and he braced to move — but the storm decided the matter. The cave mouth lit white and the thunderclap came so hard it felt physical, a fist against the rock, and somewhere above them something cracked and boomed — avalanche country waking up.

"We're leaving," Wendell said. "Now, all together, or nobody goes. That's not a negotiation. That's weather."

Dray looked at the stove, at the ash, at Wendell, and Wendell watched him do the arithmetic of a man whose career had just burned at six hundred degrees. Whatever he concluded, he kept the flare pistol where it was. "Move," he said. "All of you. If anyone slows us down—"

"Then we slow down together," Mara said. "That's how rope teams work. You'd know that if you'd ever been on one."

* * *

They came down out of the cave into a world with no edges.

The whiteout had eaten the summit, the sky, the horizon. Wind hit them like water, sideways, carrying ice that stung through two layers. Wendell clipped the front of the rope and put himself first, because he knew the descent pitch and because he did not trust Dray behind him and did not trust anyone else in front. Mara took second. The two consortium porters — Vasquez and the quiet one, Okafor — came next, and Dray took the rear, which Wendell hated and could do nothing about.

Corrick wouldn't come.

"I'm staying," he said, at the cave mouth, shouting against the wind. "I'm staying with Elias."

"You'll die here."

"I've been dying here since 1948. This is the first time it's felt like a choice." He had Elias's journal in his hands, the one they'd found in the camp, and he held it against his chest like a child's toy. "I read it while you burned the survey. He forgave me, Wendell. Page after page, he wrote about forgiving me. He never sent it because he thought I'd be court-martialed. He was protecting me while I was protecting myself." The old man's face was wet, and it wasn't the storm. "Someone should stay with him. Someone should keep the fire. Go. Take them down. That's your inheritance — the going. Let me have the staying."

Wendell looked at him a long moment. Then he took the second compass — the skeleton's compass, the twin — from his pocket and pressed it into Corrick's hands.

"Keep it warm," Wendell said. "Both of them."

And he turned into the white.

* * *

The col nearly killed them at the second rappel.

The anchor Elias's team had left in 1948 — a rusted piton behind a flake — held for Vasquez and failed for Okafor, the flake letting go with a crack like a rifle. Wendell felt the rope go slack-heavy in his brake hand and did the thing you train for and pray never to do: he transferred Okafor's weight to his own anchor, a slingshot loop around a horn of granite, body as the last piece of gear. The load came on like a truck. His shoulder screamed. Ice drove into his face. He held, and Mara got Okafor's weight onto the lower station, and for thirty seconds the storm was full of nothing but rope and breath and the small animal sounds of a man who thought he was going to die and hadn't.

Then it was done, and they were all on the lower ledge, and Wendell's arm was a dead thing at his side.

"Your shoulder's out," Mara said, feeling it with quick hands.

"It's been worse."

"Liar. I'm reducing it. On three. Don't be brave, be still."

She did it against the rock, fast and clean, and the world went white at the edges and came back. He breathed. She tied his harness into the rope herself, her hands steady, her eyes furious. "Dray was ten meters back when that flake went," she said, low. "He didn't move. He watched."

"I saw."

"He was going to let Okafor die and blame the storm."

"I know."

"Then why are we roped to him?"

"Because the storm doesn't care what he is," Wendell said. "And neither do I, right now. Right now everything on this rope gets down or dies trying. What he is can wait for sea level."

They moved. Down through the col, down the fixed lines they'd come up, the storm tearing at them, Wendell navigating by a memory of the mountain he'd built on the way up — a cairn here, a lean of the ridge there — counting paces when he couldn't see, Mara calling depths of snow with her pole like a diver calling bottom. Twice the wind knocked them flat and they crawled. The mountain took its tax in everything: the food cache at the col had been ripped open and scattered, the tent gone, Vasquez's fingers going gray-white despite the mitts.

And behind them, always behind them, Dray.

Wendell heard him before he saw him — the heavy metronome of his boots on the descent, closer each hour, closing as the others slowed. Not helping. Not carrying Vasquez's pack when she staggered. Just closing distance, patient as tide, and Wendell understood with a cold clarity that had nothing to do with cold: Dray had decided the survey's destruction needed a witness who didn't survive to testify. All of them, probably. Or maybe just the two who'd seen the cave. The storm was his alibi and his accomplice, and he was herding them toward the icefall traverse where a rope could "fail" and no one would ever prove otherwise.

At the icefall traverse, Wendell made his choice.

"New order," he shouted into the wind. "Mara, you lead. You know the line. I'm last."

Dray's head turned. For a moment, through the spinning snow, their eyes met, and Wendell gave him a small nod, the nod of a man surrendering position — and saw Dray understand it as exactly that. The weak one giving up the anchor. The diver putting himself where the dead weight goes.

Mara led them across the traverse, fast and brilliant, reading the ice like water. Wendell came last, unclipped from the running line, moving free above the crevasse field because free was the only way to be fast enough for what he'd decided.

Dray came off the running line too.

He came for Wendell in the white, in the roar, where no one could see — a hand grabbing his collar, the flare pistol's muzzle jammed up under his jaw, Dray's mouth at his ear: "You should have taken the money, Lockridge. You should have been a smaller man."

Wendell had spent thirty years learning what moving ice does.

He didn't fight the man. He fought the mountain. He dropped his weight, drove his axe into the slope, and cut the rope above them — the running line, the one Dray had clipped into and trusted — with the knife he kept for tangled nets and drowned men. Not Dray's tether. The line. The mountain's own rope, the one that had been groaning under the storm's loading for an hour, the one Mara had flagged as fractured an hour before.

The slope moved.

Not an avalanche — a slough, a shedding, the mountain shrugging off a skin of storm snow. Wendell rode it on his axe, swimming, a diver in white water, and came out of it against a boulder with the world roaring past him.

Dray did not come out of it.

Wendell hung there in the aftermath, chest heaving, listening to the mountain settle, and felt for the flare pistol — gone, both of them, swallowed — and felt, beneath the adrenaline, beneath the grief he refused to examine yet, a terrible clean thing that was not quite relief.

He had not pushed him. He had cut a rope that was dying anyway, and the mountain had done the rest, and he would carry that distinction for the rest of his life in the place where other men carried trophies.

He found the others below the icefall, huddled in the lee of a serac. Mara took one look at his face, then at the empty slope above.

"Dray?"

"Gone."

She looked at him a long time. The wind filled the silence. "The line was fractured," she said at last. "I flagged it. It was failing."

"It was failing," Wendell said.

And that was all they ever said about it, then or after.

* * *

They came down out of the storm two days later, or three — time had gone soft — four figures where five had gone up, moving like survivors of a shipwreck, which was what they were. Wendell carried Vasquez on the last day when her feet went, Okafor carrying her pack, Mara navigating them through the fog line to the coast road where Imogen Vale stood waiting by a consortium truck, and Wendell watched her face do the arithmetic: five up, four down, no survey, no Dray.

She looked at Wendell. He looked back. And he saw in her eyes that she had known what the survey was from the beginning, had maybe known what Corrick was, and had come anyway, and would now have to decide what kind of lie to tell her backers.

"Permits expire Friday," she said quietly.

"I know," Wendell said. "The mountain keeps what it's given."

Behind them, high above the fog line, a fire was burning in a summit cave, tended by an old man who had finally come home — and somewhere below the headland, in water no one would ever dive again, a brass compass turned slowly toward north.
