## 10. Compass and Summit

The inquiry took nine days.

They held it in the harbor master's office, which smelled of diesel and old varnish and the particular staleness of rooms where men decide what happened. Mara went first, then the two porters, then Wendell. Dray's death was ruled a mountaineering accident — a slip on verglas above the col, a fall no rope could have caught. The storm was cited. The storm was convenient. The storm had eaten everything else: the samples, the core logs, the environmental readings that had, by every account, come back marginal at best.

"Marginal," Imogen repeated at the end of it, folding her hands on the scarred table. "That's the word we'll use. The concession area shows no economically viable concentrations. The consortium will not renew."

The permits expired on a Tuesday. Wendell watched the date pass the way you watch a tide turn — nothing dramatic, just the water quietly changing its mind. The consortium's charter vessel left the harbor that afternoon, riding high and empty, and nobody on the dock asked it questions. Men with permits, Wendell thought. Men without them were just tourists with expensive jackets.

Mara stayed a week longer. She came down to the *Pelican* on her last evening with a bottle of something amber and two glasses, and they drank in the wheelhouse while the harbor lights came on one by one across the black water.

"I've been doing this twenty years," she said. "Ore bodies, fault lines, the deep crust. You learn to read rock like a face." She turned her glass. "That deposit in the summit cave — I've never seen its equal. Not in papers. Not in the field. Never."

"I know."

"And you burned it."

"I burned it."

She nodded slowly, and Wendell braced for the argument — the geologist's grief for a thing of beauty lost, the scientist's fury at a truth erased. But Mara only looked out at the water for a long time.

"There's a village two valleys east of the headland," she said finally. "Forty families. When I was doing the preliminary transects, I saw what a mine of that size would need. Roads. Tailings ponds the size of lakes. A processing plant, because rare earths are worthless in the ground and poison coming out of it. That village would be a memory inside five years." She drained her glass. "I've watched companies promise prosperity to places like that my whole career. The prosperity never arrives. The trucks do." She stood, set the bottle down, and touched the doorframe on her way out. "You did the arithmetic faster than I would have, Lockridge. Don't let anyone tell you that was cowardice."

Then she was gone, and the harbor was quiet, and Wendell went back to the work of being a man who made his living off other people's losses.

* * *

He waited for the equinox tides.

The charts promised the lowest water of the year, and the sea kept its promise. By mid-afternoon the rocks below the headland stood exposed in their full black architecture, kelp hanging like rigging from a drowned ship, and the cave mouth at the base of the cliffs opened its dark throat to the air for the first time since he'd stood inside it with a skeleton's hand around a brass compass.

He rowed out alone in the dinghy. He'd considered asking someone to come — Mara, maybe, or old Teller from the chandlery — but some cargo you carry yourself or not at all.

The climb down was easier than he remembered, which was its own kind of grief. The first time, he'd come down those rocks not knowing what waited in the cave. Now he knew everything, and the knowing made the descent feel like walking into a room where a funeral had already happened.

Inside, the tide pool at the cave's heart lay still and green-lit, holding the sky in miniature. Elias's camp was still there where they'd found it — the rotted canvas, the rusted stove, the small tin box Wendell had already emptied. The summit cave above was sealed now; the consortium's people had left nothing but boot prints and a cold ring of stones where Dray had made his arguments. Corrick had stayed up there. Wendell had watched the old man settle himself against the cave wall beside the place where a young officer had once sat and listened to Elias Lockridge talk about the future. Corrick had said only one thing as the helicopter lifted away from the col without him: *Tell them I went to see an old friend.* Wendell had reported him missing in the storm, along with everything else. The mountain could keep that too.

He took the second compass from his jacket.

It was the twin of the one from the wreck — same brass, same glass, same needle trembling toward a north that meant nothing down here. He'd stamped the family name himself once, in his mind, on both of them. *LOCKRIDGE.* His grandfather's hands had held one of these. His grandfather's hands had frozen holding the other.

Wendell crouched at the edge of the tide pool and thought about all the things a man could do with an object like this. Sell it — collectors paid obscene money for expedition relics, and the story alone would double it. Donate it — a glass case in a maritime museum, a placard, schoolchildren filing past. Keep it — set it on the mantel above the fire, a daily reminder of what he was owed and had refused.

All of it was just different ways of making the dead useful.

Elias had come up this mountain believing in something — a survey, a future, a partnership with the navy, a young officer he'd trusted above all others. The mountain had taken his belief and kept it, along with his bones, and the world had gone on for seventy years without knowing, and the coast was still here, and the village two valleys east was still here, and the water in the tide pool was still clear.

Wendell held the compass over the water.

"Grandad," he said. The word echoed strangely in the stone throat of the cave, and he didn't say anything else, because there wasn't anything else. The letter in his breast pocket — the one he'd taken from the tin box, the one addressed in a freezing hand to *my daughter, if anyone ever comes down* — was the whole of the inheritance, and it said nothing about ore. It said: *Tell her the summit was worth it. Tell her the view was the discovery.*

He let go.

The compass turned once in the air, needle swinging, and then the water took it without a splash worth the name. The green light folded over it. The needle would find north down there in the dark for as long as the brass lasted, and the sea, which had carried his family's name on its wrecks and its losses for three generations, could hold this one too.

He stayed until the tide began to come back, filling the cave mouth with its cold gray insistence, and then he climbed out into the ordinary evening light and rowed home.

* * *

Imogen was at the dock when he tied off.

He'd half expected it. She had a way of appearing at the edges of things — the harbor office, the inquiry, the last night of the survey — never quite center stage, never quite gone. She stood at the end of the pier in a wool coat too good for the weather, watching him coil the painter.

"Mr. Lockridge."

"Ms. Vale. Come to audit my salvage?"

"Come to say the consortium is dissolved. The backers are dispersing. There will be no second expedition — the permits lapsed, and the preliminary data was, as reported, marginal." She paused. "That's the official language. You'll hear it repeated in the right rooms until it's simply true."

"And the unofficial language?"

She looked at him for a long moment. The harbor light caught the gray coming in at her temples, the fine tired lines around her eyes. When she spoke, her voice was lower.

"I read the funding trail before I ever took this job. Corrick's name was on the founding instruments — buried, but there. I knew what we were really looking for before our boat cleared the breakwater." She turned her face toward the headland, black against the last of the light. "I've spent thirty years moving money for people who want to move mountains. I told myself this one was different. Cleaner. A survey, a concession, a legacy." A dry sound, almost a laugh. "Then I watched a security chief threaten your licenses, a porter nearly die at the col, and an old man confess to a murder of omission at eight thousand feet, and I understood exactly what I'd been carrying in my briefcase."

"Why say this now?"

"Because you burned it, and I said nothing. Because I could have flagged Corrick to the whole team on day one and I didn't, and a man is dead who would be alive if I had." She met his eyes. "I'm not asking absolution, Mr. Lockridge. I'm telling you that some of us suspected all along, and the ones who suspected and stayed quiet — we're part of why you had to burn anything at all."

The wind came off the water, carrying salt and the faint diesel sweetness of the harbor. Somewhere out past the breakwater, the sea was already covering the rocks below the headland, closing the cave's throat for another season, or another decade, or forever.

"Then we're even," Wendell said. "You knew what they wanted. I knew what my grandfather found. Neither of us said the word out loud until it was too late to matter." He shouldered his gear bag. "You asked me once, on the approach, why a salvage diver would guide an environmental survey. I told you knowledge is leverage. That was true. But here's what I learned at the top of that mountain, Ms. Vale — leverage is just a weight, and it cuts both directions. The survey could have remade this coast. It could have unmade it. Same pages. The only difference was whose hands held them."

"And so you chose no hands at all."

"I chose the mountain's hands." He looked up at the headland, where the summit cave was already invisible, already a rumor. "My grandfather wrote one letter that made it down. It didn't mention the ore. Not once. Seventy years freezing up there, and the thing he wanted remembered was the view." Wendell felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight since the wreck, since the cave, since the col — not forgiveness exactly, but the end of a debt. "Some wrecks should stay sunk, Ms. Vale. Some surveys should stay burned. Not because the truth is dangerous. Because some truths belong to the place that holds them, and we were never going to carry them gently."

Imogen studied him the way she must have studied contracts — looking for the clause, the loophole, the angle. She didn't find one.

"Corrick's family will be notified," she said at last. "There's a foundation in his name. Quiet money, good causes. It will outlive all of this."

"Let it. Quiet money for quiet mountains." Wendell extended his hand, and she took it, her grip cool and firm. "You could have reported the survey as promising. Bought yourself another season, another round of permits. Why didn't you?"

Imogen smiled — small, tired, real.

"Because I read your grandfather's file too," she said. "And because I watched you come down off that mountain carrying nothing, and I thought — *there's the only inheritance worth having.*" She released his hand and stepped back toward the harbor road. "Goodbye, Mr. Lockridge. Mind the tides."

She walked away into the dusk, a woman with no permits and no briefcase, and Wendell stood a while on the deck of the *Pelican* watching the headland fade into the dark.

Then he went below, took Elias's letter from his breast pocket, and read it one more time by lamplight — *tell her the view was the discovery* — before folding it away in the drawer where he kept the only things that mattered: his diving log, his father's photograph, and now this.

Outside, the tide was coming in. The compass was finding its north in the dark water. And the mountain, keeping its secret the way it had kept it for seventy years, said nothing at all.
