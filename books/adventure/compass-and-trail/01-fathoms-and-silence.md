## 1. Fathoms and Silence

The river doesn't know I'm here. That's the arrangement.

I've been underwater for eleven minutes when the hull of the *Corvane* rises out of the silt like a whale that decided, somewhere along the way, that swimming was overrated. Forty meters of naval patrol boat, sitting in sixty feet of water it was never supposed to touch, in a stretch of river no civilian chart admits exists. The navy says there's nothing down here. The navy paid me to agree with them. Two different payments, actually—one to look, one to forget what I looked at—and I've learned in fifteen years of this trade that the second payment is always the larger one, and always the lie.

My name is Tamsin Nakamura. It is also not my name, but it's the one that fits best in the mouth, and after a while a name stops being who you are and becomes just another piece of equipment you maintain. Like regulators. Like masks. You check it for wear, you replace it when it fails, and you never, ever let anyone else breathe from it.

I check my gauge out of habit rather than need. Fifty-one feet. Visibility maybe eight, which for this river is a gift. The current up here runs lazy and brown-green, thick with spring melt, and it moves everything except the truth. The truth sinks.

* * *

Let me tell you how I keep books, because it matters later.

Most men measure their lives in years or dollars or children. I measure mine in miles logged. Bottom miles, transit miles, miles swum blind under ice with a line in my fist and a prayer I don't believe in clicking between my teeth like a loose filling. Fifteen years ago I started a ledger—paper, pencil, waterproof sleeve, kept nowhere near anywhere I sleep—and every dive goes into it. Date, coordinates rounded to the nearest lie, depth, duration, salvage recovered, salvage reported. The gap between those last two columns is the autobiography of a man who does not exist.

Because that's the other thing. There is no Tamsin Nakamura on any government roll, any tax form, any lease, any hospital intake sheet east of the mountains. I pay cash. I own a truck registered to a dead man whose death I did not cause, though I attended the funeral, because it seemed polite, and because cemeteries are excellent places to establish an alibi. My fingerprints belong to a sailor lost off the coast in '09, God rest him, and my dental records belong to nobody at all, because I had the work done by a veterinarian in a country that has since reorganized itself so thoroughly that even its regrets have new names.

This isn't paranoia. Paranoia is fear without evidence. What I have is a filing system.

Fifteen years ago someone sat me down in a room with no windows and explained, with great patience, that the person I had been had made certain errors of loyalty, and that the state had generously agreed to correct the error by deleting him. They gave me a choice of new names off a list, like ordering from a menu. I picked the one with the fewest syllables. Then they shook my hand—which I still think was a strange touch, the handshake, as if we'd closed a business deal instead of a grave—and I walked out into a life measured entirely in fathoms, because water is the only place left where nobody can ask me who I am.

Underwater, everyone is anonymous. That's not poetry. That's physics.

* * *

The *Corvane*'s stern gun mount comes first, then the bridge windows, green glass gone opaque with sixteen months of algae. She went down in November, officially due to "structural failure during a training exercise," unofficially due to whatever makes a hole in a hull below the waterline that the navy then spends four hundred thousand dollars dredging mud over. I know about the dredging because I was offered the contract to supervise it and declined, and declining things in my line of work is its own kind of signature. Somebody remembered my signature. Hence today's job.

The client contacted me the way all my clients do: a note in a bait shop cooler two hundred miles from where I live, written in block capitals on the back of a fishing license belonging to neither of us. Recover sealed case from aft compartment. Do not open. Do not photograph. Payment on confirmation of silence. The fee was triple my rate, which in salvage means either the object is worthless and they're testing me, or it's priceless and they're terrified. Experience says terrified. Fear pays better than greed; greed haggles.

I swim aft along the port rail, past a life ring still stenciled with her name, the letters soft as old fruit. The compartment hatch is where the schematic said it would be—the client had provided a schematic, which told me more than the schematic intended, because civilians don't have deck plans for active patrol boats, and neither, technically, do I.

Inside, the dark is total. I run my light along the bulkheads and watch the beam get eaten by suspended silt, that slow snowfall of a drowned world. And there it is, third locker on the right, exactly as promised: a case the size of a suitcase, matte gray, no markings, no corrosion, no marine growth.

That last part is what stops me.

Everything down here grows. Steel blooms rust like coral, rubber sprouts slime, canvas goes to rot in weeks. Sixteen months in river water and this case is clean. Not wiped clean—*sealed* clean, some kind of gasket system breathing inert gas, and the material itself isn't steel or aluminum or anything I can put a decade of experience against. It has the faint warmth-to-the-touch quality of certain polymers I've seen exactly once before, fifteen years ago, in a room with no windows, on a table between myself and the man holding my old life in a manila folder.

I should note here that I did not open the case. The instructions were clear, and I am, whatever else you want to say about me, a professional. But I will confess to holding it against my palm for three full seconds longer than the job required, feeling that hum—not sound, not vibration exactly, something between, like holding a sleeping animal—and thinking: *this is fifteen years ahead of anything that should be in a river.*

Salvage has a rule older than salvage: technology floats downstream. Whatever the military invents today washes up in civilian hands in ten years, fifteen if it's good. I have pulled sonar units, guidance chips, composite hulls, things that made collectors weep. I know the clock. This case isn't running on that clock. This case is running on a clock from a different year entirely—a year I won't see unless I live long enough to regret it.

I clip it to my harness. It weighs forty pounds dry, feels like twenty in the water, and hums against my chest the whole ascent like a second heartbeat with opinions.

* * *

Surface. Gray morning, mist on the water, the far bank a rumor of pines. My boat is a fourteen-foot aluminum skiff with a trolling motor and the personality of a rowboat, chosen precisely because nobody looks twice at it. I haul the case aboard, wrap it in a tarp, and sit for a moment letting the current talk to the hull, which is the closest thing I have to church.

Here's what a careful man does next. A careful man delivers the case to the drop point—a drainage culvert under Route 9, third grate, wrapped and weighted—a careful man collects his payment from the PO box in a town he'll never visit again, and a careful man logs the dive in his ledger as *nil recovery, poor vis, engine trouble*, and drives six hours in the wrong direction before sleeping.

I am, historically, a very careful man.

But.

I open the ledger anyway, there in the rocking skiff, and make the entry, and my pencil stops at the last column. *Salvage reported.* I look at the word *reported* the way you look at a word you've written ten thousand times until suddenly it looks foreign. Reported to whom? Paid by whom? I have worked eleven years for this particular network of dead-drop clients—federal money, I've always assumed, or federal-adjacent, the kind that launders itself through fisheries grants and disaster relief—and in eleven years they have never once asked me to recover something that hummed.

They've asked me to find bodies. To lose bodies. To confirm wrecks and deny wrecks. Once, memorably, to retrieve a single crate of documents from a flooded records annex and deliver it unopened to a man whose face I was instructed to forget, and whose face, I'm pleased to report, I have completely failed to remember, out of spite.

Never anything alive. Never anything humming.

The mist burns off a little. Somewhere upstream—upstream, always upstream, this river's whole personality is upstream—a bird I can't identify makes a sound like a hinge complaining. I take out the pencil. In the final column, under *salvage reported*, I write: *case, sealed, 1 ea.* Then, because the ledger is mine and honesty with oneself is the only luxury anonymity affords, I add beneath it, small:

*Did not open. Want to.*

There it is. Fifteen years of discipline, and the whole architecture of my caution turns out to be load-bearing only up to a certain weight, and the weight is curiosity. I hate that about myself. I also, quietly, in the place where I keep the person I used to be, love it.

* * *

The delivery goes fine, which is how I know something's wrong.

Culvert, third grate, package wedged per instructions, done in nine minutes flat with a pair of joggers passing close enough to make my heart do gymnastics—they saw a man fixing a culvert, which is the most boring thing a man can be. Confirmation photo sent from a burner phone to a number that will be dead by Thursday. Payment appears in the PO box within thirty-six hours, cash, banded, correct to the dollar plus a bonus I didn't negotiate.

The bonus is what bothers me. Bonuses are apologies. Apologies mean somebody knows they asked for something that cost more than money, and they're pre-forgiving themselves. I've gotten bonuses twice before. Both times, within a month, people connected to the job stopped being reachable. One moved away. One moved on, in the euphemistic sense.

So I do the thing a careful man does when a job smells wrong: I start pulling thread. Not on the client—I know better than to hunt the hand that feeds—but around it. Dead drops leave residue if you know how to read them: the license paper stock, the courier routes, the timing of payments against banking holidays. Eleven years of this has taught me the shape of the machine that employs me the way a mouse learns the shape of the hawk—not its face, but its shadow, its schedule, its appetite.

And the shadow is wrong this time. The payment trail, which normally dissolves into three shells and a credit union, doesn't dissolve. It funnels. Every layer peels back toward a single registration: a company called Meridian Hydrographic Survey Services, LLC, incorporated eighteen months ago, address a suite in a government office park, officers listed as three names that return nothing anywhere, which is a trick I personally know requires friends in very high, very dry places.

A survey office. Cartographers. Mapmakers.

I sit in my truck outside the PO box with the envelope in my lap and think about maps, which I have thought about professionally for fifteen years, mostly in terms of which ones to avoid. There is a stretch of this river—headwaters, high country—where every map I have ever owned, bought, stolen, or been issued, goes blank. Not empty. Blank. The deliberate white of a page someone decided not to fill, which is different from wilderness, and worse.

I have seen that blank before. Fifteen years ago, in a room with no windows, on charts spread across a table while a patient man explained my deletion to me, there was a folder with a map inside, and the same white space, and I remember—heaven help me, I remember this now, surfacing like the case itself, clean where everything else grows—I remember asking about it, and I remember the patient man smiling and saying:

*"Some water isn't for drinking."*

I didn't understand it then. I logged it away with everything else from that room, in the flooded annex of myself, and I have kept it submerged for fifteen years through discipline and distance and the honest anesthetic of hard work.

Now there's a case in a culvert humming with tomorrow's technology, paid for by mapmakers, pulled from a wreck the navy drowned to forget, and the whole of it points upstream, toward the blank.

I start the truck. In the ledger, under today's date, I add one more line, the first entry in fifteen years that isn't a measurement:

*Miles logged: 214. Miles owed: unknown.*

Then I drive—not home, since home is a word I keep in the same drawer as my real name, but north, toward the last town before the maps give up. If somebody wants a case bad enough to triple-pay for its silence, then somebody, somewhere upstream, is afraid of what happens when it speaks.

I've spent fifteen years being the silence.

It might be time to find out what I've been covering for.
