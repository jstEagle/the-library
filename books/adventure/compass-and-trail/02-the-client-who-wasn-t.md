## 2. The Client Who Wasn't

The money came the way all my money came: in a plastic-wrapped bundle wedged behind the third drainpipe at the Greybull Marina laundry, wrapped in a page of the *Klamath Falls Shopper* from eleven years ago. Someone had been saving that paper. Someone with patience and a filing system and no sense of humor about it.

I'd done four jobs for this client before I ever got curious, which by my standards was practically impulsive. Four jobs, four bundles, always cash, always the same drainpipe, always within forty-eight hours of me leaving the sealed case in the agreed locker at the Port Angeles bus depot. The instructions arrived on index cards, block capitals, cut from a magazine like a ransom note written by an accountant. No voice. No email. No fingerprints — I checked, once, out of professional vanity, and found nothing but the faint ghost of latex powder.

The jobs themselves had been strange in a way I'd told myself wasn't strange. Recover equipment from wrecks nobody acknowledged existed. Leave certain compartments unopened. Photograph nothing. The pay was triple salvage rate, which is the kind of arithmetic that makes a man agree not to notice things.

But the case from the navy wreck — the one that hummed against my palm like a sleeping hornet — had rearranged something in me. Fifteen years of not-noticing is a discipline, and disciplines hold until they don't. I sat in the cab of my truck outside the laundry with the engine off and the rain ticking on the roof, and I asked myself the question I'd been avoiding since I surfaced that night:

Who pays a man to ignore things?

Not who pays him to find them. Anyone can hire a finder. But paying for ignorance — that's a specialist purchase. That requires knowing exactly what's down there, knowing someone might bring it up, and knowing precisely which someone to buy.

Which meant whoever was behind the drainpipe knew me. Knew my routes, my rates, my particular talent for leaving things where I found them. And there are maybe six people alive who know what I used to be, and I have spent fifteen years making sure most of them were dead or scattered.

So I did something I hadn't done since the erasure. I followed the money backward.

Dead drops are designed to be untraceable, but "untraceable" is a word people use when they haven't watched a drop long enough. The bundle appeared sometime between midnight Tuesday and dawn Wednesday. Which meant a courier, which meant a route, which meant a pattern, because couriers are human and humans get comfortable. I rented a room above the bait shop across from the marina — paid cash, gave the name Dale Pruitt, which belongs to a man who died in 2009 and whose ID I keep for occasions requiring a face — and I waited.

Three nights. Rain, then fog, then rain again. On the fourth night, at 2:17 in the morning, a grey sedan rolled into the lot with its lights off, and a woman got out.

That surprised me. Not because women can't be couriers — half the best ones are — but because she moved wrong for a courier. Couriers move like they're carrying something hot. She moved like she was bored. She walked to the drainpipe, crouched, tucked the bundle in, stood, stretched her back like a person who'd just finished a shift, and got back in the car. Total elapsed time: nineteen seconds. Nineteen seconds of boredom is worth more than an hour of tradecraft, if you know how to read it. Boredom means routine. Routine means she'd done this dozens of times and expected to do it dozens more.

I let her drive away and I didn't follow. Following gets you a face, and faces get you killed. Instead I went to the drainpipe, took the bundle, and read the newspaper wrapping.

Same *Shopper*, same year. But this time there was a pencil mark in the margin of a car ad — a small tick beside a 1998 Corolla listed at $1,400. A code. My client was confirming receipt through the wrapping itself, which meant the wrapping came *from* them, which meant somewhere upstream there was a stack of eleven-year-old newspapers and a person who annotated them.

People think surveillance is cameras and wiretaps. Mostly it's stationery.

The tick mark was the first mistake my client had made in five jobs, and I nearly missed why it mattered. It took me two more days, sitting in the bait-shop room eating vending-machine crackers, turning it over. Then it landed: the tick was in *pencil*. Everything else about this operation was engineered to leave no trace — latex gloves, cut-out letters, cash, a dead man's name for me to wear. But the confirmation was penciled, lightly, in a hand that could be erased with a thumb.

My client was confirming receipts in a medium they could deny. Which meant my client was worried about being audited by someone else. You don't build deniability against the world; you build it against your own side.

I'd seen that flavor of paranoia before. Once. In a life I keep in a box at the bottom of Lake Chelan.

* * *

Mara Voss had drawn the same river four times, and every time it came out different.

She sat in the survey office in Wenatchee with the team's final charts spread across her light table — the ones submitted three days before the team went upriver and stopped existing — and she compared them against her own field copies, and the discrepancy sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

The official charts showed the headwaters reach narrowing to a seasonal creek above mile 61. Her field copy showed the same. But Rennick's copy — Rennick, who ran the bathymetric side and never let anyone touch his instruments — showed a sustained channel, thirty meters wide, flowing hard, with depth soundings that made no sense for that elevation. And Okonkwo's copy showed something else again: a channel with a flow rate logged at values Mara had only ever seen in pressurized aqueducts.

Four cartographers. Four versions of the same twelve miles of river.

"Rounding errors," DeWitt had said, when she raised it at the last briefing. DeWitt ran the office the way a man runs a machine he doesn't entirely trust — hands on the controls, eyes elsewhere. "Instrument drift. Cold weather does things to the transducers."

"Instrument drift doesn't produce a river," Mara said.

DeWitt had looked at her then, just for a second, with an expression she couldn't file anywhere. Not anger. Something closer to apology. "Voss. Some reaches you chart and some reaches you chart *around*. That's the job."

She should have quit that night. She knew it now, sitting alone in the office at 11 p.m., the rest of the building dark, the missing team's coffee mugs still on the drying rack by the sink because nobody had been authorized to pack up their desks. Missing. That was the word the incident report used, though everyone said *dead* in the hallway and *gone* in the parking lot, and nobody said anything at all after the men in the unmarked Suburbans spent two days photographing the office and interviewing each of them twice.

They'd interviewed her a third time, informally, in a diner. A pleasant man with a federal haircut who asked questions that weren't questions. *You processed their field data, Ms. Voss? Did anything strike you as anomalous?* And she — God help her, she'd been scared, properly scared, the way you're scared when you realize the nice man knows more than you do — she'd said no. Nothing anomalous. Instrument drift.

She had signed the classification order on her own team's data. Her name, her signature, her hand. *Reviewed and concurred: no substantive findings.* She'd blinded herself and called it procedure.

Now she pulled Rennick's original sounding sheets from the archive — technically she wasn't cleared to anymore, technically the whole archive was under seal, but seals are made of paperwork and paperwork is made of people forgetting to lock cabinets — and she laid them over her own tracings on the light table, and there it was, the thing she'd been circling for two weeks without letting herself see it.

The anomaly wasn't in the data. The anomaly was in the *corrections*. Every version of the chart had been corrected — smoothed, narrowed, dried up — by a different hand, and every correction pointed the same direction: away from the headwaters. Someone had taken a real river and edited it into a trickle, four times, in four hands, so that no single editor would ever hold the whole picture.

It was a map built like a cell structure. Each of them had carried one piece of the lie and none of them could assemble it alone.

Except that Rennick, stubborn, meticulous Rennick, had kept his raw soundings. And Rennick was gone.

Mara turned off the light table and sat in the dark with her heart going hard. Somewhere up that river was whatever the state had spent decades teaching its own maps to forget. And somewhere in this building, in this very archive, was enough of the truth left uncorrected to prove it — if she could get it out, and if she lived long enough to be believed.

She thought about the diver. The rumor had come through the ferry towns the way rumors do, sideways: someone was asking questions about the survey team, a salvage man, no fixed address, no records. The kind of man the government loses paperwork on because he built it that way.

She didn't trust him. She trusted almost nothing anymore, least of all herself. But a man with no records couldn't be threatened with his file, and right now that was the rarest quality on earth.

* * *

The shell company was called Meridian Hydrographic Services LLC, incorporated in Delaware, registered to a registered agent who was registered to another registered agent, the corporate equivalent of those Russian dolls where eventually you run out of dolls and there's just nothing inside. It had filed taxes for nine years. It had a phone number that rang to a voicemail with a recorded woman's voice. It had, according to the secretary of state's filings, between one and four employees.

And it had a contract.

I found it the way you find everything now — not by breaking in but by reading what people publish without thinking. Meridian held a small services agreement with the Columbia Basin Survey Office, subsumed under a water-resources assessment program. Public record. Boring. A line item: *contracted recovery and verification services.*

Recovery. There was my word. The same word on the index cards, in block capitals: RECOVERY AND VERIFICATION. My client hadn't hired me off the street. My client had hired me through a procurement process. Somebody in the survey office had written a scope of work describing exactly what I do, routed it through a shell company old enough to have wrinkles, and started feeding me jobs — including jobs against wrecks the navy officially did not have.

I sat in a library in Yakima — libraries are the last place in America where a man can exist without explaining himself — and pulled everything public on the survey office. Staff directories, annual reports, grant disclosures. And there, in a photo from a ribbon-cutting three years back, standing slightly apart from the officials with the golden shovel: Director DeWitt, and beside him a woman with a clipboard and the posture of someone holding the whole operation together through sheer refusal to sit down.

The caption named her. M. Voss, senior cartographer.

Senior cartographer. The woman who'd signed off on the survey program that employed my shell company. And per the news stories I'd been chewing for a week — the ones the papers ran small, under headlines like SURVEY TEAM SEARCH SUSPENDED — M. Voss was the only member of that team whose body hadn't been found, whose desk hadn't been packed, whose name kept appearing in the articles with the word *sole* attached to it like a hook.

Sole survivor. Sole person of interest. The papers hadn't decided which story they liked yet. Neither, I suspected, had the people hunting her.

Here's what assembled itself in my head while I pretended to read a book about bridges. The survey team went upriver and vanished clean — gear intact, meals half-eaten, no tracks, which I would learn later and which still turns my stomach. The state needed the incident contained. Containment required understanding what the team had found, which required someone who could recover things quietly and ask nothing, which required me. The wreck jobs, the sealed case, the humming cargo — all of it was the same program wearing different hats. I wasn't a contractor. I was a cleanup instrument with a boat.

And the pencil tick in the margin meant somebody inside the machine was keeping their own private ledger of what the machine was doing. Confirming my payments in erasable graphite. Building deniability against their own side, the same way the map corrections had been split four ways so no one hand held the whole lie.

Somebody inside was scared of somebody else inside.

I closed the bridge book. Outside the library windows, Yakima went about its evening, sprinklers ticking, a dog complaining at nothing, ordinary life stacked up in layers like sediment. Fifteen years I'd stayed under that sediment. No name, no trail, no weight. It had cost me everything a life is usually made of, and in exchange I'd bought the one thing I valued above all of it: the certainty that no file anywhere described me.

But files describe other people. Files described a vanished survey team. Files described a cartographer running through the woods with the state's own maps folded under her arm. And somewhere in a federal building there was a file with a pencil tick in its margin, and the man or woman holding that pencil had decided I was useful, which meant they'd also decided I was disposable, which meant the erasure I'd built my life around was worth exactly as much as they said it was.

Nothing. It was worth nothing. Anonymity isn't armor; it's just a smaller target, and targets are targets.

I walked out of the library into the last of the light and stood on the steps a long moment, doing the math I'd been refusing to do since the night the case hummed against my hand. If the client was the survey office, and the survey office's team had vanished at the headwaters, and the headwaters sat inside a blank on the chart — a blank I had seen before, in another life, on charts stamped with a classification level I was never supposed to remember — then the thread running from the drainpipe to the wreck to the missing seven ran straight through the middle of my own buried history.

They hadn't hired me despite what I used to be. They'd hired me *because* of it. Somebody had reached fifteen years down into the sediment and pulled me up by the collar, and that meant the sediment was mapped. That meant my blank spot was on somebody's chart.

Paranoia is just pattern recognition with bad posture. I'd been telling myself for two weeks that I was being careful. What I was actually being was *aimed*.

There was a ranger cabin forty miles up the Naches drainage where, if the ferry-town rumor mill was half right, a woman with a knife and a stolen archive was waiting for the state to stop looking for her. The state wouldn't stop. The state had already shown me its method: it didn't kill you first. It erased you first, and then killing you was just housekeeping.

I put the truck on the road heading upriver, and for the first time in fifteen years I wasn't running from the current.

I was swimming into it.
