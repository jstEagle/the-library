## 3. Gear Without Ghosts

The job came to me the way all my best jobs come: sideways, in cash, and with a story that didn't hold water.

A man named Corliss—no first name offered, no handshake extended, which I appreciated; handshakes leave prints—found me at a fuel dock two hundred miles downstream from where I'd been the week before. He was government-adjacent in the way that certain men are: good shoes, bad tan, eyes that catalogued exits before they catalogued faces.

"You're hard to find," he said.

"That's the product," I said. "You're buying it right now."

He wanted the survey team found. Not recovered—*found*. Located, assessed, reported back through a channel that didn't officially exist. The team had gone silent eleven days ago near the headwaters of the Verrin River, and the office that employed them had discovered, with what struck me as remarkable speed, that it could not acknowledge they'd ever been there.

"Can't acknowledge," I repeated. "But can pay."

"Discreetly."

"Everything's discreet until someone's dead. Then it's evidence." I let that sit while he recalculated. "What am I looking for?"

"Answers."

"Wrong. You're looking for deniability. Answers are what you're afraid of." He didn't argue, which told me more than an argument would have. "Double your number, half up front, and I report to you alone. If anyone official asks, you never met me and I was never on that river."

He agreed too fast. People who agree too fast have already decided what they'll say about you later. But money is money, and curiosity is the only currency I've ever valued more, so three days later I was paddling a borrowed jon boat up the last navigable stretch of the Verrin, watching the banks close in like a book deciding whether to let me read it.

I want to be honest about my state of mind going in, because honesty is the one luxury this account can afford. I wasn't scared. Scared would have been healthy. What I felt was *recognition*, that cold tickle at the base of the skull when a room smells like a room you've tried to forget. Eleven days of silence. A team of six. Equipment worth more than my boat, my truck, and my reputation combined, sitting unguarded somewhere upstream. Nobody else sent to look. Nobody official asking questions.

In my experience, gear that stays put after its owners vanish isn't abandoned. It's *curated*.

* * *

I made the last four miles on foot, following the tributary through country that got progressively less interested in being visited. The survey team's route was easy enough to read—they'd flagged their waypoints with orange tape, cartographer's habit, little bright confessions stitched along the treeline. I counted them as I went. Six flags, then seven, then a gap where the terrain steepened, then eight more climbing toward a bench of flat ground above a bend in the river.

The camp sat on the bench like a stage set between acts.

That's the phrase that kept coming back to me as I stood at the edge of it for a full five minutes without moving: *between acts*. Everything arranged. Everything mid-gesture. Two dome tents staked taut, guy-lines humming faintly in the breeze. A tarp strung over the cooking area, still shedding the morning's condensation in fat, deliberate drops. A portable weather station turning slowly on its mast, logging data to a drive nobody would ever download. A pair of boots by the nearest tent flap, laces tucked inside, the way you leave them when you expect to put them back on in ten minutes.

Nobody expects to come back in ten minutes. Not for eleven days.

I circled the perimeter twice before entering, reading the ground the way I read a river bottom—by what's missing as much as what's there. And what was missing started adding up in a way I did not enjoy.

No tracks. I don't mean few tracks. I mean none. Six people had lived on this bench for the better part of a week, hauling crates, driving stakes, arguing about contour lines, and the forest floor showed less disturbance than a church aisle. The duff around the fire ring was undisturbed. No game trails rerouted. No scat. Which brought me to the second absence, the one that raised the hair on my arms even in the warm midmorning:

No insects.

There should have been flies on the food. There should have been ants colonizing the sugar tin, gnats working the damp shade under the tarp, spiders cashing in on all that free real estate between tent poles. Instead the camp was sterile as a showroom. I waved a hand through the air over the cook table and caught nothing. I opened the cooler—steaks still pink-edged, butter still solid, eleven days past any reasonable shelf life—and found it untouched. Pristine. Preserved, almost, though not by refrigeration. By *absence*.

I've seen a lot of dead camps in fifteen years of pulling things out of places people shouldn't go. Storm camps. Flood camps. Camps where the bears got into everything and the story ends badly but legibly. This wasn't that. This was a camp that had been *edited*.

And here is the part I've never said out loud, the part that made me sit down on a crate with my head between my knees for a minute like a man reconsidering his hobbies:

I recognized the editing.

Fifteen years ago—in another name, another life, a file cabinet somewhere holding a version of me I've paid good money to keep buried—I stood in a clearing in a different watershed and saw this same signature. Tents up. Meals out. Instruments running. No tracks, no bugs, no bodies, no mess. We called it a "soft site" back then, in the flat bureaucratic dialect we used to keep from saying what we meant. Soft site meaning: people were here, and then the here was cleaned around them so thoroughly that even the ecosystem agreed to forget. Whatever we did that day—and I have spent fifteen years not finishing that sentence—the ground afterward looked exactly like this ground. Like someone had taken an eraser to reality and been careful about the margins.

I helped bury that site. I told myself for years that I'd helped bury *evidence*. Standing in this new one, smelling the nothing where the rot should be, I finally admitted what I'd always known: I'd helped bury people. Or the space where people should have been. The distinction stopped comforting me somewhere around the second year and I'd just kept walking anyway, because walking away is the one skill I've practiced daily.

So there I was, kneeling in a vanished team's kitchen, recognizing my own old work in somebody else's hands, and understanding—with the particular clarity that arrives only when it's least welcome—that whoever had done this hadn't learned the technique recently. This was craft. This was lineage. Somewhere between that clearing fifteen years ago and this bench today, the method had been taught, refined, passed down. And every instinct I owned, the ones that had kept me alive and anonymous through a decade and a half of careful nothing, were telling me the same thing:

You weren't hired to find these people. You were hired to be standing here when someone decides the site needs a second pass.

* * *

I worked the camp anyway. Call it compulsion, call it professionalism, call it the fact that if I was already bait, I might as well learn what the trap looked like.

The instruments were the strangest part. The team's gravimeter—a unit I recognized as military surplus, the kind that doesn't appear on civilian invoices—was still cycling, its display scrolling readings with the patience of a machine that doesn't know its operators are gone. I photographed the screen. The numbers meant nothing to me, but they meant something to someone, because the unit had been left running deliberately. Cleanup crews that erase everything don't leave functioning sensors behind unless the sensor itself is part of the message—or part of the monitoring. I checked it for transmitters and found one, small and dutiful, pinging home every ninety seconds.

I left it exactly as it was. If someone was watching this camp, I wanted them watching a camp with one extra ghost in it, not a camp that had suddenly gone dark.

The tents held the human residue the ground refused to. Sleeping bags rolled halfway, a paperback splayed face-down, a charging cable snaking to a power pack that had long since died. In the second tent, pinned to the wall with a carabiner, a hand-drawn map of the river above their position—and this is where my breath actually stopped, because the map was wrong in a specific way. The upper reaches of the Verrin simply *ended*. Not faded, not unexplored. Ended, with a clean margin line drawn across the channel and a notation in careful cartographer's lettering:

*Survey discontinued per directive 7-C. Do not chart beyond this point.*

Directive 7-C. I turned the paper over, looking for an issuing office, a signature, anything. Nothing. Just the notation, dated nine days before the team went silent—which meant they'd received the order to stop, logged it, and then, apparently, kept going anyway. Or been made to stop in a way that required no order at all.

Six people. One map that forbade them. One camp edited down to furniture. And me, crouched in the doorway of a dead man's tent, holding the exact shape of my own history in both hands.

I took the map. I know, I know—the professional move was to photograph and replace, leave no delta, be the ghost I've trained myself to be. But something in me, some older and stupider thing, needed it out of that tent. Needed it somewhere the next cleanup crew wouldn't find folded into the record of what happened here. Fifteen years of erasing myself, and apparently what I'd really been doing was waiting for a reason to stop.

I also took the gravimeter's data drive. The transmitter could keep pinging its empty readings; the drive rode out in my chest pocket, against my heartbeat, which had picked up a rhythm I hadn't heard since before I was nobody.

* * *

I was a mile downstream, moving fast and low along the water, when I saw the smoke.

One thread of it, thin and gray, rising off a ridgeline north of the river where the maps—at least the public ones, the honest-by-neglect ones—showed nothing but ranger tract and blank space. A cabin, maybe. A fire meant to be seen or meant to boil water, impossible to tell at distance. But smoke means a living person, and in a landscape where six people had been subtracted without leaving a smudge, a living person was either a survivor or bait, and after the day I'd had I was no longer confident I knew the difference.

I should have walked away. Every rule I live by—the rules that have kept me off manifests, out of databases, absent from the world's paperwork—said walk away, take the fee, file the report, disappear downstream. Finders locate. They don't get involved. Involvement is how names happen.

But I kept thinking about those boots by the tent flap, laces tucked in, waiting for feet that had been edited out of the world. And I kept thinking about a clearing fifteen years ago, and a younger man with a different name who had walked away from it, and every mile he'd logged since, all of them pointing, it turned out, not away from that clearing at all—but toward this one.

The smoke rose straight in the still air, patient as a signature on a line.

I cut north.
