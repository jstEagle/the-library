## 4. Mara Voss, Cartographer of Nothing

I had been dead for eleven days when the stranger came up the trail, and I want to tell you that I handled his arrival with grace.

I did not handle it with grace. I put a knife to his throat in the dark of a ranger cabin that smelled of mouse droppings and old pine pitch, and I told him, in a voice I barely recognized as my own, that if he moved I would open him like an envelope.

To be fair to myself, I'd had practice being someone else by then. Eleven days of it. You'd be surprised how quickly a person can learn to move like a ghost when the alternative is learning what happened to her team.

He didn't flinch, this man with the knife at his throat. That was the first thing wrong with him. Most people flinch. Most people do the arithmetic of their own mortality and come back with panic. This one just stood there in the doorway with river water still dripping off his gear bag, and said, "You're holding it too high. You'll tire out before you cut anything important."

Which is how I knew he wasn't navy. Navy men love procedure even at knifepoint. He'd have corrected my stance differently.

"Who sent you?" I asked.

"Nobody who knows you're alive," he said. "Which is the interesting part."

* * *

Let me back up, because I'm getting ahead of the story, and a cartographer should never get ahead of her own map.

My name is Mara Voss. It was, anyway. I was senior cartographic analyst on Survey Team Kestrel, six of us contracted through the Office of Hydrographic Assessment — which sounds like the most boring job in the world, and for nine years it was. We mapped rivers. Tributaries, floodplains, sediment loads, seasonal shifts. The kind of work that makes insurance companies happy and nobody else. I could draw you the braided channels of the Alder Fork from memory, every oxbow, every gravel bar. I loved it the way some people love chess: quietly, obsessively, without needing anyone else to understand why.

Then we went upstream past the confluence at Gray Narrows, and the river stopped making sense.

It started small. A tributary that appeared on our satellite overlay but not on the ground. Then the reverse — a channel on the ground that no imagery had ever shown. Okafor joked that the satellites were drunk. Priya ran the GPS three times against three different base stations and got three different answers, each one internally consistent, none of them agreeing with the others. That's not how error works. Error scatters. This was something else. It was as if the landscape were being edited while we stood in it.

On the fourth night upstream, I redrew the master sheet from our field notes, and when I laid the new tracing over the old one, the two maps disagreed about everything. Not drift. Not surveyor's variance. Everything. River courses that had been stable since the first federal surveys in the 1930s had simply — moved. Or rather, our record of them had moved, as though someone had reached into the archive of the world and rearranged the furniture.

I did what any good analyst does with data that breaks her model. I assumed I was the problem. I checked my instruments, my projections, my datums. All clean. I checked Priya's independently. Clean. Two instruments, two operators, same impossible answer.

The morning after that, Chen went up to the ridge to sight a benchmark and didn't come back. No radio call. No distress. Okafor went after him. Then Okafor didn't come back either, and Priya looked at me across the camp table with a face I will see until I die, and said, "Mara. There are no tracks."

No tracks. Six days of rain-softened ground around us, ground that held the print of every deer and grouse in the valley, and where two grown men had walked twenty minutes earlier there was nothing. Not obscured. Nothing. As if they'd been lifted out of the world rather than walked out of it.

Priya wanted to go to the ridge. I told her we were going downstream, and she argued, and I pulled rank, and she went down to the river to fill canteens while we packed, and that was the last time anyone spoke to her.

I ran. I'm not going to dress it up. I heard nothing, saw nothing, but I knew — the way you know a storm is coming before the first drop — and I ran, alone, downstream, off every route we'd logged, because our logged routes felt like they belonged to whoever had taken the others. I found the ranger cabin on the second day. I've been here since, eating my emergency rations, sleeping four hours at a stretch, and doing the only thing I know how to do with terror, which is to draw it.

Eleven days of maps. The blank area upstream, rendered over and over. The last known positions of five people, marked in pencil so light you'd need to tilt the page toward a lamp to see them. I drew the cabin itself once, floor plan and elevations, just to keep my hands busy, and then caught myself annotating the windows with fields of fire and understood exactly how far from a cartographer I had traveled.

So when the door opened on the eleventh night and a silhouette filled it, I did the arithmetic of survival instead of the arithmetic of courtesy, and I put my knife where it would do the most good.

* * *

"You're holding it too high," he said again, patiently, like a man commenting on weather. "Elbow in. You get leverage from the body, not the wrist."

"I will kill you," I said, which was a lie, and he knew it was a lie, and I knew he knew, and the whole exchange had the strange civility of two people pretending very hard.

"No," he agreed. "But you want to. That's worth something." He set his gear bag down slowly, showing me both hands. "Tamsin Nakamura. I was hired to find your team."

"By whom?"

"That's the question I came four hundred miles to ask you."

"The Office of Hydrographic Assessment," I said. "If you were hired through official channels, which you weren't, because official channels stopped answering my check-ins nine days ago. I've transmitted on schedule twice a day. Silence." My arm was starting to shake. I hated that he'd called it. "You're salvage. I can tell by the kit. Nobody else packs like they expect to be underwater at any moment."

Something shifted in his face — not surprise exactly. Recalculation. "You know dive gear."

"I grew up on a research vessel. I know what a rebreather looks like, and yours has been modified, and you carry a case that isn't rated for anything recreational." I lowered the knife an inch. Only an inch. "Start talking, Mr. Nakamura, or start bleeding. Your choice, but make it soon, because I've been alone with my own thoughts for eleven days and they've gotten unfriendly."

He talked. And here is the thing I want you to understand, the thing that mattered more than anything he actually said: he talked like me. Carefully. Every fact offered with its provenance attached. Every claim hedged exactly as far as the evidence supported and not one inch further. When he didn't know something, he said *I don't know*, plainly, without embarrassment, which in my experience is rarer than honesty and considerably more valuable.

He told me about the wreck. A naval vessel, sunk where no naval vessel should have been, carrying equipment fifteen years ahead of anything in the public catalogue. He told me about the payment trail that led to a shell company tied to my own office. He told me about the last camp — our camp — tents pitched, instruments running, meals half-eaten, and no tracks anywhere, and watched my face while he said it, and whatever he saw there made him stop performing neutrality.

"You already knew," he said.

"I was there when it started."

And then — this is the part I keep returning to — he sat down on the floor of that filthy cabin, cross-legged, like a man settling in for a long read, and said, "Tell me everything. Slowly. I take notes."

* * *

Here is what I learned about Tamsin Nakamura over the next six hours, by lamplight, while the river spoke its one long syllable outside: he was a man built entirely of omissions, and he maintained them the way other men maintain boats.

He gave me almost nothing true about himself, and he gave it with such precision that the shape of what he withheld became legible. Fifteen years of no records — he admitted that much, eventually, the way you'd admit to a scar. No fixed address, no tax history, no paper trail older than a decade and a half. He measured distance in miles logged and trust in verified behavior, and he watched hands more than faces, and when I asked him directly where he'd learned to read a cleanup operation — because that's what he'd called it, standing in our abandoned camp that afternoon, *cleanup*, with the flat certainty of a man naming a tool he owned — he looked at the lamp flame for a long moment and said, "Somewhere I used to work," and I let it go.

Two liars, recognizing each other's craft. That's what it was, that first night. I lied to him about how calm I was; he lied to me about how much he knew. Neither of us minded. There's a particular relief in meeting someone whose deceptions are professional-grade. You can relax around competence.

What he gave me that was real: the wreck's cargo manifest fragment, photographed. Coordinates of the shell company's registered agent. And the observation — delivered carefully, watching me as he said it — that the compound traces in the water samples he'd pulled downstream matched nothing in any civilian chemical database.

"Matched nothing," I repeated. "Or matched things that aren't in civilian databases?"

He almost smiled. It was the first expression I'd seen on him that wasn't managed. "You'd have made a good diver," he said. "You ask the question behind the question."

"I draw maps, Mr. Nakamura. The entire profession is asking what's behind the edge of the page."

"Call me Tamsin. And show me these maps."

So I showed him. Eleven days of work spread across the cabin floor, weighted with stones at the corners, and I watched a man who claimed to be a simple salvager read topography the way priests read scripture — slowly, hungrily, looking for the verse that damns. When he reached the overlay where the two versions of the river disagreed, he went very still.

"This isn't error," he said.

"It isn't error," I agreed.

"This is revision. Someone is editing the record." He tapped the older sheet. "And someone is editing it faster than the land can change. Rivers don't move like this in weeks. Geology doesn't negotiate." He looked up at me. "Your instruments weren't wrong, Voss. They were reporting a landscape that had been altered between observations. Which means whatever's upstream isn't just hidden. It's *active*. It reaches."

That word — *reaches* — settled into the room and stayed there. I thought of Chen on the ridge. Of no tracks. Of a hand reaching into the world and lifting people out of it the way I lifted features off a map with an eraser.

"There's something else," I said, and went to my pack, and took out the thing I hadn't shown anyone because until tonight there had been no one to show.

It was Priya's field notebook. I'd gone back for it — one insane hour, on the third day, moving up the valley at dusk to the camp I'd fled, because I couldn't stand not knowing, and I'd found the camp exactly as Tamsin described it and exactly as I'd left it, except for one detail. Priya's notebook, which had been in her pocket when she went to the river, was lying open on her cot. Placed there. Someone had wanted it found.

The last entry was dated the morning she died. It wasn't hydrology. It was a single line, in her cramped, beautiful hand:

*The water samples are wrong. Whatever's at the source isn't IN the river. The river is coming FROM it.*

Below that, a sketch. She'd been the team's geologist and she drew like one — sections and strata, cross-hatching, labels. But the structure she'd drawn had no business existing in a headwater valley: rectilinear, tiered, intake galleries cut into the bedrock itself, the spring not feeding the facility but passing *through* it, changed on the way.

Tamsin studied the sketch for a long time. His breathing changed. I've spent years reading terrain and I read him the same way — the slight contraction, the way his thumb pressed white against the page's edge.

"You recognize this," I said.

"I recognize the engineering philosophy," he said quietly. "Not the design. The philosophy. Everything scaled for function, nothing scaled for human beings." He closed the notebook with a care that seemed almost funerary. "There's an installation at your headwaters, Voss. It's been there longer than your survey. Longer than the blank space on the maps. And your government doesn't just know about it."

He handed the notebook back.

"They're protecting it. Possibly with people who erase other people for a living." A pause, and then, in a voice stripped of all its careful neutrality: "People like the one I used to be."

* * *

We sat with that for a while. The lamp hissed. Outside, the river kept saying the only word it knew.

"Here's my problem," I said finally. "Every instinct I have says go downstream. Get to a city, get to a press office, get in front of people. That's what a rational person does with what we have."

"And?"

"And I'm a cartographer, Tamsin. I don't publish maps of places I haven't been. Five people are dead — or worse than dead, which I didn't previously believe was a category — and the only thing I have to offer them is accuracy. If I run downstream with a sketch and a dead woman's notebook, I'm a traumatized witness with a conspiracy theory. They'll medicate the story before lunch." I looked at the blank space at the top of my master sheet, that rectangle of nothing I'd been staring at for eleven days. "But if I go upstream and *see* it — if I survey it, properly, with my own instruments, and bring back data that survives scrutiny — then I'm not a witness. I'm a source. Witnesses get discredited. Sources get believed."

He was quiet for a moment. "You understand that going upstream is what they want. It's a funnel. Everything runs downhill to them."

"I understand that they've already taken everyone I've worked with for three years, erased a wreck from the public record, and rewritten the landscape's paperwork. I understand that hiding hasn't saved me, and running won't save me, and that the only variable left under my control is whether the truth arrives somewhere intact." I met his eyes. "You came four hundred miles to ask who hired you. I'm telling you: the answer is upstream. Everything is upstream. Come with me or don't, but I leave at first light."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him do the thing he does — weighing, verifying, running some private audit of my face — and then he nodded once, and began repacking his gear with those precise, economical movements, and said:

"At first light. And Voss — we go by the water. Not the trails." He tapped the sketch of the intake galleries. "Whatever they're watching, they're watching the land approaches. Nobody watches the river. Nobody ever watches the river."

"It's what you do, isn't it?" I said. "Live in the place nobody watches."

For the second time that night, something unmanaged crossed his face. "It's what I did," he said. "Past tense. Ask me again in a week."

I rolled out my bedding in the corner farthest from the door, knife under my hand out of habit, and lay listening to the river in the dark. Somewhere upstream, in the blank space at the top of my map, something was waiting that could edit the world. Five people had walked into that blankness and become pencil marks so faint you needed lamplight to find them.

At first light, I intended to walk in after them, with the only weapon I had ever trusted: a complete and accurate record of everything they'd tried to erase.

I just didn't yet know that the eraser had a name, or that it was waiting for us with an offer I'd watch Tamsin nearly die refusing.

The river, meanwhile, kept talking. And for the first time in eleven days, I understood what it was saying.

*Come and see.*
