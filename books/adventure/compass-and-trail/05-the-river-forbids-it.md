## 5. The River Forbids It

Mara Voss drew the map on the floor of the ranger cabin with a carpenter's pencil and a sheet of waxed paper she'd salvaged from somebody's lunch. She worked fast, the way people do when they're drawing something they've already drawn in their heads a hundred times.

"Here's the last camp," she said. "Here's where we turned back. And here—" her pencil stopped, hovered, pressed down until the point snapped— "here is where the river stops existing."

I looked at the paper. The river ran north out of the gorge in confident blue strokes, then simply ended, mid-valley, like a sentence cut off by an editor who'd been paid by the deletion.

"That's not how rivers work," I said.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

She told me about the survey. Eleven days of instrument readings that kept disagreeing with each other—not malfunctioning, disagreeing, as if the water upstream were two different substances depending on which side of an invisible line you sampled it from. She told me about the night their digital charts updated themselves, unbidden, and every contour above the gorge dissolved into white space. She told me about Kessler, the team lead, standing at the edge of that blankness with his binoculars and saying, quietly, the way men say things they immediately regret: *Somebody owns this water.*

Two days later, nobody owned anything, because there was no team. There was gear without ghosts, and one cartographer who'd walked out ahead of the others to set a benchmark and come back to silence.

"And you didn't report it," I said.

"To whom?" She said it without heat, which was worse than heat. "The survey office that signed our classification order? The navy that won't acknowledge its own wrecks? I sat in that cabin for six days deciding whether I was a witness or evidence." She looked up at me. "You already know the answer to that question. Don't tell me you don't."

I didn't tell her I didn't.

* * *

Here is what I know about blank spaces on maps, and why my hands went still when Mara's pencil snapped.

Fifteen years ago—I have to count backward through the miles to find the year, which is its own kind of damage—I worked a contract on a hydrographic survey vessel. Civilian flag, military instruments, crew of nine who were all very good at not asking questions. We charted a coastal shelf for a client whose name appeared nowhere on any document. Standard enough. What wasn't standard was the chart room.

There was a drawer in that room, bottom left, that was always locked. One night I found it open—the tech had fallen asleep over his coffee—and inside was a chart of a river system three hundred miles inland from anywhere we were working. A good chart. Detailed. Every bend surveyed, every depth sounded, every shoal marked in careful hand-lettering.

And then, above a certain latitude, nothing. Not unexplored-nothing, which cartographers mark honestly with hatching and a note. Erased-nothing. The paper itself had been trimmed and a fresh sheet spliced in, seamless if you weren't looking for the seam. I was looking for the seam. It's a habit of mine, looking for seams.

Someone had taken a finished survey of that river and amputated it.

I put the chart back. I closed the drawer. I told myself it was compartmentalized work, need-to-know, the ordinary machinery of a state that liked its secrets the way I liked mine: filed deep and never spoken of. I was twenty-six years old and professionally committed to the fiction that other people's secrets were none of my business, provided they paid on time.

Three weeks later, my name stopped existing.

That's the part I've never said aloud, not to anyone, not even to the dark at four in the morning when saying things aloud costs nothing. I didn't run from something. I was *removed*. There was a man in a gray suit who met me at a dock in a town I still can't name without my jaw tightening, and he had a folder with my life in it—birth certificate, dive certifications, tax records, a photograph of my mother's grave that I had never shown anyone—and he explained, kindly, the way you'd explain a tide table, that the person named on those documents had been reassigned to a project requiring total deniability, and that I would be compensated generously for the remainder of my natural life, and that the alternative to cooperation was also available but came with fewer amenities.

He asked me what my name was. That was the test. I looked at the folder, at the photograph of the grave, and I said, "I don't remember."

He smiled like I'd passed a swim qualification.

I've spent fifteen years being right about that answer. Fifteen years of cash jobs and dead drops and leases signed with names two generations removed from whatever was on that birth certificate. Fifteen years measuring my life in miles logged because miles are honest—they don't ask who's doing the logging.

And now a woman with a broken pencil had just drawn me a picture of the same amputation, same seam, same river, three hundred miles inland, and the past wasn't knocking anymore. It had picked the lock.

* * *

"You've gone somewhere," Mara said.

"I'm deciding whether to tell you something that gets you killed faster."

"We're already moving at maximum speed toward killed. You'd just be adjusting the route." She folded her arms. In the cabin's lamplight she looked like what she was—a professional who'd been awake too long on adrenaline and grief—but her voice stayed level. I respected that. Level voices are load-bearing.

So I told her. Not everything. The drawer, the chart, the splice. I kept the dock and the gray suit and the folder in a locked room of my own, because a man has to keep *some* inventory private or he's just cargo.

But I showed her the thing I did carry: a photocopy I'd made that night fifteen years ago, folded into eighths and moved between wallets so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. On it, the lower half of the amputated chart, and in the corner, faint as a watermark, a survey designation: **HR-7 / HEADWATERS RESTRICTED ZONE**.

Mara took it in both hands like it might bruise.

"HR-7," she repeated. "Our classification order referenced a schedule code. S-7." Her eyes came up. "Same seven."

"Numbers repeat."

"They do," she said, "when the same office issues them."

She spread her waxed-paper map beside the photocopy and aligned them by the gorge. The blank on her map and the blank on mine overlapped almost perfectly—which meant the erasure hadn't been redone in fifteen years. It had been *maintained*. Somebody was still paying for the scissors.

"Do you understand what this is?" she said. "This isn't a secret facility. This is a secret *jurisdiction*. A piece of the country that legally doesn't exist. You can't subpoena a blank. You can't send rangers into a blank. If your team had walked up that river—"

"They'd have been walking off the map," I said. "Off every map. Which means whatever happened to them happened outside the reach of anyone who might look for them." I heard my own voice go flat and knew why. I'd heard that flatness before, across a table, from a man explaining tide tables. "It's clean. That's the design principle. Not hidden—*clean*. Nothing to find because there's no 'there' there."

Mara was quiet for a moment. Then: "You talk like someone who's read the manual."

"I talk like someone who's been a typo," I said, and she had the grace not to push.

* * *

We spent the evening doing what two liars do best: cross-referencing.

Her instruments had logged anomalous readings along a bearing line running straight upriver from the last camp. My wreck—that sealed case humming with technology no salvage should hold—had been sitting downstream of the same watershed, positioned like a sentry at the mouth. Supply logic, if you thought about it the way I thought about hulls and currents: anything coming out of HR-7 by water had to pass the gorge, and past the gorge, the river braided wide and shallow, easy to watch, hard to hide in. Unless you had equipment that could make watching unnecessary.

"The case," Mara said slowly. "The thing you recovered. You think it listens to the river."

"I think it listens to *something*, and I think whoever hired me wanted it back before anyone else listened to it first." I turned my cup on the table. "There's a version of this where I take the case, take the payment, and paddle away. It's a good version. Warm. Well-lit."

"And the other version?"

"The other version has a drawer in it that somebody once left open."

She studied me with those cartographer's eyes, the ones that measure distance by instinct. "You're afraid of that drawer."

"I'm afraid of what happens to people who look in drawers," I said. "Historically? They get erased. Or they get promoted. And I've seen what promotion looks like from the outside. It looks like a gray suit and a folder full of someone else's life."

Outside, the river spoke in its sleep—low, constant, patient. Rivers are the only witnesses that never sign anything, and they carry everything anyway. I'd built a whole philosophy on that once: be the river, hold everything, testify to nothing.

Mara broke the silence. "Tomorrow we go upstream to the trading post at Miller's Bend. There's a ferryman there—Olek—who's run that stretch for forty years. If anyone knows what moves on the water at night, it's him."

"Ferryman," I said. "Charon with a diesel engine."

"He'll want something. He always wants something. Last time it was silence about a crate of cigarettes that fell off a barge into his boat." She almost smiled, then didn't. "Tamsin. When we go up that river—if we go—we're crossing a line that doesn't appear on paper. Once we're past the gorge, we're not in the country anymore. Not legally. Whatever rights we think we have stay downstream."

"I haven't had rights in fifteen years," I said. "They weigh nothing. I travel light."

But that night, in the bunk by the cold stove, I dreamed in fragments for the first time since the dock. Green water. A drawer sliding open. Hand-lettered soundings marching up a river toward a latitude where the letters stop. And a voice, kind as a tide table, asking me my name while a photograph of my mother's grave sat face-up between us like a played card.

I woke with the photocopy crease-printed into my cheek and understood, finally, what the dread in that last camp had been telling me. It wasn't the signature of a cleanup I'd helped bury.

It was the signature of the people who had buried *me*.

At dawn, Mara stood at the door with her pack on, watching the mist burn off the water.

"Miller's Bend by noon," she said. "The ferryman first. Then the river."

Then the river. Which forbids it. Which had forbidden me, once, in a gray-suit voice, and was about to find out what happens when the erased man walks back onto the chart.
