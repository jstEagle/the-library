## 6. Downstream Informants

The town was called Ferrow's Landing, which told you everything you needed to know about its ambitions. There had been a ferry once. There had been a man named Ferrow, presumably. Now there was a gas station with a hand-painted sign advertising BAIT — ICE — PROPANE in letters that had given up on their middle syllables, a church whose steeple leaned like a man listening at a door, and a bar called the Anchor that had never seen an anchor and didn't care to discuss it.

Mara Voss sat in the corner of the Anchor with her back to the wall and a glass of beer she had no intention of drinking, because she'd learned somewhere around day nine of being a fugitive from her own government that alcohol made her sloppy and sloppiness got people disappeared.

She would know. She'd signed the paperwork on two disappearances herself.

That was the thing nobody warned you about complicity: it didn't feel like anything at the time. It felt like a Tuesday. It felt like a form with a signature line and a classification banner and a checkbox marked *routine*. You initialed it, you filed it, you went to lunch. The machinery did the rest, and the machinery was very good at making sure you never heard the sound it made.

Tamsin Nakamura sat across from her, doing his impression of a man with nowhere to be and no history whatsoever. He was disturbingly good at it. He'd ordered chili, eaten half of it, and arranged himself into the kind of slouch that said *I live here, I've always lived here, I will die here and no one will remember to ask my name.* Fifteen years of practice, he'd told her. She was beginning to understand what fifteen years of practice actually looked like. It looked like nothing. It looked like a man who had sanded himself down until he fit through any crack.

"You're staring," he said without looking up.

"I'm assessing."

"Assess him, then." A tilt of his chin toward the bar, where a broad old man with forearms like dock pilings was polishing glasses that were already clean. "That's Hollis. Ran the last foot ferry across the Verren for thirty-one years before they built the bridge upstream and put him out of business. He knows every eddy, every sandbar, every family that ever paid him in chickens."

"And how do you know him?"

"I don't." Tamsin finally looked up, and there was something in his face she was learning to read as humor, though it arrived with all the warmth of a tide chart. "I know *of* him. I spent two days in this town three years ago recovering a dredge winch off a scow that sank mid-river. Hollis lent me his skiff and never asked why. That's the kind of man he is. The asking-why kind of town dried up around him like a creek in August."

"So we just walk up and ask him about naval supply barges moving classified cargo through his river?"

"No," Tamsin said. "We sit here and drink our beer until he comes to us. Because he's been watching us since we walked in, and men like Hollis don't come to you when you want something. They come to you when they decide you're worth the risk. Our job is to be worth the risk."

"And how exactly does one—"

He raised one finger. Not for silence—for attention. Because the old man had set down his glass rag and was already crossing the floor toward them, moving with the unhurried certainty of weather.

* * *

Hollis smelled of diesel and pipe tobacco and the particular mineral coldness of deep water, a smell Mara recognized from her childhood summers and hadn't realized she'd missed. He pulled out a chair uninvited, sat down heavily, and looked at Tamsin the way a man looks at a fish he caught years ago and still thinks about.

"Dredge winch," Hollis said.

"That was me."

"Hauled it up in eleven pieces over four days. Sold it to a scrapyard in Corven for less than it cost you in air tanks." The old man's mouth twitched. "Always wondered what that was about."

"It was about needing rent money."

"Mm." Hollis turned his gaze on Mara, and she felt it like sonar—a pulse that went through her and came back carrying information. "And who's she?"

"A cartographer," Tamsin said.

"Government?"

The silence lasted half a second too long, and Hollis laughed, a dry sound like a rope paying out. "Everybody in this room works for somebody or used to. I asked what flavor." He leaned back. "You two have been asking questions down at the fuel dock. About barge traffic. Night traffic. You think you were subtle? Son, the fuel dock has four employees and they report to each other. You might as well have sent a telegram."

Mara kept her voice level. "We're not looking to cause trouble for anyone local."

"No," Hollis agreed. "You're looking to cause trouble for someone *upstream*, and the trouble is going to flow down here like everything else flows down here. That's the trouble with rivers. Everything arrives eventually." He signaled the bartender with two fingers, and two more beers appeared, and he pushed one at each of them like a man administering medicine. "So let's not waste each other's evening pretending. What do you want to know?"

"The barges," Mara said. "What's on them?"

Hollis drank. Set the glass down. Looked at the window, at the dark coming down over the river, and when he spoke again his voice had changed register—the way water changes when the bottom drops away beneath your anchor.

"I'll tell you what I know," he said. "And then you're going to tell me what you can do for me. Because I'm seventy-three years old, I've got maybe ten good winters left, and there's a thing sitting on my conscience that weighs more than any cargo I ever carried."

* * *

It took him an hour, and two more beers, and one long walk out onto the rotting planks of his private dock where the wind off the water covered whatever words might carry back to the Anchor.

The barges ran at night, he said. Had run at night for years now—longer than the survey team's disappearance, longer than anyone official would admit. They came up from the naval depot at Corven in convoys of three, riding low, riding heavy, under escort of gray-hulled patrol boats that didn't answer hails and didn't log transits. They passed Ferrow's Landing between two and four in the morning, running dark except for a single green light at the bow, which was against every navigation law on the books and which Hollis believed was deliberate—a signal, a courtesy, a warning to anyone who knew enough to look: *we see you seeing us.*

"They killed the foot ferry first," he said. "You know that? Before the bridge went in, before any of it. There was a regulation. Some safety inspection nonsense. My license suspended pending review, review pending appeal, appeal pending nothing. Thirty-one years and they shut me down with paperwork in five months." His jaw worked. "I thought it was just bureaucracy being bureaucracy. Took me two more years to notice the pattern. Every private boat on this stretch—shut down, bought out, or sunk. Every dock that could service a barge—condemned or acquired. They cleared the river of witnesses before they started using it."

"What's the cargo?" Tamsin asked.

"Sealed containers. White, no markings, stacked and tarped. Going up empty-weighted and coming down—" Hollis stopped. Started again. "Going up heavy, coming down light. Whatever they take up there, they don't bring much back."

Mara's mind was already working, the way it always worked, turning rumor into geometry. "Timetable?"

"Twice a month. New moon if they can get it. They hug the east channel past the Narrows because the west channel's too shallow for a loaded barge, which means—" 

"—which means they're constrained," she finished. "A loaded convoy drawing more than a meter can only use one route. That's not a shipping lane, that's a schedule." She was talking fast now and couldn't stop herself, the old cartographer's fever rising in her blood. "If they run twice monthly on a fixed draft, they're not opportunistic, they're logistical. Sustained supply chain. Which means the facility upstream isn't a field site, it's an *installation*, permanent, staffed, provisioned. And if the barges go up heavy and come down light, the product stays there or leaves by another route entirely. Air? No—no airstrip shows on anything. Rail?" She looked at Tamsin. "Is there rail?"

"There was," he said. "Decommissioned logging line along the gorge. Condemned on paper."

"On paper," Mara repeated, and felt the shape of it click together. "Everything condemned on paper is alive in fact. The rail runs. The barges run. The river runs. Three supply lines to a place that doesn't exist." She laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "I drew maps for eleven years. I was *good* at it. And the whole time there was a country inside my country with better infrastructure than half the towns I charted."

Hollis watched her with something that wasn't quite pity. "Now you know how it feels to pilot a ferry across a river that officially has no ferry."

* * *

They walked back along the shore road in the blue dark, and Hollis stopped at the point where his dock jutted out into the current, and there he turned around, and there he made his trade.

"In 'ninety-eight," he said, "there was a man named Aldous Wren. Schoolteacher, came down from the hill settlements every few weeks, bought supplies, took the ferry back up. Quiet fellow. Bad knee. One October he crossed with me and there was a car waiting on the far bank that shouldn't have been there—government plates, two men in it, no driver's door locks." The old man's hands were steady but his voice had gone thin. "Wren saw them same time I did. Grabbed my arm and said, *Hollis, take me back across, please, take me back across right now.* And I looked at those men, and I looked at my license hanging by a thread, and I told him the ferry doesn't run reverse after dark. Regulation. I quoted him a *regulation*."

The river moved beneath the dock, black and patient.

"He stepped off my boat onto that bank," Hollis said, "and that was the last time anyone saw Aldous Wren. His sister came down in November asking after him. Then she stopped coming. Then his name stopped being in the church rolls. Then, a year later, a survey crew came through and the hill settlements above the gorge weren't on their map anymore. Just gone. Blank space. And I stood on this dock and understood that I hadn't failed one man. I'd held the door open for all of it."

Nobody spoke. Out on the water, far downstream, a green light blinked once at the bend—there and gone, there and gone—and Mara's breath caught, because it was two in the morning and new moon was three days off and the convoy was early, or the schedule had changed, or the machine upstream had noticed something downstream stirring.

"He's still alive," Hollis said quietly. "Wren. Or was, as of four years ago. I've seen him. On the escort boats, sometimes, standing at the rail. Older. Gray. Alive." He turned to face them fully, and in the dark his eyes were wet and furious. "I don't want money. I don't want forgiveness—I know better than to ask for that. I want the record made true. I want Aldous Wren's name written down somewhere it can't be erased, with the date and the boat and the bank and my name next to it as the man who didn't turn around. Can you do that?"

Mara thought of the vaults she'd never seen and the orders she had signed and the blank spaces she had drawn with her own careful hands, and she said, "Yes. In ink. In public. With your name in it."

"Then ask me anything," Hollis said. "Ask me everything. I've got charts in my boathouse older than your government, girl, and thirty-one years of soundings in my head. We'll build you a map of the country that isn't there."

* * *

They worked until dawn in the boathouse, Mara drafting on the backs of old navigation charts while Hollis dictated depths and distances from memory and Tamsin cross-checked against the wreck salvage records he carried in his head like other men carry prayers. It was the strangest mapping session of her career: no instruments, no satellite fixes, no verified sources—just an old man's memory and a ghost's recollections and her own guilty expertise, building a country out of rumor.

But it held together. That was the thing that frightened her most. Rumor, laid end to end, *held together*—the barge schedules fit the channel depths, the channel depths fit the condemned rail line, the rail line pointed straight upriver toward the blank on every map she'd ever trusted. The lies interlocked. Lies that interlock are architecture.

She was drawing the gorge reach, estimating the convoy's turning basin at the confluence, when Tamsin went very still over the chart.

"Mara," he said. "The east channel past the Narrows. Hollis's soundings. Look at the depth profile."

She looked. Shoaling water, then a sudden deep hole—forty meters, maybe more, right where the channel bent. An anomaly. Rivers don't dig holes like that without reason.

"That's not natural scour," she said slowly.

"No," Tamsin said. "It's not. I've seen a hole like that before. Off the wreck. Where the navy wouldn't let divers go." He looked up at her, and in the lamplight of the boathouse his face had gone the color of the paper. "There's something on the bottom of this river. Something big enough to change the way the current digs. And the barges aren't avoiding it, Mara. They're routing their entire supply line around it like it's a harbor."

Outside, far downstream, the green light blinked again—closer now—and Hollis rose from his chair with the slow gravity of a man who has waited thirty years for a night like this one.

"That'll be them," he said. "Early. Which means they know I'm talking."
