## 7. What the Water Carries

The river town of Callow's Bend had one gas station, two churches that hated each other on principle, and a hardware store whose owner sold me a water testing kit without asking why a man with no fixed address wanted to know what was in his drinking water. That last part was the miracle. In fifteen years of living off the record, I'd learned that the people least likely to ask questions are the ones with the most to hide themselves. Old man Petrie behind the counter had a face like a man who'd buried something in his backyard and watered the grass over it every Sunday. We understood each other.

"Field kit's forty dollars," he said. "Lab-grade strips are another twenty. You want the good ones."

"I always want the good ones."

He wrapped them in newspaper like they were fish. "River's been funny lately," he offered, unprompted, which in a town like this was practically a press conference. "Frogs gone quiet upstream of the bend. Not dead, mind. Quiet. Like they're waiting for something."

"Frogs do that," I said.

"Not these frogs. These frogs have sung through three floods and a chemical spill back in '09." He rang me up. "Something changed the water."

I took my kit and my paranoia out into the noon heat, where Mara was waiting by the truck with a map spread across the hood, weighting the corners with stones she'd chosen with a cartographer's precision—flat ones, gray ones, ones that wouldn't blow away or roll.

"Well?" she said.

"He thinks something changed the water."

She looked up. "That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke. That's what worries me."

* * *

Here's what you need to know about testing water: it's diving's boring cousin. Both are about reading what's invisible until you look properly. A diver reads current, thermocline, the way silt hangs in the water column like a held breath. A test strip reads parts per million of things that have no business being there at all. Fifteen years of salvage work had taught me that water is honest in a way people aren't. It carries everything. It forgets nothing. It just moves what it knows downstream and waits for someone patient enough to ask.

We set up on the flat rocks below the old ferry landing, half a mile downstream from where the survey team's last camp sat empty and wrong. Mara handled the map work; I handled the chemistry, because apparently a life of erasing yourself leaves you with transferable skills in handling things that dissolve.

First sample: standard river water. The strips came up boring. Nitrates within range, pH unremarkable, dissolved oxygen fine. The kind of water you could bottle and sell to yoga studios.

Second sample, taken thirty meters closer to shore where an eddy swirled slow against the bank: also boring.

Third sample, and this is the one that mattered—I'd gone looking for it specifically. Where a small tributary creek bled into the main channel, there was a seam in the water, visible if you knew to look. Two bodies of flow meeting and not quite mixing, the way cold and warm water slide past each other in a lake. The tributary came down from the high country. From upstream. From the direction of a blank space on every map Mara owned.

I dipped the vial in the seam itself, where the waters braided.

The strip turned colors I had to check twice against the reference card. Then a third time, holding both up to the sun like a jeweler assessing a stone he suspected was fake.

"Mara."

She didn't come over immediately. She finished marking her grid first, because that was who she was—a woman who would finish a task while the building burned around her, on the principle that the map would outlive the fire. Then she crouched beside me and took the strip from my fingers.

"What am I looking at?"

"You're looking at a compound that doesn't have a name on this card. Or any card I've ever seen. Look at the molecular weight indicator—that band shouldn't be able to trigger at all in surface water. It's a synthetic marker. Something built in a lab, designed to be traceless except under specific reagents." I tapped the strip. "Which means whoever made it assumed nobody would ever test for it. Because why would you? It doesn't exist."

"But it does."

"It does. And it's coming down that creek from somewhere upstream, in concentrations low enough that nobody downstream would ever notice unless they were looking for exactly this." I sat back on my heels. "Trace amounts. Homeopathic, almost. But consistent. This isn't contamination. Contamination spikes and fades. This is a signature. Steady output. Something up there is producing this continuously and has been for long enough that the whole watershed downstream is carrying it."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. A kingfisher went past low over the water, blue as a struck match, and even it seemed to be minding its own business more carefully than usual.

"The team found something upstream," she said finally. "The night before they vanished, Deniz—the senior hydrologist—pulled me aside. He said the samples from the headwater seeps were 'impossible.' He said it laughing, but it was the laugh you use when you're scared and don't want your colleagues to hear it. He wouldn't show me the data. Said he wanted to verify first." Her jaw tightened. "Then everyone walked into the woods and didn't walk out."

"And their gear stayed behind. Instruments still running."

"Instruments still running." She stared at the strip in her hand. "Tamsin. If this compound is in the water supply—even at trace levels—what does it do?"

"That," I said, "is the question I've been circling since a sealed case hummed at me from inside a wrecked navy hull."

* * *

I hadn't told her about the case yet. Not all of it. There's a taxonomy of secrets, and I keep mine sorted: the ones that protect other people, the ones that protect me, and the ones I'm not ready to say out loud because saying them makes them real. The case had been sitting in category three for two weeks, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of my gear bag, humming its quiet fifteen-years-ahead-of-everything hum whenever I let myself think about it.

But the water had changed the math. Secrets are like ballast—you carry them fine until the current shifts, and then suddenly they're dragging you under.

I got the case out and unwrapped it on the flat rock between us.

Mara went very still. "You said you recovered equipment from the wreck."

"I said I recovered equipment. I didn't say what kind." I opened the latches. Inside, nested in foam cut with machine-shop precision, lay six canisters of dark glass, each sealed with a valve assembly that belonged in a hospital or a weapons program, depending on your disposition, and a control module the size of a paperback book, its casing stamped with a serial number format I recognized the way you recognize handwriting.

"This isn't salvage," Mara said. "This is payload."

"This is delivery infrastructure. Metered release valves, flow-rate governors, redundancy systems. You don't build this to store something. You build this to put something *into* moving water, gradually, at controlled concentrations, over a long period." I picked up one canister. Through the dark glass, the contents moved with a viscosity slightly too slow, like syrup deciding whether to commit. "And the wreck it came off of was positioned at a junction point in the shipping lanes. If you ran these valves on a schedule, you could dose a harbor. A municipal intake. Anything downstream."

"Downstream," Mara repeated. She was doing cartographer math; I could see it running behind her eyes. "The wreck was downstream of a major city's water intake."

"Forty kilometers. Close enough that a sustained release would register in the reservoir within weeks. Far enough that the source would never be traced to a navy hull sitting in a restricted zone nobody civilian is allowed to dive." I set the canister down carefully, the way you set down a thing that might be listening. "When I took this job, I thought I was stealing evidence of smuggling. Arms, maybe. Drugs in a fancy wrapper. Then the case started humming and I knew it was worse, but I told myself worse had a ceiling."

"And now?"

"Now I'm looking at a tributary carrying the same class of synthetic compound down from a blank spot on the map, and I'm thinking the ceiling was a lie I told myself so I could keep sleeping." I looked at her. "There's a facility upstream, Mara. Maybe not a building—maybe just a site, a source—but something up there is making this stuff, and the wreck was one node in a distribution network. Somebody is putting a designed molecule into water systems and letting the rivers do the delivery. Free logistics. Untraceable. By the time anyone tests for it—if anyone ever tests for it—it's already everywhere the water goes."

Mara picked up one of the test strips and held it to the light, and I watched a professional's discipline fight a human being's horror and win, narrowly.

"My maps," she said quietly. "Every municipality downstream of the headwaters. Every town on the main stem. That's—" She stopped. Recalculated. Started again with a smaller number, the way you do when the big number won't fit in your chest. "That's a lot of taps."

"A lot of taps."

"And nobody's sick. Nobody's acting strange. The frogs are just quiet." She frowned. "If it were poison, we'd see bodies. If it were a sedative at these concentrations—"

"We'd see something. Yeah." I turned the control module over in my hands. There, on the base plate, half worn away by salt water but legible if you tilted it against the sun: a contractor mark. Not a logo—a code, alphanumeric, the kind of stamp used on classified government fabrication contracts. I'd seen that format once before, years ago, in a life I'd paid good money to have deleted. My hands remembered before my head did. They went cold around the module.

"Tamsin?"

"I know this stamping format," I said. My voice came out flatter than I intended, which is what happens when the past puts its hand on the back of your neck. "It's internal. Defense-adjacent fabrication. The kind of contract that doesn't appear in procurement databases because the procurement database doesn't officially exist." I set the module down. "Whoever built this delivery system builds other things in the same shop. Things I've seen."

"Seen where?"

And there it was, the question I'd been swimming away from for fifteen years, finally surfaced right in front of me, patient as a pike.

"Somewhere I used to work," I said. "Before I was nobody."

Mara didn't push. That was the thing I was learning about her—she mapped people the way she mapped terrain, noting the contours, respecting the cliffs, waiting for the trail to exist before walking it. She just nodded slowly and looked back upstream, toward the hills that hid the tributary's origin, toward the blank.

"So we follow the compound," she said. "Up the tributary. Concentration gradient gives us a bearing. The closer we get to the source, the stronger the signature gets. It's not a map, but it's better than one—it's a compass that points at whatever they're hiding."

"Hydrology as navigation." I almost smiled. "Deniz would've liked that."

She flinched at the name, small and fast, and I pretended not to see, because that was the courtesy liars extend each other.

* * *

We spent the rest of the day working upstream in hops, sampling every few hundred meters, and the data did exactly what data does when it's telling the truth: it climbed. Faint at the confluence. Stronger at the second bend. By late afternoon, at a pool beneath a fallen cottonwood where the tributary slowed and deepened, the strips were triggering bands I'd only seen in the reference card's most extreme ranges—and one band beyond the card entirely, past the printed scale, into territory the manufacturer hadn't bothered to color-code because no natural water sample would ever get there.

I sat on the cottonwood trunk and looked at the strip and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from confirmation. Fear is the body anticipating. Confirmation is the body receiving.

"It's industrial," I said. "At this concentration gradient, the source isn't a spill or a leak. It's an outfall. Deliberate discharge, probably treated as waste stream from whatever process they're running. They're not even hiding it from the river—they're hiding it from the *maps*. Let the water carry it anywhere, because nobody knows to look."

Mara was sketching—fast, rough, the field-map style she probably learned before she learned cursive. Sample points, concentrations, bearings. A vector drawing itself out of scattered numbers, pointing up-canyon like an accusation.

"There," she said, tapping the line. "If the gradient holds, the source sits in the upper gorge. Which is inside—" she paused, and I watched her make herself say it— "the jurisdictional blank. The area that isn't on any public map. The area my own office filed as 'no survey required.'"

"No survey required," I echoed. "Somebody wrote that order."

"Somebody with authority over my office." She capped her pen with a click that sounded final. "Tamsin, when we find whoever's running this—what then? We're two people with a water kit and a stolen payload. They have a navy."

"They have a navy," I agreed. "But they have one weakness that no amount of navy fixes."

"Which is?"

"They believe the water keeps their secret." I stood, knees cracking, and looked up the canyon where the light was going amber and the tributary whispered its steady, contaminated whisper over the stones. "Water doesn't keep secrets. Water delivers. Everything it touches, it carries onward—downstream, downhill, into the ground, into the sea. They built their whole operation on the assumption that the current only flows one way. That what goes downriver stays dispersed, diluted, deniable."

Mara looked up at me. "And?"

"And I'm a salvage diver. My entire career is based on one fact: everything the water takes, the water also holds. Wrecks, bodies, cargo, truth. It sinks, it settles, it waits. Sooner or later somebody comes down with a light and a hook and brings it back up." I shouldered my pack. "They put their secret in the river. That was the mistake. The river is the worst place in the world to hide anything from someone who knows how to read it."

We broke camp as the light failed, and I took one last sample at the pool's edge, mostly out of habit, partly out of something darker—the compulsive checking of a man who has learned that the world is always worse than reported.

The strip triggered instantly. Deep, saturated, off-scale.

And then, as I watched, the color shifted. Banded. Resolved into a pattern I recognized—not from any reference card, but from the control module sitting in my gear bag. The compound's concentration profile matched the metered-release signature of the delivery system in the wreck. Same engineering. Same hand. Same shop that had stamped its code into the hull of my old life.

Someone upstream wasn't just making this stuff.

They were running a live test. Right now. On this watershed. On every town downstream.

On us.

Mara saw my face in the last of the light and didn't ask. She just started packing faster, and above the gorge, far off, where the blank country began, something that might have been machinery—or might have been the river changing its voice—hummed once and went quiet.
