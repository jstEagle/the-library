## 9. Patrol Lines

The river changed its mind about us two miles below the checkpoint.

I'd been reading water for twenty years the way other men read faces, and this stretch had gone from a chatty, easygoing current to something tight-lipped and formal. The reeds along the bank stood too straight. A heron that should have been fishing the shallows was gone. And on the far bend, half-hidden by willow, sat a navy patrol boat with its engine idling at a pitch I recognized from a former life: the sound of a vessel pretending to be bored.

"They've been there since dusk," Mara said. She'd spotted it an hour before I did, which annoyed me less than it should have, because Mara Voss spotting things before me was becoming the natural order of the universe. She lay flat in the bottom of the skiff with her chin on her folded arms, watching the bend through a gap in the canvas we'd rigged. "Two more upstream of it. I counted their running lights when the fog thinned."

"Three boats to guard a river nobody officially knows anything about," I said. "That's not security. That's confession."

"Everything about this river is a confession," she said. "It's just written in a language they think only they can read."

We'd been on the water for six days by then, moving at night, sleeping in the day under cut brush, eating cold food out of cans because a cooking fire was a flare gun aimed at yourself. My hands had gone soft from disuse—diver's hands are supposed to be ruined, and mine were getting *better*, which told me how long it had been since I'd earned my living honestly. Or dishonestly. Salvage sits somewhere between the two, morally speaking, like most things worth doing.

The checkpoint itself I'd scouted from the ridge above it that afternoon, lying in fern shade with binoculars while Mara slept badly beside me. It wasn't much to look at: a boom across the channel made of linked steel pontoons, a floodlight rig on a barge, the patrol boat, and a smaller rigid-hull inflatable doing lazy figure-eights downstream. What mattered wasn't what you could see. It was what you couldn't.

"Hull sensors," I said now, in the dark, keeping my voice under the sound of the current. "Towed arrays. Hydrophones strung behind each boat listening for engines, prop wash, anything mechanical in the water."

Mara turned her head toward me. In the starlight her face was all angles and patience. "So we don't use anything mechanical."

"So we go under them," I said. "On our own lungs. The way I used to get into places people paid good money to keep me out of."

She was quiet for a moment. I'd learned by then that Mara's silences weren't empty; they were load-bearing. She was calculating something, or deciding whether to tell me she already had.

"You're proposing we swim through a naval checkpoint at night," she said finally, "in water cold enough to stop your heart if you stay down too long, past hydrophones designed to hear exactly this kind of thing."

"When you put it that way it sounds reckless."

"How would you put it?"

"Surgical," I said. "With a margin for error I'll determine as we go."

That got the closest thing to a laugh she ever gave me—a single exhale through the nose, quickly suppressed, like laughter caught sneaking into a country without papers. Then she did the thing she always did when fear arrived: she got practical. "How deep is the channel at the boom?"

"Fourteen feet at the pontoon line. Deeper mid-channel, maybe eighteen. Cold. There's outflow from a culvert on the east bank—I felt it this afternoon, colder water layering over warm. That thermocline will scatter sound. If we hug it, we're ghosts."

"And if we surface inside the perimeter?"

"Then we're ghosts who got caught," I said. "Which is just regular people with better stories."

MARA

He talked about death the way other men talk about weather, and I had decided, somewhere around the third night on the river, that this was either courage or damage and probably both, and that I liked him anyway, which frightened me more than the patrol boats did.

I lay in the skiff and listened to him lay out the plan in his flat salvage-yard cadence, and I did the thing I do with maps: I built the crossing in my head, layer by layer, until I could see it whole. Tamsin thought in fathoms and minutes. I thought in contours. Between us we had, possibly, one complete person.

"The problem isn't the swim," I said. "The problem is the gear."

He nodded slowly. I could feel him being pleased that I'd seen it, and I could feel him trying not to be pleased, and I decided to let him have his small vanity.

"We can't take the pack," he said. The pack held everything: the flash drive with the personnel files, my field notes, the compound samples sealed in glass, the photographs of the last camp where eight people had evaporated out of existence leaving their dinners half-eaten. Fifteen years of his erased life and three weeks of my ruined one, wrapped in oilcloth. "Buoyancy changes. Noise changes. We'd be towing an anchor with our names on it."

"So we sink it," I said. "Weighted, sealed, on a line. Drop it upstream of the boom, let it rest on the bottom, swim through light, pick it up on the far side."

He was quiet long enough that I thought he was going to argue. Instead he said, "You've done this before."

"I've hidden things before," I said. "Maps are mostly about hiding things. You draw the road so no one asks about the bridge." I rolled onto my back and looked at the stars, which were doing their indifferent best. "My father kept documents under the floor of our house for eleven years. Paper about people the government said didn't exist. I grew up knowing exactly how much weight a floorboard can hide."

He didn't ask what happened to my father. He never asked the questions that mattered, which was, I understood now, his own form of courtesy—the courtesy of a man who had spent fifteen years making sure nobody asked him anything at all.

"Two weights," he said at last. "One on the bag, one on the line end. We sink it here, mark the spot, recover it below the boom. If we miss it—" He stopped.

"If we miss it, we've lost everything and we keep swimming anyway," I finished for him. "Yes. I know. I've done the arithmetic. Everything we carry is replaceable except the drive, and the drive is backed up in my head, in shorthand nobody alive can read but me." I had spent two nights transcribing the files into a cartographic cipher of my own design, coordinates standing in for names, contour lines for dates. Hale's archive, translated into geography. They could search us until their gloves wore out and find nothing but a woman's obsessive hobby.

"That's the most frightening sentence anyone's said to me in years," Tamsin said.

"Good. Fear keeps the hands steady."

"It doesn't, actually. But it's nice that you believe it."

* * *

We went in at 0300, because that was the hour when the watch rotations overlapped and even disciplined men let their attention sag into the wet wool of the small hours. The skiff we scuttled in the reeds a mile back—we'd bought it with cash from a farmer who hadn't asked questions, and I weighted it to the bottom so it wouldn't drift downstream and announce us. Then we walked the bank, boots in hand, until we reached the culvert outflow, and there we prepared ourselves the way I used to prepare for a working dive: methodically, silently, checking each other's kit because your own eyes lie to you when your pulse is up.

Wetsuits, thin ones, dark. Fins strapped to our packs' remains—no, no packs. Fins clipped to our belts. Weight belts with quick-release buckles, trimmed to almost nothing; we needed to sink, but not eagerly. Mara's hair was bound and greased flat. Her face in the dark was pale and absolutely calm, and I remembered what she'd said about fear keeping hands steady, and I understood that for her it was true, because her hands were steadier than mine.

"One breath at a time down there," I told her. "Never plan past the next breath. The body knows what to do if the head stays out of the way."

"You've said that four times."

"It's a good sentence. It bears repeating."

"Tamsin." She said my name—it wasn't my name, but it was the only one I owned—and waited until I looked at her. "If I go down, you take the drive and finish it. Don't come back for me. Promise me."

"I don't make promises I intend to break."

"Promise me anyway. Lie to me. You're good at it."

There was nothing to say to that that wouldn't be a lie, so I said nothing, and she read the nothing correctly, and something passed between us in the dark that neither of us would ever name, because naming it would have made it cargo, and cargo sinks.

We slid into the water together.

* * *

The cold hit like a slammed door. Fourteen degrees, maybe thirteen, the kind of cold that doesn't hurt at first because the nerves give up immediately. We swam hard for ninety seconds to push blood into the muscles, then found the thermocline where the culvert's cold outflow layered over the warmer channel water, and dropped beneath it into a stillness so complete it felt architectural.

Down there the world was simple. Dark water, faint star-glow silvering the surface far above, the boom's pontoons hanging ahead of us like the vertebrae of some drowned animal. I heard the patrol boat's array before I saw it—a towed hydrophone streamer trailing off the stern, a hundred feet of listening cable undulating in the current, sensitive enough to hear a fish blink. We were forty feet from it, hugging the cold layer, and every kick of my fins sounded in my own skull like a drumbeat in a cathedral.

Slow kicks. Wide, sculling strokes, the way you move when you're trying not to wake a house. Beside me Mara matched me stroke for stroke, and I remember thinking, with the strange clarity that cold water grants, that she had never once in her life been taught to do this, and she was doing it perfectly, because Mara Voss learned everything the way stone learns water: by being patient longer than anyone believed possible.

The first breath-hold lasted two minutes. When we surfaced—in the black shadow of the second pontoon, heads barely breaking the skin of the water—we breathed through cupped hands, barely, taking air from the boundary layer where the current broke against the steel. Above us, ten feet away, a sailor's boots crossed the pontoon deck. We hung in the water with our noses tilted back and listened to a man hum to himself, tunelessly, some pop song, and I thought: he has no idea. None of them ever have any idea. The state builds its walls and posts its guards and underneath it all the water goes on carrying whoever the water decides to carry.

Second dive. The deepest one. Mid-channel, eighteen feet, and the towed array swung closer as the patrol boat adjusted position, its cable sweeping down toward us like a slow pendulum of ears. I watched it descend and calculated, with the cold arithmetic part of my brain that has never once cared whether I lived, that we had perhaps thirty seconds before it passed through our depth. I took Mara's wrist and pulled her down and forward, and we flew through the dark water side by side, two shadows racing a cable, and the array swept past behind us close enough that I felt the pressure wake of it tug at my ankles like a curious hand.

It found nothing. Of course it found nothing. We were ghosts. We had been ghosts for years, both of us, in different ways, and tonight the state's own instruments confirmed it: nothing here, nothing to report, blank water, blank page.

We came up beyond the boom, beyond the light, in the reeds on the downstream bank, and lay gasping in the mud like landed fish, and I started laughing—silent, shaking laughter, the kind that hurts—and after a moment Mara joined me, her forehead pressed against the cold earth, her shoulders trembling, and we laughed at nothing and everything until the shivering took over and made further emotion impossible.

* * *

MARA

Afterward, recovering the sunken pack from its weighted line a mile downstream, my fingers so numb I had to work the knot with my teeth, I understood something about Tamsin Nakamura that I had suspected but never confirmed: he had loved the water his whole life and had spent fifteen years punishing himself by staying away from it, and tonight he had gone home.

I watched him in the shallows, hauling the pack up hand over hand, and there was a rightness in his movements that I had never seen on land. On land he was careful, provisional, a man perpetually ready to leave a room. In the water he was simply present, entire, a creature in its element. It occurred to me that whatever Hale had taken from him—the name, the records, the legal fact of his existence—he had not taken this. Some part of Tamsin had remained un-erasable, filed away in cold water where no bureaucracy could reach.

"You're staring," he said, without turning around.

"I'm mapping," I said. "It's what I do."

"What does the map say?"

I looked at him—at the man crouched in the river shallows wringing out his gloves—and I said, before I could decide whether to say it: "It says the blank spaces are shrinking."

He turned then. Water ran off his jaw. Behind him the river ran south toward towns full of people drinking water that someone upstream was quietly editing, and I felt the weight of the pack in my arms, the weight of the cipher in my head, the weight of the classification order I had signed with my own confident hand because they told me it was for the team's protection, and I knew that the shrinking blanks were going to cost us both everything we had left.

"Three days to the gorge," Tamsin said. "The ferryman's water. After that, no more checkpoints—just country that isn't on any map at all."

"Country that isn't on any map," I repeated. To a cartographer those words are blasphemy and prayer in equal measure.

He shouldered the pack. Somewhere upstream, a patrol boat's engine changed pitch, hunting, finding nothing, settling back into its boredom. We moved south along the bank, two sets of footprints filling slowly with river water, and behind us the checkpoint lights burned all night over a channel that had already given us up as impossible.

What we didn't know—what neither of us could know until the gorge took it away—was that the river had already begun keeping score.
