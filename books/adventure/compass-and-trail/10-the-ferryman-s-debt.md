## 10. The Ferryman's Debt

The river town was called Low Landing, which struck me as a name chosen by someone with no imagination and perfect honesty. It sat low by the landing. There it was.

Tamsin hated it on sight, which I took as a good sign. He hated every place we'd stopped since the gorge of evidence we'd lost downstream—hated the diners, hated the bait shops, hated the way people looked at two strangers with wet boots and dry mouths. But hatred, I was learning, was just Tamsin's word for attention. He paid attention to everything the way other men paid alimony: constantly, resentfully, and because he had to.

"Two nights," he said, counting coins onto the counter of the only boarding house that didn't ask questions. "Then we're gone."

"We need a boat."

"We need a miracle with a hull."

I'd learned not to argue with him until after dark. Tamsin's pessimism ran on a schedule; by morning it usually softened into something workable. So I let him count his coins and I went out into the evening to do what cartographers do when the map runs out.

We ask directions from the water itself.

* * *

The ferryman lived in a houseboat at the end of the rotting pier, in a structure that had been white sometime during the previous administration and was now the color of old teeth. His name was Emeric Voss—which made me laugh out loud, alone, in the dark, like a lunatic—and he was mending a net by lamplight with hands so scarred they looked braided.

"You're Voss," I said, before I'd decided to say anything at all.

He didn't look up. "You're the one been asking about barges. The girl at the chandlery says you got a mouth on you." He tied off a knot. "Sit down or float home."

I sat. The houseboat rocked gently, the way big things rock when they've stopped caring what happens to them.

"Emeric Voss," I said again. "Forty years on this stretch. Ran cargo up to the narrows until the navy bought the narrows out from under everyone. Retired under circumstances nobody talks about."

Now he looked up. His eyes were pale as river fog and twice as hard to see through. "Who told you all that?"

"A woman who reads charts for a living," I said. "And who knows that when a man retires suddenly from a river he's worked his whole life, either his health broke or something upstream did."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere behind us, in the town, a dog announced its opinion of the night.

"You want passage," he said. Not a question.

"I want the truth about the barges that go up at night."

"Those are different wants."

"They're the same want wearing different clothes."

For a long moment he studied me the way you study a chart with an error in it—looking for where the coastline stops matching the paper. Then he laughed, one dry syllable, and poured two glasses of something that smelled like it had been distilled in a grudge.

"Your name," he said.

"Mara Voss."

His eyebrows did something complicated. "Any relation?"

"To you? Almost certainly, somewhere back. Half this valley is related to half the other half." I took the glass. "It's my real name, if that's what you're asking. One of the few things about me that is."

"And the rest?"

"The rest," I said, "is why I'm here."

* * *

Here is what I told him, sitting in that lamplit cabin while the river breathed against the hull. And here is what I have never told anyone else, which is maybe why the telling came out so easily, the way a tooth comes out once it's loose enough.

Three years ago, I signed a document.

That's the whole confession, really. Everything else is furniture around it. I was a senior cartographer with the government survey office, and a classification order crossed my desk regarding the headwaters survey—the one that would eventually send six people, including me, up a river that officially did not exist past marker forty-one. The order redrew our operational maps. It blanked the upper reaches. It instructed field teams that instruments returning anomalous readings above the gorge were to be logged as equipment fault, recalibrated, and resubmitted.

I read it. I understood it. And I signed it, because the signature line said *Cartographic Authority* and that was me, and because the memo attached used the phrase *national hydrological security*, and because I was thirty-four years old and very good at my job and very tired, and because the alternative was asking questions, and asking questions in that building was how you ended up reassigned to coastal erosion data for the rest of your natural life.

So I signed. And then I handed the blanked maps to my own team—good people, careful people, people who trusted me—and sent them upriver into a country I had personally erased from their instruments.

Six went up. Five stopped existing. I survived because I'd stayed at base camp with a sprained ankle, and because whatever took them apparently had opinions about round numbers, or about me specifically, or simply hadn't finished its list.

"That's not complicity," Emeric said, when I finished. "That's a clerk's mistake."

"It wasn't a mistake. I knew the blanks were wrong. A mapmaker knows. You can feel a lie in a contour line the way a musician hears a wrong note. I felt it and I filed it anyway."

"What did you feel, exactly?"

I turned my glass. "That the river was being taken away from the river. That someone upstream wanted the water to belong to them and only them, and had spent enough money to make the geography agree."

Emeric nodded slowly, and refilled my glass without asking, which in his language was absolution of some kind. Maybe not forgiveness. Just acknowledgment that the debt existed.

"My turn," he said.

* * *

What Emeric Voss carried wasn't guilt exactly. It was inventory.

In his last year running cargo, before the navy closed the narrows, he'd made eleven trips he never entered in his logbook. Night runs. Naval escort both times—two gray boats riding his wake like dogs that had been told to heel but not to trust. His cargo was always the same: sealed crates, manifest marked *hydrological instrumentation*, heavier than instrumentation had any right to be, and always, always traveling upstream against the current of commerce. Nobody ships heavy things to nowhere unless nowhere is actually somewhere.

On the eleventh trip, they made him dock at a concrete apron below the gorge and unload onto a funicular—a rail line climbing straight up the cliff face into trees that weren't on any map he owned. He saw, briefly, men in coveralls moving crates along the platform. He saw a pipe, thick as his torso, running down the cliff into the water, humming. Not mechanically. Musically. A note held so low he felt it in his fillings.

Then a lieutenant put a hand on his shoulder, thanked him for his service to hydrological security, and informed him that his ferry license had been reviewed and found to contain irregularities. He could retire comfortably or contest the review uncomfortably. He retired. Forty years on the river, ended by a hand on his shoulder and a word he'd never heard before that week.

"Hydrological," I said.

"Security," he agreed.

We drank to the absurdity of it. Two liars and a dead word, toasting each other in a houseboat the color of surrender.

"So the barges still run," I said.

"Twice a month. Sometimes three. Bigger boats now, navy hulls, but same route, same hour—always between two and four in the morning, always hugging the east channel where the sonar shadow off the bluff covers them." He said it flatly, a man reciting tide tables. "They think nobody watches anymore. They think the watching retired when I did."

"Why tell us? Why help us?"

He set down his glass and looked at me with those fog-colored eyes, and for a moment I saw the young man he must have been, poling a loaded barge through fog at dawn, reading water the way I read contour lines.

"Because I'm seventy-one years old," he said, "and I have kept quiet for nine years, and keeping quiet has started to feel less like prudence and more like practice. Like rehearsal. Like I'm rehearsing for a confession I keep postponing." He tapped the deck with one braided finger. "This boat is my last one. Sold the others when my wife passed. She always said the river would settle its own accounts someday, and I thought she meant flood season." A pause. "She meant you, probably. Or someone like you."

"We can't pay you."

"I don't want money." He leaned forward. "I want the record true. That's all. You get up there, you find what's eating my river, and you make the record true before I die. Put my eleven trips in it. Put my name in it. Emeric Voss, master of the *Maren K.*, eleven unlogged runs, and why. I don't care if it's a court document or a newspaper or graffiti on the dam. True is true."

From the doorway behind us, Tamsin's voice, dry as August: "Graffiti's underrated. Longevity, low overhead."

I hadn't heard him come aboard. Of course I hadn't. The man moved like a rumor.

"How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough to know we're taking the barge." He stepped into the lamplight, and I watched two professional liars take each other's measure across a cabin full of smoke. Tamsin had that trick—I'd seen it with the ranger cabin knife, I'd seen it with the patrol boat captain—of making silence do the negotiating. Emeric matched him. For nearly a minute neither spoke, and the river filled the gap, slapping the hull, patient as bookkeeping.

Finally Emeric snorted. "Salvage diver."

"Retired ferryman."

"Heard you ran a checkpoint under a hull sensor last week."

"Heard you ran eleven night shipments for the people who built that sensor."

Another silence, shorter, and then both of them almost smiled, which between those two constituted a treaty.

* * *

We spent two days preparing, and I spent them doing the thing I do best, which is drawing maps that tell the truth.

Emeric gave me his memory and I gave it ink. Eleven runs, plotted by moon phase and current speed, cross-checked against the supply schedules Mara-the-cartographer had once processed without question and Mara-the-fugitive now read like scripture. The picture that emerged was beautiful and obscene: a logistics artery, beating twice monthly for nine years, feeding something at the headwaters with enough tonnage to build a small city—or a large machine. The compound traces Tamsin had flagged in our downstream samples fit the timeline too neatly to be coincidence. Whatever came out of that pipe Emeric had heard humming, it had been coming out for the better part of a decade, in quantities measured in tons.

"You understand what this means," Tamsin said on the second night, spreading my chart on Emeric's table. "It's not a lab. Labs don't need funiculars. This is production. Industrial scale. Which means staff, power, redundancy—" he tapped the gorge "—and defenses."

"Which means," I said, "we're not sneaking into a secret. We're sneaking into an industry."

"Cheerful."

"I'm a cartographer. Accuracy over comfort."

Emeric listened to all of it with his net-mending hands folded, and when we finished he said only: "The *Maren K.* handles eight tons and draws four feet empty. East channel, past the third bluff, there's a cut in the reeds—old logging slip, pre-war. You can lay up there in daylight and nobody from the water will see you. From there it's six miles on foot to the gorge." He looked at his own hands. "I'll take you as far as the slip. After that, an old man and his boat stay out of the story."

"You could come," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because it sounded like pity.

But Emeric shook his head without offense. "My part is the beginning and the end. I carry you to the truth, and someday—if you live—someone carries the truth back to me. That's a fair division of labor at my age." He stood, joints announcing themselves like a lock mechanism. "We cast off at two. Sleep if you can. Divers and draftsmen—you're both used to working in the dark, so maybe you already know: the dark doesn't scare honest people. It's the light afterward that does the damage."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat on the stern with my feet over the black water and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the ranger cabin.

Every bad thing upstream traced back, in some sense, to a pen in my hand. Not the facility itself—that predated me, those decades-old survey markers proved it—but the blindness did. The five who vanished walked into blankness I had drawn. If the maps had shown the funicular, the pipe, the concrete apron, any of it, my team would have refused the assignment, or reported it, or at minimum died knowing what killed them instead of dissolving into an equipment fault.

Tamsin found me there around one o'clock. He didn't sit so much as materialize beside me, which I suppose is what fifteen years of erasing yourself teaches you—how to arrive without disturbing anything.

"You signed a piece of paper," he said.

"You were listening."

"I'm always listening. It's my worst quality." He watched the water. "You want to know what I did? I helped bury a cleanup. Bodies, records, a whole village's worth of evidence, packed into a wreck and sunk where divers like me knew better than to look. I took payment to forget the coordinates. I've spent fifteen years being excellent at forgetting." He shrugged. "Between the two of us, you're the amateur."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"It's supposed to make you accurate. Guilt that isn't proportional isn't useful—it's just another kind of noise." He turned his head, and in the dark his eyes caught the shore lights, two small fixed points. "You signed one order under pressure. Fine. Now you're spending everything you have to correct it. That's not complicity anymore. That's repayment."

"And you?"

"Me?" Something moved across his face—not a smile, more like weather. "I'm still deciding what I am. Check back after the gorge."

At two o'clock precisely, Emeric cast off the *Maren K.* with a ceremony consisting entirely of spitting over the side, and we slid upstream into the navy's own shipping hour, hidden in plain traffic, carrying a dead ferryman's logbook, a living liar's confession, and a chart with nothing blank left on it.

The gorge was thirty miles ahead. The patrols, we'd learn soon enough, were closer than that.
