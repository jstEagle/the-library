## 11. Gorge of the Unmapped

The ferryman had a name he never used—out on the water everyone just called him Old Sedge—and when he handed over the barge at the last public dock above the gorge, he did it the way men hand over things they expect to grieve about: quickly, without looking back.

"She's not much," he said. "Hull's older than both of you put together. Engine coughs if you ask her to do anything rude." He spat over the rail with the precision of long practice. "But she'll get you to the narrows, and past the narrows nobody's boat goes but mine."

"And yours is going where?" Mara asked.

"Wherever they don't look." He said it lightly, but his eyes went downstream toward the gorge the way you'd look at an old dog you knew was dying. "You make the record true. That's all I want. I've got sins enough that a true record would bury me twice over, and I find I don't care anymore."

I'd heard that tone before. It was the sound of a man who had spent decades being careful and had finally run out of reasons.

We loaded what mattered. The flash drive with the personnel files went into a waterproof case strapped inside my wetsuit, against my sternum, where I could feel it like a second heartbeat. Mara's copied charts—the ones she'd redrawn from rumor, from the ferryman's stories, from the survey markers we hadn't reached yet—went into a second case. Her field notes. The water samples. Fifteen years of my own careful nothing, and one week of accumulated evidence that weighed less than a bag of groceries and was worth more than everything I'd ever salvaged.

"Weight distribution," I said, stowing it all low and central on the barge. "Keep the heavy stuff amidships."

"You always this fussy?"

"Fussy keeps you alive. Careless gets you scuttled."

I want that noted for the record, wherever the record ends up: I said it before it happened.

* * *

The gorge announced itself an hour later as a change in the river's voice. Upstream it had been murmuring, conversational. Now there was a low grinding undercurrent, stone on stone, the sound of a lot of water being forced through a place it didn't fit. The walls rose on either side—black basalt streaked with mineral stain, close enough in places that the barge's wake slapped back at us from the rock.

Mara stood at the bow with her map case open, though there was nothing on any map she owned that matched what we were seeing. "The gorge isn't here," she said. "Officially. This whole stretch is a rendering gap. Cartographers call it a data void." She laughed, short and bitter. "I called it one myself, once. Signed off on the blank space. Thought it was sensor error."

"What was it actually?"

"Somebody's budget line." She closed the case. "Blank space doesn't happen by accident, Tamsin. Somebody has to pay for it to stay blank."

The narrows came up fast after that—a throat of white water maybe forty meters across, the river dropping through it in three successive steps, each one throwing up spray that hung in the air like weather that couldn't commit to falling. Old Sedge had said nobody's boat went through but his, and I believed him, because looking at it, I wouldn't have taken anything through there I wasn't prepared to swim away from.

Which turned out to be the correct attitude.

Mara saw them first. She had good eyes and better instincts, and she'd been scanning the upstream bend every ninety seconds since we entered the gorge, a habit she'd picked up somewhere I hadn't asked about.

"Tamsin." Just my name, flat, no inflection. That was how she said things that mattered.

I looked. Around the bend, riding high and running dark except for a single running light they clearly didn't care who saw, came a patrol boat. Naval gray, shallow draft, a hull-mounted searchlight that swept the gorge walls with the bored confidence of something that owned them.

"They've been shadowing us since the dock," I said. "I figured they'd wait for open water."

"Why would they wait?" Mara was already moving, pulling the evidence cases from their lashings. "They don't need a reason. They need a result."

The patrol boat accelerated. There was no hail, no loudspeaker announcement, none of the theater of law enforcement. That told me more than any insignia would have. Real police announce themselves. This was housekeeping.

"Scuttling charge," I said. "Or they'll ram us and let the gorge do the work. Either way—" I looked at the water below us, black and violent and, God help me, familiar. "Either way we're swimming."

"I can't swim rapids like that."

"You can't. But you can dive them, if someone holds your hand." I was already stripping down to my wetsuit, checking seals with fingers that were, I noticed distantly, perfectly steady. Fifteen years of discipline paying its dividend at last. "Listen to me. The river under whitewater isn't one current—it's layers. Surface water moves fastest and does the stupidest things. Two meters down it slows and sorts itself out. Three meters down, in a gorge like this, there are seams—channels between the boils where the water is almost calm. You find a seam and you ride it like a handrail."

"You've done this."

"I've done worse." True, and not the half of it. "When I say go, we go off the upstream side, before they're in range. We go deep, we go together, and we do not come up until we absolutely have to. Whatever you're carrying, you hold it against your body with both hands. If you have to choose between the case and breathing—" I looked at her. "Don't choose the case."

She looked back at me, and I watched her decide to lie to me, and I decided to let her. We were both cartographers of our own omissions.

The patrol boat was two minutes out. Maybe less. Its searchlight found us then, pinned us like specimens, and a voice came across the water—not amplified, just shouted, which somehow made it worse:

"Stand by to be boarded!"

"Go," I said.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about diving rapids: it is quiet.

Above the surface, the gorge was a riot—engine noise, shouted orders, the roar of the falls building as we dropped toward them. Then I rolled off the barge's gunwale, pulled Mara under with me, kicked hard for depth, and the world shut its mouth.

Fifteen years, and this was still the only place I ever felt honest. Underwater there are no records, no names, no jurisdiction. There is only pressure and current and the next breath held in trust. My body knows things down there that my mind has spent a career forgetting. Read the water through your skin. Let the current tell you where it's going instead of arguing. Sink before you struggle.

Mara was beside me, a pale shape in the murk, arms locked around her case, eyes wide behind her mask. I gripped her wrist—three squeezes, our old signal from the checkpoint run, meaning *hold, stay, breathe*—and pointed downstream into the green-black channel.

The seam was there, exactly where the physics said it should be. A lane of slower water hugging the outside of the first drop, beneath the churning boil, marked by a drift of leaves and foam flecks moving with unnatural calm while chaos thundered overhead. I took her hand and we rode it.

Through the mask, muffled into something almost gentle, I could hear the barge die.

It didn't sound like an explosion. It sounded like a door closing—a deep concussive *whump* that I felt in my chest more than my ears, followed by a rain of small debris sifting down through the water column around us: splinters, a shredded life ring, a piece of painted rail turning slowly like a leaf. Bubbles came up in a silver wall, thousands of them, the barge exhaling everything it had, and for a moment we were inside a rising curtain of light.

They hadn't even waited to board. Hadn't checked for survivors. Hadn't pretended.

*Loss clarifies*, I thought, absurdly, watching a cabin cushion drift past me trailing stuffing. *That's the thing loss is for.*

Because here's what went through my head, hanging in the cold seam-water while a government erased the last neutral ground between us and it: I felt the cases' absence like a wound, and underneath the wound, something else. Something that had been waiting fifteen years for permission.

Anger, yes. But cleaner than anger. *Direction.*

I had spent my whole adult life perfecting disappearance. No name, no address, no trail. And these people had taken my finest work—my beautiful blankness—and used it as a template. Every disappeared person in Hale's archive had been given my treatment, refined, industrialized. My craft, scaled up into a weapon. And now they were erasing a barge, and two divers, and a week of evidence, with the same casual competence.

Fine. They were better funded. They had boats and charges and a director with a philosophy of water.

But I had been invisible longer than they'd been looking, and I knew—better than anyone alive—exactly what it takes to make a thing vanish.

Which meant I knew exactly what it takes to make a thing *reappear.*

* * *

The second drop nearly killed us.

The seam we were riding ended where the river pinched against a rock fin, and the water did something ugly—folded down and back on itself in a hydraulic, a rolling cylinder of recirculating foam that eats swimmers and keeps them. I felt the pull grab my fins and understood, with the calm clarity that only comes at the edge, that we had about four seconds.

No time to signal. I simply hauled Mara with me, straight down, abandoning the seam for depth, trading safety for survival. The hydraulic's reach extends down, but not far—not compared to a diver willing to go to six meters in black water beside a rock wall.

Down meant downcurrent drag, though, and down meant the third drop's plunge pool, and the plunge pool meant turbulence with no pattern at all. We tumbled. I lost contact with her hand for ten seconds that lasted a season. I found her again by the glint of her mask strap and caught her harness, and then the pool spat us out the way pools do—all at once, gracelessly, into the tailrace below the falls where the river gathered itself back up and remembered it was a river.

We surfaced in an eddy behind a boulder the size of a chapel, gasping, deafened by the falls, alive.

Mara came up spitting and immediately, before anything else, checked her case. Of course she did. So did I.

Mine was there. The flash drive, against my sternum, sealed, intact. Hers—

Her face told me before her words did. The case had been in her left hand when the hydraulic hit. The latch had held; the case itself had torn free, or she'd had to let go—I never learned which, and she never said. Her redrawn maps, her field notes, the chain of rumor and inference she'd been building since the ranger cabin: gone into the gorge, into the same water that was busy digesting a barge.

"The samples," she said, when she could talk. "Some of the sample vials were in my suit pocket." She patted herself, found them, counted. Four of nine. "Four. It's enough to re-run the assay. It's—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, I could see her hating it. "The maps took me a week."

"We'll rebuild them," I said. "Better ones. Because now we know what the blanks are hiding, and knowing is the part they can't sink."

She stared at me through the spray. "How can you be calm? They just tried to kill us."

"Oh, I'm furious." I tasted blood in my mouth and realized I'd bitten my lip somewhere in the tumble. "I'm so furious it's come out the other side into focus. There's a difference between losing something and having it taken, Mara. Losing something, you grieve. Having it taken—" I looked upstream, at the falls, at the country beyond them that appeared on no map made for public eyes. "Having it taken tells you exactly where to go."

* * *

We climbed out on the south bank, into terrain that was technically nothing at all. No jurisdiction, no survey, no name. The gorge walls rose sheer above us, and somewhere up there, past the third drop, past the old survey markers we hadn't found yet, past decades of deliberate blankness, sat the source.

Mara wrung out her hair and sat on a wet stone, holding her four surviving vials up to what little light filtered into the gorge, like a woman counting coins after a shipwreck.

"They'll report the barge destroyed with no survivors," she said. "By tomorrow we won't exist. Again." She glanced at me. "Sorry. Poor choice of verb."

"Don't apologize. It's accurate." I checked the flash drive case one more time—compulsion, ritual, the diver's habit of trusting nothing—and felt its small weight against my chest. One drive. Four vials. Two people the state had just formally deleted.

And one direction. Upstream.

"Mara," I said. "How are you at walking through unmapped country?"

She stood up, dripping, and looked at the gorge walls the way she'd once looked at blank spaces on charts—with the particular hunger of someone who has decided that empty places are personal insults.

"Tamsin," she said, "I've been redrawing this river from rumor for a week. Show me where the rumors end."

We started climbing.
