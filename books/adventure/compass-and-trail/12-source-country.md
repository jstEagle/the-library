## 12. Source Country

I have been making maps since I was nine years old, when I traced the coastline of a country I'd never seen onto the back of a cereal box and decided that knowing where things were was the same as being safe. Twenty years later I held a federal cartography license and a security clearance, and I still believed it. Maps were how you kept the world from surprising you.

The world had been surprising me for eleven days now, and I was starting to take it personally.

We climbed out of the gorge on the third morning after the barge went down, Tamsin ahead of me with the rope wrapped twice around his forearm, moving through the rapids' spray like a man walking through a memory of water rather than water itself. He'd lost his dive bag in the scuttling—most of our evidence with it—and he hadn't said a word about it since. That silence worried me more than shouting would have. A man who shouts is still arguing with his losses. A man who goes quiet has started carrying them.

"Handhold at two o'clock," he called down, without looking back.

"You say that like I know what o'clock means in a waterfall."

"It means grab the rock before you die."

Fair enough. I grabbed the rock.

* * *

The gorge spat us out into a valley that shouldn't have existed.

That's not poetry. I mean it as a professional observation. I had spent four years mapping this watershed at a resolution of one meter per pixel. I knew every tributary, every bench of flat ground, every place the river widened or narrowed or forgot itself. And the valley we stood in—the wide green throat of land above the gorge, ringed by ridgelines I could name from memory—was not on any of them. Not on my maps. Not on the public topographic series. Not on the survey plates I'd signed my initials to, back when signing things felt like an honor instead of a confession.

"Tamsin." My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Look at the walls."

He looked. The valley's sides weren't natural—not entirely. There were terraces cut into the slopes, old ones, softened by decades of moss and root, but regular as stair steps. And along the valley floor, half-swallowed by grass, ran a line of posts: iron, rusted to the color of dried blood, spaced at intervals too consistent to be accidental.

Survey markers.

I walked to the nearest one and knelt. The cap was stamped with a designation code from an agency that had been reorganized out of existence before I finished graduate school. Below the code, a date.

Nineteen fifty-eight.

I sat back on my heels. "This marker predates my parents' marriage."

Tamsin crouched beside me. He didn't touch the post. He looked at it the way I'd seen him look at hull welds underwater—reading failure before it happened. "How far back does your agency's mapping go?"

"Nineteen seventy-one for aerial coverage. Sixties for some cadastral work. Why?"

"Because whoever put this here didn't want it found, and they've had sixty-five years to make sure it wasn't." He straightened. "Your maps aren't wrong, Voss. They're curated."

Curated. I turned the word over and felt something in my chest tilt, slow and structural, like a shelf giving way one book at a time. I had spent my career believing in the honest blankness of unmapped space—the humility of the white margin, *here be dragons*, the admission that the world exceeds our knowledge. But there are two kinds of blank on a map. The kind where nobody has looked.

And the kind where somebody looked, and paid to make sure you never would.

* * *

We followed the line of markers upstream all day.

They climbed with the river, patient as rosary beads, each one stamped with the same defunct agency and dates marching backward and forward through decades—1958, 1961, 1963, then a gap, then 1974, 1979, 1983. Someone had been coming up here every few years for most of a century, checking their claim, maintaining their erasure. At one point the line passed through a stand of pines so old and so even-aged that I stopped counting rings on a fallen trunk because the number made my hands shake.

Nobody logged this forest. Nobody surveyed these markers into any database I'd ever touched. Which meant the people who did this work existed outside the entire apparatus I had given my life to—a shadow civil service, running its own surveys, keeping its own records, answering to no map but its own.

"I helped build the machine," I said, somewhere past midday. We'd stopped at a stream junction to drink, and the words came out of me the way water comes out of a cracked pipe—under pressure, in all directions. "Four years. I digitized forty thousand plates. I quality-checked coordinates down to centimeters. And none of it—" I gestured at the valley, the markers, the whole curated nothing, "—none of it touches this. It's not a gap in my data. It's a wall around it."

Tamsin wrung out his shirt and didn't look at me. "You signed the classification order on your own team's findings."

It wasn't a question. He'd dragged it out of me two nights ago on the ferryman's barge, in the dark, when I finally told him what I'd done—how the anomaly readings from our upstream instruments had come back flagged, how I'd been instructed to seal them, how I'd obeyed, and how my team had gone up the river anyway, because good cartographers hate a blank more than they fear an order.

"Yes."

"And you thought that was the worst thing in the world. Sealing one report."

"Yes."

He tied off his shirt at his waist and started walking again. "Then recalibrate."

Recalibrate. It's what we say in my profession when new data breaks the old model. You don't mourn the model. You don't argue with it. You let it break, and you build a truer one, and you feel like an idiot for however long it takes your hands to stop shaking.

My hands were shaking. I built the truer model anyway, walking, in my head, while the markers counted off the years beside me like a metronome set to a century.

* * *

Near dusk we found the cabin.

Not a ranger cabin—nothing so innocent. It sat in a clearing above the river, log-walled, sod-roofed, built to be invisible from the air under canopy. Inside: bunk frames, a wood stove cold for years, and a long table bolted to the floor. On the table, under a sheet of oilcloth, lay charts.

I unrolled them with the reverence I usually save for dead languages.

They were beautiful. That's the horror of it—I can't show you what I saw without you understanding that it was *beautiful*. Hand-inked bathymetry of the headwaters spring, contour lines flowing like calligraphy. Chemical assays in a draftsman's hand going back to 1959, columns of compounds with concentrations tracked seasonally, annually, across six decades of meticulous, patient science. Cross-sections of the aquifer. Flow models. And annotations, marginalia in three different generations of handwriting, discussing extraction yields, dilution ratios, delivery infrastructure downstream.

Municipal delivery infrastructure.

I read the phrase three times before it detonated. Then I read the columns again—the compound names, the concentrations, the seasonal variation curves—and matched them against the trace analysis Tamsin and I had run ourselves on the river, two weeks and a lifetime ago, downstream. Our numbers fit inside theirs like a key inside a lock.

"They've been measuring this since before I was born," I said. "Before my *parents* were born. This isn't an operation. It's an institution. It has *succession planning*."

Tamsin stood at the window, watching the light die over the ridge. When he spoke, his voice had the flat calm I'd learned to read as his version of screaming. "Fifteen years ago someone erased me. Wiped my records, rebuilt my identity, taught me to live off the books. I spent fifteen years thinking I was the exception. The loose thread." He turned. "There's a table in this room older than my whole erased life, Voss. I'm not a loose thread. I'm a *stitch pattern*. They've been doing this to people since before there were computers to do it with."

I looked down at the charts. At the careful, loving, monstrous precision of them. Sixty-five years of someone's life's work, and the work was making water lie.

Awe curdles, if you let it sit. It had taken me one afternoon. I had come up this river ready to be furious at a cover-up—a scandal, a crime, something with edges you could photograph. What I found instead was a civilization's worth of patience. An erasure so old it had grown its own ecosystem, its own cabins, its own succession of draftsmen. You cannot expose a thing like that with one leak. You can only expose it the way you drain a swamp: slowly, publicly, and knowing it will fight you the whole way down.

"Tamsin," I said. "The last assay entry. Look at the date."

He leaned over the table. The final column of numbers was fresh—ink barely oxidized, paper still bright where the pen had pressed.

Three weeks ago.

Someone maintained this cabin. Someone climbed this valley recently, on foot, past the patrols and the checkpoints and the drowned barge, and sat at this table, and wrote down what the spring was doing. And unless they were very careless—which the previous sixty-five years suggested they were not—they knew we were coming.

Or they didn't care.

Tamsin picked up the pen that lay beside the chart. He weighed it in his palm like a diver weighing a lift bag before the ascent. "Whoever writes here," he said, "believes no one will ever read it."

"Are we going to prove them wrong?"

"We're going to do better than that." He set the pen down exactly where it had lain, aligned to the millimeter, and I understood: he wasn't being tidy. He was leaving a message. *We were here. We read you. We're still coming.*

Outside, far upstream, where the valley narrowed toward the source, a light came on. Just one. Steady. Unhurried. The light of a place that had been expecting visitors for sixty-five years and saw no reason to start rushing now.

Tamsin watched it for a long moment. Then he shouldered his pack.

"Mile zero," he said. "Let's go introduce ourselves."
