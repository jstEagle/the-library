## 13. The Installation

The last three miles, we didn't talk. There's a kind of quiet that happens when two people are both doing the same math and neither wants to say the answer out loud. The river had narrowed to something you could shout across, cold enough to make your teeth ache just looking at it, and the air had that mineral smell I knew from the inside of pressure hulls—stone and iron and old water that hadn't seen sun in a long time.

Mara stopped first.

She didn't say anything. She just put her hand out flat against my chest, the way you'd stop someone walking into traffic, and looked up at the cliff face ahead of us where the valley pinched shut.

I looked. And then I wished, briefly and uselessly, that I hadn't.

The spring came out of the mountain the way springs do—except this one came out through a wall. Not around it, not over it. Through it. A concrete face forty feet high, poured to look like rock from a distance, with the water emerging from a series of intake grates arranged in a fan pattern that no accident of geology would ever produce. Above the grates, set back into the cliff like an eye socket, was a structure: low, windowless, the color of wet slate, its roofline following the contour of the mountain so precisely that from the air—and there would be aircraft, there were always aircraft—you'd read it as shadow.

"Tell me I'm wrong," Mara said quietly.

"You're not wrong."

"I keep hoping one of these days you'll tell me I'm wrong about something."

"Not today."

We crouched in the alder scrub at the valley's throat and did what we'd been doing for eleven chapters' worth of country: we watched. It's the skill nobody writes ballads about, watching. Running is dramatic. Fighting is dramatic. Watching is just hours of your knees hurting while you memorize the way light moves across a thing you intend to survive.

Two sentry positions, disguised as survey monuments. A vehicle track that entered the cliff face through a door painted to match the stone—the paint was good, but paint doesn't weather honestly, and this paint was too honest by half. Power lines running up-valley from a transformer station camouflaged as a ranger cache. And the sound. Under the river noise, if you got still enough, there was a hum. Low, steady, patient. The hum of machinery that had been running for years and intended to run for years more.

"That's the source," Mara said. "That's what my maps couldn't hold."

I nodded, but I wasn't really listening anymore, because the fan pattern of those intakes had snagged something in me, some hook set deep, and now the line was pulling.

I'd seen that pattern before. Not the grates themselves—I'd never been here—but the *logic* of them. Even distribution across the flow profile to avoid creating preferential channels. Staged extraction so the upstream chemistry never showed a drawdown signature. You don't take all the compound; you take a fraction, everywhere, so the river downstream never knows it's been robbed. It was elegant. It was the kind of design you'd only build if you understood fluid dynamics the way a locksmith understands locks—not academically, but with your hands.

I understood it with my hands.

* * *

We went in at dusk, because dusk is when cameras get stupid and guards get human. Mara had spent two days on her maps—her real ones now, the ones she redrew from rumor and ferryman's gossip and the survey markers we'd found dated back before either of us was born—and she'd found us a seam. A maintenance access on the downstream side, where the original builders had cut into the spring before they'd gotten careful about appearances. The door was newer than the tunnel. Newer doors mean older secrets behind them.

The lock was electronic, which sounds like bad news until you've spent fifteen years learning that electronic locks are just honest people wearing dishonest uniforms. This one wanted a code. Codes come from patterns, and patterns come from people, and people are lazy in beautifully predictable ways. The maintenance panel had a service date stenciled beside it. I tried the date. Then the date reversed. Then the date with the year doubled, because somewhere a technician had thought he was being clever.

Third try. The bolt sighed back like a man giving up an argument.

Inside, the tunnel smelled of chlorine and cold stone, and the hum was no longer under anything. It was the walls. It was the floor coming up through our boots. Mara clicked on her penlight and swept it along conduit runs labeled in a stencil font I recognized from navy spec sheets, and I felt my stomach do something slow and structural, like a hull taking on water below the waterline.

"Tamsin." Her voice had gone flat, which for Mara meant frightened. "These are your schematics."

"They're not my schematics."

"They're your *handwriting*."

She was right, and that was the problem. Not literally mine—I'd never drawn these—but the conventions were mine. The labeling hierarchy. The way flow arrows curved instead of angled, a habit I'd picked up from a mentor whose name I had paid good money to forget. Somewhere in the fifteen years I'd spent erasing myself, the things I'd built had kept building without me. My habits had grown up and gotten government funding.

We followed the conduit toward the sound.

* * *

The main chamber opened up like a cathedral that hated God.

The spring emerged from raw bedrock at the far end—a genuine spring, ancient and blameless, pouring out of the mountain's heart in a column of water so clear it looked solid—and everything else in the room existed to feed on it. Extraction arrays fanned across the outflow in tiered banks, each one humming its note in the chord we'd heard from outside. Pipes ran from the arrays into processing units the size of shipping containers, and from those into a refinement line I could trace with my eyes closed, because I had helped design its grandmother.

Here's what I need you to understand, and I need you to understand it the way I understood it standing there, because there's no gentler version:

They weren't mining the water. They were *editing* it.

The compound—the one we'd found in trace amounts eighty miles downstream, the one that shouldn't exist, the one that had rewritten Mara's instruments overnight—wasn't a contaminant. It was a product. The facility pulled it from the spring at parts per billion, concentrated it, refined it into a stable suspension, and the end result went into those containers stacked along the north wall in neat, patient rows. Municipal markings. Water district codes. Shipment manifests, if the paperwork in the office alcove was any indication, routing to treatment plants in cities I could name and you could probably name too.

Untraceable. That was the genius of it, and I use the word with all the revulsion it deserves. At dilution, the compound did nothing detectable. No taste, no odor, no reaction on standard tests. But layered over weeks, months—it tuned people. Mood, compliance, suggestibility. Nothing you could point to. Nothing you could sue over. A whole city gently adjusted, the way you'd adjust a thermostat, and no one would ever know the temperature had been touched.

Controlled water is controlled peace. I didn't know yet that Hale said that—I wouldn't hear his voice for another few days—but I stood in his cathedral and heard the sermon anyway, preached in pipe diameters and pump schedules.

"Mara," I said. My voice came out strange. "Look at the intake staging."

She looked. She was a cartographer; she read systems the way I read wrecks. It took her maybe thirty seconds.

"The drawdown never shows," she said. "Downstream gauges would read normal. You could run this for decades and every monitoring station in the watershed would file clean reports." She turned to me, and her penlight caught her face from below, hollowing it out. "Tamsin. Who designs something like this?"

"Someone who learned it from people like me."

And there it was. The recognition, arriving right on schedule, right on cue, dressed exactly as I'd feared it would be. Fifteen years ago I had worked on projects I told myself were defensive—countermeasures, detection systems, ways to find contamination rather than cause it. That was the story I'd slept on. But design logic doesn't care about your stories. Design logic propagates. You teach a system how to hide its own footprint, and ten years later someone uses your hiding places as blueprints. Every clever trick I'd ever devised to make a signal disappear was sitting in this room, promoted, weaponized, given a budget line and a shipping schedule.

I had spent fifteen years measuring my life in miles logged, telling myself distance was the same thing as innocence. Standing in that chamber, I finally understood the arithmetic was wrong. You can't outrun your own handwriting. It waits. It gets funding.

I put my hand against one of the extraction arrays and felt the hum travel up my arm, and I thought: *this has my fingerprints on it, and I have never once touched it.*

"Tamsin." Mara's hand on my sleeve. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're breathing like a man taking depth readings. Come up."

I came up. That's the thing about salvage divers—we're trained to come up slow, no matter what's pulling us down.

* * *

We found the records annex off the processing floor: a climate-controlled room behind a second door, racks of documentation going back further than either of us wanted to calculate. Mara went straight to the shelves with the hunger I usually reserved for air supplies. I hung back at the threshold, because the room had a smell—paper and toner and cold filtered air—that reached back down fifteen years and found the part of me I'd buried, and it knew the address.

There were personnel files. I saw that much before I made myself look away. Rows of them. Names, or the absence of names, tabbed and cross-referenced in a hand that was meticulous in exactly the way I was meticulous, and I felt the dread arrive ahead of its explanation, the way pressure changes arrive before the storm.

"Mara. We can't stay. This room is a trap that hasn't sprung yet."

"Five minutes."

"Mara."

"Ten minutes, and I'm copying everything that matters." She was already working, photographing pages with the steady hands of someone converting fury into method, though neither of us had the vocabulary for that yet. "You watch the door. That's the division of labor. You haunt thresholds, I commit crimes."

So I watched the door, and I listened to the shutter click of her camera, and beneath it I listened to the hum of the arrays feeding on the spring, patient as tide. And in the margin of my fear, one thought kept surfacing like a body that won't stay down:

Somewhere in this building was a record of who I used to be. I could feel it the way you feel a wreck under you in black water—the certainty of mass, the certainty of shape, everything you need to know except whether finding it will save you or finish you.

The corridor lights changed. Not flickered—*changed*, stepping down to night-cycle amber in a smooth cascade from the far end of the hall toward us, floor by floor, like the building closing its eye.

Someone had initiated a lockdown sequence.

Or someone knew we were here.

Mara looked up from the files. I looked at her. Down the hall, a door we hadn't heard open made the small, polite sound of a door that expected to remain unheard.

Footsteps. Unhurried. Two sets, maybe three, and the particular cadence of men who are not searching for anyone because they already know exactly where everyone is.

I killed Mara's penlight with my palm and pulled us both into the dark behind the racks, into the paper-smelling dark where my old life was filed alphabetically, and waited to see who had come to collect the past.
