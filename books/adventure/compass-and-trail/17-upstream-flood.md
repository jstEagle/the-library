## 17. Upstream Flood

**MARA**

The vault had a smell I will never forget: cold paper and ozone, like a library struck by lightning. Every drawer I opened held a life folded flat—photographs, fingerprints, a single typed sheet that began *Subject is hereby relieved of identity pursuant to*—and then the number of the order, and then nothing. Hale signed them all. Same pen pressure on every signature, decades apart. A man who never got tired of unmaking people.

I copied everything twice. Once to the drive in my boot, once to the drive sewn into my jacket lining, because Tamsin had taught me the first rule of salvage: anything you can't afford to lose, you lose twice before you lose it for good.

The third rule—the one he'd taught me with his hands shaking around a cup of bad coffee in the ranger cabin—was about water. *Every system built by men has a valve,* he'd said. *Find the valve.*

The facility's valve was not hidden. That was the beautiful, arrogant thing about it. It was painted safety-yellow, stenciled EMERGENCY DISCHARGE — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY, mounted behind glass beside the main pump gallery on sublevel two, because no one in sixty years had imagined an unauthorized person would get this far. The discharge was designed to flush the lower levels in case of contamination—a fail-safe to protect the compound from *them*, the workers, the bodies. Sixty years of engineering devoted to the water's purity and not one hour to the people standing next to it.

I broke the glass with my elbow. The alarm that followed was almost polite—a soft chime, as if apologizing for the interruption.

Then the floor shuddered, and somewhere below us, the river came home.

* * *

**TAMSIN**

Solitary confinement, when your jailers are the state, is a room with a cot and a light that never quite turns off. When your jailers are Director Hale, it is a room with a cot, a light, and a speaker through which a calm voice occasionally reminds you that your name is waiting for you upstairs, all you have to do is say yes.

I had been saying nothing for—I didn't know how long. Hours. Days. Time down there was measured in meals, and the meals were identical, which made counting pointless. I lay on the cot doing what I do best, which is nothing, which is being no one, and I told myself this was strategy and not surrender.

Then the lights died all at once, and the door seal exhaled, and water came through the gap under the door like a rumor spreading.

Cold. Fast. Ankle-deep in four seconds, knee-deep in ten. I've spent half my life underwater; I know its moods. This wasn't flooding the way a hull floods—this was flooding the way a river floods, with intent, with memory. Somewhere below me the emergency discharge had opened the facility's veins to the headwater spring itself, and the spring was taking back every room they'd carved out of it.

I put my shoulder to the door. Sealed. Put my back against the wall opposite and kicked where the hinges weren't. Third kick, the frame gave—not because I'm strong but because sixty years of water vapor had been quietly eating the building from inside, patient as rust, patient as me.

The corridor was chaos in the specific flavor institutions achieve: red emergency lighting, sprinklers hissing uselessly at a flood, technicians running upstream—away from the water, toward the stairs—and guards shouting into radios that answered only with static. Nobody looked at the wet man in gray coveralls wading against the current. Fifteen years of practice at being invisible, and it turns out all I ever needed was a disaster.

Except one person looked. A technician, young, soaked, pressed against a junction box with her arms full of data drives. Our eyes met. She could have shouted. Instead she hugged the drives tighter and ran, and I understood she'd read the situation faster than any of her supervisors: whatever was happening, the water was on someone's side, and it wasn't Hale's.

I went down. Against instinct, against the crowd, down the stairwell into the rising dark, because Mara had gone down—or up, or somewhere—and because fifteen years of running had finally pointed somewhere, and it pointed at her.

* * *

**MARA**

The pump gallery on sublevel two was a cathedral of pipes, and the river was holding its service. Water thundered through the discharge channel in a brown-green rope thick as a truck, tearing past the walkways, and the whole floor hummed like a struck bell. I stood at the rail with both drives against my chest and felt something I hadn't felt since the night my team vanished: useful.

Then hands closed on my shoulders and I did the sensible thing, which was to drive my elbow backward into whoever it was.

"Ow," said Tamsin. "Good form."

"You're supposed to be in a cell."

"I flooded out." He was dripping, gray-faced, grinning like a man who'd just remembered he owned a face. Behind him the corridor lights flickered and died section by section, the dark walking toward us at the speed of the short circuit. "Tell me you got the archive."

"Twice." I held up the drives. "Tell me you know the way out."

"I know the way *down*. Everything else we improvise."

"That's not a plan, that's a biography."

"It's worked so far." He pulled me away from the rail as a pipe joint somewhere overhead let go with a bang and a fan of steam. We ran along the gallery, past the extraction lines—all those gleaming columns where the compound was pulled from the water, refined, concentrated, made ready to disappear into municipal reservoirs a thousand miles away—and Tamsin slowed, just for a second, looking at them with an expression I couldn't name until I realized I'd seen it before. On my own face, in the mirror, the morning after I signed the classification order. Recognition. The awful kind.

"Later," I said, and he nodded, and we ran.

* * *

**TAMSIN**

Here is what nobody tells you about spending fifteen years erasing yourself: you get very good at exits. Not dramatic ones—no window ledges, no motorcycle chases. Small ones. Service doors propped with coffee cups. Loading docks at shift change. The geography of buildings as seen by people who are never meant to be seen in them.

Hale's facility had been built by engineers who thought like engineers, which meant its escape routes were maintenance routes, which meant they were marked, because even secret installations obey the fire code. Sublevel three, past the drowned records annex where the water stood waist-deep and file cabinets floated past us like icebergs of other people's erased lives—we waded through them, Mara and I, hip-deep in the paper of the disappeared, and neither of us said a word about it, because there was nothing to say that the water wasn't already saying.

The maintenance tunnel ran east, upslope, toward the gorge side of the mountain. Emergency lighting, battery-backed, amber and steady. My legs knew the rhythm before my brain caught up: run, listen, run. And underneath the rhythm, underneath the alarms still chiming their polite apologies three levels up, another sound was growing.

The river.

Not the discharge channel—that was a controlled wound. This was the real thing, the spring itself, finding the new cracks the flood had opened, following them down through the mountain the way water always does, without negotiation. The installation had spent sixty years bending the source to its purposes. The source had apparently kept receipts.

"Mara." I stopped at the tunnel junction where the amber light met black. "How much time do you think this building has?"

She listened the way cartographers listen—with her whole body, triangulating. "If the lower galleries are already breached? Hours. Maybe less if the spring finds the old survey adits." She paused. "My team found adits. Old mine workings, pre-facility. I mapped them before—" Before her maps were rewritten overnight. Before she learned what her signatures were worth.

"Then the spring knows the shortcuts we don't," I said. "Which means we don't take the tunnel all the way. We go up and out through the extraction level, overland, down to the gorge."

"The gorge is crawling with patrols."

"The gorge is about to be crawling with something else." I tilted my head at the sound rising beneath our feet. "Patrol boats don't do well in flash floods."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then her face did the thing it does when she's converting fear into method, some internal recalculation I could nearly hear. "The drives stay dry," she said. "Whatever happens, the drives stay dry."

"Whatever happens," I agreed, "the drives stay dry."

And then we ran again, upward this time, two liars-turned-witnesses climbing through the guts of a dying machine, and I want to record this accurately, because accuracy is the whole point now: for the first time in fifteen years, I was not running *from* anything.

I was running *toward* the truth with copies of it zipped inside a cartographer's jacket, and the difference between those two kinds of running is the difference between drowning and swimming. I know. I've done both.

* * *

**MARA**

The extraction level was evacuating in orderly panic—Hale's people were good at procedure right up until procedure stopped applying. White-coated staff streamed toward the vehicle bay while klaxons finally abandoned politeness and started screaming. We moved against the flow, hugging the pipe runs, wearing the gray anonymity of maintenance coveralls Tamsin had liberated from a wall locker with a pry bar and zero remorse.

At the vehicle bay doors stood two guards checking badges. Badges we didn't have.

Tamsin looked at me. I looked at the fire suppression manifold above the doors—it was standard design, I'd surveyed enough government facilities to draw one blindfolded, back when surveying was my sin—and I looked at the manual release, and Tamsin followed my gaze and his eyebrows went up in genuine admiration.

"You want to trigger a deluge on a room full of electrical vehicles."

"I want to trigger a deluge alarm," I said. "The sprinklers will do the rest. Everyone clears the bay for thirty minutes minimum. Procedure."

"Fifteen years of hiding and you're the one who weaponizes paperwork."

"Sixty years of their paperwork built this place. Fair's fair."

He popped the manifold access panel while I counted guards' sightlines, and thirty seconds later the bay was shrieking, the sprinklers were raining, and two guards were herding forty scientists out the far doors with the fervent energy of men who believed the building was trying to kill them. Which, to be fair, it was.

We crossed the emptying bay through ankle-deep water—everything here was ankle-deep water now; the mountain had opinions—and slipped out the vehicle exit into night air that hit my lungs like a blessing. Rain, real rain, the natural kind. Above us the facility clung to the headwaters canyon, its windows lit frantic yellow, and below us the river roared through the gorge, higher than it had any right to be, brown with torn-up bank, carrying branches whole.

"Tamsin." I grabbed his sleeve. "Look at the water line."

He looked. Even in the dark I saw him understand. The river was already a meter above its banks and rising, and the gorge downstream—the gorge where the ferryman's scuttled barge still sat, where our patrol friends were moored—was about to become a very exclusive swimming lesson.

"We ride it?" he said.

"We ride it."

He grinned, and it was the first honest grin I'd seen on him, unguarded, fifteen years of careful blankness cracked open by something that wasn't grief. "You know," he said, "when I hired on to salvage a sealed case off a dead navy wreck, I was promised boring."

"You were promised money."

"I was promised *boring*, and honestly, the false advertising is the part I resent most."

We scrambled down the maintenance trail along the canyon wall, the facility's alarms fading behind us into one long note, the river's voice rising ahead of us into many. At the water's edge, wedged against a rock shelf, lay a piece of the ferryman's barge—hull planking, tarred oak, big enough for two and buoyant enough for a miracle. His last barge, giving us its last favor.

Tamsin shoved it free and held it steady against the current's pull. "Drives?"

"Dry." I patted my jacket. "Me, less so."

"Get on anyway." He pushed off, and the gorge took us.

The water was everything at once—violence and lift and a roar that filled my skull and erased every other sound, including, blessedly, my own doubt. We shot through standing waves, spun, righted, spun again. Tamsin steered with his hands and his weight and fifteen years of reading water like text, shouting directions I mostly felt rather than heard. Once we passed a patrol boat pinned sideways against the gorge wall, crew scrambling up the rocks, searchlight sweeping uselessly across spray. Once we passed the drowned mouth of the maintenance tunnel, water pouring INTO it now instead of out, the mountain drinking its own architecture back.

And once—just once, in a lull between rapids, under a sky going gray at the edges with the coming dawn—Tamsin looked back upstream at the shrinking, smoking silhouette of the installation and said, not to me, maybe to the river:

"Fifteen years. You could have mentioned the current runs this direction."

The river, being a river, declined to answer. But it carried us. Downstream, fast, toward the low country, toward towns and towers and newsrooms full of people who had never heard of Tamsin Nakamura or Mara Voss or Director Hale's quiet yellow valves.

Two drives against my chest. One story between us. And behind us, the sound of sixty years of secrets learning to swim.
