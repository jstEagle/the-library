## 19. The River Decides

The core went the way I'd always imagined my own death would go: quietly at first, then all at once, then with a sound like God clearing his throat.

Mara and I were already running when it happened. We'd made it up two levels of stairs, through a corridor that smelled of scorched insulation and river water, and we were maybe forty meters from the outer blast door when the extraction column let go. Not an explosion, exactly. More like the mountain deciding to exhale after holding its breath for sixty years.

The pressure wave caught us in the stairwell and threw us against the wall like two coats hung on hooks. My ears filled with a high white tone. Dust came down in curtains. Somewhere below us, pipes the diameter of subway tunnels were tearing loose from their housings, and the spring—the actual spring, the one they'd spent decades plumbing and capping and refining—was doing what springs do.

It was coming for us.

"Mara!" My voice sounded like someone else's, tinny and far away. "Stairs! Higher!"

She was already moving. That was the thing about Mara Voss that I'd learned over three hundred kilometers of river and one impossible gorge: she didn't panic, she *recalculated*. Her face in the emergency lighting had the same expression it wore over her maps—total concentration, total refusal to accept that the terrain had won.

We climbed. Behind us, water found the stairwell and started climbing too, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about destroying something evil: it doesn't feel like triumph. It feels like being inside the machine while it dies.

I'd spent fifteen years telling myself that if I ever got the chance to undo even a fraction of what I'd helped bury—if I could put one dent in the architecture of erasure—I'd feel clean. Redeemed. Some word like that. Words are cheap when you're swimming; you have a lot of time to spend them.

But standing in that stairwell with the facility screaming itself apart beneath us, I didn't feel clean. I felt *present*. Completely, unbearably present, in a way I hadn't been since I was twenty-six years old and still had a name on my driver's license. Every nerve I owned was lit. The dust tasted like copper and limestone. The water rising around our boots was cold enough to hurt, and I loved it, every freezing inch of it, because it was honest water. It wasn't refined. It wasn't dosed or metered or piped into anyone's municipal supply to make them docile. It was just the river, taking back its own house.

That's the thing Hale never understood. He talked about controlled water like it was controlled peace, like a sedated population was a gift he was wrapping for civilization. But water isn't a tool. It's a witness. It carries everything—silt, memory, compound, corpse—and it delivers all of it downstream eventually, no matter how many valves you install. You can dam a river but you can't make it forget which way is down.

The spring remembered. And now it was testifying.

* * *

We burst out through the blast door into gray morning light and stopped dead, both of us, because the headwaters valley had changed shape overnight.

The installation had been built into the hillside above the spring pool—a concrete face, loading docks, ventilation stacks disguised as rock outcrops. Half of that face was gone now. A wound of rebar and shattered stone gaped where the lower galleries had been, and from it poured a torrent that hadn't existed an hour ago, brown-white with pulverized concrete, slamming into the pool below and throwing spray thirty feet in the air. The whole valley floor trembled under our feet like a struck bell.

And the compound—it was everywhere, glittering in the mist. I knew what Mara was thinking because I was thinking it too: *all those years of dosage, all those secret refinements, and now the entire stockpile is going into open water at once.* Except. Except the math was on our side, and I'd done the math myself back in chapter seven of this disaster, testing trace compounds downstream with a kit I'd bought from a fishing supplier. Dilution. The river was the one thing bigger than the operation. The spring's own volume, multiplied by the gorge, by the tributaries, by the long brown miles to the sea—by the time any of it reached a town, it would be chemistry so faint it wouldn't calm a housefly. The facility had spent sixty years concentrating the water's secret. The river was spending it all in one morning, and spending it on nothing but noise.

"Downstream," Mara said. She was shouting over the roar. "If any of Hale's people survived, they'll seal the valley."

"If any of Hale's people survived," I said, "they're having a very bad day."

Below us, the access road along the pool's edge was already flooding. And floating in the churn, knocking against the rocks with a sound like lazy applause, was wreckage. Sections of pipe. A door off its hinges, big as a barn door, steel-faced. Part of a walkway grating bent into a rough raft shape. Sixty years of infrastructure, coming apart into pieces just about the right size for two people who needed to be somewhere else fast.

I looked at Mara. She looked at me.

"I've ridden worse," she said.

"You haven't," I said. "Nobody has. But I appreciate the spirit."

* * *

Choosing your wreckage is a skill. Fifteen years of salvage diving teaches you to read a floating object the way other people read faces—where it's strong, where it's lying to you, whether it will hold your weight or hold a grudge. I picked the walkway grating, lashed to a length of pipe with electrical cable we tore from a drowned junction box, because the grating let water through instead of trying to fight it. Fighting the river is how the river kills you. You want to be something it can move through, not something it has to argue with.

We shoved off from the rocks into current that immediately made the decision for us.

The first kilometer of the flooded gorge was less like boating and more like being swallowed slowly by an animal that respected us. Water stood in standing waves eight feet tall. The wreckage groaned and spun. Twice we hit submerged debris hard enough that I saw white, and both times the grating flexed, shed the impact through its open lattice, and kept going. I kept one hand on the cable lashings and one hand fisted in Mara's jacket, and between us we had exactly enough buoyancy and no more.

And here is the part I'll deny saying if anyone ever asks: I laughed.

Not a small laugh. A full-body, ugly, whooping laugh, torn sideways out of me by the wind and the spray, the kind of laugh I hadn't produced since before the erasure—since before I learned that joy leaves a record too, and records get people disappeared. For fifteen years I had been careful in my bones. Careful about names, careful about addresses, careful about letting anyone see me be glad about anything, because gladness is a fingerprint. And now I was riding a piece of a secret prison down a flood of its own making, soaked to the soul, half-deaf, holding onto the only person alive who knew the whole truth about me, and I was laughing like a man at his own funeral who's just read the will and likes it.

Mara looked over at me—at me laughing, this man she'd met at knifepoint, this careful ghost—and something cracked in her face too. She started laughing back. Neither of us could hear the other. It didn't matter. Some conversations don't need audio.

* * *

The gorge narrowed. Of course it did. Gorges are contractually obligated to.

The walls closed in until they blotted out most of the sky, and the flood compressed between them into something with opinions. The wreckage began to surf. I've surfed wreckage before—in the tidal bore behind the old naval yards, once, stupidly, young—but this was different. This was a river that had been held underground and throttled for six decades, and it was running its first free rapids in living memory, and it wanted to show off.

We went through a hole I will not be able to describe to anyone without lying at least a little. The bow of our grating-raft dug into a wave the size of a chapel, and for one long, luminous second we were airborne—not falling, *flying*, spray sheeting off the grating in silver wings, the gorge walls streaking past on either side—and then the world turned over twice and we were underwater.

Underwater. Where I live.

People think drowning is panic. Real drowning—the professional kind—is quiet. The flood had me tumbling in brown turbulence, no up, no down, my lungs already filing their complaint, and some ancient part of my brain that predated the erasure, that predated everything, simply took over. Relax. Count. Let the river sort you. Debris floats, bodies float, everything finds the surface eventually if you stop fighting the sorting. I counted to eleven and broke into air and daylight, coughing half the gorge out of my lungs, and grabbed the grating as it wallowed past, and there was Mara already on it, hauling me aboard with both hands and language that would have gotten her fined in three jurisdictions.

"You," she gasped, "are the worst," she gasped, "salvage diver," she gasped, "in recorded history."

"I'm the best," I said, "you just don't like the genre."

* * *

By afternoon the flood had spread itself thin and reasonable, and the gorge opened into country I recognized from the wrong direction—from below, from maps, from rumor. The barge channel. The ferryman's water. Somewhere ahead, around the next bend or the one after, was the shallows where his last barge had died against a patrol hull, where we'd lost most of our evidence and nearly ourselves. The river was carrying us back through our own story, in reverse, at speed.

The wreckage rode easier now. We lay on the grating side by side, wrung out, watching the walls slide past. The light came down into the gorge at a slant and turned the water the color of hammered brass.

"Do you feel different?" Mara asked. She said it carefully, the way she asked map questions. Wanting the real answer, not the comfortable one.

I thought about it honestly, which is not something I'd had the luxury of doing in fifteen years.

"I feel like I exist," I said. "Not as a file somewhere. Not as a blank space on a chart. Just—" I flexed my hand, watched the water run off my knuckles. "Present tense. I haven't been present tense since I was twenty-six."

"They'll come for us," she said. Not a question. "Hale's network, whatever's left of it. The government. Everyone whose job it was for that valley to stay blank."

"I know."

"And you're not going to run."

It wasn't a question either. Three hundred kilometers ago it would have been. But she'd seen me choose the core over the exit. She'd watched me stand in front of sixty years of hidden machinery and pick up a wrench.

"No," I said. "I'm done measuring my life in miles logged. From here on, I measure in truths told." I looked at her. "Starting with all of them. Every page you copied. Twice."

A slow grin moved across her exhausted face. "Twice," she agreed. "Redundancy is a cartographer's virtue."

The river carried us on. Behind us, faint now, almost tender, came the sound of the facility finishing its collapse—a long rolling thunder, like a door closing at the bottom of the world. Ahead, the gorge widened toward the lowlands, toward towns, toward newsrooms, toward the law waiting at the mouth of the water with its cuffs and its questions.

Let them wait. The river was ahead of them. The river had always been ahead of them.

It was carrying everything downstream now—every secret, every name, every page of the archive Mara had copied twice—and it would deliver all of it, the way it delivered everything, to the sea.

All we had to do was arrive first, and start talking.
