## 1. The Thief in the Dry Country

The depot at Salt Ladder had been hit sometime between the second watch and dawn, and whoever had done it was good.

Cyrus Quintana crouched at the edge of the loading yard and read the ground the way other people read a page. The fence cut was clean—bolt cutters, one pass, no hesitation—and the padlock had been rehung on its hasp afterward, which told her the thief wasn't running scared. He'd taken his time. He'd wanted the breach to look like nothing until someone actually counted stock.

Someone had. The depot manager, a soft-handed man named Ferrow who smelled of ink and anxiety, had counted twice before calling it in, and by then the thief had eleven hours on her.

"You'll want the road," Ferrow said, wringing his cap. "Road's fastest to the crossing towns. He'll take the road."

"He won't." Cyrus stood, brushing grit from her knee. "Anyone who takes the road out here is asking to be seen by every relay between here and the Verge. He went east."

"East is nothing. East is the dead country."

"That's why he went there."

She let him argue with himself about it while she checked her kit: four liters of water, water purification tabs, the long rifle she'd owned longer than her current name, a tarp, salt tablets, a week of pressed ration bars that tasted like compressed regret. Ferrow watched her pack with the expression of a man who had never once slept outside and suspected everyone who did was slightly mad.

"The service will want him brought to the magistrate at Corren," he said.

"The service wants a lot of things." She settled the pack straps across her shoulders, adjusting until the weight sat right, until it disappeared. "I'll bring back what he stole."

"And him?"

Cyrus looked at the eastern horizon, where the land lifted into low red mesas and then gave up entirely, flattening into the dry washes of the old river country. Nothing out there but heat-shimmer and bones and the kind of silence you could drink.

"If I can," she said, which was as much of a promise as she intended to make.

* * *

The trail was beautiful, if you knew how to look.

Most people wouldn't have found it at all. The thief had done everything right—walked on stone where he could, kept off the skyline, moved through the cool hours and holed up in the heat. But nobody walked the dead country without leaving something, because the dead country remembered. It held footprints in the clay pans for months after rain. It caught threads of cloth on the saltbush and kept them. It collected what people dropped and filed it away under silt and sun-bleach, patient as any archivist.

Cyrus had spent ten years learning to read that archive, and ten years making sure none of it ever recorded *her*.

She picked up his sign two miles past the last windmill, where he'd crossed a clay pan in the gray hour before dawn. His stride was long but not hurried—a man covering distance, not fleeing. Boot prints, worn heels, left foot landing heavier. Maybe forty kilos of gear on his back, maybe more; the prints were deep and the spacing stayed even anyway, which meant strength or desperation, and usually both.

He'd stopped once at a stand of ironwood and built no fire, but the ash of a small one from the night before was still warm when she pressed her fingers into it. Warm meant hours, not days. She was gaining.

Good. She wanted this over clean. In and out, recover the stock, file a report under a name that existed only on paper, and return to the station cabin at the end of the world where nobody asked her anything except whether the water tanks needed checking.

That was the whole architecture of her life now, and she loved it the way you love a roof over your head in a storm: completely, and without ever quite forgetting what it cost.

She ate a ration bar walking and drank sparingly, and the country opened around her like a hand uncurling.

* * *

By late afternoon the mesas fell behind and the land became what the old survey maps called the Sandwash Reach—the bed of a river that had died before Cyrus was born, or so the official record claimed. A great pale scar of it, braided channels splitting and merging like veins in a leaf, edged with tamarisk and salt-killed earth. In the wet years it ran ankle-deep with snowmelt from mountains nobody visited. In the dry years it was a highway for anyone who didn't want to be followed.

Which was exactly why the thief had come to it, and exactly why Cyrus wasn't surprised.

What surprised her was how well he traveled it.

She found his camp at dusk in the lee of a collapsed bank—not a fire ring, not a cleared space, just a body-shaped hollow in the sand and the faint mineral smell of a man who'd been careful even about sweat. He'd buried his waste. He'd shaken out his bedding downwind. He'd eaten sitting on a flat rock so the sand wouldn't hold the impression of his knees.

And he'd gone upstream.

Cyrus stood in the middle of the dead riverbed as the light went copper and then violet, and felt the particular satisfaction she never got anywhere else settle over her like a well-fitted coat. This was the transaction she'd made with the desert, the only honest one she'd ever struck: it asked nothing of her but survival, and she asked nothing of it but room. No neighbors. No colleagues remembering her old name. No partner's ghost sitting across the fire.

Out here, a person could be reduced to essentials—water, shade, distance, judgment—and those essentials were enough. They had been enough for ten years.

She made cold camp herself, a mile short of his, and lay watching stars come out in their brutal multitudes while she planned tomorrow. Upstream, following the channel. He'd have to cross the narrows at Redgate Cut within a day, and after that the terrain would squeeze him against the canyon walls where there was nowhere left to be clever.

Sleep came easy. It always did out here. It was only in towns, under roofs, near records and registries and the soft hum of databases, that she dreamed.

* * *

Dawn found her moving again, and midmorning found her confused.

His trail did something trails weren't supposed to do. Instead of continuing up the main channel toward Redgate, it turned hard south into a side wash—one of the dead river's ancient distributaries, a slot of red sandstone barely wide enough for sky. And there, at the mouth of it, the thief had done something deliberate: he'd dragged a dead tamarisk branch across his own tracks, backward, sweeping them into a smear.

It was a decent false trail. It would have fooled a green ranger, maybe even a mediocre experienced one. It would not have fooled her, because the smear was too neat. Guilty men overdid it. And because twenty meters further on, on a shelf of bare sandstone that would hold nothing, she found the thing he couldn't sweep away: a single drop of wax, bluish-white, still tacky at the edges. Candle wax. Nobody carried candles in the desert unless they needed light they could shield—light for reading, or writing, or looking closely at something small.

She crouched over the wax drop for a long moment, turning possibilities over.

A smuggler would have run the main channel. A prospector would have run the main channel. Even a fugitive with sense would have run the main channel, because the side washes south of the Sandwash led precisely nowhere—to box canyons, to salt flats, to the drowned ruins of country that had been officially deleted from every map forty years ago and patrolled by nobody since.

Deleted country attracted a certain kind of person. Cyrus knew, because she had spent a decade becoming that kind herself.

She turned south into the slot canyon, and the walls closed around her, and the temperature dropped ten degrees as if the stone had swallowed the sun.

* * *

The side wash twisted for most of the day, and the thief's trail grew strange.

He stopped hiding. That was the first oddity. After the swept tracks and the candle wax, his sign simply continued—boot prints plain in the sand, a handprint on a boulder where he'd climbed, a scuffed slide down a gravel chute. Either he'd given up, or he'd decided that whatever lay ahead mattered more than being followed.

The second oddity was the marks on the walls.

At first she took them for flood debris—old high-water lines, driftwood scars. Then she noticed they repeated. Small carved glyphs, waist-high, spaced along the canyon like punctuation: a spiral split by a vertical line. Three horizontal strokes over a chevron. A circle with a tail, like a fish seen from above. They were old—the stone around them had weathered smooth—but some had been recut recently, fresh chisel-bright edges inside the older grooves, as though someone maintained them. As though someone still used them.

Cyrus had worked this district for six years under three different names, and she had never seen these marks in any survey, any archive, any ranger briefing. Which meant either they'd been missed—which was impossible, because rangers catalogued petroglyphs the way bankers catalogued money—or they'd been deliberately left out.

Deliberately left out of records. Her area of expertise.

She touched one of the fresh-cut spirals and felt a faint, unwelcome thrill move through her, the feeling of a door she'd thought painted shut swinging open an inch. She shut it again. Whatever this was—smuggler's code, cultist nonsense, the hobby of some dead eccentric—it wasn't hers. She had a thief to catch, stock to recover, a report to file under a name that would evaporate next season like everything else about her.

But she noted the direction the marks trended, all the same. South-southwest. Deeper into the deleted country.

Toward the source of the dead river, if the old pre-drowning maps she'd once glimpsed in a restricted drawer could be believed. Not that she'd ever had reason to go there. Not that anyone had, in forty years.

* * *

Near sundown the wash opened into a bowl ringed by broken cliffs, and she saw his fire.

Small, shielded in a rock alcove, burning dry tamarisk that smoked hardly at all—he knew what he was doing. He sat beside it with his back to her, a narrow figure in a patched coat, hunched over something in his lap. Forty meters of open sand between them, no cover, no wind to cover sound. If she rushed him now he might run, and a man who traveled like this one did could outrun her through broken country for days.

So instead she did what the desert had taught her, which was to be patient, and quiet, and absolutely certain.

She circled wide through the cliff shadow, closing the arc while he fed his little fire, and came down behind the alcove with her boots placed like thoughts. She had the rifle slung and the short baton out—she wanted him talking, not bleeding—and she was five steps away, close enough to hear him murmuring to himself in a language she didn't recognize, liquid and rhythmic, like water over stones—

"Stand up slowly," she said. "Hands where I can see them."

He froze. Then, to her lasting irritation, he laughed—a short, cracked sound, half relief and half despair—and said, without turning around:

"I wondered when you'd catch up. You're better than the last ones."

"Last ones?"

"The ones the company sends." He turned then, and the firelight showed her a young face, early twenties, sun-scoured, with dark eyes that held the specific exhausted steadiness of someone who had been afraid for a very long time and had gotten used to living there. His hands came up, empty. "You're not company. Company wears boots that squeak."

"Depot at Salt Ladder," Cyrus said. "Eleven days ago. You took supplies and something else. Stand up."

He stood, slowly, and she saw the pack at his feet—military-frame, overstuffed, and beneath the food and water she could make out the shape of something rigid and rectangular, wrapped in oilcloth.

"What did you steal?" she asked.

"Nothing." His eyes flicked to the canyon walls, to the carved spiral glowing faintly in the last light, and back to her. Something in his face shifted—the calculation of a man deciding how much truth a stranger could carry. "I took back," he said. "There's a difference. Ask your own records, Ranger. If you can find them."

The words landed harder than he could have known. Cyrus kept her voice level and her baton steady and did not think about drawers full of files that did not contain her.

"Turn around. Hands on the rock."

He obeyed. And as she stepped forward to bind his wrists, the last of the daylight slid off the canyon rim, and the firelight caught the oilcloth bundle where the flap had worked loose—and showed her, stamped deep into aged bronze, a seal she had never seen and recognized instantly anyway, because some images survive even the burning of every book that described them.

A river, coiled into a knot. A crown above it, upside down. Drowned.

The seal of Vessia.

Behind her, far to the north, so faint it might have been thunder, something heavy passed overhead in the darkening sky—and the young man went rigid under her hands, whispering a word in that liquid language, a word she would hear many times in the weeks to come:

"They've found the trail."
