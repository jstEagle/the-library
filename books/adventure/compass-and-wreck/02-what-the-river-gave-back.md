## 2. What the River Gave Back

The trail went cold at noon and came back to life an hour later, which told Cyrus two things: her thief was smart enough to walk a ridgeline in full sun where his prints would bake shut behind him, and dumb enough to drop down into shade when the heat started eating him alive. Everyone broke eventually. The desert was patient that way.

She followed him into the broken country east of the depot, where the mesas came apart into fins and hoodoos and the ground turned to something like shattered pottery. His tracks were faint here — a scuff on a sandstone lip, a displaced pebble with damp earth still showing underneath — but they were there, and they were heading downhill, and downhill meant water.

Cyrus drank from her own canteen without stopping, measured what was left against the distance she'd come, and kept moving. This was the part of the work she loved, though *loved* was too warm a word for it. It was more that this part of the work asked nothing of her. No name, no history, no explanation. The desert didn't care who Cyrus Quintana had been before she'd spent three years and a great deal of forged paperwork becoming nobody. Out here she was just a shape moving through heat shimmer, reading the ground the way other people read letters from home.

She'd been a ranger for eleven years and a ghost for ten of them. The ranger service thought Quintana was a transfer from a district office that had burned its records in an electrical fire; the district office, such as it existed on paper, thought she was seconded elsewhere. She paid her taxes through a shell address in a town that had mostly emptied out. She filed her reports late, in flat language, about nothing interesting. She had built herself a life with no load-bearing walls, and every year she checked the structure, and every year it held.

A thief in the dry country was a problem, but a small one. A man stealing medical stores and water filters from a supply depot was either desperate or selling, and either way he'd leave a trail a child could follow once you knew what to look for. She'd find him by tomorrow night, take back what could be taken back, and decide whether the courts needed to meet him or whether the desert had already punished him sufficiently. That was the whole shape of it. Simple. Contained.

Except the trail didn't behave.

* * *

Near dusk the fins gave way to a broad wash, and the wash became a riverbed — a real one, not one of the dead channels that stitched the low desert. This was the Serrana's upper reach, or what passed for a river in a country that had forgotten how to have them: a wide trough of pale gravel and silt, bone-dry now, but carrying water three months of the year when the high country snowmelt came down. In monsoon season it ran brown and violent enough to move boulders. Right now it was a road of cracked mud printed with the tracks of everything that had crossed it since spring.

Her thief's prints were among them, and they were wrong.

He hadn't walked the riverbed the way a fleeing man walks — long strides, straight lines, eyes on the horizon. He'd wandered. Doubled back. Stopped at the bank and stood there long enough to leave heel-dug divots, the stance of someone staring at something. Then his trail left the channel entirely, climbed the far cutbank, and ended at a stand of tamarisk where the ground was soft and private.

Cyrus found his camp in the last light.

It was a good camp, better than she'd expected from a depot thief. He'd tucked himself under the tamarisk canopy where the smoke would filter through the branches and disperse. There was a fire ring made with actual care, stones fitted so the heat would reflect inward. A bedroll. An empty tin of depot rations, licked clean. And beside the bedroll, pressed flat into the sand as if he'd slept with his hand on it all night, a piece of paper gone soft with sweat.

She picked it up with two fingers. It was a rubbing — charcoal over paper, taken from some carved surface. She'd seen grave-rubbings done that way, and property-marker rubbings, and once, memorably, a rubbing of an obscene graffito inside a survey tower that had cost a junior ranger a week's pay. This one showed a design: a circle, and inside the circle a stylized wave curling over what might have been a bell, or a drowned tower, or both. Around the rim, characters in an alphabet she didn't recognize — angular, water-worn-looking even in charcoal, letters that seemed designed to be read half-eroded.

She knew the shape of it anyway. Everyone out here knew the shape of it, the way everyone knew the shape of a skull. You weren't taught it. You absorbed it from the places it wasn't supposed to be anymore — a stamped beam in a salvage yard, a tile in a creek bed, the way old-timers would go quiet and change the subject if you asked about certain valleys upriver.

Vessia.

Forty years ago, before Cyrus was born, there had been a nation in these mountains. Not a big one — a river nation, a string of terraced valleys and stone towns along the Serrana's headwaters, famous for nothing much except bells and bridges and an stubborn genius for living with water. Then the dam went in downstream. Not their dam. A corporate water project, licensed by a government that had decided the Vessians' water rights were an inconvenience, and when the reservoir filled, the valleys filled, and forty thousand people were moved out or moved under. The compensation was scandalous even by the standards of the time. The erasure afterward was thorough. Maps were redrawn. Records were sealed. The name stopped appearing in schoolbooks, then in archives, then anywhere at all except in the mouths of people who'd promised not to say it.

Officially, Vessia had never existed. Unofficially, it existed everywhere — in the salvage trade, in the rumors of valley people who never took the buyout, in the fact that every few years somebody found a bronze seal or a chest of documents and the survey companies descended on the site like ants on a dropped sweet, and whatever they found was never seen again.

Cyrus turned the rubbing over. On the back, in pencil, in a young man's hurried hand, were numbers. Elevations. Distances. A note in the same alphabet, and beneath it, in careful Common script, as if copied out phonetically by someone who'd learned the words before he'd learned their spelling:

*Before the water closes. Before the passes close. Before.*

She stood there in the failing light holding a dead nation in her hand, and the hair rose on her forearms despite the heat.

Because this changed things. A thief running with stolen filters was a Tuesday. A thief running with Vessian material was a different animal entirely. The survey companies paid — she'd heard the figures, always in the tone people used for obscene jokes — for authenticated Vessian artifacts, and they paid considerably more for the ones that came with documents attached. Documents proved claims. Claims were dangerous. There were lawyers in the lowlands who had built careers on making sure no Vessian claim ever got standing, and there were men with rifles who handled the cases lawyers couldn't reach.

And her thief had been sitting in this riverbed, sleeping next to a rubbing of the seal, writing *before* like a prayer.

Cyrus put the paper in her pack, looked at the cold fire ring, and did the thing she'd trained herself to do for ten years: she considered walking away. Follow the thief tomorrow, recover the depot goods, file a report that mentioned nothing but theft. Let the Vessian business belong to whoever wanted it. Ghosts were not her jurisdiction. Her whole life was arranged around the principle that the past stays buried, hers included.

But the thief had doubled back toward the river. Fleeing men don't return to the scene unless they've left something behind that matters more than freedom. Which meant the thing was still here, or had been hidden here, and if she walked away she'd spend the rest of her invisible life wondering what a dying nation had left wedged in the mud of a seasonal river.

Curiosity, she reflected, was the one vice she'd never managed to forge paperwork against.

She waited for full dark, then worked the riverbed by lantern, low-beamed, moving upstream from the camp in slow transects. The silt near the cutbank had been disturbed — not recently, but recently enough that the crack-pattern over the dig marks hadn't fully healed. Someone had dug here and then, cleverly, had let the wind start erasing the evidence before abandoning the spot.

She dug with her hands. Two feet down, fingers struck wood.

* * *

It took her an hour to free it, and by the end she was sweating in air gone cold, kneeling in a hole in the bottom of a dead river like a gravedigger working a shift she hadn't applied for.

The chest was iron-banded oak, small — the kind of thing one person could carry at need — and it was wrapped in waxed canvas that had kept the worst of the wet season out. But it was the stamp that stopped her hands.

Bronze, set into the lid, green with age: the circle, the wave over the bell, the angular characters ringing the rim. Not worn. Not salvaged. Stamped with authority, by someone whose job it had been to stamp official things, in a country that officially did not exist.

Cyrus knelt in the riverbed and felt the unease move through her like cold water through gravel. This was what she'd been circling all evening, the thing she couldn't name: the past didn't stay buried out here. It surfaced. It surfaced like a body coming up out of a reservoir when the drought dropped the waterline — bloated, undeniable, wearing a face everyone pretended not to recognize. Forty years of maps and seals and burned records, and the river had simply held all of it in its silt, waiting, patient as only moving water is patient, until someone came looking.

She should close the chest. She should rebury it, mark the spot, and report nothing, because reporting it meant the survey companies, and the survey companies meant men with rifles, and men with rifles meant questions, and questions were the one thing her life could not survive.

She opened it anyway. Of course she opened it. She was who she was.

Inside, packed in wool gone gray with age: documents, dozens of them, oilcloth-wrapped bundles tied with cord — land grants, she guessed, or water rights, the paper skeleton of a drowned state. A ledger with entries in that angular alphabet. And a ring, heavy gold, set with a seal matrix that matched the bronze stamp exactly, resting in a depression in the wool as if it had been placed there by careful hands for careful reasons.

And beneath the ledger, wrapped separately, a brass compass.

Old-fashioned, heavy, hinged case gone dark with handling. She flipped it open out of pure reflex, ranger habit, checking her bearings even here, even now, in a hole in a riverbed with a dead nation in her lap.

The needle swung. Settled. Swung again, trembling, and pointed — steadily, insistently, ignoring the magnetic north her training said it must find — upstream. Toward the river's source. Toward the high country where the maps went blank and the passes closed with the first snow.

Toward the place the rubbing had said *before*.

Cyrus sat very still. Somewhere above the cutbank, a coyote began its evening argument with the dark, and the sound was ordinary, and the compass in her hand was not. She thought: I am going to close this box, bury it deeper, and forget the direction it points. She thought: I have spent ten years learning to forget directions.

Then she heard the gravel shift on the cutbank above her — one soft, involuntary step, the step of someone who has crept back to reclaim what he buried and found a lantern burning in the hole instead.

Cyrus closed the compass, laid her hand on the chest, and said quietly, into the dark, "You can come down, or you can run. But if you run, know that I'm faster than the river is."

Silence. Then, resigned, the sound of a young man sliding down the bank into the moonlight, coming to surrender himself to the woman holding his country's heart in a hole in the ground.
