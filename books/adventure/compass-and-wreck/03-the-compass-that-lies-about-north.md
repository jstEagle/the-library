## 3. The Compass That Lies About North

The chest came out of the silt harder than it went in.

Cyrus worked it loose with the flat of her entrenching tool, kneeling in the wet margin of the sandwash where the thief's camp had been, taking her time, because things buried in rivers were buried for reasons and she had learned — a decade ago, in another life, with someone else's blood on her hands — that reasons deserved respect. The bronze stamping resisted the mud at first, then gave all at once, and the chest swung up into the light trailing black water like something surfacing to breathe.

She set it on a flat stone above the flood line and sat back on her heels and looked at it for a long time.

It was not loot. That was the first thing she understood, and it bothered her more than the theft had. Loot was electronics, ammunition, medical stock, the small portable wealth of the frontier. This was a sea chest, an immigrant's chest, the kind families carried when they expected never to go back. Dovetailed cedar under plates of green-patinaed bronze, corner fittings worn bright by hands, a hasp broken open recently and crudely — the thief's work — but the rest of it old, deliberate, cared for. Someone had oiled this chest within the last year. Someone had carried it a very long way.

And stamped into the lid, centered, deep enough to catch shadow even in flat light: a seal.

Cyrus knew seals. Part of a ranger's job was knowing them — grazing cooperatives, mining claims, water districts, the little heraldries of a country that had been carved up and sold off so many times that every rock had three owners. She knew them the way she knew tracks, and this one she did not know, which meant it was either fake or forbidden.

It showed a river. A river rendered as a single unbroken line, running through a gate, and around the gate a border of script too fine and too corroded to read. Below it, one word in an alphabet she half-recognized from old survey plaques in canyon country, the kind you weren't supposed to photograph:

*Vessia.*

She said the word aloud, once, just to hear how stupid it sounded in the empty wash.

There was no Vessia. There had been no Vessia for forty years, and before that there had barely been one at all — a river nation, a valley state, drowned on purpose when the great dams went in upstream, its towns flooded, its people relocated under emergency resettlement acts that Cyrus had read excerpts of during her certification and never once heard mentioned since. It wasn't a suppressed history exactly. It was worse than that. It was a *tidied* one. The maps didn't show ruins where Vessia had been; they showed nothing. The reservoir had been drawn as open water for twenty years, and then the water had failed, and the maps had simply kept saying water long after the riverbeds went dry, as if the cartographers had agreed among themselves to stop looking.

Officially erased from all records. She of all people understood what that cost, and what it took, and what kind of person survived it.

The wind moved down the wash, carrying the smell of wet silt and creosote. Somewhere upstream a raven said something ugly to the canyon walls.

Cyrus looked over her shoulder — habit, thoroughness, the reflexes of a woman who had spent ten years making sure no record of her existed anywhere but in her own head — and then she opened the chest.

* * *

The documents were wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth had done its work.

She lifted them out in bundles and laid them on the stone with the care of an evidence tech, though she was not on duty, had not technically been on duty in years, and could not call in what she'd found without explaining why a ranger named Cyrus Quintana had been anywhere near this wash at all. Paper first: a census ledger, water-stained but legible, columns of names in that same fine script. Land grants. A folded map so old it had gone soft as cloth, hand-inked, showing the river system in detail no government survey had ever matched — every side canyon named, every spring marked, every bend annotated with symbols she couldn't read but could tell were *instructions*. Warnings, maybe. Or invitations.

Under the paper, a ring.

Heavy gold would have been too obvious; this was darker, an alloy gone almost black with age, set with a signet face bearing the same river-through-the-gate. It was sized for a large man's hand. It had been worn smooth on the inner band by generations of wearing, and then it had been wrapped in a scrap of linen and hidden beneath the census like something too dangerous to look at and too important to lose.

Cyrus held it in her palm and felt its weight and thought: *this is not a thing you steal. This is a thing you carry.*

Which raised the question of what the thief actually was.

At the bottom of the chest, nested in rotted wool, was the last item, and it was the one that made her forget, briefly, to check the light.

A compass. Brass cased, hinged lid, glass intact, the maker's name worn to ghosts. It looked ordinary. It looked like ten thousand compasses rattling around the frontier in ranger packs and survey kits and dead men's pockets. She opened it out of completeness, because she was cataloguing, because thoroughness was the only religion she still practiced.

The needle pointed southwest.

She frowned. The wash ran northeast to southwest; she'd been following it downstream since dawn, and downstream here meant south-southwest toward the seasonal flats. But the needle wasn't pointing along the wash. It was pointing *upstream* — hard upstream, into the high country, off at an angle from true north by a good forty degrees, steady as if nailed there.

She turned. The needle turned with her, patient, indifferent, holding its bearing toward the mountains.

"Okay," she murmured.

She walked thirty paces east, watching it. It corrected without waver, swinging back to the same line like a dog returning to a scent. She walked west. Same. She held a knife against the case, checking for ferrous trickery, and got nothing. She tilted it, spun it, covered it with her hand and let it settle blind. Every time, the needle came back to that one bearing: upstream, into the source country, into the closed highlands where the maps said *water* and the water had died.

Compasses pointed north. That was the whole contract. You could buy ones that lied about declination, ones ruined by magnetite, ones broken by a drop on granite. She had never held one that lied about north *on purpose*, with this kind of calm, this kind of insistence — pointing not at a pole but at a place, the way a finger points.

Toward the river's source. Toward whatever was left at the top of a drowned nation's map.

Cyrus sat down on the stone beside the opened chest and did the arithmetic she hated doing, the arithmetic that always ended with her walking away. Walk away, report nothing, let the company or the service or whoever owned this grief come collect it. That was the smart play. That was the play she had executed flawlessly for ten years: see everything, touch nothing, be no one. The landscape asked nothing of her but survival, and she had built her whole life inside that bargain, and the bargain worked, and she was good at it, and she was —

— still sitting there. Turning the compass in her hands. Watching the needle hold its impossible line with the stubbornness of a living thing.

The wind came up the wash again, from the direction the needle wanted, carrying cold air off the high country, and Cyrus thought about a census ledger full of names, and a ring worn smooth by hands that were all dead or scattered or pretending, and a young man who had broken into a supply depot and taken — what? Bandages, according to the depot manifest. Bandages, salt, and a water filter, and left everything else untouched, including a cash box sitting in the open.

Nobody stole bandages and a family's drowned homeland in the same night.

She was still turning it over when the gravel spoke behind her.

Not a sound, exactly — a wrongness. Ten years of reading ground will teach you the difference between a wash settling and a wash being stepped in, and this was the second thing. She didn't turn. She let her hand drift to the rifle across her knees and said, to the riverbed in general, "You're standing in my sightline, and I've been waiting for you since noon."

Silence. Then, defeated, wretched: "Please don't shoot."

He came out from behind the fallen slab with his hands up, and he was younger than she'd guessed from the trail — nineteen, twenty, all elbows and hunger, dark-haired, sunburned, wearing a depot jacket two sizes too big with the name tape torn off. He'd doubled back, of course. They always doubled back. She'd known he would; the camp had been abandoned too neatly, the fire too well buried, a boy who wanted his trail found *cold* rather than hot, which meant he planned to return. You don't bury a fire that carefully unless you're coming back to it.

"You're the thief," she said.

"I'm not—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I borrowed."

"You broke a depot lock and removed inventory."

"Yes." His eyes went past her, to the stone, to the open chest, and she watched something break open in his face — fear first, then a naked, unguarded relief so total it frightened her more than a weapon would have. "You found it. You — it's okay. It's okay, you found it." He took a step forward and she brought the rifle level and he froze, hands rising again, but his eyes never left the chest. "Please. I'll take whatever punishment. Just don't let anyone else have it. Please."

"Sit down."

He sat. On the silt, in the wet, immediately, like a boy who'd been ordered around by professionals his whole short life. Up close she could see the exhaustion on him, the cracked lips, the way his weight listed like a man who hadn't eaten properly in days. And she could see, now that she looked, other things. The way he'd instinctively knelt facing the chest. The way his fingers moved, absently, in a small repeated gesture against his knee — tracing a shape, over and over. A line through a gate.

Vessian river-sign, she'd have called it, if she'd known the term yet. She didn't. Not yet.

"Name," she said.

A pause. She watched him decide whether lying was worth it, watched him arrive at the truth the way you arrive at deep water — reluctantly, all at once.

"Ede," he said. "Ede Vaskarian."

The surname meant nothing to her. The *form* of it, the shape, belonged to the same country as the script on the map.

"Ede Vaskarian," she repeated. "That a Vessian name?"

His head came up sharply. For a moment the exhaustion burned off and she saw what was underneath, which was iron. "You know about Vessia?"

"I know there's no such place."

"There is," he said, simply, with the terrible directness of the young. "It's just underwater. Most of it." He looked at the compass in her hand, and his voice dropped. "Where did you find the chest?"

"Wedged in the silt. Half a mile downwash. Your camp was sloppy."

"It wasn't my camp. It was my grandmother's route. She marked the caches before I was born." He said it without self-pity, a fact laid flat, and Cyrus felt the unease in her chest deepen by a degree. A dead woman's route. A dead nation's road. A boy walking it alone with bandages and salt.

"And the compass," she said. "It's broken. Points upstream."

Ede went very still.

Then he laughed — one breath of it, startled out of him, half hysteria and half something else, something like hope — and said, "It's not broken. It's the only honest compass in the world. It doesn't point north. It points home."

"The north star isn't home."

"For your people, maybe." He was looking at the needle now, at its steady insistence on the source country, and his voice had gone quiet and certain in a way that made her want to argue just to break it. "For mine, home is upstream. Always was. Every Vessian compass ever made points to the source. That's how you find each other. That's how you've *always* found each other." He swallowed. "It's how I'm going to find the others. Before the monsoon season closes the high passes. There are people up there, ranger. People who never left. And I'm late."

Cyrus looked at him for a long moment.

Everything in her training said the boy was delusional or conning her, and probably both. Everything in her decade of practiced invisibility said: stand up, close the chest, walk south, be no one. The landscape asks nothing of you. Keep it that way.

But the needle lay against its glass pointing upstream, patient as a pulse, and the census ledger lay open on the stone with its hundreds of names, and Ede Vaskarian knelt in the mud of a dead river looking at her with the expression of a man who had finally, after a very long walk, been found.

"How many days to the source?" she heard herself ask.

His whole face changed. "Four. Five if the storms come early."

"And the monsoon?"

"Seals the high country for a year. Maybe longer, the way the climate's gone." He hesitated, then said, carefully, testing her: "You could come. If you wanted. The compass—" He stopped himself, embarrassed. "No. Forget it. You're a ranger. You have to arrest me or whatever."

"I'm not a ranger," Cyrus said, before she could stop herself, and then cursed inwardly at the ease of the lie, at how fast it came, at what it revealed about which life was closer to the surface these days.

Ede blinked. "Then what are you?"

It was a fair question, and she had no answer she'd ever spoken aloud, and she was saved from needing one by the sky.

It came as a sound first — a thin insectile whine, far off, riding the wind down the wash. Cyrus was on her feet before she'd decided to move, rifle up, scanning the northern haze, and there they were: two specks against the pale blue, high and slow, moving in the deliberate grid pattern that no bird flies and no casual aircraft bothers with. Survey drones. Company birds, running a search pattern, and the center of that pattern was drifting — unhurried, inevitable — toward the sandwash.

Toward them.

Ede saw her face and scrambled up. "What is it? What's—"

"Down." She kicked the chest shut, swept the documents into her pack in two brutal armfuls — sorry, ancestors — and shoved him toward the fallen slab and the deep undercut beneath it where the floodwater had hollowed the bank. "In. Now. Don't breathe loud."

They folded themselves into the dark together, shoulder to shoulder in the cold wet shadow, and the whine grew, and grew, and passed almost directly overhead — so close she could hear the servo tick of the gimbal as the camera pod traversed, slow as a lighthouse beam, across the sandwash below. Across the flat stone. Across the churned silt where a chest had lain until an hour ago.

Cyrus counted to two hundred with her hand flat on Ede's shoulder, feeling his heart hammering through the stolen jacket, and the drones droned on southward, hunting the downwash, hunting the trail of a boy who had doubled back one time too many.

When they were gone she crawled out into the light and stood in the empty wash with her pack heavier by one drowned nation and her options shorter by exactly two. She could cut the boy loose and vanish south, and the company would sweep him up by tomorrow. She could take the chest and leave him, and live with it, the way she lived with other things.

Or she could go upstream.

Ede climbed out after her, blinking, silt to the elbows, and looked at her with that awful open hope.

"Don't," she said. "Don't look at me like that. I haven't decided anything."

But her boots, she noticed, were already pointed north-northwest. Forty degrees off true. Exactly where the needle wanted.
