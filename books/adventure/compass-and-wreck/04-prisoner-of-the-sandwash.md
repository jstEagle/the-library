## 4. Prisoner of the Sandwash

The sandwash was a good place for a prisoner and a bad place for a jailer, which suited Cyrus fine. She'd walked the length of it twice before settling him: high clay banks on either side, one way in, one way out, and her sitting between him and both. The afternoon sun had gone off the walls and left them the color of old bone. Somewhere upstream, water still moved under the gravel — she could hear it if she held her breath, a whisper like someone turning pages.

The boy sat where she'd told him to sit, wrists bound in front of him with the same cord he'd used to cinch his bedroll, which struck her as a small justice. He was maybe twenty, twenty-two. Sunburned across the cheekbones, cracked lips, hands too soft for the desert and blistered now from two days of walking like a man who'd learned his map out of a book. His pack lay open ten feet away where she'd dumped it. She'd gone through it twice already.

"You want some water?" she said.

He looked up. "You're offering me water."

"I'm asking if you want some. Those are different things."

He considered that with an earnestness she found more irritating than his silence would have been. "Yes," he said finally. "Please."

She uncapped her canteen, poured into the lid, and held it out. He drank like he'd been rationing himself for days — small sips, deliberate, making it last — and when he handed it back he said, "Thank you," again, as though the first time hadn't counted.

"Here's how this goes." She sat back down on her heels, forearms on knees, close enough that he had to look slightly up at her. "I'm going to ask you questions. You're going to answer them. If I believe you, you get to keep your teeth. If I don't, we'll negotiate from there."

"That's not —" He stopped himself. Swallowed. "That's not how rangers do it. In the training materials. You're supposed to offer me water first, then food, then —"

"In the training materials." She let that sit. "Where'd you read ranger training materials?"

His jaw worked. He was bad at this, she thought. Bad at lying, bad at hiding, bad at being hunted — he'd doubled back for the chest like a man returning to a burning house for a photograph, which told her more about him than anything he might say. But he wasn't stupid. There was a carefulness underneath the clumsiness, a sense of a person who had spent his whole life being told what not to say.

"My mother worked for the service," he said. "Before."

"Before what?"

"You know what."

Cyrus didn't move. Out past the mouth of the wash, a hawk turned once against the white sky and slid away. She made herself breathe slow, because the other thing — the thing he'd just done, dropping *before* like a stone into still water — wanted to make her lean forward, and leaning forward was how people got lied to.

"Say it," she said.

"Vessia." He said it the way you'd say the name of a dead parent. Flat, practiced, worn smooth from handling. "My mother worked for the ranger service before the drowning. Before they took the name off everything. She kept the manuals. She taught me the signs, the river-signs, the —" He gestured vaguely with his bound hands, frustrated by the size of what he was trying to say. "You found the chest. You saw the seal. You know what I am."

"I know what a stamp looks like. Stamps can be faked. I've faked better ones." She watched his face for the flinch and got it. Good. Honest flinch. "Talk. Start at the beginning and don't editorialize."

* * *

He started, as it turned out, forty years ago, which was eleven years before he was born, and he told it like a story he'd been given rather than one he'd lived — the valleys, the terraced river country in the northeast corner of three maps that had since agreed to show nothing there at all, the water rights, the dam commissions, the flood. He had the cadence of a recitation. Certain phrases came out polished from repetition: *the year the maps went blank*, *the resettlement that wasn't*, *the forty years of quiet*. Other parts he stumbled through, and those were the ones she believed.

"They didn't drown all at once," he said. "That's the thing nobody — there's no record, so people think it was one day, one wall of water. It was three years. They bought the lowlands first, then the terraces, then the orchards. People held out. My great-grandmother's family held out until the last season, and then the water came up the valley faster than they'd been promised it would, and the roads were already closed, and —" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Some got out. Most didn't get far. The ones who got far stopped using the name. That's why you've never met one. We're not supposed to exist. That's the whole design of it. A country doesn't have to be destroyed if everyone agrees it was never there."

"And you." Cyrus tipped her chin at him. "You exist pretty loudly, for a man who isn't supposed to. Stealing from supply depots. Walking open desert in daylight."

"I needed the compass."

"The compass." She reached into her coat pocket and brought it out, held it flat on her palm between them. Brass case gone green at the hinges, glass scratched milky, the needle swinging its slow wrong arc — not north, never north, but steady as a plumb line toward the head of the canyon, toward the source, toward whatever waited up there that her own government's maps showed as a survey error and nothing else. "This compass lies about north."

"It doesn't lie." Something changed in his face when he looked at it — hunger, and under the hunger something older, almost grief. "It points home. It always pointed home. Every Vessian compass ever made does. They were tuned to the source valley. Pilgrims used them. When the flooding started, my grandmother's generation carried them out so the company could never prove where the valley was — a compass that only points for us is worth more than any map." He looked up at her. "It's why I went back for it. You'd have taken it to the station. It would have ended up in an evidence locker, then in a company warehouse, and then the last thing my family ever made would be inventory."

Cyrus turned the compass over in her fingers. On the back, half worn away, a line of script in an alphabet she didn't know, cut deep enough to survive a drowning.

"Sanctuary," she said. "You said sanctuary. Above the source. Survivors. Forty years, Ede. Forty years, in country I have personally patrolled, and nobody's seen smoke, nobody's found a trail, nobody's buried a body wrong. You're telling me there's a nation hiding in a drainage basin I could cover end to end in six days?"

"Yes."

"That's your whole answer?"

"It's the true one." He leaned forward despite the cords, urgent now. "You think hiding is hard? Hiding is easy. Hiding is what we were built for — terraces into hillsides, houses into canyon walls, gardens that look like scrub. The company flew over the valleys for three years and called them empty right up until the water covered them. We are very good at not being seen. Better than you are." He caught himself, color rising. "Sorry. I mean —"

"No," Cyrus said slowly. "Keep going. Say that part again."

He blinked. "Better than —"

"Than me." She stood, brushed grit off her knee, and began to pace the width of the wash, because standing still had suddenly become difficult. "Ten years ago I decided to stop existing. Do you know what that takes? Scrubbing every record, every payroll line, every witness. I did it thoroughly. Professionally. And you're telling me a whole country did it first, with less warning, while drowning."

"I'm telling you we had practice." He said it without pride. That was the strange thing — no pride at all, just the tired fluency of inherited fact. "We'd been hiding from surveys since before the drowning. The flood just made it permanent."

Cyrus stopped pacing. The compass needle trembled against its glass, patient as a heartbeat, pointing up-canyon at nothing she'd ever found and everything he claimed was there.

She didn't believe him. She catalogued that honestly, the way she catalogued weather. She didn't believe in sanctuaries or surviving nations or brass needles with loyalties. What she believed in was the depot theft report with its clean boot prints, the chest stamped with a dead country's seal, and a young man who'd risked his neck doubling back for a hunk of brass because — because. Because people didn't die for fakes. Not like that. Not with that face.

And underneath the disbelief, unwelcome as a splinter, sat another thought: *if even half of it is true, then the company that drowned his country is the same kind of company that erases people like me.*

She hated that thought. She'd built ten years on not having thoughts like that.

* * *

She fed him at dusk — jerky, a strip of dried apricot from her own stores, which surprised her more than him. He ate carefully, thanking her between bites until she told him to stop, and then they sat in the blue hour listening to the river whisper under the gravel, and she asked smaller questions because smaller questions catch liars better than big ones.

His mother's name. Mara. His father's — he hesitated, then gave it, and it was a Vessian name, long and liquid, the kind of name that doesn't survive translation into border-town paperwork. Where he'd grown up: a freight town two hundred miles south, a family that ran a salvage outfit licensed to pick over the drowned country's margins. Smugglers, essentially, she said, and he didn't argue. Who else was left: he went quiet at that one, long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"Enough," he said finally. "Enough to fill a valley. Not enough to fill a room. Scattered. That's why the compass matters. That's why the sanctuary matters. Everyone who's left is waiting for someone to find the way back and hold the door open. Once the route is proven, they'll come. All of them. From everywhere." He looked at her with an expression she recognized and resented on sight, because she'd worn it herself in mirrors, years ago: the face of a person asking to be believed by someone who has every reason not to. "You're a ranger. You know this country better than anyone alive who isn't one of us. Come and see. That's all I'm asking. Come and see, and if there's nothing there, arrest me properly."

"Prisoners don't set terms."

"I'm not setting terms. I'm begging. It just sounds like terms because I practiced."

Despite herself — genuinely despite herself — Cyrus laughed. One breath of it, quickly killed. He smiled anyway, and the smile made him look six years younger and infinitely more breakable, and she turned away from it and looked up-canyon instead, at the dark coming down between the walls, at the direction the needle insisted on.

The monsoon was weeks out. The high passes would seal in September. If a man were going to cross into country that didn't officially exist, he'd need to go soon, and go fast, and go with someone who knew the water.

She was still pretending she hadn't noticed herself thinking that when she heard it.

A sound above the wind. Thin, insectile, wrong — a note that didn't belong to hawks or wind or anything living. Her head came up. Ede saw her face change and went still.

"What is it?"

"Shut up."

She climbed the bank on her elbows, slow, keeping her silhouette below the lip of the wash, and looked south along their back-trail. At first: nothing. Then, against the last violet band of sky, three lights in formation, running dark except for their nav strobes, moving the way nothing in the desert moves — straight, indifferent, grid-patterned. Survey drones. Company airframes, the heavy mapping kind, flying a search ladder over the exact ground she and the boy had covered today. As she watched, the lead unit banked, dropped, and hung above the sandwash's downstream mouth like a wasp deciding about a door.

They weren't mapping. Mapping flights don't hover. They were looking for something, and the something was small, and warm, and currently breathing behind a clay bank with a stolen compass in its pocket.

Cyrus slid back down. Ede had heard it too now; he was pressed against the wall, eyes huge, and when she landed beside him he grabbed her sleeve with his bound hands.

"That's them," he whispered. "The survey company. They've been behind me since the depot. If they find me —" He swallowed. "They don't take survivors to court, Ranger. Survivors are evidence. Evidence gets processed."

"Let go of my sleeve."

He let go. She crouched in the dark, doing arithmetic she didn't want to do. Option one: walk away tonight, alone, the way she'd planned to walk away from everything for ten years. Leave the boy trussed for the company to collect, keep the compass as salvage, be a ghost again by morning. Clean. Safe. Exactly the life she'd built, and exactly the life she deserved.

Option two involved a monsoon, a mountain range, a sanctuary she didn't believe in, and a fugitive who thanked her for water.

Above them, the drone's whine shifted pitch — circling, widening, patient. Machines could wait all night. Machines had all night.

Ede was watching her in the dark. She could feel him watching, feel the question in him, and she realized with genuine anger that she already knew what she was going to do, had probably known since he'd said *before* in that flat, handled voice, and that the boy had known it too, which meant she'd been legible this whole time, which meant her invisibility was failing her in ways she hadn't budgeted for.

"Here's what happens," she said quietly. "We move in twenty minutes, upstream, in the dark, on the gravel so we leave no sign. You stay within arm's reach of me and you speak when spoken to. If we're spotted, you run where I run and you don't argue." She pulled her knife and reached for his bonds. "Because I'm not letting a company retrieval team process a kid in my jurisdiction. That's the only reason. Don't make it weird."

"It's a little weird," Ede said.

"Then we're even, because everything about you is weird." The cord parted. He rubbed his wrists and looked at her with an expression she refused to examine. "Twenty minutes. Eat. Drink. Then we go see your sanctuary."

Upstream, the drone's light swept the wash mouth once, twice — and held, red and steady, on the dark water whispering under the stones, as if it had heard her promise and meant to hold her to it.
