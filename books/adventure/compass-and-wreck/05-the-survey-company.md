## 5. The Survey Company

The dust came first.

Cyrus saw it from the station's water tower at midmorning—a pale plume rising off the hardpan road twelve kilometers south, moving at the steady, unhurried pace of a convoy that had nowhere to hide and no need to hurry. Three vehicles. She counted the spacing between them out of habit: lead truck, transport, something boxy riding high on all-terrain tires that threw a shadow too wide for its frame.

She'd been expecting this for two days, ever since she'd spotted the drone circling the sandwash in long, patient ellipses like a bird of prey that had learned to wait. What she hadn't expected was how fast they'd come. The survey companies didn't work fast. They worked thorough. Fast meant someone above them was nervous.

She climbed down the tower rungs, went inside, and did what she always did when the world reached for her: she checked her walls.

The ranger station at Dry Fork wasn't much—two rooms, a cistern, a radio mast patched with three different kinds of solder, and a wall of filing cabinets that held the administrative ghost of her. Ten years ago, Cyrus Quintana had begun the slow work of becoming someone else. Not a new name—that drew attention—but a smaller one. Ranger C. Quintana, transferred from the northern districts after an incident file she'd paid a clerk in Vell's Crossing to misfile. Reduced duties. Reduced visibility. A service record thinned down to gristle: commendations removed, citations lost to a records fire that had conveniently also destroyed the personnel roster of her old unit. Every year she'd filed the same request—*transfer to remote posting, reduced oversight*—and every year some tired administrator had stamped it without reading, because nobody wanted the desert districts and everybody wanted to forget they existed.

She had made herself into a clerical error. It had taken a decade, and it had worked, because she was still here and no one had ever come looking.

Until now.

She pulled her own file from the third drawer—the thin one, the one that would bore a stranger to tears—and read it again the way she read tracks. *Quintana, C. Ranger, second class. Stationed Dry Fork, eleven years. No disciplinary actions. No commendations since transfer. Annual evaluations: adequate, adequate, adequate.* Adequate was the word she'd cultivated like a crop. Adequate men and women were never audited, never promoted, never remembered.

It should have been enough. She told herself it was enough while she cleaned the station, stowed Ede's confiscated pack under the floor panel beneath the cot, and put the chest back in the corner where she'd found it—or a chest very like it; she'd swapped the Vessian-stamped lid for a rusted salvage top from the scrap pile behind the cistern. The compass rode against her sternum now, inside her shirt, warm as a second heartbeat. She kept telling herself she was holding it only until she decided what to do with it. She was aware that this was a lie. She let it stand anyway.

Ede sat where she'd left him, wrists loose in the soft restraints she'd rigged—not rope, nothing that bruised or marked, because marks invited questions—on the bunk across from the stove. He watched her move through the station with those dark, patient eyes that didn't match his mouth. His mouth wanted to argue. His eyes were willing to wait years.

"They're coming," he said. Not a question. He'd heard the trucks too, or felt them, the way river people probably felt weather in their bones.

"Company retrieval," she said. "You know anything about that?"

"I know they've been looking for the chest since before I stole it." He shifted, testing the restraint, then gave it up. "I know they burned a settlement two valleys east of my people's route last spring. Not burned—cleared. Everyone relocated, everyone processed. Nobody signed anything." His voice stayed flat, reciting facts he'd carried so long they'd gone smooth as river stones. "They don't want the seal, Ranger. They want the idea of it dead."

"Then you're the most dangerous man in the desert."

"I'm the most *inconvenient* one." Almost a smile. "There's a difference."

There was. She'd spent ten years learning it.

* * *

The convoy arrived at noon, when the heat stood straight up off the ground and everything living was in shade. Cyrus met them in the yard, hat low, hands visible, wearing the whole weight of eleven years of adequacy like armor.

Three of them got out of the lead vehicle. The driver stayed. The fourth climbed down from the boxy transport last, and Cyrus knew her immediately—not by face but by gravity. Odile Marsh was a small woman, gray at the temples, dressed in field khaki so precisely pressed it looked like a uniform for a country that didn't exist yet. She wore no company insignia, which meant she outranked insignia. Her eyes did a full circuit of the station, the water tower, the scrap pile, the horizon, in about four seconds, and Cyrus understood that everything Marsh saw, she filed.

"Ranger Quintana." Marsh extended a hand. Her grip was dry, brief, calibrated. "Odile Marsh, Meridian Survey and Reclamation. I hope we're not intruding on your district."

"You are," Cyrus said pleasantly. "But the road's public."

Something flickered at the corner of Marsh's mouth—appreciation, maybe, or the memory of it. "May we impose on your shade for a few minutes? My people could use water, and I have some questions. Routine ones."

"Water's free. Questions cost."

"What do they cost?"

"Time. And I'm short on it. Monsoon season's six weeks out and I've got forty klicks of fence line down past the narrows."

Marsh laughed—a real laugh, low and unforced, and that frightened Cyrus more than any coldness would have. Cold operators performed their warmth. This woman actually had some, buried somewhere, which meant she could deploy it like a tool instead of a mask.

"Forty klicks of fence," Marsh repeated, gesturing her team toward the cistern pump. "I admire it. The maintenance of things. Most people out here let the desert take the ledger back." She turned a slow half-circle, taking in the station again. "We're looking for salvage, Ranger. Stolen property, technically, though the ownership question is—complicated. A chest. Bronze fittings, old workmanship. It may bear a stamp." She produced a folded sheet from her breast pocket, opened it with two fingers, and held it up.

A drawing of the seal. The triple wave over the keel-line, the broken crown above it. Rendered in clean corporate linework, annotated with measurements, like a specimen pinned to a board.

Cyrus looked at it for exactly as long as a bored ranger would look at anything—two seconds, a shrug built into the shoulders. "Vessia," she said. "My grandfather used to talk about it. Drowned before I was born. You're chasing forty-year-old junk?"

"We're chasing *property*. Junk is a valuation." Marsh folded the sheet away. "The chest was taken from a depot at Corben's Wash nine days ago. The thief came north along the dry country. Your station is the only structure on that line."

"So you think he walked past my door."

"I think he walked past *someone's* door. Desert's empty, Ranger, but it isn't vacant. People see things. Fences get cut. Tracks get made." Marsh's gaze drifted, casual as weather, to the scrap pile. To the cistern. Back. "Did you see anyone?"

"A kid, maybe. Week ago. Came begging water at dusk, took a look at the station, moved on. Could've been him. Could've been any of the wash rats that drift through. They all look alike—hungry and quick."

"And the chest? If he'd cached it, or dropped it—salvage law says found property within a registered district reverts to the district authority. That's you."

"Salvage law says a lot of things." Cyrus hooked a thumb toward the scrap pile. "I pull rusted tin out of the washes every season. Half of it's stamped with somebody's logo. You want me to call Meridian every time I find a dented crate?"

"No," Marsh said mildly. "I want you to call us when you find something that matters. That's all this is, Ranger. Housekeeping." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, and Cyrus thought: *she practices this in mirrors, or worse, she doesn't have to.* "If you happen across the chest—or anything bearing that mark—you'll contact our regional office. There's a finder's consideration. Generous."

"I'll keep it in mind."

"Wonderful." Marsh checked a slim watch. "One more thing, and then I'll take my dust elsewhere. Purely administrative. We cross-check names against regional registries—it saves time later, avoids awkward jurisdictional questions. May I confirm your service details? Quintana, C., second class, Dry Fork station, eleven years."

The words were harmless. The words were her own file read back to her in a courteous voice. But something in the cadence snagged, a burr under the saddle blanket, and Cyrus felt her pulse tick up one beat before she killed it.

"That's right."

"Eleven years is a long posting. Most rangers rotate after four."

"I requested remote. I like the quiet."

"Yes," Marsh said softly. "I can see that you do." She paused, and the pause had weight to it, deliberate as a stone set on paper to keep it from blowing away. "Your file came up in our cross-reference, you know. When the theft report flagged this corridor. Standard procedure—we screen every registered officer within two hundred klicks." Another smile, thinner now, more precise. "Yours was remarkably clean. Almost *surgical*, one might say. Commendations expunged in a records fire. An incident file that exists as a reference number but no content. Transfers approved by administrators who retired before the paperwork cleared." She tilted her head. "You must have excellent luck, Ranger Quintana."

"Or excellent record-keeping."

"Mm." Marsh looked at her for a long moment, and whatever lived behind those eyes was doing arithmetic Cyrus couldn't see the operands of. Then she stepped back, clapped her hands once—a small, bright sound—and called her team to the vehicles. "We'll be going. Thank you for the water, and the conversation. Both were refreshing."

At the lead truck's door she stopped, one boot on the running board, and spoke without turning around.

"The monsoon will be early this year. Our meteorological section is very good. Six weeks, perhaps less, and then the high country closes for the season—roads wash out, passes seal, nothing moves until spring." Now she turned, and her expression was pleasant, almost kind. "Whatever anyone intends to do upriver, they should do it soon. After the rains, well. The desert forgets. That's rather its charm."

The convoy pulled out in the same unhurried order it had arrived, dust climbing into the white sky, and Cyrus stood in the yard until the last of it faded, not moving, doing her own arithmetic.

* * *

Inside, Ede was sitting up straight, all the patience gone out of him. "What did she say? What did she—"

"Quiet." Cyrus crossed to the filing cabinet, pulled her own file, and laid it on the table under the lamp even though the sun was high. She read it the way she'd read it a hundred times, and for the first time in ten years, she read it as an enemy would.

The records fire. Vell's Crossing, year six of her disappearance. She'd started it herself—a trash barrel, a slow afternoon, a building with old wiring and no sprinklers. It had erased her unit roster, her commendations, the mission file with her partner's name in it. Clean. Surgical. She'd been proud of it, in the way you're proud of a scar that proves you survived something.

But fires leave ash, and ash gets sampled, and somewhere in some archive there was a residue analysis, or a clerk who noticed that the burned files were all *hers*, or simply a pattern-recognition algorithm that had chewed through forty years of regional data and coughed up one anomaly: a ranger whose history had more holes than document.

Marsh hadn't come for the chest. Or not only. Marsh had come to look at *her*, and Cyrus understood now that the visit itself had been the test—the questions weren't questions, they were calipers, measuring how she'd flinch.

"She knows," Ede said quietly. It wasn't a question either.

"She suspects. There's a difference." His own phrase, handed back to him, and he had the grace not to smile. Cyrus pressed her palms flat on the table. "She flagged my file. Which means there's a copy somewhere in a Meridian office with my name on it and a red border around it, and every checkpoint, every registry query, every supply requisition I file from here on goes past a pair of eyes that's waiting for me to make a mistake."

"So you run."

"I don't run." The words came out automatic, and hearing them, she heard how old they were—older than the decade, older than the erasure, worn smooth from use. "Running is what guilty people do. I stay. I file my reports. I fix my fences. In three months she's reassigned and I'm a footnote."

"And him?" Ede nodded at the floor panel, at the hidden pack, at the whole fragile architecture of the life she'd built. "And the chest? And whatever it is you're keeping against your chest right now, because I can see the shape of it through your shirt and I'm not stupid?" He leaned forward, restraints pulling taut, and his voice dropped low and urgent. "Ranger. She told you the monsoon comes early because she wants us to run *now*, on her schedule, down roads she's already watching. She gave you the timeline because she thinks fear makes people predictable. Does it?"

Cyrus didn't answer. Outside, the wind had come up from the southeast, smelling faintly, impossibly, of wet stone—from mountains she couldn't see, carrying water she wouldn't taste for weeks. Early. Marsh hadn't been bluffing about that part. The desert itself was changing sides.

She thought about the sandwash, and the chest surfacing from the silt like something the river had swallowed and refused to digest. She thought about a boy smuggled out of a drowned valley at six years old, carrying a ring for people he couldn't remember. She thought about ten years of adequacy, of being a clerical error, of a landscape that asked nothing of her—which was, she admitted now, in the privacy of her own skull, another way of saying a landscape that needed nothing from her, that would never notice if she vanished, that had accepted her invisibility because invisibility was all she'd offered.

The trap wasn't closing. The trap had closed years ago, quietly, one stamped form at a time, and she had built it herself and called it freedom.

Cyrus took the compass out from under her shirt and set it on the table between them. The needle swung once, twice, and settled—unerringly, insolently—upstream. Toward the source. Toward the high country that would seal in six weeks, or less.

"Get your pack," she said.

Ede stared at her. "You're letting me go?"

"I'm coming with you." She was already pulling the floor panel, already reaching for the maps she'd sworn she'd never open again. "Because if I stay here, I'm a footnote. And I have spent ten years being a footnote, and I am *done*—" She stopped. Breathed. Made her voice flat and professional, ranger to prisoner, lie to lie. "Because the company wants that compass, and the only way to make sure they never get it is to take it somewhere they can't follow. Somewhere the monsoon closes. So we move tonight, ahead of her schedule, and we don't stop until the river runs out of country."

Ede was smiling now, wide and unguarded, and it made him look six years old himself. "You won't believe any of it. The sanctuary. The source. Any of it."

"No," Cyrus agreed, stuffing the maps into her pack. "I won't."

But the needle pointed upstream, and the wind smelled of rain, and somewhere south a gray-templed woman was writing her name in a file with a red border, and for the first time in a decade, Cyrus Quintana knew exactly which direction she was going.
