## 6. Upstream

They left the sandwash before first light, while the cold still held the dust down and the drones—company drones, survey-company drones with their patient white wingspans—were grounded by a low ceiling of cloud that had come up from the south in the night.

Cyrus went first. That was not negotiable, and she didn't bother making it sound like anything other than what it was: she knew this country, and Ede did not, no matter what bronze seal he carried or what drowned nation he claimed as kin. She moved along the eastern rim of the wash where the rock was hard enough to hold a boot print without giving it away, and she kept them below the skyline, in the long blue shadow of the ridge, until the sun cleared the mesa to the east and made shadows lie flat.

Ede followed well. She'd give him that much. He was lighter than he looked, sure-footed on the broken talus, and he didn't talk. Most people she tracked talked—nervous chatter, questions, apologies—and every word of it was a trail of its own. This one walked with his mouth shut and his eyes moving, checking behind them at intervals so regular they might have been drilled into him. Which meant someone had taught him to run. Someone had taught a six-year-old to run, once, and the lesson had never worn off.

She hated how much that told her. Hated more that she'd started cataloguing it.

By midmorning they were three miles into the canyon system above the dead river, where the walls closed in and the sky became a bright ribbon overhead, and Cyrus called the first halt in the lee of a fallen slab whose face was cool to the touch. She handed Ede water from her own bottle because his was already half gone and she hadn't offered to refill it and wasn't going to. Rationing was rationing. Sentiment was for people who could afford weight.

He drank two swallows, capped the bottle, handed it back. His eyes were on the slab beside them.

"You're staring at rock," she said.

"I'm reading it." He crouched, and she saw then what he was looking at—a mark cut into the stone at knee height, half-filled with mineral stain, so old its edges had gone soft. A vertical line, crossed by three short horizontal strokes descending in length, and beneath it a small triangle pointing upstream. "That's a river-sign."

"There's nothing carved there but lichen."

"Look again." He wet his thumb and touched the triangle, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "The line is the river. The strokes are flood years—the water rose three times past this height before the carving was made. The triangle means *safe crossing below*, or it used to." He glanced up at her. "My grandmother's hands taught me these. I was small. I thought everyone's grandmother drew pictures in stone."

Cyrus looked at the mark for a long moment. Then she stood, walked twenty feet downstream along the wall, and found another one: a spiral with a gap in it, cut deep, deliberate. And another, fifty feet on, half-eroded by wind but unmistakable—a fish shape rendered in four economical lines, tail toward the source.

They were everywhere. Once she knew to look, the canyon walls were crowded with them, spaced like fence posts, a conversation conducted in bronze-age shorthand across forty years of silence. Her own service maps marked this canyon as *unnamed drainage, seasonal flow, no resources*. The Vessians had annotated it like a book.

"How far do they go?" she asked, and heard the curiosity in her own voice and disliked it.

"The signs?" Ede came to stand beside her, following her finger to the fish. "Upstream to the source. Downstream—" he hesitated, "downstream they end where the water ended. Where the valleys flooded out. There was no reason to sign a country that was going under." He said it flatly, without performance, which somehow made it worse. "The old ones carved as they walked. Every family walking out took turns with the chisel. So anyone coming after would know the way."

"Coming after to where?"

"To the sanctuary." He said it simply, without the salesman's lean she'd been braced for since the sandwash. "Or wherever the signs led. My grandmother said the last walkers signed all the way to the high passes before the water took the low country. After that, nobody came back down to carve anymore."

Cyrus turned back to the wall. The fish stared upstream with its four-line face, patient as everything else in this canyon.

Her own government had mapped this drainage twice—once by horse survey eighty years ago, once by satellite thirty years after that—and between the two efforts had produced a map so indifferent it might have been drawn by someone filing paperwork rather than looking at land. No crossings noted. No flood history. No safe water, no shelter, no names. The Vessians, walking out of their drowning country with a chisel and whatever time they had left, had left a better record in an afternoon than the republic had managed in a century.

It shouldn't have surprised her. Erasure was a specialty of hers too. But there was something obscene about standing in front of a civilization's whole surviving literature—cut into stone, addressed to strangers, meant to save lives—and knowing that her own agency's archive held a single line about it: *Vessia, former territory, delisted.*

"We should move," she said.

* * *

They moved. The canyon narrowed through the middle of the day, walls rising sheer on either side, and the temperature dropped ten degrees the moment the sun lost the slot, so that they traveled in a corridor of blue shade that smelled of wet stone and creosote. The old riverbed underfoot was cobbles now instead of silt, rounded and pale, and here and there pools lingered in the deep scours—green, cold, alive with the small frantic economy of tadpoles and water striders. Water this far up. Water that had never gotten the memo that the river was dead.

At the second pool Cyrus stopped and filled bottles, and Ede crouched across from her and pointed at the overhanging wall without touching it.

"That one's a warning."

She looked. A cluster of marks: a zigzag, a circle with a dot, and below them a shape like a bird with folded wings. "Warning of what?"

"Flood pulse. The circle-dot means *look up*—check the sky, check the high ground. The zigzag is sudden water." He nodded at the narrow slot downstream, the way they'd come. "This canyon drains a huge country above us. It can be dry here and a wall of water comes through in twenty minutes because it rained sixty miles away. The Vessians who walked this route learned that the hard way, I think. There are a lot of warning signs in the narrows." He paused. "And a lot of little marks near them. Small ones. Low on the wall."

Cyrus looked low on the wall. Small marks, yes—handprints, actually, dozens of them, outlined in pigment that had survived where pigment had no right to survive, pressed into the stone by hands smaller than hers. Children's hands. Families marking passage, or marking grief, or just insisting *we were here* to a canyon that would outlast the insistence.

She finished filling the bottles faster than she needed to.

They climbed out of the narrows in the late afternoon via a goat track Ede spotted before she did—he read the terrain differently than she did, less like a ranger and more like a swimmer, always oriented to where the water would go—and came up onto a bench of red earth where the canyon opened into a wide amphitheater walled with stratified cliffs. On the far side, a side-canyon entered at an angle, and where the two drainages met, an enormous fig tree grew out of a crack in the rock, older than any tree had a business being in this dryness, its roots wrapped around the confluence like fingers around a throat.

Beneath the fig, someone had built a cairn. Not a ranger cairn—those were functional, ugly, stacked by survey crews. This one was built with care, stones fitted, and set into its top was a flat slab carved with a sign Cyrus was beginning to be able to read on her own: the vertical river-line, a triangle pointing up-canyon, and beneath them a symbol she hadn't seen yet—a square with a smaller square inside it.

"What's that one?"

Ede stopped walking. For a moment he didn't answer, and when he did, his voice had changed—quieter, careful, the way people speak inside churches or hospitals.

"A house within a house. It means *shelter ahead, hidden*." He looked at the cairn a long time. "There should be a cache nearby. Food, tools, maybe rope. The walkers stocked them. If any of it lasted..."

Cyrus circled the fig tree slowly, reading the ground the way she read everything—not for meaning, for disturbance. And there, beneath a shelf of root and rubble on the north side, a seam of worked stone: a slab fitted flush into the earth, its edge grooved for lifting. She got her knife under it and levered, and the slab came up with a grinding protest and a breath of air that smelled, impossibly, of cedar.

Inside: a clay jar sealed with pitch, a coil of sinew rope gone brittle but intact, two iron hooks, and a bundle wrapped in oilcloth that proved to be dried meat and hardtack, forty years old and, when she broke a corner off and tasted it, still technically food. Somebody had planned for travelers who wouldn't arrive for decades. Somebody had salted meat against a future they wouldn't see, and packed it into the dark, and trusted.

Cyrus sat back on her heels and looked at the cache and felt something shift in her chest that she refused to name.

"They stocked the whole route," she said.

"As far as anyone got." Ede knelt opposite her, and in the slanting light she saw how young he actually was—younger than his competence, younger than his grief. Twenty-two, maybe. A child smuggled out with a compass and a ring and a language of stone. "My grandmother said the caches were the last thing the walkers did. They couldn't carry the country out with them. So they buried pieces of it along the way and told each other: whoever follows will find them. Whoever follows will know we thought about them." He smiled, briefly, and it cracked something in his face. "I used to think that was a story. Something grandmothers say."

"And now?"

"Now I'm eating their bread." He turned the hardtack over in his hands. "Forty years, and it's still feeding people. That's not a story. That's engineering."

Cyrus repacked the cache—she took the rope and the hooks, left most of the food, replaced the slab exactly as she'd found it, and swept their traces from the sand. Habit. Invisibility was habit, the way breathing was. But halfway through sweeping she caught herself and stopped, because she realized she was erasing evidence that anyone had opened the cache, and some stubborn part of her wanted the next traveler—Ede's hypothetical followers, the sanctuary's hypothetical scouts—to know someone had been here, had taken only what they needed, had honored the arrangement.

A dead nation's etiquette, observed by a woman who'd spent ten years making sure no record of herself existed anywhere.

She finished sweeping anyway. Old habits kept you alive. But she noted the impulse, filed it somewhere she didn't look often, and shouldered her pack.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow above the confluence, back from the firelight, and Cyrus took first watch because she always took first watch and because sleep had been leaving her earlier and earlier since the drones. The fire was small, screened by rocks, mostly heat and hardly any light. Ede slept the way soldiers and children sleep—suddenly, completely, one breath on the wrong side of the world and the next breath on the right one.

She watched the canyon walls go silver as the moon rose, and the signs with them. Even in moonlight she could pick them out now, here and there along the cliff faces: the river-line, the triangles, the fish, the warnings. A road built for people who hadn't arrived yet. A country that had drowned and kept talking anyway.

Her own records, if anyone ever assembled them—and someone was trying; the flag on her forged file had seen to that—would show a decade of clean, empty competence. Transfers. Commendations with no context. A partner's name redacted. Nothing carved anywhere. Nothing that said *she was here, she thought about you, take what you need.* She had engineered her life to leave no trace, and she had succeeded, and sitting in the moonlight under a dead nation's handwriting she felt the success for the first time as what it was: not safety. Just silence, self-inflicted, forty years early.

Ede stirred. Not waking—turning, murmuring something in a language she didn't know, soft and liquid, river-speech. Then quiet again.

She should wake him for second watch. She didn't. She sat with the rifle across her knees and let him sleep, and told herself it was tactics: a rested asset traveled faster. The excuse had holes in it big enough to drive a company drone through, and she let it stand anyway.

Near midnight, movement on the far rim.

Not an animal—wrong rhythm, too upright, too patient. A figure at the lip of the amphitheater, black against the star field, standing motionless at the exact point where a person would stand to look down into camp. It stayed there for the length of ten slow breaths. Then it stepped back off the skyline and was gone, without sound, without light, with a economy of movement that Cyrus recognized because it was her own.

Someone had been following them. Not from behind—from above. For how long, she couldn't say. Since the sandwash? Since the depot?

Her hand found Ede's shoulder and shook him, and he came awake instantly, silently, eyes open and aimed at her face, and she put one finger to her lips and pointed at the empty rim.

He looked. He looked for a long time. And then, very quietly, in the dark, he began to laugh—not frightened laughter, something closer to relief.

"She found us," he whispered.
