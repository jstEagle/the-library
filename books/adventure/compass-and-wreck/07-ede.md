## 7. Ede

They made camp early that third day, in the lee of a fallen shelf of sandstone that had peeled off the canyon wall sometime in the last century and come to rest like a sleeping animal. The river ran shallow and green-gray over its stones, talking to itself. Above them the walls narrowed to a slit of sky gone copper with evening.

Cyrus had built the fire small — half the size she'd have built for herself alone, because Ede was still, in some part of her ledger, a prisoner who hadn't been informed of it. She'd caught two chub from the shallows and cooked them on flat stones, and now they ate in the particular silence of two people who had run out of questions that were safe to ask.

He was twenty-six, maybe. It was hard to tell. He had the kind of face that looked unfinished, as if grief had gotten to it before adulthood could. His hands were careful hands — he handled the compass the way other people handled infants, and when he wasn't touching it he kept glancing at the chest to confirm it was still there, the way a man checks a wound.

She watched him eat. She was good at watching; it was most of what a decade of erasing yourself taught you. And what she saw was this: he didn't eat like a hungry man. He ate like a man performing hunger for an audience, taking each bite deliberately, chewing thoroughly, because somewhere in him lived a child who had learned that food might be the last thing offered for a long time.

"You were going to tell me," she said, "why a drowned country needs a compass."

Ede set down the fish skeleton with the same care he gave everything. For a while she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he said, "You'll think I'm lying."

"Probably."

"Will you at least wait until I'm done?"

Cyrus turned her stick in the fire. "I've waited three days to hear it. I can wait ten more minutes."

* * *

"It's not a story with a beginning," Ede said. "That's the first thing. Everyone wants it to start with the water coming, but the water coming was just the end of something that started before I was born. Before my mother was born. Vessia was already dying when it was still alive, if that makes any sense."

"Try me."

He pulled his knees up, wrapped his arms around them. In the firelight he looked younger than six.

"The treaties were signed when my grandmother was a girl. The Meridian Compact — you've never heard of it, nobody has, that was the point of it. Four nations upstream signed agreements to take more water than the rivers could give, and Vessia was downstream, and Vessia was small, and Vessia had the bad manners to be rich in exactly nothing anyone wanted except water rights nobody knew we held." A thin smile. "We held them anyway. Old law. Older than the Compact. My family kept the documents. That was our trade, sort of — keepers. Not priests, not lawyers. Keepers. We kept the papers that said the valleys belonged to us, and everyone above us spent forty years pretending the papers didn't exist, and then they spent one year making sure they couldn't exist anymore."

"The flooding," Cyrus said. "The official record calls it a dam failure cascade."

"The official record calls it a lot of things." He said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "Three years of engineered drought first. Then the diversion works upstream, all of them 'emergency measures.' Then the winter the snowpack failed and every reservoir above us released at once — an accident, they said, a tragic confluence of weather and infrastructure. Forty-one days. The low valleys went first. Then the terraces. Then the bell towers, which you could see underwater for almost a month, ringing sometimes when the current moved the clappers. People said the country was saying goodbye to itself."

Cyrus found she had stopped turning her stick.

"I was six," Ede said. "I don't remember the decision to send me out. I remember my mother braiding my hair — I had long hair then, all the keeper children did, it was a marker so the network would know us — and I remember her putting a ring on a cord around my neck and telling me I was carrying the valley. Not leaving it. Carrying it. Those were her words. *You are not leaving. You are carrying.*" He touched his collarbone, where the cord would have sat. "I remember the boat. I remember a woman who wasn't my mother holding me under a tarpaulin while soldiers' lights swept the water. I remember thinking the lights were fish. I remember being very angry about the braid coming undone."

"What happened to your mother?"

"She stayed to carry the rest of it." He looked at the fire. "There were eleven thousand people in the lower valleys when the water came. The number they use officially is four hundred displaced. You understand what that means."

Cyrus understood. She'd read enough scrubbed reports in ten years of ranger service to know exactly what a number like that meant: not that four hundred people had survived, but that four hundred people had been deemed worth accounting for.

"And you?" she asked. "Where did the network take you?"

"Nowhere. Everywhere. That was the design. There were maybe two hundred children smuggled out over five years, scattered along the old caravan routes, given to families who owed Vessia debts no one remembered incurring. I was passed between six households by the time I was twelve. Each one knew only the next link in the chain. Each one got a piece of me and none of them got a name for what I was. By fifteen I'd stopped using my real name even inside the chain, because names travel." He glanced up. "You'd know about that."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Make it mutual. I'm asking the questions here."

But he only smiled, tired and unfooled, and went on. "When I was nineteen the last keeper of the chain died — an old woman in a mudbrick town two hundred miles south of here who had carried a sealed box for thirty years without ever being told what was in it. She left it to me. Inside was the chest, and instructions: bring it to the source before the last of the old generation is gone. The people who remember the routes are dying faster than the people who remember why the routes matter." He drew a breath. "So I've been walking for seven years. Mostly toward. Sometimes away, when there were watchers. The compass came to me two years ago, from another survivor, a man who'd kept it since the drowning and whose heart finally gave out in a hospice in Corvane. He told me it doesn't point north. It points home. And it only points for—" He stopped.

"For what?"

"For whoever it decides belongs." He said it carefully, like setting down something breakable. "I know how it sounds."

"It sounds like a story people tell themselves so the walking has a destination."

"Yes," Ede agreed mildly. "And the mountains are full of people who told themselves stories and arrived anyway. Sefa says—"

"Sefa." Cyrus kept her voice flat. "You've mentioned her twice now. Who is she?"

"A circuit-walker. One of the old ones. If she's following us, we're luckier than we deserve." He must have seen something in Cyrus's face, because he added, "I'm not sure she is following us. But the bells have been rung recently. Someone's marking the route ahead of us."

"We'll discuss the ghost later." Cyrus poked the fire down. "Here's what I don't understand, and I want you to actually answer it instead of going poetic. If your sanctuary exists — if these survivors have been sitting above the source for forty years — why does anyone need a boy with a box? Why hasn't someone gone down and gotten them? Why does the compass matter at all?"

Ede was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, his voice had changed — flatter, older.

"Because the sanctuary isn't a place you find. It's a place that finds you. The settlement above the source moves. Every few years, when survey flights get too regular, they pull up stakes and go somewhere else in the high country, and the only way to follow them is the waymarkers — the river-signs, the bells, the marks on the old walls. And the waymarkers are keyed. They mean nothing unless you arrive carrying the right things, knowing the right words, at the right season. The compass is part of it. The seal is part of it. I am part of it." He spread his hands. "They didn't build a fortress, ranger. They built a lock. And I'm a key that's been walking for seven years because keys don't get to choose their doors."

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned to a red eye and Ede slept with the chest's strap wrapped twice around his wrist, Cyrus sat up and did the thing she hated doing, which was thinking about herself.

She tried to map the difference between them and found the map kept folding back on itself. He had been smuggled out of a drowned country at six years old and given six false households. She had walked out of a burning station house nine years ago and built herself six false identities, each one sanded smooth, each one forgetting a little more. He carried a ring for people he could barely remember — he'd admitted as much tonight, in the pause before the boat, that his mother's face had gone soft-edged, that he remembered the braid better than the woman who'd made it. And what did Cyrus carry? Nothing. That had been the whole point. She had spent a decade ensuring that if she died in a wash tomorrow, no one would come looking, no file would flag, no name would be spoken over a grave because there would be no grave attached to any name at all.

She had called it freedom. Sitting there in the dark with a boy asleep beside a bronze-stamped chest, she examined the word the way you examine a coin you suspect is shaved.

He grieved a country. She had grieved one person, once, badly, and had responded by deleting herself from the world's paperwork as if the world kept records of guilt. Maybe it did. Maybe hers was the same exile wearing different clothes: he walked toward what he'd lost, carrying it; she walked away from what she'd lost, carrying nothing, and called the lightness survival.

The wind came down the canyon and the river answered it, and somewhere far upstream — or she imagined it did — a bell moved in the current and rang once, thin as a wire.

Ede stirred. Without opening his eyes he said, "You're doing it again."

"Doing what?"

"Watching me like I'm a report you're writing." He yawned. "Ask the question you've been circling since the fish."

Cyrus looked at the coals. "Your mother. When she put the ring on you. Did you ever go back? Even once? To look?"

"No." Simple, immediate, no shame in it. "Going back is how they catch you. The company keeps a list of everyone who ever visited the drowned districts. Sefa walks the circuits precisely because she never stops anywhere long enough to be a visitor." He opened his eyes then, and in the dark they were just shine. "Why? Would you go back? To yours?"

"I don't have a drowned district."

"Everyone has a drowned district." He said it sleepily, without cruelty, the way you state the weather. "Yours is just dry."

She should have corrected him. She had a whole decade of practice correcting people, deflecting, filing herself under false headings. Instead she sat with it, and the silence lasted long enough that his breathing lengthened back into sleep, and the question hung over the camp like the slit of sky over the canyon — narrow, and getting narrower.

Because the truth, which she would not say aloud, was this: she knew exactly where her drowned district was. She drove past the turnoff for it every time she took the eastern patrol route. There was a name on a gate there that she had not spoken in nine years, and a report she had never finished reading, and a partner named Ade Varo who was buried under a stone with someone else's account of how he died.

Keys don't get to choose their doors, the boy had said.

Toward dawn, Cyrus heard it clearly this time and knew she wasn't imagining it: a bell, far upstream, ringing twice — pause — twice. A signal. Someone was marking the route ahead of them, and whoever it was had been close enough, for longer than two days, to know exactly where they slept.
