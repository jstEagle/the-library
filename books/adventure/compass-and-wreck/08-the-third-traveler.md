## 8. The Third Traveler

Cyrus noticed the third set of tracks on the second morning, in the cold gray hour before the sun cleared the canyon rim.

She had stopped to refill the water skins at a seep where the sandstone wept a thin silver thread, and while Ede dozed against his pack she walked the bank the way she'd walked a thousand banks before—eyes down, reading. Their own prints from yesterday were there, hers long-strided and deliberate, his shorter, dragging slightly on the left where his boot sole was wearing through. And crossing them, overprinting them, a third line of footprints: narrow, shallow-heeled, spaced with an economy that said *old woman* as clearly as a signature.

They hadn't been there yesterday morning.

"Someone's behind us," she said, when Ede woke.

He came alert the way he always did, too fast, hands first. "Marsh?"

"No. Marsh's people walk like they're being paid by the pound." She crouched over the prints. "One person. Small. Been following since at least the narrows—we crossed their trail twice yesterday and I read it as runoff spillover." That annoyed her more than the tracks themselves. A decade of moving through empty country without being seen, and she'd filed a human being under *geology*.

Ede crouched beside her. When he touched the print's edge, something in his face changed—not fear. Recognition of a kind. He didn't say anything, though, and Cyrus had learned in six days of traveling with him that Ede kept his knowledge the way other people kept knives: close, and only drawn when needed.

They made distance that day anyway, harder than they needed to, cutting up a side drainage and doubling back along a shelf of wind-scoured rock where no track would hold. Cyrus picked the route specifically because it would kill a follower's certainty. By evening she was almost pleased with herself.

Then, at dusk, they found the fire.

It was small and clean, built in a hollow of stone where its light wouldn't carry, and it was still warm. Beside it sat three flat stones arranged as seats. On one of them, someone had left two dried figs and a twist of salt wrapped in waxed cloth.

Ede stared at the figs for a long moment. Then he laughed—a short, disbelieving sound—and said a word in Vessian that Cyrus didn't know yet but understood anyway, from the shape of it, from the way his shoulders dropped half an inch.

"What," she said.

"That's how my mother used to say it." He looked at her. "It means: *well, obviously.*"

* * *

The third traveler let them see her the next noon, and she did it on her own terms, which Cyrus both respected and resented.

They came down out of a fold of red hills into a wide bend of the riverbed where a lone tamarisk threw a pool of shade, and the woman was simply there, sitting cross-legged in the shade with a walking staff across her knees, as if she had been waiting for them since the founding of the world and considered their arrival mildly late.

She was old—older than Cyrus had guessed from the tracks, sixty or past it—with a face browned and creased to the texture of old leather, white hair bound back with a strip of faded blue cloth. Her gear was absurdly slight for the country: a blanket roll, a gourd, a satchel. Her eyes were the pale gray-brown of the river stones, and they went to Ede first and stayed there, and something moved through her face like water under ice.

"You walk like your grandmother," she said. In Vessian-accented Common, each word placed like a stone in a wall.

Ede stopped as if he'd hit a wire. "You knew my grandmother?"

"I knew everyone's grandmother." The old woman rose without using her hands, smooth as a door swinging. "I am Sefa. I have been walking this country for forty-one years. You are going to the source."

"We're—" Ede began.

"Don't bother." She turned the pale eyes on Cyrus then, and Cyrus felt the look go through her clothes, her gear, her silence, and take inventory. "The boy is obvious. The compass pulls him like a fish on a line. But you." A slow tilt of the head. "You are the interesting one. You walk like someone who has erased a name. I have met four of you in forty years. You all walk the same."

Cyrus said nothing. Saying nothing was a skill; she'd sharpened it for a decade.

Sefa seemed entirely satisfied by this. "Good. The talkative ones die in the high passes. They spend breath they'll want later." She picked up her staff. "I'm coming with you."

"You're not," Cyrus said.

"Yes, I am."

"This isn't a pilgrimage. There are people hunting us. Armed people, with aircraft. Anyone with us is a liability."

"Anyone *without* me is a corpse," Sefa said, with the mildness of someone correcting a child's arithmetic. "You are following the river to its source through country your maps show as blank. Your maps are blank because the people who drew them never asked the river anything. I have walked every circuit of the old water. I know where the seeps are that don't appear in any survey. I know which canyons flood in a cloudburst thirty miles away. And I know," she added, tapping the staff once against the sand, "the signs. The boy knows some. He knows them like a man knows a lullaby his mother sang—by heart, not by meaning. I know them like a mason knows stone."

Ede had gone very still. "The signs," he said. "You could teach me what they mean?"

"I could teach you what they mean," Sefa agreed, "if you survive long enough to learn, which currently you will not, because your ranger here plans your route like a soldier and not like a walker." She looked at Cyrus again. "Yesterday you took the shelf above the scree. It saved you from leaving tracks. It also put you in the open for two hours with no cover from the east, where the weather comes from. Lucky for you the monsoon is late. It will not be late much longer."

Cyrus felt her jaw tighten. The worst part—the genuinely infuriating part—was that the old woman was right about the shelf. She'd chosen it for concealment and told herself the exposure risk was acceptable. It had been acceptable. It had also been the choice of someone thinking like a pursued woman instead of a traveler, and Sefa had read it off the ground in ten seconds.

"Fine," Cyrus said, because the alternative was arguing with someone who had clearly been arguing with the desert for four decades and winning. "You walk with us until the Forks. After the Forks we reassess."

"After the Forks you won't reassess," Sefa said placidly, "because by then you'll have realized you can't find the source without me. But yes. Agreed." And she started walking, upstream, at a pace that was unhurried and somehow faster than theirs.

* * *

The friction began within hours, which Cyrus had expected, and took forms she hadn't.

Sefa did not ask permission. She rearranged their loads while they slept—redistributed weight, re-wrapped Ede's blistered heel with a poultice that smelled of juniper and something sharper, moved Cyrus's knife from her hip to a position she claimed was faster to draw and Cyrus privately thought was slower. She cooked, uninvited, from a pouch of dried things that Cyrus couldn't identify and refused on principle the first night and ate on the second, because it was better food than either of them had managed, and refusing good food in the desert was a vanity the desert punished.

She talked constantly, in a way that wasn't talking so much as narrating. Every landmark had a name in Vessian and a story attached, and she dispensed both whether or not anyone wanted them. That split boulder: a ferryman's marker, the crack aligned to the winter solstice sunrise. That terrace of collapsed walls half-swallowed by the wash: a mill town called Arenn, drowned last, its people walking out through the snow. That notch in the eastern rim where the light pooled green at dusk: *the river watching itself*, her grandmother had called it.

"The whole valley system was one instrument," she said, the third evening, gesturing with her spoon at the canyon walls. "Walls, weirs, bell-towers, cut channels. Not decoration. Reading. The country told its own people where the water was, where the danger was, when to move. Your government burned the records and drowned the valleys, and they thought that erased it. But the country remembers. The country was built to remember." She pointed her spoon at Cyrus. "That's why your survey company wants the seal destroyed, girl. Not the metal. What the metal says. It says the drowning was legal theft, documented by the thieves themselves."

"I know what it says," Cyrus said. "I've read the documents in the chest."

"Have you." Sefa's eyebrows rose a fraction. "And what did you feel, reading them?"

"Nothing. They're paper."

"Liar," Sefa said, without heat, and went back to her cooking.

Cyrus lay awake a long time after that, listening to the river's ghost running somewhere under the sand, and hated how warm the fire had been, and how little she had minded the old woman's snoring.

* * *

On the fourth day Sefa saved their lives, and did it so quietly that neither of them understood until afterward.

They had come to a place where the canyon narrowed and the going looked easy—a broad gravel floor, smooth, inviting, the kind of corridor a tired body chooses without asking questions. Cyrus was already twenty paces into it when Sefa's staff cracked against a stone, once, sharp.

Both of them froze. Sefa said nothing. She simply stood at the entrance, looking at the gravel floor with an expression of profound distaste, the way another person might regard a rug they suspected hid a hole.

"Flash flood deposit," she said finally. "Fresh. See the scour line on the walls—two meters up, mud still damp at the base? Water came through here recently. Could be days. Could be hours. Upstream weather, not local. The sky above us is blue and the sky above the headwaters is doing something else entirely." She turned. "We climb. The floor lies."

They climbed the talus on the south side, slow and swearing, and camped that night on a ledge forty feet above the wash, and at some black hour before dawn Cyrus woke to a sound like a freight train made of water. The canyon below was full—churning, brown, tearing tamarisks out by the roots and rolling boulders the size of carts. It ran for perhaps an hour and subsided. Where they would have been walking, at the pace they'd have been making, was a wall of moving debris.

In the gray dawn light Ede looked down at it and then at Sefa, and said nothing, and Cyrus saw him file it away the way he filed everything—but differently now. Not as a curiosity. As a debt.

Cyrus herself said, "How did you know the sky above the headwaters was wrong? You can't see the headwaters."

"I can't," Sefa agreed. "But the seep we passed yesterday morning was running colder than the day before. Cold water comes off the high snowfields first, ahead of the storms that feed it. The river tells you what its sky is doing, if you ask." She was already packing her bedroll. "Your instruments tell you too, probably. Drones and gauges and satellites. But the instruments belong to people who would drown this valley twice if the paperwork allowed. The river belongs to whoever listens." She glanced at Cyrus, and for the first time there was something in the old woman's face besides imperiousness—something drier, almost kind. "You listen. Don't think I haven't noticed. You check the water, the wind, the light, before you check yourself. Whatever name you buried, girl, it was a ranger's name, and rangers listen. That part of you didn't burn."

Cyrus turned away and busied herself with the rope coils, and did not answer, and was aware, with a clarity she found deeply unwelcome, that she was glad the old woman was there.

* * *

They made the Forks on the sixth day, where the dead river divided into two dry arms climbing toward different ranges, and the country changed character around them—the red sandstone giving way to gray limestone, the walls steepening, the air carrying at night a new coldness that had teeth in it.

At the junction stood a pillar of stone older than any of it, a fin of rock the floods had spared, and it was carved. Rows upon rows of signs, worn shallow by centuries of weather and newer ones cut crisp over them, generations deep—a ledger written in stone. Ede stood before it and touched the marks one by one, mouthing sounds, and Sefa watched him with an expression she tried and failed to keep neutral.

"Left arm goes to the Bell Gorge," she said. "Three days' hard walking. Then the high country. Right arm is the survey road—graded, bridged, and crawling with company men. We take the left."

"Obviously," Ede said, and she laughed, a real laugh, rusty as an old gate, and cuffed him gently with the back of her hand like kin.

Cyrus stood apart, checking the backtrail out of habit, and found herself cataloguing the group without meaning to: the boy who carried a nation in a ring he barely remembered earning; the old woman who had spent forty-one years walking the circuits of a drowned country so that the country would have at least one witness; and herself, who had spent ten years ensuring that no witness existed for anything she'd done. Three travelers. Two of them impossible to lose and one of them professionally lost.

It should have felt like exposure. Instead, standing in the shadow of the carved pillar with the cold coming down off the high country, it felt—for one unguarded second—like a crew.

Then she swept the southern rim with her binoculars, and the feeling died in her chest.

Far off, at the limit of the glass, a dust plume rose from the right-hand arm of the Forks. Vehicle dust, moving fast, kicking high off the graded road. And above it, catching the afternoon sun, a glint that might have been a drone—or might have been nothing.

"Sefa," she said quietly. "Which way to the Bell Gorge. And how fast can an old woman walk?"
