## 9. Marsh at the Confluence

The river told them before Sefa did.

Cyrus heard it first as a change in the water's voice—a low, doubled note beneath the single thread of current they'd been following for two days. The canyon walls leaned back, and the light came down in a wider sheet, and the dry wash they'd been walking became a river again, shallow and quick over red stone, splitting around a bar of pale gravel where a smaller stream came in from the northeast through a slot of shadowed rock.

The confluence. Sefa had been calling it *the meeting* in her flat, unhurried way, as if the waters were old acquaintances. "We cross here," she'd said that morning, tapping the gravel bar with her staff. "North bank climbs. South bank is a dead end that pretends otherwise. The company will know that too, if they've read the old surveys."

Cyrus had read the old surveys. She'd read everything the service had on this drainage, which was almost nothing, which was the point. The blank places on the map were where she'd built her life.

Now she stood on the north bank with her binoculars up and felt the morning go cold in her chest.

There was dust on the south rim. Not the soft, drifting dust of weather—a column of it, disciplined, moving at vehicle pace along the old track that switchbacked down from the plateau. And below it, at the mouth of the southern slot, three figures on foot, spread in a line the way trained people spread when they were sweeping rather than searching.

"Down," she said, and didn't wait to see if they obeyed.

She took Ede by the collar and Sefa by the elbow and pulled them both behind a shelf of fallen rock the color of old blood. The river ran six feet away, loud enough to cover voices, loud enough to cover a mistake if they made one soon.

"How many?" Ede whispered. He'd gotten better at whispering in the last week. He'd gotten better at a lot of things. He still held the compass like it was a bird that might startle.

"Vehicle on the rim, three on foot, and I'd bet the vehicle isn't alone." Cyrus eased up until she could see through a crack in the rock. "That's a retrieval pattern. They're not chasing us anymore. They're herding us."

Sefa sat down on her heels with the unhurried gravity of a woman arranging herself for a long wait. "The meeting," she said. "Of course they'd come to the meeting. Everything in this country comes to a confluence eventually. Water. People. Trouble."

"They don't know we're here yet," Ede said.

"They know we're *in* the canyon," Cyrus said. "They've known since the pass. What they're doing is closing the exits and letting us walk into the middle of the net on our own feet, because it's cheaper than running us down." She kept her voice level and hated how level it was, how much it sounded like the ranger she used to be, briefing a team she no longer had. "There's a track on the north rim too. If they're smart—and Marsh is smart—there's a second vehicle up there, and we're standing in the one place in ten miles where both rims can see the bottom."

Sefa nodded slowly. "Then we don't stand in it."

Cyrus studied the water. The confluence ran fast over the gravel bar, knee-deep at the tail, deeper in the channel where the two currents argued. Cold water. Snowmelt from the high country, still weeks from the monsoon that would turn all of this into a brown roaring thing. On the far side, the north bank rose in broken terraces, and somewhere above the terraces the canyon forked again into country she knew only as contour lines.

"We go up the north bank," she said. "Fast, before the foot team crosses. If the rim vehicle is there, we'll hear it before we see it. If we're lucky, we're through the fork before they can bracket us."

"And if we're not lucky?" Ede asked.

"Then we're not lucky." She checked the water bottle at her hip, the rifle's action, the knife at her belt—the small liturgy of her old competence, the one thing about herself she had never tried to erase. "Sefa. Can you make that climb?"

Sefa looked at her with an expression that had been making Cyrus feel about twelve years old for three days. "I made that climb when the company that employs your friend Marsh was a rumor and the river ran the other way around this bend. Lead."

They went.

* * *

The north bank was a staircase the river had built and abandoned—gravel terraces stepping up through tamarisk and deadfall, each one crumbling at the lip. Cyrus took the lead, choosing footing, setting a pace that was fast without being reckless, the kind of pace that ate distance and left nothing behind. Ede followed in her prints. Sefa came last, using her staff, making no sound at all, which was its own kind of unsettling.

Two terraces up, Cyrus heard the engine.

It came as a vibration first, through the soles of her boots, then as sound—a high, clean whine quite unlike the ranger service's diesel equipment. She flattened against the terrace wall and looked up through a screen of tamarisk.

The vehicle was a survey company crawler, low-slung and pale gray, riding the north rim track on articulated wheels. Mounted on its back was a mast, and on the mast was a sensor array that swung slowly, patiently, like a head turning.

"It's scanning," Ede breathed.

"It's mapping thermal," Cyrus said. "Three bodies moving uphill in cold morning air. We're lit up like signal fires." Her mind ran the arithmetic she'd spent a decade not needing: distance to the fork, speed of the crawler, time for the foot team to cross the bar and come up their backtrail. The numbers did not come out well. The numbers had not come out well since the pass, and she'd kept moving anyway, because moving was the only verb she had left.

"Then we stop being three bodies," Sefa said.

Cyrus looked at her.

"The river," Sefa said, and pointed her chin downstream, back toward the confluence, where the two currents met and fought and threw up a standing wave of white noise. "Cold enough to kill the heat of you. Loud enough to kill the sound. The old people used to travel the water when the sky had eyes. That's what the river-signs at the last camp meant—the spiral with the bar through it. *Go by water when watched.*"

"The water's snowmelt," Cyrus said. "That's a hypothermia bath."

"Yes," Sefa said placidly. "It is."

Ede was already looking at the river with the expression of a man doing sums he didn't like. "How long?"

"Two hundred yards of current, then the bar on the south side, then the slot canyon where the foot team isn't, because the foot team is behind us." Sefa stood, staff in hand, and for a moment the age fell off her like a coat. "Or we stay here and are found in an hour. Choose."

Cyrus chose. It was the thing she was for, whatever else she had unmade. "Down to the water. Boots on, everything buttoned, rifle slung tight. Ede—compass inside your shirt, against your skin. If you go under, you let the current take you to the bar; don't fight it, it's smarter than you. Sefa and I will be on either side of you. We move on three."

She took one last look up through the tamarisk. The crawler's mast had stopped sweeping. It was pointed down into the canyon.

Pointed, she was almost sure, at them.

"Three," she said, and went over the terrace lip in a controlled slide of gravel, and the world became the cold.

* * *

The river hit her like a hand.

It found every seam in her clothing in the first three steps and filled them, and by the fifth step she was not walking through cold but *being* cold, a solid fact from collarbone to bootsole. The current took her weight and argued with her balance. She kept her rifle high, kept her eyes on the white water of the confluence ahead, and put her body between the current and Ede, because that was what the shape of her bones insisted on even now, even after ten years of practicing otherwise.

Sefa moved through it like a heron. Cyrus caught glimpses of her between the standing waves—staff planted, lean frame angled into the flow, unhurried, impossible.

Halfway across, the crawler spoke.

It wasn't a sound so much as a pressure—a flat electronic bleat that cut through the river's roar and landed on the water like a dropped stone. Then a voice, amplified, courteous, and horribly familiar.

*"Travelers on the water. This is Odile Marsh of the Continental Survey and Water Rights Consortium. You are not in trouble. You are in a river. There is a difference, and I'd like to discuss it with you."*

Ede's head came around, eyes wide, and the current shoved him a half-step off his line. Cyrus caught his pack strap and hauled him straight.

*"I know one of you is a ranger,"* the voice said, warm as a lamp. *"I know her name. Not the one on her forged permits—the real one. The one with the commendation and the incident report. I have it right here."*

The cold in Cyrus's body changed quality. It stopped being the river's cold and became her own, rising from somewhere under the sternum, and for three full steps she stopped being in the water at all and was back in a burning vehicle on a ridge she never named, hearing a radio call she never answered.

*"Walk out to the gravel bar,"* Marsh said. *"Dry off. Drink something warm. We'll talk like civilized people about a chest and a compass and a nation that, I'm assured by everyone official, does not exist."*

Sefa's staff cracked against stone beside Cyrus's hip. Cyrus looked down. The old woman had planted the staff and was pointing, once, downstream—*keep moving*—and her face held no fear at all, only a fierce, ancient contempt, the look of someone who had watched empires drown and had not been impressed.

Cyrus kept moving.

The last fifty yards were the worst, because the current shallowed and slowed and the crawler's voice followed them the whole way, patient, almost kind, repeating the offer in variations. *No one needs to be hurt. The company has no interest in refugees. The company has an interest in artifacts and in personnel who have gone off the books.* And then, as Cyrus's boots found the gravel of the south bar and her legs remembered how to be legs:

*"Ranger Quintana."*

The name hit her like a second immersion.

She hadn't heard it in ten years. She had buried it under two aliases and a forged service number and a decade of being no one in particular, and she had done it so well that sometimes, in the last years, she'd half-believed the erasure herself. Now a stranger said it over a canyon in a voice like poured cream, and every careful year came apart at the seam.

Ede was staring at her. Even waist-deep, even blue-lipped, he was staring at her. "Quintana," he said. "That's—"

"Keep moving," Cyrus said, and her voice came out as gravel, and she climbed the south bank with her hands shaking in a way the cold did not explain.

* * *

The slot canyon took them in like a wound closing.

It was narrow enough in places that Cyrus walked sideways, pack scraping both walls, the sky a jagged blue thread overhead. The water of the side stream ran ankle-deep and warmer here, fed by a spring, and the walls were streaked with old mineral stains—black, rust, bone-white—so that the stone seemed to be bleeding slowly upward. Vessian river-signs marked the turns: the spiral with the bar, the chevron that meant *high water path*, a small carved hand that Sefa touched as she passed, every time, without looking.

They climbed for an hour. When the walls finally opened into a shelf of slickrock with a view back down the side drainage, Cyrus made them stop, and made herself look back.

The confluence was a bright knot of water half a mile below and a thousand feet down. The crawler sat on the north rim like a pale beetle. The foot team had crossed the gravel bar—she could see them, three small figures, standing where the three of them had stood, examining the ground. One of them crouched. One of them held something up to the light.

Her stomach dropped before her mind caught up.

The credentials pack. She'd stripped it before the water—of course she had, you didn't take a service-issue pack into a river—and she'd stashed it in the rocks at the head of the bar, meaning to come back for it, meaning it the way you mean things when you believe you'll have the luxury of a return trip. Her forged identity papers were in it, the good ones, the ones that had survived three audits. And beneath them, in the waterproof sleeve she had never once in ten years let out of her reach—

Her real service record. Her real name. Her real number. The incident report, sealed, that she had copied the night she walked out of the service and had carried ever since the way other people carry a stone in the chest. She had told herself it was leverage. She had told herself it was evidence. She had never once opened it.

"Which one of them has it?" Ede asked quietly. He had come up beside her without her hearing, which frightened her more than the crawler had.

"Doesn't matter," Cyrus said.

"It's you, though." He wasn't accusing. That was the terrible thing. He was just standing there, wet and shivering and young, looking at her with those eyes that had watched a country drown. "Quintana. That's the name in the stories Sefa's circuit people tell. The ranger who went into the Vessian country before the drowning and came out alone." He paused. "The one who had a partner."

The wind came up the slot and cut through their wet clothes. Down at the bar, the figure with the raised object turned toward the rim, toward the crawler, and Cyrus knew with a certainty that needed no binoculars that the sleeve was open and the photograph was out and her face—the face she had before the beard of aliases and the gray in the hair, her face at twenty-eight, alive, whole—was being transmitted to a woman who collected other people's names the way her company collected water rights.

"I was going to tell you," Cyrus said, and heard how thin it was.

"No, you weren't," Ede said. "But you came back for us at the pass. And you got in the river. And you're here." He looked down at the tiny figures on the bar. "They can have the name. It's just a name. The person carrying it is up here."

Cyrus turned away because there was nothing in her face she wanted anyone to see, and what she found instead was Sefa, sitting on a flat stone with her boots off, rubbing her feet back to life, watching Cyrus with an expression that had been waiting for her.

"Now you understand," Sefa said.

"Understand what."

"Why we scattered. Why we hid. Why a whole nation agreed to become a rumor." Sefa pulled a sock back on, wincing. "They take your name and then they own the shape of you. Forty years ago they took a country's name off every map, and look what it bought them—a country they still have to chase. A compass they still have to steal. Old women they still have to hunt through canyons." She stood, and took her staff, and pointed up-canyon, toward the fork, toward the high country, toward the source. "You spent ten years erasing yourself, ranger. Now they've done it for you. There's nothing left to hide behind. There's only what you do next."

Cyrus looked at the slot canyon going up into the blue, at the thread of water that had carried her name down to the bar and given it away, at the two impossible people she had somehow accumulated like burrs on a coat.

The fear was still there. It would always be there; she was built that way. But somewhere in the last hour it had changed its chemistry, the way fear does at the exact moment when the worst thing has already happened and you are still standing. There was no invisible way out anymore. No alias to slip into, no blank on the map to dissolve into. Marsh had her name, her record, her face. Every door behind her was closed, and the strange thing, the thing she would think about for years, was how light she felt with all of them shut.

"Then we don't hide," Cyrus said. She shouldered her pack, and the motion felt different, unburdened, like setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying. "We go to the source. We go fast. And when Marsh catches up—"

"She'll catch up," Sefa said.

"—when she catches up, she finds out what's at the source." Cyrus looked at Ede, at the compass hanging against his chest under wet cloth, needle straining upstream even now. "And so do I. All of it. No more half-stories."

Ede held her gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and turned up-canyon, and the three of them started to climb.

Behind them, far below, the crawler's engine changed pitch—beginning, patiently, to move.
