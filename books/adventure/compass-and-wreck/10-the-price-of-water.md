## 10. The Price of Water

The river left them on the fourth morning above the confluence.

It didn't announce its departure. It simply thinned between one bend and the next, the current going glassy and slow over pale stone, and then there was no current at all—only a chain of shrinking pools strung through a wash of white rock like beads on a snapped cord. By noon the last pool was a hand's width deep and warm as bathwater, clouded with silt and the droppings of something that had drunk there in the night.

Cyrus crouched at its edge and did not drink.

"Endorheic," Sefa said behind her. The old woman had come up without a sound, the way she did everything, her walking staff clicking once against stone and then going quiet. "The basin closes. Everything that falls here stays here until the sun takes it back. The Vessians called this stretch *the Ledger*."

"The Ledger."

"Because it keeps accounts." Sefa's face was brown and seamed as old boot leather, and she gave nothing away with it. "Every drop you take, you owe. Every drop you waste, the country remembers. We are four days from the springs at Karst Teeth if we walk well. Three if we're lucky. Five if we're stupid." She looked at the three canteens hanging from Cyrus's pack, then at Ede, who was downstream kneeling at the pool with his hands cupped, about to drink without thinking. "Boy. Not yet."

Ede froze, water trembling in his palms. He was twenty-three and built like a whipstock, all tendon and stubbornness, and he had been carrying the seal ring against his sternum for seventeen years without once letting anyone see him touch it. He looked to Cyrus now—the way he still did, though less often than before—and she hated that look, because it asked her to be the kind of person who made good decisions for other people, and she had spent ten years being the kind who made fast ones instead.

"Filter first," she said. "Then two swallows each. We measure everything from here."

She saw the calculation move behind his eyes, quick and involuntary: how much, divided by three, by four days, by the heat. She'd done the same math an hour ago and hadn't liked the answer either.

* * *

They walked the Ledger through the heat of the day because there was no shade worth stopping for—a single juniper every mile or so, hunched and gray, casting shadows the size of a spread hand. The country opened around them into a high tableland of rust and bone-white rock, cut by dry channels that braided and rejoined like argumentative rivers of sand. The sky was enormous and offered nothing. Heat came up off the stone in visible waves, so that the horizon shimmered and the distant buttes seemed to swim.

Cyrus set the pace. She always set the pace; it was the one authority she'd never surrendered, not to the service, not to anyone. Two miles, five-minute rest. Two miles, five minutes. She counted steps without meaning to—an old habit, a ranger's metronome—and kept the count running under everything else like a second heartbeat.

Sefa walked beside her in the afternoon and spoke without preamble, which was her way.

"You've already decided who drinks last."

Cyrus didn't answer.

"I've watched you ration for two days. You give yourself half measures and call it discipline. You gave the boy your salt tab yesterday when he cramped and told him it was extra." Sefa's staff clicked. Clicked. "You think I don't see. I walked these circuits for thirty years. I know what a woman looks like when she's spending herself down to make the arithmetic kinder for everyone else."

"It's not kindness. It's logistics."

"No," Sefa said. "Logistics is what you tell yourself. What you're doing is paying. You pay in yourself because it's cheaper than paying in other people. But there will come a point on this stretch where the math doesn't close, and you'll have to decide what you're willing to spend instead." She turned her head, and her eyes were pale as weathered bone. "I'm telling you now so you can't pretend later that you didn't know the choice was coming."

"What choice?"

Sefa only smiled, thin as a crack in glaze, and dropped back to walk with Ede.

* * *

The first night they camped in the lee of a fallen slab, and Cyrus measured out the water with the deliberation of a priest dispensing sacrament. Four hundred milliliters each, drunk slowly, in sips held in the mouth. She watched Ede drain his too fast and saw him catch himself, ashamed, and she said nothing, because shame was a better teacher than anything she could say.

The second day the wind rose out of the south and brought the temperature up with it, a dry furnace breath that pulled moisture from their lips and the corners of their eyes. They walked with scarves over their faces. The pools were gone entirely now; the Ledger was pure arithmetic, rock and distance and the declining weight of the canteens.

It was mid-afternoon when Ede stumbled.

Not badly—a toe caught on a lip of stone, a lurch, a recovery. But Cyrus had spent a decade reading bodies in bad country, and she read his in one glance: the slight widening of his stance, the way his head stayed down a beat too long, the shine of sweat that wasn't sweat anymore but the body's last honest accounting. He was past thirsty. He was entering the country where people start making mistakes, and mistakes out here were paid for in full.

She called the rest early. They sat in the meager shadow of a boulder, and she took stock openly, because hiding the numbers would be worse.

"Two canteens and a third gone soft," she said. "Call it three liters total. Springs are—" she glanced at Sefa.

"Tomorrow night, if we push. Day after, comfortably."

"Then tomorrow night." She worked it through aloud, because Ede deserved to hear the math even if he wouldn't like it. "Three liters, three people, thirty hours. A liter each gets us there if nothing goes wrong."

"And if something goes wrong?" Ede asked.

His voice was flat. Not accusatory—not yet—but she heard the question underneath it, the one he was really asking: *and if one of us goes wrong?*

"Then we deal with it," she said.

He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him decide not to say whatever was rising in him. He shouldered his pack and walked on.

* * *

Something went wrong an hour before dawn.

Cyrus woke to silence—which was wrong, because Sefa snored like a landslide and had complained for two days that neither of her young companions appreciated the music—and found the old woman sitting upright against the slab, both hands pressed flat to her belly, breathing in small careful sips.

"Cramps," Sefa said before Cyrus could ask. "Since midnight. It's nothing."

It was not nothing. Cyrus got the tarp back and the lantern lit and saw the gray in Sefa's lips, the fine tremor in her hands. Dehydration stacking on top of seventy-one years and a body that had spent decades walking a drowned country out of grief. Cyrus gave her a full half-liter, breaking her own rationing law without hesitation, and sat watching the old woman's color come back by degrees while the stars wheeled overhead and Ede slept on, exhausted past waking.

And in that watching, coldly, precisely, the way she did everything, Cyrus ran the numbers again.

Three liters minus the half-liter given. Twenty-six hours to the springs. Sefa's pace would drop—had already dropped; she'd hidden it well, but Cyrus had seen the shortened stride, the rests taken standing up so no one would notice. If Sefa slowed them past tomorrow night, they'd be walking the last hours of the Ledger in the dark on empty canteens. If Sefa went down entirely—and at her age, in this heat, that was not a remote possibility—someone would have to stay with her, and someone would have to go on, and the water would not divide three ways among two destinations.

The arithmetic closed one way only, and Cyrus had known it would since Sefa had named the choice on the first day. One person could carry enough water for a fast push to the springs and back: herself. She was the strongest, the most acclimated, the one whose body cost the least per mile. She could leave the others water, go hard through the night, reach Karst Teeth, fill everything, and return inside eighteen hours. It was clean. It was survivable. It was—

It was also, she realized, sitting in the dark with the lantern turned low, not the plan forming in her mind at all. The plan forming in her mind was older and simpler and wore her own voice: *if Sefa fails on the trail tomorrow, you leave her. You mark the spot, you take the boy, you keep moving. Water is life and life is arithmetic and you have done this before.*

She had done it before. That was the thing. There was a canyon in her service record—or in the record that used to exist—that had taught her exactly what she was, and she had spent ten years walking away from the lesson without ever once disproving it.

* * *

She told Ede at first light, while Sefa dozed.

Not the whole truth. She told him the operational part: Sefa's condition, the revised plan, the possibility that they might need to split the party. She laid it out in ranger's cadence, calm and complete, and she watched his face change as he understood what she was actually saying underneath.

"You'd leave her." His voice was very quiet. "Out here. Alone. With a quarter-liter and a prayer."

"If she can't walk, staying won't save her. It'll just make it three graves instead of one."

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "That's not arithmetic. That's just you deciding some people are costs."

"Ede—"

"No." He was on his feet now, and his exhaustion had burned off into something hotter. "I've watched you for two weeks. You're the best tracker I've ever seen, you saved my life twice, and you would put a bullet in any one of us the moment we became inconvenient, and you'd feel terrible about it afterward, and you'd do it again anyway. Because that's what you are. You survive. It's the only thing you know how to do."

The words landed where they were aimed. She let them.

"You don't know what you're talking about," she said finally, and heard how thin it sounded.

"Don't I? You know what I am? I'm the one they saved instead of the ones they couldn't. My mother put me on a boat and stayed on the dock. Somebody made your exact calculation the night Vessia drowned, Cyrus—somebody with a clipboard decided who fit in the boats—and I've spent my whole life being the remainder." His hands were shaking, and he clenched them. "So don't stand there and tell me it's just math. Math is what cowards call their choices when they don't want to own them."

Behind him, Sefa had woken. Neither of them had noticed. She was sitting up against the slab with her staff across her knees, watching them with those bone-pale eyes, and when Cyrus met her gaze the old woman said nothing at all—only looked at her the way you look at a wound you've been expecting.

"We move in ten minutes," Cyrus said. "All of us. Together, as far as she can go."

Ede stared at her. "You just said—"

"I changed the plan." She was already rolling the tarp, not looking at either of them, because looking at them would mean showing them what the changing had cost. "We stay together. If she goes down, we carry her. And if carrying her kills us, then it kills us, and at least the ledger will show we paid our debts." She cinched the strap tight. "Ten minutes."

* * *

Sefa saved them at noon.

They had walked four hours on will and short rations, the sun climbing toward its murder, when the old woman stopped at a place that looked identical to every place they'd passed for a day and a half—a shallow pan of white stone, heat-shimmered, featureless—and said, "Here. Dig."

"Dig where?" Ede asked. "There's nothing here."

"There is always something here. That is the entire point of my country." She planted her staff and leaned on it. "The Vessians didn't just build dams and drown valleys. We made promises to the desert. Every dry crossing on the old circuits has a cache—a sealed jar, buried below the caliche line, marked so only river-people could read it. Look at the stone. Really look."

Cyrus knelt. And slowly, the way a face comes out of a crowd, she saw it: a pattern in the stones, too regular for chance, a spiral of darker cobbles winding inward to a center point two arm-spans away. River-signs. Ede had taught her six of them in the canyons, carved in walls a century dead, and here was the seventh, written in the land itself, legible only to someone who knew the grammar.

They dug with hands and the pan of a pot. Eighteen inches down the soil turned to a hard mineral crust, and below the crust, intact after forty years or more, wrapped in waxed cloth and pitch: a ceramic jar, cool as a grave, holding four liters of water and a strip of oiled leather on which someone long dead had written, in a neat Vessian hand, *Drink and remember us.*

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Ede made a sound that was half laugh and half sob, and Sefa said, softly, in her own language, something that sounded like a thank-you addressed to no one living.

Cyrus drank her share slowly, holding each mouthful, tasting dust and faint mineral and forty years of patience. *Drink and remember us.* Whoever had buried this jar had done it knowing they'd likely never drink from it themselves. Knowing it was a gift sent forward through time to strangers. To her.

The arithmetic, she thought, had never accounted for this. There was no column in her ledger for what people gave away.

* * *

They reached the springs at Karst Teeth an hour after dark, guided the last miles by Sefa singing under her breath—an old route-song, she said, that mapped the final bends in its melody—and fell upon the seeps in the black shadow of the limestone fins like animals. Cyrus filled every container they owned, treated, filtered, measured, and then, last of all, drank her fill, the cold water aching down her throat like something swallowed sideways.

Ede sat apart on a rock, feet in the shallows of the spring pool, and did not look at her.

She went to him anyway. Stood a few paces off, water dripping from her chin.

"For what it's worth," she said, "you were right. About the math. About me."

He didn't turn. "I know I was right. That's not the same as wanting to be."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

Silence, except the small music of the seep. Above them the Teeth stood up against the stars, jagged and pale, and beyond them, higher still, the country climbed toward the source and the storms gathering somewhere past the edge of sight. She could smell it coming—rain on far air, a green metallic ghost of a scent, days away yet but unmistakable. The monsoon, early. Racing them.

"Ede." She waited until he glanced back, his face half-lit by starlight, guarded still. "There's a town past the Teeth. Checkpoint town—company territory, survey roads, a magistrate's post. We need supplies we can't carry and a map of the high trails we don't have. It's a risk."

"How big a risk?"

She hesitated. And in the hesitation she admitted to herself what she'd been refusing to admit since the confluence, since Marsh's drones had found them, since the file with her real name had gone into company hands: the risk wasn't the company. The risk was that in that town, posted on a board or displayed on a terminal, her true record would be waiting—her name, her service history, the mission she'd abandoned, the partner she hadn't carried out. Everything she'd spent ten years burying, hung up in public like a skin nailed to a barn door.

"Cyrus," Ede said. "How big?"

"Big," she said. "Bigger than water."
