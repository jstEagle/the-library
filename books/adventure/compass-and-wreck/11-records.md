## 11. Records

The town was called Ferrous Junction on the survey maps and nothing at all on the ranger maps, which suited everyone who lived there. It sat where two haul roads crossed a dead rail spur, a scatter of tin roofs and diesel generators in the shadow of a company water tower, and it existed for one purpose: to be passed through. Trucks came down from the northern claims heavy with ore and went back up empty. Nobody stayed longer than a fuel stop unless they had a reason to disappear, and the town made its quiet living off people with reasons.

Cyrus had used it twice before, years apart, both times at night, both times paying cash to a fuel man who asked no questions because questions were bad for business. She'd meant to do the same again. In and out before dawn, water cans filled, batteries swapped, gone.

But Ede needed boots, and Sefa's knee had swollen to the size of a grapefruit somewhere in the last canyon stretch, and the monsoon's leading edge had turned the sky over the high country the color of a bruise going bad. They had perhaps four days before the storms sealed the passes. Sefa said three. Sefa had been saying three for two days with the flat certainty of a woman who had walked these circuits for forty years and did not need a barometer.

So they came in at dusk instead of midnight, hooded and dusty, three travelers among twenty, and that was the first mistake. The second was the notice board.

It stood outside the company commissary under a caged floodlight, a sheet of galvanized iron bolted to posts, layered with paper the way such boards always were—haul schedules, wage notices, a missing-prospectors flyer gone gray in the sun. Cyrus had seen ten thousand of them. She glanced at it the way you glance at anything in a town you intend to leave.

Her own face looked back at her.

Not the face she wore now—the cropped hair, the dust-cured skin, the name she'd worn like a coat for a decade—but her service portrait, eleven years old, taken in a ranger depot she could still smell in her memory: gun oil and coffee and the particular staleness of government air conditioning. Younger. Softer around the mouth. The kind of face that still believed the uniform meant something.

Below it, in the survey company's clean corporate typeface:

**QUINTANA, CYRUS M. — FORMERLY RANGER SERVICE, DISTRICT SEVEN. STATUS: DESERTED, ACTIVE INVESTIGATION. REWARD FOR VERIFIED IDENTIFICATION OR DETENTION: 40,000 SCRIPT. SUBJECT IS ARMED AND CONSIDERED DANGEROUS. REPORT TO ANY SURVEY CHECKPOINT.**

And beneath that, because the company wanted its money's worth from one sheet of paper, the summary of her file. Her real file. The one that had lived in a locked drawer in a district office she'd spent ten years making sure never thought about her.

*Subject deserted post during active search operation, Dry Fork drainage. Partner Ranger T. Okafor deceased. Subject failed to render aid. Subject subsequently falsified separation records and assumed false identity.*

The words hadn't changed. Of course they hadn't. Words like that didn't change; they just waited, patient as silt, for someone to dredge them up. But seeing them printed in company type, pinned under a floodlight for truckers and prospectors to read while they waited for their fuel—seeing them made *public*, made *merchandise*—did something no amount of preparation had done. Her body reacted before her mind could govern it. Heat up the throat. Hands going cold and precise, the old ranger reflex, threat assessment running without her permission: sightlines, exits, who was standing near the board, who was reading.

Nobody was reading. A couple of haul drivers argued about tire chains by the pumps. The floodlight buzzed. Her own young face stared out of the paper, accusing her of exactly what she'd done.

"Cyrus." Ede's voice, low, behind her left shoulder. "We should get the water and go."

She didn't move. There was a strange gravity in the page. Ten years of careful work—burned pay stubs, forged separations, a new name built syllable by syllable until even she answered to it without flinching—and all of it undone by one sheet of paper and forty thousand script. Marsh hadn't needed to hunt her. She'd only needed to make her legible. Pin her to a board in a town like this and let the whole desert read her aloud.

"They put up the Okafor part," Cyrus heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away, level, ranger-flat. "They don't usually put that part up. Desertion's a civil matter. Dead partner makes it worth reading."

Ede stepped up beside her. She should have stopped him. Some part of her registered that clearly—that the correct move was to turn him away from the board, steer him toward the fuel shed, keep this the way she'd kept everything, private, buried, hers. But her feet wouldn't take the step, and some colder, more honest part of her knew why. Because he was going to find out eventually. Because Sefa already watched her with eyes that missed nothing. Because the family she hadn't agreed to join was going to see the worst of her sooner or later, and some exhausted corner of her soul was tired of carrying it alone.

He read it. All of it. She stood beside him and felt him read it, the way you feel weather change.

*Partner Ranger T. Okafor deceased. Subject failed to render aid.*

His jaw worked once. His hands, scarred from the sandwash, hung loose at his sides. He was twenty-six years old and he had been carried out of a drowning country in a smuggler's bilge hold at six, and he knew something about being the one who lived.

He said nothing.

That was the worst of it. Not anger—anger she could have handled, anger was a currency she understood, you paid it and moved on. Not fear, not disgust, not the slow recalculation of a man deciding whether the woman with the rifle was worth walking beside. Nothing. He just finished reading, looked at the portrait of her younger self for one more second, and then looked at the actual her, standing in the floodlight's spill with dust in her eyelashes, and his face did the thing faces do when they close a door gently rather than slam it.

"Water," he said again, quietly. "Before the fuel man closes."

He walked toward the pumps. After a moment, she followed.

* * *

Sefa had claimed a bench against the commissary wall, foot propped on a crate, working the swelling in her knee with thumbs that knew every tendon in the human body the way a locksmith knows pins. She took one look at Cyrus's face and then at Ede's, and said nothing either, which from Sefa was its own kind of verdict. She simply shifted down the bench to make room, which was not forgiveness but was not exile, and in Sefa's grammar that gap between the two was where you lived while she decided.

Cyrus sat. The tin wall was warm at her back from the day's heat. Across the yard, the notice board glowed under its cage like an altar to a god she'd spent a decade trying not to worship: the old self, the record, the name.

"I should have told you," she said finally. To neither of them. To the dust.

"You should have," Sefa agreed, not unkindly, kneading her knee. "Ten years ago. To yourself, if no one else."

"It's not—" Cyrus started, and stopped, because the sentence available to her was *it's not what it looks like*, and that was a lie, and lies were how she'd gotten here. She tried again. "The operation. Dry Fork. We were looking for a missing survey crew, two rangers, nine days out. On day six we found them. Or what was left. And Okafor went down into a slot canyon after a survivor signal, and I—" Her hands opened on her knees, empty. "I was on the rim. The radio said the walls were moving. Flash flood season. Every protocol says you hold the rim, you don't go down, you wait for the water to pass. I held the rim."

"And?" Sefa said. Not cruel. Just waiting, the way the desert waits.

"And the water came early. And I stood on the rim and listened to it happen, because going down would have killed me too, and the protocols were right, and none of that has ever mattered even once at three in the morning for ten years." She heard her own voice go dry and mechanical, the briefing voice, and hated it, and couldn't stop it. "I reported it straight. I filed everything straight. And then I couldn't—I couldn't be the ranger who did that correctly. Who did the right thing and let it be right. So I unmade myself instead. New name. New records. A decade of being no one, because no one can't fail anyone."

The generators hummed. Somewhere a truck door slammed. Ede sat on the far end of the bench, elbows on his knees, looking at the ground between his boots.

"My mother pushed me into a boat," he said at last, still not looking up. "I've told you. Six years old. There wasn't room for my sister. My mother chose me. Do you understand what that means, that I'm alive? It means my sister's name is a hole in every record of our family. It means I carry a ring for a country that exists now only in the heads of scattered old people, and half of them are wrong about details, and every year fewer of them remember, and someday it will all be holes." He looked up then, and his eyes in the floodlight's edge were very clear. "You erased yourself so you couldn't hurt anyone. I was erased so I could live. It's not the same thing. But I know what it is to be a person made of gaps."

Cyrus's throat closed. She had expected—she didn't know what she'd expected. Condemnation. Pity. Both were easier than this: being met, precisely, at the exact depth of the wound, by a boy she'd pulled out of the silt a week ago.

"Forty thousand script," Sefa said into the silence, practical as gravel. "That's a year's wages for every driver in this yard. By morning, half this town will know your face is worth that. We cannot come back through here. We cannot use the southern road—they'll have checkpoints on it inside two days, the company moves fast when there's a bounty." She worked her knee once more, grimaced, and stood, testing her weight on it with the expression of a woman negotiating with her own body and driving a hard bargain. "Which means the gorge."

"The gorge is flooded country," Cyrus said automatically. "When the monsoon breaks—"

"The gorge is the only way north that doesn't have a road in it," Sefa said. "And the bells are in the gorge. And the bells are Vessian, and whatever else your company understands, it does not understand the bells." She picked up her stick. "Sleep in shifts. We move at first light. And Cyrus." She paused, and for a moment the old woman's face was not prickly or remote but simply ancient, carved by forty years of circuits through a drowned nation's bones. "The file is true. That's what makes it a wound. But a file is not a person. Even theirs."

She went to find a wall to sleep against. Ede rose too, then hesitated, and did the thing that undid Cyrus more thoroughly than any accusation could have: he took the water detail, both five-gallon cans, without being asked, because her hands were still not entirely steady, and he pretended not to notice that, and she pretended not to notice him pretending.

At the corner of the commissary he stopped. Didn't turn around.

"Okafor," he said. "Was that their real name?"

"Yes."

"Then they're not a line in your file to me. They're a person whose partner is walking us to sanctuary." A beat. "That's all I'm going to say about it. Get some sleep."

He went. Cyrus sat alone on the bench under the buzzing light with the whole dark yard in front of her and her own face glowing on the board across the way, and understood that something had shifted and could not shift back. They had seen it now. The worst of her, printed and posted and priced. And they had not left. Which meant that from tonight forward, every step she walked beside them, she would walk knowing they knew—and that the knowing had not been enough to make them go.

That was more frightening than the bounty. She'd survived being hunted before. Being *kept* was new.

* * *

She slept two hours against the tin wall with her rifle across her knees, dreamed of rising water and woke with her hand already on the stock, and lay listening to the pre-dawn yard: wind in the power lines, the tick of cooling engines, and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound she had spent a decade training her ears for.

Engines. More than one. Coming down the northern haul road at speed, at night, running dark.

She was on her feet before the thought finished forming. From the shadow of the fuel shed, Ede was already awake, watching her, and beyond him Sefa stood leaning on her stick with her head cocked toward the road like a bird, and her face in the starlight had gone very still.

"That's not freight," Sefa said quietly. "Freight doesn't run dark."

Headlights bloomed at the edge of town, sweeping the commissary wall, the water tower, the notice board with its forty-thousand-script promise. Three vehicles. Maybe four. Cyrus counted doors, counted men silhouetted against the cab lights, and saw, stepping down from the lead vehicle with unhurried courtesy, a figure in a pale field coat who consulted a folded paper and then looked directly, across the whole dark yard, at the bench where Cyrus had slept.

Odile Marsh had not needed the bounty to find her.

She had needed it to make sure Cyrus had nowhere left to run but north—into the gorge, toward the bells, toward the source.

"Back door," Cyrus breathed. "Now. The way we came in. Don't run until the buildings break your outline."

Behind them, the floodlight over the notice board flickered once, and went out.
