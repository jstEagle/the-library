## 12. The Gorge of Bells

Sefa woke them an hour before dawn by throwing a pebble at Cyrus's boot.

"Up," she said. "Today we walk through the throat of the old country, and I will not do it in the dark with strangers who trip."

Cyrus was already sitting up, rolling her bedroll with the economy of ten thousand mornings. "You could just say good morning."

"I could say many things." Sefa was already moving, her pack settled on her back like a growth she'd stopped noticing decades ago. She was seventy if she was a day, built like a fence post left in the wind, and she had been following them for two days before Ede spotted her, and she had joined them without asking because, as she put it, they were walking her road wrong and it offended her. "Eat. The gorge has no kindness in it for the slow."

Ede was already awake. He'd barely slept since the confluence — since Marsh's team had come out of the side canyon at dusk and Cyrus had taken a graze across the forearm and dropped her credentials pack into the water rather than let it be taken off her body, which meant that somewhere downstream, wrapped in oilcloth and sinking into silt, lay her real name, her real service number, her real record, all of it now company property. He hadn't said much about it. He'd cleaned and bound her arm with hands that shook only after he finished, when he thought she wasn't looking.

They climbed out of the wash as the sky went from iron to ash to a thin, reluctant gold. Ahead, the land folded itself into a cleft between two walls of red sandstone, narrow enough that Cyrus could have touched both sides with a spear. Sefa stopped at its mouth and stood there a long moment, head bowed, and Cyrus thought at first she was praying.

She wasn't. She was listening.

"You'll hear them before you see them," Sefa said. "When the wind comes down the gorge. Don't startle. They're only bells."

* * *

The bells began a mile in.

At first Cyrus took them for rock chimes — the random percussion of wind on stone — but the sound was too sweet for that, too deliberate. Bronze, she decided. Old bronze, the kind that goes dark green and soft-voiced with age. They came around a bend and she stopped walking.

They hung everywhere.

From iron brackets driven into the cliff face. From natural stone shelves. From crooked gallows-poles of juniper that some patient hand had planted in the canyon floor a lifetime ago and that had grown up around the bells instead of away from them. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Small ones no bigger than a thumb, great dished ones that would take two men to lift, all of them green with forty years of weather, all of them silent until the wind moved, and then not silent at all. The sound rolled down the gorge ahead of them and behind them, layered and overlapping, so that walking through it was like walking through weather made of memory.

"What is this place," Cyrus said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, as if the gorge demanded quiet.

"A waymark," Sefa said. "After the drowning, when the survivors scattered, they couldn't put names on maps anymore. Maps were burned. So they put the names here instead." She reached up and touched one of the small bells — a hand's-breadth thing on a leather thong gone black with age — and did not ring it. "Every family that lost someone in the flooding walked this gorge. Every family hung a bell. You listen for your own name in the wind. Some people walked this canyon for years before they heard it."

Ede had gone very still. He stood in the middle of the path with his head tipped back, and the light came down the cleft in long dusty shafts, and the bells spoke over him in their slow bronze language, and Cyrus watched a nineteen-year-old boy stand inside the sound of everything he'd lost.

"My mother talked about this," he said. "I thought — I thought she was making it up. A story to keep me quiet on the boats." His voice cracked on *boats*. "There were bells?"

"There are always bells," Sefa said. "People who lose a country need somewhere to hang the grief where the wind can get at it. Otherwise you carry it whole, and it crushes you." She looked sideways at Cyrus when she said it, and Cyrus pretended not to notice, and failed.

They walked on. Sefa led them past the bells without ringing any of them — "you don't ring another family's bell; that's their sorrow, not yours" — but she read them, murmuring under her breath, translating the stamped inscriptions for Ede: *Vessia remembers. The river takes, the river keeps. For Anseli, who loved the high water.* Names, dates, small fierce messages scratched into bronze by people who knew the nation they were mourning had been officially declared never to have existed. Forty years of erasure, and here was a canyon full of evidence, hanging in plain air, singing.

Cyrus found herself thinking about records. About the depot file she'd spent a decade thinning, the service entries she'd had quietly amended, the name changes and address ghosts and the careful cultivation of being nobody in particular. She had built her invisibility the way these people had built their bells — one piece at a time, patiently, out of loss. The difference was that theirs sang and hers didn't say anything at all.

Halfway through the gorge, Sefa stopped at a bell unlike the others. It was large, waist-high, mounted on a stone plinth in the center of the path, and it bore no inscription except a single line in Vessian script that Sefa read aloud with her hand flat against the metal:

"*Whoever passes, carry us.*"

"That one's for travelers," she said. "For the ones going to the source. You ring it when you go up, so the dead know you're coming, and you ring it again when you come down, so they know you made it back." She stepped aside. "Ede."

Ede looked at the bell. Then at her. Then, briefly, at Cyrus — asking something with his eyes that Cyrus couldn't answer and didn't want to try.

He struck it with the heel of his hand. The note went up the gorge like a bird released, and every other bell seemed to lean toward it, sympathetic harmonics shivering down the canyon walls, and for three or four seconds the whole drowned nation rang at once.

Cyrus's eyes stung. She blamed the dust.

* * *

It was Ede who heard the drones.

He froze mid-stride, one hand up, and Cyrus followed his gaze to the strip of pale sky above the cleft: two specks, high and patient, riding the thermals. Survey-company frames. They'd seen them before, distant, ambiguous. These weren't distant. These were working a grid.

"Sefa," Cyrus breathed. "How far to the far end?"

"Two hours. There's a scramble at the end, then open ground to the switchbacks." Sefa hadn't raised her voice at all. "They can't bring vehicles in here. Too narrow. If they come, they come on foot, from both ends."

"Then we move fast and quiet—"

"No." Sefa's hand closed on Cyrus's sleeve, and her grip was stronger than her age suggested. "Listen. In this gorge, running makes noise. Noise rings the bells. The bells tell them exactly where we are." She pointed up at the thousands of bronze throats hanging silent around them. "This canyon is an instrument. We have to cross it like thieves in a house full of glass."

So they crossed it that way — slow, single-file, placing each footfall like a debt being repaid, while above them the drones ticked back and forth across the strip of sky. Twice the wind rose and set the bells whispering, and twice Cyrus's heart slammed against her ribs until the sound died away. The gorge stretched. The light crawled. Somewhere behind them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots on stone.

Not theirs.

"They've entered the near end," Sefa said quietly. "Four, maybe five. Moving fast. They know the drones can't hear anything over the bells either — that cuts both ways." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Old country protects its own."

"They're not country," Cyrus said. "They're a retrieval team with a warrant and a helicopter on standby. And they're between us and the exit."

They came around a long bend where the gorge widened into a chamber — a natural amphitheater, walls rising sheer on three sides, bells dense as fruit on every surface — and Sefa stopped so abruptly that Ede walked into her back.

At the far side of the chamber, where the canyon narrowed again toward the exit, four figures stood in the shade. Dark company field gear, no insignia, weapons slung low and casual. And in front of them, hands clasped behind her back like a woman admiring architecture, stood Odile Marsh.

"Cyrus Quintana," Marsh called, and the name went up the walls and came back down again, ringing off the bells, spoken by a thousand bronze mouths. "Or should I say Ranger Quintana? We recovered your credentials pack from the confluence. Fascinating reading. You've been very busy with paperwork over the years."

Cyrus's hand found the pistol at her hip. Four guns in the narrows. Maybe more behind them. Behind her and Ede, the way they'd come, the second team would be closing.

"Odile," Cyrus called back. "You're a long way from a desk."

"I'm a long way from a lot of things." Marsh took a step forward, unhurried. "Here's the shape of the afternoon. You give me the compass and the seal, and the woman and the boy walk out of this canyon free. You're not part of my mandate, Ranger. You're just a complication with a gun."

"She's not a complication," Ede said. His voice was steady. His hand, at his side, was not. "And the seal isn't mine to give."

"It isn't yours at all," Marsh said gently. "That's rather my point."

* * *

Later — in the weeks after, in the nights after, in the ledger of things Cyrus Quintana could not forgive herself — she would replay what happened next a hundred times and understand it fully only once.

Sefa had drifted, during the talking. Drifted back along the wall, unhurried, an old woman shuffling among the bells, beneath everyone's notice. That was the first thing. The second thing was that her hand had never left the largest of the small bells near her shoulder — a cracked old thing on a rusted bracket, positioned directly beneath a shelf of stacked boulders that some ancient flood had wedged against the cliff face.

The third thing was that Sefa had spent forty years walking this country, and knew exactly which bell, rung hard enough, carried farthest.

"Boy," she said, in Vessian, and Ede later translated it for Cyrus word by word, because Cyrus demanded every word: *"Take the compass and run up the throat. Do not stop. Do not look back. This is my gorge and my bell and my choice, and I have been waiting forty years for a reason to ring it."*

"Sefa—" Ede started.

She hit the bell with everything she had.

The crack in it gave the note a jagged edge, and the note went up the chamber wall and struck the boulder shelf and the shelf, old and balanced and waiting forty years for exactly this, shifted. Not fell — shifted, groaned, and began to shed stones, and the sound of it was thunder in a canyon built to amplify thunder, and every bell in the Gorge of Bells answered at once.

The world became noise.

Cyrus grabbed Ede's collar and ran. Stones came down the far wall where Marsh's team stood; the team scattered; Marsh's voice cut through the din shouting orders that the bells ate alive. Behind them the whole gorge was ringing — thousands of bells, a nation's grief shaken loose all at once, deafening, glorious, terrible — and under it, fainter than anything, Cyrus heard Sefa's voice, clear and furious and unafraid, shouting something else in Vessian that Ede refused ever afterward to translate.

They ran up the throat of the gorge with the dust boiling around them and the bells screaming overhead, and Cyrus kept hold of Ede's sleeve because letting go was unthinkable, and the exit narrowed, and narrowed, and then they were through — scrambling up the far rampart into open ground and gray daylight and wind — and Cyrus turned, gasping, to cover the old woman's retreat.

No one came out.

The ringing faltered, section by section, as the wind died. The last note hung in the air and faded the way all bronze fades, slowly and then completely. From deep in the gorge, muffled by stone, came voices — several, organized, unhurried. Then silence.

"They have her." Ede was on his knees at the rim, staring back down into the cleft. His face had gone the color of the dust. "They have her and I ran. She told me to run and I *ran*—"

"You ran because she bought it with the only coin she had." Cyrus's voice came out flat and cold and she hated it, hated how steady it was, hated that ten years of erasing herself had also erased whatever part of a person is allowed to shake at a time like this. Her hands, though. Her hands were shaking. She looked at them as if they belonged to someone else. "That's the arithmetic. One member at a time. That's what this family costs."

Ede looked up at her, and there was something in his face worse than grief — accusation, or the raw material of it, still soft. "You could have gone back."

"Yes," Cyrus said. "I could have."

Below them, at the mouth of the gorge, a figure emerged into the open ground. Small, erect, hands still clasped behind her back. Odile Marsh looked up the slope directly at where they crouched — too far to see clearly, close enough to be certain — and raised one hand, not in threat but in acknowledgment, almost courtesy. Then she turned and went back into the dark of the bells, taking Sefa with her.

The wind came up the valley and touched the nearest bells at the gorge rim, and they rang, softly, once — a sound like a question asked in a language Cyrus was only beginning to learn.

Ede stood. He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, and when he lowered it his expression had hardened into something Cyrus recognized from mirrors: the look of a person deciding what they are willing to become.

"The camp," he said. "Marsh's retrieval camp, down-valley. Three days' walk."

"We're two people with one pistol."

"Then we'd better be clever." He turned east, toward the storm-light building over the high country, and started walking. "We're getting her back."

Cyrus stood a moment longer at the rim, listening to the bells settle, feeling the weight of the compass in her jacket like a second heartbeat. Then she followed him down into the gathering dark.
