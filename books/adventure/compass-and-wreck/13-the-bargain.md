## 13. The Bargain

The note came with the morning wind, weighted under a stone at the edge of their camp, and Cyrus knew before she unfolded it whose handwriting it would be. Odile Marsh wrote the way she spoke—courteous, precise, each letter placed like a knife set on a white tablecloth.

*Ms. Quintana. Or shall I say Ranger Quintana, badge 4-1-9-2? We have your colleague. She is comfortable, well fed, and asking after you with more spirit than sense. We also have your file, all forty pages of it, including the incident at Kessel Wash. I am authorized to exchange: the compass and the seal for the woman and your records, no questions asked, no pursuit. You have until the moon rises over the western rim tomorrow night. Come alone. Come unarmed. I think we both know you are a woman who honors her bargains.*

Cyrus read it three times. The wind worried the edges of the paper. Behind her, Ede slept with the compass case strapped to his chest the way other men slept with a hand on a weapon, and Sefa's absence lay over the camp like a cold stone—her bedroll still rolled, her tin cup still hanging from its peg, her voice still somehow in the canyon walls, telling them to walk faster, that they walked like tourists.

Kessel Wash. Two words on a page and ten years came off her skin like old paint in the rain.

She folded the note along its creases until it was small enough to swallow.

* * *

Ede woke to the smell of Cyrus burning paper, which was a thing she did, he had learned—burned things when she couldn't say them. He sat up slowly. She was crouched at the fire pit, feeding the note into the coals a corner at a time, watching it curl with an expression he recognized from the mirror of his own worst mornings. The expression of a person doing arithmetic with their own life.

"The company has her," he said.

Cyrus didn't startle. He'd noticed that about her early on—she never startled, only recalibrated, like a compass needle finding north again after a blow. "They have her," she agreed.

"And they wrote to you."

"They wrote to me." She fed the last of the paper to the coals. "Compass and seal for Sefa and my file. No pursuit. A clean trade."

Ede stood. The strap of the compass case bit into his chest where he'd slept on it, and he was glad of the small pain. "You're going to say yes."

"I'm going to think about saying yes."

"You're going to say yes, and then you're going to walk into their camp alone and unarmed, and Odile Marsh is going to take everything and kill us all, because that is what her company does. That is what companies *do*, Cyrus. They drowned my country for a reservoir and then they drowned the paperwork that said it was a country. You think a note under a stone is a bargain? It's a receipt. It's a receipt for *us*."

She turned to look at him then, and her face in the gray dawn light was the face of someone standing at the edge of a very old habit. "Sefa is seventy-one years old," Cyrus said. "She has a cracked rib and a heart that's been walking circuits of a dead country for forty years. She was captured covering our retreat. That's on me. The trade is clean, Ede. One old woman and a dead ranger's file for a compass that points at a valley full of people who never asked for any of this. Any competent officer makes that trade in a heartbeat."

"Any competent officer," Ede said, "left Kessel Wash."

The words came out of him before he could weigh them, and he watched them land. Cyrus didn't flinch. That was the terrible thing—she took the blow the way she took all of them, standing straight, absorbing it into some deep reservoir where she kept everything she refused to feel.

"Yes," she said quietly. "Any competent officer like me."

* * *

Cyrus walked the rim of the canyon while the sun came up, because she thought better in motion, and because she did not want Ede watching her think. The river ran below, brown with the first melt-freight of the season, hurrying toward the drowned valleys. Somewhere upstream was the source. Somewhere upstream was a valley of people who had hidden for forty years, and a shrine, and a future she had no part in—and somewhere behind her, in a survey company's field camp, an old woman sat in a cage asking after her with more spirit than sense.

The old math was simple. The old math was the only math she'd trusted for a decade: cut the loss, save the self, keep moving. She had done it at Kessel Wash. She had left her partner's radio signal fading in a flooded cut and walked out alone because the water was rising and the mission was blown and the math said *go*. The service had called it an acceptable outcome. She had called it nothing at all, and resigned her commission in everything but name, and spent ten years sanding herself down to a woman with no file, no history, no one to lose and no one to lose her.

The old math said: trade the compass. Get Sefa back. Get the file back. Burn both, vanish, start over somewhere the survey company's drones didn't fly.

The old math had never once accounted for the way Ede slept with the compass against his heart. Or the way Sefa had looked at her on the third night of the circuit walk and said, *You walk like someone being chased by a memory*, and been exactly right. Or the way the river-signs on the canyon walls had felt last week like a language she was almost remembering rather than one she was learning—*water here, shelter here, the dead are honored here, keep going*.

The old math said she was looking for permission to quit. And Cyrus Quintana, who had built a whole self out of never asking anyone for anything, realized with a lurch like a floor giving way that she had been waiting for Ede to say no. Waiting for him to refuse the trade so she wouldn't have to be the one who abandoned Sefa. So it would be his fault, his stubbornness, his people, his war—and she could keep her hands clean the way she'd kept them clean at Kessel Wash, technically, procedurally, in every way that didn't matter.

She stood at the rim until her legs ached. Then she went back down to the camp to tell him the truth, because apparently that was who she was now.

* * *

Ede was mending a strap when she came back, and he knew from her walk—level, deliberate, the walk she used when she'd decided something—that the day had turned.

"I'm not making the trade," Cyrus said.

He set down the strap. "Because you believe me. About the sanctuary."

"Because I'm a coward," she said, and sat down across the fire pit from him, close enough that he could see the decade of careful nothing in her face start to crack along its seams. "I wanted you to say no. Last night, when I read the note—I wanted you to refuse, so it wouldn't be my choice. That's what I did at Kessel Wash. The math said leave, and I left, and I've spent ten years telling myself the math was right. And now the math says trade the compass, and I was going to do it, and I was going to tell myself it was for Sefa, when really it was because a clean trade is a way to quit. Trade everything away, get your file back, burn it, disappear, be no one again. No valley. No ledger. No people." She looked at the river. "I wasn't asking your permission to save her, Ede. I was asking your permission to quit, and I hate that I know that."

Ede sat with it. Below them the river said what the river always said, in the language of water on stone, which was the first language his mother had taught him and the only one that had never lied.

"My grandmother used to say," he said finally, "that Vessia didn't drown because the water came. It drowned because everyone who could leave, left, and everyone who stayed pretended they were the only ones. Forty years of people being the only ones." He turned the seal ring on his finger, the bronze worn bright where a hundred hands had held it before his. "Sefa walked circuits of a dead country for forty years so that no one would have to be the only one. And you were going to trade her for a compass and a file."

"I know."

"And now?"

"Now," Cyrus said, "we get her back the hard way."

* * *

They planned it the way Cyrus planned everything: on the ground, in dirt, with sticks and stones. She drew the survey camp from memory of a hundred camps like it—perimeter wire, generator trailer, the drone rack, the holding pen they'd use for a prisoner, because companies never built anything new when an old pattern would do. Marsh ran disciplined teams. Disciplined teams had schedules, and schedules had seams.

"Parley," Cyrus said, placing a flat stone at the camp's edge. "I walk in under a flag. I offer what she wants to hear—that I'll trade, that I'll testify about the seal's destruction, whatever buys me inside the wire and buys time. Marsh is courteous. Courteous people listen."

"And while she's listening?" Ede said.

"You come in from the downstream cut. Sefa mapped it for me before—" Cyrus stopped. Started again. "Before they took her. She said the company camp is on the old Vessian weir road, and the weir road has a maintenance cut through the scree that no survey drone has ever flagged because it isn't on their maps. It's on *ours* now. You take the cut, you get to the pen, and Sefa—"

"Sefa will already be working on the lock," Ede said. "She's been picking locks since before your company had a name. She'll be insulted if we're on time."

Cyrus almost smiled. It was a strange thing on her face, like a bell rung in a room that had never heard one. "Then we're on time," she said. "And Ede—if it goes wrong, if I give you the signal, you take her and you go upstream and you don't come back for me. That's not the old math. That's the mission. The compass reaches the valley. That's the whole of it."

Ede looked at her for a long moment. "You know what my mother said when she put me on the boat?"

"No."

"She said, *you are not the one who escapes, you are the one who arrives.*" He held out his hand, and after a moment Cyrus took it, her grip dry and hard and, for the first time he'd felt it, not entirely alone. "So we both arrive. All three of us. I'm not leaving you in that camp, Cyrus, and I'm not arguing about it, and if you give me that signal I'm going to ignore it, and you can put that in your file."

"I burned the file," Cyrus said.

"Good," said Ede. "Write it on your heart instead."

* * *

That night the moon rose over the western rim, full and white and punctual, and somewhere downriver a lantern burned in a survey company's camp where an old woman sat awake, working a lock with a bent pin and muttering curses in a language the water remembered.

Cyrus stood at the edge of the dark with a white cloth tied to a stick in one hand and nothing else, and listened to Ede and the river breathe behind her.

Then she walked toward the light.
