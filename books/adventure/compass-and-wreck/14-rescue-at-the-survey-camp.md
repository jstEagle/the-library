## 14. Rescue at the Survey Camp

The survey camp sat in the mouth of a side canyon like something that had been dropped there from a great height and had never quite recovered its dignity. Four tents of corporate grey, a generator humming behind a windbreak of sandbags, two ground trucks with their hoods up against the heat, and a perimeter of sensor stakes driven into the gravel in a neat semicircle — the whole arrangement lit by floodlamps that turned the night into a stage set with no audience.

Cyrus lay on a ridge two hundred meters above it and counted heads through her field glasses until counting stopped being useful.

Nine she could see. Marsh's retrieval team had been eight when they'd hit the confluence; someone had called for reinforcements, which meant someone above Marsh was paying attention now, which meant the clock on all of this had gotten shorter. A drone hung at altitude over the camp's north corner, running a slow racetrack pattern, its running lights blinking red every four seconds like a patient heart.

Beside her in the shadow of a boulder, Ede shifted his weight and the seal ring clicked against his belt buckle. He'd taken to wearing it outside his shirt since Sefa was taken, as if he could not bear to have it hidden anymore.

"Tell me again," he whispered.

"You know it again."

"Tell me anyway."

Cyrus lowered the glasses. Below them, a figure moved between the tents — tall, unhurried, hands clasped behind her back the way Marsh carried them, as though the desert were a corridor in an office building and she were merely late for a meeting. Odile Marsh did not sleep much. Cyrus had watched enough of these camps from enough ridgelines to know that the person who paced at night was the one you negotiated with, because the person who paced at night was the one who wanted something badly enough to be awake for it.

"She wants the source," Cyrus said. "She's had our trail since the confluence. She has my file, my name, my face, and a warrant with my service number on it. What she doesn't have is the route past the Gorge of Bells, because Sefa took that knowledge into a cage and hasn't given it up." She paused. "And she doesn't have the compass."

Ede's hand went to his jacket, where the brass instrument rode in an inner pocket. "You're going to offer it to her."

"I'm going to *pretend* to offer it to her. There's a difference, and the difference is about forty minutes."

"And if she takes it?"

"She won't take it tonight. That's the point." Cyrus put the glasses away and looked at him — young, exhausted, three days of hard climbing written on him in dust and split lips, and still carrying that stubborn Vessian spine that would not bend even when bending was the only sane thing to do. "Marsh is careful. Careful people don't grab an object out of a parley tent. They verify. They document. They call their superiors and wait for permission while the permission sits in somebody's inbox. I need her sitting still and looking at me. Every eye in that camp on the parley flag, and none of it on the wire on the south side."

"And Sefa."

"And Sefa." Cyrus checked the sky. The moon was a thin nail-paring, already down behind the western rim; the stars were bright enough to walk by, which was how Sefa liked to travel and how Ede had learned to. "She'll be ready. She's been ready since before we were."

"She's seventy years old, Cyrus."

"She's seventy years old and she walked circles around a drowned country for thirty of them without being caught once. Don't insult her by worrying." She said it more sharply than she meant to, and saw it land, and made herself soften. "We get her out. That's the whole design. Everything else is decoration."

Ede nodded slowly. Then: "What if she doesn't let us go? After. What if she follows?"

Cyrus looked back down at the camp, at the neat grey geometry of it, at the drone stitching its patient red stitches across the dark.

"Oh, she'll follow," she said. "That's the part I'm counting on."

* * *

They'd argued about who should walk in under the flag, and the argument had lasted exactly as long as it took Cyrus to say *me* and mean it in the voice she used for terrain that killed people. Ede had wanted to go himself — *she'll talk to me, I'm the one with the ring* — and Cyrus had told him the truth, which was that Marsh would take one look at a frightened twenty-year-old Vessian and start squeezing, and everything Ede knew would come out of him inside an hour, not because he was weak but because he was honest, and honesty under pressure was just another word for bleeding.

Cyrus, on the other hand, had spent ten years learning how to be nobody. Lying to Odile Marsh across a folding table was, in its way, the most ranger thing she'd done in a decade.

She went down the scree alone, hands visible, the white rag tied to her staff — an actual rag, torn from the hem of her shirt, because some protocols outlived the institutions that wrote them and parley was one of them. Fifty meters out, the sensor stakes began to chirp, and a spotlight swung onto her and pinned her like a moth.

"That's far enough." A voice amplified from the camp edge, flat and professional. "State your business."

"Cyrus Quintana. Formerly Ranger Service, Third District." She let the name sit in the air — her real name, the one that was worth money now, the one on the bounty sheet in the checkpoint town. If you were going to sell a lie, you sold it wrapped around the truth. "I'm here to see Odile Marsh. Tell her I've brought what she's been chasing."

The silence stretched long enough for her to count her own heartbeats. Then the spotlight cut, and a different voice — unhurried, faintly amused, carrying the particular clarity of someone who had never once needed to raise it — said:

"Well. Let her come in, then. And make tea. It seems rude to negotiate with a thirsty woman."

* * *

Marsh's command tent smelled of generator exhaust and good coffee, which was somehow more menacing than gun oil would have been. A folding table stood in the center under a work lamp, and on the table, laid out with archival precision, were the contents of Cyrus's lost credentials pack: her ranger commission card, her district passes, her medical record, the photograph from her service intake — a younger woman with shorter hair and eyes that hadn't yet learned to look through things.

Marsh sat across from the table with a tablet in one hand and did not rise. "Ms. Quintana. Or do you prefer Quintana-Rojas? Your full file is very thorough. The Service kept excellent records before you started burning yours."

"Cyrus is fine."

"Please, sit." Marsh gestured to the camp chair opposite her with the graciousness of a hostess, and Cyrus sat, keeping her hands loose and visible, keeping her breathing at the pace of a woman with nothing to hide. Two of Marsh's people flanked the tent entrance. The third — the one whose rifle had opened Cyrus's shoulder at the confluence — stood at the table's end, watching her with the flat patience of a man who enjoyed his work.

"You came alone," Marsh observed.

"I came to talk."

"People who come to talk usually bring lawyers or leverage. You brought neither. Which tells me you're desperate, and desperate people are my favorite kind." She set the tablet down and folded her hands. "But you said you brought what I'm chasing. I confess I'm intrigued. Because what I'm chasing, according to my last inventory, is a bronze chest, a brass compass, a seal ring, and two Vessians — one of whom is currently refusing meals in my detention tent in what I assume is an attempt to bore me into releasing her. It isn't working. She's telling me folk stories instead. Very good ones, actually. I've started taking notes."

"She'll keep telling them until you let her go."

"She'll tell them until she dies, I suspect. Remarkable constitution." Marsh tilted her head. "So. Your offer."

Cyrus reached slowly into her jacket — saw the guard at the table's end tense, saw Marsh's small raised finger calm him — and laid the compass on the table between them.

It was a risk. It was the whole plan balanced on a brass hinge: give Marsh the object without giving her the *use* of it, because the compass pointed only along the river, only upstream, only toward the source — and here, in this side canyon off the main wash, with no river water within a kilometer, the needle would do nothing but tremble. A compass that pointed nowhere was just a promise. And promises were currency.

Marsh looked at it for a long moment. Then she picked it up — carefully, the way one handles evidence — and turned it in the lamplight, and Cyrus watched her watch the needle swing lazily, aimlessly, pointing at nothing, pointing at everything, and saw the exact moment the calculation crossed her face.

"It doesn't work here," Marsh said.

"It works where the river is. It points along the water. Upstream. Always upstream." Cyrus leaned back. "You've got my file, so you know I'm not sentimental and I'm not stupid. So hear the trade. The compass stays with me. In exchange: Sefa walks out of your camp tonight, unharmed, and my file goes back in whatever drawer it came from. You get the source route from me — the actual route, past the gorge, past the high country — drawn and annotated, in my hand, verified by a serving ranger's knowledge. Nobody else alive can give it to you. Not the girl's stories, not the old woman's riddles. A professional survey of the approach corridor, which is what your company actually needs if it's going to file that water claim and defend it."

Marsh was quiet. Outside, the generator hummed. Somewhere a truck door closed.

"You're offering to guide us," Marsh said at last, "to the sanctuary you spent the last week running away from."

"I'm offering to survive. It's the only thing I've ever been good at." Cyrus held her gaze. "You read the file. You know about the mission I abandoned. You know I left a partner on a mountain rather than miss my extraction window. I'm telling you the same person is sitting in that chair. I'll trade the old woman and my record for my life and a head start. That's the deal. Take it or spend six months dragging those sensor stakes up canyons that eat survey teams."

Something flickered behind Marsh's eyes — not belief, never belief, but *interest*, the cold appetite of a woman who had just been handed a map of her opponent's soul and found it legible. Cyrus felt the familiar shame of it, the old self standing in the light saying *yes, this is what I am,* and made herself hold still and let the lie do its work.

Marsh picked up the compass again, weighed it, and set it back down on Cyrus's side of the table.

"Here's my difficulty, Ms. Quintana," she said pleasantly. "A compass that only works near water is a curiosity. A guide who admits she abandoned her partner is a liability. And a trade offered at midnight by a woman with a bounty on her head is, historically, a stalling tactic." She smiled — a genuine smile, warm at the edges, which was the worst thing Cyrus had seen all week. "But I admire the craftsmanship. So here is *my* counteroffer. You stay the night as my guest. In the morning, we discuss terms like civilized people. And if your friends are planning anything clever—" she glanced, almost idly, toward the south side of the camp, and Cyrus's blood went to ice— "they should know my sensors cover the wire, and my people are awake."

* * *

Later — much later, lying on a cot in a guest tent with the guard's silhouette visible through the canvas and the drone's red blink strobing the roof — Cyrus replayed that glance toward the south wire and could not decide whether it had been a bluff, a guess, or a trap already sprung.

She lay still and listened. Generator. Wind. Boots on gravel, making a slow circuit — two sets, spaced, professional. No shouting. No alarm.

Either the plan was intact, or it was already dead, and there was no way to know which from inside a canvas box. This, she thought, staring at the strobing red pulse overhead, was the flaw in every plan she'd ever built: eventually you had to stop moving and trust other people to move for you. Ten years of solitude had been, among other things, ten years of never having to feel this — this helpless arithmetic, other lives subtracted from your control.

She thought of Sefa in the detention tent, seventy years old, telling folk stories to a woman taking notes. She thought of Ede somewhere in the dark with the seal ring clicking against his belt, waiting for a signal that might already be meaningless.

*Trust the design,* she told herself. *You built it. Trust it.*

The drone blinked. The boots passed. The night went on.

* * *

At what her body swore was three in the morning, the tent flap moved.

Not the guard — the guard's shadow still stood at his post, motionless. The flap lifted at its weighted corner, the way it would if someone who knew canvas had studied it for an hour, and Sefa's face appeared in the gap: seamed, filthy, luminous with a fury that looked almost like joy.

"You," she breathed, "are the worst planner I have ever met, and I once traveled with a man who navigated by the smell of water."

Cyrus was across the cot before she knew she'd moved. Her hands found the old woman's shoulders — real, solid, thinner than they'd been five days ago — and gripped hard enough to bruise, and Sefa let her, and for three seconds neither of them said anything at all.

"How," Cyrus managed finally.

"The south wire. Your boy cut it at the stake line where the drainage crosses — the sensors there read moisture, not metal, a lazy installation, I told you these company people map with machines instead of feet." Sefa's whisper was rapid, alive, the voice of a woman running on pure grievance. "He is at the rocks past the trucks. We have perhaps ten minutes before the watch changes. Can you walk?"

"My shoulder's fine."

"Your shoulder is a ruin and you limp. Can you *walk*?"

"Yes."

"Then we go now, and you will tell me on the way why Odile Marsh let us do this, because that woman does not lose prisoners by accident, and I have been thinking about it all the way up the wire, and I do not like any of the answers I am finding."

Cyrus froze with one boot half on. "What do you mean, *let* us—"

"The guard rotation changed an hour early. The drone drifted east. The drainage crossing was left unwatched after I watched it watched for two days." Sefa's eyes were ancient and absolutely clear in the dark. "That woman opened a door for us, child. The only question is what's standing on the other side of it."

And Cyrus understood, all at once, with the cold clarity of a starved animal scenting weather: the counteroffer, the guest tent, the glance at the south wire. Marsh had never intended to hold them. She had intended to *release* them — tracked, followed, at distance, at leisure — because a compass that pointed only near water was useless in a cage, and so were guides, and so was an old woman's memory. Marsh didn't want prisoners.

She wanted pilots. She wanted them to fly, and she meant to ride their wake all the way to the source.

"We go anyway," Cyrus said.

"Obviously we go anyway." Sefa was already holding the flap. "But we go knowing. That is the difference between escaping and being *spent*."

They slipped out through the gap in the wire where the drainage crossed, past the sleeping trucks, to the rocks where Ede crouched with a cutters still warm in his fist and tears he refused to acknowledge freezing on his face when he saw Sefa alive. No alarm rose behind them. No spotlight swung. The camp let them go the way a fisherman lets line run.

Cyrus looked back once, from the ridge, at the neat grey geometry shrinking below — at the single lamp still burning in the command tent, where Odile Marsh sat awake, watching them climb into the dark with the patience of a woman who had already written the ending.

Above them, the high country waited, black against the stars. And far to the southeast, past the rim of the world, the first bruised band of monsoon cloud had begun to gather — days early, moving fast, racing them to the source.
