## 15. Above the Tree Line

The storm found them two days before it should have.

Cyrus saw it first as a bruise on the southern horizon, a smudge of violet-gray where the desert's clean blue had been all morning. By noon the bruise had shoulders. By mid-afternoon it had teeth.

"That's not supposed to be here yet," she said, stopping on the switchback with her hands on her knees. The air had gone thin and strange over the last thousand feet, and every breath felt borrowed. "Monsoon's not due for another week. Maybe ten days."

"Vessians said the mountains keep their own calendar." Ede stood above her on the trail, looking back the way they'd come. The wind pulled at his jacket, at the strap of the salvage chest across his back. He'd carried it himself since the confluence, refusing help, the way he refused most things now that Sefa couldn't carry her share. "The old river-signs mark flood years. My grandmother—" He stopped, corrected himself. "The woman who raised me used to say the water remembers when we forget."

"The water can remember somewhere else." Cyrus straightened, checked the sky again, and did the arithmetic she didn't like: three days' climb to the cleft Sefa had described, and a storm front that would close it in one. "We move fast or we don't move at all."

Behind them, Sefa laughed — a dry, cracked sound, like wind through a dead reed. She was sitting on a boulder with her hands folded in her lap, which was itself an alarm. For six days she had not once sat down voluntarily on the trail. For six days she had walked with her broken ribs strapped in Cyrus's spare shirt and her face arranged into an expression of patient contempt, as if the mountain were a slow student who would eventually learn.

"You climb fast," Sefa said. "You always have. I've watched you for two weeks now, girl. You walk like someone being chased by her own shadow."

"I am being chased by something."

"Not anymore." Sefa nodded south, toward the storm. "That's chasing you now. Everything else is behind us." She pushed herself up, and Cyrus saw the effort cost her — saw the hitch in her breath, the gray under her brown skin, the way her right arm stayed pressed against her side where Marsh's man had kicked her at the gorge. "So we stop climbing fast. We climb smart. There's a cave above the tree line, half a day up. We shelter there through the worst of it and come down the other side when the rivers settle."

"Through the worst of it," Cyrus repeated. "How long is the worst of it?"

Sefa smiled, and there was something in the smile that made Cyrus want to hit something. "However long it wants to be."

* * *

They crossed the tree line at dusk, and the world changed its mind about what it was.

Below had been canyon country — red rock, juniper, the smell of dust and sun-baked stone, a landscape Cyrus knew the way she knew her own hands. Above was another country entirely. The trees gave out all at once, as if a line had been drawn and nothing green had permission to cross it. What remained was granite and scree and patches of snow that had no business existing this early in the season, lying in the shadows like something hidden. The light came long and gold and then, abruptly, left.

The cold arrived to take its place. Not desert cold, which burned off with the sun. This cold came up out of the ground and out of the sky at once, a cold with weight behind it, and Cyrus understood within an hour why the Vessians had built their sanctuaries below and their shrines above. Nothing lived up here. Nothing was meant to.

Ede found the cave just as the first rain hit — fat, freezing drops that struck the rock like thrown gravel. It was less a cave than a deep overhang behind a fallen slab, but it faced away from the storm and it held the memory of an old fire, a ring of blackened stones and a niche in the wall where something had been kept once. Carved above the niche, small and precise, was a river-sign: the symbol for *shelter*, with a second mark beneath it that Sefa had taught them meant *given freely*.

"Somebody stood watch here," Ede said, running his fingers along the carving. His voice had gone soft in the way it always did around the old signs. "Forty years ago. Fifty. They cut this so anyone running would know they could stop."

"Anyone running," Cyrus said, "who could read it."

"Anyone running who was worth teaching." Sefa lowered herself onto the driest stretch of floor with Ede's help, and this time she didn't disguise how much it cost her. "That was the whole country, girl. That was what Vessia was. Not the valleys. Not even the river. The teaching. You learned the signs, you belonged. Nobody asked your blood. Nobody asked anything." She looked at Cyrus with those flat, ancient eyes. "We would have taken you forty years ago. We'll take you now. That's the part you still don't understand."

"I understand fine."

"No," Sefa said, almost gently. "You understand survival. Different animal."

Cyrus didn't answer. She got the stove going instead, melting snow in the dented pot, and busied herself with the small competent work that had kept her alive and alone for ten years. Wind screamed past the mouth of the overhang. Somewhere above them, thunder rolled down the granite like boulders being dropped.

When she looked up, Sefa was watching her with the firelight moving on her face, and Cyrus had the sudden, unwelcome certainty that the old woman was counting something. Days. Breaths. Maybe both.

* * *

The storm held them for two days.

It should have been three hours of bad weather. Instead the monsoon's edge parked itself over the high country and stayed, wringing itself out in waves — rain, then hail, then rain again, the temperature dropping until the snowline crept down the slopes toward them like a tide coming in. They couldn't move. Moving above the tree line in whiteout was dying with extra steps.

Cyrus rationed without discussion. She'd done it before; she knew the choreography. Food halved, then halved again. Water free, because snow was everywhere and fuel was the true currency. Heat shared, because heat was survival and pride was a luxury the mountain didn't stock.

What she hadn't planned for was Sefa getting worse.

It started quietly. The old woman slept more than she spoke, and Cyrus told herself that was rest, that was sense, that was a body in its seventies recovering from cracked ribs and a forced march. But on the second night Sefa couldn't get warm. She sat wrapped in both blankets beside the stove with her jaw tight and her hands shaking, and when Cyrus touched her wrist the skin was cool and the pulse beneath it fluttered like a moth against glass.

"Pneumonia," Cyrus said. Not a question. She'd seen it enough times in the service — rangers caught out in wet cold, the infection settling into lungs already punished by altitude. "Or the start of it."

"Sepsis, if we're naming things." Sefa said it the way she said everything, flat and unimpressed, as if her own death were a mildly incompetent surveyor. "My mother died of it. On the road north, the year after the drowning. Everyone died of everything, that year." She coughed, and the cough went too long, and Cyrus heard the wet rattle underneath it and felt something clench behind her sternum that she refused to name.

"We go down as soon as this breaks," Cyrus said. "There are settlements on the south slope. I know a route—"

"You know a route that takes four days." Sefa closed her eyes. "I have maybe two in me, girl, and I'd rather spend them here than in some clinic with fluorescent lights and strangers asking my nationality." A pause. The wind filled it. "Don't waste my last good argument arguing with me."

Ede had gone very still by the chest. In the stove-light his face was fifteen years old and forty at once.

"There has to be medicine," he said finally. "In the kit. You're a ranger, you carry—"

"Antibiotics, yes. Broad-spectrum, expired eight months ago, and I've been dosing her since the gorge." Cyrus kept her voice level, clinical, the voice she used for incident reports. "It's not enough. It might have been enough if we'd gotten her warm sooner, if we'd gotten her down faster, if—" She stopped. The list of ifs was a list of her own decisions, and they both knew it, and saying so out loud would help no one and break something she needed intact.

"If," Sefa agreed comfortably. "The whole world runs on if. Now come here. Both of you. I'm tired of talking to the ceiling."

* * *

She made them sit on either side of her, which Cyrus suspected was tactical — the old woman arranging her own funeral party the way she arranged everything, uninvited and immovable.

"Forty years I've walked the circuits," Sefa said. "Every river-sign from the delta to the source. Every bell-gorge, every shelter-cut, every shrine. I did it because somebody had to, and because the company erased the maps, and because a country that lives only in old women's feet is a country with one foot in the grave." Her breath whistled on the inhale. Cyrus counted it without meaning to. "Ede carries the ring because he asked to. Do you know why he asked?"

"He's Vessian," Cyrus said. "It's his."

"It's the country's. He's a boy who was smuggled out in a grain crate." Sefa's hand found Ede's knee and gripped it. "He asked because he thought carrying it would make him remember better. And it does, a little. But memory isn't a thing you hold, child. It's a thing you do. And I'm about to be done doing it."

"Don't," Ede said. Just that. One word, cracked down the middle.

"Hush. Let me finish while finishing is cheap." Sefa worked her right hand free of the blanket, fumbled at the cord around her neck, and drew out the seal ring.

Cyrus had seen it before — at the gorge, when Sefa had shown it to the bells as if introducing an old friend. Bronze, heavy, worn smooth on the inner band by generations of fingers. The seal of Vessia: the river and the mountain, the sign that had started everything, stamped on a salvage chest in a dead riverbed and stricken from every map that mattered.

Sefa held it out. Not to Ede.

To her.

Cyrus looked at it and did not move. "No. It goes to him. It was always going to him."

"It was always going to whoever would *do* the work." Sefa's voice had gone thinner, but the iron under it hadn't rusted at all. "The boy will carry it home, yes. But home needs a warden, and the boy is a letter, not a keeper. He's the message. You—" she coughed, waved off Ede's reaching hands, "—you are the one who reads maps nobody else can read. You are the one who lies to companies and wins. You are the one who carried me when I couldn't walk and never once said so. I've spent forty years learning to know people in ten minutes, girl. It's how I stayed alive. And I'm telling you: you belong to this whether you believe in it or not. Belief is optional. Belonging isn't."

"I erased myself," Cyrus said. The words came out low and rough. "Ten years. There is no record of me anywhere. That was the point. That was the whole—"

"Yes. And look how well it served you. Alone in the sandwash, chasing thieves, sleeping in stations with your boots by the door in case you had to run." Sefa pressed the ring into her palm and closed Cyrus's fingers over it with both of her own, cold hands, terrible strength. "A person who cannot be found cannot be kept. I'm keeping you. Wear it or carry it, I don't care. But take it."

The ring was warm from the old woman's skin. It weighed nothing and everything.

Cyrus looked at Ede. She expected grief, or betrayal, or the particular anger of an heir watching an inheritance diverted. What she saw instead stopped her breath: Ede nodding. Slowly, tears cutting clean lines down his dirty face, but nodding, certain, as if some argument had been settled between him and Sefa long before this cave, long before the gorge, maybe before Cyrus had ever put boot-prints in the sandwash.

"She told me on the third day," he said hoarsely. "Before you caught me. She said, *the ranger will matter more than either of us.* I thought she was crazy."

"She is crazy," Cyrus said.

"I'm thorough," Sefa said, and laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that bent her double, and when it passed she sagged against the stone with her eyes half-closed and a smile still hanging on her mouth like a hat on a hook. "Now. Listen. The cleft above the storm-line — you'll see three peaks like teeth. Between the second and third there's a gully the signs call *the throat*. Don't camp in it. Flash-flood channel, even up here. Go past it to the shelf, and the shrine is under the overhang — you'll know it by the bells. Small ones. Leave the compass there when you reach the valley. It only points for people who already belong, and it's rude to make it point at strangers."

Her voice was slowing. Cyrus heard it slowing and hated that she heard it.

"And the valley," Sefa murmured. "When they ask who you are — and they will ask, they ask everyone, it's the only gate they have left — don't give them the forged name. Don't give them the ranger number. Give them the one your mother used." Her eyes opened, found Cyrus's, and held them with the last of her ferocity. "The real one. Say it out loud. First time in ten years. Say it to people who will write it down."

The wind screamed. The stove ticked. Cyrus sat with a dead nation's seal biting into her palm and could not speak.

"Sleep," Sefa said, closing her eyes. "Both of you. Storm breaks tomorrow, if the mountain keeps any faith at all. And Cyrus—" she was nearly gone now, words dissolving into breath, "—you were never invisible. I found you in a week. The valley will find you in a day. Being lost was always just a story you told yourself so the leaving wouldn't hurt."

* * *

She died an hour before dawn, between one wave of the storm and the next, so quietly that Cyrus only knew because the rattling breath stopped and did not start again.

They buried her above the tree line because there was nowhere else. The ground was half stone, and digging took hours with a broken entrenching tool and bare hands in the freezing dark, and neither of them suggested waiting. The Vessians, Ede said, buried their dead high — above the flood line, where the water that had taken everything could never take them too. They stacked a cairn over her, and Ede cut a river-sign into the largest stone with his knife: the sign for *keeper*, the one Sefa had used for herself whenever anyone thanked her.

*Given freely*, Cyrus added beneath it, with a different knife, badly.

Then the storm broke — all at once, the way mountains do, the clouds tearing open onto hard blue and a sun so bright off the snow it hurt — and the route south lay open and gleaming, and the cleft with its three teeth stood clear against the sky.

Cyrus hung the seal ring around her neck, tucked it under her shirt against her skin, and picked up her pack.

"Three peaks," she said. "Like teeth."

Ede shouldered the chest. His eyes were swollen and steady. "She'd say we're wasting daylight."

"She'd say worse." Cyrus took the first step downward, toward the shrine, toward the valley, toward the name she hadn't spoken in ten years waiting at the end of it like a door. "Come on. Let's not make a liar of her."
