## 16. The Source

The storm found them two hours below the pass and drove them sideways along the spine of the ridge, and Cyrus learned in that white roaring hour that Sefa had not been exaggerating when she said the monsoon would seal the high country. It did not rain so much as arrive—a wall of water walking up the valley behind them, swallowing the trail they'd climbed, erasing the world they'd come from as thoroughly as if it had never existed.

They went through the notch at the top of the pass with their heads down and the wind trying to peel them off the stone, and on the far side the wind stopped. Not gentled. Stopped, like a door closing.

Cyrus lifted her face out of her collar and forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

Below them lay a valley that should not have been there.

She had spent ten years learning every fold of this desert—the service maps, the survey overlays, the satellite passes she'd memorized and then helped falsify to hide her own movements. She knew this range as a line of barren granite with no water and no reason to come. And yet here was a river running out of the mountain's heart, broad and green-edged, threading down through terraces cut into the slopes—old terraces, stone-walled, some collapsed and some so recently mended that the mortar looked wet in the rain. Orchards clung to the lower benches. Smoke rose from chimneys among the ruins of what had once been a town: arches and towers drowned to their shoulders in ivy and moss, and built into them, around them, out of them, smaller houses with slate roofs and lit windows.

People moved along the paths between the terraces. She could see them even through the rain—figures in oilskins bringing in harvest baskets, a child running the length of an aqueduct with something bright trailing from one fist.

"It's real," Ede said beside her. His voice cracked on the second word. He had said *it's real* four times since the pass, each time as if testing whether the valley would dissolve under scrutiny. "Cyrus. It's real."

"I see it." Her voice came out flat, which was the only way she could get it out at all.

Sefa leaned on her staff, gray-faced, her injured side soaked through where the escape wound had wept again during the climb. She looked down at the valley the way other people looked at graves—or altars. "Forty years," she said. "I walked the circuits. I walked the drowned country for forty years telling myself someone had stayed. I never let myself believe it." She laughed, a small broken sound. "And they were here the whole time. Above me. Laughing at my old knees."

"They weren't laughing," Ede said quietly. "They were hiding. Same as everyone who survived. Just—" he gestured at the walls, the terraces, the whole impossible green architecture of the place, "—better at it than we were."

*Better at it than I was,* Cyrus thought, and the thought had teeth.

They descended. The rain softened as they dropped below the cloud deck into the valley's own weather, a fine warm drizzle that steamed off the stones. The trail became a road—paved, ancient, the paving worn into a channel by centuries of feet—and the road passed under an archway whose keystone bore a carving worn nearly smooth: a river in three braids, and above it a star.

Ede stopped under the arch and put his hand flat against the stone. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. Cyrus watched his shoulders move once, twice, and understood that he was doing the thing she herself did at the edges of places that mattered—pressing yourself against the fact of it until you believed it.

Then a voice called out in a language Cyrus didn't know, sharp with challenge, and half a dozen figures came down the road with staves and hooked poles and the unhurried confidence of people defending ground they had held for a very long time.

* *

They were taken, politely, to a courtyard.

That was the word for it, though the courtyard had been a temple once, or a market hall—its roof gone, its columns standing open to the sky, rain falling through into a pool that had been engineered to receive it, overflow channels glittering. Lanterns hung from the column capitals. Under them stood perhaps thirty people, and every one of them was looking at Ede.

An old man came forward. He was small and straight-backed, with white hair bound at the nape and hands like knotted rope, and he wore the same three-braid-and-star carved into a wooden pendant at his throat. He looked at Ede for a long time. Then he spoke—not in the language of the challenge but in trade-tongue, accented, careful.

"You carry the ring."

Ede fumbled it out from under his shirt. The seal ring caught the lantern light, the Vessian crest engraved in its face, and a sound went through the courtyard—not loud, nothing dramatic, just a collective intake of breath, thirty people remembering at once.

"My name is Ede Vassian-Maro," he said, and his voice shook and held. "My mother was Lira Maro, of the lower valleys. My grandmother sent me out of the country when I was six. I've carried this ever since. I came to find the sanctuary."

"There is no sanctuary," the old man said.

Cyrus felt Ede flinch beside her like a struck animal.

The old man raised a hand before the boy could break. "There is no sanctuary because there was never anywhere else. There is only here. We are the sanctuary, or we are nothing—we decided that forty years ago, when the last of us who remembered the old law climbed up here and asked ourselves what a nation is, if not the people who refuse to stop being one." He turned his gaze then to Cyrus, and it was not unkind, but it was thorough, the way she herself assessed strangers: weight, weapons, weariness, intent. "And you are the ranger."

Not a question. Cyrus's hand moved toward her knife before she could stop it, and stopped.

"How—"

"The compass." The old man nodded toward the pack on her shoulder, where the brass instrument rode wrapped in oilcloth. "It has been pointing at this valley for forty years. It points at whoever carries it who belongs here. It has never once pointed at anyone else." His eyes stayed on hers. "We have watchers on the rim. They saw three travelers come over the pass in a storm, and they saw the needle-sense go mad in the shrine house—the way it does when the compass comes home. So. You are the ranger who carried it. Tell us why."

The courtyard waited. Rain ticked into the pool. Somewhere a child was shushed.

Cyrus had rehearsed lies her whole adult life. She had forged documents, buried identities, answered company interrogators with a smoothness that had gotten Odile Marsh to smile and believe her—for a while. She opened her mouth to do it now, to give them the version of herself that was safe, and found that she couldn't. Not because they'd catch her. Because Sefa was swaying on her feet with exhaustion and blood loss, and Ede was looking at her with an expression she recognized too well—the look of someone waiting to learn whether the person they'd trusted was real—and because these people had spent forty years being hunted by exactly the kind of machinery she had served.

"I'm a deserter," she said. "Ten years ago I walked away from a mission and my partner died because of it. I erased myself afterward. Forged records, burned trails, the whole craft. I chased Ede because he stole supplies, and I kept going because—" She stopped. Started again, plainer. "Because I wanted to see where the compass pointed more than I wanted to be safe. That's all of it. I have no right to be here. I know that."

Silence. Then the old man did something she hadn't expected: he smiled, faintly, tiredly.

"A nation of survivors," he said, "and you think we will turn away someone for having survived badly." He looked at Sefa, and his composure broke open entirely; he crossed the courtyard and took her hands in both of his. "Sefa of the circuits. You old fool. You walked past us eleven times."

"Twelve," Sefa said, and cried without any apparent awareness of it.

* *

They gave them a house that night—one of the small slate-roofed dwellings built into the shell of a drowned tower, warm from a stove, dry, smelling of woodsmoke and some herb Cyrus couldn't name. A woman brought food: grain cakes, river fish, greens from the terraces, and tea brewed from needles of the high pines. She set it down, looked at Cyrus with frank curiosity, and said, "You're the first outsider inside the walls since the drowning. The children will want to look at you tomorrow. Let them. It does no harm."

Then she left, and Cyrus sat with a bowl in her hands and did not eat for a while.

*A nation is the people who refuse to stop being one.*

She thought about the decade she had spent becoming no one. The forged service file. The rented rooms paid in cash. The deliberate mediocrity of her performance reviews, the friendships declined, the letters unanswered until they stopped coming. She had told herself it was survival—that the mission she'd abandoned had taught her that attachments got people killed, that invisibility was armor. And it was true. It had worked. She was alive, and free, and utterly alone, and she had called that freedom right up until a thief led her into a dead riverbed and a brass needle swung away from north and pointed, for the first time in forty years, at home.

Ede sat across the room turning the seal ring over and over. He hadn't spoken since the courtyard.

"Ede."

"I keep thinking about my mother," he said. "She used to tell me stories about the valleys like they were somewhere far away. Like the old country was a place you visited in stories. And it was *here*. Forty kilometers from where she died. They were here the whole time, growing apples." His jaw tightened. "If we'd known—if anyone had told us—"

"They couldn't," Cyrus said. "A secret that size only survives if nobody knows where the edge of it is. Not even the people inside it." She heard her own voice and hated how much authority was in it, how much of the ranger still lived in her throat. But it was true, and he needed it to be true, and after a moment he nodded and put the ring away against his chest.

Later, unable to sleep, Cyrus went out into the drizzle and walked the lanes.

The valley at night was lamplit and quiet. She passed the aqueduct, the orchards silvered with rain, a watchpost built into a ruin where a young woman with a crossbow nodded to her without alarm—word had already traveled, apparently, that the stranger was known. She came at last to a small stone building near the valley's head, its door open, lanterns burning within, and inside it she found the shrine.

The compass hung from an iron bracket above a basin of river water, its lid open, its needle swinging slowly, lazily, describing circles that touched every direction and committed to none.

She stood in the doorway a long time before an old woman tending the lamps spoke without looking up. "It does that now. Rests. It pointed for forty years and nobody came, and now it's come home, so it rests." She glanced at Cyrus. "You want to know if it would point for you."

"Yes."

"It wouldn't." No cruelty in it. Just fact, delivered the way Sefa delivered facts about water. "The needle doesn't measure worthiness. It measures belonging—it finds the people the river already claims. You could live here fifty years and it would sit quiet in your hand." The old woman smiled. "But the river has drowned smarter people than you, ranger, and it keeps what it keeps. Belonging isn't always given at the door. Sometimes it grows."

Cyrus looked at the resting needle and felt something loosen in her chest that she didn't have a name for. Displacement, maybe. Or the beginning of its opposite.

* *

She woke before dawn to shouting on the rim road.

For one disoriented second she was ten years younger, reaching for a weapon in a ruined station, and then she was here, in a warm room in a hidden valley, pulling on her boots while Ede did the same and Sefa—already awake, already moving despite the wound, because of course she was—said, "The watchers. Something's spooked them."

They came out into gray light and found the lane filling with people, faces turned upward toward the western rim, where the storm clouds of the retreating monsoon still piled black against the peaks. A runner came down the terraces at a dead sprint, and Cyrus caught enough of the shouted Vessian to understand the shape of it before the old man translated, his voice carrying flat and cold across the courtyard:

"Riders on the rim. Survey-company colors. A dozen or more, with equipment." He turned, and his eyes found Cyrus with terrible accuracy. "They followed you."

"No," Cyrus said, and then, honestly, "Maybe. Marsh let us go at the camp. I knew it, and I ran anyway, because there wasn't another way to get you both out." Her stomach had gone to iron. "She wanted us to lead her here. And we did."

The valley that had sung to her all night—the lamplight, the apples, the children—went silent around her, thirty faces and then a hundred, all of them arriving at the same arithmetic. Forty years of hiding, ended by the stranger at the gate.

Ede stepped forward and stood beside her, shoulder to shoulder, facing them. "She brought us home," he said. "Whatever follows, that's also true."

The old man looked from him to Cyrus, and then up at the rim, where the first light of morning glinted off something metal and deliberate, moving along the ridgeline like a slow blade.

"Then we decide," he said, "what kind of nation we are. One that scatters again—or one that stands."
