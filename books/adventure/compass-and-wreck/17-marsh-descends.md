## 17. Marsh Descends

The storm did not break so much as lean.

Cyrus felt it first in her teeth, the way she'd learned to feel weather in the low country—a pressure behind the jaw, a taste of metal on the wind. But up here, above the tree line where the valley of the source sat cupped in its ring of stone, the monsoon announced itself differently. It came as a green smell rolling down the glaciers, a warmth that had no business at this altitude, and a sound like the whole sky clearing its throat.

The settlers heard it too. She watched them from the terrace above the longhouse—watched them stop their work one by one, faces turning south toward the cleft in the rimrock, the way a herd turns toward the direction of the wolf. An old man set down his hoe. A woman pulled her children out of the irrigation channels with a hand on each neck, gentle and absolute. Nobody spoke. They didn't need to. Forty years of hiding had taught these people to read the sky the way Cyrus read sign, and the sky was telling them something had come up the mountain behind the weather.

"They followed the storm front," Sefa said beside her. The old woman's voice was thin now, worn down by the altitude and the injury she refused to name, but her eyes were still sharp enough to cut. "Marsh's drones can't fly in this. So she walks. She's been walking for three days."

"You can't know that."

"I know Odile Marsh." Sefa said the name the way you'd spit out a seed. "She doesn't send people to do what she can do herself. And she doesn't climb mountains for nothing." She turned her head, slow, and looked at Cyrus with an expression that was almost pity. "She climbed it for you."

*For the seal,* Cyrus wanted to say. *For the documents, for the water rights, for forty years of buried paper.* But the words wouldn't come out honest, because Sefa was right. Marsh had let them go at the survey camp with a courtesy so perfect it had taken Cyrus two days of hard climbing to understand it for what it was: a leash. Every cairn they'd built, every spring they'd drunk from, every Vessian waymark Ede had traced with reverent fingers—Marsh had been reading the same trail, one ridge back, patient as silt.

And now the patience was spent.

Below the terrace, the valley went about its preparations with a terrible quiet competence. This was not the first time they had done this. Cyrus understood that within minutes of watching them—the way the granary doors were barred from outside so they'd look abandoned, the way the drying racks came down and vanished into cellars, the way a dozen small fires were banked and scattered so no smoke column would betray the settlement from the air. These people had rehearsed disappearing since before Ede was born. It was choreography older than any of them, passed down like the river-signs, like the bells, like everything else the drowned nation had carried uphill in its hands.

It made her sick to watch. Not because it was done badly—it was done beautifully—but because she recognized it. She had spent ten years doing exactly this to herself. Erasing the fires. Barring the doors. Making the record say *no one lives here.*

She knew how well it worked. And she knew what it cost.

"Cyrus." Ede came up the terrace steps two at a time, the seal ring hanging on its cord against his chest, his face flushed from running. "The watchers on the south rim signal movement in the cleft. A lot of movement. Twenty, maybe thirty, with pack animals and—" He stopped, swallowed. "Cutting equipment. Survey-grade. The kind that takes a valley apart."

"How long?"

"An hour. Maybe less if the rain holds off. They'll want to be on the rim before the ground turns to soup."

Cyrus looked down at the valley again. At the woman pulling her children clear of the water. At the old man with the hoe, standing in his own field, looking at it the way you look at a thing you're deciding whether to leave forever. At the shrine where the compass hung on its bronze hook, needle swinging gently, pointing—not north, never north—at whoever stood before it.

At this moment it pointed at her.

"Where do they go?" she asked. "When they scatter. Is there a second valley? A fallback?"

Ede's silence answered before his face did.

"There isn't," Sefa said flatly. "There was talk of one, forty years ago. The high caves past the glacier. Nobody's used them. There's nothing there—no water rights, no fields, no ledger. You don't survive forty years twice." Her hand found the wall and stayed there, knuckles whitening. "If they scatter now, they scatter into weather and winter and country that has already eaten one nation. Some will live. Most won't. And the ones who live will be ghosts, which is what Marsh wants anyway. A ghost is as good as erased."

"That's not true," Ede said, sharp. "Ghosts remember. I'm a ghost. I remembered enough to find this place."

"You found it because the compass let you," Sefa said. "And the compass only points for those who belong. Marsh doesn't need the compass, girl. She needs the paper. Burn the paper, scatter the people, and in fifty years there'll be no one left who remembers why the compass pointed anywhere at all." She pushed off the wall, straightening with visible effort. "Go down. They're calling a gathering. If we're going to dissolve, we should at least watch ourselves do it."

* * *

They gathered in the longhouse, and it was the strangest assembly Cyrus had ever stood inside—stranger than ranger tribunals, stranger than company hearings, stranger even than Marsh's immaculate parley tent with its folding table and its offered tea.

There were perhaps ninety of them. Farmers, mostly. Weavers, a smith, two women who kept the valley's ledger in a script Cyrus had seen only carved into canyon walls. Old people who had been born under Vessian law and young people who had been born in these mountains and had never once, in their lives, shown their faces below the tree line. They stood close together in the lamplight, and the thing that struck Cyrus—the thing she couldn't stop staring at—was how much they looked like a family. Not metaphorically. Literally. Cousins and in-laws and grandparents, an entire nation folded down into one crowded room, related by blood or by shared hiding until the distinction had stopped mattering.

At the head of the room stood a broad-shouldered woman named Ilva, who had met them at the gate four days ago with a crossbow leveled and then lowered it when the compass swung toward her. She was the closest thing the valley had to a head of household, and she spoke the way people speak when everyone in the room is kin: without preamble, without performance.

"The company is on the rim," she said. "You all know what they carry. Papers, mostly. An old claim from before the drowning, bought cheap from a dead government's creditors. And guns, because papers alone have never moved anyone off their land." A murmur ran through the room, low as groundwater. "We have three choices, same as always. Fight, scatter, or talk."

"We can't fight," someone said from the back. Not cowardice—arithmetic. Ninety farmers against thirty professionals with cutting equipment and air support waiting out the storm.

"We can't talk," said another voice, older. "Talking means names. Names mean records. Records are how they drowned us the first time."

Ilva nodded slowly, as if each word cost her. "Then we scatter."

"No." Ede stepped forward, and Cyrus saw the whole room flinch slightly—not at him, but at the sight of him stepping forward, the boy smuggled out at six, the one who'd come back. "No, listen to me. I've walked the circuits. I've met the others—Sefa's people, the bell-keepers, the families in the western gorges. If this valley scatters, there is nowhere left. This is the last lit window. You put it out, and we're all just stories."

"Stories survive," said the old man with the hoe. He had come in with dirt still on his hands, and he held them up now, palms out, as evidence. "I am seventy-one years old. I have been ready to leave this valley since the day I helped dig its first cellar. My grandfather taught me the routes when I was smaller than your youngest, Ede. We are always ready. That is what it is to be Vessian. The country drowns; the people become water. Water goes around."

"And freezes," Sefa said from beside Cyrus, very quietly. "Water freezes, Toma. Ask me how I know."

The room stilled. Cyrus realized, watching them, that she was watching a family argue about its own death—and that every person in it had already survived one version of this argument, inherited from parents who'd had it in flooded streets and burning archives. The dread in the room wasn't fear of Marsh. It was the exhaustion of doing this twice.

Ilva raised a hand. "Then it's decided unless someone gives me a reason not to decide it. We scatter at first light, ahead of the worst of the rain. The ledger comes apart—three copies, three routes, as the old plan says. The shrine stays. Let the company find an empty valley and a compass that points at nothing." Her eyes moved across the room and snagged, briefly, on Cyrus. On Ede. On Sefa leaning against the doorframe. "Unless our guests have something to add."

Ninety faces turned.

Cyrus had faced hostile boards, tribunal panels, Marsh's courteous smile across a parley table. None of it had prepared her for ninety pairs of eyes belonging to people who had every reason to expect nothing from her and were, despite everything, hoping.

She opened her mouth to say something useless—I'm sorry, or I'll help you carry—

"I have something to add."

The voice came from the doorway. Cyrus turned, and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Sefa had straightened to her full height, which was not much, and her face was gray with pain and absolutely calm. In her raised hand, catching the lamplight, was the seal ring.

Not on its cord. Off it. Held up the way you hold a torch.

"Sefa," Ede breathed. "What are you—"

"Forty years I've walked the circuits," she said, and her voice carried further than it had any right to. "Forty years carrying this for people dead and scattered, waiting for someone to give it to. I gave it to the boy because he asked. Now I'm giving it to the valley, because the valley needs to hear what I should have said decades ago." She looked around the room, and Cyrus saw several of the oldest settlers go pale, recognizing the ring, recognizing what it meant that it was here, uncovered, in a lit room. "We keep saying *scatter* like it's a plan. It's not a plan. It's the shape dying takes when it wants to take its time. I have watched this nation scatter for forty years, and every scattering has been called survival, and none of it has been. I am done calling it that."

"You're feverish," Ilva said gently. "Sit down, old mother. No one doubts—"

"I doubt!" Sefa's voice cracked like ice going out. "I doubt every day! And today I watched a company climb my mountain to erase the last house of my people, and I watched us reach for the old answer, and I am telling you the old answer is how they win. Not with guns. With our own readiness. They don't even have to push. We fall over ourselves, politely, generation after generation, until there's one of us left walking circuits and talking to bells." She thrust the ring toward Ilva. "So before we scatter—before we do it one more time, one more beautiful, practiced time—somebody in this room tell me why we've never once tried the other thing."

Nobody answered. The rain chose that moment to arrive.

It came down on the longhouse roof like applause, like stones, like the whole sky finally letting go of what it had been holding, and under that sound Cyrus felt the mountain itself shift—the cleft filling, the gorge below waking, the river remembering what it was for. The monsoon had broken early, exactly as the signs promised, and somewhere on the south rim thirty people with pack animals and cutting equipment were about to learn what Vessian weather did to an invasion schedule.

But Cyrus wasn't listening to the rain. She was looking at the ring in Sefa's shaking hand, and at the ledger-women with their three-copy plan, and at Ede's face—furious, terrified, alive—and she was feeling, rising in her chest with the pressure of the storm itself, the thing she had spent ten years making sure she would never feel again.

Responsibility. The kind with a name attached.

Because she knew what the other thing was. She'd known since the confluence, since the checkpoint town, since she'd read her own file nailed to a board with her real name on it and hadn't died of shame after all. The other thing was testimony. The other thing was standing in the open with your record showing. The other thing was a serving ranger's oath, pre-drowning law, and a box of documents that proved ninety people owned the water under their own feet.

The other thing was her. And it was going to cost everything she'd built, and the storm was already here, and Marsh was already on the rim, and there was no more time to pretend she hadn't been looking for permission since the day she'd picked up a thief's trail in the dry country.

"Sefa's right," Cyrus said.

Her voice came out rough, and ninety faces turned to her again, and this time she didn't look away.

"Don't scatter. Not yet. Give me until the rain stops—and give me your papers."

* * *

Outside, the valley roared with water. Inside, the ledger-women unlocked their chests, and Cyrus began, with hands that almost didn't shake, to build the case that would either save a nation or finish burying it—while on the south rim, through the sheets of rain, the lights of Odile Marsh's camp came on one by one, patient and white, like eyes opening.
