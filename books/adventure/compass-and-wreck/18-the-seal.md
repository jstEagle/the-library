## 18. The Seal

The valley had gone quiet in the way Cyrus had learned to distrust most.

Not the quiet of emptiness — the quiet of held breath. All morning the settlers had been moving through the terraces with the deliberate calm of people packing a house they loved: seed jars wrapped in oilcloth, the loom dismantled, the shrine's bronze fittings pried loose and crated. They were not preparing to fight. They were preparing to dissolve, to scatter up goat trails into country where a survey company's drones would lose them in a week, and to stay lost for another forty years if that was what it took.

Cyrus stood at the edge of the barley field and watched an old man fold a flag she didn't recognize — deep green, a white river running through it — with the tenderness of someone folding a shroud.

*Forty years,* she thought. *They did it once already. They'll do it again. And I can walk away before it starts, because walking away is the one thing I've never failed at.*

The compass hung at her hip in its oilskin wrap. The seal ring sat in her breast pocket, warm from Sefa's hand, heavier than brass had any right to be.

She had spent ten years building a person made of nothing. Forged service numbers. A name filed down to two syllables. A decade of small erasures, each one sensible, each one survivable, until there was so little of Cyrus Quintana left on any record that even she sometimes had to check.

And now she stood in a valley about to be erased the same way, by the same machinery, and understood — with the flat clarity of a compass needle settling — that there was exactly one thing she could do about it.

It was impossible. That was almost comforting. She'd survived on impossible rations before.

She turned and went to find Ede.

* * *

Ede found her first, actually, which he would later insist was a coincidence and which Sefa, from her cot by the shrine wall, called "the river doing what rivers do."

He came down from the eastern terraces with his sleeves rolled and dirt to the elbow, helping load crates onto the backs of the sure-footed valley ponies, and stopped when he saw her face.

"You've decided something," he said.

"I've decided something."

"Is it the bad kind or the other kind?"

"Sit down," Cyrus said.

They sat on the low stone wall at the field's edge, and below them the settlement went on with its careful dismantling, and Cyrus laid it out the way she'd have laid out a patrol plan — because that was the only way she knew how to say things that frightened her.

"The company's claim is a water-rights filing," she said. "Marsh will have it papered clean. Pre-drowning law supersedes it — if anyone can prove continuous occupation and prior grant. The chest we pulled out of the sandwash. The documents. The seal." She took the ring out and set it on the wall between them, and the afternoon light caught the crest, the drowned river running through the drowned nation's name. "This isn't a relic, Ede. It's a signature. It's authority. Whoever holds this seal speaks for Vessia's grants, and Vessia's grants were never legally dissolved — they were *administratively* erased. There's a difference. Marsh knows there's a difference. It's why she wants the seal destroyed instead of just collected."

Ede was very still. "You're saying the valley has a claim."

"I'm saying the valley has always had a claim. It's been sitting in a silt bank for forty years waiting for somebody to read it."

"And you'd—" He stopped. She watched him get there ahead of her, watched his face change. "You'd have to testify. As a ranger. Under—"

"My real name," Cyrus said. "My real record. All of it. The mission I walked away from. The partner. The forged years. Everything in my file becomes public the moment I stand up in a water-rights proceeding and swear to it. Marsh won't just be fighting a claim anymore. She'll be fighting *me*, and she'll burn me down to do it, and everyone in this valley will know exactly what I am before the first witness is called."

The wind moved through the barley. Somewhere below, a child laughed, quick and bright, and was hushed.

"You don't have to," Ede said quietly.

"Yes, I do. That's the whole point." She looked at the ring between them. "I spent ten years making myself into nobody because nobody can be blamed, nobody can be hurt, nobody can be *asked for anything*. And it worked. It worked beautifully. And now I'm watching these people pack up their entire lives rather than be seen, because being seen is what got their country drowned, and I recognize it, Ede. I invented it. Hiding isn't survival. I told myself it was for ten years. It's just dying slowly somewhere no one can watch."

He picked up the ring. Turned it over. His thumb found the crest the way it must have found it a thousand times, for people he could barely remember.

"Sefa gave this to you," he said.

"Sefa gave this to me because she's dying and she doesn't get a vote anymore. You do. It's your seal. Your family carried it out of the water." She met his eyes. "If you tell me to trade it, I'll trade it. If you tell me to run, I'll run. But if you ask me what I think — I think we stop paying for our lives in disappearances. Both of us. Starting now."

For a long moment Ede said nothing. Then he laughed — a short, cracked sound, nothing like joy.

"Do you know what I did when the flood came?" he said. "I was six. My mother put me in a grain cask and sealed the lid, and I sat in the dark listening to the water take our street apart, plank by plank, and I swore — I swore on everything — that when I got out I would never hide from anything again." He closed his fist around the ring. "And then I spent twenty-two years hiding. In cellars. In freight cars. In other people's names. Because hiding kept me alive and my family dead, and I couldn't make those two facts live in the same body." He looked up at her. "You're the first person who ever said it out loud where I could hear it."

"So that's a yes?"

"That's a yes." He pressed the ring back into her palm and folded her fingers over it. "But hear me, ranger. If we do this, we do it all the way. No half-testimony. No convenient omissions. You give them the true name and the true record and you let it cost what it costs."

"It'll cost everything."

"Then we'll finally be spending the right currency." He stood, and offered her his hand, and after a moment she took it, and they walked down together into the valley to tell people who had been ready to vanish that they might, impossibly, be allowed to stay.

* * *

That night the settlement gathered in the long hall, and for the first time since Cyrus had arrived, no one packed anything.

She stood at the head of the room with the documents spread on a trestle table — the grant scrolls, brittle as moth wings; the registry pages in Vessian script that Sefa, propped on cushions and gray-faced but fierce, translated line by line in a voice like dry gravel; the seal itself, gleaming under lamplight. And she told them the plan, and she watched hope move through the room like a dangerous animal — wanted, feared, impossible to cage once loose.

An old woman near the back spoke the fear aloud. "Last time men with papers came, we drowned. Now you bring us more papers."

"Papers are how they erased you," Cyrus said. "Papers are also the only weapon they can't ignore. Marsh's backers aren't soldiers. They're accountants. Accountants can be beaten with arithmetic, and this—" she touched the grant scroll, "—is arithmetic. Prior grant. Continuous occupation. Sealed authority. Their claim dissolves the moment a sworn officer confirms it in open hearing."

"And you would swear? Knowing what they hold over you?"

"I would swear knowing *exactly* what they hold over me." She let the silence sit. "My name is Cyrus Quintana. Not the name on my current credentials — my real one. Ranger, second class, eleven years' service. I abandoned a rescue operation eight years ago. My partner died in it. There is a warrant for my testimony that has never been served because I made myself too expensive to find. Tomorrow all of that stops being leverage and starts being the truth, and the truth doesn't flinch."

The hall was silent. Then the old man who had folded the green flag rose, crossed the room, and stood before her. He was past eighty and moved like a man carrying furniture.

"My name is Aldous Ren," he said. "I was harbormaster at Kel Vessa. I signed the last order the nation ever issued — the evacuation that saved four hundred and eleven souls and lost nine thousand." He extended his hand. "If the seal speaks, then let it speak through all of us. You will not testify alone."

One by one, they stood. Farmers, weavers, two old engineers who had kept the aqueduct running in secret for four decades. Ede last of all, beside Sefa's cot, her thin hand gripping his wrist.

Cyrus had spent a decade learning how to be alone in a crowd. Standing in that hall, she discovered the opposite skill had been waiting in her the whole time, unpracticed, patient, like water finding a channel.

* * *

Later — much later, when the hall had emptied and the lamps burned low — Ede climbed to the ridge above the shrine where Cyrus kept watch, because of course she kept watch; some habits were load-bearing.

She heard him coming and didn't turn. He settled beside her on the cold rock, and for a while they watched the southern sky, where the storm front had begun to build — a bruised violet rampart crawling up over the desert, lightning flickering inside it like something trying to get out.

"Monsoon's early," Ede said.

"Two days early. Maybe three." Cyrus checked the wind out of habit. "It'll break over the high country before Marsh expects it. Her drones fly worse in rain than she thinks."

"You're smiling."

"I'm planning."

Below them the valley slept — really slept, not the tense pretending-sleep of the past week. Fires banked. Goats quiet. The shrine bell stirring once in the wind, a single low note that rolled down the slope and faded into the dark.

"They'll come tomorrow," Ede said. "Marsh. Before the storm, because she'll want it finished clean."

"She'll come at dawn, with her claim served and her team deployed and that polite voice of hers offering relocation terms nobody can refuse." Cyrus turned the seal ring over in her fingers. "And we'll be standing on the valley floor with four-hundred-year-old paperwork and a sworn ranger, and I am going to enjoy watching Odile Marsh do arithmetic she loses."

Ede laughed softly. Then he went quiet, and she felt him working up to something, the way he did.

"Cyrus. When this is over — whatever happens — the name. Quintana. Will you still want it?"

She considered the question honestly, the way she'd learned to consider things in this valley: slowly, and without an exit planned.

"A year ago I'd have said the name was the wound," she said. "Now I think the wound was cutting it out." She looked south, at the storm gathering over the drowned country's bones. "Ask me again after the monsoon."

Lightning walked the horizon, closer now, and far off in the gorge below the tree line, faint as memory, the memorial bells began to ring — stirred by the rising wind, all of them at once, a sound like a nation clearing its throat.

Dawn was coming. So was Marsh. So was the rain.

Cyrus put the seal away, shouldered her rifle, and started down toward the light.
