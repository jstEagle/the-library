## 20. The Naming

The rain stopped on the third morning after Marsh's retreat, and the valley came out of its clouds like something surfacing from deep water.

Cyrus stood at the door of the guest house they'd given her—a low stone room that had been a granary once, back when the Vessian valleys were still allowed to have granaries—and watched the light move down the terraces. The flood had left the gorge scoured and rearranged, boulders shifted like furniture in a house robbed overnight, but the settlement itself had held. The settlers had built for water the way her own people built for drought: everything raised, everything anchored, every channel and culvert cut with an old precision that predated the drowning by centuries. When the monsoon broke over the gorge, the water had gone where their ancestors told it to go.

She had watched it happen from the rim with Ede's hand locked around her wrist, both of them screaming things neither would later remember, while below them Sefa lay dying in the shrine house and Odile Marsh's team scrambled up the far side of the storm like ants fleeing a drowned nest.

Three days. She was still counting time in days since the storm, the way you counted time since a death or a birth.

"You're standing in the doorway again." Ede came up the path with two clay cups steaming against the cold. He'd stopped limping yesterday. "Mira says if you're going to haunt the threshold, you should at least do it holding something useful."

He handed her one of the cups. Barley tea, bitter and hot, the way they made it here. Cyrus wrapped her hands around it and said nothing, which he'd learned by now to accept as conversation.

Below them, the valley was working. That was the thing that kept catching in her chest like a fishhook—how ordinary it all was. Women hauling storm debris off the seed terraces. Two men repairing a section of aqueduct with fitted stones that hadn't moved in forty years of hiding. Children—there were children, eleven of them, she'd counted twice because she hadn't believed it the first time—running messages between work crews along paths worn smooth by generations of feet that had never once appeared on a government map.

A nation officially erased. A nation that had simply declined to stop existing.

"Mira wants you at the ledger house before midday," Ede said. He was looking out at the terraces, not at her, which was his way of making a request survivable. "The elders are meeting. They asked for you."

"Asked for me," Cyrus repeated. "Or asked for the ranger?"

Ede turned then. He was twenty-three years old and carried a country's grief in a body too young for it, and he had gotten very good, these three days, at looking at her without flinching from what the bounty notices had shown him. *Quintana, C. Service record attached. Subject abandoned post under fire. Partner deceased.* He'd read it in the checkpoint town and said nothing, and then he'd walked beside her for six more days through freezing high country anyway, and somehow that silence had been worse than any accusation and better than any forgiveness.

"They asked for you," he said. "Whichever one shows up."

* * *

The ledger house was the oldest building in the valley, older than the drowning, its walls hung with the same river-signs Cyrus had learned to read in the canyons—carved marks that mapped water through stone, a language of channels and depths. Inside, it smelled of cedar oil and lamp smoke. Along one wall ran shelves of bound volumes, hundreds of them, spines cracked and re-stitched, some of them visibly ancient.

Mira Vess sat at the head table. She was seventy-some years old and had the stillness of someone who had spent four decades being unremarkable on purpose, and Cyrus recognized the craft of it the way she recognized her own hands. Beside Mira sat five other elders, and behind them, propped upright in a chair wrapped in blankets because she had refused to be anywhere else, Sefa.

Sefa was dying. Everyone knew it; no one said it as if it were news. The wound from the ambush had gone bad in the cold, and whatever the valley's healers knew about drawing infection, they couldn't outrun what a body decides for itself. Her skin had gone the color of the ash they burned in the shrines. But her eyes were clear, and when Cyrus entered, they fixed on her with the same appraising sharpness she'd used the day Cyrus caught her shadowing them through the canyon—the day a prickly old woman inserted herself into Cyrus's life without invitation and never once asked permission.

"You look terrible," Sefa rasped.

"You look worse," Cyrus said.

"Good. Means I'm doing it right." She gestured Cyrus closer with two fingers. "Come here. I'm not going to shout."

Cyrus crossed the room. Up close, Sefa smelled of cedar oil and fever, and her hand, when it closed around Cyrus's wrist, had almost no strength left in it at all.

"The ring," Sefa said.

"I have it." It hung on a cord under her shirt, against her sternum, where it had lived since the pass above the tree line. She'd offered it back to Ede twice. Both times he'd shaken his head. *She gave it to you,* he'd said. *In Vessia, you don't hand back what someone gives you to carry. You carry it.*

"The ledger," Sefa went on, and her voice dropped into something formal, cadenced, a recitation worn smooth by repetition. "Every person who winters in this valley is written in the book. Has been since before the drowning. Names are how we survived—not maps, not borders, not treaties. Maps got us drowned. Names kept us." Her grip tightened, or tried to. "You stood on the rim and testified under your true name. In front of the company. In front of the water. That testimony is binding in this valley whether their courts ever hear of it or not. So now we write you."

Cyrus's mouth went dry. "I haven't decided—"

"You've decided," Sefa said. "You just haven't admitted it yet. It's the last thing you'll surrender, girl. I know. I surrendered mine sixty years ago, walking circuits of a dead country so no one would know a Vessian was still alive to be hunted." She released Cyrus's wrist and leaned back, exhausted by the effort, but her eyes didn't soften. "The difference between us is that I hid because they took everything. You hid because you gave it away yourself. That's harder to come back from. And braver, when you do."

Mira opened the current volume—a book perhaps thirty years old, its pages soft with handling—and dipped a pen. "We'll need the name you were born with," she said, without ceremony, without mercy. "Not Quintana. Not the name on the forged papers. The one your mother used."

The room was quiet. Rain ticked somewhere on the roof, the tail end of the season, almost done. Cyrus became aware of Ede in the doorway, and of the other elders watching, not with expectation exactly—with patience. They had waited forty years in a hole in the world. They could wait out one woman's silence.

Her true name. No one had spoken it in ten years. She had buried it under forged service records and burned supply manifests and a decade of careful, methodical erasure, and she had told herself it was survival, and part of it had been, and part of it had been the mission she abandoned and the partner she'd failed and the simple animal need to stop being anyone who could be blamed for anything. For ten years, *Cyrus Quintana* had been a costume worn by nobody at all.

But on the rim, in the teeth of the storm, with Marsh's claim hanging over the valley like a blade, she had stood up and spoken that buried name aloud, and the sky had not fallen, and the water had risen to meet her words instead of swallowing them.

"Cyrille," she said.

Her voice cracked on it. Ten years of dust coming off a word.

"Cyrille Anselm Quintana. My mother called me Cyrille."

Mira wrote it. The pen scratched, small and enormous, and Cyrus felt something shift in her chest that she had no training for—rangers were taught to manage fear, pain, exhaustion, cold—but not this. This was the feeling of a lock opening that she herself had welded shut, and the air that came through it was terrifyingly sweet.

"There," Sefa murmured. "Was that so hard?"

"It was the hardest thing I've done in this whole cursed valley, and I've been shot here twice."

Sefa laughed, which turned into coughing, which Ede moved to stop with a cup of something, and the moment broke apart into ordinary human noise, and Cyrus stood there in the ledger house with her name wet on a page and did not know what to do with her hands.

* * *

They asked her formally that evening, at the long fire in the center of the settlement, with the whole community gathered—forty-one people, plus eleven children, plus one dying woman carried out on a chair because she refused to miss it.

Mira spoke the offer plainly, the way Vessians seemed to do everything: winter was coming to the high country within weeks. The passes would seal with snow until spring. Anyone staying stayed until thaw. They were asking her to stay. Not as a ranger, not as a witness, not as the woman who'd broken Marsh's operation. As herself.

"We don't need protecting," Mira said. "We've hidden for forty years without a soldier. What we don't have is enough hands for the sealing—closing the channels, banking the stores, the work every winter demands. If you stay, you work. That's the whole bargain."

Around the fire, faces turned toward her. Old ones, young ones. A boy of maybe eight stared at her with open fascination, having heard from other children that she'd fought soldiers in the storm. Ede sat near Sefa's chair, and when Cyrus looked at him he didn't nod or smile or signal anything at all. He just looked back, steady, letting it be hers to choose. He'd learned that from her, she realized. Or she'd learned it from him. Somewhere in six hundred miles of canyon and cold, they'd taught each other the same lesson from opposite directions: you cannot make another person belong. You can only leave the door open and keep the fire lit.

Ten years ago, she would already have been gone. Ten years ago she'd have thanked them politely, walked out ahead of the snow, and erased even the memory of this valley from her own mind with the same discipline she'd used on everything else. Survival meant traveling light. Survival meant owing nothing to anyone, because debts were leashes and leashes got people killed—got partners killed, on missions she should never have led, in gorges she should never have entered.

But Sefa was dying in a chair by the fire, and had given Cyrus a bronze ring to carry, and Ede had read her worst sin off a bounty notice and walked beside her anyway, and forty-one people were asking her to help bank their grain for winter as if she were a person and not a problem.

"I'll stay," Cyrus said. "Until thaw. And I'll work."

The sound that went around the fire wasn't cheering—it was quieter than that, a settling, like a house accepting a new timber. Hands reached out to grip her shoulder as she sat down among them. Someone pressed a bowl of stew into her hands. The boy with the fascinated stare crept close enough to touch the compass hanging at her belt, and when she caught him at it, he froze, and she said, "Careful. It lies about north," and he grinned like she'd given him a gift.

Later, much later, when the fire had burned to coals and most people had gone to their houses, she found Ede at the shrine wall where the old compass from the salvage chest now hung on iron hooks, its needle swinging slowly, pointing—at whoever stood before it. That was the test Sefa had explained. It pointed at belonging, not north. For ten years it had pointed at nothing but silt and thieves.

It was pointing at Ede.

"It pointed at me first," Cyrus said, coming to stand beside him. "Back in the sandwash. Before I understood what it meant. I thought it was broken."

"It wasn't broken." Ede's breath fogged in the cold. "It just doesn't point at what you want. Only at what's true." He glanced at her sideways. "My grandmother used to say that about rivers, too."

They stood together in the dark while the needle swung between them, undecided, pointing first at him and then at her and then at the space they shared, and Cyrus thought: *this is what peace feels like,* and found that it frightened her more than Marsh's drones ever had. Peace meant something to lose.

* * *

She woke before dawn on the fourth morning, as she had woken before dawn every morning for fifteen years, some habits deeper than names. Frost furred the window. Below, the valley slept under a skin of white, chimneys beginning to breathe.

And lying there in the warm dark of the granary room, listening to a settlement she belonged to get ready for winter, Cyrus thought about the ranger station in the lowlands. About the forged records still filed there under her alias—the clean false history she'd built, the second self still sitting in those cabinets like a body waiting to be identified. Marsh had seen it. Marsh's people had copied it. And Marsh had retreated, but Marsh was not finished; Cyrus had watched her face across the flooding gorge and seen a woman recalculating, not resigning.

As long as those records existed, the lie existed. And as long as the lie existed, some part of Cyrille Anselm Quintana was still living a double life in a filing cabinet two hundred miles downstream.

There was a window before the passes sealed. Maybe a week. Maybe less.

She dressed in the dark, took down the pack she'd sworn she was done needing, and went to tell Ede she'd be back before the snow.
