## 21. What She Burned

The descent took four days, and Cyrus made it slowly on purpose.

There was no one chasing her now. That was the strange part—the absence of it, like a tooth gone from the jaw, the tongue returning again and again to the gap. For ten years she had moved through the desert as a woman being hunted by paper: warrants that didn't exist yet, files that could be pulled, a name she'd buried so deep even she sometimes had to dig for it. Now the valley was behind her, sealed under its first winter snows, and the paper had lost its teeth, and she kept catching herself listening for drones that weren't there.

She carried little. The compass stayed in the valley—Sefa's people had hung it in the shrine at the gorge's mouth, where it swung lazily on its brass pivot and pointed, always, at whoever stood before it. Ede had laughed when he saw it do that to a settler child, the needle swinging like a dog turning to greet its owner. *It only points for those who belong,* he'd said. *It's been pointing at us our whole lives. We just couldn't read it.*

Cyrus had stood before it too, on her last morning in the valley. The needle had come around slow, deliberate, like something deciding. She hadn't told anyone what that did to her chest. Some things you carried without naming them, or they got heavy.

What she carried down were three things: Sefa's walking staff, which the old woman's family had pressed into her hands at the funeral with a look that permitted no argument; a copy of the water-rights documents, inked on rag paper older than the ranger service itself; and her own credentials pack, retrieved at last from Marsh's evidence lockers by terms Cyrus still didn't fully understand—a clause in the settlement, Marsh had said, smiling her courteous smile, *you'd be amazed what falls out of a contract when a corporation decides a valley isn't worth the litigation.*

The staff she would leave at the lowland shrine. The documents were already lodged with an archivist in Corriander who worked for fees, not loyalties. That left the third thing, and the third thing was the reason she was walking back to a ranger station she had spent a decade learning how to avoid.

* * *

The station at Dry Fork sat where the dead riverbed widened into scrub flats, a low building of adobe and corrugated tin that looked like it had been dropped there by accident and never picked up. Cyrus remembered it differently from her last visit—remembered it as a place of threat, Odile Marsh standing in the doorway with her questions folded neat as letters. Now it was just a building, sun-bleached, half its weather vane missing, a windmill turning with the arthritic patience of machinery nobody loved.

The duty officer was a young man named Pell, new since her time, with the particular earnestness of someone who still believed the uniform meant what the recruiting posters said. He looked up when she came in, took in the dust on her, the staff slung across her pack, and reached for the logbook.

"Help you, ma'am?"

"I'm here to resign my commission." She set her credentials pack on the counter. "Cyrus Quintana. Ranger, second class, out of the Corriander district. Ten years' service, most of it—" she paused, and found she could say it now without flinching, "—most of it filed under other names."

Pell looked at the pack. Looked at her. She watched him do the arithmetic every young ranger eventually had to do: the ghost stories about rangers who erased themselves, the rumors that were mostly rumor, and here one standing at his counter smelling of woodsmoke and high-altitude cold.

"You're supposed to be dead," he said, then went red. "I mean—the file says—"

"The file says whatever the file says. I'm telling you what's true." She slid a folded paper across the counter, written out by hand at the valley's fire the night before she left, witnessed by two settlers whose names predated the drowning. "Formal resignation, effective today. I'm told the service prefers these things typed. I'm also told the service has bigger problems than my handwriting."

He didn't laugh. He opened the pack instead, and she let him, because that was the whole point of this—that someone official would open it, would see what was inside, would enter it into a record that could no longer hurt anyone she loved.

Inside: her original commission, issued to a twenty-four-year-old woman who had believed the desert asked nothing of her but survival and had not yet learned the desert asked everything. Her service citations, including one for a flood-season rescue on the Vessia survey line that had never been officially acknowledged because the operation it belonged to had never officially happened. And beneath those, wrapped in oilcloth, the forged identities—four of them, each a life she had worn like a coat and shed like one: Mara Voss, depot clerk. Ansel Reyes, well surveyor. T. Calloway, freight rider. And the thinnest one, the one she'd worn longest, a woman with no first name at all, just initials and a pay code, a person so faint on paper she could walk through checkpoints like weather.

Ten years of becoming no one. It weighed less than she expected. It had always weighed less than she expected; that was the trick of it, the way a heavy pack feels lighter the longer you refuse to set it down.

"Quintana," Pell said, testing the name. "You're the one from the Marsh inquiry. The valley."

"I'm the one."

"They're saying the company pulled out of the whole high-country claim. Saying the water rights go back a hundred years and the settlers hold them." He looked up, and there was something in his face that wasn't suspicion—it was the look of a young man discovering the world was stranger and better-ordered than his training manual. "They're saying a ranger testified. Under her real name."

"Rangers say a lot of things."

"Yes, ma'am," he said, and stamped the resignation anyway, and entered her into the log in full, unhidden letters, and the sound of the stamp was small and enormous, like a door closing gently in a house she was finally allowed to leave.

* * *

She burned the rest that evening behind the station, in the iron burn barrel the rangers used for classified waste, because some things deserved to die in official fire rather than drift away as ash on private wind.

Pell brought the oil himself and then, with a tact she wouldn't have credited him an hour ago, withdrew to the porch and pretended to study a maintenance schedule. Cyrus fed the forged identities into the barrel one at a time.

Mara Voss went first. The paper caught with a soft whump, and for a moment the inked face seemed to warm, almost to smile, before curling black. Cyrus had liked being Mara Voss. Mara Voss had been patient, unhurried, a woman who mended her own gear and asked no questions at depots. Goodbye, Mara. You served me well and I never once thanked you.

Ansel Reyes next. Ansel had taught her to read well-drilling signs, had gotten her into places a woman alone couldn't go. The flames took his careful forged signature and turned it to lace.

T. Calloway burned fast, a light life, barely lived.

Last she unwrapped the oilcloth from the fourth identity—the initials, the pay code, the ghost—and held it over the barrel without letting go. This was the one that hurt, and she examined the hurt with the same attention she gave a wound in the field: locate it, clean it, decide if it needed stitching or just pressure.

It wasn't grief exactly. It was more like the moment at the end of a long climb when you take off your boots and your feet keep aching in the shape of them. Ten years of moving unseen had kept her alive, yes—but it had also kept her alone, and somewhere in the last year she had stopped believing those were the same thing. Survival wasn't solitude. Survival was what Sefa's people had done for forty years: hiding together, singing together in a gorge hung with bells, keeping a nation alive not in archives but in each other.

The monsoon had broken over the valley while she testified. She thought of it often—the wall of rain coming down the gorge like a verdict, the bells ringing under it, Sefa's grave already green-edged on the slope above the settlement. The river forgives nothing, Sefa used to say. But it carries everything downstream eventually, and what it leaves behind is ground you can build on.

Cyrus let the last identity fall into the fire.

She watched until the barrel held nothing but gray flakes lifting on the heat, and then she stood there a while longer in the blue dusk, hands flat on the barrel's warm rim, feeling the weight go out of her shoulders the way it goes out of a rope when the load finally reaches the ground.

* * *

She smelled the cigarette before she heard the voice.

"You always did like a ceremonial fire."

Odile Marsh stood at the edge of the yard, past the boundary stones, where the scrub started—far enough out that no reasonable person could call it trespass, close enough that there was no pretending she hadn't seen everything. She wore traveling clothes instead of company gray, and she looked tired in a way that sat oddly on her, like a borrowed coat.

"Pell will run you off," Cyrus said.

"Pell is inside reading your file with the expression of a man meeting a saint. No one's running me anywhere." Marsh drew on the cigarette, exhaled. "I came to see it done. I told myself that, anyway. On the drive out I tried a few better reasons and none of them held."

"Then why are you here?"

Marsh considered the question honestly, which was the most dangerous thing about her—she never lied when the truth would cut deeper. "Because I spent eleven months trying to erase a valley, and you spent ten years trying to erase yourself, and we both failed, and I wanted to watch someone fail correctly." She flicked ash. "The backers disavowed the operation. You know that. What you may not know is that they offered me a transfer. Singapore desk. Water futures." A thin smile. "I declined. I find I've lost my appetite for owning things that don't want to be owned."

"That'll be a short career."

"It was always going to be." Marsh looked at the burn barrel, at the last gray spirals rising. "Your records are flagged closed. Resignation logged, identities voided, the bounty file pulled this morning. As far as the service is concerned, Cyrus Quintana resigned honorably and disappeared into private life—which, given the circumstances, is nearly true." She paused. "I saw you burn the forgeries. All of them. I could report an unauthorized destruction of service documents."

"They weren't service documents. They were mine."

"That's precisely what makes it reportable." Marsh let the silence sit, then crushed the cigarette under her heel with unnecessary care. "I won't report it. Not out of mercy—I don't think you need my mercy, and I'd resent you for accepting it. I won't report it because a file that says *Cyrus Quintana, resigned, whereabouts unknown* is worth nothing to anyone, and a file that says *Cyrus Quintana, burned her own names and walked into the mountains* is worth a great deal—to the wrong people, and to the right ones, and I'm done deciding which is which." She turned to go, then stopped. "For what it's worth. The testimony was good. Clean, sourced, unshakable. You'd have made a fine lawyer."

"Worse things have been said about me."

"I know. I wrote most of them." Marsh walked toward her vehicle, then, without looking back, added: "The compass. In the reports afterward, the settlers described it pointing at whoever stands before it. My analysts called it a folk mechanism. A test disguised as an instrument." A beat. "I've been wondering all the way out here what it pointed at when you stood in front of it."

Cyrus looked at the darkening east, where the mountains stood up against the last light like a wall with a door in it.

"It pointed at me," she said.

Marsh nodded once, as if a figure had finally balanced, and drove away down the dead riverbed with her headlights sweeping the silt where a drowned nation's chest had once waited forty years for someone to dig it out.

* * *

Cyrus slept that night in the station's bunk room, at Pell's insistence—"it's empty, ma'am, and it's going to freeze"—and woke before dawn out of habit, out of ten years of habit, and lay in the dark listening to the windmill turn.

No alarms. No drones. No name but hers, and hers only where she had put it.

She rose, packed the staff and the empty oilcloth, and ate breakfast in the mess with Pell, who asked shy, careful questions about the gorge of bells and wrote her answers in the margin of his maintenance schedule as though they were regulations. When she left, he saluted. She returned it, awkwardly, the first salute she'd given or received in a decade, and felt it land somewhere in her chest like a stone dropped down a well—long falling, then, at last, water.

Outside, the desert was turning pink. East, the high country stood white to its shoulders, and somewhere beyond those shoulders a valley was sealing itself under snow, and in that valley a ledger waited in a warm room, and in that ledger, in a settler's careful hand, her true name was already written—the first record in ten years she hadn't forged, fled, or burned.

She shouldered her pack. It was light. It was astonishingly light.

Spring would come early this year; Sefa's circuits said so, and Sefa's circuits had never once lied about water. Which meant supplies to buy in Corriander, a mule to hire, and a climb she already knew by heart—up the dead river, past the confluence, through the gorge where the bells hung waiting to be rung again.

Cyrus Quintana turned her back on the station, on the burn barrel, on the whole architecture of the woman she had been, and started walking east, toward the door in the wall, toward the gate where a young man with his family's ring would be watching the pass for her the way he'd promised.

Toward home—not because it needed her, but because she had chosen it, and meant to keep choosing it, every season, for as long as the river ran.
