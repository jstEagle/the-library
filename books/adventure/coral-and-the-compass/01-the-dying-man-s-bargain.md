## 1. The Dying Man's Bargain

The trouble with fueling docks is that they attract people, and I have spent twenty years building a life that runs best on empty.

I'd tied the *Magpie* up at Cutter's Landing just past dawn, meaning to take on diesel, take on water, and take on nothing else. No passengers. No cargo worth insuring. No conversation longer than it took to count out coin. That was the whole architecture of my existence—load, run, unload, vanish—and it had kept me alive through two decades of debts, deputies, and men who said my name like a curse or a bounty. The river forgives everything except being known, and I had made myself a rumor with a boat attached.

So naturally, before the first hose was even coupled, a dying man fell across my gangplank like the river itself had thrown him there.

He came down the dock at an angle, one hand out for balance he no longer owned, and his knees went at the exact spot where my hull meets the planks. I heard it happen more than saw it—the wet slap of a body giving up its argument with gravity—and by the time I got to him he was already trying to get up, which is the thing about the stubborn ones. They die slower and louder.

"Captain." He had gray in his beard and gold dust ground so deep into the creases of his knuckles it looked like the man himself had been panned for. "Captain Winterbourne."

Nobody used that name at landings. Nobody used it anywhere. I crouched anyway, because he was bleeding from somewhere under his coat and because there's a kind of fever you can smell, sweet and wrong, like fruit going off in a closed room.

"You've got me confused with somebody reputable," I said.

"No." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that brought up something dark. "No, I don't. That's rather the point."

He weighed nothing. That's what struck me, hauling him up the gangplank with his arm over my shoulders—he weighed what a man weighs when the river's already started taking him back, ounce by ounce, into the current of things. I got him into the wheelhouse and onto the bench under the window, and I got water into him, and none of it mattered. I could see that plain as charted channel. Some part of me had known it from the angle he fell at.

"I need a boat going up," he said. "Not down. Everybody goes down. Down is easy."

"Up's shut," I told him. "Dry season's coming early this year. Ask anyone."

"I'm not asking anyone. I'm asking you." His eyes were doing something fever does—burning too bright, focusing past me at things on the far side of the bulkhead. "They say Tamsin Winterbourne will take anything upriver if the price is right and the questions are few."

"They say a lot of things about me. Most of them are flattery."

"Then hear the questions anyway." He fumbled inside his coat, and every instinct I own—which is a considerable fleet, well-maintained—went to general quarters. Men reaching inside coats at fueling docks have historically meant me nothing but paperwork and pain. But what came out wasn't iron. It was oilcloth, wrapped tight and tied with waxed cord, soft-edged, hand-sized. He laid it on his knee like it was made of eggshell.

"A map," he said.

"I don't deal in maps."

"Everyone deals in maps. You just do it by omission." He looked at me then, clear-eyed for one terrible second, the way the dying get when they spend their last good coin of lucidity all at once. "Twenty years, Captain. Twenty years and the upper river's still blank above the Narrows. Every survey skipped it. Every charter lost the pages. Every pilot swears the falls eat boats. And here you are, running cargo nobody asks about to places nobody can find, year after year, like a woman walking backward sweeping her own footprints."

I didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a confession, and I've survived this long on the strict discipline of never confirming anything, including the weather.

"I'm Hollis Vane," he said. "I found the source. Or near enough. And there are three men who intend to find it through me, and I intend to disappoint them permanently, but I have a daughter in the high country who deserves better than to learn it from them."

"Then send her a letter."

"The letter's written. It needs delivering, and letters travel by post, and post travels by road, and the road passes through the hands of the men who killed—" He stopped. Closed his eyes. Started over, quieter. "Who want what I have. The map doesn't go by post. It goes by *her*. By someone with a fast boat and no fixed address and a talent for being unfindable. Which is, I'm told, your entire résumé."

Somewhere on the dock a deckhand shouted numbers at the fuel pump, ordinary as church bells, while my whole careful life sat there weighing its options.

"How much?" I asked, because money was the safe question. Money I could refuse cleanly.

"Everything I have left, which isn't much, and something better than money." He put his hand flat on the oilcloth bundle. "A trade. The map shows where the source is—and more than that, it shows the reaches above the Narrows that no chart has ever held. Channels, depths, the safe line through the falls country. You've spent twenty years keeping that river blank. This puts it in your pocket instead of the government's. No surveyor ever gets it. No steamboat company. It stays yours, and it makes you the only captain alive who knows the way up."

"And the promise?"

"You carry it to my daughter. Mari Vane, Willowmere claim, high country, before the dry season ends and the upper water drops below the bars. You give it to her hands, no one else's. That's the whole contract. One trip. One delivery."

"And then?"

"Then you burn it, if she says so. Her choice, not yours, not mine." His breath caught. "It was always going to be hers. I just needed it to reach her."

I stood up. I walked to the window and looked at the river, because looking at rivers is easier than looking at men who are dying in your wheelhouse. The water was already dropping—you could see it in the mud lines on the pilings, a fresh stripe of exposed bank like the river showing its ledger. Dry season early. Low water. A run upstream through country I'd spent half my life pretending didn't exist, carrying the single most dangerous piece of paper between the mountains and the sea, pursued by men who killed prospectors for claims.

Every instinct I own voted no. Unanimous. The vote wasn't even close.

But he'd said *killed*, hadn't he, right before he caught himself. And he was dying on my bench with gold dust in his knuckles and a daughter waiting in the high country, and there was a version of me—a younger one, one I keep locked in the hold with the other ballast—who would have understood exactly what it costs to have something you can't deliver yourself.

"What did they do to you?" I asked. "The men."

"Shot me twice back in the spring. Missed everything important, which offended them, so they tried again last week and hit something that matters." He said it mildly, like reporting weather. "I've been running downriver for six days hoping to find a boat fool enough. The fever's just finishing their work."

"Doctor at Bend Landing could—"

"Would ask questions. Questions kill faster than bullets, Captain. You know that better than anyone on this river." He smiled, and it was a real smile, which was somehow worse. "That's why it has to be you. Not because you're good. Because you're *gone*. No name on any register that matters, no home port worth checking, no past you'll admit to. You're the only person on this water who's invisible. I need the map carried by someone invisible."

And there it was. Twenty years of careful erasure, held up to the light and called exactly what it was—not freedom, just hiding—and offered back to me as a qualification.

I should have refused. Let me be clear about that for the record I'll never keep: the correct answer, the survivable answer, the answer consistent with two decades of disciplined self-erasure, was *no*. Take his money for fuel, leave him at the landing for the river law, keep my blank map and my blank life.

Instead I heard myself say, "Show me."

He unwrapped the oilcloth with fingers that shook, and inside was paper—good paper, waterproofed with wax, covered edge to edge in a draftsman's hand gone slightly wild. Reaches I knew only as rumor, drawn in ink with soundings marked, channels threaded through the falls country, and at the top, past everything, a small blue star and one word: *SOURCE*. He'd drawn the river the way you draw a person you love, every bend remembered.

"It stays wrapped," he said, retying the cord. "Until Willowmere. Swear it."

"You have my word."

"Your word." He tested the phrase in his mouth like a coin he suspected. Then he nodded. "From what I hear, that's the only currency you've never devalued."

He died four hours later, at the fueling dock, aboard my boat, with his head against the wheelhouse bench and his hand resting on the rail like he was holding the *Magpie* steady for the next pilot. I closed his eyes and took the letter from his coat—the one addressed to Mari Vane in a hand gone shaky but legible—and I put it with the map, and I paid the harbormaster to bury him under a name I suspect wasn't entirely his, because a man who draws a river that lovingly deserves a plot with a view of water.

Then I cast off at dusk, ahead of schedule, running light, headed up.

Which is how, two days later at Bend Landing, taking on the last of my stores before the Narrows, I looked up from my manifest to find a woman standing on my gangplank with a government seal on her document case and a surveyor's eye already measuring my freeboard—and felt the bottom drop out of twenty years of careful, invisible living.

* * *

She had hair the color of dry reeds and the particular stillness of someone who watches for a living, and she said, "Captain Winterbourne? Coral Ashford, Office of Rivers and Boundaries," in a tone that suggested she already knew the answer to whatever she asked next.

I looked at the upper river, low and glittering and full of secrets, and then at the sealed case in her hands, and I thought: *Hollis, old man, your timing was always terrible.*

"Depends who's asking," I said, and stepped aside to let the future aboard.
