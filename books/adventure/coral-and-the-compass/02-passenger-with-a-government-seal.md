## 2. Passenger with a Government Seal

The landing at Kestrel Bend was nothing but a plank walkway, a fuel drum or two, and a man in a gray coat who stood too straight for the kind of town that grew up around river mud.

I saw him from the pilothouse while I was still a quarter mile out and told myself he was waiting for the mail boat. Then I told myself he was waiting for a funeral. By the time I'd swung the *Magpie* alongside and thrown her lines to the dock, I had run out of comfortable lies, which is generally how my mornings go.

He had a leather case at his feet, brass-buckled, government-issue. I've robbed enough of them to know.

"Captain Winterbourne?" he called up, and his voice carried the particular polish of somebody who'd never had to shout over machinery to be heard. "Coral Ashford. I have passage booked as far as you'll take me."

"Depends how far that is."

"The headwaters."

I looked at him for a long moment. He was younger than I'd guessed from the dock—thirty, maybe, with sunburn just starting on the back of his neck where his hat didn't reach, and eyes the flat green of river glass. He smiled like he was pleased with everything he surveyed, including me.

"No boat goes to the headwaters," I said. "River gets mean past the Narrows. Shifting bars, no soundings worth trusting. Ask anybody."

"I did ask anybody. Anybody said you were the only captain fool enough or skilled enough to make the run." He tipped his hat back. "So which is it?"

"Foolishness is cheaper," I said, and started to turn away.

"Captain." The word came out quiet, and quiet made it worse. "I'm not asking as a passenger. I'm asking as an officer of the Territorial Survey."

There it was. Twenty years I'd kept that stretch of water blank—every map I'd ever signed, every chart I'd ever sold, every drunk story I'd ever told a lawman with a notebook, the upper river simply didn't exist. It was fog and rumor and a hundred miles of *nothing to see here*. I'd paid bribes in three towns to keep it that way. I'd watched a man get beaten half to death in a saloon for sketching the wrong bend in the wrong ledger. Blank water was the only inheritance I had, and this boy in a gray coat wanted to draw lines all over it.

"Survey's got no business up there," I said. "Nothing up there but rocks and weather."

"Then you won't mind taking me to see them."

* * *

I should have refused. Let me say that plainly, because everything that came after—the dead man's map, the gunpowder men, the girl at the source, all of it—started with the fact that I didn't. I could have cast off his lines and left him standing on that plank walkway with his brass buckles and his polished questions, and maybe the *Magpie* would be a coal barge by now instead of what she is.

But there's a thing about being seen that works on a person like rust works on iron. You can paint over it. You can tell yourself you don't want it. And then somebody looks at you—not through you, not past you, but *at* you, like you're terrain worth mapping—and something in you gives way before your pride can stop it.

He paid in advance. That helped. Gold coin, counted onto my palm without ceremony, enough to cover fuel and then some. Debts don't care about principles, and mine had been circling the boat like gulls since spring.

"One condition," I said. "You stay out of my pilothouse. You keep your instruments off my deck unless I say otherwise. And when we pass water I tell you not to look at, you look somewhere else."

His eyebrows went up, just slightly. "That's an unusual contract for a commissioned surveyor."

"That's the only one on offer."

He considered me for a moment longer than was polite, and I had the distinct, unpleasant sensation of being measured—depths taken, banks noted, hazards marked. Then he put out his hand.

"Agreed, Captain. For now."

*For now.* I shook on it anyway. I've signed worse contracts with better handwriting.

* * *

He settled into the small forward cabin like a cat deciding whether a room belongs to it yet. Unpacked with military neatness: instruments wrapped in oilcloth, notebooks bound in canvas, a sextant he handled the way some men handle a lover's wrist. And books. Charts. Dozens of them, rolled and stacked, and every one I glimpsed showed the upper river as I'd kept it—a smear of hatching and the words *UNSURVEYED — NAVIGATION IMPRACTICABLE*, in a clerk's tired hand.

Twenty years of my work, summarized in two lines of somebody else's ink.

"You've been up there," he said from the doorway that first evening. Not a question. He'd caught me looking at the charts, and I suppose my face did what faces do.

"I've been everywhere this river goes."

"Mm." He leaned against the frame, arms crossed, watching me with that maddening patience. "The stories say a woman ran a boat through the Devil's Throat fifteen years ago, alone, at flood stage, and came out the other side without scratching her paint. The survey tried to find her afterward. She didn't exist either."

"Stories exaggerate."

"They usually start somewhere." He tilted his head. "You have pilot's hands, Captain. Old rope burns. And you knew the name of the Narrows before I said it, though officially nobody's named them yet."

I turned back to the wheel. "You're a long way from your office, Mr. Ashford."

"Coral," he said. "And yes. Deliberately so. My superiors think the upper river is a waste of appropriations. I think it's the last honest water in the territory, and somebody's been working very hard to keep it dishonest." A pause, long enough for the paddle wheel to slap the current twice. "I intend to find out who, and why."

I held the wheel steady and gave him nothing but my back, which took more discipline than running any rapids ever has. Because here's the ugly truth I haven't told anyone, not even the page you're reading now: for one black moment, moored in the dark off Kestrel Bend with a dead man's map sewn into my coat lining and a surveyor's questions at my neck, I thought about the river. About taking the *Magpie* up past the last landing and scuttling her in deep water, walking away from the debt and the promise and the whole wet mess of my life. Starting blank again, the way I always had.

Then Coral Ashford said, good night, Captain, in that courteous voice, and went aft to his cabin, and I stood alone in the pilothouse with the lamp turned low, and I realized the thought hadn't been about escape at all. It had been about him. About not wanting those green eyes to find what I'd hidden, because some part of me—the part I keep locked below the waterline—wanted very much to show him.

That's the danger of a man like that. Not the commission. Not the charts. The danger is that he makes invisibility feel like drowning.

* * *

We made forty miles the next day, easy water, and I learned things about Coral Ashford the way you learn anything worth knowing—sideways, by accident, when he wasn't performing.

I learned he got seasick in the least funny way possible, going quietly green below decks and coming up an hour later immaculate and daring anyone to mention it. I learned he talked to the river under his breath, naming its moods like they were horses. I learned that when I cursed the engine—and I curse it fluently, in languages I deny knowing—he laughed, once, a short surprised bark, and then pretended he hadn't.

And I learned he watched me. Constantly, professionally, hungrily. Every time I read the water—every feather of current, every submerged rock I steered around a half-second before it showed itself—I felt his attention land on me like a hand on the shoulder. He kept a notebook open on his knee in the pilothouse doorway, and I knew, I *knew*, that half of what he wrote in it was me.

"You anticipate the channel," he said on the second afternoon, appearing at my elbow with coffee he'd made himself, which was terrible. "The charts end at Mile 60. How do you know what's past it?"

"Memory."

"Of a river you've never officially traveled."

"Officially," I agreed, "I've never been past Mile 60 in my life."

He sipped his terrible coffee and studied me over the rim of the cup, and I swear to you the man *smiled*, slow and satisfied, like a prospector seeing color in his pan. "Then you're the finest natural talent on this water, Captain, or you're lying to a federal officer. Either way, I find myself very interested in you."

"There are laws against that second one."

"There are laws against most interesting things." He set the cup down on the sill, close enough that our fingers nearly touched, and whether that was accident or survey I couldn't say. "I'll find out which you are eventually. I'm patient."

"Patience sinks boats," I said, and rang the engine bell louder than I needed to.

* * *

That night I lay awake in my bunk listening to the river talk against the hull, doing arithmetic I didn't like. A dying prospector's promise stitched into my lining, due upriver before the dry season ended. A surveyor aboard with a commission to unmake the only country I'd ever kept safe by erasing it. And behind us both, somewhere downriver, whatever it was Silas Hale had been running from when he collapsed onto my deck with blood in his spit and a map in his fist—because men don't sweat like that over geology.

Two passengers, I thought. One dead, one dangerous. And me in the middle, holding the wheel, holding the secret, holding a course I hadn't chosen toward a place I'd sworn no chart would ever find.

Above the last landing, the hills were already turning gold at the edges. Dry season coming early. Low water coming early. Everything coming early this year, like the river itself knew what we were carrying and wanted it done with.

In the morning, when I came up to relieve the watch, Coral Ashford was already in the pilothouse doorway, hat in hand, morning light catching the brass of his instrument case.

"Captain," he said pleasantly. "I hope you slept well. Because last night, while you were sleeping, a boat passed us going the other way, and the men aboard it asked after a prospector named Hale." His green eyes were calm and absolutely awake. "They described him in detail. They also asked whether anyone aboard this vessel might be carrying his effects."

The paddle wheel slapped. Somewhere downstream, a loon cried like a woman grieving.

"And what did you tell them?" I asked.

"What do you think I told them, Captain?" Coral Ashford smiled, and for the first time I understood that the smile was a weapon he kept holstered. "I told them the truth. That I hadn't seen any prospector." A beat. "It wasn't the whole truth. But it was true."

He stepped aside to let me take the wheel, and I gripped it and said nothing, because the only thing louder than the engine was the sudden knowledge that the man standing beside me had just lied to armed strangers on my behalf—and that I had no idea yet whether that made him my ally, my captor, or the most dangerous thing this river had carried in twenty years.
