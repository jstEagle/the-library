## 3. Men Who Smell of Gunpowder

I have read enough government reports to know that most men lie in the same direction as their boots point. These two lied toward the door.

They came aboard at Miller's Landing while the *Kestrel* took on coal, and they came the way trouble always comes—separately, then together, then all at once. The tall one called himself Mr. Quill, which was a lie so lazy it was almost charming. His companion was shorter, wider through the shoulders, with a face like a fist that had been used. Neither of them had come upriver for the scenery.

"Looking for a man," Quill said to the deckhand, loud enough for the whole boat to hear. "Prospector. Older fellow, gray beard, half out of his wits. Family's worried."

The deckhand, who had been raised on this river and therefore trusted nothing that arrived by land, said he hadn't seen anybody and went back to his coal.

I was sitting in the saloon with my charts spread across two tables, doing what I am paid to do, which is make the river legible to men in offices who will never smell it. I looked up when they boarded because looking up is also part of my job. And I knew them.

Not their names—the files I'd read had given me names, but names are the cheapest thing a man carries. I knew them by their work. Two years ago a survey party I'd trained under had documented a string of jumped claims along the middle fork: wells poisoned, stakes pulled, one claimant missing and presumed drowned. The report had included statements from witnesses, and the witness descriptions had matched these two men the way a key matches a lock—Quill with his soft voice and his hard eyes, the other one whose knuckles were scarred like a plowed field. Claim-jumpers. The kind of men who made the wilderness dangerous not despite its emptiness but because of it.

And here they were, asking after a prospector, on the boat of a captain who had taken that prospector aboard at a fueling dock three days ago.

I know this because I watch everything. It is a professional habit and a personal failing.

I said nothing. I want that recorded plainly, because everything after it grew out of that silence. I sat at my table with my compass and my ink-stained fingers and I let two murderers walk past me, because telling them what I suspected would have meant explaining how I knew, which would have meant explaining why I was aboard, which would have meant handing Captain Winterbourne exactly the weapon she'd need against me—and worse, it would have meant admitting that I already believed Tamsin Winterbourne was hiding something worth killing over.

Duty is a strange animal. It is supposed to be a compass. Some days it behaves more like a noose.

The short one lingered in the saloon doorway. He looked at my charts—really looked, the way a man looks at a thing he intends to take later—and asked me if I was with the government.

"I'm a surveyor," I said. "Charts and soundings. Nothing interesting."

"Upper river?"

"Eventually."

He smiled at that, and I did not like his smile. It had too many teeth for the amount of warmth behind it. Then he left, and I sat very still and listened to my own heart do arithmetic.

Here is what I could not stop calculating: if those men were hunting a prospector, and the prospector had died aboard this boat, then whatever he'd carried had value enough to kill for. And if Tamsin Winterbourne—a woman who has spent twenty years making sure her boat appears on no manifest, no ledger, no map—had accepted something from a dying stranger, then she was carrying it still. People like Tamsin don't take cargo out of charity. Charity is a luxury of the anchored.

Unless she did. Unless there was something in that woman that the river hadn't managed to grind down yet. I found I wanted that to be true, and wanting it made me suspicious of myself, which is the worst kind of suspicion there is.

* * *

Evening found us moored below the narrows, where the banks pinch together and the water runs deep and quick. The crew ate on deck. Tamsin ate last, standing at the wheel even though we weren't going anywhere, chewing bread like it owed her money.

She is not an easy woman to look at, and I mean that as a compliment. She has a face that has decided against being handsome and settled instead on being unreadable, which takes more discipline. Her hands are a map of their own—every line a rope burn or a frost scar or a story she'd deny. When she stood at the wheel in the lamplight with her collar open and her jaw set, I caught myself charting her the way I chart coastlines: noting the features, estimating the depths, warning myself about the shoals.

I am a careful woman. Careful women should not fall for captains. It never ends well; there are precedents.

"You eat standing up," I said, because silence between us had started to feel like a debt.

"Eating sitting down is how you get old." She didn't look at me. "You going to chart the whole river or just the parts you like?"

"The parts nobody's charted. That's the assignment."

Something moved behind her eyes—quick, gone, like a fish turning under the surface. "Nobody's charted it for a reason."

"Rivers don't keep secrets," I said. "People do."

That earned me a look. Not hostile. Worse—appraising. The look of a woman deciding whether I was weather to be endured or a reef to be avoided. "Goodnight, Miss Ashford," she said, and turned back to her wheel, and I went below feeling like I'd lost a hand of cards I hadn't known we were playing.

* * *

I couldn't sleep. The river talks at night—it slaps the hull, mutters over the shoals, carries voices from the far bank—and my cabin was hot and close and full of the memory of Quill's smile. So near midnight I took the lamp I wasn't supposed to need and went up to the pilothouse, thinking I'd sit with the wheel and the dark and my own dishonesty for a while.

The pilothouse lamp was already lit.

Tamsin stood at the chart table with her back to the door, and she was holding something flat and folded, and she was studying it the way I have only ever seen people study things that frighten them. I saw the paper before I understood it—I have spent my life around maps; I know their weight in a hand before I know their content. Hand-drawn. Ink gone brown with age and sweat. A river running north into country that every official survey ends with a small apologetic blank space and the words *unexplored*.

My breath went out of me quietly. Because I knew that blank space. I had been commissioned to fill it. And here was its outline, drawn by some dead or dying man's hand, glowing under a lamp in the pilothouse of a boat that officially did not exist.

She heard me—or felt me; I think the woman can feel a change in air pressure—and turned. For one long moment neither of us moved. The lamp hissed. The river ran.

"Well," she said finally, and her voice was level as a still pool, "you're up late, Miss Ashford."

"So are you." I nodded at the paper. "That's a map."

"It's a grocery list."

"It's the source of the river."

We looked at each other across the chart table, and I watched her decide things. I watched her decide not to lie outright—that was new for her, I'd learn later; lying outright was usually her preference—and instead to stand very still and let me draw my own conclusions, the way a card player shows nothing and lets your greed do the betting.

"And if it were?" she said. "What would you do? Report it? Put it on one of your pretty charts so every speculator and land-grabber between here and the capital can come up and carve it into claims?"

"That isn't what surveys are for."

"No?" She set the paper down, deliberately, face-up, which was either trust or a dare. "Then what are they for, Miss Ashford? Who pays you? What do they want with the high country? You board my boat with a government seal in your pocket and ask me questions like a customs man, and now you find a map in my pilothouse and you want me to believe you'll file it away nice and honest." She shook her head slowly. "I've spent twenty years keeping that river off of paper. Every map you draw is a wound. Paper bleeds."

I should have told her then. About Quill and his friend, about the files, about the letter I'd received before boarding naming the territory and the deadline and the men who wanted it kept blank. I opened my mouth to say some version of the truth, and what came out instead was:

"Who gave it to you?"

Her jaw tightened. "A dead man."

"A dead man gave you a map to the source of the river, and you just happened to be at the right dock at the right time."

"He was dying. He asked. I said yes." She said it flatly, daring me to make it sordid. "There's a girl up there waiting on him. Waiting on word. That's all this is."

"That's never all anything is."

"No," she agreed, softly, and something in her voice cracked open just enough to show me light underneath. "It never is."

We stood there in the lamplight with the truth between us like a third passenger, and I understood that we had each just shown the other a card, and that neither of us had shown our whole hand. She didn't know I'd recognized the men at Miller's Landing. I didn't tell her. She didn't know what my commission actually ordered me to do with uncharted sources—what the men in the capital intended to do with them. I didn't tell her that either.

Two liars, keeping honest watch together. The river ran dark below us, carrying everything downstream except the past.

"Captain," I said at last, "those two men who boarded today—"

"I know who they are," she said.

"—they asked after a prospector."

"I know that too." She picked up the map and folded it away into her coat, unhurried, watching me the whole time. "Question is, Miss Ashford, what you're going to do about any of it. Come morning. Come whenever you write your next report." She moved past me to the door, close enough that I could smell coal smoke and river water on her, and paused. "I'd take it kind if you slept on it first."

Then she was gone, and I stood alone in the pilothouse with the lamp and the wheel and the wide black water, and I did sleep on it—or lay awake on it, which amounts to the same thing.

Because here is what I wrote in my logbook that night, in my neat surveyor's hand, the entry I would later tear out: *Position uncertain. Crew of vessel unreliable. Captain knows more than she admits. So does this officer.*

And beneath it, after a long time, in smaller letters: *So do the men who smell of gunpowder.*

By dawn the *Kestrel* would be casting off into the narrows, low water already showing her ribs of sandbar, and somewhere behind us Quill and his friend would be finding a boat of their own. I stood my watch till the sky went gray, holding the wheel I had no right to hold, steering nothing, going nowhere, and understanding—with the particular clarity of the sleepless—that I had stopped being a surveyor somewhere in the last three days, and had not yet become anything else.

Whatever I chose to be, I would have to choose it soon. The river was dropping fast, and it does not wait for anyone to finish deciding who they are.
