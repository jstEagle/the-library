## 4. Low Water

The river told me first. It always does, if you've spent enough years listening to it lie.

I was on the foredeck at dawn with a cup of coffee going cold in my hands when I noticed the water had gone quiet against the pilings at Cutter's Bend. Not low-quiet, not the polite murmur of a river minding its manners, but the flat, sullen silence of a river holding its breath. Twenty years of reading that water, and I'll tell you what it said: *I'm about to disappoint you.*

"Dry season's come early," I told Odile, who was coiling rope on the stern like a woman who'd been born knowing how.

"Early how early?"

"Three weeks early. Maybe more." I looked upstream, where the morning haze sat thin over water that should have been running brown and fat and generous. "The high country snowmelt's failed. Somebody up there is going to be very thirsty this year."

"Somebody down here too," Odile said, and nodded at the fuel gauge through the wheelhouse glass, which was lying to us both in the optimistic way gauges do.

I didn't say what we were all thinking, which was that low water meant slow going, and slow going meant the men behind us—whoever they were, whatever they wanted with Hollis Vane's map—would be closing the distance we'd bought with two days of hard steaming. A riverboat runs from trouble about as well as a coffin runs from a grave. We were built for cargo, not speed. Built to endure, not escape.

I went below and checked on our passenger anyway, because hope is a habit I've never managed to break.

Hollis Vane was dying by inches in my cabin, propped on pillows I'd stolen from my own bunk, his map rolled tight in an oilcloth sleeve he kept one hand on even in sleep. The fever had burned him down to something birdlike. When I came in his eyes opened, gray as river fog, and found me with unsettling accuracy for a man three-quarters gone.

"Water's dropping," he rasped.

"You can hear it from in here?"

"I can hear everything now. Dying sharpens the ears." He coughed, wet and long. "Tamsin. The narrows above Sallow Rock. If the season's turned—" He stopped, gathered himself. "There's a channel. Starboard side, past the dead cottonwood with the lightning scar. My old pilot marked it. Nobody else knows it."

"Your old pilot," I said. "The one you haven't mentioned in four chapters of dying."

"The one whose daughter you're carrying this to." His hand closed on my wrist, dry as kindling but strong as sin. "Starboard. Lightning-scarred cottonwood. Promise me you'll remember."

"Captain Winterbourne doesn't forget channels," I said, which was true, though I resented being reminded of it by a man who'd known me back when I was somebody's deckhand instead of somebody's problem.

He smiled, which cost him, and slept.

* * *

We reached Sallow Rock by mid-afternoon, and the river had stopped pretending.

Where in a normal year there'd be six feet of water over the gravel bar, there were maybe eighteen inches, and the current had gone strange, braiding itself into threads around sandbars that hadn't existed last month and wouldn't exist next week. Shifting bars are the river's way of reminding pilots that every chart ever drawn is a rumor. I stood at the wheel and read the water the way my mother taught me—by the color, by the ripple lines, by the little V of disturbed current that marks a snag just under the surface waiting to open your hull like a letter.

"Sandbar, port bow," Coral Ashford said from beside me.

I'd have snapped at anyone else for stating the obvious. But she'd said it exactly right—not panicked, not guessing, but reporting, the way a surveyor reports, and her eye had caught it a half-second before mine. That annoyed me more than if she'd been wrong.

"I see it," I said.

"I know you see it." She didn't look at me. She was watching the water with an expression I was starting to recognize: the look of a woman cataloguing everything, filing it somewhere I couldn't reach. "I'm practicing sounding by eye. It's on the commission."

"Is everything on the commission?"

"So far, yes." And then, quieter, almost to herself: "It used to be enough."

I put that away with the rest of the things Coral Ashford said that I didn't know what to do with, and took us into the narrows.

For half a mile it went beautifully, which on this river is the universe clearing its throat before it spits. Then the dead cottonwood rose up on the starboard bank—black trunk, lightning scar silvering down its heart—and I remembered Hollis's channel and swung us toward it, and the river chose that exact moment to remind me who was captain.

The boat struck with a sound I will hear until I die: a long grinding shudder, wood on gravel, the whole hull groaning like a church organ played by an angry god. The wheel bucked in my hands. Somewhere below, a crate of tools went over with a crash, and Odile swore in three languages, two of which I suspect she invented.

Grounded. Hard aground on a bar that had no business existing, in a channel that had no business failing me, with the sun going down and—I raised the glasses toward the south bend—no sign yet of the men behind us. Yet. The word did a lot of work.

"Well," Coral said.

"Not one word."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were composing it. I could hear you composing it."

A pause. Then, with terrible mildness: "I was going to observe that your government charts mark this entire reach as navigable year-round."

"And your government charts," I said, "are written by men who surveyed it from a launch in flood season and never came back. Welcome to the real river, Miss Ashford. Mind the gravel."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being grounded: the boat doesn't care that you're in a hurry. The river doesn't care that men with guns are a day behind you. The boat weighs forty tons and the river has decided it lives here now, and between those two facts stands a captain with a windlass and a bad attitude.

We worked through the night.

Odile rigged the spare anchor out astern—a kedge, we call it, though what we mostly called it that night was other things—and we hauled on it by turns, trying to walk the stern off the bar while the bow stayed stuck like a fly in amber. The engine turned over uselessly, churning brown water. Every pull of the winch gained us a foot and lost us six inches when the hull settled back.

By midnight my palms were raw. By two I'd stopped counting bruises. Odile worked like a machine with a grudge, and young Pieter, our engineer, crawled along the keel line in the dark with a lantern, calling depths in a voice that got smaller and smaller as the night got longer.

And Coral Ashford stripped off her surveyor's coat, rolled her sleeves, and hauled.

Nobody asked her to. I want that on the record, carved somewhere, because it mattered to me then and it matters to me now. She wasn't crew. She was a government woman with clean hands and a commission in her trunk, and she had every right to sit in the wheelhouse with a lamp and take notes on our incompetence. Instead she planted her boots in the mud at the end of the kedge line beside Odile, wrapped the rope twice around her forearms, and pulled until I could see the tendons stand out in her neck like bridge cables.

"You don't have to do this," I told her, somewhere around three in the morning, when we'd all gone quiet except for breathing.

"I know." She reset her grip. Pulled. The line creaked; the boat shifted a grudging inch. "But I read the charts, Captain. I wrote half of them. This is the first time I've felt what they cost." She looked at me across the taut rope, mud to her knees, hair plastered to her forehead, and her eyes were bright with something that wasn't exhaustion. "I'd like to feel it properly."

So we hauled. And near dawn, with the sky going the color of unpolished pewter, the *Maggie Bright*—because of course that's her name, I've never named a boat anything I couldn't apologize to—gave a long shudder, slid her stern free, and floated.

The cheer woke Hollis Vane, who called faintly through the deck boards asking if we'd won a war. In a sense, I suppose.

* * *

We anchored in the deep pool below the narrows to let the engine cool and let ourselves collapse. I sat on the foredeck with my ruined hands wrapped in rag strips, drinking coffee that Odile had laced with something medicinal, watching the light come up gold on water that had, overnight, become beautiful again simply by being deep enough to float a boat.

Coral came and sat beside me without asking. That was becoming a habit too.

"Your hands," she said.

"Are hands."

"Let me see them." And because I was too tired to fight, I let her. She unwound the rags with a care that undid me worse than any argument, cleaned the rope burns with water from the canteen, and wrapped them again, neat as a field dressing manual. Her fingers were blistered in the same places mine were. Surveyor's hands, learning river work the honest way.

"Hollis says there's a channel past the lightning-struck cottonwood," I said. Why I told her, I couldn't say. Maybe because she'd bled for the boat. Maybe because the night makes liars honest. "Starboard side. His old pilot cut it years ago. It might save us a day above the shallows."

Coral tied off the bandage and sat back, and for a moment she just looked at the river, and I watched her decide something.

"There's a launch behind us," she said finally. "Two men. I saw their wake at the south bend yesterday, before we grounded. They're running dark, no lights, but they burn coal, and coal smoke smells different than wood." She met my eyes. "I should have reported it to you immediately. I'm telling you now."

"How far?"

"A day. Less, if they know these waters better than we do."

I looked at my bandaged hands, at the smoke rising thin from our stack, at the narrows glittering ahead where a dead cottonwood held the secret to a channel only a dying man remembered.

"Then we'd best go find that tree," I said, and rang for the engine.
