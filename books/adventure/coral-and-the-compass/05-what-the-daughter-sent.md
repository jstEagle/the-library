## 5. What the Daughter Sent

The river post came to us at Miller's Landing in the shape of a boy on a mule and a canvas sack that smelled of mildew and kerosene. He held up three envelopes like a man offering a hand of cards. One was addressed to the *Maggie Bright* — Tamsin's boat, Tamsin's name, Tamsin's debts — one to the cook, whose mother wrote him weekly sermons he never opened, and one to me.

Nobody wrote to me. I had been in the territory eleven months, and the only correspondence I'd generated was official, carbon-copied, and filed under a department that officially did not exist. So when the boy said "Ashford?" with the particular boredom of someone who delivers death notices and seed catalogs with equal ceremony, I took the envelope the way you take a live coal.

It wasn't from my department. That was the first surprise. The second was the handwriting — a woman's, slanted hard left, pressed deep enough into the paper to read from the back like braille. The return name meant nothing to me: *E. Calloway, Highwater Fork.* The third surprise took longer, because it arrived in pieces over the next six hours, and by the time it finished arriving I had done something I would spend the rest of this voyage trying not to think about.

But I'm ahead of myself. Surveyors are trained against that. We record what happened, in order, or the map lies.

* * *

I didn't open it on deck. I waited until evening, until Tamsin had gone forward to argue with her engineer about a fuel line, until the crew had scattered to their various vices, and then I sat in the little cabin they'd given me — four feet wider than a coffin, which felt generous — and broke the seal.

The letter began the way letters do, with courtesies that were clearly agony for their author. She thanked me for my inquiry. She thanked me twice more, as if once hadn't convinced her. Then she stopped thanking me and started talking, and what she said rearranged the furniture of everything I thought I knew about this river.

Her father — Elias Calloway, though up here everyone had called him Latch, on account of a gate he'd built in '61 that still swung true — had come downriver in the spring to file his claim papers in person, because the high country post was unreliable and a claim is only as good as its paper trail. He'd carried his assay samples with him. Gold, but modest gold; the kind that keeps a family fed, not the kind that starts wars.

Except it did start a war. Because the samples weren't modest. Because Latch, who could read rock the way I read contour lines, had salted his own report downward out of an old prospector's superstition, and the men who bought his supplies — the men who weighed his dust every season, who knew his habits better than his daughter did — had taken one look at the discrepancy between what he claimed and what he carried and drawn the obvious conclusion.

He was found at the fueling dock below the narrows, she wrote. Not robbed. His dust pouch was still on his belt. But his head was wrong, she wrote, the way heads get when they've met a boat hook, and his papers were gone from inside his coat, and the map — the map he always kept folded against his heart, the map to the source, the map that made the claim mean anything —

She didn't finish that sentence. She just moved on, the way grieving people do, stepping over the hole in the floor without looking down.

She named the men. Two of them. Descriptions so precise they might have been dictated by a surveyor: one tall, gray at the temples, a scar splitting his left eyebrow, answers to Harrow or sometimes to nothing at all; one shorter, soft through the middle, missing two fingers on his right hand, laughs before his own jokes. Last seen working the landings between the narrows and Miller's Crossing, asking after a prospector who never arrived and a map nobody would admit to carrying.

I set the letter down on my bunk.

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is the only virtue I've ever managed to keep, and I could feel it slipping.

I knew those faces. Not from the river — from the files. From the drawer in the survey office where we keep the people who complicate our work: squatters, claim-jumpers, the violent residue that accumulates wherever something valuable sits unguarded. Harrow and the fingerless man had surfaced in three separate reports over two years, always adjacent to trouble, never quite inside it. I had read their descriptions over bad coffee in a government building and thought, idly, *someone should do something about them,* and then I had boarded a steamboat to chart a river, and I had not thought about them again.

Until now. Until they were real, and upstream, and closing on a boat captained by a woman who was almost certainly carrying the dead man's map in the false bottom of her chart case, because I had seen her reading it by lamplight two nights ago and I had said nothing then either.

There was a pattern forming. I recognized it the way you recognize weather.

* * *

Here is what I should have done: I should have walked the letter forward to the pilothouse and put it on the wheel in front of Tamsin Winterbourne and watched her face while she read it. That was the job. That was, if I'm honest, the decent thing. The daughter had written to me — to the government seal, to the idea of me — because she believed the machinery of law reached this far upriver. It doesn't, mostly. But I was the closest thing to it, and machinery that conceals itself stops being machinery and starts being a trap.

Instead I folded the letter along its original creases, put it in the oilskin wallet where I kept my commission papers, and went out on deck to help Tamsin coil lines, because the wind had come around and she'd asked no one in particular for hands and I had discovered, somewhere in the last hundred miles, that I liked being useful to her.

That's the part I keep circling. Not the concealment — concealment is easy, concealment is practically my native language. The liking.

Tamsin Winterbourne is not an easy woman to like, which is probably why she works so hard at being difficult instead. She gives orders like insults and thanks like accusations. She has spent twenty years making herself illegible — no fixed address, no registered home port, a boat that appears on manifests under three different names depending on the year — and watching her do it is like watching someone hold their breath for two decades. You start wanting them to exhale. You start, God help you, wanting to be the reason.

And she had let me see the map. That was the thing. She hadn't known I was there, standing in the companionway shadow with a cup of coffee going cold, watching her trace that hand-drawn line upriver with one finger, her lips moving around names that appear on no chart I've ever touched. She hadn't known, and when she looked up and saw me, she hadn't hidden it. She'd just looked at me — a long, level, measuring look, the look of a woman deciding whether the thing in her hands was safer hidden or shown — and then she'd turned the lamp down and said, "You're up late, surveyor," and that was all.

She had decided to show me. Or she had decided I already knew too much to matter. With Tamsin it was genuinely difficult to tell the difference, and I found I didn't care, which should have frightened me more than it did.

* * *

Two days above Miller's Landing, moored for the night against a willow bank with the current talking to itself in the dark, I stood my watch and thought about the letter the way you probe a sore tooth.

The facts were simple enough. A man was murdered for a piece of paper. The paper was on this boat. The murderers were behind us, moving faster than we were, because they traveled light and we traveled with a hundred tons of stubborn iron. And I was sitting on the only document that could hang them, hoarding it like the gold it described, because giving it up meant explaining why I had it — which meant explaining who I was, which meant the end of whatever fragile, unnamed thing had been growing between me and the woman at the wheel.

Guilt, I have learned, is not a loud emotion. It doesn't announce itself. It just moves in quietly and starts rearranging things — makes you laugh a half-beat late at Tamsin's jokes, makes you meet her eyes a half-second short, makes you stand your watch facing downriver toward the men you're hiding knowledge of instead of upriver toward the country you were sent to chart. Nobody noticed. I made sure of it. I had gotten very good, over eleven months, at being exactly the amount of visible my work required and not one degree more.

Tamsin noticed anyway. Of course she did. Reading people is the survival skill of a woman who owes money in nine towns.

"You've gone quiet," she said, coming aft on the third night with two cups of coffee and handing me one without asking, which was its own small revolution. "Quieter. You have a baseline, and you're under it."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're *professionally* quiet. This is different." She leaned on the rail beside me, close enough that our shoulders nearly touched, and looked downriver instead of at me, which was a kindness — she gave people exits the way other captains gave orders. "Whatever it is, you'll either tell me or you won't. But don't stand there composing your face. It's a good face. Waste of effort."

I laughed. Late, and a half-beat wrong, and she heard it, and neither of us said anything else, and we drank our coffee in the dark while the river ran silver under the moon like spilled mercury, going somewhere neither chart nor conscience had yet mapped.

I told myself I'd give her the letter at the next landing. There was always a next landing. Landings were how I forgave myself — each one a fresh deadline, each deadline a fresh failure, each failure easier than the last. The oilskin wallet rode against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and every time Tamsin's shoulder brushed mine on the way past the wheel, I thought: *tomorrow.* And every tomorrow came and went, and the wallet stayed shut, and the river narrowed ahead of us into country where the water ran fast and shallow and the banks drew close enough to hear whatever waited on them.

On the fourth day, the engineer reported smoke astern. Woodsmoke, then coal-smoke, then the flat gray smear of a steamboat running hard against the current, gaining.

Tamsin stood at the wheel a long time, studying it, and then she looked at me — that long, level, measuring look — and said, very quietly:

"Surveyor. Whatever you're carrying in that wallet of yours, I'd think hard about whether it's worth more than the people on this boat."

She turned back to the river before I could answer. Which was fortunate, because I didn't have one.
