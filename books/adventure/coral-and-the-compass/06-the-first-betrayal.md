## 6. The First Betrayal

I have been robbed by professionals, amateurs, and one memorable bishop, so I like to think I know the signs. The professional robs you quick and leaves you your boots. The amateur leaves fingerprints and regret. The bishop left me a pamphlet on forgiveness.

What I never learned to spot was the friend.

Fen Okafor had run my deck for six years. Six years of hauling lines in flood season, of standing beside me through two lawmen, one wedding I fled mid-ceremony, and the entire ugly business at Copper Landing that I will not be detailing here. She knew my coffee order, my tells, and the exact lie I used at every toll station. When she started asking careful questions about our fuel stops, I told myself it was prudence. When she volunteered for night watch more often than any sane person would, I told myself it was loyalty working overtime.

That is the trouble with trust. It doesn't just blind you. It writes you a whole library of excuses and shelves them where you can reach them easy.

* * *

It was Brick who noticed first, because Brick notices anything involving weight. He came up to the pilothouse at dawn with his arms crossed and his jaw set in the way he saved for genuine catastrophes, as opposed to the daily ones.

"We're light," he said.

"Everything's light. Dry season."

"Fuel's light. Rations are light. My good block-and-tackle is *gone*, Captain." He said it like the tackle was family. "And Fen's dory isn't tied at her berth."

I stood there at the wheel with the river sliding past gray and low, and I did the arithmetic every captain does and hates. Half our diesel, siphoned in the night. Half our stores. The tackle, which meant somebody planned to haul something overland, or expected to be paid in equipment instead of paper. And gone before first light, without a note, because notes are for people who intend to come back.

"She asked me last week," Brick said quietly, "which landing we'd make tomorrow. I told her. I thought—" He stopped. Brick does not finish sentences about feelings; it's against his religion.

"You thought nothing," I said. "You answered a crewmate's question. That's not a sin." I said it loud enough for the pilothouse door, which stood open, and for the woman standing just beyond it with a chart case under her arm.

Coral Ashford had that look she got when she'd already done the math and was waiting politely for everyone else to catch up. "The men who boarded at Marlow's Bend," she said. "They asked the same question. Which landing, which day."

The river ran quiet under us. Somewhere upstream a kingfisher made its idiot rattling call, indifferent to the fact that my life had just developed a hole in it shaped exactly like six years of friendship.

"They'll know where we are," I said. "They'll know what we're carrying, roughly how fast we burn fuel, and that we're down to two able hands and a surveyor who claims she can't lift an engine block."

"I can lift an engine block," Coral said.

"That's not the point."

"It's slightly the point."

* * *

We could have turned back. I want that on the record, somewhere, in some ledger of decisions I'll answer for: turning back was on the table, and I looked at it, and I pushed it off. Because turning back meant the map went nowhere, meant Elias Wren's promise died with him unkept, meant the jumpers simply waited us out at some other landing while I aged another twenty years into a smaller and smaller boat. There is no going backward on a river anyway. There's only down, and I don't do down.

So we ran on toward the narrows above Sallow Bend, and I spent the day teaching Coral the wheel while Brick nursed our shortened fuel like a man rationing his own blood. She took to it the way she took to everything—fast, silent, slightly terrifying. By afternoon she could hold us in the channel through a stretch I'd normally not hand to a paying pilot.

"Where'd you learn water?" I asked her, mostly to hear something besides my own thoughts, which had become a courtroom with Fen Okafor in the dock.

"Surveys. You can't chart a river you can't read." She glanced at me. "You read it differently than anyone I've worked with. You don't look for the channel. You look for what the channel's hiding."

"That's not reading. That's paranoia with a tiller."

"It's why you're still alive."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I gave her the wheel and went aft to count cans of beans, which is a form of prayer.

* * *

Sallow Bend is named for the willows, and the willows were doing their part, hanging low and gold over water gone thin as dishwater. The bend itself is a horseshoe around a gravel bar, slow water, easy water, the kind of place a pilot relaxes. Which is, I now understand, precisely why they chose it.

I saw the smoke first—a cookfire on the point, casual as Sunday. Then the skiff coming off the shingle, two men in it, one standing with a rifle held in that particular way that says *this is a conversation*. Behind them, half-hidden in the willows, a third man with a second rifle, positioned to cover the bend's far throat. Not robbers. A tollgate.

"Brick," I called. "Coral. Company."

Brick came up the companionway already reaching for the shotgun we kept under the chart drawer, and Coral stepped to the rail with her field glasses up, and for three long seconds nobody breathed.

Then the standing man raised his voice across the water, and I knew it. Knew it from the fueling dock, from the fevered hours when Elias Wren was dying in my cabin and these same voices had come aboard asking after him with too much interest. The taller one. Corliss, the daughter's letter would later name him, though I didn't know that yet. All I knew was the shape of him and the patience in his stance.

"Tamsin Winterbourne!" he called. "We only want what the old man stole! Hand it over and you steam on! Nobody needs to bleed!"

"He's lying," Coral said. Flat, certain, no performance in it. "There's a file on him. He shot a claimholder at Redstone and told the man's widow the same speech."

"You keep files on everybody?"

"Everybody interesting."

Downstream, the third man shifted in the willows, and the muzzle caught sun. We had perhaps ninety seconds of conversation left before it became geometry.

Here is what I knew about the water ahead. Below Sallow Bend the river drops through a cut called the Devil's Staircase—half a mile of boulder garden that every pilot on this river gives a wide, respectful berth, running the long way around through the oxbow even though it costs a day. I had never run it. I had watched it once from the bluff at nineteen years old and decided, with the clarity of youth, that I preferred growing old.

Here is what else I knew: the oxbow route put us broadside to that rifleman for a full minute at forty yards. And the dry season had dropped the Staircase lower than it had been in anyone's memory, which meant rocks showing like teeth, which meant—

"Coral," I said. "Your charts. The government surveys. Do they cover the Staircase?"

She was already opening the case. "Soundings from '61. Low-water line marked." She looked at me, and I watched her do the same arithmetic I was doing, and I watched her arrive at the same insane sum. "Tamsin. Those soundings are thirty years old and the river's lower than the datum."

"So the rocks are where the chart says, plus whatever the drought took away."

"Yes."

"And if we run the oxbow, that man in the willows writes his name on us."

"Yes."

Brick had gone very pale, which for Brick is a full emotional breakdown. "Captain," he said, "no sane pilot—"

"No sane pilot," I agreed, and spun the wheel, and put the bow at the Staircase.

* * *

I have run rapids since. I will run them again. But I will spend the rest of my life hearing that first minute: Coral Ashford at my shoulder with the chart flat against the binnacle, reading the water aloud in a voice that never once shook, though her knuckles were white as bone on the rail.

"Entry left of the split rock—chart shows nine feet, assume seven—hard to starboard past the ledge, then let her fall."

I let her fall. The *Magpie* dropped into the first chute like a stone with opinions, and the world became noise and white and the smell of wet iron, and I steered by Coral's voice because my eyes were useless with spray.

"Boulder ahead, chart shows it center—no, it's moved, it's right of center now—port! PORT!"

I ported. Something huge slid past my hull close enough to comb the paint, and then we were in the trough between chutes and Coral was flipping pages with wet hands, shouting numbers like a woman praying in mathematics.

"Second drop—chart shows a hole below the tongue—pull left, pull LEFT—"

We pulled left. The hole took our stern anyway, swung us sideways, and for one endless second the *Magpie* surfed down the face of the drop broadside, which is how boats die. Brick threw his whole weight on the starboard line, Coral screamed a heading I no longer remember, and I fed the wheel inch by inch until the river forgave us, which rivers almost never do.

Halfway down, the rifle cracked. One shot, wild, slapping water off the wave-crest behind us—they hadn't believed we'd try it, and by the time they ran the oxbow we'd be a rumor. But the bullet found something. Brick grunted and sat down hard against the rail, holding his forearm, blood between his fingers, and kept hold of his line with the other hand because Brick is Brick.

"Third chute," Coral read, and her voice finally cracked, just once, just enough to prove she was human. "Chart ends here. Chart ENDS, Tamsin."

"Then read the water," I said. "You said you could."

She put down the chart. She leaned out over the rail into the spray with her hair whipping loose, and she read the river the way she'd promised—not the ink, the water itself—and called me down the last of the Staircase by eye alone, and I swear on my mother's grave that when we shot out into the long green calm below it, both of us were laughing. Laughing like lunatics, soaked to the skin, with Brick bleeding quietly on the deck and half our gear gone to the bottom and a murderer somewhere behind us.

She grabbed my sleeve. I grabbed back. For a moment neither of us said anything, because everything worth saying had just been said at the top of our lungs in the language of headings and holes, and terror, it turns out, burns off into something warmer if you survive it next to the right person.

Then Brick cleared his throat, held up his ruined arm, and said, "This is comin' out of somebody's pay," and the moment broke, but it didn't break *clean*, if you follow me. It broke like a seed.

* * *

We patched Brick below the elbow and beached at dusk in a willow slough no chart names, to breathe and bail and take stock of what survival had cost. The tally: one block-and-tackle, half our fuel, most of our beans, a coil of line, Brick's near-certainty of infection, and my last comfortable illusion about the difference between friends and weather. Weather, at least, doesn't ask which landing you'll make.

Coral sat beside me on the roof of the cabin, charts drying on the deck below, and for a while we just watched the dark come up the valley.

"There's something I should tell you," she said.

I had heard that sentence before, from Fen, in a different key. I braced.

But what she said was: "Not tonight. Tomorrow. Above the last surveyed reach—I want to tell you where the maps end, because after that, everything's true."

Above us, stars were coming out over water no government had ever bothered to measure, and I found, to my considerable irritation, that I trusted her anyway.

"Tomorrow, then," I said, and handed her the coffee.
