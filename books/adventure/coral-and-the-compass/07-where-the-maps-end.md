## 7. Where the Maps End

The last survey marker was a rusted iron post driven into the bank like a bad tooth, and I had put it there myself three years ago, on the expedition that ended with two men drowned and my report sealed in a government drawer. Passing it now aboard Tamsin Winterbourne's grubby, beloved, debt-ridden riverboat felt like walking past my own grave and finding it occupied.

Beyond the post, the river changed its mind about everything.

Below, it had been wide and brown and patient, a working river, barge traffic and fueling docks and the slow commerce of small ambitions. Above the marker it narrowed, quickened, turned a green so deep it looked lit from underneath. Cliffs rose on either side, striped with mineral stain, hung with ferns that caught spray and held it like jewelry. The water talked to itself constantly—over rocks, under ledges, around bends that arrived without warning—and every voice in it said the same thing: *no one who matters comes here.*

I stood at the rail with my notebook open and made no notes at all.

"You're not writing," Tamsin said behind me. She had a way of arriving silently for a woman who captained a boat that sounded like a chest complaint. "First time since you boarded. Should I be worried?"

"I'm off duty," I said. "Officially, this reach doesn't exist."

"Convenient." She came to stand beside me, close enough that our shoulders nearly touched, which was as close as she ever let anything come voluntarily. "That's how I like my rivers. Nonexistent."

"You'd like the whole world nonexistent if you could manage it."

"It's a goal," she said. "I'm working toward it one map at a time."

I laughed before I could stop myself, and she glanced sideways at me with something that wasn't quite a smile but lived in the same neighborhood. That was the trouble with Tamsin Winterbourne. She made laughing feel like a conspiracy.

* * *

We tied up that evening in a pool below a waterfall that fell in three silver threads down a black rock face. The crew—what remained of them after Low Water and the ambush—cooked beans and argued about cards, and the light went gold, then copper, then gone, and the cliffs became shadows stacked on shadows.

I couldn't eat. I sat with my tin plate cooling on my knee and felt the letter in my coat pocket the way you feel a stone in your boot: small, specific, unbearable.

It had been four days since the river post canoe found us at the narrows landing. Four days of carrying Marisol Vane's letter against my ribs while Tamsin steered us upstream toward a daughter who had written, in a hand that shook only once, *They killed him for the claim. I have seen their faces. One is missing teeth on the left side. One wears a red scarf even in heat.* Faces I knew. Faces from my own files, from the claim-jumping docket I'd been quietly building since my first year in the service—Harrow and Finch, the Vane claim, the pattern of dead partners stretching back nine years like a trail of smoke.

Four days of watching Tamsin lie to me by omission, and lying to her by omission, and calling the balance between those lies *trust.*

"Coral." Tamsin was standing over me, lamplight behind her. "You've been staring at that plate like it owes you money. Come take the middle watch with me. I don't sleep well above the falls anyway."

It was an invitation and a summons both. With Tamsin they were usually the same thing.

* * *

The middle watch was hers alone, traditionally; the boat sat high enough on her moorings that one person could hear trouble coming. But she'd taken to asking me along, and I'd taken to going, and neither of us had named what that meant because naming things was how Tamsin believed you lost them.

We sat on the roof of the pilothouse with a blanket between us and the whole sky doing its unsupervised business overhead. No towns up here meant no light but starlight, and the stars came down thick and shameless, doubled in the still water of the pool until we seemed anchored in space.

"No chart records these," I said, tilting my head back. "Not properly. My office has a star table for navigation, but it stops at the last surveyed reach. Everything above it might as well be—" I stopped.

"Might as well be what?" Tamsin said softly. "Say it."

And I understood that she already knew what I was going to say, had perhaps known for days, and that this was the moment the river had been carrying us toward since the iron post.

"Tamsin," I said. "I need to tell you why I'm really on this boat."

Her profile didn't move. "I know why you're on this boat. You told me. Surveying."

"That's the commission, yes. But not all of it." I pulled the letter from my coat and held it out. "There's more. And I should have given you this four days ago."

She took it without reaching for the lamp. In starlight she read it twice, her lips moving slightly on the second pass, and when she finished she folded it along my creases with terrible precision, the way people fold things when they are holding themselves together with their hands.

"The daughter," she said. "Marisol Vane."

"Yes."

"And she wrote to you. A stranger. A government surveyor passing through, and she handed you her father's murder like a parcel."

"She didn't know who I was. River post forwards letters addressed to 'the boat going upriver.' She's been writing every landing for weeks, trying to catch anyone heading to the source." I swallowed. "Tamsin, the men following us—the ones who asked about a missing prospector at Miller's Bend—I know them. Harrow and Finch. They're in my files. They've killed before, for claims exactly like this one. I recognized them the day they boarded our dock, and I said nothing, because I didn't yet know what you were carrying or whether you were part of it."

The silence lasted long enough for a shooting star to cross half the sky and die.

"Part of it," Tamsin repeated. Her voice was very level, which with her was worse than shouting. "You sat across my own table from me and let me feed you and pay your passage, wondering if I was in league with the men hunting us."

"I wondered for one day. Then I watched you study that map by lamplight and I saw your face, and I knew you were grieving a man you'd known for an hour. Nobody grieves like that for a partner in crime."

"You spied on me."

"I kept watch," I said. "Yes. Both things are true."

She stood up so fast the blanket slid. "You want credit for the distinction? You sat on a murdered man's name for four days. His daughter wrote to you begging, and you tucked her letter in your pocket next to your government seal and said nothing, while I steered us straight toward his killers, and you let me."

"Because I was afraid!" It came out louder than I intended, loud enough that the card game below went quiet, and I stood too, and we faced each other on the pilothouse roof with the whole indifferent galaxy spinning above us. "Because the moment I gave you that letter, this stopped being my commission and became your promise, and I have seen what happens to people who stand between Tamsin Winterbourne and the thing she's decided to do. Because every hour I didn't tell you was an hour I got to pretend I was still a surveyor and not a—a conspirator. An accessory. Whatever I am now."

"You're a coward with good posture," she said.

"And you're a liar with excellent instincts," I shot back. "Go on. Your turn. Since we're being honest tonight, Captain. Tell me what's in the oilskin packet you sleep with under your mattress. Tell me what a dying stranger bought with his last breath at the fueling dock, and why a woman who has spent twenty years erasing herself from every record in the territory suddenly agreed to carry anything anywhere."

I expected fury. What I got was worse—she deflated, all at once, like a sail losing wind, and sat back down on the roof planks and put her face in her hands.

"A map," she said, muffled. "To the source. He pressed it into my hands and made me swear, and I was so busy being annoyed at him for dying on my deck that I swore before I thought about what I was swearing to." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "His name was Aldous Vane. He was dying of the lung-rot from twenty years in the high country, and he knew exactly who was coming for him, and he chose me because I was nobody. Because I was the one captain on this river with no allegiances, no debts anyone could call in, no history anyone could trace. He bought my invisibility with his last coin, Coral. And I took the payment, because I am exactly the coward you say I am, and because some fevered old man looked at me like I was the last honest person on the river and I could not bear to prove him right."

The word *Vane* went through me like cold water. The daughter's letter had signed itself *M. Vane*, and I had filed it away as coincidence, because I wanted it to be one.

"He was murdered for that claim," I said quietly. "Harrow and Finch. Marisol has seen their faces. She names them in the letter."

"I know." Tamsin wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, roughly, angry at them. "I figured out the connection two days ago. I've been waiting for you to tell me, to see if you would. Congratulations. Four days. I won by two."

"We're both liars," I said. "We're just bad at it in different directions."

Something cracked in her then—not a break, a thaw. A laugh escaped her, ragged and unwilling, and she pulled the blanket back up over both our knees as if nothing had happened, as if we hadn't just detonated everything, and maybe that was its own kind of honesty: the decision to stay.

"So," she said. "Your commission. Chart the upper river. Open it up. Steamboats, dredges, land offices, the whole gray tide." She stared at the black water. "And my promise runs straight through the middle of it."

"Yes."

"If you chart it, they find the source. If they find the source, Harrow and Finch get rich, and Aldous Vane died for nothing, and his daughter gets a road to her front door with lawyers on it."

"If I refuse the commission, I lose my position, probably my career. If I complete it, I lose—" She stopped. The stars filled in the rest of the sentence for her.

For a long time neither of us spoke. Below us the river ran on in the dark, making its arguments with the rocks, patient as geology. Somewhere upstream, a night bird called once and got no answer.

"I keep thinking about the marker," I said finally. "The iron post. I drove it myself, and I remember thinking, *this is as far as the world should go.* Everyone in my office thought I was being poetic. I wasn't. Some places are only themselves if they stay unrecorded." I turned to look at her. "I don't know what I'm going to do yet, Tamsin. I want to be honest about that too. But I know I'm not writing anything down tonight."

She studied me for a long moment, and whatever she was looking for, she appeared to find it, because she nodded once and settled back against the pilothouse wall, her shoulder finally, deliberately, touching mine.

"Then watch the stars with me," she said. "The uncharted ones. They're better company."

So we did. We sat shoulder to shoulder under stars no office had catalogued, above a river no honest map admitted to, two women who had spent the evening burning down their own disguises and were somehow still sitting in the ashes together. I felt lighter and more frightened than I had in years, and I understood that these were the same feeling, and that I was not going to give it up.

It was nearly midnight when Tamsin went rigid beside me, her hand closing on my wrist.

Downstream, far below the falls, where the surveyed world began—a lantern. Moving. Then a second, keeping pace with it along the opposite bank.

Traveling upstream. Toward us.

"Harrow and Finch don't travel at night," Tamsin breathed.

"No," I said, watching the lights climb. "But someone does."
