## 8. The Source

The boat died a mile below the gorge, and I let her go with less grief than she deserved.

We'd grounded her twice already that morning—once on a gravel bar that hadn't existed when my father ran this stretch, once on a submerged shelf of slate that scraped her hull with a sound like a coffin lid closing. By noon the *Magpie* sat wedged in six inches of water over a shoal that went on longer than some marriages, and the river around her had shrunk to a brown thread arguing with itself through the rocks.

"She won't float again this season," Odell said, which was the kind of thing Odell said because it was true and somebody had to.

I stood at the rail and looked upriver at the gorge walls rising gray and sheer, and I thought about all the years I'd spent making sure no chart in any government office showed what lay beyond them. Twenty years of blank space. Twenty years of telling surveyors, tax men, and one memorable Methodist missionary that the upper river was unnavigable, unprofitable, and haunted. And now here I was, about to walk into my own blank space carrying a dead man's map like a lit fuse.

"Captain?" Coral stood beside me, her survey case slung across her back, her sleeves rolled like she'd been born to river work instead of government offices. She'd been hauling line beside my crew for three days without complaint, and every hour of it had made me trust her more and dread her more, in roughly equal measure. "How far?"

"On foot? Two days. Maybe three if the trail's washed out." I squinted at the sun's angle. "Four if we're being followed."

"Are we being followed?"

"I'd be insulted if we weren't."

* * *

We stripped the *Magpie* to essentials: rope, rifles, hardtack, the medical kit, and the map, which rode against my ribs in an oilskin wrap where it had ridden for the better part of a month, warm as a second heartbeat. Odell took the tiller one last time out of sentiment—he was the sentimental type, which is why I kept him—and we left the old girl wedged in her shoal with a tarp over her wheelhouse and a note pinned to the door that said GONE UPSTREAM, BACK SOON, DON'T TOUCH, which would deter absolutely no one but made me feel better.

The climb began in earnest by mid-afternoon. The trail—if you could call a goat's argument with gravity a trail—switchbacked up through scrub pine and loose shale, and by the first hour I had stopped talking, by the second I had stopped thinking, and by the third I was operating purely on the animal machinery of put-one-foot-in-front-of-the-other. Coral climbed like she did everything else: methodically, quietly, with a stubbornness that I was beginning to suspect was the most dangerous thing about her. Not the charts. Not the commission. The stubbornness.

Odell brought up the rear with young Petch, who complained about his boots, his knees, his lot in life, and the fundamental injustice of mountains, in that order, on a rotating schedule.

We camped that night in a hollow below the tree line, and I took first watch because I didn't trust myself to sleep. Somewhere behind us, down in the dark along the river, I kept imagining lanterns. Men who smelled of gunpowder. They'd found the *Magpie* eventually—they always found things eventually; it was the one talent the world never failed to reward—and they'd read the note and smiled, and started climbing.

Coral couldn't sleep either. She came and sat with me at the edge of the firelight, close enough that our shoulders touched, and neither of us said anything for a long while, because everything that needed saying between us had been said two nights ago above the last surveyed reach, raw and ugly and honest, and there wasn't anything left to add yet. Just the mountain breathing around us, and the stars doing their indifferent bookkeeping overhead.

"They'll come tomorrow night," she said finally. "If they're a day behind."

"If they're smart."

"They're not smart. They're patient. It's worse."

She was right, of course. She was usually right, which was another thing I held against her, right after holding it close.

* * *

The second day the trees gave out and the world opened up into high country I had no business knowing as well as I did and knew anyway, bone-deep, from a life I didn't talk about. We crossed a plateau of wind-bent grass the color of old coins, followed a stream that grew colder and clearer with every mile until it ran so transparent it seemed less like water than like the idea of water, and near dusk on the third day we came around a shoulder of granite and stopped, all four of us, without arranging to stop, the way you stop when the land itself puts its hand on your chest.

There it was.

The spring rose from living rock at the head of a bowl-shaped valley—a seam in the mountain's gray flank, maybe ten feet wide, from which the river emerged whole and certain, cold and silver, already moving with the authority of something that intended to reach the sea if it had to move every stone on earth to do it. The water came up glowing in the late light, lit from beneath somehow, or from within, and where it broke over the first ledge it threw spray that caught the sun and held it.

Petch said a word he'd have been fined for aboard the *Magpie*. Odell took off his hat. I just stood there with my hand pressed flat against my sternum, over the oilskin, feeling like a man who has carried a stranger's letter into a cathedral and only now understood what church the address referred to.

Twenty years I'd kept this place off the maps. I'd told myself it was self-preservation, and mostly it was. But standing there, I admitted what I'd never admit to anyone, least of all myself: part of it was that some things shouldn't be written down. Some things survive only as long as nobody official knows they exist.

Then I saw the camp, and the awe curdled.

Below the spring, half-tucked against the rock wall, stood the ruins of a claim camp: a collapsed cabin, a rusted sluice box half-swallowed by grass, a scatter of tools gone orange with rot. Abandoned years ago, by the look of it—but not empty now. Smoke rose thin and straight from a cook fire, and a figure stood at the edge of the ruin with a rifle held low and ready, watching us come.

A woman. Young. Dark-haired, sunburned, with the hollowed-out look of someone who has been grieving in the wilderness alone for longer than grief can reasonably be survived.

"That's her," I said. "That's Briony Hale."

"You're sure?" Coral asked.

I was sure. I'd carried her father's dying words for a month; I'd know his face rebuilt in hers anywhere—the same set of the jaw, the same way of standing like the ground might try something.

"Hold up!" I called, spreading my arms wide. "We're friends of Elias Hale! He sent us!"

The rifle didn't waver, but her voice did, when it came—cracked, suspicious, hungry. "My father's dead."

"Yes, ma'am. I know. I was there." I walked forward slowly, hands open, the way you approach a spooked horse or a heartbroken woman with a gun, which are nearly the same problem. "He died on my boat. Fever, mostly. And a bullet wound that had gone bad. He gave me something for you, and I've come a long way to keep that promise."

She let me get within twenty feet before she spoke again. "Show me your hands. All of you. Slowly."

We showed her. Even Petch, who'd have shown her his soul if asked politely enough. And when she saw the oilskin packet I drew out and held up, she made a sound I'll hear until I die—not a word, not quite a sob, something older than either—and the rifle barrel dropped toward the ground.

"He told me if anyone came, I should shoot first," she said. "He said it like a joke. He joked about everything."

"So did mine," I said. "Come sit down, Miss Hale. We've got news, and you're not going to want to hear it standing."

* * *

We made camp inside the ruin of the claim cabin, because the walls still stood on three sides and because Briony had chosen it, weeks ago, for exactly the sight lines it offered. She was twenty-three years old and had been living up here since spring, waiting—she wouldn't say for what, but the rifle and the fortified position said it plainly enough. Waiting for the men who killed her father, or waiting for someone from him. Whichever came first. The wilderness is generous that way; it lets you wait for anything.

I gave her the map at full dark, by firelight, and watched her open it with hands that shook and then steadied themselves, the way hands do when they decide grief is a luxury they'll resume later.

She read it for a long time. Her father's handwriting, his careful soundings, his little annotations in the margins—*good water here*, *B. would like this bend*—and I watched her find those, the ones addressed to her, and I looked away, because some moments you witness and some you guard.

"It's real," she said finally. "The source. The claim. He found it." Her voice went strange. "And they killed him for paper."

"They killed him for what the paper could buy them," Odell said grimly. "Which is worse."

Briony looked up at me across the fire, and her eyes were her father's eyes, and they were asking the question I'd been dreading since the fueling dock. "Why bring it to me? You could've sold it. You could've used it. My father said—" she paused, working it through— "he said you were the kind who keeps off other people's treasure."

"He said right," I said. "But he made me promise. And I'm weak that way about promises."

It was more true than she knew, and Coral, sitting across the fire, looked at me like she knew exactly how much more true, and I held her gaze and didn't flinch, which by then counted, between us, as a kind of tenderness.

It was Coral who said it, quiet, into the fire: "They're coming tonight."

Everyone turned. "You're sure?" Briony asked.

"Lanterns," Coral said, nodding toward the southern rim of the valley, where the trail we'd climbed descended into blackness. "Three of them. Moving slow. They think we don't know."

I looked, and after a moment I caught it—faint pinpricks in the dark, bobbing, patient. Men who smell of gunpowder, coming up their own trail, following our sign, following the map they believed we carried.

"How many rounds you got?" I asked Briony.

"Enough," she said, in a voice that meant not nearly enough, and I recognized the voice, because it was mine.

I laid the rifles out and counted what we had, and it came to this: four guns, one ruined cabin, one grieving girl, one government surveyor who'd turned her back on her commission somewhere on the river and hadn't looked back, one superstitious engineer, one boy too young for any of this, and me—Tamsin Winterbourne, twenty years invisible, sitting at the source of everything, waiting for the endgame to walk up the trail by lantern light.

The first lantern reached the mouth of the valley as the moon rose.

"Positions," I said.
