## 9. Cold Water Rising

*Tamsin*

The spring came up out of the rock like it had somewhere better to be.

I'd spent twenty years on that river and I'd never seen water so clear it looked like an apology. It rose from a seam in the granite, cold enough to make your teeth ache just looking at it, pooled in a basin no wider than my pilothouse, and spilled over the lip to begin its long lie downriver — a lie I'd helped tell, keeping every tributary above the Gorge off the charts for two decades. And here was the truth of it, silver and indifferent, not caring one whit who'd bled or lied or run to stand beside it.

The daughter stood on the far side of the pool with a rifle held the way you hold something you've been holding since you were twelve. She had her father's jaw and none of his softness left in her. Her name was Edie Marchand, and she'd been waiting at the abandoned claim camp for eleven days with dried meat, a crate of cartridges, and what she later described as "a reasonable expectation of murder."

"You have it," she said. Not a question. Her eyes went straight past me to Coral, then to the satchel at my hip. People always found the map before they found the person carrying it. I'd stopped taking it personally around year six.

"I have it," I said. "And I have news you're not going to like."

"About Papa."

"About how he died." I told her plain, because she struck me as a woman who'd rather be cut than coddled. The claim-jumpers hadn't robbed him. They'd beaten him and put him on a raft with a hole in his heart and let the river do the paperwork. When I finished, she didn't cry. She checked the angle of the sun against the ridge line, which told me everything about how she'd spent those eleven days.

"They'll come at dusk," she said. "They always do. Cowards like the light behind them."

Coral, crouched at the edge of the spring filling a canteen, said quietly, "Then we've got four hours to become very difficult to kill."

* * *

*Coral*

I want it recorded — by someone, somewhere, since my own government will never hear it — that I gave away the most valuable thing I owned while sitting on a rock eating jerky.

My survey case held three years of work. Instrument readings, triangulation notes, photographs of the upper river, sworn statements from two witnesses to Marchand's death, and the legal description of the claim that proved — beyond any argument a land office could dodge — that the Harker brothers' filing was fraudulent from the first signature. I'd carried it upriver intending to deliver it to the Territorial Survey Office and watch the blank space on the map fill in with my name attached.

Instead I laid it open on a flat stone between Edie Marchand and Tamsin Winterbourne, and I watched both of them understand what I was doing before I finished saying it.

"This makes the claim yours," I told Edie. "Legally. Documented, witnessed, defensible. The Harkers can't jump what's already patented."

"And you?" Edie said. "Your commission?"

"My commission was to chart the river." I turned a page so the light caught the ink. "It doesn't say whose name goes on the land."

Tamsin was looking at me with an expression I'd learned to read over a thousand miles of bad water: suspicion giving way, grudgingly, to something warmer and considerably more inconvenient. "You're burning your career," she said.

"I'm reallocating it," I said. "There's a difference. Ask me again if we survive the night."

Because we would have to survive the night. That was the arithmetic of it. A patent filed in a territorial office meant nothing until the paper arrived, and the paper was still nine days' travel downriver, and between us and the law stood two men named Harker who had already killed once and would find killing twice no more difficult than their first attempt at honest work.

So we fortified the camp as the light went long and amber. Edie knew the ground — she'd chosen it well, a shelf of granite above the spring with talus slopes on three sides and one approach through the trees. We cleared firing lanes, stacked stone, filled every container with water because Edie said sieges were won by thirst more often than bullets, and Tamsin — who claimed, loudly and repeatedly, to be a boat captain and nothing else — moved through the preparations with the competence of a woman who had been in trouble before and intended to be in less of it this time.

"Trench here," she said, scuffing a line with her boot. "If they come up the draw, they'll bunch where the rocks narrow. Anyone who bunches is anyone who dies."

"You've done this," I said.

"I've *been* this," she corrected. "On the wrong side of it, mostly. Which is why I know how it ends."

"How does it end?"

She looked at me then, and the last of the daylight caught the gray in her hair, and she said, "Badly, usually. But not tonight. Tonight we're meaner and we know the ground."

I believed her. That was the strange thing. Twenty years of institutional skepticism, and I believed a riverboat smuggler because she said so while standing in falling light with dirt on her hands.

* * *

*Tamsin*

They came at dusk, exactly as advertised, because men like the Harkers are predictable the way weather is predictable — you can't stop them, but you can damn well get under cover first.

There were five of them, not two. The brothers had hired muscle along the way, river rats with cheap rifles and expensive appetites. I counted them coming up the draw through the gap in the rocks and felt the old familiar cold settle into my chest, the one that used to mean *run*. For twenty years running had been my answer to everything. Debts, lawmen, grief — I'd steered away from all of it, kept my wake clean, kept my name off things.

But there was nowhere to steer tonight. There was only high ground, and a dead man's promise, and a woman beside me loading a rifle with steady hands, and a girl below us who had every right to ask why the world worked this way and no expectation of an answer.

"Hold until they're inside forty yards," Edie called softly. "Nobody wastes powder."

The first shot, when it came, wasn't ours. A rifle cracked from the trees — a wild shot, nervous — and the night opened up.

I won't dress it up. Sieges are ugly, tedious, terrifying work. You fire when you must, you move when you can, you listen to bullets crack off stone and try to guess which of your people is screaming and whether the scream means wounded or dead. Our cook, Baptiste, took a ball through the shoulder in the second hour and swore at the Harkers in three languages while Coral bound the wound by matchlight. One of the hired men got brave around midnight and made the talus slope, and Edie shot him in the leg with a marksman's calm, and after that nobody else got brave.

Somewhere past two in the morning, the shooting paused — that exhausted, mutual pause where everyone reloads and reconsiders — and I heard Harker's voice carry up through the dark, smooth as bilgewater.

"Marchand girl! Your father signed over his rights fair and square! Hand over the papers and the map and walk away rich!"

"He died face-down on a raft," Edie called back, "with your boot print on his back. Come take the papers."

Silence. Then, softer, aimed upward like a knife: "And the captain. Winterbourne, isn't it? We know what you're carrying. We know what it's worth. Whatever she's paying you, girl, we'll triple it."

And here is the part I'll admit to no one but this page: for half a heartbeat, I considered it. Not the money. The exit. The old door swinging open — hand over the map, walk away, be nobody again, invisible, safe. Twenty years of habit reached for that door like a hand reaching for a rail in rough water.

Then Coral's hand found mine in the dark. She didn't squeeze it. She just held it, the way you hold a mooring line — not pulling, just *there*, saying without saying that some things are worth staying tied to.

"No deal," I shouted down the mountain. "Come and get it."

* * *

*Coral*

They came at dawn, because desperate men always believe the light will favor them.

We heard them on the talus before we saw them — the scrape and clatter of boots on loose stone — and then the gray light showed them spread across the lower slope, five reduced to four, moving fast and low. Edie fired first and dropped the nearest one's rifle arm. Tamsin rose out of our trench like something the river had finally decided to give back, and I understood in that moment that she had never once in her life planned to die hiding.

What happened next happened quickly, and I have replayed it slowly ever since.

One of the Harkers — the younger, the one with the quick smile I'd seen in a wanted circular two years ago — broke from the others and came straight up the center, not at Edie, not at the trench, but at the flat stone where we'd left the survey case and the map wrapped in oilcloth. Straight at the papers. He was ten yards out when Tamsin stepped into his line.

I shouted her name. He raised the pistol. She turned — not away, *into* it, putting her body between him and the map like a hull turned broadside to take the wave — and the gun went off.

The ball kissed her along the ribs, a red furrow laid open across her side, and spun away into the rock. She staggered, did not fall, and hit him across the jaw with the barrel of her rifle hard enough that I heard it from six feet away. He went down and stayed down, and Edie's rifle covered the rest of them until, one by one, they threw their guns over the talus and put their hands up.

By noon the river law arrived — two marshals' deputies and a territorial magistrate, summoned nine days ago by a letter I had sent from the last landing, because I am a surveyor and I believe in redundancy. The Harkers went down the mountain in irons, and the wounded went down on litters, and the magistrate took my evidence, and Edie Marchand's statement, and Tamsin's, given sitting down and gray-faced with a bandage around her middle and a flask of Edie's brandy in her fist.

"That's everything," the magistrate said, closing his case. "Claim's hers now. Patent will issue within the month."

Edie stood at the edge of the spring, looking down into the water that had started all of it — her father's obsession, his death, this whole bloody season. Then she unwrapped the map from its oilcloth, and Tamsin went very still.

"You don't have to," Tamsin said. "The promise was delivery. It's delivered. What happens next—"

"It was never about the river," Edie said. "He drew this because he thought owning the source would make him somebody. It made him a corpse instead." She struck a match, touched it to the corner, and held the burning map out over the pool until the flames took it all, and the ash drifted down onto the water and was pulled, gently, into the current that runs from here to the sea.

Tamsin watched it burn with an expression I couldn't name — grief and relief and something like vertigo, a woman watching the last wall of her old life come down. Then she swayed, and I caught her, and she laughed once, painfully, against my shoulder.

"Don't," she warned.

"Don't what?"

"Don't say you told me so."

"I would never," I said, easing her down onto the flat stone. "I'm simply going to spend the entire trip downriver mentioning it occasionally."

Below us, the spring ran silver over the rock, unowned now by anyone, headed for the low country and the sea. And Tamsin Winterbourne — bleeding, bankrupt, and free for the first time in twenty years — looked up at me and asked the question I'd been waiting all season to hear:

"So. How do you feel about riverboats?"
