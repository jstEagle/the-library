## 10. Downriver

The daughter's name was Wren Halloway, and she burned the map at dawn.

Not on the boat—she was particular about that. She carried it up the slope from the spring to a flat shelf of granite where the morning sun hit first, and she knelt there with the paper in both hands like it was something that might still be saved. Which, in a way, it was. That was the whole trouble with maps. They were always saving things for the wrong people.

I stood back and gave her the room. Twenty years of practice at giving people room; it was the one skill I'd ever mastered completely.

"You could do it," she said, without turning around. "You've had every right for two hundred miles."

"It was never mine to burn."

"No." She struck the match. "It was his. And now it's mine. That's how promises work—they come down the river whether you want them or not."

The paper caught, curled, went orange at the edges. I watched the source line go first—the little inked spring my old passenger had drawn with a shaking hand in his last week of life—and then the claim boundaries, and then his signature, which he'd written twice because the first attempt had been more fever than letters. The wind off the high country took the ash straight up, no drama to it, just gone.

I felt lighter. That surprised me. I'd expected grief, or at least its cousin, but what came instead was something closer to the feeling after you finally set down a load you'd forgotten you were carrying. My shoulders actually ached from the absence of weight.

"Captain," Wren said, standing, brushing ash off her knees. She had her father's jaw and none of his softness left in her—the dry season and the siege had burned that out of her. "You kept your word. He'd have liked you. He liked stubborn women who pretended not to be."

"I don't pretend."

"You pretend beautifully. It's why you're still alive." She looked past me, down the slope, to where Coral stood at the waterline talking to the river law men. "And her?"

"That," I said, "is a longer story."

* * *

The jumpers went down the mountain in irons, which is a sentence I intend to repeat to myself on cold nights until it stops feeling borrowed.

Coral had summoned them properly—no gunslinger theatrics, though there'd been enough shooting the night before to satisfy anyone's appetite for drama. She'd done it with paper. A survey warrant, a sworn statement, the murdered man's letter with its careful description of faces, and her own field charts annotated in that small, ruthless handwriting of hers: *claim boundaries falsified, see attached, see also everything.* The two river law men who came up from the lower station read it all by lantern light and then looked at Halloran and Vetch the way men look at a snake they've nearly stepped on twice.

Halloran cursed Coral by name. Vetch just stared at me across the fire smoke with an expression I recognized, because I'd worn it myself for twenty years: the look of a man watching someone else get away with living.

"They'll hang or they won't," Coral said afterward, rolling up her documents. "That part's out of our hands."

"You could've turned me in too," I said. "Unlicensed pilot. Falsified registry. A riverboat that officially doesn't exist."

"I know." She didn't look at me. She was watching the law men load their prisoners into the longboat, and the morning light was doing unfair things to her profile, and I let it. "I decided some maps are better left blank."

"That's my line."

"I stole it. I steal everything worth taking from this river." Then she did look at me, and neither of us said anything else, because we'd earned the silence fair and square.

* * *

We took three days getting the *Maggie June* back into fighting shape, and by fighting shape I mean floating. The rapids had cost us a rail section, half the starboard planking below the waterline, and the last of my dignity. Wren's people knew the high-country camps better than I knew my own deck, and between them and Coral's endless practical competence—we hauled timber, steamed boards, caulked seams, and argued about all of it.

Wren gave us timber from her father's old claim camp. "He'd want it carrying somebody downstream," she said. "He spent his whole life trying to get upstream. Somebody ought to balance the books."

On the second evening she found me alone at the rail, checking caulking by feel because the light was going.

"I'm keeping the spring," she said. "What's left of the claim. It's legal now—Coral's papers settled that. I'll file it quiet, work it small. No boomtown. No river of strangers."

"That's the smartest thing anyone's said on this water in twenty years."

"Don't flatter yourself, Captain. You're the second-smartest." She paused. "She's coming with you, isn't she."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it as one. Wren nodded anyway.

"My father ran from everything," she said. "Ran upstream until there was no more up. You ran sideways your whole life—I can tell, it's in how you hold a wheel. Maybe running toward is the only direction either of you hasn't tried." She put out her hand, and I shook it, and her grip was exactly as strong as I'd guessed. "Come back through someday. If the river lets you be found."

* * *

We cast off on a morning so clear it felt like showing off.

The *Maggie June* rode high and strange without the supplies we'd lost to betrayal and rapids—lighter than she'd been since I bought her, and I felt every pound of it missing the way you feel a pulled tooth. Odd, how a boat can feel wrong when she's finally easy. I'd spent two decades loading her heavy: cargo, debts, aliases, the whole ballast of a hidden life. Now she sat shallow in the current like a woman who'd finally exhaled.

Coral stood at the bow with a sounding pole, calling depths, and I'll admit I watched her do it longer than navigation strictly required.

"You're steering badly," she called back.

"I'm admiring the scenery."

"The scenery is a government surveyor with a criminal record of association."

"Former surveyor."

"Former nothing. I resigned by letter. It's waiting at the lower station post office, along with about six months of other mail I'm afraid of." She planted the pole, swung herself back aboard with that economy of motion that had stopped surprising me somewhere around the narrows and started feeling like home instead. "They'll want reports. There won't be any."

"What will you do?"

"Chart," she said. "Just not for them." She leaned against the rail beside me, close enough that her shoulder touched mine, which on my boat constituted a public declaration. "There's a hundred miles of water between here and the delta that nobody's ever mapped honestly. Sandbars that move every season. Channels that exist for three weeks a year. A pilot who knows them would be worth more than any commission."

"A pilot like that would have to trust somebody eventually."

"She's working on it."

I laughed—out loud, unguarded, a sound I genuinely did not recognize coming out of my own chest. Twenty years of keeping my face closed and my name short, and here I was laughing at the wheel in broad daylight like some Sunday excursionist.

Below the high country the river widened and slowed and remembered how to be kind. We passed the narrows where we'd grounded; the sandbar that had held us prisoner had already shifted, erased, moved fifty yards east like it had never happened. Rivers keep no records. That used to be the thing I loved most about them.

We passed the bend where Dex had slipped away in the night with half our stores, and I found I wasn't angry anymore, just tired in the old way and grateful the tiredness was fading. We passed the landing where a dying man had collapsed against my gangplank with a map and a promise, and I touched my hat brim to it, because you should honor your dead even when they've ruined your life in interesting ways.

"You're going to miss being invisible," Coral said. We were through the worst water, and I'd let her take the wheel while I sat on the rail like a loafer. "Being nobody. No name, no record, no one looking for you."

"I already miss it," I said. "Ask me again in a year."

"And in ten?"

"In ten years I'll be fifty-three years old, Ashford, and I'll probably still be arguing with you about channel markers, and I expect I'll be glad about it." I heard myself say it and didn't take it back. That was new. Everything was new. "Life measured in years instead of miles. Someone told me once it was possible."

"Someone wise."

"Someone stubborn. Same thing."

She steered us through the last set of riffles without asking, because she'd learned my water faster than I'd ever taught anyone, and the *Maggie June* ran clean and light toward the delta haze, and for the first time in twenty years I wasn't watching the banks for whoever might be following.

Nobody was following. That was the whole miracle of it. Nobody was following, and somebody was beside me, and the river went on ahead the way rivers do—not hiding anything, not promising anything, just running toward the sea with its whole heart.

"Well, Captain," Coral said. "Where to?"

I looked at the open water, and then at her, and I found that for once in my life I had absolutely no idea—

—and it felt like the best chart I'd ever read.
