## 1. Miles, Not Years

There are two ways to measure a life. Years, or miles. I gave up on years a long time ago — they only tell you how long you've been losing. Miles at least have the decency to be honest. Miles tell you how far you've run.

That morning I was measuring in fathoms, which is a third kind of unit entirely: the depth of water beneath your hull, and therefore the depth of trouble you can afford.

The squall came in off the Sable Shelf like a drunk coming home late — loud, sloppy, and promising damage. I had the Wren's nose pointed straight at it, which is the opposite of what any sane man does, but the Meridian Salvage Syndicate doesn't pay for sanity. They pay for salvage, and salvage is just a polite word for standing where other people had the good sense not to.

"Barometer's falling like my credit rating," I said to the wheelhouse, because after eleven years of running alone you get in the habit of narrating. The Wren didn't answer. She's a forty-foot trawler older than my debt, which is the only thing aboard older than my debt, and she communicates exclusively in groans.

The radio crackled. "Wren, this is Meridian dispatch. You reading the cell off Sable Point?"

"Reading it," I said. "She's a pretty one."

"She's worth eight thousand if you pull the trawl gear off her before she goes down. Renk says don't come back without it."

Aldous Renk. My creditor. My employer. The distinction matters to him, so I pretend it matters to me. Renk owns a little over two hundred thousand dollars of my future, and he likes to remind me of it the way a man reminds a dog of a whistle — not cruelly, just often enough that I come when called.

"Tell Renk I'll bring him his eight thousand," I said, "and a receipt."

Dispatch laughed the way dispatch always laughs — the laugh of a man who gets a salary whether the boat comes back or not. I killed the radio and put the Wren into the weather.

Here's what I know about storms, learned the way you learn anything worth knowing: by paying for it. A squall is a negotiation. It tells you what it wants, and if you're smart you give it a little of everything — a little sail area, a little speed, a little of your dignity — and it lets you keep the hull. The men who lose boats are the men who argue. I never argue anymore. Arguing costs money, and I don't have any.

The rain hit like a thrown bucket. The Wren shrugged into it, her old diesel muttering below, and I worked the wheel through the chop with the particular pleasure of a man who is, at least, good at the one thing he does. That's the dirty secret of a life like mine. It's a bad life, but I'm excellent at it. Give me a failing boat and falling weather and I am the most competent man on this coast. It's only in the calms, when the water goes flat and the miles stop adding up, that I start counting years again. And years are unbearable. Years have a balance due.

I found the trawler off Sable Point at half past two, wallowing on her beam ends with her deck gear awash. Her crew had already taken to their own boat — I could see them, a mile east, running for harbor with the good sense I'd just been admiring. Their loss. My eight thousand.

I went over the rail in my slicker, worked the trawl winch loose, and got the gear slung aboard the Wren in forty minutes flat, which even by my standards was showing off. The whole time the squall stood over me like a landlord, and the whole time I thought, as I always think, the same thought I've thought for eleven years: *this is the run that pays it off. This is the one.*

It never is. I know that. Knowing it doesn't stop the thought. Hope is the most expensive thing I carry, and I can't seem to throw it overboard.

* * *

By dusk the weather had spent itself and I was running home up the coast with the salvaged gear lashed down and a thermos of coffee that had gone bitter as regret. The coast out there is all mangrove and limestone, the Sable Keys strung along it like a broken necklace, and in the last light the water goes the color of hammered tin. Beautiful, if you're the kind of man who finds beauty in things that could kill you. I am. It's the only kind of beauty I get much of.

The radio woke up at 6:40.

"Wren, Wren, this is Meridian dispatch, come in."

I keyed the set with a hand still wet from the deck wash. "Go for Wren. And if this is about the trawl gear, it's on deck, and I want it noted that I got it off in under an hour."

"Noted. Forget the gear." A pause. Dispatch pauses are never good. "Orsen, we've got a priority job. Government vessel, the *Halcyon Survey*, out of the coastal authority. She's gone dark."

"Dark how? Radio trouble?"

"Dark how," dispatch repeated, "is the question. Last position was off the Sable Keys, outer shoals. She was running a hydrographic survey — charting work, mapping the seafloor. Scheduled to check in at noon. Didn't. Coast guard's been pinged but they're saying forty-eight hours before they mount anything, weather considerations."

"Forty-eight hours," I said. "In monsoon season."

"Renk wants a syndicate asset on site first anyway. Standard salvage retainer. If there's wreckage, we claim it. If there's gear, we secure it." Another pause, longer. "He says to tell you personally: this one's worth real money, Orsen. Enough to matter."

Enough to matter. I turned that phrase over the way you'd turn a coin you suspect is clipped. Renk didn't say things like that for free. Renk's words were like his contracts — every generous clause hiding a tooth.

"Any distress call from the *Halcyon*?"

"None. That's the thing. She didn't go down, Orsen. She went quiet."

I looked out at the darkening water. Somewhere out past those keys, a government vessel full of scientists and surveyors had stopped answering her radio, and the first official help was two days out, and the man I owed my life to wanted me there first.

There's a version of me — the one I'd have been if I'd never signed Renk's papers — who'd have said no. Who'd have said *let the coast guard handle it, let the government find its own boat.* But that man is twelve years and two hundred thousand dollars underwater, and the man who actually exists needs the work, and the work was there, glowing on the radio like bait on a hook.

"Course and position," I said.

Dispatch read them out. I wrote them in the log with the little stub of pencil I keep tied to the chart table, and my hand was steady, because my hands are always steady. It's the rest of me that shakes, privately, at three in the morning, when the miles stop.

"Orsen," dispatch said, before I could key off. "Renk's coming out himself. He'll meet you at the fuel depot at Gannet Point, oh-six-hundred. He wants to brief you in person."

Renk never briefed anyone in person. Renk communicated through intermediaries the way a bishop communicates through priests — it preserved the sanctity and kept his hands clean.

"Tell him I'll be there," I said.

I killed the radio and stood a while in the wheelhouse with the engine talking to me through the deck plates, that familiar diesel heartbeat, and I did the arithmetic I always do. Fuel to the Sable Keys and back. The retainer against the interest. The interest against the principal. The principal against the rest of my natural life. The numbers came out the same way they always come out, which is to say: short, but survivable, if I don't stop moving.

That's the trick of it. You don't outrun a debt. You just stay faster than it. The day I stop moving, it catches me, and I've seen what it does to men it catches — I've watched them come into harbor with that particular stillness, that settled look, men who stopped being chased and started being owned. Renk owns a dozen of them. They smile at him across his card tables and fetch his coffee and their eyes are the eyes of dogs that have been whipped past the point of flinching.

I put the Wren's head north, toward Gannet Point and the dark, and I told myself the usual lie: *this run's different. This one pays.*

The Sable Keys slid by on my port side, black against a blacker sky, and somewhere beyond them a ship full of strangers had gone silent, and I felt it then — the first cold thread of it, the thing you feel in your back teeth before a storm, the thing I'd spend the next weeks learning to name.

The sea out there wasn't just dark.

It was *arranged*.
