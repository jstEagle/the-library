## 2. The Optimists

The Meridian Salvage Syndicate kept its offices in a converted customs house on the Vell harbor front, all brass fittings and salt-bleached shutters, the kind of building that had spent a century watching men sign away their futures and had developed a taste for it. I'd been summoned there twice before. The first time, they'd bought my debt from three smaller creditors in a single afternoon — a courtesy, they called it. The second time, they'd explained the terms of that courtesy while a man named Pell refilled my glass until I stopped counting.

This was the third time, and nobody offered me a drink.

Aldous Renk received me in a room with a view of the anchorage, which I took as deliberate. From his desk you could see every boat in the harbor, including mine, including the Wren sitting low on her mooring like a dog that knew it had done something wrong. Renk stood at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, a slim man in a linen suit the color of dry sand, silver at the temples, not one line on his face that hadn't been put there by smiling.

"Orsen Winterbourne." He said my name the way a man reads a ledger entry he already knows by heart. "Sit down, please."

I sat. There were two other men in the room. One was Pell, who had the build of a dock crane and the expression of a man doing long division behind his eyes. The other was younger, soft-handed, introduced only as Mr. Ferrow, who did something for the syndicate that required him to be described as doing something for the syndicate. They flanked me like bookends on a shelf that held exactly one book.

"You've had a good season," Renk said, still facing the window.

"I've had a season."

"Squall work off the Teeth. Two hull recoveries. A cargo net off Serrano Point worth more than your boat." He turned then, and gave me the smile — the famous one, the one the harbor pilots warned green hands about over cheap rum. *Renk smiles when he's measuring you*, they said. *Worry when he stops.* "You're good at this, Winterbourne. That's the tragedy of you."

"The tragedy of me," I repeated. "That's generous. Most people just say *the debt*."

"Your debt is a fact. Facts aren't tragedies. Tragedies are facts arranged badly." He settled into the chair behind the desk, and Pell moved to stand at his shoulder, and I understood that whatever came next had been rehearsed down to the pauses. "Tell me what you know about the survey vessel *Halcyon*."

The name landed somewhere under my ribs. Everyone on the monsoon coast knew about the *Halcyon* by now — government hydrographic team, eleven souls, gone silent off the Sable Keys nine days back. The official word was equipment failure compounded by weather. The unofficial word, traded across harbors in the low voice men use for things they don't want the sea to overhear, was that there wasn't enough wreckage to match the story. A ship doesn't vanish clean. The sea is a sloppy killer. It always leaves something.

"Public knowledge," I said carefully. "Silent since the ninth. Search stood down four days ago. Weather window's closing."

"And the search found nothing."

"The search found nothing they've announced."

Renk's smile deepened by a degree, the way a knife comes out of a sheath by degrees. "You see, this is why I like you. You don't insult me by pretending the world is simple." He slid a folder across the desk. I didn't touch it. He opened it anyway, angled toward me: charts of the Sable Keys, tide tables, a list of instruments — sonar rigs, magnetic survey gear, sealed data drives — the kind of equipment that costs more than most boats and tells you everything about the bottom of the sea. "The *Halcyon* was carrying government property. That property is now unaccounted for. Certain parties — myself included — have an interest in its recovery."

"What kind of interest?"

"The kind that pays." He let that sit. Outside, a gull screamed once and thought better of it. "Here is my offer, Orsen, and I'll make it only once, so attend. You will go to the Sable Keys ahead of any official recovery effort. You will find the survey gear. You will bring it to me, intact, before anyone else knows where it sleeps. In exchange—" he tapped the folder twice, almost tenderly, "—half your debt dies. Fifty thousand marks, gone. Not deferred. Not restructured. Dead."

Fifty thousand marks. I did the arithmetic I do every night when the bilge pump wakes me — seasons worked against interest accrued, miles run against the tide of it, twelve years of chasing squalls for men like Renk and never once gaining water. Half. Gone. I could sell the Wren, buy something small and honest, and stop measuring my life in nautical miles.

That's how the offer felt. Like a gift. Like a door opening after twelve years of walls.

And that's exactly how a trap is built. Nobody builds a trap that feels like a trap. They build it to feel like the way out.

"Why me?" I asked. "You've got crews. Better crews. Men who don't owe you anything — or men who owe you so much they'd swim to the Keys for you."

"Because you're good," Renk said simply. "And because you're motivated. And because—" and here he paused, and the pause was the truest thing in the room, "—you ask questions like a man who wants answers, not like a man who wants trouble. I need someone who'll look closely at what's out there and bring me what matters. Not everything. What matters."

*What matters.* Not the gear. Something in the gear. Data, maybe. Survey data. And a government survey team goes missing, and the syndicate that owns my life wants its instruments recovered quietly, ahead of the government that owned them, and I'm supposed to believe this is salvage.

Mr. Ferrow spoke for the first time, voice smooth as varnish. "There are other interested parties, of course. Officials. Insurers. If the equipment surfaces through the wrong channels, there could be — complications. Legal ones. For whoever's holding it." He smiled at me with real warmth, which was somehow worse than Renk's professional version. "We'd hate to see complications attach themselves to a man already carrying so much."

There it was. The gift and the gallows in the same breath. Find it for us or find it for them and drown slower.

Pell shifted his weight, and the floorboard under him creaked like a warning.

I looked at the window, at the Wren riding her mooring, at the gray-green line of weather stacking up beyond the harbor mouth — the monsoon coming, twelve days out if it was honest, sooner if it lied. I looked at the folder with its neat charts and its list of expensive sleeping instruments. I looked at Aldous Renk, patient as erosion, waiting for me to do the only thing I could do, which we both knew, which was the whole architecture of the arrangement. My debt wasn't a rope around my neck. It was a rudder. He'd been steering me for years, gently, and now he wanted a specific heading.

A smart man says no. A smart man finds a lawyer, finds a hole, finds a different coast.

I have never claimed to be a smart man. I claim to be a moving one.

"Half," I said. "Forgiven, on delivery. In writing. And I want it in writing that anything else I find out there stays mine to know."

Something flickered behind Renk's eyes — not surprise; I doubt the man has been surprised since boyhood — but a kind of satisfaction, the satisfaction of a fisherman feeling the line come tight. "Anything else you find," he said, "is yours to know. Whether it's wise to know it is another question entirely." He pushed a pen across the desk. It was heavy, silver, engraved with the syndicate's mark: a stylized wave curling into a hook.

I signed. My hand didn't shake, which surprised me. Twelve years of debt teaches you a certain steadiness — you learn to keep your hands level because shaking wastes energy you can't afford.

"Winterbourne." Renk rose, extended his hand, and shook mine with the careful pressure of a man handling something he intends to use up completely. "One more thing. The *Halcyon*'s last position was logged near the northern Sables, but currents in a monsoon season move things. Bodies. Boats. Conclusions." His smile returned, full wattage, warm as a lamp in a cold room. "If you happen to find anyone alive out there — a survivor, a stray crewman, anyone at all — you'll radio it in before you approach. Standard rescue protocol. We wouldn't want you taking unnecessary risks."

"Of course," I said.

"We're optimists here at Meridian," he added, walking me to the door, Pell opening it with the tenderness of a man who enjoyed doors. "We believe every problem, properly handled, resolves itself. You'll find that out there, I think. Everything resolving." He patted my shoulder once, lightly, the way you'd test fruit. "Fair winds, Orsen. Bring me what matters."

* * *

The rain started as I crossed the harbor road, fat warm drops that hit the planks like fingers tapping. I stood under the customs house awning and watched the water darken, and I made myself say it plainly, inside my own skull, where no one could smile at it:

You just agreed to hunt for a missing survey team on behalf of the men most likely to have made them missing.

The rain kept tapping. Down the quay, the Wren rocked on her mooring, and beyond her the sea lay flat and innocent as a filed report. Fifty thousand marks. Half my life, forgiven. All I had to do was find some drowned men's instruments and not ask what drowned them — and if anything out there was still breathing, I was to call it in first, so that whatever happened next could happen tidily, at a distance, to someone else.

I pulled my collar up and walked for the boat, counting the steps between me and the water. Forty-two. I'd counted them before, leaving that building. It's always forty-two, and it always feels longer going out than coming in.

By the time I reached the Wren, the rain had committed to the afternoon, and somewhere east, past the harbor lights, the Sable Keys were waiting under the same gray ceiling — eleven people gone quiet, a folder full of charts, and a debt half-cancelled against a whole lot of questions.

I untied the lines. The current was already turning.
