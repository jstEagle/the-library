## 3. Gear and No Tracks

The Sable Keys don't look like a place where eleven people could disappear. They look like a place where you'd lose your hat.

Low green humps strung along the reef like beads someone dropped and couldn't be bothered to pick up. The water around them is that impossible turquoise the brochures lie about — except out here nobody prints brochures, so the color just sits there being beautiful for no one, which struck me as about right.

I came in on the falling tide with the Wren's engine coughing like an old man climbing stairs, and I'll admit I talked to her the whole way in. Not affectionately. More like a man negotiating with a landlord.

"Come on. Just get me there. You can fall apart after."

She got me there. She always gets me there. It's the getting back part she has opinions about.

The survey camp was on the largest of the keys, tucked behind a windbreak of casuarina trees, and I saw it before I saw anything wrong with it. That was the first wrong thing, actually. From two hundred yards offshore it looked *occupied*. A tent ridge-line taut against the sky. Equipment staged in neat rows under tarps. A generator sitting square on its pallet like it expected to be asked to work any minute now.

Camps that have been abandoned don't look like that. Abandoned camps sag. They lean. They have the posture of things that have given up. This one had the posture of a camp that had stepped out for a moment and would be right back.

I anchored off the sand spit and took the dinghy in, and I want it noted for the record that I did not like the quiet. Monsoon coast quiet isn't quiet at all — it's a held breath. The birds go somewhere else. The surf keeps talking but stops saying anything. I've worked enough wrecks to know the difference between a place where nothing is happening and a place where something already happened and left.

This was the second kind.

* * *

The first thing I checked was the instruments, because that's what Renk was paying me to check, and because checking them gave my hands something to do while my stomach caught up.

They were all there. That was the second wrong thing.

A differential GPS unit, worth more than everything I own including the Wren and possibly my teeth. A side-scan sonar towfish, crated, foam-packed, dry as a sermon. Two laptops under a rain fly, battery indicators still showing charge. A watermaker. A medical kit with the seal unbroken. Radios — three of them — racked and dry.

Eleven people had walked away from roughly forty thousand dollars of government equipment and hadn't taken so much as a multitool.

Now, I've salvaged enough sites to know how abandonment actually goes. When people leave in a hurry, they take what matters: water, comms, weapons, anything with a resale value. When they leave dead, they leave everything, but they usually leave *evidence* too — blood in the sand, drag marks, the ugly arithmetic of violence. And when they're evacuated properly, there's paperwork, a note, a marker, some official fingerprint saying *we were here and we handled it.*

This was none of those. This was a fourth thing. This was a camp that had been *tidied*.

I walked the perimeter twice. The tent stakes had been pulled and reset — I could see the fresh scuff over old holes, somebody striking camp and then thinking better of it, or wanting it to look struck. The fire pit had been burned down to white ash and raked flat. Raked. Flat. Nobody rakes a fire pit when they're running for their lives. You rake a fire pit when you don't want a forensic team reading your garbage.

There was a latrine trench, filled in. A refuse pit, filled in. Even the footprints on the beach had been swept by hand — I found the sweep marks near the landing point, broad strokes through the sand like someone had gone at it with a palm frond and all the patience in the world.

No bodies. No blood. No tracks.

And no lifeboat.

That one stopped me cold, standing there in the raked sand with the rain starting to think about getting serious. The davits on their survey launch were empty. The tender was gone. So either eleven people had crowded onto one small boat and motored off into the teeth of a building monsoon — possible, not probable — or somebody had taken it, and the somebody who took it had also taken the time to erase a camp behind them.

You don't do that if you're fleeing. You do that if you're *burying*.

I stood there a long moment, listening to the surf say nothing, and I thought about Aldous Renk's smile. The way it sat on his face like it had been installed by professionals. *Find the gear before anyone else does,* he'd said. *Half your debt, Winterbourne. Consider it a gift.*

Gear was here. Gear had been here the whole time, sitting in plain sight, waiting for someone to find it.

So what exactly was I being paid to find?

* * *

I spent two hours cataloguing anyway. Habit, mostly, but also because a man in my position learns to build records — dates, times, serial numbers, photographs on the battered camera I keep for insurance claims. If this went sideways, and every instinct I owned was filing a formal complaint saying it would, I wanted a paper trail that didn't depend on anyone's word. Especially mine. My word had a lien on it.

The team's logbook was the one thing missing. There was a shelf in the command tent sized for it — dust-free rectangle, four binder clips waiting — and nothing on it. Everything else accounted for. Everything except the one item that would say what they'd found.

I stood in that tent with the rain drumming the fly and felt the shape of the absence the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue. Somebody had taken the logbook. Somebody had left forty thousand dollars of sonar to protect a notebook.

I'd been paid to find gear. The gear wasn't lost. The gear was bait.

"Okay," I said out loud, to the tent, to the rain, to nobody. "Okay."

The tide was turning. I knew I should get back to the Wren, radio it in — *site located, equipment intact, no sign of crew* — collect my half-forgiveness, and let the current carry whatever happened next to somebody else's shore. That was the smart play. That was the play a man twelve years in debt makes, the man who measures his life in nautical miles because miles can be logged and logged miles can be paid off.

Instead I found myself walking the shoreline, north along the key, looking at caves.

Because here's the arithmetic that wouldn't leave me alone: the tender was gone, but the sea cave systems on these keys run deep, and if even *one* person from that team had stayed behind — hiding, waiting, watching whoever came next — they'd need water, shelter, and a sightline to the anchorage. And if I'd come here to make eleven people into zero people, I'd have swept the shoreline first.

Which meant if anyone was alive on this rock, they were watching me right now, and they'd watched me catalogue their abandoned camp, and they were currently deciding whether I was rescue or executioner.

I made sure my movements looked bored. Hands in pockets. Ambling. A man killing time before the weather. I drifted up the beach toward the black basalt shoulder at the island's north end, where the lava had cooled a million years ago into shelves and hollows, and I let my eyes do casual laps of the shadow lines.

Third pass, I caught it.

Not a person. A *tide line*. A scatter of crab shells and fish bones below a dark opening in the basalt, picked clean in a way that said *someone's been eating here for days*. And above the shells, faint as breath on glass, a smudge of smoke staining on the rock face. Small fire. Very small. Someone who knew that smoke kills you faster than hunger out here.

I should have turned around. I want that on the record too. Every smart cell in my body voted to turn around, get on the Wren, and become a man who found an empty camp and went home rich-ish.

But the mystery had a heartbeat now. I could hear it. And I've spent twelve years learning that you don't walk away from a thing once it's started beating — you just tell yourself you will, and then you keep walking.

I stopped ten yards from the cave mouth, well outside grabbing range, and I crouched down on my heels where anyone watching could see my hands, empty, resting on my knees.

"I'm not with whoever emptied your camp," I said to the darkness. "My name's Orsen Winterbourne. I'm a salvage contractor, which is a polite word for a scavenger, and I'm working for people I don't trust, which is why I'm telling you the truth. Your equipment's intact. Your logbook's gone. And the monsoon's about thirty hours out."

Nothing. The surf. The rain thickening.

"If you're armed," I added, "and I'd bet my boat you are, now would be a great time to decide I'm more useful alive."

The silence stretched long enough that I started composing my exit — and then the darkness shifted, and there was a sound like a shell being chambered, small and mechanical and absolutely certain, and a voice came out of the cave. A woman's voice. Hoarse from disuse and salt air, but level. Level the way a drawn wire is level.

"One step closer and I'll show you exactly how useful you are."

I stayed crouched. Rain ran off my nose. Somewhere behind us the sky went the color of wet slate, and the first real gust of the coming monsoon moved through the casuarinas like a rumor getting organized.

"That's fair," I said. "For what it's worth, I counted your fire smoke before I counted your gun. Whoever taught you fieldcraft taught you well."

A pause. Then, quieter, almost to herself:

"Whoever taught me fieldcraft is why I'm the only one left."

She stepped forward into the gray light — thin as a whipstock, sunburned, a pistol held low and steady in both hands, eyes that had been awake too long — and I understood, all at once and completely, that the empty camp had never been the mystery.

She was.

"Coral Vane," she said. "Government survey team. And you're going to tell me exactly who sent you, Mr. Winterbourne, before I decide whether you leave this island."
