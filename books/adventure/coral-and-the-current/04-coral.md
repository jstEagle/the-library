## 4. Coral

I had a knife at his throat before he cleared the mouth of the cave, which I counted as a point in my favor. Eleven days alone on a rock with half a canteen and a dead man's radio will sharpen a person.

"Easy," he said. Hands up, palms out, the universal language of *please don't*. Rain dripped off him in ropes. "I'm not with them."

"That's what someone with them would say."

"It's also what someone not with them would say. You see my problem."

I saw his problem. I also saw the trawler at anchor beyond the surf line, the syndicate's gull-and-hook mark stenciled on her hull, and the salvage rigging that meant Meridian money had bought every rope on deck. So I pressed the knife a little closer and asked him who he was, and he said Orsen Winterbourne, like the name should mean something, like I hadn't spent the last two years hearing salvage men say it with a mixture of pity and respect. The debt man. The storm chaser who owed more than his boat was worth and kept working like the sea would eventually forgive him.

"Renk sent you," I said.

"Renk sent me to find survey gear." His eyes did something complicated. "I'm beginning to suspect Renk didn't tell me everything."

"Beginning."

"I'm a slow learner. It's a documented condition."

I should have cut him and run. There was nowhere to run — my boat was a burned hulk on the far side of the atoll, my team was gone, my radio was dead, and the barometer had been falling all morning like it had somewhere to be. The monsoon was twelve hours out, maybe less. The cave would flood to the ceiling when it came. So I did the arithmetic nobody wants to do: one armed, starving cartographer versus one storm and one syndicate, or one armed, starving cartographer plus one debt-ridden stranger with a boat.

I lowered the knife. I didn't put it away.

"Sit down," I said. "Don't touch anything. And if you're lying to me, I want you to know I've had eleven days to think about how I'd kill the next person who came through that cave, and I've gotten very creative."

He sat down on a wet ledge and looked, for the first time, at the camp I'd built out of the cave's back pocket. The survey instruments, wrapped in tarps. The data drives, sealed in waterproof bags. Dr. Amara's field journal, its cover swollen with damp. He looked at all of it the way a man looks at a thing he was sent to find, and then he looked away from it, deliberately, and I'll admit that bought him more than the knife had.

"You found the camp," he said. "The main one."

"Someone found it before me. They took the bodies."

His head came up. "Bodies."

"There were nine of us. I buried two that the surf gave back. The other seven, someone collected. Neatly. Professionally." I let that sit. "You don't collect bodies unless you're cleaning up. You don't clean up unless there's a mess."

"Or unless someone's paying by the mess."

"Renk's men don't get paid by the hour, Mr. Winterbourne. They get paid by the outcome."

He was quiet for a while. Outside, the surf was changing its mind, the long slow swell coming in ahead of the storm, and I watched him do his own arithmetic — I could see it moving behind his face like weather. When he spoke again, his voice had lost the wryness. What was underneath was tired in a way I recognized.

"The syndicate's been running quiet dredging on this shelf for three years," he said. "Everyone in the trade knows it, nobody can prove it. Renk calls his crews optimists. They go out with permits that don't cover what they pull up, and the officials who'd notice have all got new roofs."

"And your job was to find their lost equipment."

"My job was to find the survey gear *before anyone else did*. His words." He rubbed his face. "I told myself it was salvage. Instruments, charts, whatever the team left. I told myself a lot of things on the way out here."

"What changed?"

"You did." He said it plainly, no charm in it. "A dead survey team and a woman with a knife who knows what Renk's men do with bodies. That's not salvage. That's a grave someone's still digging."

I wanted to hate him for the flattery of it, the way he made his ignorance sound like virtue. But I'd been eleven days with no one to talk to except the dead, and there's a kind of hunger worse than the one in your stomach. I gave him a canteen half full of rainwater and half a tin of condensed milk, and while he ate I told him what my team had found — not all of it, because I still didn't trust him, but the shape of it.

* * *

"We were mapping the shelf," I said. "Sable Keys, the deep channels, the whole drowned geography. Routine work. Six weeks of it, and it was good work, and then in week four we started finding scars."

"Scars."

"Dredge scars. On the seafloor, in the sonar, plain as handwriting." I traced it in the air between us — the long parallel furrows, the tailings fans, the way a dredge writes its name across a reef when nobody stops it. "Except there's no license for dredging inside the Sable reserve. Not one. Not for Meridian, not for anyone. The whole shelf is supposed to be untouched."

"So the data was wrong."

"The data was *right*. That was the problem." I pulled Dr. Amara's journal out of its bag, because some things you have to show and not tell, and opened it to the pages where she'd cross-referenced our sonar passes against the shipping registers. Her handwriting got smaller and angrier as the weeks went on. "She matched the scar patterns to vessel drafts. Three ships, running at night, running dark, working the shelf in rotations. Every one of them flagged to a holding company, and every holding company traces back — if you're patient, and Amara was patient — to Meridian Salvage."

"Renk."

"Renk." I closed the journal. "She filed it. Encrypted transmission to the ministry, the day before we went silent. And twelve hours later our tender wouldn't start, our radio had a fault nobody could find, and a boat with no lights came into the lagoon at two in the morning."

He was staring at me. "You think they came for the data."

"I think they came for the data and stayed for the witnesses. I think Amara's transmission was intercepted before it ever reached the ministry, because the man who was supposed to receive it is on Meridian's roof-receipt list. And I think the only reason I'm alive is that I couldn't sleep and went out to check a tide gauge, and when I came back the camp was on fire."

I hadn't said any of it out loud before. Saying it made it real in a way eleven days of silence hadn't, and my hands were shaking, and I was furious at them for shaking in front of a stranger.

"They'll say the storm took you," Winterbourne said quietly. "A survey team lost in a squall. It happens every season. Nobody asks questions about the sea."

"Nobody except a cartographer who's very good at making maps of where things *actually* are." I tapped the waterproof bags. "There are three drives in there. Raw sonar, vessel tracks, Amara's cross-references, and the transmission logs showing what was sent and what was never received. Renk didn't lose equipment on this shelf, Mr. Winterbourne. He lost a confession, and I'm carrying it."

* * *

He didn't touch the drives. I want that on the record, wherever records go when the sea takes everything else. A man sent by Aldous Renk to recover exactly those drives sat on a wet rock with them three feet away and didn't touch them, and when I asked him why, he looked surprised by the question.

"Because they're not mine."

"They're worth more than your boat."

"Everything's worth more than my boat. That's not the argument you think it is." He laughed, but it didn't land as a laugh. "You want to know what I owe Renk? Four hundred and sixty thousand. Twelve years of interest on a salvage contract I signed at twenty-three because I was stupid and my father was dying and Meridian was the only outfit that would front the gear. I've paid it down to nothing and the nothing keeps regrowing. Renk owns my mornings. He owns my weather. He owns—" He stopped, and looked at the rain coming down harder outside the cave, and finished differently. "He owned me. Past tense. I'm deciding about the present tense."

"And if you go back and tell him you found nothing?"

"He'll know I'm lying. Renk always knows. That's the terrible thing about him — he's patient. He doesn't threaten. He *waits*." Winterbourne shook his head. "He'll send men to this cave whether I report it or not. The storm's coming, and after the storm, they'll sweep these keys. If you're here when they do—"

"I won't be here when they do."

"Then we should probably discuss the fact that your boat is a rumor and mine is at anchor with a syndicate's paint on her hull, and in twelve hours there's going to be more water in the air than in the ocean."

I looked at him for a long time. Here is what I knew: he was Meridian, through and through, marked and mortgaged. Here is what else I knew: he'd had eleven days of my silence and seven of my team's graves to learn what side of this he stood on, and he'd walked into my cave and told me the truth about his own debt without my asking, which is either honesty or the best con I've ever been inside of, and I've been conned by professionals.

"You understand," I said, "that if I come on your boat, and it turns out you're Renk's, I will sink her with you aboard. I'm a cartographer. I know exactly where the rocks are."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's said to me in years."

"I'm not being romantic. I'm being precise."

"Precise," he repeated, like the word was a rope he was testing his weight on. He stood, and looked out at the surf, where the Wren rode her anchor with the light going strange and green behind her — the storm light, the one fishermen don't like to name. "Twelve hours," he said. "We can make the mainland coast if we run ahead of it. Maybe. If the Wren holds together, which is a theological question."

"And if Renk's people are watching your boat?"

"They're watching my boat." He said it without flinching, which I made myself respect. "There's a chase tender that's been shadowing me since the Sable channel. Renk doesn't send people out here alone. He sends people out here *accompanied*."

"So we're not running from a storm. We're running from a storm with an audience."

"Now you're getting it." He held out his hand, and I looked at it, and I did not take it, because I wasn't ready for that and he seemed to understand, and turned the gesture into picking up Amara's journal instead, carefully, like it was something that could break. "We should move now, while the light's bad enough to hide us and good enough to run the channel. There's a gap in the reef on the north side that isn't on the charts."

"It's on my charts," I said. "I made them."

"Then today you get to be useful *and* forgiven half a debt."

"Mr. Winterbourne." I shouldered the drives, and the knife, and eleven days of grief, and looked him full in the face. "If we live through this, I'm going to make you a map of every dredge scar on that shelf, and you're going to help me nail it to Renk's door. That's the only payment I'm offering."

For the first time since he'd walked into my cave, Orsen Winterbourne smiled like it wasn't a defense.

"Deal," he said. "But we should hurry. Because I think our audience just moved."
