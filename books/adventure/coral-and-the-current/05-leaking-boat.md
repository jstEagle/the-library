## 5. Leaking Boat

The Wren had opinions about weather, and she shared them the way old women share complaints about their knees — constantly, and with the implication that you should have listened sooner.

I'd owned her nine years. Long enough to know that the groan in her stern came from a cracked stringer I couldn't afford to fix, and that the rattle in the wheelhouse was a loose brass fitting I'd never bothered to tighten because the rattle told me my heading better than the compass did. She was forty feet of rusted stubbornness, and she was, as of four hours ago, carrying the only person in the world I couldn't afford to lose.

Coral Vane sat in the wheelhouse with her arms crossed, watching the western sky like it owed her money.

"That cloud," she said, "has a green edge to it."

"Green means it's serious."

"Green means it's angry. There's a difference."

"There is," I admitted, easing the throttle forward another notch. "Angry is worse."

We were running ahead of the monsoon the way you run ahead of a debt collector — not because you'll outrun it, but because the running feels like a plan. The Sable Keys were forty nautical miles astern, the mangrove channels of the coast sixty ahead, and between us and either was a stretch of open water that the barometer had been cursing since dawn. The Wren bucked and slapped through the building chop, and every third wave threw a handful of itself over the bow as if testing whether we'd flinch.

Coral didn't flinch. She'd been at sea too long to flinch at water. But I noticed her hand kept drifting to the canvas bag between her feet — the one holding the drives, the coordinates, the whole rotten architecture of Aldous Renk's optimism — and pressing down on it, like she could hold the truth in place by weight alone.

"You should sleep," I said.

"You should look at your engine temperature gauge."

I looked. The gauge was climbing with the enthusiasm of everything else that day. I said nothing, which she correctly interpreted as agreement, and went aft to check the bilge.

I heard her before I saw what she was looking at. A short, flat exhale — not a gasp, Coral didn't gasp — the sound of a cartographer confirming a bad reading.

"Orsen."

The bilge had four inches of water in it. That was the first problem. The second problem was that four hours ago it had had two.

* * *

Here is a thing they don't put in the brochures about owning a boat: a boat is not an object. It is a slow-motion negotiation with the sea, conducted on your behalf by a hull that is always, always losing. You pump the water out; the sea sends more. You patch the seam; the sea finds another. Every boat afloat is a temporary agreement, and the Wren's agreement was, as of that afternoon, entering renegotiation.

I got the manual bilge pump working and stood at it like a man at confession, working the handle in long strokes while Coral read me the numbers off the depth of water. Two inches an hour. Three, when the weather picked up. The electric pump chugged along underneath it all, faithful and insufficient, a metronome counting down to something.

"Through-hull fitting," I said. "Aft, port side. It's been weeping since I bought her. I packed it with epoxy two seasons ago."

"And the epoxy?"

"Is currently demonstrating why you don't buy discount epoxy."

Coral crouched at the bilge opening, studying the weeping seam with the same focused attention she'd given the dredging charts. She had a way of looking at problems — not with fear, exactly, but with the flat professional interest of someone who has already decided the problem will be solved and is merely negotiating the terms.

"I can get to it," she said. "From inside, if I pull the storage panel."

"It's under the waterline."

"I noticed."

"It'll come in like a fire hose."

"Then I'll want something to plug it with and you'll want to stop pumping for thirty seconds so I can hear where it's actually coming from." She looked up at me, rain-wet hair stuck to her forehead, salt dried in white crescents under her eyes. "Unless you'd rather argue about it. We have time for one or the other."

We did it her way, because her way was right. I handed her the tapered softwood plug I kept for exactly this prayer, and she stripped off her jacket, wedged herself into the bilge space with her cheek an inch from cold seawater, and found the fitting by feel while I counted thirty seconds in a language I don't speak. The inflow went from a hiss to a roar to a muffled gurgle, and then her fist came up out of the dark with the plug seated, and I put the pump back to work, and the Wren's slow surrender paused, for the moment, at four inches.

Coral climbed out soaked to the waist, shivering, and accepted the dry shirt I kept in the wheelhouse locker without comment. There was something in the way she took it — the way she'd taken the food I'd rationed her, the way she'd taken the wheel when my hands cramped — that I was starting to recognize. It was the economy of a person who has learned that accepting help quickly is the only way to keep from owing it.

I knew that economy. I'd invented it.

* * *

The rain found us an hour before dark.

Not the monsoon itself — that was still out west, a bruise on the horizon with lightning working inside it like a thought too big to say — but the outriders, the long gray curtains of it that come ahead of the front and turn the world the color of unwashed linen. Visibility dropped to a quarter mile. The wind swung around and started shoving the Wren sideways, and I corrected into it and watched the fuel gauge do its own slow arithmetic.

Coral was at the window, staring astern.

"Orsen. There's a light."

I looked. There was. A white running light, riding high, holding position off our quarter at maybe three hundred yards — close enough to see, far enough to plead innocence. It had been there, I realized, for some time. It had been matching our speed.

"Could be a fisherman," Coral said, in the voice of someone who did not believe it.

"Fishermen don't hold station in building weather. They run for harbor like everyone else."

"Then who—"

"Renk's people." I said it flat, because saying it any other way would have cost me something I couldn't spare. "He put a chase boat on the water the day the survey went silent. I told you he'd want to know who found you first."

"You told me he'd want the gear. You didn't tell me he'd want *you*."

"He wants what I have. What I have is you." I adjusted the wheel, and the Wren groaned her stringer-groan, and somewhere aft the bilge pump chugged its faithful, insufficient chug. "There's a channel ahead. The Cut at Marrow Point. It runs between the mangroves — shallow, twisting, no room for a boat his size. If I can make it before full dark—"

"He'll just wait at the other end."

"He'll have to guess which end. Or send men in a dinghy. Or give up." I heard how that sounded and didn't bother correcting it. Renk did not give up. Renk waited. Renk's patience was the load-bearing wall of his entire operation — I'd been paying interest on it for twelve years.

Coral was quiet for a while. The rain ticked on the wheelhouse roof, a thousand small fingers. The light off our quarter burned steady, patient, keeping its distance the way a creditor keeps his distance — never threatening, never leaving.

"You could still hand me over," she said at last. Not accusing. Just laying the fact on the chart between us, the way she laid everything down. "Half your debt, gone. Maybe all of it, if you bring them the drives too. That was the offer."

"That was the trap."

"It was both. Most things are." She turned to look at me, and in the dim of the wheelhouse her eyes were the gray-green of the water itself. "I've been doing the math since the cave. You're not a rich man, Orsen. You're not even a solvent one. Everything you're doing right now — the detour, the fuel, the bilge — you're spending it all on me. I want to know why, before the storm makes the decision for us."

I watched the rain. I watched the light astern. I thought about twelve years of nautical miles, and how a man can measure his whole life in distance covered and still never arrive anywhere.

"Because I've been owned for twelve years," I said. "And I know exactly what it feels like. And I'm not going to hand them a person just because they asked politely."

Coral didn't say anything. But she moved — just slightly, just enough that her shoulder came to rest against my arm, and stayed there, and the Wren drove on into the rain with the only dry thing aboard being the two of us.

* * *

We made the Cut at last light, and it was worse than I remembered.

The channel twisted through the mangroves like a dropped rope — thirty feet wide in the good places, fifteen in the bad, the water the color of steeped tea and hiding stumps and sandbars that the charts, in their optimism, called *unverified*. I ran it on memory and instinct, throttle down to a crawl, Coral on the bow with a spotlight calling out the water ahead.

"Starboard! Hard starboard — no — *easy* starboard —"

"Easy starboard," I muttered, easing the wheel. "You're a natural."

"Shut up and steer."

The chase boat's light appeared once, far back through the curtain of rain, hanging at the mouth of the Cut like a patient eye. Then the mangroves closed over it, and it was gone, and I let myself believe — for about nine minutes — that we'd slipped it.

The Wren knew better. The Wren had been trying to tell me all day.

It was Coral who heard it first — a change in the bilge pump's rhythm, a hollower note, the sound of a machine working harder to move less. I shut down the engine in a wide bend of the channel, and we listened, and beneath the rain on the canopy and the drip from the mangroves there it was: water, moving. Not weeping. *Coming.*

The aft fitting had let go around my softwood plug. The epoxy had failed everywhere at once, the way discount epoxy does — all at once, and with a certain dignity. Water was coming through the hull in three places now, and the electric pump was keeping pace with none of them, and the manual pump was buried under the storage panel Coral had torn out, and the Wren was settling by the stern with the slow, apologetic air of a thing that has held a secret as long as it could.

I stood in the flooding bilge in the dark, up to my ankles, then my shins, and did the math I didn't want to do. We were two miles from open water and the open water was two hours from shelter and the storm was one hour from everything.

"Orsen." Coral stood at the bilge hatch, the spotlight in her hand, the drives in the bag on her shoulder. Her face in the light was calm the way deep water is calm. "Tell me what you need."

What I needed was a miracle with a hardware-store inventory. What I had was a woman who'd survived nine days alone in a sea cave, a boat that was dying on her own schedule, and a storm coming up the channel behind us with all the patience of Aldous Renk himself.

And then, through the rain, from the mouth of the Cut — a searchlight, sweeping the mangroves, coming on slow.

They hadn't waited at the other end.

They'd come in after us.
