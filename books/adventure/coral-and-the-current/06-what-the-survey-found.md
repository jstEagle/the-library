## 6. What the Survey Found

The mangrove channel was the ugliest safe place I'd ever hidden in, and I had hidden in some contenders. The trees grew right out of the water like they'd given up on land entirely, their roots making a lattice of knuckles and elbows that swallowed the Wren whole. Orsen eased her in on the last of the light, engine down to a mutter, and cut it before the propeller could kiss anything it would regret. Then there was just the rain on the canopy — a thousand small polite hands drumming — and the creak of a boat that sounded, frankly, worse than it had that morning.

"Cozy," I said.

"I've slept in worse."

"I believe you. That's the tragedy."

He didn't laugh. He'd been not-laughing for six hours, in a way I was learning to read. Orsen Winterbourne had a whole taxonomy of silences, and the one he was wearing now was the kind that meant he was doing arithmetic he didn't like. I recognized it. I'd been doing the same arithmetic since the cave.

I climbed down into the cabin with the survey drives wrapped in oilcloth against my chest like a feverish child. Three of them. My team's entire final week, sealed in salt-corroded aluminum, waiting for someone patient enough to make them confess.

I had a hand-crank decrypt rig that Prentiss — our comms officer, Prentiss who ironed his shirts on a research vessel and called it "standards" — would have wept at. It had belonged to my father. It was older than me and twice as stubborn, and it worked the way old things work: slowly, resentfully, and only if you asked nicely.

"Food first," Orsen said, and put a tin in front of me. Beans. Again.

"Data first."

"Food. You're shaking."

"I'm always shaking. It's called being alive."

He put the tin opener down between us with the deliberate calm of a man who had dealt with difficult cargo before and wasn't going to be out-stubborned by me. So I ate the beans. He watched the channel through the porthole the whole time, one hand resting on the throttle housing like a man holding a sleeping dog's collar.

* * *

The rain thickened after dark, which was good. Rain was noise, and noise was cover, and I needed to talk to machines that had been told to keep secrets.

I set up on the chart table because it was the only flat surface and because Orsen's charts were already ruined by mildew and a coffee ring shaped like Australia. The first drive took forty minutes of cranking and cursing. It gave me telemetry — engine logs, dive profiles, sediment samples. The second gave me half a corrupted video file and a great deal of nothing.

The third drive was warm.

Not metaphorically. I put my palm flat on the casing and it was warm, faintly, the way a thing is warm when it's been running recently. My stomach did something complicated. Drives don't run themselves. Someone had powered this one — recently, deliberately — and either they were dead now, or they weren't, and I genuinely could not decide which possibility frightened me more.

"Coral." Orsen's voice from the cockpit, low. "Light off."

I killed my lamp. Through the porthole, out in the black sheet of the channel mouth, a boat light swept past — a long, patient arc, the kind of light that isn't looking for anything so much as confirming. It moved on. We breathed.

"Renk's," Orsen said. Not a question.

"Or the survey's friends," I said. "The government's been so helpful so far."

"That's not a thing you get to joke about yet."

"Everything's a thing I get to joke about. It's the only equipment I have left."

He came down into the cabin and sat across the chart table from me, and in the dark his face was just an outline and a pair of eyes that had decided, somewhere in the last day, to stop pretending he was only in this for the money. I cranked. The drive surrendered.

* * *

The final transmission was a data burst, compressed, timestamped eleven days ago — four hours before the *Meridian Dawn* went silent and the government started calling my team "unaccounted for" in that tone officials use, the one that means *we have decided what happened and the paperwork agrees with us*.

It was addressed to a relay I didn't recognize. Prentiss had routed it wide — broadcast, not pointed — because he'd stopped trusting the official channels, and I understood now that Prentiss had stopped trusting them about a week before the rest of us caught up.

I decrypted. The coordinates came up first, and I almost laughed, because they were wrong. They were wrong in the specific, boring way that coordinates are wrong when someone has transposed digits, and I spent a full minute being the professional cartographer, the woman who corrects other people's maps for a living, before I understood that they weren't wrong at all.

They were a dredge track.

A long, clean, machine-straight scar running along the seafloor off the Sable Keys — eleven kilometers of it, cut through a shelf that appears on every licensed chart as protected coral. My team had mapped it on the second dive. I had mapped it. I remembered the sonar return coming back looking like someone had dragged a comb through a garden, and I remembered Voss, our lead, going very quiet and saying, "That's not on any permit," in the voice of a man reading his own obituary.

The drive had more. Dive logs. Sample manifests. And then the thing I had to read three times because my brain kept refusing it: a chain of correspondence, mirrored from a syndicate server — internal Meridian Salvage traffic, dredging schedules, fuel requisitions, and payment authorizations signed with a name I knew from the news, from the port authority's letterhead, from the very agency that had dispatched my team to survey those waters.

They'd sent us to find evidence of the crime. And when we found it, they sent the storm after us.

"Orsen," I said.

"I'm reading over your shoulder. I've been reading over your shoulder for a while."

"The survey wasn't lost. It was *scheduled*."

"I know," he said, and his voice had gone somewhere flat and far away, and that was when I realized he was reading something else — a different part of the file, the part with the fuel requisitions, the part with the chase-boat registrations. The part with his own debt in it.

* * *

He sat back from the table like a man who'd been struck, quietly, somewhere that doesn't bruise.

"Say it," I said.

"Twelve years ago I took a loan from Meridian to buy the Wren. Renk personally signed it. I always thought — " He stopped. Started again. "I thought I was a bad bet they took a chance on. That's the story they let me tell myself. Bad luck, bad rates, interest that grows like kelp. Twelve years and I've paid back double what I borrowed and owe more than I started with. I did the math a hundred times and always assumed I was the mistake."

"It's not a loan," I said. "It's a leash. The debt was designed to never clear. You weren't a bad investment, Orsen. You were the investment. They weren't lending you money — they were buying a man who'd chase anything they pointed at and ask no questions, because a man with questions checks the paperwork."

He was quiet for a long time. The rain filled it in.

"The survey job," he said finally. "Renk offered to forgive half my debt if I found the gear first. I thought I was lucky."

"You were *selected*. They needed someone deniable on the site before the official salvage teams got there. Someone who owed them everything and knew it. Someone who'd bring back the drives and never ask why the government was paying a salvage syndicate to clean up a government crime." I watched him take that in, watched it land in the place where a man keeps the story of his own life. "They didn't send you to find us. They sent you to erase us."

"And instead I found you."

"Congratulations. You're now the second-least expendable person in this channel."

Something moved through his face — not a smile, but the shadow a smile leaves. Then it went, and what was underneath was harder and better. I'd seen that look before, on my father, the one time he talked about the war. It's the look of a man who has just learned the map he's been navigating by was drawn by the enemy, and who has decided to draw his own.

"They'll have a chase boat on the fuel depot at Corren's Landing," he said. "And the one after that. And the harbormaster, and the coast station, and every relay between here and the capital, because they paid for all of it." He tapped the drive. "This doesn't go to the authorities. The authorities are the invoice."

"Then who?"

"Everyone." He looked up at me, and there was something almost frightening in how fast the arithmetic had finished. "You don't hand this to a man who can be bought. You hand it to the whole ocean at once. You broadcast it wide, the way your Prentiss tried to. You make it so public, so everywhere, so *already heard*, that drowning it costs more than confessing."

"That requires a transmitter with reach. And a storm to hide behind."

"The season's last one makes landfall in four days." He said it the way other men say *I have a plan*, which is to say, the way men say *I have a way to die that at least makes sense*. "And the Wren's mast still stands."

"Your boat is leaking from three places I've personally bailed."

"Then we'd best find the fourth before it finds us."

I should have argued. I want to tell you I weighed it, that I thought about survival, about the inquiry I could trigger from a safe harbor, about all the sensible corridors out of this. But I had spent eleven days in a sea cave eating barnacles and hating myself for a distress call that may have brought the killers to my team, and I had learned the one lesson this whole coast teaches: the current doesn't negotiate, it only carries, and you choose early what it carries you toward.

"Orsen," I said. "Your debt. If we do this — if you do this — Renk will never forgive it. You understand that. It's twelve years of your life."

He was quiet. Then he said, "I've been paying it off my whole adult life. It's about time I spent it on something."

Outside, the rain kept up its patient applause. Somewhere north, a chase boat was turning slow circles around a channel it thought was empty. And on the chart table between us, a warm aluminum drive held the whole rotten map of it — the dredge scar, the signatures, the leash with my cartographer's hands and his debtor's neck caught in it.

Four days. One storm. One mast.

I started planning the route north, and for the first time since the *Meridian Dawn* went dark, the map in my head was mine.
