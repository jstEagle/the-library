## 7. The Long Rain

**Orsen**

The monsoon didn't arrive so much as move in, the way a creditor does — politely at first, then permanently.

We found the mangrove channel on the third hour of the blow, a green throat of water threading back from the coast where the Wren could sit low and the rain couldn't reach us except sideways, which it did anyway, from every direction at once. I killed the engine and listened to the storm work the canopy overhead like a man shuffling cards he never intends to deal.

"Cozy," Coral said, wringing out her shirt over the bilge.

"That's the word for it."

It was not the word for it. The word for it was *trapped*, or possibly *screwed*, but she'd been aboard two days and I hadn't yet taught her my full vocabulary of despair. There was time. The forecast said five days of this, maybe six, before the system staggered inland and left us a sky we could argue under.

Five days pinned in a leaking trawler with a woman who'd watched her whole team vanish and still kept her sidearm cleaned to parade standard. I'd had worse company. I'd also had better-armed company, though not by much.

The pump ran every twenty minutes. The hull wept through a seam aft that I'd been meaning to weld since March, because March-me had been an optimist too, and look how that turned out for everyone.

* * *

**Coral**

Day two of the rain, I learned three things about Orsen Winterbourne.

One: he rationed food like a man who'd once run out. He counted the tins twice, wrote the number on the inside of his wrist with a grease pencil, and gave me the larger share without making a ceremony of it, which somehow made it worse. Kindness performed quietly is harder to refuse than kindness performed loudly.

Two: he talked to the boat. Not sweetly. "Come on, you rusted argument," he told the bilge pump, "you've outlasted worse than me." I got the impression the boat disagreed.

Three: he slept sitting up, one hand on the wheel, like a man who expected the sea to try something while he wasn't looking. Maybe it had. Maybe it would again.

I didn't trust him. Trust is a currency I stopped carrying after the survey camp went quiet and I spent nine days in a sea cave eating raw shellfish and rehearsing alibis to nobody. But distrust gets heavy, and the rain made everything heavier, and somewhere around the fourth tin of beans I heard myself say:

"Why does Renk own you?"

Orsen looked up from the engine manual he wasn't reading. "Own's a strong word."

"It's the accurate one. I've read your face when his name comes up. Men don't make that face about a bank."

He laughed — short, no humor in it, just air escaping. Then he did something I didn't expect. He put down the manual.

"Twelve years ago I had a brother," he said. "Dario. Good sailor, terrible gambler. He borrowed against our mother's house to cover a marker in Renk's back rooms. When he couldn't pay, Renk offered him a job instead. Salvage runs. Deep ones. Unlicensed ones." His thumb moved along the wheel spoke, tracing wood grain that wasn't there anymore. "Third run, the weather turned early. Dario went down with four other men and a hold full of copper ingots nobody ever claimed. Insurance wouldn't touch it — unlicensed cargo. So the debt came to me. All of it. Plus interest, plus Renk's condolences, which cost extra."

The rain filled the silence for a while.

"You've been paying it since?"

"I've been *chasing* it since. There's a difference. Paying implies you get somewhere." He shrugged, a gesture that contained an entire decade. "Renk doesn't want the money. Money ends things. Renk wants the miles. A man running from a debt will go anywhere and ask nothing, and that's exactly the kind of man you want finding lost survey gear off the Sable Keys."

I thought about my team. About Priya checking the magnetometer a final time, about Okafor laughing about the coffee, about all of them walking into a morning that had been arranged for them by men who smiled. Renk collected people the way the current collects driftwood — patiently, and always toward the same shore.

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it, and hated how small it sounded.

"Don't be sorry. Be worth the beans."

* * *

**Orsen**

On day three the rain eased to a steady gray whisper and I let myself believe, briefly, that we might simply sit in this channel until the world forgot us. That was the kind of thinking the channel encouraged. Mangroves are nature's conspiracy: everything in them is hiding from something.

Coral spent the afternoon with the drives, wrapped in a tarp, decrypting by lamplight like a smuggler in a bad novel. I welded the seam aft — badly, in the pauses between squalls — and when I came back dripping, she was staring at the screen with an expression I recognized from mirrors.

"What is it?"

"The final transmission." Her voice had gone flat, the way voices do when they're holding something down. "Not the coordinates. The one before it. My transmission."

"You sent one?"

She nodded slowly. "Night before the camp went quiet. We'd found the dredging scars — miles of them, fresh, where nothing was licensed. I panicked. I keyed a distress call on the government band. Full position, full detail." She closed the drive case with exaggerated care, the way you close a door on a room you can't look at. "Twelve hours later, our boats were gone, our radio was gone, and I was swimming."

"Coral—"

"If I'd stayed quiet, they'd have had no reason to move. They were watching us the whole time, waiting to see if we'd talk. I talked." She finally looked at me, and her eyes were dry, which was somehow worse than the alternative. "Nine days in a cave, and I did the math so many times it wore a groove. Every version ends the same way. My call is why they died."

I've heard a lot of confessions in harbor bars, most of them lies, the rest boring. This was neither. This was a woman handing me the worst thing she owned and watching to see what I'd charge her for it.

"Dario's run," I said. "The one that killed him. I talked him into it. Told him one more trip would clear us both. So either we're both guilty, or guilt's a bad navigator and we should throw it overboard."

"That's not the same."

"No. It's worse. But we're both still here, and the data's still real, and dead men don't get to decide what happens next. Living ones do." I handed her a tin. "Eat. Guilt keeps better on a full stomach."

She almost smiled. Almost counts, out here.

* * *

**Coral**

That night the rain came back hard enough to drown conversation, which was fine, because we'd said the unsayable things and needed the excuse. But storms end, even monsoons pause, and in the pause after midnight I found him on deck checking the anchor line, and instead of going below I stood beside him under the tarp and watched the water.

"You know what's stupid?" I said.

"Everything, generally."

"I feel safer here than I did at the camp. With my own team. With the government behind us." I shook my head. "Two days in a stranger's sinking boat, and it's the first time since the Sable Keys I've slept more than an hour."

"Sleeping's easy when you're exhausted."

"That's not what this is."

He didn't answer right away. Lightning walked along the horizon, far off, counting something.

"No," he agreed finally. "It isn't."

And that was the whole conversation, and it was enormous. Two people with debts and dead men on their backs, standing in the rain, admitting the one thing neither of us could afford. Love doesn't check your schedule. It arrives during the monsoon, in a leaking boat, with a syndicate's chase boat somewhere out in the dark, and it asks for room you don't have.

"We should be sensible about this," I said.

"We should."

Neither of us moved. The rain came down. After a while his hand found mine, grease-stained and cold, and held on like the anchor line.

"Sensible tomorrow," he said.

"Sensible tomorrow."

* * *

**Orsen**

Day five, the rain thinned to rags and the barometer started climbing, and I allowed myself the luxury of a plan: run north at first light, make the relay tower at Cape Verren before the season's last storm — the big one, the one the fishermen name — and put Coral's data into every ear on the coast.

I was checking fuel levels when Coral touched my arm and pointed south.

Through the thinning rain, at the mouth of the channel, a boat sat motionless on the gray water. White hull. Too clean for these seas. It hadn't come in — it had been waiting, patient as interest accruing, and now that the weather was lifting it simply sat there, the way Renk's men did everything.

On its stern, painted neat as a signature: *Meridian Salvage*.

"They followed the storm," Coral said quietly.

"No," I said, reaching for the ignition. "They followed me. They always have."

The white boat's engine turned over, unhurried, and began to come in.
