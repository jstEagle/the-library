## 8. Renk's Patience

The fuel depot at Halfway Reach was the kind of place that existed because the map had a gap in it and somebody got there first. A concrete apron gone soft with salt rot. Two rusted tanks wearing their warning labels like old tattoos, half scraped away by weather and indifference. A tin-roofed office where a man named Pell sold diesel, ice, and — if you asked nicely and paid extra — not asking questions.

We came in low on fuel and lower on options, which is how most decisions get made on this coast.

Coral stood at the bow as we slid into the berth, scanning the tree line the way she'd been doing for three days, like the mangroves might confess something if she stared hard enough. She'd slept maybe four hours since the channel. She ate standing up. She checked the drives in their waterproof bag the way other people check a pulse.

"Two minutes to fill," I said. "Then we're moving."

"You said that an hour ago."

"I say a lot of things. It's a character flaw."

She almost smiled. Almost. Lately her smiles were arriving like rare birds — glimpsed once, then gone for days, leaving you unsure whether you'd actually seen them or just wanted to badly enough that your eyes invented one.

Pell shuffled out with the hose, took my cash without counting it, and asked no questions, which cost me double. The Wren sat in the water like an old dog too loyal to mention its own pain. I'd patched her hull twice since the channel. The bilge pump was running on stubbornness and prayer, mostly mine.

That's when I heard the engines.

Not one engine. Three, throttled down to a purr, coming around the point through the rain-haze with the unhurried confidence of something that has never once needed to hurry. I knew the sound before I saw the boats. You spend twelve years around a syndicate's fleet, you learn its voice the way you learn your mother's footsteps on the stairs.

"Coral." My voice came out flatter than I intended. "Inside. Now."

She looked at the tree line, then at me, and didn't argue, which told me she'd already done the math. She went below with the drives strapped against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

The first boat was Renk's *Patience* — and yes, he named it that himself, and yes, he enjoyed explaining why. "Because everything worth having comes to a patient man," he'd told me once, over brandy I couldn't afford and he knew I couldn't afford, watching me drink it anyway. The other two were his usual muscle: gray hulls, gray men, mounted lights that weren't legal anywhere with laws.

They fanned out across the mouth of the depot channel with the ease of men closing a purse. No rush. No weapons showing. Just geometry.

Aldous Renk stepped onto the apron before his boat even finished tying off, which was a flex in itself — the man never touched a line, but today he wanted me to see him do it. He wore a rain jacket that probably cost more than the Wren's engine, unzipped, because rain was for other people. His smile arrived first, the way it always did, warm as a heat lamp over day-old food.

"Orsen Winterbourne," he said. "You look terrible."

"It's the monsoon. Very unforgiving on the complexion."

"And yet here you are. Alive. Dry-ish. In the company of government property." He glanced at the Wren's cabin window, where Coral's silhouette stood very still. "Miss Vane. How is our cartographer? Eating well? I did worry. We left rather abruptly, some of us."

"Some of you left. Her team didn't get the option."

Renk sighed, a rich, theatrical sound, the sigh of a man burdened by other people's misunderstandings. "Orsen. May I call you Orsen? I've called you Orsen for twelve years, so I suppose the answer is rhetorical." He walked the apron slowly, hands clasped behind his back, admiring the rusted tanks like a buyer at auction. "Do you know what I admire about you? You're honest about what you are. A man who owes money. Most people dress it up. Call it a partnership, an investment, a favor between friends. You never did. You just kept rowing. Twelve years of rowing. That's character, Orsen. Rare character."

"Pell," I called, without taking my eyes off Renk. "How much longer on the pump?"

"Slow today," Pell said, which meant *I heard nothing, I saw nothing, please don't involve me.*

"Twelve years," Renk repeated, softer now. He stopped near me, close enough that I could smell the brandy under the cologne. "Four hundred and thirty thousand, originally. Interest compounding like mold in a wet wall. Do you know what the number stands at today? I'll tell you, because I find it clarifying. One point one million. One point one million miles, if you prefer to think in miles. You always did."

The pump thudded. Rain ticked on the tin roof. Somewhere below, Coral was listening to every word, and I hated that, and I loved that, and both feelings fit inside the same breath somehow.

"What do you want, Aldous?"

"The drives. And the woman delivered to the survey office at Port Meridian, unharmed, where she'll be treated as a distressed survivor of a tragic accident. There was a storm, Orsen. Storms eat survey teams. Everyone knows this. It will be in the report." He spread his hands, a man offering simplicity itself. "And in exchange—"

"Don't."

"—full forgiveness."

The word landed in my chest like an anchor dropped from a great height.

"Not half. Not a restructuring. Not another decade of hauling salvage for men who sneer at your boat. *Full.* Every mile erased. The Wren titled clean in your name — she's collateral, did you know that? Of course you did. She becomes yours outright. And a bonus, because I'm feeling generous in this weather: fifty thousand, cash, enough to put a new hull under that leaking coffin and go wherever currents take men who owe nothing to anyone." He smiled again, gentler now, almost kind. "One signature. One bag of drives. One woman who was going to drown in this story anyway, with or without your help."

And here is the thing I'm not proud of, the thing I've turned over a thousand times since, examining it like a stone found in a catch net:

For one long minute, I wanted it.

I want you to understand the minute properly. It wasn't a flash. It wasn't weakness striking sudden. It was slow, and reasonable, and it made excellent arguments. It said: *you have been rowing since you were twenty-six years old.* It said: *your knees ache, your boat leaks, your hands are ruined, and the debt grows faster than you can bail it, faster than any storm, faster than anything except death itself.* It said: *she is one person. One. And the sea is full of people, and most of them are already lost, and you cannot save the ocean, Orsen, you can barely save yourself.*

It said: *twelve years. Twelve years, and all you have to do is hand over one bag.*

I stood there on that rotten apron and I let the minute run. I let it have its whole say. I looked at the gray boats and imagined them gone. I looked south and imagined open water with nothing chasing me across it. I felt the specific, shameful sweetness of a man imagining his own chains unlocked — and I'll tell you something true about chains: after enough years, the weight starts to feel like structure. Like a spine. Take it away and you wonder what holds you up.

The minute lasted sixty seconds. It felt like six years, which is to say it felt fair.

What broke it wasn't heroism. I want to be clear about that, because if this story ever gets told, someone will make it heroic, and it wasn't. What broke it was smaller than that. The cabin door opened a few inches — just a few — and Coral looked out at me. She didn't speak. She didn't plead. She just looked, with those surveyor's eyes that measured everything and surrendered nothing, and I understood that whatever I chose, she would watch me choose it, and she would carry the sight of it the way she carried her dead team's coordinates: precisely, permanently, without flinching.

I could live with owing Renk a million. I had proof of concept.

I could not live being seen choosing this.

"Well?" Renk said, and his patience finally showed a hairline crack, the way a hull shows one right before it shows many. "You're a businessman, Orsen. Be one."

"Yeah," I said. "Here's my business."

I picked up the fuel nozzle Pell had left on the drum, walked to the nearest tank ladder, and hung the nozzle on the top rung where it dripped diesel slowly, theatrically, onto the apron. Then I turned back to Renk.

"That's not an answer," Renk said quietly.

"No. This is." I raised my voice toward the boats, toward the gray men with their gray rifles. "The drives are copied. Three copies. One's already on a relay schedule — if we don't check in, it broadcasts. Wideband. Coast guard, fisheries, every news desk from here to the capital. Kill us and you kill nothing. You just pick the day the data goes public."

It was maybe forty percent true. The copy existed; the schedule was a promise Coral had made to a machine, and machines keep promises only as well as the women who program them. But Renk's face did the arithmetic anyway, and I watched him arrive at the same sum I had: a dead cartographer proves nothing, but a *silent* cartographer might prove everything.

"You're bluffing," he said.

"Probably. Want to bet a million dollars on it?"

For a moment — a real one, this time, and much less pleasant than mine — nobody moved. Rain fell. Diesel dripped. Pell studied the interior of his office with the focus of a monk.

Then Renk laughed. Softly. Genuinely, even, which was worse.

"There he is," he said. "There's the man I loaned money to. I always said you were worth the investment, Orsen. I'm sorry it's come to this particular dividend." He stepped back toward his boat, and his men began, unhurriedly, to reposition — not leaving, circling, the way sharks circle when they've decided the meal is worth waiting out. "The season's last storm makes landfall tomorrow night. Big one. After that, the coast goes quiet for months, and quiet things can be handled. Enjoy your leaky boat. Enjoy your cartographer. Enjoy the last night of a debt you could have walked away from."

He paused at the gunwale, one foot on his deck, and gave me the smile one last time, free of charge.

"For what it's worth — I hope she's worth it. I truly do. I've never owned anything worth it myself."

The *Patience* pulled away into the rain, and the gray boats went with her, and the channel emptied like a held breath released.

Coral came up on deck. She didn't ask what I'd almost done. She'd heard every word; she had a cartographer's memory and no gift for pretending otherwise. Instead she looked at the fuel gauge, then at the sky stacking itself black and enormous over the western keys, and said the thing I was already dreading:

"We can't outrun them in open water. And we can't outrun that storm either."

"No," I agreed. "But we might outrun them *in* it."

She turned and looked at me for a long moment, rain running off her chin, and I watched her decide — the way I'd decided on that apron — that some insane things are also the only things.

"Tell me about this relay tower," she said.

* * *

There is a tower on Gullwatch Point, thirty miles north, a coastal relay built back when the government still pretended to maintain things out here. It broadcasts wide. It sits high. And the only approach in a storm is a channel no sane pilot runs — which is exactly the kind of sentence that used to end conversations between Coral and me, and lately has been ending them differently.

I spent the evening patching the Wren's third leak and thinking about Renk's parting arithmetic. Tomorrow night the last storm of the season makes landfall, and everything either ends or begins inside it.

Coral spread her charts on the chart table, weighted the corners with coffee mugs, and drew our course straight through the worst weather this coast has produced in a decade.

"Hold on to something," she said, "for the rest of your life."
