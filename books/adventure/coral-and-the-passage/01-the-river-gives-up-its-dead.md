## 1. The River Gives Up Its Dead

The river had been misbehaving for three days, which is the kind of thing you learn to notice when your entire social calendar consists of arguing with it.

I'd built my cabin — *squatted* is the word the county would use, and has used, twice — on a shelf of gravel forty feet above the old Corren spur, high enough to stay dry in a normal spring, low enough that I could watch the water the way a doctor watches a patient with a history. And the Corren had a history. Every river does, but this one had been fed by a glacier with a temper, and every May it came down out of the mountains brown and muscular and looking for something to break.

This year it wanted my dock.

I'd built the dock myself, six years back, out of pilings salvaged from a washed-out trestle eleven miles down the line. It was the closest thing I had to a hobby, which tells you most of what you need to know about my life. By noon on the third day it was leaning at an angle that suggested either surrender or a very ambitious diving board, and by two o'clock it was gone, and I stood on the bank with my arms crossed like a man at a funeral who'd never much liked the deceased.

"Fine," I told the river. "Take it. See if I care."

The river did not answer. Rivers never do. It's one of the few relationships I've managed to sustain.

I should probably introduce myself properly, since introductions are something I haven't done in years and have gotten out of practice at. My name is Astrid Ashgrove. If that means anything to you, you're either a railway historian, a lawyer, or a very bored archivist, and in any case I'd ask you to keep it to yourself. For the last six years I have been nobody, which is a profession I took up after leaving my previous one — engineer, third class, Corren & Vellmark Northern Railway — under circumstances we'll get to, or won't, depending on how much I can stand to tell you.

I live on a closed spur. The line was abandoned the same year I was, more or less — the company pulled the rail in '09, sold the ties for fence posts, and left the grade to the alders. There are eleven structures along it, counting mine: nine collapsed, one occupied by a family of martens with strong opinions about my flour stores, and one that is mine, built from the bones of a brake-house with a stove that smokes when it rains and a roof that leaks when it doesn't. I keep goats. I keep a garden that the goats keep. I keep to myself, which is the only one of the three that's ever gone according to plan.

The trick to invisibility, I'd learned, is not hiding. Anyone can hide. The trick is being so boring that hiding becomes unnecessary. I mend nets for the fishing families downriver at fair prices and never ask questions. I fix the millrace at Brassenford every other autumn and take payment in flour and silence. I am, as far as the valley knows, a half-mad tinker of no fixed gender and no fixed past, useful the way a good mule is useful and about as interesting.

I had been nobody for six years, and I was very good at it.

Which is why, when the river gave up its dead, I was the only one there to receive them.

* * *

It happened on the fourth morning, after the water dropped a foot overnight and began the long business of pretending it had never been anywhere else. I went down to the bank with a rake to see what the flood had left me — driftwood, usually, and once an entire chicken coop, intact, which I still maintain was the best day of my life — and there it was.

A chest.

Not floating. Lodged. It had come down in the freshet and jammed itself in the root wad of a fallen spruce at the water's edge, wedged so firmly that it had clearly been there since the crest, and the river had spent three days trying to take it back and failed. It was maybe two feet long, iron-banded, and — this was the part that stopped me with the rake half-raised — it had been painted, once. Under the mud and the scum of the flood, in patches where the water had scoured it clean, there was coral. A coral-pink paint, faded to the color of a sunset remembered by someone who wasn't paying attention.

Nobody paints a chest coral. Nobody paints anything coral. It's not a color that survives contact with the world. Which meant someone had wanted this box to be *found*, or wanted it to be beautiful, or both, and either possibility sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

I looked upriver, the way you do, as if the answer were going to come strolling down the bank. Nothing but water, alder, and the gray shoulders of the Correns, where the snowline was already creeping downhill a month ahead of schedule. I looked downriver. Nothing. The nearest neighbor was old Sefa at the fish camp, four miles off, and Sefa had the eyesight of a well-meaning bat.

"Leave it," I said to myself, in the tone of a person who has said this to themselves before and never once meant it.

Here's the thing about me that six years of nobody-hood never managed to sand off: I am an engineer, and an engineer looks at a thing wedged in a root wad and does not see a mystery. An engineer sees a problem with a solution. The chest weighed — I'd guess now, having lifted it — about eighty pounds, most of that iron. The root wad gave me a natural davit. The rake gave me a lever. Ten minutes of undignified grunting later, I had it up the bank and into the alder shade, and I was sitting beside it, breathing hard, telling myself that I would open it tomorrow. Or never. That I'd drag it up to the cabin and let it sit there as a conversation piece for the martens.

I opened it that afternoon. Of course I opened it that afternoon. I have never once in my life left a box unopened, and I am too old to start developing character now.

The lock was a simple brass tumbler, flooded but not seized — the chest had been underwater, but not long, and it had been built well. Whoever made it built it like I build things: on the assumption that someone, someday, would try very hard to get in, and should fail. It took me twenty minutes with a pick I use for cleaning goat hooves, and when the lid came up, the smell that rose out of it was wax and oilcloth and something older, the smell of a room that had been closed on purpose.

Inside: oilcloth bundles, tied with waxed cord, packed the way survey crews pack — I knew that packing the way you know your own handwriting. Under them, a layer of wool felt, and on the felt, nested like eggs:

A brass theodolite, the lens cracked but the gimbal still turning smooth as butter.

A sextant, smaller, the kind issued to route scouts.

And a photograph, in a tin frame, of a girl of maybe seven years, gap-toothed and scowling, standing on a railway platform in a coat two sizes too big for her, holding someone's hand — only the hand and the coat sleeve are in the frame, and on the sleeve is a brass pin the size of a thumbnail.

I knew the pin. I knew it the way you know a scar.

A circle, and inside the circle, a mountain with a star above its peak, and around the rim, letters I hadn't read in twenty years and had never once managed to forget: *Vellmark. Vellmark. Vellmark.* Around and around, the way the name used to go around and around in my head in the years after they took it off every map and every timetable and every uniform in the Corren & Vellmark Northern, and told us all, very politely, that it had never been there at all.

The Vellmark crest. The survey corps crest. The crest that used to sit on the left breast pocket of a coat belonging to the man who taught me everything I know about how a mountain holds itself up, and how it doesn't.

"Corvus," I said, out loud, to an empty riverbank, and the name came out of me sounding like something I'd coughed up.

* * *

I didn't touch anything else that day. That's the truth, whatever I might like to claim about my nerve. I closed the lid, sat back on my heels in the alder shade, and watched my own hands shake for a while, and then I went up to the cabin and drank half a cup of willow-bark tea that I'd let steep too long, because I wasn't paying attention, because I never wasn't paying attention and now I was.

Here is what I knew, and what I knew I knew, in the way of a man doing inventory of his own dread:

Corvus Halden had been my mentor. That word is too warm for what it was — he was my examiner, my tormentor, and the only person in the company who ever looked at my survey work and said *yes, exactly* — but mentor is the word, and it will do. He wore the Vellmark pin on his coat the whole time I knew him, openly, in the years after the erasure, which was either the bravest or stupidest thing about a man who contained a great deal of both. When I asked him about it — once, early, and never again after the look he gave me — he said: *A country isn't a line on a map, Ashgrove. A country is a set of debts. You can erase the line. The debts keep.*

I was twenty-three and thought he was being poetic. Corvus Halden was never poetic in his life. He was being precise.

He disappeared eleven years ago. The company said desertion. The company said a great many things that year, and I was in no position — I was never in any position — to argue. I said nothing, which is the thing I have been saying ever since, in a cabin on a dead spur, with goats.

And now his crest had come down the river in a coral-painted box, and the box had been packed by survey hands, and the survey hands had been his, and the question that rose in me like groundwater was the one I'd spent six years not asking:

*Where did you go, Corvus? And what did you find that someone needed to paint coral and throw in a river?*

Because that was the other thing. The coral paint. I turned it over all evening, the way you turn a stone over to see what's dying underneath. You paint a thing coral so it can be found. You paint a thing coral and then it ends up in a flood, jammed in a root wad four miles from the nearest living soul — either the finding failed, or the finding was never meant to be by whoever painted it. Either Corvus had sent it and it had gone astray, or someone had taken it from him and gotten rid of it, badly.

Both possibilities had teeth. I lay awake under my leaking roof cataloguing them, the way I used to catalog load tolerances, and around midnight I got up, lit the lamp, and went back down through the alders with the lantern swinging, because there is a limit to what a man can know about himself at midnight and I had reached it.

I opened the chest again, properly this time, and untied the first oilcloth bundle.

Maps. Survey plates, heavy paper, rolled tight, and I knew the format before I'd unrolled a hand's width of it — the old company standard, the one I'd been trained on, title block in the lower right, contour intervals in the margin. I unrolled the first one by lantern light with my hands steadier than I had any right to expect.

It was Halden Pass.

Of course it was Halden Pass. The pass had his name before he ever did — the tunnel through it was the Halden bore, named for some company founder or other, and Corvus used to say it was the only immortality the company ever handed out for free. The plate was dated — I checked twice — nineteen years old. Two years before the collapse. Two years before the night the mountain came down on the working face and killed two hundred men, and the company said *unstable strata, unforeseeable, acts of God*, and held a memorial where they read out a list of numbers instead of names, because most of the dead had been Vellmarki, and Vellmark, by then, officially did not exist, and you cannot read out the names of people who do not exist. I remember standing at the back of that memorial, twenty-two years old, in my brand-new third-class engineer's coat, thinking: *that's not right.* And then thinking: *say nothing.* I have been thinking the second thought ever since, in rotation, like a wheel that never finds a brake shoe.

I unrolled the second plate. Then the third. The lantern burned low. The plates were working surveys — bore alignment, strata logs, water tables — and they were annotated in a hand I knew, a small fierce hand that slanted uphill like it was trying to escape the page, and the annotations got stranger as they went. *Void at chain 440 — natural? Re-check.* And then, in the margin of the strata log, pressed so hard the pen had nearly gone through the paper: *Not water. Cut. This face was cut.*

I sat back in the mud.

I am not a brave man. I want that on the record, in ink, notarized. I am a careful man, a coward with good handwriting, and every instinct I had cultivated for six years — every instinct that had kept me alive and invisible and boring — stood up inside me at once and said, in one voice: *put it back. Tie the oilcloth. Close the lid. Take it up to the cabin and bury it under the floor and go back to the goats.*

And I would have. I swear to you I would have, if it weren't for the photograph.

I took it out of its tin frame and held it up to the lantern. The gap-toothed girl, scowling on her platform, holding the sleeve with the pin. And on the back, in that same uphill-slanting hand, a line of writing. I'd like to tell you I read it calmly. I read it three times, and my hands were not steady at all.

*If I am wrong about this, burn it. If I am right, they will come for it, and for me, and for anyone who has ever touched it. I'm sorry. You deserved better than to inherit a debt. — C.H.*

The river talked to itself in the dark below the bank. Somewhere up in the Correns, the snowline crept down another few feet, patient as arithmetic. And I sat in the mud with a dead man's apology in my hands and understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only when it is least welcome, that my six years of nobody were over — that they had ended, in fact, the moment the river decided to give up its dead, and that everything I did next would be done as somebody. As Ashgrove. As the engineer. As the person in the valley who had, at long last, touched the thing that people come for.

I put the photograph back in its frame. I retied the bundles, badly, because my hands were shaking, and closed the lid on the coral paint, and sat there a while longer listening to the water.

Then, up the bank, through the alders, from the direction of the old fish-camp trail — four miles from the nearest living soul, four miles, I checked, I always check — I heard a sound I had not heard on this spur in six years.

A footstep. Deliberate. Unhurried.

Coming toward the light of my lantern.
