## 3. The Debt Collector

The chest went into the culvert at dawn, because I have always believed that if a thing is going to ruin your life, it should at least be hidden competently.

I'd spent the night thinking about it the way an engineer thinks about anything: load-bearing capacity, drainage, access. The old culvert under the closed spur line was three feet of corrugated steel half-swallowed by the hillside, dry in spring except for a trickle that ran cold and clean off the granite. It had a rusted grate at the downstream mouth that I could bend back with a pry bar and bend shut again so the bend wouldn't show. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and wedged behind a stone I rolled into place, the chest would sit below the sightline of anyone walking the track bed. Anyone not crawling.

I crawled. I'm not proud of how fast I crawled.

By the time the light came up gray over the Correns, the chest was underground and the grate was bent shut and I was standing in the shallows of the river washing river mud off my hands like Lady Macbeth with a surveyor's squint, telling myself the whole thing was nothing. A box. Some old papers. A seal on a country that didn't exist anymore.

My hands were still shaking, which made the lie harder to sell to myself than usual.

* * *

The settlement — I use the word generously; it was nine buildings and a ferry landing pretending otherwise — woke up around me the way it always did. Smoke from the Kessler stovepipe. The Halls' dog announcing the sunrise as though it were an invasion. Old Marta sweeping her porch with the same broom she'd been sweeping with since before the lines closed, out of what I can only assume was spite toward leaves.

I did my rounds. That's the ritual. Feed myself, check the fish traps, walk the spur line as far as the washout and back, touch the milepost at the boundary like a man touching wood. Twenty years of living invisibly teaches you rituals. Invisibility isn't a state, it's maintenance. You do it daily or it fails.

I was halfway through the maintenance when the boat came upriver.

It wasn't the supply boat. The supply boat came Thursdays, poled by a boy named Anselm who talked enough for four people and charged accordingly. This was a company launch — I knew it before I could read the name on the hull, because there is a way a company boat moves on water. It doesn't travel. It *inspects*. It comes upstream like a bill.

CORREN CONSOLIDATED RAIL & MINERAL, said the hull, in letters that had probably cost more than my cabin.

I stood very still by the fish traps and watched it come, and I felt something I hadn't felt in years settle over my shoulders like a coat cut exactly to my measurements.

Fear. The old fear. It fits better than any coat I own.

* * *

He introduced himself as Ivo Renner, which meant nothing to me, and as "an adjustor for Corren Consolidated," which meant everything.

An adjustor. There's a word the company uses the way a surgeon uses a knife — precisely, and never in front of the patient. Adjustors don't adjust anything. They arrive after something has gone wrong and they make the wrongness *tidy*. I'd seen two of them in my working life, before. Both times, afterward, there was a settlement, a signature, and a family that stopped asking questions. I used to think the word was a euphemism. Now I think it's a confession nobody reads.

Renner came ashore at the landing wearing a gray coat too good for the mud and boots too sensible for the coat, which told me he'd dressed for the terrain but couldn't quite bring himself to look like he belonged in it. He was perhaps fifty, lean in the way of men who burn everything they eat and most of what they feel, with a face like a ledger page — ruled, ruled again, no entry out of place. He carried a leather document case and nothing else. No rifle. Men who carry rifles are worried about animals. Men who carry document cases are worried about you.

"Morning," he said to the landing generally, and then his eyes found me specifically, the way a plumb line finds true. "You'd be local."

"I'd be something," I said.

"Astrid Ashgrove."

It wasn't a question, and that was the first thing wrong with the morning. Not *are you*, not *might you be*. Just the name, laid down flat between us like a rail section waiting to be spiked.

"Don't know them," I said.

"Mm." He opened the document case without hurry. He took out a paper. He did not offer it to me. He held it angled so I could see it was official and nothing else, which is its own kind of cruelty, or courtesy, depending on how much you enjoy being toyed with. "Corren Consolidated is conducting a review of legacy assets along the closed northern spurs. Flood season displaces things. Company property, mostly. Records, instruments, occasionally remains." He said *remains* the way another man might say *receipts*. "We're asking after anything unusual the river may have given up."

"The river gives up dead fish and other people's fences," I said. "Mostly fences."

"And people?"

"People keep themselves."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. A notation. "There was an engineer," he said. "Ashgrove. Worked these mountains twenty-odd years ago. Brilliant, by all accounts. Then the Halden Pass disaster, and the trial in absentia, and—" he made a small gesture, a closing of accounts, "—nothing. Vanished. The company would very much like to close that file. Files left open have a way of attracting attention. Attention is expensive."

"You've come a long way to chase paperwork."

"I've come a long way because paperwork is where bodies go to hide." He looked past me then, up the slope at the settlement, cataloguing it. Nine buildings. One dog. Marta's broom frozen mid-sweep. "You live here?"

"I pass through."

"Where do you pass *to*?"

"That's more of a second question than a morning usually earns."

He closed the case. Clicked it shut with the finality of a switch thrown. "I'll be at the landing house for two days," he said. "Reviewing. If the river gives anything up — a chest, a crate, papers — the company pays fair salvage. Generously, even." He let that sit. "And if it turns out someone found something already and failed to mention it, well." He adjusted his collar against the river damp. "That becomes a different kind of conversation. Less generous. More conversational."

He walked up toward the landing house. His boots found the dry line across the mud without looking for it, which told me he'd read the ground the way I read drawings, and I hated him a little for being good at it.

* * *

Marta caught me at the edge of her yard, broom planted like a halberd.

"That one asked after you," she said. "By name."

"He asked after a name. Names are common."

"Not that one. Not said like that." She looked at me with the flat blue appraisal of a woman who had buried a husband in a mine collapse and a son in a war and had run out of patience for men who lied badly. "Astrid. What did you pull out of the flood?"

"Driftwood."

"You've got driftwood hands?" She took one of my hands without asking and turned it over. Rope burns from the dredge line, still angry red. "Heavy driftwood."

"Marta—"

"I don't want to know," she said, releasing me. "Understand? I'm too old to know things. Knowing things gets your bridge burned." She said it lightly, the way you say terrible things when you mean them completely. "But whatever it is, it came out of the water, and water remembers. So does that man's kind. Be smart."

She went back to sweeping. I stood there a moment longer than was safe, then went to be smart.

* * *

Being smart, in my experience, looks a great deal like doing manual labor while panicking internally.

I spent the afternoon being loudly, visibly ordinary. I mended the fish trap in full view of the landing. I hauled water. I argued with Anselm about Thursday's rates at a volume calculated to establish me as a person whose largest concern was the price of flour. Anselm, bless him, rose to the occasion magnificently and accused me of trying to bankrupt him through sheer stubbornness, which, in fairness, was accurate.

All the while, part of my mind was down in the culvert with the chest, running calculations I didn't want to run.

Because here was the thing I kept circling and refusing to land on: Renner hadn't come for the chest. He couldn't know about the chest. Nobody could know about the chest — I'd pulled it out of the river alone at night, and the river tells no tales, whatever Marta says about water remembering. Renner had come for *Ashgrove*. For a file twenty years open. Which meant either the company periodically swept the closed lines for ghosts, which was possible, or—

Or something had surfaced. Something recent. Something that pointed here, now, this season.

I thought about the chest. About the brass instruments stamped with the Vellmark crest. About the photograph of the child I hadn't been able to make myself look at twice. About the survey plates in their waterproof sleeves, and the numbers on them, and what those numbers proved about the night the mountain came down on two hundred men, and about who had carried those plates for twenty years, and who had been supposed to destroy them, and who had instead gone into hiding like a coward with a compass.

Soren. My mentor. Missing twenty years, presumed dead, presumed worse. If Soren was dead — truly dead, recently dead — then the chest in the river wasn't lost cargo. It was a message. It was the last act of a dying man sending his sins home to the one person who'd understand them.

And if Soren had sent it, Soren might have sent word besides. Letters. A name spoken to the wrong person. A death registered somewhere that required paperwork, and paperwork, as Renner himself had so helpfully explained, is where bodies go to hide — until somebody goes looking.

Invisibility is maintenance. And somewhere, somehow, I had missed a day.

* * *

Dusk came down purple off the glacier fields. I waited until the landing house lamp lit — one lamp, steady, a man who worked evenings — and then I went out along the spur line in the last gray light, moving the way I'd learned to move in twenty years of practice: unhurried, purposeful, a local checking traps, nothing in my hands, everything in my gait.

At the washout I dropped off the track bed into the willows and doubled back low, and reached the culvert from downstream, from the water, because if anyone had marked the approach they'd have marked it from the trail.

The grate was as I'd bent it. The stone was as I'd rolled it. The oilcloth bundle sat in its hollow, dry, untouched, exactly as I'd left it.

I put my hand on it anyway. Just to feel it solid. Just to confirm the world still contained what I knew it contained.

That was when I saw the boot prints.

Not at the culvert — at the top of the bank, on the soft ground above the track ballast, where the willow roots made a shelf of bare earth. Two prints, side by side, facing the river. Someone had stood there. Stood, not walked — the prints were deep and even, weight settled, a person at rest, looking down at the water. Looking at the stretch of river directly across from the culvert's downstream mouth.

They weren't Renner's. I'd watched those boots all afternoon; he wore city leather, narrow-heeled, and these prints were broad, patched, the tread of someone who lived on boats and rocks. Small, too. A small frame carrying itself carefully.

And they were fresh. The edges hadn't crumbled. Hours old at most — hours during which I had been performing ordinariness at the fish traps while someone stood above my hiding place and looked at the water and thought.

I crouched there in the dark with my hand on the chest and my heart going like a loose drive rod, and I did the arithmetic every fugitive eventually does. One stranger who knew my face was a problem. One stranger who knew where I hid things was a catastrophe. One stranger who stood quietly above your secrets without digging for them was worse than either, because it meant they weren't searching.

They were deciding.

I re-bent the grate, waded the shallows, and climbed the bank well downstream of the prints. At the top of the rise I paused, and looked back once at the dark seam of the river, and the dark seam of the river looked back at me with nothing at all, which is the most unsettling thing a river can do.

Two days, Renner had said. He'd be reviewing for two days.

I had the sudden, certain feeling that I was being reviewed by considerably more than one party — and that the river, having given up its dead, had just started giving up everything else.
