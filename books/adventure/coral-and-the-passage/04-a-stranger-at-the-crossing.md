## 4. A Stranger at the Crossing

I have been ferrying people across the Corren for eleven years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things about the living: they lie about their weight, they lie about their destination, and they always, always look back at the water as if it owes them something.

The man who came to my crossing on the morning the river gave up its chest did all three.

He was tall in the way of people who have decided to be smaller than they are — shoulders drawn in, eyes down, a coat that had once been good and had since been punished. He paid with company scrip from a line that hadn't run in six years, which told me he'd been hoarding it or stealing it, and I took it anyway because I am not a charity. He said he weighed less than he did. He said he was headed nowhere in particular. And when the current took us out over the deep channel where the flood had turned the water the color of strong tea, he looked back upstream — not at the crossing we'd left, but further, toward the bend where something large and dark had lately come ashore.

I know what looking-back means. I grew up in a country that no longer exists, among people who spent every day looking back at a border that had been redrawn without asking them. Looking back is how you find your dead.

So when he looked back, I looked too. And I saw, on his cuff, where the mud had dried and cracked away like old paint, a small stamped crest: a mountain split by a river, crowned with three stars.

Vellmark.

My hands went still on the oars. The boat drifted. He didn't notice, because he was still looking backward, and because men like him never imagine that the ferry-woman might be watching them instead of the water.

Let me tell you about that crest, since apparently no one else will.

There are children in the valley settlements who wear it now as a joke, scratched into school desks, because Vellmark has become one of those words adults go quiet around — a country that was on maps for four hundred years and then, twenty years ago, wasn't. Not conquered. Not destroyed. *Erased.* The treaties were signed, the borders absorbed, the name struck from the atlases so smoothly that within a decade you could buy a map of the Corren Range printed by respectable firms and find nothing where a nation used to be, just a polite blank labeled TERRITORY ADMINISTERED BY THE CONSORTIUM. As if the mountains themselves had agreed to forget.

My grandmother kept her atlas hidden inside a false bottom of her flour bin, and once a year she would take it out and make me point to Vellmark with my finger, and if I pointed wrong she'd rap my knuckles, and if I pointed right she'd cry, which was worse. That is what erasure does. It turns geography into grief work.

The man in my boat wore the crest of that grief on his sleeve, mud-stained, and he didn't even seem to know it was there.

* * *

His name — or the name he gave, which I have learned to treat as a rough draft — was Ashgrove. Astrid Ashgrove. I filed it away the way I file everything: under suspicion, cross-referenced with usefulness.

Here is what I knew already, before he ever stepped into my boat, because I make it my business to know things, and my business has been very good lately:

Three days earlier, the spring flood had torn open an old rail embankment two miles above my crossing and delivered, onto a gravel bar behind a squatter's cabin, a sealed ironwood chest bearing the Vellmarki survey office stamp. I knew this because a boy who traps muskrat along that stretch had come to me asking if I'd ferry him down to sell "a funny heavy box" to the scrap dealer in Brassenford, and when I asked what made it funny, he said it had stars on it, and when I asked what kind of stars, he drew them in the dirt with a stick, and I sent him home and sat very still for a long time.

Because I have hunted that chest my whole life. Not that chest specifically — I didn't know it existed until the boy drew stars in the dirt — but *that chest*, the idea of it, the certainty of it. Every exile family has its own ghost story, the thing that explains why we have no country, and ours had always had a hole in the middle of it. My father died insisting there were papers. Survey documents, engineering records, proof of what happened at Halden Pass the winter they closed the tunnel with two hundred workers inside and told the world the mountain had done it. He insisted until the drink finished what the Consortium started, and I inherited his insistence along with his ferry license and his bad knee.

Two hundred workers. Say it enough times and it becomes a number, and numbers are how the Consortium wins — they are very good at arithmetic, at ledgers, at the cold little algebra of acceptable losses. But my grandmother could say the names. All afternoon. She'd learned them from a survivor who made it out through the ventilation adit, a man named Tomas who came through our village in the spring of the collapse with frostbitten hands and a list written on the backs of pay chits, and she copied them into the flyleaf of her hidden atlas, and when she died the atlas came to me, and the names came with it.

So yes. When a mud-stained stranger wearing a Vellmarki survey crest rowed away from my crossing toward the bend where a boy had seen a starred box, I did what any reasonable woman would do.

I followed him home.

* * *

Following someone along a river you've worked for eleven years is not stalking; it is hydrology. There are only so many paths the land allows. The north bank trail runs through alder scrub to the old culvert at mile marker nine, then climbs to the derelict spur line, and anyone heading inland from my crossing must take it, because the south bank is a cliff and the river doesn't negotiate. I put my boat up on its rack, told the trapline boy I needed him to check snares downstream — a lie, but a small one, and I have made peace with the exchange rate of lies to truths in this life — and walked the north bank with a creel on my arm like a woman gathering ferns.

He moved like a man who had once known how to move and had let the knowledge rust. He checked his backtrail twice, both times wrong — looking behind instead of ahead-and-above, which is where anyone competent watches from. He stopped at the culvert at mile marker nine and stood over it for a long moment, too long, the way a person stands over a thing they've hidden rather than a thing they're passing. Then he climbed toward the spur, and I marked the culvert in my mind with a little flag, the way surveyors do, and thought: *there.*

You see, this is the part people get wrong about me. They assume, because I'm a ferry-woman, because I'm a woman at all, because I'm Vellmarki and therefore supposed to be either pitiable or picturesque, that I act from passion. Grief, rage, the long slow burn of the dispossessed. And I won't pretend there isn't fuel in me — there is, there's a refinery's worth — but fuel is not method. My father had fuel. It burned him down to a stain on a mattress in a room over a tavern. What I have is colder and better: patience, arithmetic of my own, and eleven years of watching every soul in this valley pass over my water and tell me, without meaning to, exactly who they are.

Astrid Ashgrove. I turned the name over while I walked. It snagged on something. Ashgrove — there had been an engineer by that name, years ago, before my time on the river, a prodigy out of the Brassenford works who'd designed half the drainage systems on the western lines and then vanished from the record after some scandal at Halden. Disgraced, the story went. Blamed for a tunnel failure. The timing interested me enormously. The company does not disgrace engineers for failures; companies forgive failures the way kings forgive taxes, routinely and with contempt. Companies disgrace engineers for *truths*. I made a note to find out which kind Ashgrove was, and I felt the first genuine warmth of the day, which for me is roughly equivalent to another person's joy.

The cabin, when I finally saw it, sat above the old spur in a stand of birch, one wall log-rotted, smoke rising thin and disciplined from a stovepipe patched with tin. A squatter's cabin on a closed line, occupied by a disgraced engineer. Invisibility as a career. I recognized the architecture of it because I had built the same thing myself, one river over: a life designed to cast no shadow, maintained with the diligence other people spend on their reputations.

And now there was a chest in it. A chest with stars on it, hauled out of the flood by hands that presumably knew what the stars meant, hidden — I'd have bet the boat — somewhere near that culvert at mile marker nine.

I crouched in the alders and let myself feel it, briefly, honestly, the way you let yourself feel a burn so you can respect it. Twenty years. Twenty years of a country that wasn't, of a name I had to spell for clerks who'd look at me like I'd sneezed nonsense, of my grandmother's atlas and its two hundred names in the flyleaf going soft at the edges from handling. Somewhere in that rotten cabin was the reason. The papers. The proof. The thing that would turn our ghost story into a court case, our grief into evidence, our erased nation's single loudest wound into a document with a stamp on it that no Consortium lawyer could call sentiment.

And between me and it: one wary, mud-cuffed, lying-about-their-weight stranger who had pulled it from the river and not yet run to the authorities, which meant they understood, at least dimly, what it was worth and to whom. Which meant they could be reasoned with. Or outmaneuvered. Or, if it came to it —

No. I put that door shut in my mind, gently, the way you close a door on a cold draft. If it came to it, I'd decide then. I am capable of more than I advertise, but I prefer to keep my capabilities like my atlas: hidden, maintained, and opened only for serious occasions.

* * *

I watched the cabin until dark, which is less romantic than it sounds. Mostly I was cold. The alders held the river damp, my boots leaked in the honest way of old boots, and Ashgrove — I'd begun thinking of them as Ashgrove, gender being one more thing the dusk made irrelevant — moved around inside doing the small domestic liturgy of the invisible: splitting wood, boiling water, checking the window twice, banking the fire low. Careful habits. Habits of a person who expects to be hunted and has gotten tired of expecting it.

Around full dark, a lantern came bobbing up the north bank trail from the direction of my crossing, and I flattened into the mud so fast I got a mouthful of last year's leaves, and lay there breathing like a stone while it passed.

It passed toward the cabin. Three men. Company coats, though they wore them under plain oilskins, which fooled nobody who'd spent a decade reading silhouettes against water. The lead one carried the lantern high, the way men carry light when they want to be seen carrying it — a message, not a tool. They knocked. I heard voices, low and pleasant, the particular pleasantness of men who get paid whether or not they're pleasant. And after a long interval, the door opened, and Ashgrove stepped out to meet them, and I heard one word clearly across the birches:

"Ashgrove."

Not *friend*. Not *neighbor*. The name itself, spoken like a summons. Someone had talked, or someone had read a ledger, or someone had simply done the same arithmetic I'd done — flood, embankment, gravel bar, squatter's cabin — and arrived at the same sum. The company's enforcers were already here. Whatever window I'd imagined for careful negotiation was closing at the speed of lantern-light.

They talked for ten minutes. I caught fragments. Something about "routine inquiry." Something about "the old lines." Something about a name — Renner — delivered with the flat reverence employees give to the name of the man who signs their wages. Then the lanterns went back down the trail, unhurried, and the cabin stayed dark, and I lay in the alder mud and did the coldest, clearest thinking of my life.

Option one: wait. Let the company men come back with more men, take Ashgrove, take the chest, bury it deeper than the river ever had. Result: my grandmother's atlas keeps its soft-edged names forever, and Vellmark stays a word that makes clerks blink. Unacceptable.

Option two: go in tonight, take the chest by force, vanish upriver. Possible. I'm stronger than I look and meaner than I need to be. But Ashgrove knew these closed lines the way I knew my river — you could see it in how they'd placed the cabin, sightlines and escape routes, an engineer's paranoia expressed in firewood. Force would cost me, and worse, it would leave me holding a chest full of documents I couldn't fully read. Survey plates, my father had said. Engineering records. I needed the person who could read them, or at least the person who'd risked drowning to pull them out of the flood, which suggested they could read them well enough to matter.

Which left option three, the one I liked least and trusted most: knock on the door.

I gave it an hour, until the moon set and the company men were certainly gone, and then I walked up out of the alders, across the clearing, and rapped on the rotten planks with the iron ferrule of my creel pole — three knocks, even, unhurried, the knock of someone with business rather than malice.

Silence. Then, from inside, a voice pitched carefully neutral: "Crossing's closed till morning."

"I'm not a customer," I said. "I'm the ferry-woman. You crossed my water today and lied about your weight."

Another silence, longer. Then the sound of a bar lifting, and the door opened a hand's width, and one eye regarded me through the gap with the wariness of a creature deciding whether I was predator or weather.

"You followed me," Ashgrove said.

"Your backtrail-checking invited it." I held up my hands, empty except for the creel pole. "I know what came out of the river, Ashgrove. I know what the stars on it mean. And I know the company just found your door, which means whatever you're planning to do with that chest, your time to do it is shorter than you think."

The eye blinked. Behind it, I could hear the almost-silent arithmetic of a cornered mind recalculating — and underneath the fear, faint but unmistakable, something else. Recognition, maybe. Or hope, which is recognition's dangerous cousin.

"My name," the voice said finally, "isn't Ashgrove."

"No," I agreed pleasantly. "But it was, once, in the Brassenford works registry, and I'd wager the company remembers it better than you'd like. Now — are you going to let me in out of the wet, or shall we conduct the fate of a murdered nation on your doorstep?"

The door opened wider. I stepped through into the smell of woodsmoke and old paper, and I remember thinking, with the strange clarity that arrives at the hinge of a life: *this is the night everything turns.*

I was right. Though neither of us, standing there — the disgraced engineer with their hand still on the door, and the exile with river mud on her knees — could yet see which way it would break.

Somewhere below us, black and patient, the culvert at mile marker nine held its secret. And somewhere beyond the mountains, a man named Renner was already reaching for a ledger.
