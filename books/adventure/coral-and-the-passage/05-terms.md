## 5. Terms

She came back at dawn, which told me two things. First, that she hadn't been frightened off by my performance with the poker. Second, that she was the kind of person who thought dawn was a reasonable hour to conduct negotiations, which meant she'd spent time in mountains or armies, and I didn't like my odds against either.

I heard her before I saw her — not footsteps, because there weren't any, but the particular quality of silence a river makes when something is standing on its bank listening to it. I'd lived alone long enough that silence had stopped being empty and started being legible. Hers read: *patient, armed, and better at this than you.*

I put water on the stove anyway. Hospitality is a habit even hermits keep, like an old scar that itches in cold weather. When I opened the door she was sitting on my woodpile as if she owned the deed, hands loose on her knees, watching the mist come off the current.

"You followed me home," I said.

"You led me here," she said. "There's a difference. A careful man doesn't lead."

"I'm not a careful man. I'm a careful woman, and I led you through three false turns and a dead-end gully first."

"And doubled back along the gravel bar where your prints wash out." She almost smiled. It wasn't a friendly almost. "I grew up reading ground like this. You're good. I'm better. That's not an insult. It's arithmetic."

Her name, she said, was Mira Vell. She said it plainly, no apology, the way people say names that have been illegal in living memory. Vell. The second half of Vellmark, worn like a medal or a wound — I couldn't tell which, and I suspected she'd decided long ago that nobody would ever get to tell.

I let her in because the alternative was standing in the doorway all morning pretending I had somewhere better to be, and because — I'll be honest, since honesty is the one luxury this story keeps forcing on me — because she'd said *arithmetic* and I trusted people who reduced things to numbers. Numbers don't flatter. Numbers just are.

* * *

She drank the tea I gave her without comment on its quality, which was gracious of her, since the tea was mostly pine and regret. She sat across the table from me with the chest between us like a third party at a trial, and she looked at it the way I've seen starving men look through restaurant windows.

"You opened it," she said. Not a question.

"Water had already gotten to it. I just finished the job."

"The seal?"

"Broken. Soft. The wax held the crest, though." I turned my cup. "I knew that crest before I knew what it meant. My mentor wore it. Stamped inside his hatband, of all places. He thought it was funny — a Vellmarki surveyor working for the company that erased Vellmark, wearing the proof under his own hat."

Something moved behind her eyes at his name — I didn't offer it, and she didn't ask, and that restraint cost us both something, though I couldn't have priced it then.

"Here is my offer," she said, and folded her hands like a woman laying cards face-up. "You cannot cross the Corren Range alone. You know the lines, yes — the rails, the grades, the culverts. But above the last tie, the mountain doesn't care what you know about railroads. The glacier route opens maybe seven days a year, if the calving goes kindly. Go early, the ice bridge drops you into a crevasse. Go late, the snows close behind you and they find you in spring, looking surprised. I know the route. I've walked it twice. I know where the meltwater runs under the tongue, I know which seracs are rotten, I know the shelter cairns and which ones still hold a stove. I will take you over alive."

"And in exchange."

"The chest. The surveys. Everything in it."

I looked at her for a long moment. Steam rose off our cups and bent in the draft from the door seam, and outside the river kept up its one-sided conversation.

"That's it," I said. "Passage for papers."

"It's more than passage. It's twenty years of a dead nation's memory of those mountains. There isn't a map in any office that shows the high route correctly. The company burned them. My grandmother carried what she could in her head, and I carry what she carried. What I'm offering you doesn't exist anywhere else, Ashgrove. Same as what's in that chest."

It was a fair trade. That was the trouble. Any assessor worth their stamp would have signed it on the spot: one set of irreplaceable knowledge for another, both parties enriched, transaction closed. And if I'd been the person I'd been trying to be for six years — the invisible woman, the squatter, the engineer who had retired from mattering — I'd have signed too. Hand over the plates, cross the range with a guide who actually knew it, come down the far side lighter by forty pounds of brass and guilt, and go back to growing potatoes by a dead spur until the river took the cabin or I did.

But I'd opened the chest. That was the whole problem with opening chests.

"No," I said.

Mira didn't blink. I'll give her that; most people blink when a deal dies, some small tell of recalibration. She just watched me the way she'd watched the river, patient, armed, better at this.

"May I ask why? Not to argue. To understand what I'm negotiating with."

"You may not," I said, and then, because apparently six years of solitude hadn't cured me of talking, "All right. Here's why. Those plates aren't mine to trade. They're not yours either. There are two hundred people in them. Two hundred workers who went into the Halden tunnel on a Tuesday and never came out, and whose deaths were written up as the fault of a man who wasn't there when it happened — a man who spent whatever was left of his life carrying these documents around like a disease he refused to be cured of. That chest is the only testimony they have. The only witness the company missed. And witnesses don't get traded, Mira. They get heard, or they get killed, but they don't get traded."

"To whom?" she asked, softly, and it wasn't softness, it was a blade wrapped in cloth. "Heard by whom? There is no court that wants this. There is no country to petition — you're holding evidence from a nation that has been *unmade*, Ashgrove. Do you know what that means, practically? It means there is no legal person left to be wronged. The company didn't just bury a tunnel. It buried the plaintiffs. Every family scattered, every name struck from the rolls, every record of who owed what to whom dissolved into 'relocation' and 'administrative settlement.' You cannot testify on behalf of people who officially do not exist. Believe me." Her mouth twisted. "My people have tried. For twenty years we have tried. We wrote petitions. We got receipts."

"So the answer is to sit on the truth forever? Bury it deeper than they buried the tunnel?"

"The answer is that truth needs a *holder* before it needs a *hearing*. The exiles have spent two decades becoming that holder. There are communities — small, scattered, but real — that have kept the lists of the dead, kept the language, kept the names. When Vellmark stands again — and it will, or the trying will kill every one of us, which amounts to the same effort — those surveys will be the cornerstone. Proof of the crime, proof of the cover-up, leverage for restitution. Handled correctly, that chest rebuilds a country. Handled—" she glanced at me, and I felt the assessment land like a cold fingertip, "—handled by one disgraced engineer with a rifle and a grudge, it gets us both killed and the plates end up in Renner's safe anyway."

"You know about Renner."

"I know about Renner the way fish know about water. He's been asking after the Ashgrove engineer from here to the valley towns. He burned a bridge yesterday to make a point. He'll do worse to make a deadline." She leaned forward. "I'm not your enemy. I'm the best thing that's happened to you since that chest floated into your life, and possibly the best thing that's happened to you since before it. But I am not going to watch you die on a glacier holding papers hostage out of pride."

"It's not pride."

"Then what is it?"

And there it was, the question I'd been walking around since I pulled the chest out of the flood, the question I'd hoped a night's sleep would answer and instead it had just rearranged itself into more uncomfortable shapes. What *was* it? Because it wasn't grief exactly, though grief was in it, the old grief for Halvorsen with his ridiculous hatband crest and his terrible handwriting and his faith in me, faith I'd repaid by doing exactly nothing while the company ate him alive. And it wasn't guilt exactly, though guilt was in it too, the specific guilt of the competent person who could have done something and calculated the cost and chosen survival, and had been choosing survival ever since, so long and so consistently that survival had stopped being a virtue and become a kind of sloth.

I think — and I say this now with the benefit of everything that came after, which is cheating, but narrators cheat — I think it was the photograph. The child in the chest, gap-toothed, squinting into a sun that fell on a country that no longer existed. Somebody had loved that child enough to keep the picture in a work chest, in a tunnel, underground, two hundred meters under a mountain pass. And when the charges went off, that picture had been the last thing in the world that child's parent touched. Not the child. The picture. Because the child was somewhere else, somewhere safe, and the parent had gone down into the dark with a rectangle of paper and a brass instrument and the ordinary expectation of coming home.

The company had counted them as costs. Halvorsen had carried them as papers. And I had spent six years counting myself as nothing at all, which was just another way of keeping books for them.

"It's testimony," I said finally. "You called it that yourself. And testimony has a shape, Mira. It has an order. First the facts, then the names, then the judgment. You want to skip to the end — hold it sealed until there's a country to receive it. But countries don't get built on sealed rooms. They get built on hearings. On things said out loud where everyone can hear them and no one can unsay them." I put my hand flat on the chest lid. Cold brass under my palm, colder than the room. "I won't give it to you. But I'm not telling you to leave, either, so before you reach for whatever you're carrying under that coat, hear the counter-offer."

She hadn't reached for anything. But she'd shifted her weight, a quarter-inch, the way a cat does before deciding, and I noted it and filed it under *fair*.

"Go on," she said.

"Come with me. Keep your offer — the route, the knowledge, all of it. I need it and I'm not fool enough to pretend otherwise; I can read a grade sheet like scripture but I've never crossed a living glacier in my life. In exchange, you don't get the chest. You get a promise: we decide together at the summit. Both of us stand at the wire station above Halden Pass, and we decide what happens to the testimony — broadcast, sealed, carried down, burned if it comes to that — with both voices in the room. Nobody trades it behind anybody's back. Nobody buries it. The dead get a hearing, and the hearing has two witnesses instead of one."

"And if we disagree at the summit?"

"Then we disagree at the summit. Two hundred miles of mountain to argue it out first. That's more deliberation than most courts get."

She was quiet for a while. The stove ticked. Down at the crossing, someone's dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"You understand," she said at last, "that this is a worse deal for me than the one you refused."

"I know."

"You understand I could simply take it. Tonight, while you sleep. I could be over the low pass with it in four days and you'd never catch me."

"I know that too. I've been thinking about it since you sat down. It's the flaw in my position and I don't have an answer for it except this:" I met her eyes. "If you were going to rob me, you'd have done it last night, before I knew you were in the valley. You wanted me awake. You wanted terms. People who want terms want consent, Mira, even when they tell themselves they don't. Whatever you're planning to do with those surveys, part of you knows it counts for nothing taken by force. Otherwise why am I still breathing?"

A long pause. Then she laughed — one short syllable, surprised out of her, gone as fast as it came, but real.

"My grandmother had a saying," she said. "*The mountain doesn't care who's right.*"

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have made a fine engineer."

"She'd have hated you. Engineers built the line that erased us." She stood, and stood there a moment, looking down at the chest with an expression I recognized because I'd been wearing a version of it for six years: the look of someone measuring the distance between what they want and what they'll allow themselves. "Terms accepted. Provisionally. We cross together. We decide at the summit. And Ashgrove — if you slow me down past the calving window, if your stubbornness costs us the ice bridge, I will take the chest and leave you. I want that stated plainly, so that later neither of us can pretend it wasn't."

"Stated plainly," I agreed. "And if your shortcut drops me into a crevasse, I want it stated plainly that my ghost gets to haunt you specifically, and at length."

"Your ghost would bore me within a day. All railroads and grievances." She pulled her coat on. At the door she paused, back to me, mist moving behind her. "Pack for cold you don't believe yet. Three days. I need three days to settle things at the crossing, and you need three days to decide what you're leaving behind, because you're leaving all of it. This life ends when we walk up that spur."

Then she was gone, down through the alders, quiet as weather, and I stood in my cabin with the chest and the cold tea and the strange new weight in my chest that took me a while to identify because I hadn't felt it since before the inquiry, since before the disgrace, since before the river.

It was purpose. Small, stubborn, probably doomed — but it had a direction, and direction was more than I'd owned in years.

I started packing. And that was how, on the second morning, while I was hauling my father's compass down from the rafter beam, I heard the sound every squatter learns to dread above all others: hooves on the old bridge road, many of them, unhurried, and the particular creak of a wagon built to carry tools.

Lots of tools.
