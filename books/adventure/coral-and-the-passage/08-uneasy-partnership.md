## 8. Uneasy Partnership

I had watched the Ashgrove engineer for three days before I let them see me watching, and in that time I learned exactly three things: they talked to their kettle, they mended their boots with fishing line, and they lied beautifully about small things and terribly about large ones.

The large lie was "I don't know what you're talking about," delivered when I asked about the chest.

We were standing in their cabin — my cabin now too, apparently, since I'd invited myself in and they'd been too tired or too curious to throw me out. The chest sat between us on the table like a third negotiator. Rain came off the river in sheets. Somewhere down the valley, a train that wasn't supposed to run was running, which meant Renner hadn't left. Which meant neither of us could.

"Let me say what I think you are," I said, "and you can correct me."

Astrid folded their arms. They had a way of folding them that suggested they were bracing a structure, not defending one. "Go ahead."

"You're the engineer who walked out of the Halden inquiry twenty years ago. You didn't testify. You didn't sign the report. You just — left. And everyone assumed you ran because you were guilty." I turned my cup on the table, once, twice. "But guilty people don't live like this. Guilty people take the pension and move somewhere with a coastline. You stayed next to the corpse of the railway. That's not guilt. That's penance, or surveillance, or both."

For a long moment the only sound was the rain hammering the tin roof, and Astrid's face did something complicated — the look of a person hearing their own diagnosis read back to them by someone with no medical training whatsoever.

"And you," they said finally, "are Mira Vell. Which is either your real name or the least subtle alias in the history of exile. You've been hunting this chest since before you could legally hunt anything. Your people have songs about it. Don't look surprised — ferry-women hear everything on the water, and you row like someone who grew up reciting grievances for rhythm."

"That's fair," I admitted. It was. My grandmother had taught me the rowing cadence using the names of the dead. Two hundred strokes to Halden, two hundred names. Children learn arithmetic that way in Vellmarki households: *if the company killed two hundred and lied about two hundred, how many truths remain?*

"So we understand each other," Astrid said.

"We understand each other's files," I corrected. "That's different."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about negotiating with an engineer: they don't haggle. Ordinary people bargain the way rivers erode — sideways, patiently, wearing each other down. Engineers negotiate like blasting. They identify the load-bearing point of the argument and put all the charge there at once.

"The chest doesn't leave my hands," Astrid said. "Not for passage, not for protection, not for anything. If you want to walk away from this table, walk. But if you want what's inside it, you come with me, and when we reach the summit wire station, we decide together what happens to it. Together. One vote each."

"And if we disagree?"

"Then we keep deciding until we agree, because there are only two of us up there and I refuse to spend the rest of my life looking over my shoulder at a Vellmarki ferry-woman with excellent rope skills and a grudge."

It was, I will confess, the first honest offer anyone had made me in years. Every other party to this hunt wanted something simple from me: silence, labor, or my name on a document renouncing claims my grandparents' generation had signed in blood. Astrid wanted something difficult. A partnership. With *me*. A woman they'd known for four days and had caught going through their coat pockets on day two.

In my defense, I was looking for the seal stamp. In my further defense, I found it.

"One condition," I said. "If it comes to a choice between broadcasting those surveys and getting them safely to Vellmarki hands — to living hands, to people who can use them —"

"No."

"You haven't heard the condition."

"I don't need to. The answer is no, and here's why, so listen once." Astrid leaned forward, and I saw for the first time not the disgraced functionary from the inquiry photographs but the person who must have existed before the photographs — someone who had loved a thing enough to be destroyed by it. "Two hundred people went into that tunnel believing the mountain above them was sound. Believing the survey work above them was *honest*. Their testimony is in those plates. Not mine. Not yours. Not Vellmark's, and certainly not the company's. The dead get the deciding vote, and the dead voted for daylight. Everything else is negotiation about method."

I should have hated that speech. It was self-important. It was theatrical. It stole my people's grievance and rebranded it as personal conscience, which is the specialty of people who have never had a nation erased out from under them.

Instead I heard myself say: "Method. Fine. We decide the method together at the summit. But I navigate. Every route, every camp, every rope length across the ice — my call. You're a railroad engineer, Ashgrove. You know lines. You do not know ice."

"I know loads," Astrid said. "Ice is just a bridge that lies about its schedule."

Despite myself — genuinely against my will, I want that recorded — I laughed. It came out wrong, rusty, like a gate hinge in a house where no one visits. Astrid looked as startled as I felt.

* * *

We spent the evening doing the thing engineers and sailors do instead of bonding, which is arguing about numbers, and I want to be clear that this was the actual foundation of the partnership, more than any speech about the dead.

Astrid unrolled their map on the table and weighted the corners with the brass instruments from the chest — sacrilege, probably, using a martyr's altimeter as a paperweight, but practical, and I was learning that Astrid's religion was practicality with a thin devotional glaze over it.

"The company believes there are four ways through the Correns," they said. "The Halden line, closed. The southern road, watched. The high shepherd trails, snowed under by now. And nothing else." A finger traced a fifth path, faint, drawn in pencil so old it had gone the color of rust. "There's a fifth. The dead spur. An incline railway they started in the boom years and abandoned when the money dried up. Nine miles of track at grades up to forty percent, three brake-houses, and a summit approach that hasn't seen a boot print in fifteen years."

"Why does the company believe it's impassable?"

"Because I told them so." Astrid said it flatly, without pride, which somehow made it worse. Or better. "Final survey of my career before I — left. I wrote 'structurally unsound, recommend permanent closure.' What I meant was 'the only route Renner won't think to guard.'"

"You falsified a safety report."

"I improved one."

"There's a difference?"

"In this case? About nine miles of head start." They tapped the map. "Your turn. The glacier. When does the tongue calve?"

I told them. I gave it to them straight, because lying about ice is the one sin my people don't forgive even among ourselves — we lost too many to optimistic estimates. "Early melt season, the calving face lets go in pieces. Before it goes, the ice bridge across the melt channel is solid. After, the high route opens for maybe a week before the crevasse fields rot through. This year the melt is early. Our window is early. We have perhaps ten days, and every forecast says snow before then."

"So we leave tomorrow."

"We leave tonight," I said. "Renner burned the bridge today. That wasn't pressure, that's a man clearing his workspace. Next he burns something with people in it."

Astrid looked at me for a long moment, and I watched them do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic I'd done, the one where the variables are named after people you've just met and the sum is whether you trust them with your life.

"Tonight," they agreed.

* * *

Packing took an hour, mostly because Astrid packed the way they seemed to do everything: with a quiet, infuriating competence that made me want to check their work, followed by checking their work, followed by finding it correct, followed by resentment.

They took the survey plates and wrapped them in oilcloth, then waxed cloth, then — I am not making this up — slid them into a section of rubber hose sealed at both ends, so that the chest's most precious contents would survive total immersion, fire, and presumably moderate artillery.

"You've done this before," I said.

"I've lost documents before." A pause, and then, quieter, the first voluntary piece of their past they'd offered me: "Different documents. Ones I was supposed to destroy. I didn't destroy them. I hid them badly, and the flood took them, and I spent twenty years assuming they were gone." They cinched the pack closed. "Then the flood gives them back. Rivers keep better archives than governments. Remember that, if you ever found your country again."

I didn't answer. There was a whole speech in me about what it means to lose a country — not the land, the *fact* of it, the way a name stops working on forms, the way your grandmother's birthplace becomes "formerly" everything — but I'd learned in exile that grief offered too early gets spent like small coin. So I said, "Your knot is wrong," and retied it, and we understood each other perfectly.

* * *

We left at dark, down to the culvert where the chest had spent its hiding days, and I'll give Astrid this: they retrieved it without flinching at the water, hauled it out, and stood there a moment with their hand flat on the lid, the way you'd steady a horse, or a coffin.

"It's heavy," they said. Not complaining. Noting. The way you note a structural load.

"It's two hundred people," I said. "It was always going to be heavy."

Something passed between us then that I'm still not sure I have the right word for. Not trust — trust takes seasons, and we had hours. Not friendship; I've had friends, and none of them involved this much mutual suspicion. It was closer to what two climbers feel when they tie into the same rope: a formal, almost legal recognition that your survival now has another person's mistakes in it, and theirs has yours.

"Terms restated," Astrid said, shrugging into the pack. "We cross together. We decide together at the summit. You navigate, I engineer. Neither of us hands the chest to anyone — company, exile, court, or God — without the other's voice in it."

"And if one of us dies on the mountain?"

They considered this with the same seriousness they'd given the knot. "Then the survivor decides alone. And lives with it. That's the real terms of every partnership — the fine print is written in whoever's left."

"Warm," I said.

"Honest," they said. "You'll learn to prefer it. Everyone does, eventually. Usually right after the alternative kills someone."

We went up along the dead spur in the last grey light, two figures and a chest, and behind us the river kept talking to itself in the dark, keeping its records, giving up its dead on its own schedule and no one else's.

I had hunted this chest my entire life. I had imagined the moment of taking possession of it a thousand ways — seized in a raid, bought with favors, inherited from some dying elder's shaking hands. Never once had I imagined *carrying it uphill with its self-appointed guardian*, sharing the weight by unspoken agreement, each of us pretending the arrangement was temporary.

By the second mile, Astrid stopped pretending first.

"The brake-house is another hour," they said. "There's a stove that might still draw, if the chimney isn't nested."

"Might still draw," I repeated. "You surveyed this line. Didn't you check the chimneys?"

"I checked the *grades*, Mira. Chimneys are below my pay grade. All of them were, eventually. That was rather the problem."

And there it was again — the wryness laid over the wound like a tarp over machinery, keeping the rain off, fooling no one. I found, to my considerable irritation, that I was beginning to read them fluently. Four days. It had taken me four days to learn a language I'd resisted learning, and the fluency went both ways, because when I stumbled on the loose rail spike that the dark had hidden, Astrid's hand was already out, already catching my arm, already saying nothing about it, which was somehow more intimate than saying something.

At the brake-house door, I paused.

"One more term," I said. "When this is over — whatever we decide at the summit, however it ends — you tell me the truth about Halden. All of it. What you knew, when you knew it, why you ran. Not for the inquiry. For me. My people have spent twenty years building our reckoning out of rumor, and I'll not carry home another rumor when I could carry a witness."

Astrid stood in the doorway with the wind pulling at their coat, and for a moment they looked every year of their exile, every winter of the river, every name they must have recited to themselves in the dark the way my grandmother taught me to row.

"All of it," they agreed. "Even the parts that make you put me off this mountain."

"Especially those."

Inside, the stove drew on the third match. Outside, far down the valley, a light moved where no light should be — steady, methodical, sweeping the treeline in the pattern of a man reading a landscape he intends to own.

Renner, or his men, or his ledger walking around in boots.

Astrid saw it too. We didn't discuss it. We banked the fire low, laid out our gear in the dark by touch, and settled in for the first night of the crossing — two strangers tied to one rope, one chest, and one decision waiting for us at the top of the world.

Sleep came hard for me, and briefly, and full of names.

It would be the last easy night either of us got.
