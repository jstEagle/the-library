## 10. Up the Dead Spur

The dead spur began where the river gave up pretending to be useful.

I had known about the incline railway the way you know about a scar on someone else's body — aware of it, uninterested in it, until the day it turns out to be your own. Ashgrove stood at its foot with their hands on their hips like a doctor regarding a patient they intended to save out of spite.

"Rope-hauled incline," they said. "Two thousand feet of grade at better than forty percent. The company built it to haul ore down and men up, and when the ore ran out they hauled the men down too and left everything else to rust."

"Charming," I said. "Do we climb it or do we admire it?"

"We climb it. Carefully. Every fourth tie is rotten through, and the cable—" They touched the great frayed hawser that lay along the trackbed like the spine of some enormous slaughtered animal. "—the cable is decorative now. We do not trust the cable."

"You say that as though I were planning to."

"You'd be surprised what people plan to trust when they're tired." Ashgrove said it lightly, but I had learned by then that everything this person said lightly was a gravestone for something said heavily once, long ago, to somebody who hadn't listened.

We climbed.

I will not pretend it was dignified. The mountain does not care for dignity; it cares for purchase. We went up on all fours much of the way, hands on rails gone orange with rust, boots finding ties that held and avoiding the ones that didn't — Ashgrove testing each one ahead of me with a tap of a walking staff, calling *good* or *dead* over their shoulder in a voice flat as a schedule. Good. Dead. Good. Good. Dead. It became a kind of liturgy. I found myself praying to it, which embarrassed me, because I don't pray, or hadn't since the maps changed under my feet.

Below us the river shrank to a gray thread. Above us the Correns stacked themselves against the sky, white-capped already, and I did not let myself think about what early snow meant for the calving window, because Ashgrove was doing enough arithmetic for both of us and their face was getting worse at hiding it by the hour.

Halfway up there was a maintenance ledge, a shelf cut into the rock where the winding house had once stood, and Ashgrove called a halt there and shared out dried fish and hard bread without ceremony, the way you feed a mule you've decided not to hate.

"You knew this line," I said. "Before."

"I surveyed it." They chewed. Their eyes stayed on the valley. "First job out of apprenticeship. I was nineteen and certain the mountains existed to be improved." A pause. "The company agreed with me. That should have been the warning."

"And then Halden Pass."

Something moved behind their face — not flinching exactly. More like a door closing gently so as not to wake anyone.

"And then Halden Pass," they said, and stood, and brushed off their trousers, and said, "Dead," to a tie that deserved it, and we climbed on.

* * *

The brake-house squatted at the top of the incline like a thing that had died mid-sentence. Half its roof remained, the half over the machinery bay, where the great brake drum still sat on its axle, furred with rust, big enough to roll a barrel into. The other half of the roof had gone into the gully sometime in the last twenty years, and the wind came through the gap and made the loose sheet metal talk to itself in a language of small complaints.

Ashgrove got a fire going in the corner behind a windbreak of salvaged plate — small, disciplined, the fire of a person who has been hunted before and intends to remain unhunted. I watched them build it and thought, not for the first time, that there was something wrong with the picture. Fugitives are supposed to be careless. This one banked coals like a housewife.

"There's straw under the tarp," Ashgrove said. "Dry-ish. Take the wall side. The floor slopes toward the door and the door doesn't close."

"Generous."

"I'm told I have a talent for hospitality."

I took the wall side anyway. The straw smelled of mice and old creosote, and I lay down on it in my coat and discovered that my body, which had spent three days being furious with me, had decided to accept whatever it was given.

Ashgrove sat up against the brake drum with their map across their knees, tracing routes by firelight with one finger, lips moving. Counting miles. Counting days. Counting snowfall they couldn't see yet but could smell coming, the way farmers smell rain.

"Mira Vell," they said, without looking up.

"That's my name when I'm being formal."

"It's the name on your ferry license. The one you showed me at the crossing, very casual, very practiced." Now they looked up. Firelight is honest light; it finds every seam in a face. "Vell isn't a common name in the lowlands. Vellmarki families kept it after the erasure. Some of them changed it. Yours didn't."

"No," I said. "Mine didn't."

They waited. I want to record, for accuracy, that they waited without pressing — that they went back to their map and let the silence sit there between us like a third traveler, and that this, more than any question, is what undid me. I had planned to tell no one. I had told no one for eleven years. And here was this disgraced, mud-stained, stubborn engineer not even asking, and I heard myself begin to speak into the dark above the fire.

* * *

"You know the official version," I said. "Everyone knows the official version. Vellmark defaulted on its war debts. Vellmark's government collapsed. Vellmark petitioned for annexation by its neighbors, who divided the territory 'for administrative convenience,' and the name was retired from the maps as a courtesy, because keeping it would only confuse trade."

"The version on the stamps," Ashgrove said.

"The version on the stamps. Here is the version underneath." I pulled my knees up. The sheet metal overhead chattered. "Vellmark didn't default. Vellmark was made to default — the loans were called all at once, in a season, by banks in three capitals acting in concert, which banks owned, among other things, controlling shares in the Northern Continental Railway. The same railway that needed a pass through the Correns. The same railway whose surveyors had been refused permission to route through Vellmarki land, because our assembly — a real parliament, elected, quarrelsome, alive — voted it down twice."

Ashgrove's finger stopped moving on the map.

"When the economy broke, the parliament couldn't pay its police. When the police went unpaid, the borders went unpatrolled. When the borders opened, the neighbors walked in — politely, with clerks, with census forms. There wasn't a battle, Ashgrove. There was a filing system. Our towns were renamed in six weeks. Our language was struck from the schools in a year. Our records were transferred to regional archives, where they were, and I use the archival term here, *consolidated*. Which means burned, mostly. The men who signed the transfer orders were given pensions. The men who protested were given sentences. And the country itself was given a funeral with no body — just the absence, on every map, growing more complete each year, until children born ten years later looked at the space where we had been and saw only mountains."

The fire popped. Ashgrove had gone very still.

"My father was a stationmaster," I said. "On the Vellmarki state line — our line, the one your company wanted. He kept the last timetable ever printed. Framed it. Hung it in our kitchen like an icon, which is exactly what it was. When the consolidation men came for the archives in our district, he drove forty kilometers in the night and took back every document he could carry from the burning shed. Surveys. Land titles. Pay ledgers. Petitions. Eleven years of a living country's paperwork, carried out of the fire in grain sacks on a handcart."

"What happened to him?"

"He was caught on the second trip." I said it evenly. I had practiced saying it evenly for eleven years and the practice held, mostly. "They didn't hang him. Hanging makes martyrs and martyrs make trouble. They sentenced him to a work detail — road construction, they called it. On the new alignment through the Correns. The company's alignment." I watched Ashgrove's face and saw the moment the arithmetic completed, saw them arrive at Halden Pass the way you arrive at a place you've been dreading. "He was assigned to the tunnel crews. Two hundred men on that shift rotation, and most of them Vellmarki, because the new administration found it convenient to fill dangerous work with people whose complaints wouldn't be heard in any court that existed."

"Astrid's voice, when it finally came, was almost nothing at all. "Mira. The surveys in that chest—"

"Were his. Some of them. His initials are on four of the plates. He was a surveyor before he was a stationmaster — it's how he knew which documents mattered." My voice did something I didn't authorize; it cracked, and I let it, because there are costs to evenness too. "And when the tunnel was blown with two hundred men inside it, the company needed a story, and the story they chose was a Vellmarki engineer — a saboteur, a foreigner, a man with a grudge. Your mentor. They erased him the way they erased us. Made him the monster so the crime would have a face that wasn't theirs."

The silence went on long enough that the fire burned down a full degree of brightness.

"I was fourteen," I said. "My mother took us east, over the low passes, before the border closed fully. She carried the family documents sewn into her coat, and she taught us the names of our towns the way you teach a child a prayer — every night, in order, until the names lived somewhere the maps couldn't reach. She died in exile. In a country that spelled our surname wrong on her death certificate and refused to correct it, because officially there was nothing to correct." I looked at Ashgrove across the fire. "So when I saw that seal on your coat cuff, I didn't think: treasure. I thought: *there is a piece of my father that survived*. And I have spent my whole life learning that pieces of my father do not survive. They get consolidated. So yes, Ashgrove. I followed you home. I would follow anyone home carrying that chest. I would follow the devil himself, and argue terms."

Ashgrove sat with all of it. I'll grant them that — they didn't rush to comfort, didn't perform sympathy, didn't say *I'm sorry* the way people say it to move past a thing. They fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch, and when they spoke it was slow, like a person picking their way across rotten ties.

"I met Halvorsen when I was twenty-two," they said. "He hired me onto the Corren survey. He taught me everything — how to read ground, how to listen to a mountain before you argue with it. He used to wear a ring with that crest on it. I asked him about it once and he said, *a country is not its government*, and I was young and thought it was just something old men said." Their jaw worked. "They pinned the blast on him and he ran, and I believed the charges, Mira. For years I believed them, because believing them was easier than believing my own company would kill two hundred men for a schedule. Then I lost my commission for asking the wrong questions about Halden, and I came out here, and I stopped believing anything except that the river gives things back eventually." They looked up. "Those papers in the chest. If they're what you say — if they're what I think they are — then Halvorsen didn't run from the crime. He ran carrying the evidence, and he hid it, and he's been missing twenty years, and I—" They stopped. Started again. "I owe him. And I owe two hundred men I never met. And those debts point the same direction. Up."

"Up," I said. "Through the snows. To a glacier that opens for one week if it opens at all. Carrying a chest that a professional killer wants badly enough to burn bridges over."

"When you put it that way it sounds reckless."

"When I put it that way it sounds like Tuesday."

That surprised a sound out of them — not quite a laugh, but the fossil of one, and I counted it as payment received.

* * *

We settled by degrees after that, the way sediment settles. Ashgrove banked the fire and spread the map out flat between our bedrolls, and for an hour we planned aloud — the dead spur's upper terminus, the ridge route, the water sources marked in their cramped sure hand — and it was strange how easily our voices fit together in that work, hers and mine, the engineer's numbers and the exile's memory of ground. I knew these mountains as a child knows a grandmother's face. Ashgrove knew them as a lover knows a body. Between us we nearly had the whole truth of them.

Near midnight the wind shifted and brought the smell of snow — that mineral emptiness, cold with nothing alive in it. Ashgrove lifted their head like a deer.

"Early," they murmured. "Too early."

"How many days to the snowline?"

"At this pace? Two. Three if the weather argues." They looked north, toward the black serration of the high country, where the stars were already going milky at the horizon. "The window's shrinking, Mira. Whatever happens between us — the chest, the summit, all of it — the mountain gets a vote too. And the mountain votes first."

I lay back on the straw and pulled my coat to my chin and did not sleep at once. I listened to the sheet metal complain, and to Ashgrove breathing in the rhythm of a person forcing themselves to rest, and I thought about my father's framed timetable hanging in a kitchen that no longer existed, and about how grief carried alone gets heavy as ore, and how tonight, for the first time in eleven years, someone else had taken hold of one end of it without being asked.

Trust, I decided, watching the firelight gutter on the brake drum's rusted curve, is not a thing you give. It's a thing you spend, and hope the purchase holds.

Above the pass, the first flakes were already falling — fine as ash, silent as consolidation — and neither of us yet knew it.
