## 11. The First Snowline

The snow came in the night, the way bad news does — quietly, and all at once.

I woke in the brake-house with my breath hanging over me like a small personal ghost and thought, for one uncharitable moment, that the roof had finally given up and was shedding its shingles on us. Then I put a hand out of the sleeping roll and it came back white.

"Snow," I said, to no one in particular, because Mira was already awake and lacing her boots with the grim efficiency of a woman who had been expecting this since the river.

"I told you," she said. "The window is shrinking."

"You told me the glacier would calve early. You did not tell me the sky would start dropping winter on our heads like an unpaid bill."

"The sky does not send bills, Ashgrove. It sends collections." She said it without looking up, which was how she said most of her bleakest observations, as though they were instructions for tying knots.

I sat up and took inventory of myself, which at fifty-one years old is less a matter of pride than of engineering: joints reporting stress fractures in the design, back complaining about load tolerances nobody had signed off on, left knee filing a formal grievance. Otherwise sound. The chest went into its oilcloth wrap. The survey plates rode against my spine, where I'd taken to carrying them, because if the mountain wanted them back it would have to go through my vertebrae first, and I liked its odds slightly less than mine.

Outside, the world had been redrawn in pencil. The dead spur we'd climbed yesterday — a rusted incline railway stitched up the mountainside by men who believed, in the old arrogant way, that grade was merely a suggestion — now ran white to its shoulders. The crossties were still visible, black teeth in a pale gum. Higher up, where the spur bent around the shoulder of the Correns toward Halden, the snow thickened until the line simply stopped being drawn at all, as if the cartographer had run out of ink or nerve.

Mira stood at the edge of the brake-house platform reading the ridgelines the way Renner read ledgers: for what could be collected, and when.

"How long?" I asked.

She held up three fingers, then folded one down. "Three days of climbing weather. Maybe four if the wind stays kind." Another finger folded. "After that, we're walking into a season, not a storm."

"And the calving?"

"If the ice goes before the snow closes the high route, we have our week. If the snow comes first—" She shrugged, a gesture she performed with her whole body, like a door swinging shut. "Then we wait until spring, and so does everyone hunting us."

There was a certain comfort in that, if you squinted. Winter as a moat. But moats keep garrisons in as well as sieges out, and I had eaten brake-house rations before. They were the reason I could now identify sawdust by texture alone.

We ate anyway. Oats, river water, a heel of bread gone hard enough to use as a shim. Mira chewed and watched the pass. I chewed and watched Mira watching the pass, and between us we achieved a complete surveillance of the situation.

"We should make the saddle by dark," she said finally. "There's a cut in the ridge where the old maintenance path crosses. Stone walls on both sides. If snow comes again tonight, it'll hold us above the drift."

"You've walked this route."

"My father's people walked it before there were rails. And after." She shouldered her pack. "Everyone walks it after, eventually. That's what the company never understood about mountains. They think a closed line is a forgotten line."

* * *

The morning taught us the difference between snow as weather and snow as verdict.

Above the tree line the drifts lay in the lee of every cut and culvert, soft as sifted flour and about as trustworthy. I went first, because I weighed more and because I had spent thirty years learning what ground does when you lie to it, and I wanted the lying done to me rather than to Mira. Every step was a conversation. Probe, listen, commit. The mountain answered in the only language it had: solid, solid, hollow, solid.

By noon we reached the long traverse below the shoulder of the peak, where the old rail bed had been blasted out of the living rock and then abandoned to it. The snow here was thinner, scoured by wind, and the rock showed through in patches like bone through skin. We moved along the bed itself, on the ballast, because stone underfoot beats stone overhead.

That was where I saw it.

At first I took it for a boulder wearing a shroud — a rounded shape just off the bed, half-drifted, with a darkness at its center that didn't match the geology. Then the darkness resolved into cloth. Then the cloth resolved into a coat. Then the coat resolved into a man.

He had been dead a long time. The cold had kept him the way a ledger keeps a figure: exactly, impersonally, without opinion. He sat against the cut wall with his legs drawn up, as though he'd decided, somewhere in his last hour, that the most dignified thing left to do was wait properly. Frost had built a rime on his beard. His eyes were closed. Whatever he'd seen last, he'd chosen not to look at it anymore.

Mira crouched beside him and did not touch him. "A year," she said. "Maybe two. The freeze-thaw hasn't touched him much. He died late in the season, after the snow set in."

I looked at his hands. One was tucked inside his coat, against his chest, the way you hold something you can't afford to lose. I worked it free gently, apologizing under my breath in the manner of people who disturb graves professionally, and found what he'd been keeping warm.

A pay chit. Brass-stamped, company issue, the kind issued to contract laborers on the high lines — good for redemption at any company office, redeemable in wages owed. The stamp was crisp. The date was twenty years old.

Halden. The chit was drawn on the Halden tunnel works.

I sat back on my heels in the snow and let that settle, the way you let a load settle onto a bridge before you trust it.

"He was one of them," I said. "One of the two hundred."

"No." Mira's voice was flat and careful. "The two hundred are under the mountain. In the tunnel." She nodded at the slope above us, where somewhere beneath a hundred feet of rock and ice the collapsed bore of Halden Pass lay sealed like a throat. "This man was paid. This man walked away."

I turned the chit over. On the reverse, in pencil gone grey with time, someone had written a number. Not a sum. A count. *199.*

"One hundred ninety-nine," I read aloud.

Mira went very still, the way water goes still before it decides to be ice.

"He was counting," she said. "Or someone was counting him. The last one out." She took the chit from me and held it close to her face, and for a moment I saw her not as the ferry-woman, the exile, the cold calculator of the crossing, but as a girl who had grown up on stories of a country that no longer existed, hearing numbers recited like prayers. "They paid them off. The ones who wouldn't go down, or the ones who came up. Final wages, sign here, forget the names. And this one—" her thumb moved across the brass stamp, "—this one couldn't spend it. Carried it until the mountain took him too."

"Or he was going back down to spend it," I said, "and the weather disagreed."

"Does it matter?"

It did, actually. It mattered enormously, and I couldn't have told you why yet, only that the engineer in me refuses loose ends the way some men refuse drink. A man dies on a closed line with a twenty-year-old pay chit clutched to his heart and a count written on the back — that isn't an accident. Accidents don't carry arithmetic.

We buried him as well as the ground allowed, which is to say we piled stones over the drift and Mira spoke something in Vellmarki, low and rhythmic, that sounded older than the rails beneath us. I added what I could, which was the truth: "You waited. Someone will finish your count."

The chit went into my breast pocket, next to nothing, because I owned nothing else worth carrying against my heart.

* * *

We made the saddle cut by late afternoon, and the wind made sure we earned it.

It came down the col like a debt collector with a schedule, straight from the icefields, and it found every gap in our clothing with the thoroughness of an auditor. We pressed into the stone-walled cut Mira had promised — old masonry, company-built, mortared by hands that had expected to be thanked — and got a fire going in the lee of the wall with fuel we'd hauled up from the stunted pines below the snowline. The flames burned sideways. Everything up here burned sideways. It seemed a fair metaphor for the whole enterprise and I chose not to examine it further.

Mira sat with her back to the stone, knees up, hands open to the fire, and stared at the pass ahead of us. From the saddle you could see it clearly for the first time: the notch in the Corren Range where Halden Pass had been, the approaches fanning out below it like the skirts of a dropped dress, and above it all the glacier — a white tongue hanging out of the high country, blue-veined, patient.

"That's it?" I asked. "The calving face?"

"That's the tongue. The face is around the shoulder, where it meets the meltwater. When it calves, the route opens along the lateral moraine — seven days, maybe eight, before the melt swells and the ice bridges rot." She glanced at me. "You've never seen a glacier calve."

"I've seen a bridge fail," I said. "Twice. Once on purpose." I fed a stick to the fire. "I imagine it's similar, only honest."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face and melted faster than the snow around us. "Bigger," she said. "Slower. It groans first. Like something waking up that has been listening for a very long time." She pulled her collar tighter. "My grandmother heard it the year before the erasure. She said the mountain was clearing its throat to speak. Everyone laughed at her."

"Do you laugh now?"

"No," Mira said. "Now I bring engineers to listen."

The fire popped. Somewhere below us, far off, a sound rolled up the valley that might have been thunder and might have been rockfall and might have been nothing, and we both turned our heads toward it and both pretended we hadn't.

"Ashgrove." She said my name the way she always did, like a specimen label. But her voice had changed weight. "The man today. With the chit."

"What about him?"

"He wasn't lost. Lost men climb down. He was climbing up. Toward Halden." She looked at me across the fire, and the wind chose that moment to drop, so that her words arrived undiluted. "Twenty years ago they paid the witnesses to walk away. He took the money and spent twenty years trying to give it back. What does that make you think about the papers in your pack?"

I thought about it honestly, which is a discipline, like surveying: you don't get to choose the readings.

"It makes me think," I said slowly, "that testimony doesn't want to be safe. It wants to be delivered." I turned my palms to the fire. My fingers were cracked and the cold had gotten into the old break in my right hand, the one I'd earned on the Merrow viaduct, and it ached the way it always ached when the truth was nearby. "And it makes me think the mountain keeps better records than the company does."

"The mountain keeps everything," Mira said. "That's the problem. It keeps the workers, and it keeps the snow, and it keeps its own schedule, and it does not care whose reckoning falls due." She kicked a knot of snow over the fire's edge, hissing it down to embers. "Sleep. Tomorrow we cross under the seracs, and tomorrow night, if the weather holds, we sleep in the station."

Halden Station. The name went through me like a spike through a tie.

I lay in my roll with the wind rebuilding outside the walls and did not sleep so much as fall briefly unconscious and climb back out. Above me, past the stone, the glacier hung in the dark doing whatever glaciers do at night — growing, groaning, remembering. Below me, under a hundred feet of collapsed rock, two hundred men lay in the dark of the tunnel where the company had put them, and one of their number had spent his last years walking back toward them with his wages unspent, counting himself down from the total.

*One hundred ninety-nine.*

Which meant somebody had been *one*. Somebody had written the number. Somebody had stood at the mouth of that tunnel while the dust was still settling and begun the only audit that mattered, and then carried it up into the snows until the snows closed the account.

Tomorrow we would sleep in the station above that tunnel. Tomorrow night, if the walls still stood, I would be lying directly over the place where my mentor's name had been dragged through the mud of a cover-up, and where two hundred other names had been left without even the dignity of mud.

The wind rose again. The snow ticked against the stones like a telegraph key, patient, indifferent, sending the same message over and over to anyone fluent enough to read it:

*The mountain is keeping score. Come higher, and see the ledger.*
