## 12. Halden Station Ghosts

The storm found us an hour before the station did.

I'd like to tell you I navigated to Halden Station through skill and surveyor's instinct, reading the mountain's bones the way I once read track charts. The truth is that Mira smelled it. Woodsmoke, she said — old woodsmoke, soaked into timber for twenty years and never quite washed out — and then the wind shifted and I caught it too, a ghost of a ghost, and we turned upslope into the white with our heads down and our hands hooked into each other's pack straps like two drunks leaving a funeral.

The station appeared all at once, the way drowned things appear when the river finally gives them up. A roofline first, sagging under a foot of old snow. Then the platform edge, heaved and cracked by frost, still stubbornly level where the rails used to run. Then the whole gray bulk of it: Halden Station, elevation four thousand one hundred feet, population — according to the faded enamel sign bolted by the door — *forty-seven souls served daily*.

Forty-seven souls. Two hundred more beneath it.

"We can't," Mira said. Not a question. She stood at the threshold with snow crusting her eyelashes and her jaw set in that way she had, the way that meant she'd already decided something and was waiting for me to argue so she could enjoy refusing.

"It's the only shelter inside three miles."

"Then we walk three miles."

"In this?" I tilted my chin at the sky, which had gone the color of dishwater and was dispensing its contents with the enthusiasm of a man settling a grudge. "Mira. It's a building. It's cold and it's empty and it's been empty for twenty years."

"That's not what it is." She said it quietly, and that was worse than shouting. Mira shouted about crevasses and bad rope work and my habit of walking backward while talking. She went quiet about Vellmark. "You know what's under us. You've known since the culvert, since the map, since the moment you pulled that chest out of the river and didn't sleep for three nights after. This isn't a station. It's a lid."

I looked at the door. It hung on one hinge, swollen shut and then swollen open again across two decades of freeze and thaw, and behind it I could see the pale rectangle of a waiting room floor, drifted with snow that had come in through a broken window and never left.

"I know what's under us," I said. "That's why we're going in."

She stared at me for a long moment. Snow ticked down between us, soft as ash.

"You engineers," she said finally, shouldering past me through the doorway. "You think everything is a problem with a solution. Even grief."

"Grief is exactly the kind of problem engineers are worst at," I said. "We keep trying to brace it."

Inside, the waiting room had kept its dignity better than most ruins manage. Benches along the walls, their slats gone silver-gray. A ticket window with its brass grille intact, green with verdigris. A timetable still pinned to a corkboard, curled like a dead leaf: *Halden–Corren Summit, departures hourly, first service 6:10.* Somebody had written *GONE HOME* across it in pencil, and somebody else, later, had crossed out GONE and written nothing at all, so that it read only *HOME*, which struck me as either a terrible joke or the truest thing anyone had ever said about the place.

There was a stove. That was the miracle of it. A squat iron potbelly in the corner, and when I opened the door a nest of mouse skeletons tumbled out and half a chimney's worth of draft came down that tasted of rust but not of blockage. I fed it splinters from a bench leg — forgive me, dead of Halden, I'll carve you new ones — and then the good dry planks of a luggage rack, and within twenty minutes the room had gone from tomb to almost-room again, orange light jumping on the walls, our wet things steaming on a line I strung from the ticket grille.

Mira sat with her back against the wall and her boots off and did not speak for a long time. I let her. There's a discipline to silence, same as anything else. You learn it living off the grid: when to fill a quiet and when to let it stand. Hers needed standing.

It was me who broke it, because I'm weaker that way.

"The tunnel mouth is maybe six hundred feet down the grade," I said. "Under the platform end. They built the station right over it so passengers could step off the train and be underground in ninety seconds. My mentor designed the transfer. Said it was the most elegant piece of passenger engineering on the whole Corren line." I fed the stove another plank. "He used to say that about everything he built. Most elegant. He had a limited vocabulary of praise and he spent it freely."

"Edda Kessler," Mira said.

I looked at her.

"You say 'my mentor' like a man saying 'my saint,'" she said. "Everyone in the exile camps knew the name Kessler. Chief engineer of the Corren expansion. The one they blamed." She turned her hands toward the stove, palms open, warming fingers that were probably beyond warming. "My father had a saying about her. *They gave the crime a face so the crime could keep its hands.*"

"He didn't do it." The words came out of me flat and immediate, the way a spike comes out of a rail when you hit it true — one blow, no thought. "I've read every page in that chest twice. The blast charges were set from the ventilation adit. Edda hadn't been in the adit in a week — there's a signed access log, there's a—"

"Astrid." Mira wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the far wall, at the water stain shaped like a country nobody drew anymore. "I know. Why do you think I've spent eleven years and two continents hunting a box?"

Fair enough. I shut up and poked the fire.

But here's the thing about poking fires in haunted rooms: eventually your eyes wander. And mine wandered to the wall behind the ticket window, where the plaster had fallen away in a great ragged sheet, exposing the lath underneath, and someone — long ago, before the plaster fell or just after, I couldn't tell — had covered that lath with writing.

Not graffiti. Graffiti has a looseness to it, a swagger. This was ruled. There were faint pencil guidelines still visible, horizontal lines spaced with a draftsman's hand, and the names marched along them in ink, row after row, small and upright and patient. I crossed the room without deciding to cross the room. My legs made the decision the way legs do.

Names. Two hundred and eleven of them, though I counted twice and got two hundred and nine and then two hundred and twelve, because the ink had run in places where the roof leaked and some names were more suggestion than text. Men's names, women's names, names in the Vellmarki style with their soft doubled consonants, names in the lowland style, a few that were clearly borrowed or invented by immigrants trying to sound like they belonged to a country that would hire them and then bury them.

At the bottom of the last column, alone, set slightly apart as if the writer had stepped back to look at the whole list and then added one more line the way you sign a letter:

**E.K. — I counted them myself. Forgive me. I was late.**

And above the E.K., scratched into the lath itself with something sharp — a knife point, a compass leg, God help me, possibly a scribing awl from her own kit, the twin of the one sitting forty feet away in a flooded chest I'd dredged out of a river — the initials she put on every drawing she ever approved:

**EK**

I don't know how long I stood there. Long enough that the fire burned down a register. Long enough that Mira got up and came to stand beside me, and I heard her breath change when she read it.

"She came back," I said. My voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing in a different room. "After. Everyone said she ran. Every paper, every inquiry, every company man on every witness stand — *Kessler fled the district on the night of the eleventh.* But she came back. She climbed up here, to the station over her own tunnel, and she—" My throat closed around it. I tried again. "She wrote their names. All of them. By hand. In ink."

"Two hundred and eleven," Mira said softly. "The official count was one hundred and ninety-six. The company said the rest were day laborers, unregistered." Her finger hovered near the wall without touching it, the way you hover near a shrine. "She counted the unregistered too. She went and found out who they were."

*I counted them myself.*

Edda Kessler, who taught me that a survey is a promise you make to everyone who will ever stand on the ground you measured. Who told me once, drunk on plum brandy at a midsummer fair, tapping my sternum with one finger: *Ashgrove, the numbers don't belong to us. We just hold them for a while. The trick is knowing who to hand them back to.* Who vanished off the face of the earth two years later, leaving me a career in ruins and a name I couldn't use and a lifetime habit of flinching whenever anyone said the word *engineer* with respect in it.

She hadn't fled. She'd come back up the mountain in whatever season followed the collapse — winter, it must have been winter, the pass closes in October — and she'd sat in this freezing room with a bottle of ink and a straightedge and she'd written the dead down, one by one, in her beautiful insufferable draftsman's hand, and then she'd asked forgiveness of a wall.

And I had spent fifteen years asking forgiveness of nobody, because I'd believed the papers in that chest were hers to destroy and mine to forget, and shame is a load-bearing wall, you can live your whole life inside a house built of it.

"They'll want to see this," Mira said. "The exiles. When this is over. They'll want to stand here."

"Then it stays standing." I heard my own voice go hard and was surprised by it. "Whatever happens with the chest, the courts, Renner, any of it — this wall stays. I'll draw up stabilization plans myself. Shore the roof, cap the chimney, put a—"

"Astrid."

"—a proper flashing on the north slope, that leak is what's eating the plaster, if we—"

"*Astrid.*" Mira took my shoulder and turned me. Her face was very close and very serious, lit orange from below like a saint in a bad painting. "Listen to the mountain."

I listened. Wind. The tick of the cooling stove. Our own breathing.

Then, underneath it, faint but unmistakable, coming up through the floor, through the platform, through twenty years of rubble and rock:

A whistle. Distant, descending in pitch, moving fast. The kind of whistle a steam kettle makes, or a signal flare, or—

Or a party horn. Three short blasts, a pause, three more. Mountain code. Old guide code, pre-radio, the kind local crews still used even after the companies went to wire.

*Mira,* I signed with my hand before I said it, because some habits cross languages. *Party below. Moving.*

She was already at the broken window, glass gone these twenty years, peering down-grade into the murk. The snow had thinned to a fine malicious drizzle of grains, and through it, far below on the switchback trail, I saw what she saw: lights. Lantern light, swinging with the particular rhythm of people who walk for a living, spaced out along the trail in the disciplined interval of a guided rope team. Four points of light. Maybe five.

And behind them, higher up the trail than any lantern, a single steady white beam that didn't swing at all. An electric torch. Expensive. Patient.

Nobody in the settlements carried electric torches. Nobody in the exile camps did either.

"Ivo Renner doesn't climb mountains," I said. It came out steadier than I felt, which is the engineer's curse: the voice holds while the hands shake. "He takes the funicular. There's a working freight incline on the western spur, the Brassenford Mining Company never decommissioned it. He'll have hired guides because he has to, but he won't follow them. He'll *lead* them. Slowly. On his schedule."

"Then we have until morning," Mira said. "Maybe less. The storm breaks at dawn — I feel it in my knees, and my knees have never lied to me about weather, only about men."

I looked back at the wall. At the ruled lines and the patient names and the initials scratched above them, and at that last line in ink that was fading now, going the way of all ink, all paper, all testimony that isn't spoken aloud while there's still someone alive to hear it.

*I counted them myself. Forgive me. I was late.*

"Not this time," I said.

Mira looked at me. "What?"

I was already pulling the chest out from under my pack, already lifting the lid, already reaching for the survey plates with their brass edges and their beautiful, damning precision. Outside, the lanterns climbed. Inside, the fire popped and threw its light across two hundred and eleven names, and I laid the first plate flat on Edda Kessler's station floor and began, with numb fingers and a stub of carpenter's pencil, to copy.

Because somewhere below us, a telegraph line ran down the western spur, and somewhere along that line was a relay station with a live wire, and I had fifteen years of Morse in my hands and a debt on this wall that interest had been accruing on since before I was born.

"Mira," I said, copying, not looking up. "How do you feel about learning to tap code tonight?"
