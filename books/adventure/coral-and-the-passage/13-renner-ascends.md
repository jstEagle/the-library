## 13. Renner Ascends

I have always believed that most men fail at their work not because they lack talent but because they mistake urgency for competence. They run when they should read. They shout when they should count. Ivo Renner does not run. Ivo Renner reads.

By the third morning after the bridge fire, I had counted everything worth counting in that river settlement, and the arithmetic was simple: Ashgrove had gone up the dead spur with the chest, and they had taken the Vellmarki woman with them, which meant either a hostage or an alliance, and neither possibility improved my mood. A fugitive alone is a problem. A fugitive with local knowledge and a grievance older than my employment is a project.

So I did what any competent man does with a project. I hired help.

* * *

The Brassenford guide-house smelled of tallow, wet wool, and the particular despair of mountain men between seasons. Six of them sat around the stove playing cards for coins none of them could afford to lose, and when I pushed the door open with my boot, four of them looked up with the flat appraisal of men who had been paid before by men like me and remembered exactly what it cost them.

The fifth didn't look up at all. He kept dealing.

"Tomas Varga," said the house's keeper, nodding toward the dealer. "Best route-man on the western face. If he'll go."

"And if he won't?"

"Then you've got the other five, and God help you."

I set my ledger on the table — not the company ledger, the other one, the one bound in grey calfskin that made even the keeper stop chewing — and opened it to a blank page. I wrote a figure. I turned it around. The room went quiet in the way rooms do when money arrives like weather.

Tomas finished dealing his hand first. That was my first note about him: he finishes things. Then he looked at my figure, then at my face, and something moved behind his eyes that I catalogued and did not yet understand. Recognition, I thought at the time. Of money. Men always recognize money.

"I know these mountains," he said. "I don't know you."

"You don't need to know me. You need to know where a closed spur climbs above the flood line, whether the brake-house at the switchback still has a roof, and how many days' lead two people on foot can open on six people with mules."

"That last one," Tomas said, gathering the cards with a soft, deliberate snap, "depends on the two people."

"One of them is the engineer who drew half the abandoned lines in this range."

That got him. Not greed — interest. The particular hunger of a craftsman hearing about another craftsman. I filed that away too. My file on Tomas Varga was getting thick, and I hadn't yet written a word in it.

"The other one?" he asked.

"A ferry-woman from the crossing. Vellmarki."

The cards stopped. Even the stove seemed to burn quieter. Twenty years is long enough for a country to vanish off every map and not nearly long enough for it to vanish off a face, and every man in that room had grown up on stories about the pass, the tunnel, the two hundred who went in and did not come out. Some of them had fathers who'd worked the Halden contract. One of them, I noted, had gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with altitude.

"They're going for the summit wire," I said, because a lie works best when it's mostly true. "They mean to broadcast something. Sedition, forgery, I don't much care which. My employer cares. My employer pays."

"And if we catch them?" Tomas asked. "What happens then?"

"What happens," I said, closing the ledger, "is a trial. A clean one. In a courtroom, with lawyers, with paper." I let that sit while the men absorbed the novelty of it. "I am not a butcher, gentlemen. Butchers leave messes. I intend to bring back two living prisoners and one intact chest, and I will pay bonuses accordingly. Dead prisoners pay half. Damaged chests pay nothing."

It was the chest clause that convinced them, I think. Mountain men understand cargo. A man might lie about why he's climbing, but never about what he's carrying.

* * *

The telegraph operator was harder.

Her name was Odile Ferrand, and she worked the relay shed at Corren Junction, a station so far down the company's priority list that her line hummed with more weather than traffic. She was perhaps fifty, built like the furniture she'd inherited, and when I walked into her shed unannounced she did not stand up. She simply rotated her chair a few degrees, the way a gun turret rotates, and waited.

"Miss Ferrand. I need an operator in the field."

"We have a field operator. He's drunk in Brassenford."

"He's drunk in Brassenford because he can't read a gauge tape. I need someone who can take dictation at speed, maintain a portable key and battery in cold, and splice a dead line without being told twice."

"Then you need me," she said, "and I'm not going."

I had prepared three approaches for her. I discarded all three, because I make a habit of discarding plans the moment the ground contradicts them, and her ground contradicted them immediately. She wasn't afraid of me. Fifty-year-old women in relay sheds are the least frightened demographic in the entire empire; they have survived everything already, including, in many cases, husbands.

"Why not?"

"Because the last time the company sent a man up that mountain with a wire key, the mountain kept him." She turned back to her board. "I knew two of them. Halden crew. Good fists, both. Clean senders." Her hand rested on the brass of a relay, absently, the way you'd touch a grave marker. "You want me to climb up there and tap out messages over their bones. Find yourself another ghoul."

I could have threatened her position. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, since no one else will ever record anything true about me: I considered threatening her position, and I did not do it. Partly because a coerced operator sabotages quietly and irrecoverably, and partly because — well. Because.

Instead I put the photograph on her desk.

It had come out of the chest, weeks ago now, in the first inventory. A child on a station platform, squinting into sun, holding a tin locomotive. On the back, in pencil faded almost to nothing: *Halden, spring.* It was not evidence. It was worse than evidence. Evidence convicts; photographs accuse.

"Where did you get this," she said. It was not a question. Questions rise at the end. Hers fell.

"It was in a chest pulled from the flood at Ashgrove's settlement. Along with survey plates from the Halden contract. Originals." I watched her hands. They had gone still on the relay, both of them, as if the line itself had died under her fingers. "The engineer who pulled it out is running for the summit to broadcast those plates to every station on the network. My employer would prefer that not happen. I would prefer—" and here I paused, because the next part was true and I have learned to use truth the way surgeons use blades, precisely and only where needed— "I would prefer the names get where they're going. All of them. Properly. Through channels. With the men responsible standing in a dock when they arrive."

She looked at me for a long time. Operators look at you like that sometimes, as though they're reading a transmission behind your face, checking your checksum.

"You're lying about something," she said finally. "I just can't find where."

"Everyone's lying about something, Miss Ferrand. The question is whether their lies move in the same direction as yours." I tapped the photograph. "Mine currently point at a courtroom. Come and check my work."

She came. She packed her battery, her spare key, two hundred feet of light field wire, and — I noticed, and said nothing — a small oilcloth bundle she handled the way you handle letters from the dead. I did not ask. An operator who brings relics to a mountain is an operator with a reason to reach the top, and reasons, unlike loyalty, survive bad weather.

* * *

We went up the valley road in rain, six guides, one operator, myself, eleven mules, and a collapsible field telegraph kit that cost more than the guides would earn in a year. I mention the cost not from vanity but because it mattered tactically: expensive equipment forces discipline. Men guard what costs. I had learned that from railroads, which are, in the end, nothing but the art of making men guard what costs.

On the second day we reached the burned bridge.

Ashgrove's work. I stood at the near abutment and studied it the way I'd once studied rival surveys — professionally, almost affectionately. The fire had been set at the chord joints, not the deck. Anyone can burn a deck; the company rebuilds decks in a week. Burn the chords and the span drops into the gorge, and the gorge keeps it. That's not arson. That's engineering with a grudge.

"She wanted a night," Tomas said beside me. "Maybe two."

"She bought three." I pointed with my chin at the scuff patterns on the mud below the abutment, the drag marks, the place where a culvert grate had been pried and replaced — badly, in haste, or so I judged at the time. I was wrong about that, as it happens. I record my wrongnesses here because they are rarer than my rightnesses and therefore more instructive. "Culvert's empty. They took the chest and went up the spur. Mules can't follow the incline, so we go light from here. Two days to the brake-house if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then she gains a day, and I gain a lesson in humility, which I'm told is good for a man." I turned to the guides. "Varga takes point. We move at guide-pace, not soldier-pace. I want them found tired, not warned."

Odile, behind me, was watching the burned bridge with an expression I pretended not to see. Grief has a signature, even on strangers. I had seen it often enough in twenty years to recognize it at a distance, and close up, and in mirrors, briefly, at three in the morning, before the lamp goes out.

* * *

Here is what I have never told anyone, and what this account exists, I suppose, to hold: I did not take the Halden contract expecting it to end the way it ended.

I was thirty-one. I had a surveyor's license and a supervisor's contempt for sentiment, and the company gave me a deadline carved in penalties. The rock was wrong — everyone knew the rock was wrong, the cores had said so for a month — and the honest options were abandonment, reroute, or a delay that would have eaten the contract whole and my career with it. There was a fourth option. It was presented to me by men whose names are still in ledgers, still respectable, some of them still alive, still giving money to churches. They presented it in numbers, the way cowards always present things. Tonnage of explosive against tonnage of delay. Two hundred lives rendered as a rounding error against a fiscal quarter.

I want to be precise here, because precision is the only virtue I have left: I did not refuse. I did not hesitate long enough for refusal to be possible. I lit the fuse myself because I did not trust anyone else to time it correctly, and because — this is the part that has kept me awake for twenty years, the part I dress up in competence and ledgers and clean trials — some small, unforgivable part of me wanted to be the man who solved it. Who beat the rock. Who met the deadline.

Two hundred workers went into that tunnel on a Tuesday morning. The mountain closed behind them like a book.

And the company pinned it on a surveyor named Ede Kessler, who had objected in writing, loudly, repeatedly, and who had the poor judgment to be right in a way that was inconvenient. Kessler ran. Kessler vanished. And I signed the report that buried him alongside the workers, because the alternative was joining them, and I was thirty-one, and thirty-one feels immortal and terrified in equal measure.

Every choice since has been maintenance. That is all my career has been — the maintenance of a single catastrophic decision, patched and repainted and defended, year upon year, until the maintenance itself became a life. When I heard that a chest had come out of the river bearing the Vellmark seal and Kessler's instruments, I did not think: *the past returns.* I thought, with a clarity that still shames me: *at last. It ends here, one way or the other.*

I meant it to end with the chest in a furnace and Ashgrove in a cell. I have begun, climbing, in the rain, listening to Odile's battery jars slosh against the mule packs, to suspect it may end otherwise. Suspicions are dangerous. I keep mine in the same calfskin ledger as everything else, and I do not let them out except at night.

* * *

On the fourth day we found the brake-house.

Empty. Cold stove, two bedrolls gone, but on the wall — Tomas found it, and called me over with a strange gentleness in his voice, as though delivering news of a death — initials scratched into the rotting plaster beside a tally of some kind. Long rows of short strokes. I counted them without meaning to. Old habit. Counting is how I pray, if I pray.

Two hundred strokes. And beneath them, in a different hand, smaller, older: *E.K. remembers.*

Kessler had been here. Years ago. Before the vanishing, maybe — or during whatever the vanishing actually was. He had stood in this rotten room and cut two hundred marks into the wall, and then he had gone down the mountain and disappeared, leaving behind an apprentice who grew up to pull his chest out of a river.

"Well," said Odile, behind me. She had read it too. Operators read everything; it's the occupational disease. "Now you know where they're going."

"I always knew where they were going."

"No." She nodded at the wall, at the strokes, at the small stubborn initials. "*They* know where they're going. You just know where they are." She shouldered her wire spool and walked back out into the snow-light, and I stood alone in the brake-house for a moment longer than a competent man should, looking at two hundred tally marks and thinking about deadlines.

Then I went out and gave the order to move, because that is what I do. I give orders. I meet deadlines. I climb.

Behind us, far down the valley, the weather was turning white along the high ridges, early and ugly, and ahead of us two figures were already dots against the scree, gaining the switchbacks, carrying two hundred names toward a wire station that didn't yet know it was about to speak.

I intended to take them alive. I want that understood. Whatever else is written about me afterward — and something tells me a great deal will be written — let it include this: I meant to take them alive.

Or what passes for alive.
