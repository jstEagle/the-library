## 14. The Wire

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in high country after a storm passes. It isn't peace. It's the mountain holding its breath to see what you'll do next.

Mira and I had climbed out of the brake-house at first light with our collars up and our breath streaming behind us like pennants, and by midmorning we'd gained the shoulder of the ridge where the old Halden spur once ran its last desperate mile toward the pass. The rails were gone — pulled up decades ago for the war effort, or for spite, or for whatever reason companies invent when they want a place forgotten — but the roadbed remained, a long gray scar stitched across the scree, and alongside it, sagging between two poles that leaned together like tired old men sharing a secret, ran the wire.

I stopped dead when I saw it.

"You're staring at the line like it insulted your mother," Mira said.

"It might have." I walked to the nearest pole and put my hand against the wood. Wet, but sound. "This is a spur off the main trunk. Company maintenance wire. It shouldn't be live."

"Shouldn't be," she repeated, in the tone of a woman cataloguing all the things I'd said shouldn't be true since we'd met.

I fished the pocket set out of my pack — a little brass-and-bakelite tapper I'd built myself from salvage, back when building things from salvage was the only honest work available to me. The earpiece was cracked and held together with waxed thread. It had never been elegant. It had always worked.

The trick with tapping a live line is patience. You don't cut in. You don't ground yourself anywhere obvious. You clip on, you listen, and you pray to whichever god governs bored telegraph operators that they're running traffic slow enough to read. I found a dry spot under the pole's crossarm, clipped the leads to the wire with all the tenderness of a surgeon and none of the cleanliness, and pressed the earpiece to my head.

Static. The long hiss of an open circuit carrying nothing but weather.

Then, faint as a moth against glass: dots and dashes.

* * *

Reading code through a bad earpiece in the wind is like trying to eavesdrop on a conversation happening one room over during a hailstorm. You get fragments. You fill in the rest from context and hope your imagination is more honest than it usually is.

The first traffic was routine. Waybill confirmations. A complaint about feed prices at some valley station whose call sign I didn't recognize. An operator down the line — Brassenford relay, by the rhythm of him, quick and sloppy, the fist of a man who learned on a school set and never had to send anything that mattered — passing along a weather advisory. Snow above four thousand feet. Calving season early this year.

Mira crouched beside me, watching my face the way people watch a fisherman's bobber.

"Well?" she said.

"Shh." I held up a finger. "It's mostly freight chatter. Nothing—"

Then the rhythm changed.

You can tell when a message matters by how carefully it's sent. The sloppy Brassenford fist slowed down. Cleaned itself up. Each character placed like a stone in a wall. And the preamble — station-to-station, priority flag, request for acknowledgment from every point down the trunk — told me this wasn't waybills.

I closed my eyes and let the code come through.

*ALL STATIONS ALL STATIONS PRIORITY. NOTICE. REWARD OFFERED. TWO THOUSAND CROWNS. INFORMATION LEADING TO APPREHENSION OF FUGITIVE ENGINEER ASTRID ASHGROVE, FORMERLY OF THE HALDEN DIVISION. TRAVELING WITH ONE COMPANION, FEMALE, VELLMARKI. CONSIDERED ARMED AND—*

I pulled the earpiece away from my head like it had bitten me.

Two thousand crowns. My name. My actual name, spelled out letter by careful letter, going down a wire to every station between here and the coast. Not "the Ashgrove engineer," which was the ghost-name the settlement had used for me, half-affectionate, half-euphemism, the name you give someone you've agreed not to look at too closely. Not "a fugitive surveyor, description attached."

Astrid Ashgrove. Formerly of the Halden Division.

Which meant somebody had talked.

* * *

"What is it?" Mira said. She'd read my face before I'd read the wire, or maybe she'd just read the way I yanked the earpiece off. "What did they say?"

"They know who I am." My voice came out flat, which surprised me. I'd expected it to shake. "Full name. They're offering two thousand crowns. They know about you, too — 'one companion, female, Vellmarki.'" I laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry little bark with nothing behind it. "They even got my old division right. That's not a description. That's a personnel file."

Mira took the earpiece from me without asking and held it to her own head, though of course she couldn't read code — I'd never taught her, hadn't thought to, and now I was cataloguing that oversight alongside everything else I'd failed to think about. She listened to the static anyway, as if the wire might confess to her directly.

"Who knew?" she asked.

I started counting on my fingers, then stopped, because the list was short enough to hold in my head and long enough to make me sick.

"The settlement. Half of them guessed years ago; old Sefa used to call me 'engineer' when she was drunk, and drunk people are the only honest census-takers. Renner knows — he called me 'the Ashgrove engineer' to my face at the crossing, which means he knew before he ever left the valley. The exiles at the shrine camp—" I paused. "Some of them. Maybe."

"And?"

"And you."

The word sat between us on the cold roadbed like something dropped.

Mira didn't flinch. I'll say that for her. She looked at me with those flat gray eyes of hers, the ones that had watched me across a ferry crossing and decided I was worth following, and she said, "You think I sold you."

"I think two thousand crowns buys a lot of passage out of a country that erased yours. I think you've spent your whole life hunting this chest, and I'm the only thing standing between you and it. And I think—" I made myself finish, because cowardice was the one luxury I couldn't afford anymore. "I think I've known you three weeks. That's not trust. That's proximity."

For a long moment the only sound was wind moving through the sagging wire, a low harmonic, the whole line humming like a plucked string nobody had touched.

Then Mira said, "If I wanted to sell you, engineer, I wouldn't need the company. I'd have taken the chest at the culvert while you were sleeping, and you'd never have known until you woke up in a culvert with no chest." She handed the earpiece back. "Your wire. Your ghosts. But don't hang them on me. I've carried enough false accusations for one lifetime — my people wrote the book on it."

She stood and walked ten paces down the roadbed and stood looking north toward the pass, arms folded, radiating the specific fury of the wrongly suspected, which is hotter than ordinary fury and burns longer.

And I sat there under the leaning poles with the earpiece in my hand, feeling the accusation drain out of me almost as fast as I'd poured it, leaving something worse behind: clarity.

Because she was right. If Mira had wanted to betray me, the culvert ambush was her moment, and instead she'd been standing next to me in the flood-water with a rope in her hands. And if the exiles had talked, Renner would have come up the glacier with thirty men, not sent word ahead like a gentleman. No. This had the smell of something smaller and older.

Sefa. Old Sefa, who'd fed me stew every winter for nine years and called me engineer when she was drunk. Or her nephew, who ran goods over the ridge and always asked too many cheerful questions. Or any of a dozen kind, tired people who'd sheltered me without ever quite agreeing to lie for me, because kindness has limits and two thousand crowns is a very persuasive number when your roof leaks.

That was the thing about invisibility. I'd spent nine years believing it was a wall I'd built. It wasn't. It was a debt I'd borrowed against other people's discretion, and the interest had just come due.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about being betrayed, or something adjacent to betrayal, or whatever you call it when the world finds out your name: the sting fades faster than you expect, and what it leaves behind is arithmetic.

I clipped the earpiece back on. Because if the wire was talking about me, the wire would keep talking, and a live line is the cheapest intelligence network ever devised. All you need is patience and a good ear and the willingness to sit still in the cold, and I'd had practice at all three.

The reward notice repeated twice more — different operators, different fists, the message propagating down the trunk like ink through water. Then came the interesting part.

Brassenford relay, the sloppy-fisted operator, sending to a station upline — Corren Junction, I guessed, from the routing. And the content was logistics. Men. Supplies. A requisition for climbing rope, pitons, cold-weather rations for eight, and — this was the detail that made my stomach drop through the roadbed and into the mountain — *one field telegraph set, portable, with linemen's splicing kit.*

Renner wasn't just chasing us anymore. He was wiring the mountain behind us. Every station, every junction, every working spur between here and the summit would be an ear pointed in our direction. He'd turned the railway itself into a net, and the railway was the one thing in this world I had ever loved, and he was using it against me, and there was a poetry in that so bitter I nearly laughed out loud.

Eight men. Rope and pitons. He meant to come up the high route himself, or send men who could. Which meant guides. Which meant he'd hired locals, which meant the guides would know the routes, which meant—

Which meant the Closed Lines Map in my pack was worth exactly as much as the time it bought us. No more.

I keyed nothing. I wanted to — God, I wanted to tap out something obscene on that line, sign it with my own name, let every operator from here to the coast choke on his tea. Nine years of silence makes a person hungry for noise. But discipline is just hunger wearing a uniform, so I unclipped the leads, coiled them, and put the set away.

Mira had come back while I listened. She stood over me, and whatever had been in her face on the roadbed had cooled into something more useful than anger.

"So," she said. "Someone talked."

"Someone always talks. That's the first law of hiding places." I shouldered the pack. "Second law is that you find out too late to do anything about it except climb faster."

"Does it change anything? The plan?"

I looked north. The pass was still hidden behind the shoulder of the ridge, but I could feel it the way you feel a bruise — a pressure, a weight, a place where the world had been struck and remembered. Somewhere up there was Halden Station, and the tunnel, and the names scratched into the wall, and the truth sitting in the dark like a coal that refused to go out. Somewhere below us was Renner, patient as a ledger. And strung between us, humming in the wind, was a wire that now carried my name in both directions.

"No," I said. "It changes everything and it changes nothing. They knew who I was before, they just hadn't admitted it. Now the paperwork's caught up with reality." I tightened the strap another notch. "But it does clarify something."

"What?"

"Who still matters." I nodded down-slope, toward the valley, toward the settlement I'd never see again. "Nine years I told myself I was protecting them by staying invisible. Turns out they were protecting me, and one of them couldn't afford the price. I can't even be angry. Two thousand crowns is two years of wages down there." I looked at Mira. "You matter. The chest matters. The names matter. Everything else is just weather."

Something moved behind her eyes — not softness exactly, but the thaw that precedes it, the first wet gleam on a frozen surface.

"That's the closest thing to an apology I'm getting, isn't it."

"It's the closest thing to an apology I've issued since 1909. Cherish it."

The corner of her mouth twitched. On Mira, that was a standing ovation.

We climbed.

* * *

By late afternoon the roadbed ended where the old spur met the first of the switchbacks above the tree line, and beyond that there was only the mountain: talus slopes the color of old bone, snow patches holding on in the shadows like stubborn debts, and above everything, the glacier's white shoulder catching the last light so brightly it hurt to look at.

We made camp in the lee of a rockfall, and while Mira coaxed the little stove alive — she was better with fire than I was, another entry in the long ledger of ways she was more competent than me — I took out the map and studied it by the stove's orange light.

The route ahead crossed the glacier's tongue. Mira had described it at the brake-house: crevasse country, blue ice under thin snow, the kind of ground that forgives nothing and forgets less. Beyond it, if the calving had opened the high route, the week-long window to the summit. Behind us, somewhere down in the gathering dark, eight men with rope and pitons and a portable telegraph set, wiring the mountain shut behind us one station at a time.

Two thousand crowns. I turned the number over in my mind. When I'd been chief assistant on the Halden division, I'd earned eleven hundred a year and thought myself rich. Now I was worth two thousand, dead or preferably alive, and the market had spoken: I was worth more caught than employed. There was a lesson in there about how companies value engineers, and I resolved to mention it at my trial, if I lived to have one.

"Astrid." Mira's voice, quiet, from across the stove.

"Hm."

"When we reach the summit. When you send your names down the wire." She poked the coals. "They'll be listening. Renner. The company. Everyone. Whatever you say will go out over their lines, through their stations, past their operators. You understand that. Your testimony will travel on their property."

I hadn't thought of it that way. Or rather, I had, and I'd filed it under problems-for-later, and now later was sitting across the fire from me with soot on her knuckles.

"Good," I said.

She looked up. "Good?"

"Let them carry it. Let every mile of their precious wire do the work. They built the lines to move freight and orders and lies. They can move the truth for once — it weighs less than coal and it pays better dividends." I rolled the map closed. "Besides. A wire doesn't care whose hand is on the key. That's the whole beauty of the thing. It was the first honest institution the railway ever built, and nobody noticed."

Mira considered that for a while, feeding sticks to the fire.

"My grandmother used to say the mountains remember everything and tell no one," she said finally. "Maybe she was wrong. Maybe they were just waiting for someone to bring a key."

High above us, out on the glacier's shoulder, something boomed — distant, deep, a sound like a cathedral door closing. We both went still. The calving had begun, days early, ice letting go of ice in the warming dark.

The window was opening. And somewhere below, so was the hunt.

I banked the fire small and lay back against my pack with the earpiece coiled beside me like a sleeping snake, and the last thing I thought before sleep took me was that tomorrow we'd step onto the glacier, and the mountain had already started keeping score.
