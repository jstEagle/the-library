## 15. Crevasse Country

I have crossed the Corren Range eleven times in my life. I have been shot at twice, arrested once, and married for four months to a man who turned out to be an informer, and none of those experiences frightened me the way the glacier's tongue does when you first step onto it.

The trouble with ice is that it lies beautifully.

From the valley floor, the glacier looks like a road — a broad white tongue lolling down from the high col, smooth as poured milk, practically begging you to walk it. Every year some fool from the lowland towns looks up at that tongue and thinks: *there's my shortcut.* And every year the mountain collects its toll. The Vellmarki have a word for the people the ice takes. We don't say it aloud, because saying it is an invitation, but we carve it into the shrine-stones all the same: *the ones who trusted the surface.*

"Rules," I said, turning back to Ashgrove before we stepped on. "You don't move until I tell you. You don't stop where I don't stop. You rope up exactly as I show you, and if I shout a word — any word — you throw yourself flat and hold."

"Hold what?"

"The rope. Me. Your own opinion of yourself. Whatever's nearest." I was feeding the line through my hands as I talked, tying the harness loops the way my grandmother had tied them, the way her mother had, back when Vellmark still existed on paper and our family guided survey crews across this same ice for wages instead of survival. "And you don't argue. Arguing is for solid ground."

Ashgrove looked at the glacier, then at me, with that expression they wear like a coat — the one that says *I was an engineer, I understand load-bearing structures.* "Glacier's just a river," they said. "Slow one."

"A river that remembers being a mountain," I said. "Get that wrong and it reminds you personally."

To their credit, they shut up and let me tie them in. Their hands were steady. I noticed that. I'd spent two weeks by then cataloguing Ashgrove's habits the way you catalogue the habits of anyone whose life might end up braided into yours by forty meters of hemp: how they checked knots twice but never three times, how they ate standing up, how they went quiet — not sullen, just *inward* — whenever the pass came into view through a break in the trees. They were afraid of Halden Pass. Not of dying there. Of something worse than dying, which told me everything about what was in that chest.

We stepped onto the ice at first light, when the surface was still frozen hard enough to hold a boot print without slumping.

* * *

The morning was, I will admit, magnificent. There are mornings up there that make even exile feel like a kind of wealth. The sun came over the eastern shoulder of the range and set the whole glacier burning white-gold, and the peaks above us stood up out of the world like the shoulders of something sleeping. Ashgrove stopped once — just once, against my rules — and stared north along the line of the ice toward the col.

"That's the route," they said. Not a question. They'd drawn it themselves, on that mad beautiful map of theirs, every dead spur and abandoned incline in the Correns inked in a hand so precise it looked printed.

"That's the route," I agreed. "Three days on the tongue, one day up the icefall if the calving's opened it, then the col. If the weather holds and the mountain's feeling generous."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we learn what my people learned twenty years ago," I said, "that the world doesn't owe you a window just because you need one."

They didn't answer that. But I felt the rope go taut and slack again in a small rhythm, and I understood they were testing it, the way you test anything you're going to have to trust your weight to. Engineer to the bone. Everything measured, everything proofed against failure. It should have annoyed me. Instead it did something I didn't have a name for yet.

For six hours the crossing was almost pleasant. The glacier's lower tongue was old, consolidated ice, gray-blue under its dusting of snow, seamed with melt channels that sang quietly as water found its way down into the dark. Ashgrove walked well — better than I'd expected from someone who'd spent years hiding in a river valley. They read the surface the way I did, though they'd never admit the similarity. Where I saw the faint depressions that meant air beneath, they saw the sag lines; where I watched for the dirty bands of rockfall debris, they watched for the stress fractures feathering out from the lateral moraine. Twice they pointed at something before I'd said anything, and twice I pretended not to be impressed.

It was on the third such occasion that I made my mistake.

Not a big mistake. A human one. I was watching Ashgrove watch the ice, and thinking — with something dangerously close to warmth — *maybe this one won't die stupidly after all,* and in that moment of softness I took my eyes off my own feet.

The snow bridge gave no warning whatsoever.

One stride it held me. The next, the world rotated, white became blue became black, and I dropped.

Here is what nobody tells you about falling into a crevasse: it is very fast and very quiet. There's no dramatic sliding, no scrabbling. The bridge simply stops existing, and gravity does the rest, and the last thing you see from above is a hole in the world shaped exactly like you. Then cold air rushing up past your ears, the rope snapping taut with a crack like a rifle, and the jolt that tries to separate your shoulder from its socket — and then hanging.

I hung.

Forty meters of rope between me and Ashgrove, who had done exactly what I'd told them to do, which was throw themselves flat and hold. I could hear them up there, breath sawing, boots scraping for purchase on the lip, and below me was nothing — not darkness exactly, because light came down the shaft in pale columns, but a depth my eyes refused to finish measuring. The walls of the crevasse were that deep glacier blue that doesn't exist anywhere else on earth, blue like the inside of a wound, blue like the hour before dawn on the worst day of your life.

"Mira!" Ashgrove's voice, cracked with adrenaline. "Mira, talk to me!"

"I'm here." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Rope's holding. Don't come to the lip. Do you hear me? Stay flat, spread your weight, elbows and knees wide."

"What do I do?"

What did they do. What did the lowland surveyors' guides always do, the ones my grandmother trained, the drill she'd made me recite before I was ten years old, kneeling on the kitchen floor of a house that would soon be another country's? *Anchor first. Always anchor first. A rescuer who isn't anchored is just a second victim.*

"Behind you there's a moraine ridge — rocks, dirt, anything that isn't ice. Take the spare coil off your pack. Wrap it around the biggest rock you can find, three times minimum, and clip the rope to it. Then get behind the anchor and pull."

Silence, except for wind. Then the scrape and clatter of them doing it, and I confess I closed my eyes for a moment and let myself hang in the blue dark and feel — not fear. Fear had come and gone already, quick as a door slamming. What I felt was older and stranger. My grandmother's hands over mine on the rope. The smell of the shrine candles. Two hundred names, and somewhere under the ice of this same range, men who'd gone into a tunnel alive because a company man decided a schedule mattered more than a heartbeat.

*I am not going to die in a hole in Vellmark's bones,* I thought. *Not before the reckoning.*

"Anchor's set!" Ashgrove shouted.

"Good. Now listen carefully, because I'm only going to explain this once and you're going to do it perfectly, engineer." I spat the last word out and immediately regretted the taste of it. "You're going to haul. Slow, steady, hand over hand, and you're going to keep your body low and your weight against the anchor. If you feel the anchor shift, you stop and you shout. You do not be a hero. Heroes end up in holes."

"How deep are you?"

I looked down. The light columns reached maybe ten meters further and then gave up. "Deep enough. Haul."

* * *

It took them eleven minutes. I know because I counted, the way you count when hanging in the dark is the only thing left to do. Eleven minutes of the rope creaking, of snow and grit trickling down the shaft to patter on my shoulders, of watching the blue walls slide upward centimeter by centimeter while my boots braced against the ice and my arms burned and somewhere above me a disgraced railroad engineer who owed me nothing hauled me out of the mountain's throat with nothing but stubbornness and decent knot-work.

When my head cleared the lip I rolled sideways onto solid ice and lay there gasping, staring up at a sky so clean and indifferent it was almost funny.

Ashgrove knelt beside me. Up close their face was gray, and their hands — I noticed this with a strange, detached clarity — were shaking now, when they hadn't shaken during the fall or the hauling or any of it. Shaking only now, when it was over and safe to shake.

"You fell," they said.

"Observant."

"The bridge just—" They gestured helplessly at the place where I'd vanished, a smooth white field with a ragged hole in it, ringed by the crusted edges of the collapse. "There was nothing. No sign. I'd have walked right over it."

"Yes," I said. "That's rather the point."

"We need to go around. Can we go around?"

I sat up slowly, working my shoulder, which was going to be a spectacular bruise by nightfall. "Around adds two days. Two days puts us at the icefall after the window closes." I looked at the hole. Then at Ashgrove. Then at the hole again, and made the calculation I'd been avoiding since the valley: either we crossed the broken middle of the tongue, probing every stride, or we lost the season and possibly the chest and certainly the reckoning.

"There's a way through," I said. "The old guide route. My grandmother ran survey crews through the broken zone for nine seasons and never lost anyone." I paused. "Well. Never lost anyone twice."

"That's not reassuring."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be accurate." I got my feet under me, tested the ice, and started re-coiling the rope. "New rule. From here to the far side, you lead on the rope and I probe. If I go down again, you're anchored and I'm lighter than you, which means the arithmetic favors us. If you go down—"

"I've seen the arithmetic," Ashgrove said quietly.

We looked at each other across forty meters of coiled hemp, and something passed between us that I didn't have a Vellmarki word for and couldn't find in the trade-tongue either. It wasn't trust. Trust takes longer than a morning. It was more like — recognition. The moment when you realize the person on the other end of the rope has exactly one incentive in the world that matters right now, and it's you.

My grandmother used to say you never truly know someone until you've hung from them.

* * *

The broken zone took us the rest of the day.

It was country from a fever dream — seracs leaning like drunkards, melt channels cutting blue canyons you could drop a wagon into, and everywhere, everywhere, the bridges: snow roofs stretched over emptiness, some thick enough to bear a horse, some thin as pastry. I probed ahead with the pole, striking the ice in a rhythm my hands remembered better than my head did — *two steps, strike, strike, listen* — teaching Ashgrove the sound of solid ice versus hollow. *Dum. Dum. Dum-dum.* Solid ice answers like a struck post. A bridge answers like a drum skin, and a thin bridge answers like a lie.

"Dum," Ashgrove repeated, striking. "Dum. Dum-dum." A pause. "That one's a lie."

"That one's a lie," I confirmed, and we detoured eight meters east around a patch of snow that looked identical to every other patch of snow, and I caught myself almost smiling.

We roped at fifteen meters through the worst of it — close enough to reach each other, far enough not to share a grave. And somewhere in the long white afternoon, conversation started. I don't remember who began it. That's the strange thing about rope teams; the rope does something to silence, stretches it thin until it breaks and words come through.

Ashgrove asked about the shrine-stones. I told them about the valley camps, the names carved in slate, the way the exiles' children learned to read from the lists of the dead because those were the only books we were allowed to keep. Ashgrove listened the way engineers listen — completely, silently, filing everything — and then, near dusk, said:

"When we get to the summit station. When I put the surveys on the wire." A pause, the probe striking ice. "Your elders want the chest buried. You heard them at the camp."

"I heard them."

"If I broadcast, Vellmark gets its reckoning — but公开, public, messy, in courts run by the countries that erased you. If we bury it, you keep it clean. Yours. Until there's a Vellmark to use it." Another strike. "Which do you actually want?"

I walked four strides before answering, which in probe-rhythm is a long time.

"My grandmother died waiting for a Vellmark to use it," I said finally. "I'm not willing to wait for permission from a country that doesn't exist. The dead don't care whose flag flies when the truth comes out. They just want it *out*." I struck the ice. *Dum.* "Broadcast it. Burn the whole rotten ledger down. And then—" I struck again. *Dum.* "And then build something worth carving names into."

Ashgrove said nothing for a while. But that evening, when we camped in the lee of a serac and split the last of the flatbread, they gave me the larger half without comment, and I took it without comment, and that was its own kind of treaty.

* * *

We made the far side of the broken zone as the light was failing, and I'll say this for the mountain: it has a sense of theater. The sun dropped behind the western peaks and lit the whole glacier rose-red, and the crevasse that had nearly eaten me threw one long shadow across the snow like a signature on a confession.

Ashgrove stood looking back at it, rubbing their rope-burned palms.

"You saved my life today," they said. "Both directions."

"You anchored correctly. That's half of it."

"The other half?"

"The other half is that you hauled for eleven minutes without stopping, and most people would have stopped." I fed the rope into its coil, hand over hand, and found that my own hands had finally stopped shaking. Somewhere in the day's work, without my noticing, the fear had changed shape. It had gone in as fear for myself, hanging in the blue dark. It had come out as something with a different address — fear *for*, aimed outward, at this stubborn, careful, guilt-ridden engineer and their impossible chest and their two hundred names.

Loyalty, my grandmother would have called it. She believed it could be manufactured in a single afternoon, given sufficient shared danger. I'd always thought that was sentimentality. Standing on the safe side of the ice with the rope coiled warm in my hands, I revised my opinion.

"Tomorrow we hit the icefall," I said. "If the calving opened it early, we climb. If it didn't, we wait, and Renner gains on us, and everything gets worse." I looked north, where the col glowed faintly in the last light, a notch of gold between two walls of shadow. "So tonight we pray to whatever gods listen to exiles and fugitives."

"Do you pray?"

"I carve," I said. And that night, on a flat stone at the edge of camp, with Ashgrove pretending not to watch, I carved the eleventh name I'd added to my own private list since leaving the valley — not a dead man's name this time, but a living one, scratched small and crooked in the corner of the stone where only someone who knew to look would ever find it.

*A. Ashgrove.*

The mountain keeps score. So, now, did I.

We were breaking camp before dawn when the sound came rolling down the glacier from the heights — a deep, cracking boom, then a second, then a long rumbling roar that we felt through our boot soles before we heard it properly. Ashgrove's head snapped up. Mine did too, and my heart went with it, because I knew that sound, and I knew what it meant, and it was impossible.

Impossible, and happening.

"The icefall," I breathed. "It's moving. Ashgrove — the calving's starting *early*."
