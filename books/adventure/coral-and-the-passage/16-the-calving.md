## 16. The Calving

The glacier announced itself before we saw it — a sound like a door slamming somewhere under the world, then a long groan that traveled up through our boot soles and settled in the teeth.

Mira stopped on the moraine ridge and held up a hand. Not for silence. For reverence. I'd learned to read her hand signals over six days of crevasse country: *rope*, *ice*, *stop*, *you're an idiot*. This one was new.

"Listen," she said.

"I'm listening."

"No. Listen with your bones."

I did. Under the wind there was a pulse in the ice, slow as a sleeping heart, and beneath that a ticking — thousands of small stresses releasing, like a house settling, if the house were four hundred feet thick and older than every nation on the map.

"That's it opening," Mira said. "The tongue. It's been holding all winter, and now the meltwater's eating it from underneath." She looked at me, and for once there was no calculation in her face, only something rawer. "We're early. Or it is."

* * *

We'd come down off the spur at dawn, following the dead line's last survey stakes until the mountain gave up pretending to be railway country and became simply ice. The glacier's tongue lay below us like a fallen cathedral nave — blue-white, fractured, running two miles from the calving face down to a lake of meltwater the color of skim milk. Beyond the face, if the maps in my head were honest, the high route climbed through the notch the calving would open: a week of passage, maybe less, before the summer melt made the seracs above it shed like a molting bird.

I'd planned this crossing around that window the way other men plan around tides. My whole map of the closed lines had one route through the Correns that didn't end in a collapsed tunnel or a washed-out bridge, and it ran through this notch, and it was open only when the glacier decided to be generous.

Engineers hate working with clients who don't negotiate.

"Camp here?" I asked.

Mira considered the light, the wind, the particular quality of the ticking in the ice. "Here. And tonight, probably, we don't sleep."

"Why not?"

"Because when it goes, it goes fast, and I want to see which way the wave runs." She was already driving her ice axe into the gravel of the moraine, pitching camp with the brisk economy of someone who'd done it in worse places. "My grandmother saw a calving on the Vellmark coast once. She said the sea stood up to look at itself."

"Your grandmother sounds like she exaggerated."

"She was Vellmarki. Exaggeration was all they left us." She said it flatly, without the bitterness that line would have carried a week ago. Somewhere between the frozen body with the pay chit and the ruined station at Halden, between my initials-hunting and her name-telling, the sharp edges between us had worn down to something almost companionable. Neither of us had said so out loud. Saying so felt like it might jinx it, and I'd had enough things jinxed for one lifetime.

* * *

We spent the afternoon doing what mountaineers do instead of admitting they're waiting: fussing with gear. I re-coiled rope that didn't need coiling. Mira re-waxed her boots twice. I took out the compass — my father's, brass-cased, the needle still true after everything — and shot bearings across the lake just to have numbers to write down. Numbers are how I pray. I've never been able to manage the kneeling part.

Around mid-afternoon the ticking changed key.

Mira heard it first. She always heard things first; twenty years of listening for footsteps behind you will do that. She came and stood beside me at the moraine edge, close enough that I could feel the cold coming off her jacket.

"It's going to be soon," she said. "Hours. Maybe less."

"The route — can you tell yet if the notch opens?"

She shook her head slowly. "Not until it happens. The face either calves clean and drops the ice bridge below it, or it hangs and we climb around the east seracs and lose three days and maybe some fingers." A pause. "Or it calves wrong and buries the whole approach and we go home."

"We're not going home."

"You say that like it's decided."

"It was decided the day I pulled that chest out of the river." I watched the face of the glacier, a sheer wall of compressed centuries, streaked with gray moraine bands like the strata in a cut bank. "Everything since has been logistics."

Mira snorted. "You engineers. You think everything's logistics." But she didn't argue, and I noticed she'd stopped arguing with me about the chest somewhere around the crevasse that nearly ate me. Saving my life buys a person a certain standing in your councils. I hadn't thanked her properly. There's a kind of thanks that's too big for words and too awkward for anything else, so you just keep walking next to somebody and hope they understand.

* * *

Evening came down the valley like water filling a basin. We ate cold oats and harder bread. The light turned the glacier rose-gold, then ash, then a deep bruised blue that seemed to generate its own dim luminescence, the way snow does at dusk. The ticking grew louder. Once, a block the size of a barn sloughed off the lower face and hit the lake with a boom that rolled back and forth between the valley walls for a full minute, and the wave it sent across the milk-colored water lifted the shore ice and set it chiming against itself.

"That was nothing," Mira said. "That was it clearing its throat."

"How big will the real one be?"

She looked at the face — four hundred feet of it, maybe more, hanging over water sixty feet deep. "Big enough that we should move camp back another fifty yards."

We moved camp back another fifty yards.

I want it recorded that I moved camp because it was sound engineering practice and not because Mira told me to. History may judge otherwise.

* * *

It happened in the dark, which nobody expects. You'd think a thing that size would wait for daylight, would want witnesses, would put on its show for an audience. It doesn't care. Glaciers are the only honest thing left in these mountains. They do what they do whether anyone watches or not, which is more than I've ever managed.

I woke to silence.

Not quiet — silence. The ticking had stopped. The whole valley was holding its breath, and I lay in the tent knowing, the way you know a train is going to jump the rails half a second before it does, that the pause was the worst part.

"Mira."

"I hear it," she whispered. "Out. Now."

We came out into starlight. The glacier face stood black against the faintly luminous ice field behind it, and for ten seconds — I counted them, because counting is what I do when I'm terrified, I count rivets and ties and seconds — for ten seconds nothing moved.

Then a crack ran up the face like lightning in reverse, ground to sky, and the world came apart.

I've seen bridges fail. I stood on the Kessel viaduct the day a flood took the middle span, and I thought I knew what large things sounded like when they died. I knew nothing. The face of the glacier did not fall. It *left* — a section of the wall five hundred feet wide simply detached, tilted outward with a terrible unhurried grace, and dropped.

The sound arrived a beat behind the sight, and it wasn't a sound so much as a change in what sound was. It went through my chest and rearranged things. The impact threw a column of spray and shattered ice two hundred feet into the air, white against the stars, and the wave that raced out from the base of the face caught the shore ice and crushed it flat and kept going, climbing the far shore, tearing out scrub pine like weeds.

And then — this is the part I'll tell until I die, and the part no one will believe — the iceberg that surfaced from under the collapse rose higher than the original face. It rolled, slowly, majestically, shedding centuries of compressed blue ice, and where the starlight caught it, it glowed from inside. Blue like nothing. Blue like the color the word was invented for.

Neither of us spoke. What was there? Every word I own suddenly seemed like a tool for smaller work.

The echoes went on for minutes, bouncing between the walls, diminishing, returning, diminishing again, until the valley settled into a ringing quiet. The lake heaved and slopped. Down at the base of the truncated face, where the collapse had scoured everything away, I could see it: the notch. Open. A ramp of fresh-fractured ice rising toward the high route, steaming faintly in the cold, laid out like a road that had been waiting under the surface for someone to ask.

"It opened," Mira said finally. Her voice was rough. "Days early. Astrid—" She stopped. Started again. "My grandmother's sea, standing up to look at itself. That's what she meant."

I nodded, because my throat wasn't taking dictation.

We stood there a long time, two small warm animals on a gravel bar, watching the biggest thing either of us would ever see finish settling into the water. And I'll confess what I felt, because this account doesn't work otherwise: I felt forgiven. Not by any god — I don't keep accounts with gods, they're worse creditors than railways. Forgiven by the scale of it. Whatever I'd done, whatever had been done to me, whatever the chest carried and whatever Renner wanted and whatever waited at the summit — all of it fit inside the palm of this valley, and the valley didn't notice. The mountain had been keeping score, Tomas's pay chit and the two hundred names and my own small avalanche still waiting ahead of me somewhere in the arithmetic. But the glacier didn't keep score. It just let go.

There was a lesson in that, probably. I was too tired and too awed to dig it out. Some lessons you leave buried. I've learned to respect burial.

* * *

Dawn found us packing in record time. The window was open now, but windows close, and this one would close with seracs.

The route up the fresh ramp was better than I'd dared hope — solid fracture faces, good footing, the meltwater already threading silver veins down the new surfaces. By mid-morning we stood at the top of the notch, looking back down the length of the glacier to the lake, where the newborn iceberg sat grounded like a ship run aground, still glowing faintly blue even in full sun.

Mira touched my arm. Pointed east.

Smoke. Thin, deliberate, a signal fire burning on the ridge two valleys over — too regular to be a traveler's cookfire, too visible to be accidental.

"Somebody's announcing themselves," she said.

"Renner?"

"Renner doesn't signal. Renner arrives." Her eyes narrowed. "That's exile smoke. That's *our* smoke."

I looked at the fire, and at the route beyond it — the high traverse, three days to the summit wire station, with the whole story strapped to my back in a coral-painted chest. Ahead of us lay the pass. Between us and it lay a camp of people I'd never met who had been hunting the same truth for twenty years, and who, according to Mira, were not entirely agreed on what to do with it once they had it.

"Well," I said, shouldering the pack. "Logistics."

Mira laughed — actually laughed, short and surprised, the first time I'd heard it — and we started down toward the smoke.
