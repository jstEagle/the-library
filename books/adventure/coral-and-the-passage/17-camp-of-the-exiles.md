## 17. Camp of the Exiles

I had been told about the camp my whole life, in the way you're told about a country that no longer exists — which is to say, constantly and uselessly. My grandmother described it as a smell: pine smoke and barley porridge and wet wool. My father called it a rumor with a fire pit. Aunt Sela said it was where the names went when there was nowhere else for them to go.

It was all of those things, and none of them prepared me for the moment we crested the moraine ridge and I saw it: eleven tents pitched in the lee of a granite shoulder, laundry lines strung with wool dyed the blue of Vellmark's old flag, and at the center, on a shelf of stone facing the glacier, a wall of slate panels covered edge to edge in carved names.

Two hundred of them. The same two hundred. Carved over and over by different hands across twenty years, because apparently grief is a thing this family does in duplicate.

"There," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I intended. "That's it."

Astrid stood beside me with the chest strapped to their back like a coffin they'd grown fond of, squinting against the glare off the ice. "You didn't mention the laundry."

"The laundry is new."

"The laundry is *blue*, Mira."

"I know what color it is." Blue was not a subtle choice. Blue was a flag flown from a clothesline, a declaration stitched in wool by people who had decided, somewhere along the way, that hiding was no longer the point. I felt something twist under my ribs that was either hope or indigestion. With exiles it is always difficult to tell the difference.

A sentry spotted us before we reached the tents — a boy of maybe fourteen with a slingshot and the posture of someone who had practiced looking dangerous in a mirror. He shouted a word in Vellmarki that I hadn't heard spoken aloud since I was his age, and the camp turned toward us the way a body turns toward pain.

Then an old woman came down the slope, moving with the unhurried authority of weather, and every instinct I had inherited from three generations of fugitives screamed at once.

"Aunt Sela," I said.

"Mira Vell." She stopped an arm's length away and looked at me the way you look at a door you'd given up knocking on. "Your grandmother wrote to us. Twenty-two years ago. We wrote back. The letter came back unopened."

"I know. I'm sorry."

"Sorry is a valley word." Her eyes went past me to Astrid, to the chest, to the mud-stained coat cuff where the seal showed through worn thread. Something moved through her face — recognition, then a calculation so fast and cold it made me proud and ashamed in equal measure, because I knew exactly whose arithmetic that was. It was mine. It was the family face. "So," she said. "The chest found its way home after all."

"It found its way to a river," Astrid said. "I pulled it out. Mira did the navigating."

"Sela," I said carefully, "this is Ashgrove. The engineer."

"I know who they are." She said it flatly, and I watched Astrid absorb the blow without flinching, which told me they'd already done their own arithmetic about how this visit would go. "The company's engineer. The one who surveyed the spur lines after. The one who signed the closure orders."

"One of them," Astrid said quietly. "Yes."

Sela looked at them for a long moment, and then she did something I did not expect. She stepped aside. "Come in out of the wind. Whatever else you are, you carried it up the mountain, and the mountain doesn't carry things for people it dislikes."

* * *

The shrine sat above the camp on its stone shelf, close enough to hear the glacier groan in its sleep. Slate panels leaned against a low wall, each one dense with carved letters, some fresh and pale, some weathered to shadow. At the base lay offerings arranged with terrible precision: a rusted lunch pail, a child's boot, a brass whistle gone green with age, a railway lantern with its glass cracked but whole.

Astrid walked the length of it slowly, reading. I watched them read. I have seen people stand before graves; I had never seen anyone stand before a list and take each name personally, the way you take a debt personally.

"You knew some of them," I said. Not a question. Chapter twelve of our shared misery, the station above the tunnel, the initials scratched beside the names. They hadn't talked much since.

"My mentor's handwriting is on the third panel." Astrid touched the carved letters of a name near the top — *Halvard Osten* — and I saw their jaw tighten. "He taught me everything. He taught me to love the mountains. And then he taught me, by dying badly and being blamed for it, what the company does to people who love the mountains too loudly."

"And now you've brought us the proof."

"Now I've brought you the proof."

Sela joined us at the wall, and behind her, quietly, gathered perhaps thirty others — elders in patched wool, young men with glacier-polished hands, women who looked at the chest the way starving people look through a bakery window. A girl no older than ten stood at the front, holding a piece of chalk, and I understood with a lurch that she was here to add names, that adding names was a chore children were raised on, that this was what my childhood would have been if my grandmother's letter had been opened.

"The surveys," Sela said. "Show us."

Astrid unbuckled the straps and opened the chest right there on the stone shelf, in the wind, with the glacier listening. The survey plates caught the light — those beautiful, damning plates, the blast charges marked in red ink, the timing notes, the order signed and countersigned. Thirty people leaned in as one body.

For a long time nobody spoke. Then a man near the back — broad, gray-bearded, with the forearms of someone who had spent decades hauling nets or timber — said, in accented Common, "Burn it."

The word dropped into the silence like a stone into a well.

"Burn it, Dagny?" Sela didn't turn around.

"Burn it." He came forward, and up close I could see he was crying without any of the usual machinery of crying — dry-eyed, jaw set, tears happening somewhere internal. "Twenty years, Sela. Twenty years we keep our heads down, we learn the language in whispers, we carve names in the dark like criminals. And now this stranger walks in with papers, and half of you want to wave them from the rooftops? You know what happens. You know what happened to the last people who waved anything."

"That was a different world—"

"It is the *same* world. It has just gotten better at waiting." He pointed at the plates with a finger that trembled only slightly. "If this goes public, the company buries it. Lawyers, inquiries that go nowhere, and then they come for us — the ones whose names are in their files, the ones who kept the language, the ones who tend this wall. They will call us agitators. They will find reasons. There are always reasons." His voice cracked finally, one clean break. "My grandfather is on that wall. I will not put my granddaughter's name beside his."

"They are already on the same wall," said a voice from the left. A younger woman, sharp-faced, with ink stains on her fingers — a teacher, I guessed, or a forger, professions that overlap more than people admit. "That's rather the point, Dagny. Their names are on the wall because the company put them underground and then put them out of history. These papers put them back *into* history. You want to burn the only testimony the dead have?"

"I want my family alive!"

"And I want them *counted*," she shot back. "There is a difference between surviving and being erased slowly enough that you don't notice."

I stood between them and felt the argument pass through me like current through wire, because both of them were arguing with me, really. Both of them were arguing with the choice I had been carrying up the mountain since the culvert. Burn it or broadcast it. Bury the dead twice, or dig up the living. There was no third option. There is never a third option. That is the joke the mountains tell, and they tell it to everyone eventually.

Astrid broke the deadlock, which surprised me, because Astrid had spent the entire crossing perfecting the art of strategic silence.

"May I say something?" they asked Sela, and waited until she nodded. "I'm not going to tell you what to do with your dead. That's not mine to decide, and frankly, after what my profession did to them, my opinion should be worth less than most." They crouched by the open chest. "But I'll tell you what these papers are, technically. They're engineering documents. Load calculations. Charge placements. Timing sequences. Signed by four men, countersigned by a fifth. They are not a weapon — a weapon needs someone to aim it, and these have been sitting in a river for twenty years aiming themselves at nothing."

"So why bring them up at all?" Dagny demanded.

"Because a document that proves a crime isn't finished until someone reads it aloud," Astrid said. "Until then it's just paper with a grudge. I've lived off the grid for six years telling myself the same thing you're telling yourselves — that staying invisible is survival. It isn't. It's just erasure with better manners." They straightened up. "I won't burn them. I'll be honest about that. But I won't broadcast them over your objections either. This has to be your decision. All of yours."

"Ingenious," I murmured. "Delegate the impossible choice to thirty grieving people and stand there looking noble."

"It's worked reasonably well so far," Astrid murmured back.

Sela raised her hand, and the camp quieted the way water quiets. "We vote tonight, after the meal. Everyone of age. The old way." She looked at me. "You remember the old way?"

I remembered it the way you remember a song from before you could talk — not the words, just the shape of it. Stones in a circle. White for yes, black for no, and everyone watches everyone choose, because the old way held that secrecy in voting was the first small lie that let a nation rot.

"I remember," I said.

"Good." Her eyes narrowed slightly. "Because you'll be voting too, Mira. And so will your engineer. You brought the question up the mountain. You don't get to leave before it's answered."

* * *

The meal was barley porridge and smoked fish and a bread so dark and dense it could have anchored a bridge, and it tasted like my grandmother's kitchen, which is to say it tasted like a place that had been deleted from maps and survived anyway in flour and salt. I ate until I was embarrassed and then ate more, because exile teaches you that embarrassment is a luxury food.

Around the fire, the talk was Vellmarki, and I understood perhaps half of it — the dialect had drifted in twenty years, grown its own words for things like *border patrol* and *resettlement* and *the blue line*, which turned out to mean the clotheslines, which turned out to mean defiance, which meant even the laundry had politics. A boy asked me where I'd learned the language, and when I said *from my grandmother*, he nodded solemnly and said, "The grandmothers keep it alive. The grandfathers keep the grudges." Which got a laugh, and which was, I reflected, the most accurate political analysis I'd heard in either country.

Later, Sela found me at the shrine. The others had gone down; Astrid was asleep in a borrowed tent, or pretending to be, which for Astrid amounts to the same posture.

"You look like her," Sela said. "Your grandmother. When she was young and still believed the letter would reach us."

"She stopped believing?"

"She stopped writing. There's a difference." Sela ran her thumb along a carved name — her husband's, I learned later, though she offered it the way you offer weather, without ceremony. "Do you know why we built the wall up here instead of down in camp? So the glacier can hear it. Old belief. The ice remembers what men forget. When it calves, the elders used to say, it is reciting."

"That's a beautiful superstition."

"It's a beautiful *engineering observation*. Ice under stress fails along its memory lines. Your friend would agree, if your friend ever said anything warm." She studied me. "You've changed, Mira Vell. You came up that slope like a woman delivering cargo. You'll go down like something else, or you shouldn't go down at all."

"And if the vote says burn?"

"Then you burn it, and you live with it, and you come back next spring and help carve the names again, because the wall will still need tending whether the papers exist or not." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Belonging isn't getting your way, child. Belonging is showing up after."

* * *

The vote came at moonrise, in the old way, stones in a circle of lamplight.

Thirty-one voters. Sela counted us in with a verse I half-remembered and mouthed badly. One by one we knelt at the circle and placed our stone. White for broadcast. Black for burning.

I knelt with a white stone in my fist and found, to my genuine surprise, that my hand hesitated. Not from doubt — I had crossed a glacier for this, nearly died in a crevasse for this, defied my own elders' council for this. I hesitated because choosing, in front of everyone, among my own people, for the first time in my life, was heavier than I had budgeted for. Belonging, it turns out, is not a shelter. It is a witness stand.

I set the white stone down.

Dagny set down black. So did nine others. The rest — nineteen whites, scattered among the blacks like snowfall on scree — chose broadcast.

Nineteen to twelve. Not a mandate. A majority, barely, which is the most honest kind.

Sela announced the count, and the camp made a sound I couldn't parse — relief and dread braided together, the sound of people who had just agreed to stop hiding and hadn't yet priced out what that costs. The teacher with ink-stained fingers began to cry. Dagny stood, looked at the circle of stones for a long moment, and then — this is the part I will remember when I am old — he picked up his black stone, walked to the shrine, and set it at the base of the wall beneath his grandfather's name. An offering. An objection filed with the dead, where such things properly belong.

Then he looked at Astrid, who had voted white and looked like they hadn't slept in a week, and said, "Who reads them aloud?"

And Astrid said, "Someone with steady hands on a telegraph key. Someone who knows the mountain lines." A pause. "There's a wire station at the summit above Halden Pass. If the calving opens the high route, and if the line's still live from the valley —"

"If," I said.

"Engineers deal in ifs," Astrid said. "It's the whole profession."

Sela closed the circle with another verse, and the camp dispersed to tents, and I stayed kneeling at the stones a moment longer than I needed to, feeling the weight and the strangeness of having been counted. Somewhere below, the glacier groaned and shifted, reciting.

I was still kneeling when Astrid crouched beside me and said, very quietly, "Mira. The sentry boy — he's gone. Slingshot and all. And there's a lamp moving on the far moraine, down-valley. Moving the way Renner's men move. Like they're being paid by the hour."

I looked up. The lamp bobbed along the ridge, patient as arithmetic.

"Well," I said, standing, my knees cracking like the ice. "At least we won't have to wonder what to do with the morning."
