## 18. Split Counsel

The camp smelled of pine smoke and boiled barley, which is to say it smelled like every camp my grandmother ever described and none of them had smelled of Vellmark, because she'd left at nine years old and memory is a country that redraws its own borders. Still, when I walked in under the antler arch with Astrid Ashgrove beside me and a chest between us, something in my chest did a thing I didn't authorize. Homesickness, maybe, for a home I'd never stood in.

The camp sat in a hanging valley the maps called nothing at all — a fold of rock below the glacier's shoulder where meltwater ran silver over slate and nobody's survey had bothered to name the creek. Eleven tents of patched canvas and sailcloth. A shrine wall of flat stones, each one carved with names, hundreds of them, lichen filling the letters like green ink. And forty-odd people who looked up from their supper fires at the same moment, because word travels faster than feet in a place where everyone watches the approaches.

"Mira." Old Sefa rose first. She was seventy if she was a day, built like a fence post that had decided against falling, and she had been the camp's voice since before I was born. Her eyes went to the chest on Astrid's shoulder and stayed there the way a hawk stays on a rabbit. "You found it."

"We found it," I said.

"You found it." She let the correction sit there, small and heavy as a coin. "And you brought a stranger."

"Ashgrove," I said. "An engineer. The one who pulled the chest out of the river."

"I know who they are." Sefa's gaze moved over Astrid — the mud-stained coat, the river-scoured boots, the particular stillness of a person who has learned that being overlooked is a survival skill. "The whole valley knows by now. The company man put their name on the wire two days ago. Reward offered. Description given." She paused. "It's a good description. Whoever gave it knew them well."

I felt Astrid go rigid beside me. I'd felt it through the rope a hundred times on the ice; I could feel it now without touching them at all. Someone had talked. Someone close. Neither of us said anything, because there were forty people watching and a chest full of dead men's testimony between us, and some conversations you have to have behind a door.

"Feed them," Sefa said to the camp, and just like that we were folded into the evening — bowls pressed into our hands, a fire edge cleared for us, children staring at Astrid's instruments with the frank appetite of kids who've never seen brass that wasn't melted down for bullets. It should have been warm. It mostly was. But underneath the barley and the welcome I could feel the other current, the one that runs through every exile community I've ever known: *what does this cost us, and who pays?*

* * *

They convened the council that night in the long tent, and they let me sit inside the circle, which after eleven years away was either an honor or a trap depending on how the vote went.

There were nine of them around the lamp: Sefa; her brother Davor, who ran the camp's watch rotations and had hands like tree roots; Ilma the schoolteacher, who taught Vellmarki letters to children who would never need them for anything but remembering; three elders whose names I knew from the shrine wall's living end; and two younger ones, Rask and Toma, born in exile like me, who looked at the chest the way I imagined Astrid looked at a broken bridge — as a problem that wanted solving.

Astrid sat outside the circle. They'd been offered a seat and declined it with a nod that managed to be both courteous and immovable. So they sat by the tent flap with their back to a support pole, hat off, hands loose, listening the way engineers listen — like everything might be load-bearing.

Sefa opened the way she always opened: "State what was carried out of Vellmark."

"The surveys of Halden Pass," I said. "Original plates. Instrument logs. The blast charges' placement diagrams, signed. Two hundred workers sealed in the tunnel when it came down, and the company wrote it as ground failure and pinned it on an engineer named Corvin Hale, who vanished the same week." I didn't look at Astrid when I said his name. I'd learned enough on the trail to know that was a wound you don't poke in public. "The documents prove it was ordered. Prove who signed the order. Prove who lit it."

"And the world will care about this because?" That was Davor, not cruelly. He was a man who asked the question everyone else was too polite to ask.

"Because it's proof," I said.

"Proof rots," Davor said. "We are forty people in a hole in the mountains. Vellmark is a word old people use. You want to hand the only true account of Halden Pass to courts and newspapers run by the same governments that erased us from the maps — and what comes back to us? An apology printed on the inside page? A plaque?" He spread his root-hands. "Or worse. The company buys the plates, burns them, and the last testimony dies in a lawyer's safe."

"So we keep it," Rask said quickly. "Bury it here. Under the shrine. When Vellmark is restored — when the recognition comes, and it's coming, the petitions are moving — then we bring it out. Then it lands on a nation's desk instead of a fugitive's conscience."

"When," Ilma repeated softly. "Rask, my love, the petitions have been moving for twenty years. They move like glaciers."

"Then they move like glaciers," Rask shot back, "and glaciers get there."

I heard Astrid shift slightly by the flap. Not standing. Just the sound of someone deciding whether to speak and choosing, for now, not to. I admired that more than I can say. It's a discipline I've never once managed.

"And Ashgrove?" Sefa asked. "What does the engineer say?"

Everyone looked at the tent flap. Astrid considered the floorboards for a long moment, and when they spoke it was quiet and even, the voice of someone reading elevations off a contour map.

"It's not mine to say. The papers belong to the dead and to their families. I carried them this far because carrying them was the only honest thing left in my life. What happens at the top of the mountain—" they glanced at me, "—we agreed to decide together. So I'll say one thing and then shut up. A secret kept for twenty years killed those men twice. Once in the tunnel and once in every year since, every time somebody chose safety over their names. If you bury these plates again, even for good reasons, even with good intentions — you're not protecting the dead. You're doing what the company did. You're just doing it slower, and calling it patience."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside a horse shifted against its picket line.

"That's easy to say," Davor said at last, "for someone the law isn't hunting."

"It's very easy," Astrid agreed. "That's the problem with it."

* * *

They argued until the lamp burned low, and I want to be fair to both sides, because I loved both sides, which is the whole curse of being caught between them.

Davor's side was the side I'd grown up on. Keep it. Protect it. Every exile family had a story about the time they trusted the world with the truth and the world used it for kindling. My grandmother had carried a hymnal across three borders sewn into her coat, and when she finally reached asylum and showed it to an official, the official confiscated it as an "unverified cultural artifact" and she never saw it again. That story got told at every winter gathering of my childhood. *The world takes what you show it.* Bury the treasure deep. Wait for spring that may never come, but wait anyway, because waiting is the one power the powerless actually own.

But sitting in that circle with the plates six feet away, I kept thinking about the shrine wall outside. Hundreds of names cut into stone by lamplight over decades — names of the drowned, the disappeared, the ones who died in foreign mines and foreign wars with foreign names stitched on their shirts. We carved them so they wouldn't vanish. And here was a document that held two hundred names of men who died in *our* pass, on *our* mountain, building a tunnel through rock that belonged to a country that no longer existed — and the counsel wanted to put those names back underground. In the dark. For their own good.

You can't carve a name in stone and then refuse to say it out loud. That's not remembrance. That's hoarding.

"They're right about one thing," I said, somewhere past midnight, when the argument had gone circular and the elders had started doing that thing elders do, which is repeat their strongest point with increasing slowness as though volume were persuasion. "If we give the plates to the courts now, we may lose them. Renner's company has lawyers the way the mountain has rocks. But if we keep them buried, we don't *maybe* lose them. We've already lost them. Twenty years lost, and counting." I stood, because my legs wanted to stand. "My grandmother waited. She waited her whole life for Vellmark to come back so she could go home, and she died in a rented room in Brassenford with the hymnal's absence still sewn into her coat. Waiting is not a strategy. It's just dying politely."

"Mira." Sefa's voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp. "You brought this chest to us. Not the other way around. Remember whose camp this is."

"I remember," I said. "I grew up in camps like it. That's why I know what they're for. They're for keeping people alive until the world changes. They're not for keeping the world the same."

* * *

They voted near dawn.

Nine stones in a circle, white for broadcast, black for burial. I watched each hand drop its stone into the felt-lined bowl the way you watch cards turn. Sefa: black. Davor: black. Ilma — and this one hurt, because Ilma had taught me my letters — black, with an apologetic tilt of the head that somehow made it worse. The third elder: black. The fourth: black. The fifth hesitated so long the lamp guttered, then went black.

Rask: white. Toma: white.

Six to two. Burial. The surveys would stay in the camp, under the shrine, sealed in wax and oilcloth, "until restoration." Until the glaciers gave back the valleys they'd taken. Until, until, until.

"There's a middle way," Toma said desperately. "Copies. We copy the plates, bury the originals, send the copies—"

"Copies can be dismissed as forgeries," Davor said. "That's the first thing any company lawyer will say. Originals or nothing."

He was right. That was the miserable thing. He was right about the law, and Astrid was right about the truth, and the two rights pointed in opposite directions like a switchyard gone wrong.

Sefa turned to me last, formally, the way you address someone whose fate the council has just decided without their consent. "Mira Vell. The chest remains in the camp's keeping. You'll swear to it?"

I looked at the bowl of stones. Six black. I looked at the shrine wall through the open flap, gray in the pre-dawn, all those letters full of lichen. I looked at Astrid, who hadn't moved all night except once, and whose face in the lamplight was doing something I recognized from mirrors: the look of a person watching a decision get made that they will then have to live inside.

Here is what I understood in that moment, standing in my grandmother's people's council while they voted my grandmother's values at me: justice doesn't live in institutions. Institutions are just where justice goes to be processed, delayed, appealed, and occasionally released. The real thing — the raw ore of it — lives in whatever a person does when no court is watching and every lawful option is a lie. The exiles had survived by keeping faith with the dead in private. It was holy work. It was also, tonight, a tomb.

Twenty years ago, men in an office decided that two hundred lives were a line item, and they buried the evidence, and they slept fine. Tonight, nine good people decided that two hundred names should sleep a while longer, and they'd sleep fine too, because their reasons were better. That's the joke the mountain tells. The crime and the mercy can use the same shovel.

"No," I said.

The tent went very quiet.

"I won't swear it," I said. "I'll carry water, stand watch, mend your roofs, teach your children their letters, and defend this camp with my life. But I won't swear to bury the truth, because I watched my grandmother die waiting, and I watched Ashgrove nearly die twice getting these plates here, and I have read every name in that chest, and I am done—" my voice cracked, damn it, in front of the whole council, "—I am *done* letting the mountain keep score in the dark."

Sefa studied me for a long time. Around the circle, faces I loved closed like doors.

"Then you have a choice to make," she said quietly. "As we all do. Sleep on it. The chest goes under the shrine at noon."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat outside by the shrine wall until the sky went from black to iron to the pale bruised pink that means the sun is thinking about it, and I read names by touch, my fingers in the cold grooves of the carving, and I tried to find the version of myself that could walk back into that tent at noon and help lower the chest into the earth and mean it.

I couldn't find her. I looked everywhere. She wasn't there.

At some point Astrid came and sat down beside me without a word, close enough to share warmth, far enough to make no claim. We watched the light come up on the glacier above the camp, turning from gray to white to that blinding, unbearable gold.

"Noon," Astrid said finally.

"Noon," I said.

Neither of us said anything else, because there was nothing else to say that wouldn't be a confession, and confessions keep until noon.

Behind us, in the long tent, the council began to gather. And somewhere below us, down in the valley, I'd have bet my ferry license that a telegraph key was already chattering our names to anyone willing to pay for them.
