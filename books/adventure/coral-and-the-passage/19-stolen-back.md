## 19. Stolen Back

The camp slept the way honest places sleep — badly, in shifts, with dogs that dreamed out loud.

I lay awake in the guest hut listening to it: the wind worrying the shrine ropes, someone's cough three tents over, the particular silence of Mira's breathing from across the fire pit, which was not the silence of sleep but of a woman lying very still and deciding something. I'd spent eleven years learning to read silences. Rivers taught me first — the way water goes quiet just before it takes something — and people taught me after, when I stopped being worth talking to and started being worth watching.

She had brought me here believing the elders would see reason. Or she hadn't believed it, exactly; she'd believed she could manage them, which is a different faith, the kind engineers have about structures they didn't build. And for two days it had almost held. There was soup. There were questions asked gently, in Vellmarki, with Mira translating in a voice that grew flatter each time, as though she were sanding the edges off their words before they reached me. There was an old woman named Dagny who touched the coral paint on the chest and wept without sound, which is the only kind of crying that has ever made me feel like a thief.

Then tonight there was the vote.

I wasn't in the room for it. That was the courtesy they extended me — the fugitive gets to wait outside while his fate is counted on fingers he can't see. Mira came out into the blue dark afterward and stood with her hands jammed in her armpits and told me what I already knew from the shape of her shoulders.

*Buried,* she said. *Until Vellmark stands again. Maybe ten years. Maybe thirty.*

Thirty years. Two hundred men would be dust by then, and so would most of the witnesses, and the company's ledgers would have moldered into illegibility, and Renner would die a respected man with a pension and probably a plaque somewhere. I did the arithmetic standing in the cold and every column came up the same: buried means lost. Not preserved. Lost. A truth in the ground is a rumor with better posture.

I told Mira I understood. I said it kindly, or as kindly as I know how, which is to say I said it like a man reading a safety bulletin aloud. She looked at me a long moment — she has eyes the color of glacier melt, all that blue with grit suspended in it — and then she went to her tent, and I went to mine, and neither of us said anything true.

That was four hours ago.

*

Here is the thing about shame: it doesn't argue you out of anything. It just comes along for the walk, sitting on your shoulder like a crow, commenting.

I'd done this before, technically. Stolen things back. When the company collapsed my career on top of my mentor's name, I stole back my own survey journals from an evidence locker that no longer legally existed, and I told myself it wasn't theft because the papers were mine, which was true, and also because theft requires a victim with standing, which was sophistry, and I knew it even then. Eleven years later I still kept those journals under the floorboards of a cabin I didn't own, and I still couldn't open them without feeling like a burglar in my own life.

But this was different. This time the thing I meant to take belonged to other people — grieving ones, patient ones, people who had waited twenty years in exile and could apparently wait twenty more. People whose whole claim to dignity rested on doing this *properly*, through restoration, through law, through a nation reassembled like a bridge after flood damage. And I was going to take their evidence and run, because I had decided my timeline mattered more than their process.

The crow on my shoulder had a great deal to say about this.

And underneath the crow, quieter, the certainty: *the dead don't have thirty years.* The dead have whatever living mouths will carry them, and every year thins the herd. Dagny was seventy if she was a day. The children in the camp knew Vellmark as a story, not a country. You cannot restore a nation on testimony nobody alive remembers giving. The surveys in that chest weren't a relic. They were a fuse, and fuses don't keep.

I had learned that lesson at Halden Pass, in the worst classroom in the world. Somebody had decided the tunnel was more convenient closed than open, and had chosen a night, and had not consulted the two hundred men inside. The crime at the heart of everything I was carrying was a crime of scheduling. Of deciding that some future benefit outweighed some present population.

If I buried the chest for thirty years, I would be committing a smaller, kinder version of the same act. Weighing lives against calendars. Choosing the tidy outcome over the true one.

So. Shame braided with certainty. I've worn worse hairstyles.

*

I waited until the moon went behind the western ridge, which the old woman Dagny had mentioned at supper with the offhand authority of someone who has farmed weather her whole life — *the moon dies early now, boy, the season's turning* — and I mean it as no disrespect to call her a farmer of weather, because reading sky is engineering too, just with worse tolerances.

I dressed in the dark. Boots unlaced inside the laces, the trick you learn sleeping along rail lines where the night watchmen carry dogs. I took nothing but the knife, the compass, and my coat.

The chest sat in the shrine tent.

That was the part I hadn't planned for. In my head, right up until I lifted the flap, the chest had been an object — brass fittings, coral paint, iron bands gone orange with river rust. But the exiles had made it a reliquary. Someone had laid a cloth beneath it, woven in colors I didn't have names for, blues and rusts and one thread of gold that caught even the starlight. Around it, small offerings: a tin cup, a child's carved horse, a sprig of some mountain pine I couldn't identify, dried to lace. Candles burned at the corners, stubby things guttering in the draft, and the light moved on the chest's lid so that the crest seemed to breathe.

Vellmark's seal. The double mountain and the river between. The same stamp my mentor wore on a chain around his neck for the nine years I knew him, and never once explained, and I had been too young and too eager to ask, and asking questions is the entire job of an engineer, and I failed at it, and men died, and here I was.

I knelt. Not for drama — there was nobody to perform for — but because the geometry of getting a heavy chest off a low table quietly puts you on your knees anyway, and once I was down there it seemed dishonest not to admit what the position meant.

*I'm sorry,* I told the candles. To the camp, really. To Dagny and the carved horse and the gold thread. *I'll bring it back. I'll bring it back into daylight, which is the only place it ever belonged.*

The candles flickered. Wind, probably. I'm an engineer; I don't get to read omens. But I let myself pretend for one second, the way you let yourself pretend a bridge will hold right up until you've checked the third time.

The chest weighed maybe forty pounds dry, and it wasn't dry — the river had gotten into it before I did, and the survey plates inside had swelled in their sleeves, and every step it clunked against my hip like a second heartbeat with opinions. I got it out of the shrine tent, across the frozen mud of the camp yard, past the dog — who raised his head, considered me with the weary professionalism of an animal who has watched humans do stupid things all his life, and put his head back down, which I choose to interpret as a blessing —

— and then I stopped at the edge of the firelight, because of Mira.

Her tent was between me and the trail. Of course it was. She'd pitched it there the day we arrived, and I'd thought nothing of it, and now I understood it had been nothing accidental. Mira Vell does not do anything accidental. She ferries a crossing that officially does not exist; she navigates a country that officially does not exist; she had spent our whole partnership being precisely where she needed to be while looking like weather.

Her breathing had changed. I could hear it through canvas — slower, deliberate, the breathing of a person performing sleep, or possibly actually asleep and dreaming loudly. I stood there for a full minute, forty pounds of stolen history pulling my shoulder down, and I thought about walking past. About the odds. About how she'd catch me by morning regardless, because she always knew where I was, the way rivers know where the low ground is.

And I thought about what I'd say. Because I owed her that much, and because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving a scene without explaining myself — it's the teacher in me, or the coward, hard to say.

So instead of sneaking past her tent, I did the thing that will probably end me, and went to the shrine tent's little table, and unrolled my map.

*

The Closed Lines Map. My life's work, or the part of it that survived the disgrace. Every abandoned spur, washed-out trestle, flooded adit, and murdered right-of-way through the Corren Range, drawn in my own hand over eleven years of walking, in ink mixed to survive wet, on paper chosen to survive folding. The company believes three of these routes impassable. The company is wrong about two of them, and wrong about the third in an interesting way that only matters in June.

I spread it flat and weighted the corners with the candle stubs, and I drew — quickly, because the moon was dying and my hands were shaking in a way I resented — the route east from the camp. Up the goat track to the shelf, along the shelf to the old survey cairn at the shoulder of Mount Corren itself, then down into the cirque where the meltwater goes, and out. And at the end of the line, where the route surfaces onto the high valley road below the pass, I wrote two words in block letters large enough to read by candlelight:

*SUMMIT WIRE.*

Because that was the decision I'd finally made, the one that had been assembling itself in me since the culvert, since the brake-house, since the frozen body with the pay chit, since the wall of two hundred names scratched above my mentor's initials. I wasn't running anymore. Running was over; it had been over since the night I pulled this chest out of the flood and recognized the seal. I was going to the summit wire station above Halden Pass, and I was going to key every number on those survey plates into a line that still hummed with company current, and I was going to read the names, all of them, out loud, into every station from Brassenford to the coast, and let the mountains testify through the one instrument they'd left standing.

And if Mira wanted to stop me, she could find me there. If the elders wanted to bury the chest, they could dig it out of my hands at the summit. If Renner wanted me — and Renner wanted me, I'd heard the wire, I'd heard my own name priced like freight — he knew where to look too.

That was the point. That was the whole point. I wasn't stealing the truth away from anyone. I was stealing it *toward* everyone, and I was leaving the address.

It's not a defense. I want to be clear about that, in case anyone ever reads this. It doesn't make it not theft. They voted, and I overruled them, and the fact that I think I was right is exactly the fact that makes it theft, because every man who lights a fuse thinks he's right about the schedule. The difference — the whole difference, the difference I intend to spend the rest of my life proving — is that I'm lighting the fuse toward daylight, and I'm signing my name to it, and I'm leaving a map.

Renner never left a map. Renner never signed anything but payrolls.

I folded the map open to its full width so nobody could miss it, weighted it against the wind, set the child's carved horse on top of it — an apology in the only currency the camp might accept — and walked out of the Camp of the Exiles with another family's hope banging against my hip.

Nobody shouted. The dog watched me go with what I swear was pity.

*

The goat track up to the shelf was two hours of climbing I did mostly on pride and spite, because my knees had filed formal objections years ago. The chest rode slung on a rope harness I improvised from the shrine tent's guy-line, which is one more small theft to answer for, and by the top I'd stopped hearing its clunk as separate from my own pulse.

Below me the camp went dark and small, a handful of embers in a bowl of black. Somewhere down there, Dagny was sleeping the sleep of a woman who believed the vote had settled things. Somewhere down there, Mira was either asleep or pretending, and I genuinely could not tell which possibility hurt more.

I let myself feel it for exactly as long as it took to drink half my water. Then I turned east.

The shelf was old rail grade — of course it was; the company had surveyed this whole range once, greedily, before the collapse and the cover-up made the mountains embarrassing — and walking dead grades at night is the closest thing I have to prayer. Your boots learn the rhythm. Tie spacing, ballast crunch, the occasional sleeper gone soft as bread. Eleven years of this. Some men meditate; I walk infrastructure that everyone else agreed to forget.

I made perhaps six miles before dawn greyed the eastern ridges, and I stopped in the lee of the old survey cairn to eat hardtack and lie to myself about how much distance I'd opened.

Because here's what I knew, kneeling there in the freezing pre-light with my stolen reliquary beside me: six miles is nothing. Six miles is a rounding error. Mira Vell had grown up on this side of the range, in the folds of it, in the country the maps erased but the ground remembered. She could move through it the way water moves downhill — without apparent effort and without any possibility of being argued with. If she'd come after me at first light, she'd have my scent by midmorning and my location by noon, and the only real question was whether she came alone, with the elders' blessing, or alone against their orders, or not alone at all.

And God help me, some traitorous part of me hoped she came. Not to take the chest back. Just — came. Walked up over that shoulder with her glacier-melt eyes and her terrible patience and demanded, in that voice she used when she was furious and translating at the same time, to know what I thought I was doing.

Because the plan I'd written on the map was true, but it was also lonely, and I had discovered somewhere in the last hundred miles that I could no longer tell the difference between needing a witness and wanting one.

I shouldered the chest. The sun cleared the ridge and lit the cirque below — a white bowl of ice and old snow, silent as a held breath, with the faintest sound underneath it, deep down, of water moving where water isn't supposed to move yet this early in the season.

The calving window was opening ahead of schedule. The mountain was hurrying.

I started down.

Behind me, far below, a figure came up out of the camp onto the goat track — small at this distance, unmistakable anyway, moving fast and alone, and gaining.
