## 20. Mira's Choice

I left the camp before dawn, which is the traditional hour for doing something you'll have to explain later.

The elders had voted at moonset — eleven hands raised to keep the chest buried until Vellmark could be restored whole, three against, and me standing outside the circle with my arms crossed like a woman who already knew what she was going to do but wanted the dignity of pretending otherwise. Aunt Sefi didn't even look at me when I packed rope and the good ice axe. She just said, "The mountain doesn't care whose side you're on," which is the closest our family comes to a blessing.

I took it as one.

* * *

Tracking Astrid Ashgrove through fresh snow should have been easy. It was not, because Astrid Ashgrove walks like a man trying to apologize to the ground for stepping on it. Every footfall placed to leave the least mark. Every rest stop taken on stone instead of snow. Twenty years of living off the closed lines teaches you to be weather's polite guest, and the result is that by midmorning I was reading a trail that read like a rumor of a trail.

I found other things instead.

A cairn, freshly built, three stones high, beside the track of an old service road that hadn't carried anything but lichen in two decades. Someone had been this way recently and marked it — not for themselves. For whoever came after. A second cairn an hour later, at a fork where the obvious route went down into a gully and the sensible route clung to the slope. The sensible route was marked.

He was leaving me breadcrumbs. Or he was leaving Renner breadcrumbs, and I was about to follow a trap into a gully with my eyes open, which would make me the stupidest exile in the Correns, a competitive field.

I stood at the fork a long time, breathing steam, doing arithmetic. If it was a trap, he'd built it badly — no choke point, no high ground overlooking the gully mouth, nothing an engineer would actually design if engineering were what he had in mind. If it wasn't a trap, then the disgraced railroad man who'd stolen my people's evidence out from under my sleeping body was guiding me after him like a lost child.

Both possibilities made me angry. That's usually how I know I'm on the right path.

I took the sensible route.

* * *

I caught him at dusk on the shoulder below the old Halden switchback, where the mountain flattens into a shelf just wide enough for regret. He'd made camp in the lee of a fallen signal post, a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand, and he heard me coming — of course he did; I'd stopped being quiet out of spite two hours back — because he spoke without turning around.

"You shouldn't have come alone."

"Good evening to you too."

"They voted." Now he turned. Firelight on his face, and under the exhaustion something careful, the way a man looks at a bridge he's not sure will hold his weight. "You defied them."

"I did." I dropped my pack, sat across the fire without asking, because asking would have made it a negotiation and I hadn't crossed a day of broken country to negotiate. "They want the surveys kept secret until there's a Vellmark to give them back to. Eleven old men and women in a hole in the mountains, waiting for a country that exists right now only in their memories and my grandmother's recipes. They could wait forty years. The families of those two hundred workers are waiting now, and most of them don't even know they're waiting. They think their grandfathers died in an accident caused by a traitor named Ashgrove."

"That last part has a certain irony," Astrid said quietly.

"It does. I've been chewing on it for days." I pulled off a glove and held my hands to the heat. "Here's what happens now. You can say no, and I'll walk back down tomorrow, and I'll tell the elders I couldn't find you, and I'll mean it, because I'm done letting other people decide what justice looks like while it spoils. Or you can say yes, and we finish this crossing together — as partners. Not as the exile guarding her evidence. Not as the thief carrying his penance. Partners. We decide at the summit together. Whatever we decide."

"And if we decide differently?"

"Then one of us outvotes the other by walking."

He almost smiled. It got as far as his eyes and stalled somewhere around his mouth, which I've learned is where his smiles go to die. "Mira. What I did at your camp—"

"Yes. Let's do this part."

"—was wrong. Not clever-wrong. Not necessary-wrong. Wrong. I told myself it was for the dead, and maybe some of that was even true, but mostly I told myself a story where taking the choice away from everyone else was the same as making the right choice. I know how that story ends. I've read it before. My mentor wrote it."

"Your mentor," I said, "who they hanged in effigy in every station town from here to the coast."

"Who they blamed for Halden Pass. Who let them blame him, because the truth would have gotten two hundred more people killed — the witnesses, the widows, anyone who'd signed the survey plates. He chose to be the monster so the real ones could stay hidden." Astrid fed a stick to the fire. "And then he disappeared, and I spent twenty years believing I was carrying his shame around, and it turns out I was carrying his evidence. There's a version of me that finds that funny."

"Is that version here tonight?"

"No. Tonight it's just the version that stole your chest and left you a map."

"The map helped," I admitted. It had. When I'd woken to find the chest gone and my pride gone with it, the map pinned under a stone — the route, the timing, *the window opens early, come or don't* — had kept me from doing something permanent and stupid. "It also confirmed you're insufferable. 'Come or don't.' As if I climbed out of a council of my own elders to be given permission to sulk."

"You didn't sulk. You tracked me across a glacier's apron in half a day. I'm choosing to be flattered."

We sat with that a moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere above us, the ice groaned — that long tectonic complaint the glacier makes when the temperature drops, the sound I grew up hearing in lullabies, the sound our exiles call *the mountain counting*. I used to think it counted losses. Lately I've wondered if it counts debts.

"Astrid." I waited until he looked at me. "I'm not forgiving you because you apologized well. Your apology was adequate. Barely. You led with self-flagellation and finished with a joke, which is a coward's structure and I'll thank you to fix it next time."

"Noted."

"I'm forgiving you because you left the map. Because you marked the fork for whoever followed. Because everything you've done since the culvert says the same thing: *I don't trust myself to hold this alone.* And you're right not to. Nobody should hold this alone. That's how Halden Pass happened — one man with a fuse and a ledger and nobody standing close enough to see what he carried." I turned my palms to the fire again. Scars on both, from ropes and ice and one memorable disagreement with a ferry winch. "So. Partners. Say it plainly, because I've had enough of engineers communicating in blueprints."

He was quiet long enough that I started composing the speech I'd give at his funeral. Then he said, "Partners. Decided together at the summit. And Mira — if we get there and you vote to bury it, I'll walk down with you and help carry it."

That surprised me. I let it show, which I almost never do, and which he noticed, which I minded.

"You'd bury it? After all this?"

"After all this, I'd trust you to know when your people are ready. That's the deal. Truth isn't a thing you force open like a door. It's more like—" he gestured vaguely at the black mass of the glacier above us, "—like calving. It goes when it goes. All you can do is be standing in the right place when it does."

"That's a terrible metaphor. Calving kills people."

"So does truth, sometimes. Slowly, though. Usually." He looked up at the ice. "Usually."

* * *

We shared the watch in shifts, which is the first sacrament of partnership as far as I'm concerned — sleep only comes easy to me when someone else is holding the dark. I took midnight to dawn. Around three, with the fire down to coals and the stars sharp enough to cut, Astrid spoke from his bedroll without preamble, the way he does when he's been rehearsing something and finally gives up on the rehearsal.

"My grandmother was a Vellmarki."

I sat very still. "Say that again."

"She never said the word. She called herself 'from over the range' and she cooked things none of the valley wives cooked, and once, drunk on plum brandy at my naming day, she sang a song in a language my father pretended not to recognize. After Halden Pass, after the erasure, she burned every letter she'd ever received. I found the corner of one she missed, years later, cleaning out her things. Just a corner. Enough to see the crest." His voice stayed level, engineer-flat, which is how I knew it was costing him. "The same crest on the chest. On your coat cuff, the day you recognized mine. I've been wondering since the crossing whether the mountain sent me a stranger or a cousin."

"We're not cousins," I said. Then, because lying to a man across a dying fire felt like a debt I didn't need: "But my grandmother had a sister who married a valley man and changed her name and never wrote again. Sefi keeps a place set for her at every winter meal. Forty years of empty chairs." I poked the coals. "There are more of us than the maps admit. Erasure is never as thorough as the erasers hope. People leak. Recipes leak. Songs leak. Grandmothers leak."

"Grandmothers," Astrid said, "are the last uncollapsible structure."

I laughed — actually laughed, out loud, at three in the morning on a frozen shelf of the Corren Range, loud enough that I checked the darkness afterward out of habit. It felt strange in my chest, that sound. Like a door opening in a house I'd boarded up.

* * *

Dawn came gray and clean, and with it came the wind, and with the wind came the smell of snow that hasn't fallen yet but is thinking about it. We broke camp fast, two people who'd done this separately for years discovering they did it identically — he doused the fire while I struck the tent, I coiled rope while he buried the ash, no words wasted, no order negotiated. It should have taken a week to move that smoothly with another person. It took us one night. I decided not to examine why. Some inheritances you just accept.

We gained the ridge by noon, and that's where we saw it.

Below us, maybe two miles east along the flank of the mountain, a thread of smoke rose from the ruins of the old funicular station — thin, disciplined, banked, the smoke of people who know fires are visible and don't care who sees. Renner. He wasn't hiding anymore. He wanted us to know exactly where he was, the way a ledger wants you to know the balance.

Beside the smoke, catching the weak sun, something glinted. Glass. A lens.

"He's got a telegraph operator with glass," Astrid said, flat. "He's signaling the valley stations. Coordinating."

"He's telling them to watch the summit wire."

"He's telling them to watch everything." Astrid stood a long moment, reading the mountain the way he reads everything — lines, loads, tolerances. Then he pointed west, where the ridge bent toward a white shoulder I'd been avoiding with my eyes all morning. "The calving route opens through there. Two days, maybe less if the warm wind holds. But the high route passes directly above that station. He knows it. He's not chasing us anymore, Mira. He's sitting under the only door and waiting for us to walk through it."

"Then we find another door."

"There is no other door. There's the door, or there's turning back, and turning back means the snows close behind us and the chest dies in a culvert until someone else's grandchildren dig it out." He turned to me, and there it was — not the fugitive's cornered look, not the penitent's bowed one. Something else. The look of a man standing in front of a bridge he has decided will hold. "So we go through the door. Together. And we go faster than he thinks possible, because I know this mountain's dead lines better than any man alive, and there's one more trick in this old girl nobody's used in twenty years."

"What trick?"

He smiled — all the way to his mouth this time, which frightened me considerably more than Renner's smoke did.

"The incline railway," he said. "The one they sealed. The one everybody believes fell into the gorge." He shouldered his pack and started walking. "Everybody, Mira, except the woman who drew the map of every closed line in the Correns."

I looked at the smoke one more time, and at the glint of glass beside it, and I followed my partner up the ridge — past the point where the elders' counsel ended, past the point where forgiveness was still a question, into whatever came next.

Behind us, far below, the glacier cleared its throat.

The mountain was counting. I intended to make sure it counted us among the living.
