## 21. The Switchback

The funicular was supposed to be dead.

I had it on my map — inked in the careful hand of a woman who'd spent eleven years cataloguing every corpse of a rail line in the Correns. The Kessel Incline, built in the boom years to haul ore down from the upper valley, abandoned when the ore ran out and the company's attention turned to cheaper mountains further east. I'd walked its lower station once, six years ago. The cables were rusted to red lace. The cars sat in their shed like coffins waiting for occupants who'd never come.

So when Mira stopped on the ridge above the old alignment and said, "Smoke," I didn't think funicular. I thought campfire. I thought we had a day, maybe two.

Then I put the glasses on it and saw the counterweight tower moving against the sky, slow as a minute hand, and felt my stomach drop through the mountain.

"They got it running," Mira said. Not a question. She'd grown up around railways that ate people; she could read one at any distance.

"Somebody did." I passed her the glasses. "Company man wouldn't have the hands for it. He's hired locals. Someone who knew the incline before it closed."

"Someone like you."

"Someone exactly like me," I said. "That's what frightens me."

Because here is the thing about a funicular, and I want you to understand it the way I understood it standing there with the wind pulling at my collar: a funicular is honesty in machinery. Two cars, one cable, a drum at the top. When one goes up, the other comes down, and the mountain itself does most of the work — gravity balanced against gravity, the engine only trimming the difference. It is the most elegant lie-free device ever bolted to rock. And Renner was riding it up our spine while we slogged switchbacks on foot, gaining perhaps four hundred meters of elevation for every hour we spent earning two hundred.

At that rate he'd have us on the glacier by tomorrow night. And the calving window — the one week the high route opened — would open onto an empty summit, because we'd be in irons below it.

"We go faster," Mira said.

"We can't go faster. This is the fast way. That's the whole point of the map."

"Then we stop him."

I looked at her. She said it the way she said everything, flat and even, like reading off a manifest. But her hand was on the ice axe at her hip, and I knew what *stop* meant in her grammar, and it wasn't a polite word.

"No," I said. "Not that."

"Then what?"

And that's when I made the mistake. That's when I looked down at the incline — at the cut it had been blasted through, the steep couloir above it choked with twenty years of unconsolidated snow, the whole slope sitting there like a loaded cart on a grade — and the engineer in me, the part I'd spent eleven years trying to starve, sat up and cleared its throat.

*The slope's at thirty-eight degrees,* it said. *Wind slab over depth hoar. You could see the weak layer from here if you dug a pit. One charge, placed right, at the crown, and the whole face comes down across the lower half of the incline. Track's gone. Cable's gone. They're stranded on foot, days behind.*

*You've done this math before,* it added, helpfully. *You were very good at it once.*

*

We climbed through the afternoon with that arithmetic riding between us like a third passenger. I told myself I was only considering it. Considering is free. Considering doesn't bury anyone.

Mira, to her credit, didn't push. She'd learned something about me on the glacier — or maybe just confirmed something — and she let the silence do its work, which was more than I deserved. Once, crossing a scree field where the old maintenance path had slid away entirely, she said, "In Vellmark, before, there was a general who sealed a garrison into a pass rather than let them surrender. My grandmother sang about him. It was a proud song." A pause. "They don't sing it anymore."

"I noticed you don't sing much."

"I notice things," she said, and climbed on.

By dusk we reached the brake-house at the fourth switchback — not the rotting one we'd slept in on the way up, but a smaller structure, a relay hut for the incline's signal line, squatting under an overhang of granite. Inside: a rusted stove, a shelf of glass insulators gone violet with age, and, bolted to the wall, the terminal box for the incline's detonator circuit.

I should explain that last part, because it matters, and because I need you to know I didn't discover it by accident.

Avalanche control on railway lines used to be routine work. Before winter closures, crews would run charges along the crowns above the cuts to bring the snow down in managed pieces, on schedule, when nothing valuable stood underneath. The Kessel Incline had such a system — a wire loop running up the slope to pre-drilled charge sites, all of it keyed from huts like this one. The company had pulled the explosives decades ago. But wires don't rot as fast as reputations, and when I traced the loop with my fingers in the failing light, it hummed back at me, intact, patient, waiting.

All I needed was a charge. And in the tool locker, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, I found three sticks of dynamite sweating nitroglycerin like an old man sweats fear, left by some foreman who'd meant to come back.

"You're looking at it a long time," Mira said from the doorway.

"I'm inventorying."

"You're doing mathematics."

She was right, of course. She was always right, which was going to be insufferable once we were friends, if we ever got there. I set the dynamite down on the shelf and didn't touch it again that night, and I lay awake listening to the wind move snow around on the slopes above, and I told myself the decision hadn't been made.

But here's what nobody tells you about decisions: they're mostly made before you notice them. The deliberation afterward is just ceremony. Somewhere between the ridge and the hut, some part of me had already weighed Renner's party against a buried track and found the exchange acceptable, and the rest of me — the part that still believed in the line I drew after Halden Pass, the part that had sworn on two hundred names never to move a mountain against people again — that part was only being consulted for form's sake.

I fell asleep doing load calculations for snowpack.

*

Morning came gray and windless, which is avalanche weather, though I didn't need the sky to tell me. We climbed to the crown site above the incline's mid-section — a two-hour scramble over ground I'd mapped from below and now met face to face — and I dug the pit.

Sixty centimeters down, my crystal card told me what I already knew: faceted grains, depth hoar, the sugary weak layer that fails without warning and takes everything above it along. Above that, a meter of wind-loaded slab, dense and cohesive, a single rigid plate lying on ball bearings. The slope above the incline held perhaps forty thousand tons of it.

One charge at the crown. That's all it ever takes, when the mountain has done the rest of the work for you. That's what the men who blew Halden Pass understood, God forgive them and God forgive me for understanding it too. You don't destroy a tunnel with explosives. You place a small charge where the mountain wants to fail, and you let the mountain do the killing, and afterward everyone says *tragic, unforeseeable, the rock was unstable.* The mountain takes the blame. It always has. It's the perfect accomplice — mute, vast, incapable of testifying.

Except I had spent eleven years teaching it to testify. And now I stood on its shoulder asking it to kill for me instead.

"Astrid." Mira's voice, low. "Look."

Down-slope, the incline emerged from the shadow of the cut, and on it, absurdly, movingly, the upper car was descending — the restored car, painted company green, riding the renewed cable with the smooth confidence of machinery that has been loved back to life. Through the glasses I counted heads. Renner in the front, hatless, talking to a telegraph operator whose portable key case glinted at his side. Four guides. Six men total, ascending toward us at a speed no legs could match.

Renner looked up once, scanning the slopes, and even at that distance I felt the look land. He wasn't worried. He was surveying. Closing out a line item.

"The charge," Mira said. "Now. While they're in the cut."

"In the cut," I repeated. The cut — the blasted trench through the rib of rock where the incline passed beneath the steepest of the loading zones. If I fired now, the slide would take the car and everyone in it. Not strand them. Take them. Forty thousand tons does not leave survivors; it leaves a debris field and a spring thaw full of questions.

If I waited until they cleared the cut and started up the open traverse beyond — then the slide would cross the track behind them. Buried rails. Buried cable. Stranded men, alive, furious, and days from anywhere.

Ten minutes. Maybe twelve. The difference between murder and sabotage measured in the crawl of a counterweight.

"Wait," I said.

"For what—"

"Until they're past the cut. Then I fire."

Mira looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw in my face made her nod slowly, the way you nod at someone walking a narrow ledge. "You're sure about the timing."

"No," I said honestly. "But I'm sure about the alternative."

We waited. The wind stayed down, which was good for the charge and terrible for my conscience, because in still air I could hear the incline's motor whining faintly up the valley, could hear, or imagined I heard, voices carrying off the car — men joking, probably, guides grumbling about wages, a boy operator complaining about cold fingers on the key. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of people who thought today was a workday.

The car entered the cut. Rock walls rose around it. I watched it shrink into the trench like a coin dropping into a slot, and I knelt at the charge site with my hands steady — that was the obscene part, how steady they were, eleven years of exile and my hands still remembered their trade perfectly — and I crimped the blasting cap to the fuse, and I waited for the nose of the car to clear the far lip of the cut.

It didn't clear it fast enough.

Later I would reconstruct it a hundred times: the counterweight drum, the cable tension, the grade, the motor's governor. Later I would conclude that the driver — some local who'd known the incline in its prime — had simply pushed her harder on the open stretch than I'd allowed for, banking time in the trench. But kneeling there, watching the car emerge too slow, watching Renner's green shoulders still inside the rock walls when my window began to close, I faced the choice at its nakedest: fire late and risk the cut, or don't fire at all and lose the mountain.

I fired.

The charge cracked — a flat slap echoing off the faces — and for one full second nothing happened, that terrible pregnant pause every blaster knows, when the mountain considers your request. Then the crown released.

I have seen avalanches from below, from the side, from safety. I had never seen one from above, from inside its birth. The slab didn't fall so much as exhale — a whole hillside deciding, at once, to become liquid. It went down the loading zone in a silent white flood, gathered the loose snow beneath it, hit the incline's alignment a hundred meters short of the cut, and buried it. Track, cable, ballast, the lower traverse — gone under five meters of settled debris, the surface steaming with impact dust, the crack of it rolling away down-valley like artillery.

The car was still in the cut. The slide had stopped just short. By luck, or by the mountain's own indifferent mercy, the trench walls had shielded it.

Alive. All six of them, alive, climbing out of the cut onto the debris field, tiny figures gesturing at the white burial where their railway used to be.

Mira exhaled beside me. "You waited."

"Barely."

"Barely counts." She put her hand briefly on my shoulder — the first time she'd touched me since the crevasse — and then took it away, because we were not people who lingered. "Come on. They'll dig for the tools, regroup, come up the ridge on foot. We've bought a day. Maybe two."

I didn't move. I was looking at the debris field, at the men crawling over it like ants on a fresh grave, and the triumph I'd expected — the hot clean relief of having bought distance — didn't come. What came instead was a memory, unbidden and merciless: a survey plate from a brass chest, showing a tunnel mouth, showing a slope above it, showing charge sites marked in a dead man's careful hand. *Placed where the mountain wants to fail.* I had just done the same mathematics. I had just lit the same kind of fuse. The scale was different — six men alive where two hundred died — but the method, the method was a family resemblance you couldn't talk your way out of.

Somewhere under that white, Renner was looking up the slope, and he knew — he *had* to know — that the slide hadn't been weather. Wind slab doesn't release on a windless morning without help. He knew someone up there had read his mountain the way he read ledgers, and he knew who.

"He'll understand what happened," I said. "He'll know it was deliberate."

"Yes."

"And he'll know that whoever did it understands him. Knows his methods. Speaks his language." I stood, knees stiff, and slung my pack. "God help me, Mira, I just wrote him a letter in his own alphabet."

Mira considered this with her maddening calm. "Then he knows you're willing to bury the track," she said. "He doesn't know yet whether you're willing to bury him. That uncertainty is worth more than the day we bought." She started downslope toward the next col. "Come on, engineer. Your mountain's waiting."

I followed. But at the lip of the col I looked back once, down at the buried incline gleaming in the sun, and I understood that something had changed that couldn't be unburied. For eleven years I had defined myself against the men who moved mountains against people. Now the line between us ran through a debris field, and I was standing on the wrong side of it, and the only question left — the question that would follow me down every switchback to come — was whether I could dig my way back.

Below, faint on the wind, came the sound of shovels striking frozen debris. Renner's men, digging out their gear.

And one voice, thin with distance, shouting orders in a tone I recognized — the tone of a man who had just realized the hunt had changed direction, and was already, patiently, revising the ledger.
