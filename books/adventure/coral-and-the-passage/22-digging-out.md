## 22. Digging Out

The avalanche finished its work in about ninety seconds. The silence afterward lasted considerably longer and was much worse.

I stood at the lip of the switchback with my hands still raised from the charge, listening to the mountain settle the way a house settles — small groans, the tick of snow compacting, somewhere below a sound like a door closing that I chose not to identify. The funicular track I'd brought down ran along the shelf like a dropped ribbon, half of it hanging in air where the slope had simply decided it no longer existed.

Mira came up beside me. She didn't shout. She had stopped shouting somewhere around the moment the first crack opened above the guides' route, and what she said now was quiet in the way that ice is quiet.

"You used their method."

"I know."

"No. You don't know." She turned to look at me, and her face was doing something complicated — fury and fear and, underneath both, a kind of grief I recognized because I'd seen it in the mirror for twenty years. "Two hundred people died under snow just like this because men with your training decided a schedule mattered more than lives. And you just — you did arithmetic with a fuse and called it survival."

"It *was* survival," I said. "They were an hour behind us with rifles and a telegraph operator. By tomorrow they'd have boxed us against the ice bridge with nowhere left to run."

"And now?"

"Now," I said, "there are four men under that snow."

She stared at me. Then at the slope. Then back at me, and I watched her arrive at the same conclusion I'd been circling since the last echo died, the conclusion I'd been trying very hard not to have.

"No," she said.

"Mira—"

"*No.* You buried them so we could live. That was the transaction. That was the whole ugly point. You dig them out, you've spent everything for nothing, and Renner's men will be on our heels by noon instead of never."

"Three of them might be alive," I said. "Men can breathe under snow for a while if the pack isn't set too hard. I aimed the charge to shear the track, not the shelf. I did the arithmetic on that too."

"You can't know that."

"I'm an engineer, Mira. Knowing is the entire job. Everything else is just weather."

She grabbed my sleeve — the mud-stained coat, the cuff with the seal she'd once followed me home over — and her grip was strong enough that I'd wear the bruise for a week. "If you go down there, I am not coming with you. I want you to understand that. I have spent my whole life getting this chest up this mountain. I will not lose it because you've developed a conscience at eleven thousand feet."

"Then stay here," I said, and took off my pack, and kept the shovel.

That was the whole argument. It took less than two minutes, which is about right; the important arguments always do, and the long ones are just the important ones wearing coats.

* * *

Getting down to the debris field took forty minutes and cost me most of my dignity. The slide had come off the cornice above the middle switchback and run maybe three hundred meters before piling against the rock buttress, which meant the burial zone was a fan of consolidated snow the texture of wet concrete. Avalanche snow sets fast. Every minute I spent picking my way down the intact edge of the slope was a minute the pack hardened around whatever lungs were underneath it.

I probed as I went — ski pole driven straight down on a grid, feeling for the soft refusal of a body versus the hard refusal of rock. It's a skill I learned young, on river crews, dragging drowned loggers out of spring floods. I had hoped never to use it again. The mountain, apparently, keeps its own ledger of such hopes and enjoys settling accounts.

Third probe row, fourth column: the pole sank clean to the basket and stopped soft.

"Here," I said, though there was nobody to say it to, and started digging.

Digging out an avalanche burial is not heroic work. It is frantic, graceless labor — you dig down and forward, into the slope, because bodies get carried with the flow and end up deeper than you think, and every shovelful you throw has to be thrown clear or it re-buries the hole you're making. My breath came in ragged white plumes. Sweat froze in the small of my back. Somewhere above me Mira stood at the rim with her arms crossed, and I could feel her watching the way you feel weather changing.

I hit fabric at about a meter down. A boot. I widened the hole, cleared the leg, the hip, the torso, and hauled him out by the collar the way you'd pull a man from a river — because that's what he was, a drowning man, just drowning in a slower medium.

He was young. Twenty-five, maybe. Company-issue parka, company-issue boots, a rifle strapped across his back that he'd clearly never fired at anything more ambitious than a tin can. His lips were blue. His chest wasn't moving.

I got him on his side, cleared his airway, and did the compressions they teach you on river crews, counting aloud because the count keeps you from thinking. On the ninth cycle he convulsed and vomited meltwater and half-digested ration bread onto the snow, and then he breathed, great whooping gulps of it, and grabbed my wrist with a hand that had no strength in it at all.

"Don't," he wheezed. "Don't bury me again."

"Not planning on it," I said. "Name?"

"Tomas." He coughed. "Tomas Kral. Christ. Christ, the others—"

"That's next. Can you move?"

He could move, barely. I dragged him to the rock buttress at the fan's edge, out of the fall line, wrapped him in the spare blanket from my kit even though it meant my own night would be colder, and went back to probing.

* * *

The second one I found by his hat.

It sounds absurd. It sounds like something out of a ballad. But the slide had torn a red wool cap off somebody and hung it in a scrub pine near the fan's margin, and where there's a hat there's usually a head, and where there's a head there's usually a debt. I dug there and found a heavyset man in his forties, curled almost peacefully, one arm thrown over his face like he was shielding his eyes from sun instead of snow.

He was breathing when I uncovered his chest. He was not breathing ten minutes later, despite everything I knew and everything my hands remembered, and I knelt there in the blue afternoon light with my gloves soaked through and my shoulders shaking, and I told myself the arithmetic: two alive, one gone, better than four gone, better than the alternative where they'd caught us on the ice bridge and Mira's twenty-year hunt ended in a shallow grave.

Arithmetic is very good at telling you things. It is useless at telling you how to feel.

I sat back on my heels and looked at the dead man's face. He had a scar through one eyebrow and frost already forming in the stubble of his jaw, and he looked, in the way dead men in mountains always look, like he'd simply lain down for a rest and expected the world to continue without inconveniencing him. Somebody would be waiting for him somewhere below. There was always somebody below. That was the thing about the Halden disaster that had eaten me alive for twenty years — not the number, two hundred, numbers are how cowards grieve — but the fact that every single one of those two hundred had a somebody below who waited, and waited, and was eventually told a lie.

And here I was, having just manufactured a smaller version of the same crime, with the same tools, on the same mountain, for reasons that felt enormous from above and looked very small from inside a hole in the snow.

Mira came down while I was wrapping the body. She didn't announce herself; she just appeared at the edge of my headlamp glow, silent as she always was on snow, and stood looking at the blanket-covered shape for a long moment.

"The third one?" she asked.

"Alive. Up against the buttress with Tomas. Fourth man's still missing — I'll keep probing until dark."

"You could have been killed."

"Yes."

"You *will* be killed, doing this. Eventually. Some day you'll probe into a crevasse or dig into a slab that hasn't finished sliding, and all of this—" she gestured upward, toward the summit, toward the chest, toward the two hundred names waiting in a brass-bound box for their turn on the wire, "—ends in a hole in the snow, and nobody digs *you* out."

"Probably," I agreed.

She made a sound that was half laugh and half disgust. "You're impossible. You're genuinely impossible. Do you know what my grandmother would call you? *Bláznivec.* A fool who fools even himself."

"What would she call someone who watched four men suffocate to protect a box of papers?"

"A survivor," she said flatly. "Which is worse, isn't it? That's the trick of it. Survival always looks reasonable from the inside." She crouched, pulled a glove off with her teeth, and pressed bare fingers to the dead man's throat, holding them there far longer than any check required. Confirming. Refusing to let him be a number either. When she finally sat back, she said, quietly: "My father was under snow for six hours before they found him. At Halden. They told my mother it was quick."

I didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that wouldn't have been a lie or an insult.

"So dig your fourth man out," Mira said, standing, pulling her glove back on. "And then we carry these two up to the brake-house ruins, and we build a fire, and you explain to me — slowly, with diagrams if necessary — why I'm helping you save the men sent to kill us."

"Because you're not actually the person you pretend to be," I said. "Same reason I'm down here."

She gave me a look that would have frozen the river back solid, and shouldered the dead man's pack, and started up the slope.

* * *

We found the fourth guide an hour later, thirty meters further along the fan, alive but with a badly broken leg — the femur, by the angle of it, which meant he wasn't walking anywhere and wouldn't be for months. Between Tomas, concussed but ambulatory, and this one — whose name was Drescher and who swore at us with impressive creativity the entire time we splinted him — we had a problem of arithmetic of a different kind: two wounded men, one dead one, one ruined funicular, and a window of clear weather that the mountain would close on us within a day.

We made camp in the old brake-house ruins above the switchback, the same rotting shelter Mira and I had slept in on the way up, and built a fire in the corner where the walls still stood waist-high. The dead man we laid outside beneath a cairn of stone, because the ground was iron and digging was beyond any of us, and I marked the cairn with his name from his pay chit — Brandl, S. — scratched into slate with my knife. Two hundred names on a station wall below us. One more on a cairn above. The mountain keeping score, and me adding entries to both columns.

Tomas sat closest to the fire, wrapped in every blanket we could spare, drinking melted snow in small sips. He watched me work the splint on Drescher's leg with an expression I couldn't read until he spoke.

"You're Ashgrove," he said.

Drescher stopped swearing mid-word.

"The engineer," Tomas went on. "The one the reward notices are for. The one who—" He stopped himself, which was interesting. Men who've just been dug out of an avalanche tend to be honest, and honesty was clearly fighting loyalty in his face and winning on points. "Renner told us you were dangerous. Unstable. That you'd blown the line deliberately to kill us."

"I blew the track," I said. "Above you, not on you. If I'd wanted you dead, I'd have cut the cornice directly and none of us would be having this conversation."

"He's telling the truth," Drescher said suddenly. Everyone looked at him. He grimaced and jerked his chin at the slope. "I was on point. I saw the charge placement before it went. Shear line only. Any first-year blaster could've done worse." He looked at me with narrowed eyes. "Why bother? Why spend the powder on precision when you're trying to murder people?"

"Because precision is the job," I said. "And because I've stood at the bottom of sloppy blasting before. Once was enough for a lifetime."

Silence, except the fire. Tomas fed a stick of the rotten brake-house timber into it and watched it catch, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of a man approaching a ledge and testing it with his weight.

"How long have you worked for Renner?" I asked him.

"Four years. Before that—" He hesitated. Drescher snorted.

"Tell her," Drescher said. "She dug you out of a coffin. You going to be shy now?"

"Before that," Tomas said, "I was a tunnel worker. Halden extension crew. I was sixteen." He looked into the fire. "I was on the night shift. The shift that wasn't underground when it happened."

The wind moved through the broken wall and the fire leaned and straightened. Beside me, I felt Mira go very still.

"When what happened?" I asked, though I knew. Of course I knew. I had carried the proof of it up a mountain in a brass-bound chest, and I had read two hundred names scratched into a station wall by a mentor who'd died carrying the guilt of them.

"The collapse," Tomas said. "The one they blamed on Halvard Voss." He looked up at me, and his eyes were bright and terrible with something that had been waiting in him for twenty years, the same thing that had been waiting in Mira, the same thing that had been waiting in me. "Except I was there that morning, loading the fuse train. And the man who signed out the charges — the man who checked the blast circuit twice because he was the only one allowed to touch it —"

He swallowed.

"— wasn't Voss. It was the company's own survey chief. A little gray man with neat handwriting. I've spent twenty years telling myself I misremembered. And then Renner hired me this season, and I saw him up close, and I stopped misremembering."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, on the ridge, the wind sang over the cornice like a wire under load.

"What was his name?" Mira asked, though her voice said she already knew, the way mine did, the way the whole mountain seemed to know.

Tomas looked at each of us in turn — the exile, the fugitive, the splinted man nodding slowly beside him — and said:

"Renner. Ivo Renner. The man hunting you is the man who lit Halden."
