## 24. The Bargain at the Shrine

I have negotiated with union men, with ministers, with a railway magnate who once tried to bribe me with a horse. I have sat across tables from people who hated me and people who feared me and one memorable widow who did both while shelling peas. I thought I had seen every shape a bargain could take.

Then I offered Astrid Ashgrove everything they'd lost — pardon, reinstatement, their name scrubbed clean, a desk with a window in it — and they looked at me the way an engineer looks at a cracked bridge abutment. Not with hatred. With assessment.

"You're offering me a lie," they said, "with good joinery."

We were standing in the shrine.

I should describe the shrine, because it is not what you're imagining. No gilded icons, no incense. A shelf of flat stones under an overhang above the tree line, each stone painted with names in white and coral pigment that has outlasted twenty winters of wind. Two hundred stones. The Vellmarki exiles who tend this place come up twice a year, Mira Vell told me later, walking three days to repaint the letters before the snow eats them. It is, I will admit, the most honest ledger I have ever stood inside. Every entry accounted for. No rounding down.

I had come up to it with two guides and Tomas — the freed one, the former tunnel worker whose testimony was going to be a problem for me, though I didn't yet know how large a problem — and I had left them at the treeline on purpose. Some negotiations go better without witnesses. This one, as it turned out, went worse.

* * *

Let me back up, because I can hear you asking how I came to be standing in a dead nation's chapel offering pardons like a man handing out flyers.

The avalanche at the switchback cost me eleven hours, two guides buried — one of whom Ashgrove, in an act of insanity I still haven't fully priced, climbed down to dig out — and my funicular route. It also cost me something less measurable: the moment when my own hired men watched Ashgrove descend into the debris field after the people trying to catch them, and something shifted in the way they held their rifles.

Men will follow you to do hard things. They will even follow you to do ugly things, if you keep the ugliness abstract — line items, closures, pressure applied. But they watched a fugitive risk their neck for men sent to take them, and then they watched me stand at the rim doing arithmetic, and the abstraction started peeling.

Tomas finished it. Tomas, who spent four days buried to the chest and came out of the snow spitting names — not curses, names, the names of men he'd worked beside in Halden tunnel before the mountain ate them. He looked at me across the fire that night with the particular expression of a man doing long division, and I knew the answer had come out wrong for me.

So I moved up the timetable. If I couldn't take Ashgrove quietly, I would buy them loudly. And there was only one place left to find them before the summit: the shrine. Exiles' ground, but also the last water and the last shelter below the calved route. If Ashgrove crossed the range, they'd pass through it. People always return to the places where the names are.

* * *

Ashgrove was there alone when I walked in. Mira Vell had gone down toward the exile camp — I gathered there had been a disagreement there, a theft in the night, a chest missing from where the elders wanted it kept. Ashgrove sat on a stone with their back against the overhang, mending a bootlace with wire, which struck me as deeply characteristic. Everything about that person is improvised and load-bearing.

"Renner." They didn't look up. "You're a long way from your ledgers."

"I brought the ledgers with me." I patted my satchel. "They travel well."

"Mm." They bit the wire, twisted it, tested the lace. "Come to arrest me?"

"Come to make you rich," I said, "and respectable."

That got the look. The abutment look. I'll give Ashgrove this: they listen with their whole face, the way good engineers read gauges. Nothing wasted.

"Sit or don't," they said. "The stone's cold either way."

I sat. I laid out my terms the way I lay out all my terms — plainly, because plainness reads as honesty even when it isn't, and because complicated offers smell like traps. Full pardon for the flight, the culvert ambush, the avalanche. Reinstatement to the engineering corps, senior grade, backdated. Their mentor's name formally cleared in the company record — note the phrasing there, *company record*, not the public one; I am careful with phrasing the way some men are careful with knives. And in exchange: the chest, the survey plates, every page of it, delivered to me intact and unremarked upon, and Ashgrove walks off this mountain into a new life with a pension.

I confess I thought it would land harder than it did. From where I sit, it's a generous offer. From where I sit, most things are.

Ashgrove was quiet for a long time. Long enough that I counted the stones. Two hundred and six, if you include the six added recently in different handwriting — newer griefs, I suppose. The mountain keeps its books current.

"My mentor wore that seal," they said finally, nodding at the Vellmark crest stamped into the chest between us. "Did you know that?"

"I did."

"He taught me that a survey doesn't care who commissioned it. The numbers are true or they aren't." They turned the chest's brass latch over with a thumb. "You're asking me to say the numbers stopped being true because they became inconvenient."

"I'm asking you to recognize that truth has a market price," I said, "and that you've been paying it in blood for weeks. My men. Your guides. That guide I buried — no, that *you* buried, since you insist on the distinction. How many more? What's the exchange rate, Ashgrove? Because from here it looks steep."

Something moved behind their eyes at that. Not agreement. Something more dangerous — recognition. They know the arithmetic is real. They carried the death of that guide down the mountain in their hands; I saw them do it. You cannot argue a person out of a debt they've already counted.

But here is what I misjudged, and I want to be precise about it, because precision is all I have left now: I assumed the debt would bend them toward me. Take the pardon, close the account, stop the bleeding. That's what the debt does to most people. It's certainly what it does to me.

Instead Ashgrove said: "You're right that the rate is steep. So let's change the currency."

* * *

Their counter-offer was so simple it took me a full minute to understand why it terrified me.

"You confess," they said. "On the open wire. At the summit station, on the live line, in your own voice, keyed by your own hand. You tell every station down the valley what happened at Halden Pass twenty years ago. Who gave the order. Who lit the fuse. Where the ledgers are. Then the chest goes free — to the inquiry, to the courts, to whoever the law says. Not burned, not buried, not traded. Adjudicated."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we race for the station, and I key the surveys myself, and you spend the rest of your life hunting a story that's already loose."

I laughed. I'm told my laugh is unpleasant in negotiation; it's meant to be. "You're a fugitive proposing terms to the company's enforcer. Do you hear yourself?"

"I hear myself fine," they said. "Do you? You came up this mountain to close a line item. But the item won't close, Ivo. You know it and I know it. Tomas knows it — he's sitting at my fire telling stories to my guide right now, and every story ends with your name. Your own men know it; they dug with me yesterday and they haven't looked at you the same since. The item closed twenty years ago with two hundred workers inside it, and everything you've done since — the bridge, the reward, the hunt — is just interest accruing."

I will not pretend that landed softly. I have built a career on the principle that consequences are negotiable if you reach the witnesses first. Silence, properly maintained, is the cheapest material in any construction. And here was this mud-caked engineer explaining to me, patiently, the way you'd explain weather to a child, that the witness list had gotten away from me.

"The wire goes both ways," I said. "You know that. Whatever you key at that station, I can countermand. Company authority, emergency protocols—"

"On a live line?" Ashgrove almost smiled. "Every operator down the valley will have copied before you finish your first sentence. Telegraphy is the one technology on this mountain that works for whoever speaks first. You taught the company that lesson yourself, I'd guess. Or did you burn the bridge before anyone learned it?"

There it is. There's the thing about Ashgrove that makes them dangerous in a way no rifleman ever was: they remember everything, and they put it together. The bridge. The culvert. The pay chit in the frozen hand. They've been reading my methods off the mountain like strata, and they understand, better than my own board does, that everything I've done on this pursuit is a smaller copy of the original crime. Collapse the passage. Bury the evidence. File the deaths under geology.

"You think I lit the fuse," I said.

"I think you were there," Ashgrove said quietly. "I think you were young and ambitious and someone senior handed you the plunger, and you've spent twenty years making sure the mountain kept its mouth shut. And I think Tomas looked at your face across a fire two nights ago and recognized it."

I want to record, for whatever record survives, that my hands did not shake. My hands never shake. What happened instead was worse: my mind ran the calculation I've run ten thousand times — assets, liabilities, exposure, exit — and for the first time in my career, every column came up red.

Because here is the fear, the genuine article, stripped of contempt at last: it isn't the inquiry. Inquiries can be staffed. It isn't the courts; courts can be delayed. It's the wire. It's the image of two hundred names moving down that line at the speed of light, copying themselves into every station book between here and the coast, repeated by operators who never met me, witnessed by clerks who don't owe me anything. You can bury a chest. You can burn a bridge. You cannot unkey a message. Ashgrove understood that about telegraphy the way I understand it about avalanches: once it starts, you don't negotiate with it. You get out of the valley.

"So that's your answer," I said. My voice had gone somewhere flat and far away. "No pardon. No deal."

"That's my counter-offer," Ashgrove said. "Confess on the wire, and the chest testifies alongside you instead of against you. Refuse, and it testifies alone. Either way, Ivo — it testifies." They picked up the bootlace, finished the knot, stood. "You have until the summit. Same as me."

* * *

I walked down to my men in the grey half-light and found Tomas gone — walked off in the night, leaving his pay chit weighted under a stone, which even I recognized as a message written in the mountain's own grammar. The remaining guides wouldn't meet my eye. One of them asked, very politely, whether we'd be continuing the pursuit or whether he should start down.

I said we'd continue. What else is there? A man in my position has exactly two directions available: forward, or into the record.

And that is the fear I carried back up through the scree, the one that has replaced contempt entirely, that sits in my chest now like cold ballast: Ashgrove wasn't bluffing, and worse, they weren't angry. Anger I could work with. Anger makes mistakes. What they offered me at that shrine was something I had spent twenty years making sure no one else would ever build: a complete, accurate survey of me. Load-bearing walls identified. Fault lines mapped. And at the center of it, marked in coral paint like a name on a stone, the one fact I cannot engineer around —

that the mountain remembers,

and this time, it has a wire.
