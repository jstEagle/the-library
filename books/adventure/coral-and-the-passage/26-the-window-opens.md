## 26. The Window Opens

The storm broke on the third morning the way a debt collector breaks down a door — all at once, and with no interest in your opinion of it.

I woke to silence, which in the high Correns is never good news. Silence means either you're dead or the wind has finally decided to let you see what it's been doing to you for seventy-two hours. I lay in the ruin of the wire-shed at Halden Station, listening to my own heartbeat and the slow drip of melt off the broken roofline, and concluded I was probably still alive, because dead people don't ache this specifically.

Mira was already awake. She was always already awake. I had begun to suspect she didn't sleep so much as stand down.

"Sky," she said, by way of good morning.

I crawled to the gap where a window had once been — the glass long gone, the frame holding nothing but a view — and looked out.

The world had been remade. Three days of storm had scoured everything down to essentials: rock black as iron, snow blue-white and wind-carved into blades, and above it all a sky so clean and hard it looked machined. The kind of sky they'd have built if they'd built skies out of survey instruments. And there, eastward, where the glacier tongue came down from the shoulder of the range like a poured foundation —

The route was open.

I'd seen it closed, two days before the storm hit. A wall of ice, fifty feet of shattered serac where the glacier had shouldered against the headwall and refused passage. Now the calving had done its work. The whole face had come down sometime during the storm — we'd heard it, or thought we had, a long grinding thunder under the wind that I'd filed away as the mountain settling. It hadn't been settling. It had been opening a door.

Through the gap ran a corridor of bare ice, blue-green, wet-looking even at this distance, running up and over the saddle toward the summit ridge. The high route. The one-week window the old guidebooks wrote about in the tone men reserve for miracles and lottery wins.

"A week," Mira said. "Maybe less. That ice is already rotting."

"Then we don't sightsee."

* * *

We packed in twenty minutes, which is a personal record for two people who own almost nothing and distrust each other only moderately. Rope, the chest — lighter now, its contents copied twice over onto oilcloth in Mira's cramped hand, because neither of us would let the original out of arm's reach but both of us had learned what happens to things that exist in only one place — dried meat, the spirit stove, my father's compass, which I still carried out of habit and which still pointed, stubbornly, at a country that no longer existed on any map.

Before we dropped off the station platform, I did something I'm not proud of and not ashamed of either. I went back inside and keyed the telegraph one more time. The line was still live from the valley — I could feel the faint current humming in the key like a sleeping animal — and I tapped out a single word on the open circuit:

*Coming.*

No name. No location. Just that. Somewhere down the line, in Brassenford or Vellmark's scattered exile settlements or Renner's field office, someone would read it and know exactly who it was. Let them chew on it. There's a certain satisfaction in telling your hunters your schedule. It converts pursuit into appointment.

Mira watched me do it without comment. When I stepped back she said, "You want them to follow us up."

"I want them committed," I said. "Renner's careful. Careful men wait for perfect conditions. If he thinks I'm racing him, he stops being careful." I shouldered the chest strap. "And every hour he spends climbing is an hour his ledgers sit unguarded somewhere below."

"You're lying about half of that," she said.

"Only about half?"

She almost smiled. With Mira that's a standing ovation.

* * *

The first hour was the storm's leavings — drifts waist-deep in the lee of every ridge, the snow consolidated into something between powder and concrete that took the legs out from under you on the down-slopes. We moved roped together, forty feet apart, which after the crevasse country was less a precaution than a liturgy. You don't stop praying just because you've made it through church.

By midmorning we gained the lateral moraine and could see back down the valley we'd climbed out of, and that's when I saw them.

Two figures on the far moraine, small as punctuation marks, moving fast. Behind them, strung along the trail from the funicular head, more — four, five, six, with the pack-animals' slow bob among them. And at the rear, unmistakable even at a mile's distance because he was the only man in the Correns wearing a coat that fit him properly, Ivo Renner.

"He brought the telegraph operator," Mira said, glasses raised. "See the one with the reel on his back? He's stringing wire as they climb. He's building himself a private line up the mountain."

"Of course he is." I felt something between dread and professional admiration. "You have to understand, Mira — to a man like Renner, this whole chase is a construction project. Objectives, materials, schedule. He's not hunting us. He's *installing* us."

"Then we'd better be installed at the summit first."

We moved.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about racing across a glacier: it is the only race where the track is actively trying to kill both runners equally, and does not care who wins.

The calved corridor was magnificent and treacherous in equal measure. The seracs that had blocked it now lay in tumbled blue heaps along its flanks, and the floor between them was old ice exposed to sun for the first time in years, sweating, softening, developing that treacherous grey bloom that means the surface is water and the truth underneath is glass. We roped again. We probed every step with the staff. We moved in the rhythm Mira called "the glacier's time" — probe, weight, probe, weight — which is maddening when your blood is shouting *run*, and which is the only reason either of us was alive to be maddened.

At noon the mountain reminded us whose time it actually was.

A crack, deep and structural, rolled up the corridor from somewhere behind us — not the sharp report of a crevasse opening but the long groan of the whole glacier adjusting to its new haircut. Ahead of us, maybe three hundred yards, a section of the corridor floor sagged visibly, like a bridge taking a load it hadn't been rated for.

Mira stopped. I stopped. For a moment we stood in the enormous quiet, listening to the ice think.

"It'll hold till evening," she said at last. "Glaciers complain before they fail. It's their one virtue."

"So do I," I said, "and look how reliable I am."

We crossed the sagging section at a trot anyway, because her confidence and mine were both the performative kind, and on the far side we allowed ourselves thirty seconds of breathing hard against a serac while the corridor held behind us, groaning, complaining, holding.

That was when I saw the second party reach the glacier's edge, far below and behind. Through the glasses: Renner at the front now, having apparently decided that delegation was for people with worse schedules. He stood at the ice margin looking up the corridor — looking directly at us, though at that distance he couldn't possibly have seen anything but two dark specks against the blue. Then he turned and spoke to his people, and they began to rope up.

"He's coming straight up the corridor," Mira said. "Behind us. Same route."

"Good."

"*Good?*"

"Mira." I handed her the glasses and pointed, not at Renner, but past him — down-valley, to where the storm-scoured slopes met the tree line, to the thin dark thread of the old Halden spur road, and along it, tiny, patient, a wagon with a spool mounted on its bed. "His wire wagon can't follow him up the glacier. Every foot he climbs, his telegraph line falls further behind. By tonight he'll be out of contact with the valley. Out of contact with his office. Out of contact with whoever holds his leash." I took the glasses back. "A man like Renner without his ledger isn't a hunter. He's just a man on a mountain, cold and far from home."

"You're enjoying this."

"I'm *engineering* this," I said. "Enjoying is incidental."

It wasn't entirely a lie. But I'll admit the enjoyment was less incidental than I claimed. Twenty years of invisibility, of keeping my head down and my name out of mouths, and here I was drawing a criminal up a glacier by letting him see my heels. There are worse ways to spend a Tuesday.

* * *

The afternoon was the saddle approach, and the saddle approach was where the mountain collected its toll.

The corridor narrowed as it climbed, pinching between walls of ice older than any nation — including, pointedly, Vellmark. The sun hammered down off the walls and the heat came up off the floor until we were climbing through our own private summer, sweat freezing instantly wherever the wind found it. Twice the surface gave that hollow drum-note under the staff that means air beneath, and twice we detoured, losing an hour, watching our lead shrink in the glasses.

On the second detour, Mira said, quietly, without turning around: "When we get to the summit station. What exactly is your plan?"

"Key the wire. Send the surveys. Read the names."

"That's not a plan, Ashgrove. That's a eulogy with punctuation. What happens after? Renner arrives an hour behind you with a gun and a grudge and no wire wagon. What then?"

I'd thought about this. I'd thought about it through the whiteout, through the digging, through the long nights of the storm when sleep wouldn't take and the wind read me the casualty lists of everyone I'd ever failed. I had an answer. It was a bad answer. I gave it anyway.

"Then I surrender. On the summit, on the record, with the transmission logged at every station down the line. I hand him the chest and myself and I let the courts do the rest."

"And the courts did so much for Vellmark."

"The courts didn't erase Vellmark, Mira. Men did. Men with ledgers. And men with ledgers answer to paper eventually — I've staked my whole life on that."

She walked ten paces in silence. Then: "My elders wanted to bury the chest until Vellmark exists again to receive it. I defied them for you. Don't make me regret it by getting yourself shot on a mountaintop before the ink dries."

"I'll try to schedule the shooting for after."

"Ashgrove."

"Mira." I stopped and faced her, and the glacier light made her look carved, and I realized I was about to say something true, which is dangerous at altitude. "Twenty years ago men decided the truth was too expensive and buried two hundred people in a tunnel. Every person since who decided the truth was too dangerous — that's another layer of rubble on top of them. I am done adding layers. Whatever happens at that station, the names go out on the wire. Everything else is negotiable."

She looked at me for a long moment. Wind moved the loose hair at her temple; the rest of her might have been part of the headwall.

"All right," she said. "Everything else is negotiable. Try to remember you said that."

* * *

We reached the head of the corridor as the sun went molten behind the western peaks, and the last obstacle announced itself the way obstacles do in the high mountains: by being obvious and horrible.

Between the top of the glacier tongue and the summit ridge lay perhaps four hundred yards of steep ice — the calved face's exit ramp — swept by wind, streaked with fresh avalanche runnels, and crowned by a cornice that hung over the summit station's approach like a jaw. In the alpenglow it glowed rose-gold and utterly indifferent to our plans.

"One pitch," Mira said. "Fixed rope, front-pointing, ninety minutes if the ice is honest."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then it's a very short career change."

We cut steps. Of course we cut steps — modern methods are for people with time, and our time was spending itself elsewhere. Step, step, step, the adze ringing on blue ice, Mira leading because she was better at it and because I'd learned, somewhere in crevasse country, that the correct response to being outclassed is to hold the rope and pay attention. Below us, tiny and methodical, Renner's party had reached the corridor's mouth and begun the same climb we'd finished that morning. They'd make the head of the tongue by dark. They'd camp. They'd start up the ramp at first light.

Which meant we had one night's lead, and the summit station at the top of that ramp, and whatever the wire held.

Halfway up, the cornice above us shed. Not the whole thing — just a slab the size of a dining table, which came down the ramp in a hissing rush and passed thirty feet to our left with a sound like a freight train deciding against stopping. The rope snapped taut. Mira anchored. The slide ran out below us and left a scar down the ramp like a signature.

Neither of us said anything for a while. Then Mira said, "The mountain keeps score," which was my line, stolen fair and square, and I laughed — actually laughed, breathless and slightly hysterical, hanging off an ice ramp at fourteen thousand feet with a fugitive's chest on my back — because what else is there to do when the scorekeeper lets you live?

* * *

We topped out at full dark, under stars so close you could file a claim on them.

The summit wire station crouched on the ridge crest like a bird that had died mid-flight — a stone hut with a collapsed roof on one side, a mast leaning at an angle that violated several of my professional principles, and guy-wires singing in the night wind with a sound I knew intimately, the same note every telegraph line in the world sings when it thinks no one is listening.

I put my hand on the mast. Felt the hum.

"It's live," I said. "All the way down."

Mira got the stove going in the intact corner while I cleared snow from the door with hands I couldn't feel. Inside: a desk, a stool, a key bolted to the bench, a window facing east into the whole descending darkness of the range — and pinned to the wall, yellowed and curling, a duty roster dated twenty-two years ago, the last week the station was manned. The final operator had signed off with a single line in the log book, which still lay open on the desk:

*Line quiet. Nothing to report.*

Nothing to report. Two hundred people dead in the tunnel below us and the official record of this station's final shift was *nothing to report*. I stood there with my mittens full of snow and understood, with a clarity that felt like altitude sickness, that I had climbed my entire life to contradict those three words.

Below and behind us, down in the dark at the head of the glacier, a fire bloomed — small, orange, orderly. Renner's camp. One night between us and the most important sentence either of us would ever send.

Mira appeared in the doorway with two cups of something hot and terrible. She followed my eyes down to the fire, then to the log book, then to me.

"Dawn," she said. "We transmit at dawn. Full light, so you can work the key with feeling, and so if he comes up the ramp early we see him coming."

"Dawn," I agreed.

But I sat down at the desk anyway, and pulled the log book toward me, and beneath that final cowardly entry I wrote, in pencil, in a hand steadier than I had any right to expect:

*Line active. Something to report.*

Then I drank the terrible coffee and watched the fire down on the ice and waited for the sky to lighten, knowing that somewhere below, a careful man was watching ours.
