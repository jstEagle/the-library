## 27. The Ice Bridge

I have crossed ice my whole life. Ice on the Vellmarki lakes in winter, ice in the fjords where the fishing boats had to be chipped free by hand, ice in the eyes of men who came to our villages with papers and stamps and the polite vocabulary of erasure. I thought I knew all its moods.

The arch above Halden Pass taught me a new one: ice that is dying and knows it.

We heard it before we saw it — a groan rolling down from the calved face, deep and wet, like a cathedral clearing its throat. Astrid stopped so suddenly I nearly walked into their pack.

"There," they said, and pointed.

The high route opened by the calving was not a road. It was a bridge of ice thrown across a meltwater gorge, an arch left standing when everything around it collapsed — a rib of the glacier's old body, blue-white and translucent at its thinnest point, sagging in the middle like a tired animal. Meltwater poured off it in silver threads. The sun, three days of clear sky after the storm, was doing to it what sun does.

"That's the route?" I asked, though I could see it was.

"That's the route." Astrid crouched, pulled out the compass, checked it against the ridge line with the particular fussiness of an engineer who trusts instruments more than weather. "It's maybe two hundred meters across. The far side joins the moraine shelf, and from there it's six hours to the summit station."

"And if we go around?"

"Three days around. Down into the gorge and back up the eastern scree, which is loose, exposed, and —" they glanced at the sky, reading clouds the way I read water, "— probably fine for another day. Probably."

Probably. The whole journey had been built on probably. Probably ahead of Renner. Probably ahead of the snows. Probably alive at the end of it.

"We cross," I said.

Astrid looked at me. There was something in their face I'd learned to recognize over these weeks — the expression of a person doing arithmetic with other people's lives. They'd been carrying that arithmetic since the culvert, since the avalanche, since the moment they knelt in the snow digging out Renner's guides with their bare hands while I stood over them shouting that we should keep moving. Two survived. One did not. Astrid counted that one every night now; I could hear it in the way they breathed before sleep.

"You first or me?" they asked.

"You first. You're lighter and you're the one who knows how much weight this thing can take."

"I don't know how much weight it can take. Nobody knows. That's rather the definition of walking onto melting ice."

"Then you'll find out with your own feet instead of mine. Consider it a professional courtesy."

The ghost of a smile. Then they roped us together — twenty meters between us, Astrid testing each anchor with the pick of their hammer, driving it in at angles I would never have guessed, muttering numbers under their breath like prayers to a god of load-bearing tolerances. I watched their hands work and thought, not for the first time, that there was something obscene about how beautiful competence looks when you're afraid.

Astrid stepped onto the arch.

* * *

The first hundred meters were almost easy. The ice was thick there, opaque, crusted with old snow that gave good purchase. Astrid moved carefully but steadily, probing ahead with the hammer, calling out the footing — "solid, solid, thin here, step where I step" — and I followed, matching their prints exactly, the rope slack between us.

Underneath us, through the translucent patches, the gorge water ran green and violent. I made the mistake of looking down once. The melt had carved the underside of the arch into flutes and columns, gorgeous and hollowed-out, and somewhere below a chunk the size of a house let go and hit the water with a boom I felt through my boots.

"Don't look down," Astrid said.

"I wasn't looking down."

"You're Vellmarki. You were absolutely looking down."

"We look down. That's how we survived being erased from the maps — we kept checking whether the ground still existed under us."

Another boom, closer. A thread of meltwater that had been trickling beside my boot became a stream became a small torrent, finding a crack, widening it as I watched. Water does that. Give it a year and it will open a mountain; give it an afternoon and it will open the floor beneath your feet.

"Hurry," I said, and for once Astrid didn't argue.

We were perhaps forty meters from the far side when I heard the other sound. Not ice. Not water.

Boots. Many boots, moving fast, on the moraine behind us.

I turned. Renner's party had come up the gorge trail — Renner himself in front, his grey coat immaculate even after three days of storm, because of course it was; the man seemed to shed weather the way he shed responsibility. Behind him, four men and the telegraph operator with her kit on her back, and two of the local guides — one of them Tomas, who had dug beside Astrid in the snow and then put a hand on Renner's chest and said the words that had cracked the man's authority open like river ice in spring. But Tomas was here anyway, roped into Renner's party, because a man can know what you are and still be bound to you by wages and winter.

Renner stopped at the near lip of the gorge. He cupped his hands.

"Ashgrove!" His voice carried flat and hard across the ice. "You're standing on my warrant! Turn around!"

"He always did have a gift for metaphor," Astrid murmured. They didn't stop walking. Neither did I.

"The wire station is company property!" Renner shouted. "Whatever you think you're going to do up there, I am telling you as a representative of the Corren & Western Railway —"

"You're telling us," Astrid called back, without turning, "as the man who lit the fuse."

The name landed. Even at forty meters I saw it hit — the small hitch in his stride, the way his men glanced at him and then away. Tomas had said it in the snowfield, raw and half-frozen, and Renner had denied it with the smoothness of long practice. But denial doesn't survive altitude. Up here, stripped of ledgers and lawyers, he was just a man shouting across a dying bridge.

"Ashgrove." His voice changed. Lower now, almost reasonable, which was worse. "You buried three of my men under that slope. One of them is dead. You come back down, you surrender that chest, and I give you my word the inquiry hears the full circumstances. Self-defense. Provocation. You'll walk out of Brassenford with a fine."

"You buried two hundred," Astrid said quietly. Not shouted. Said, to me, to the ice, to the dead whose names they carried in a notebook against their heart. Then louder: "And I'm not coming back down, Renner. Not ever. The only direction left for either of us is up."

He started onto the arch.

* * *

Here is what I knew, standing thirty meters out on a melting bridge with a rope around my waist: I knew ice. I knew how it fails. And I knew, watching Renner's party string themselves along the span behind us — Renner first, then two enforcers, then the operator, then the guides — that the arch could not hold eight people. It might not hold five. Every minute the sun worked on it, the margin shrank.

Astrid reached the far lip and drove an anchor, fast, hands steady, voice not quite. "Mira. Come on. Come on."

I was ten meters from the edge when I understood what I was going to do.

There was an anchor on the near side. Renner's party had tied into it — I'd watched them do it, quick and sloppy, trusting the ice the way men trust institutions, because it has always held before. Their rope ran taut across the span behind me, a lifeline for the men still mid-arch.

And at the far end, our anchor. Our rope. The one keeping Astrid and me attached to the safe side of the world.

Between them, the arch, groaning like something in its last illness.

"Mira." Astrid had turned, had seen my face, and whatever they found there made them go very still. "Mira, what are you doing?"

"What you paid me for," I said. "Mountain knowledge."

I took out my knife.

Let me tell you what I felt, because the wry version would be easier and less true. I felt cold. Not the air — my hands were steady, my breath even, the old Vellmarki calm that my grandmother called *the lake under the lake*. Underneath the calm, I felt the arithmetic. Eight people on a failing span. Two hundred names in a notebook. One chance, closing like the calving window itself.

And underneath that, quieter than all of it: I did not want to kill anyone today. I had spent my whole life wanting the world to answer for Vellmark, and I had learned — from Astrid, God help me, from that disgraced, stubborn, self-flagellating engineer — that there is a difference between justice and a body count. Justice needs witnesses. Bodies only need graves.

So: delay, not death. Ruthless mercy. My grandmother would have approved. She'd have also told me to hurry up about it.

"MIRA." Astrid's voice cracked across the ice like the calving itself.

I cut the near-side anchor.

Not ours. Theirs. One stroke through the rope where it bent over the ice lip, and the line that held Renner's party to the solid ground went slack and slithered down into the gorge like a snake diving into green water.

The effect was immediate and, I confess, satisfying. The two enforcers mid-span froze — a man on melting ice, suddenly untethered, becomes very interested in theology. The operator sat down. The guides hauled back toward the near lip. And Renner, twenty meters out with nothing between him and the gorge but his own boots and his own certainty, stopped.

"Go," I said to Astrid. "Go now."

They went — up over the lip, onto the moraine, hauling the rope, and I followed with the knife still in my teeth like a pirate, which I regret aesthetically but it freed both hands and survival outranks dignity. Behind us the arch spoke again, a long tearing groan, and a crack ran across the span like lightning frozen mid-strike, right through the space where Renner stood.

He didn't run. I'll grant him that. He stood on his fragment of collapsing bridge and looked at me across the widening gap with an expression I had seen once before — on the face of the official who stamped Vellmark out of existence, the day he realized the paperwork wouldn't save him from the crowd outside his office. Fear wearing the mask of contempt, and losing.

"You've killed them," he said. "If the ice goes while my men are on it, that's murder, Mira Vell. That's murder, and no inquiry forgives it."

"No," I said. "If the ice goes while your men are on it, that's gravity, and gravity doesn't take sides. You had a choice, Renner. You chose to send four men onto a dying arch to catch two. I chose to make sure you couldn't follow. Count the difference."

"They'll be stranded—"

"On stable ground, with supplies, with guides who know the descent. Stranded is not dead. I'm not you." I crouched at the far lip, watching the crack creep. "But you should move quickly anyway. The middle section won't last the hour, and you're standing on the part that won't last the half-hour."

For a moment — one long, glittering moment — I thought he might actually try to rush it. Run the remaining twenty meters of groaning ice while I watched, gambling his life on my mercy holding until he reached me. Some part of me, the part that keeps the ledger of Vellmark, wanted him to try. Wanted the excuse.

He stepped back instead. Back toward his men, back toward the near shore, back toward the slow road and the law he believed he owned. The crack widened between us, and the meltwater roared up through it, and the gap grew from a meter to three while we stood there, hunter and hunted, separated at last by nothing more complicated than water running downhill.

"Brassenford," he said finally. "I'll see you at Brassenford. And there, Ashgrove, there will be no ice to cut."

"There'll be witnesses," Astrid said. "That's better."

* * *

We climbed. The moraine shelf took us up and away, and when I finally looked back — I looked down; we look down — the arch was still standing, a broken rib against the glare, with five small figures retreating along its near shoulder toward the long way round. Three days. Maybe four, with the guides' caution. The summit station was six hours ahead of us.

Astrid walked beside me in silence for a long time. When they finally spoke, their voice was strange — not angry, not grateful. Something between.

"You could have cut our rope instead. Left them the crossing, taken yours."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

I considered lying. I considered the wry answer, the practical answer, the answer about tactics and time margins. What came out was none of those.

"Because you dug them out," I said. "In the snow, with your hands, against my advice. Because you read two hundred names into a wire knowing it would hang you. Because somewhere between the river and here you taught me that the difference between us and them has to be real, Astrid, or none of this means anything. If I'd cut your rope to save time, we'd have been Renner with better handwriting."

Astrid stopped walking. Looked at me for a long moment with the sun full in their face, and I saw the arithmetic behind their eyes change — not finished, never finished, but the sum tilting toward something survivable.

"The anchor's re-set," they said at last, gruffly, which from Astrid is a love letter. "Six hours to the station. Try not to stab anything else today."

"No promises. The day is young."

Ahead of us, above the glacier's broken shoulder, the summit wire station showed itself — a black tooth of timber and rusted guy-wires against the white, its mast still standing after twenty years, its line still humming faintly in the wind. Somewhere down that line, in valley offices full of morning coffee and indifference, a key was clicking. By nightfall, if the ice and the fates allowed, every click would carry two hundred names.

Behind us, faint across the gorge, I heard the arch begin to fall.

I didn't turn around. Neither did Astrid. We climbed.
