## 28. Summit Station

The last hundred meters of the climb, I stopped counting steps and started counting heartbeats instead. It was a bad trade. The steps had been finite; the heartbeats were doing something arrhythmic and theatrical, like a drummer who'd heard about music secondhand.

Mira went ahead of me on the final pitch, her boots finding purchase in the scree with the economy of someone who'd been walking uphill since before she could form full sentences. Above her, wedged into a notch of the ridge like a bird's nest built by a government with more ambition than funding, sat the Halden Summit wire station.

I'd known it existed for twenty years. Every engineer who ever ran the Corren lines knew it existed — a relay hut perched at 3,100 meters, staffed through two brutal winters by operators who drew hazard pay and, I suspected, drank it. Then Vellmark fell, the pass closed, the company wrote off the whole eastern approach as an accounting error, and the station passed from memory into rumor.

Rumor, it turned out, had been generous. The place was a wreck. Half its roof had gone to wind, the other half to gravity, and the walls were a patchwork of tar paper and corrugated iron that some previous occupant had wired down with what looked suspiciously like telegraph line. A lightning rod leaned against the doorframe at a drunken angle, as if even it had given up on the job.

But the pole was still standing.

That's what I couldn't stop looking at. Behind the station, marching up the spine of the ridge and over the summit saddle, ran the old telegraph line — creosoted fir poles, glass insulators still intact, the wire itself slung between them in long patient catenaries. Twenty years of weather should have brought it down. Storms should have snapped it, avalanches should have guillotined it, vandals should have stripped the copper years ago.

It just stood there. Holding its breath.

"Tell me that's not live," Mira said. She'd come up beside me, and she was looking at the same thing I was.

"Can't tell you that," I said. "Because I don't know."

"You're an engineer. You're supposed to know things."

"I'm an engineer. I'm supposed to *check* things." I dug the last of my strength out of somewhere near my boots and made for the station door.

---

Inside was a time capsule with a draft problem. A cot frame rusted to lace. A pot-bellied stove that hadn't seen coal since before Mira was born. A wall calendar from a year that no longer officially existed, frozen on a month whose name I recognized only because my mentor used to curse it — the wet one, the one when the meltwater came down and tried to take the ballast with it.

And there, bolted to a desk that had been built to outlast empires, sat the key.

A brass telegraph sounder and key, mounted on a walnut base, green with oxidation but structurally sound. Beside it, a relay unit the size of a bread loaf, its housing dented but sealed. And above the desk, nailed to the beam, a laminated card — actual laminate, the company spared no expense on the things that mattered — reading:

**HALDEN SUMMIT RELAY — LINE CONDITION: OPEN — DO NOT DISMANTLE — ORDER OF THE CHIEF ENGINEER'S OFFICE**

"Do not dismantle," Mira read over my shoulder. "They ordered a mountain hut to stay loyal."

"The company doesn't dismantle anything it might want back." I set down my pack and knelt at the desk. My hands were doing the tremor thing again, so I pressed them flat against the wood until they remembered their manners. "The valley end of this line runs down to Brassenford. If the company never pulled the order—"

"Then someone might still be listening."

"Someone is always listening," I said. "That's the whole point of a wire."

I opened the relay housing. Dust, yes. Corrosion, some. But the contacts looked clean — too clean, actually, polished in a way that suggested recent attention. I frowned at that and filed it under *later*, which was becoming a very crowded folder.

The battery well beneath the desk held two glass cells, dry as old bones. But wired alongside them, spliced in with modern insulation that had no business being twenty years old, was something else: a small galvanic cell assembly, homemade, the kind of thing you build when you need current and the catalog stopped coming.

"Mira," I said carefully. "Look at this splice."

She looked. She was quiet for a moment, and then she said, in the tone of a woman rearranging her entire understanding of the morning, "That's fresh work."

"That's *this season's* work."

We stared at each other across the desk, and the wind outside found a loose sheet of roofing iron and played it like a bad omen.

---

Here is what I knew, and what I told her while we checked the rest of the hut with the thoroughness of people expecting the furniture to testify against them:

The Halden Summit relay sat on the trunk line. Not a spur, not a branch — the trunk, the artery that ran from Brassenford in the south, up through the valley stations, over this saddle, and down the eastern face toward the lowland exchanges. When the pass closed, the company had rerouted traffic around the range on the long western loop, but nobody had ever filed the paperwork to abandon the trunk itself. I knew that because abandoning a trunk line required a signature from the chief engineer's office, and I had spent three years of my disgrace learning exactly which signatures moved which steel.

The line was dead in every official sense. Which meant that if it was carrying current right now, somebody was keeping it alive unofficially. And there were exactly two kinds of somebodies who did that kind of work.

Romantics, and witnesses.

Sometimes both in the same body.

---

I built the circuit the way you rebuild a heartbeat. Cleaned the key contacts with the edge of my knife and a strip of shirt. Dried the relay contacts and gapped them to spec by feel, because I'd left my feeler gauges in another life. Mixed electrolyte from snowmelt and the emergency chemicals in the station's sealed stores — potassium dichromate, still potent after all these years, because the company bought chemicals the way it bought grudges: in quantities designed to outlive everyone involved.

Mira watched me work without speaking. At some point she built a fire in the old stove using broken crate slats, and the hut filled with the smell of burning pine and old varnish, and I realized I was humming. Some half-remembered operator's tune, the kind you pick up working night shifts next to a sounder. I stopped. She noticed I'd stopped. Neither of us mentioned it.

When the cells were wet and the wiring closed, I connected the relay to the line side and put on the headphones.

Nothing.

Static, rather — the deep oceanic hiss of an open circuit breathing. But underneath it, faint as a pulse taken through wool—

Click.

Click-click.

I pulled the headphones off so fast I nearly took skin with them.

"What?" Mira said. "What is it?"

"It's alive." My voice came out strange, flattened, like I was hearing it through the same wire. "There's traffic on the line. Weak. Long-haul leakage, maybe, or—" I stopped, because the honest answer was too large to say casually. "Or there's a station down-valley that never stopped listening either."

Mira sat down slowly on the cot frame, which complained but held. "Twenty years," she said. "Twenty years my family has shouted into rooms where nobody would answer. And this whole time there was a wire running over our heads that somebody kept warm."

"Not somebody." I was looking at the laminated card again. At the order. At the signature block at the bottom, faded but legible. "Look at this."

She crossed the room. Read it. Her face did something complicated.

"That's your mentor's office," she said. "The chief engineer who signed the closure orders. The one whose name is on the surveys. The one they blamed."

"The one they *framed*," I said, "and who then spent his last year of office signing maintenance orders for a line everyone else wanted dead. Do you understand what that means? He wasn't just hiding the evidence. He was keeping the road open for it. He was making sure that someday, whoever climbed up here with the truth would have a way to send it down."

The stove crackled. Somewhere below us, buried under two hundred meters of ice and rock, the tunnel slept with its dead.

"He knew," Mira said quietly. "He knew someone would come."

"He hoped," I corrected. "Engineers don't know things like that. We hope them, and then we build for them anyway, and we call it design margin." I touched the brass key, cold under my fingertip. "This is his margin."

---

We rested. Or rather, we lay down in the ruins of the operators' bunks and pretended to rest while our bodies argued about the last nine days. Mira slept first — she'd earned it twice over, once on the glacier and once at the ice bridge, though she'd deny both debts. I lay staring at the ceiling beams and listening to the static in the headphones I'd hung on the bedpost, waiting for my own pulse to agree to slow down.

It didn't. Because here's the thing about a live wire: it changes the shape of everything downstream of it.

If the line was live, then the valley stations were manned or at least monitored. If the valley stations were monitoring, then Renner's party — descending or circling, delayed but not defeated, because men like Renner don't get defeated, they get *reassigned* — would also know the line was live. Which meant he'd be moving faster now. Which meant our window wasn't just the weather anymore. It was him.

And there was a third thing, the third thing I kept circling like a crow over roadkill: if I keyed that transmitter, I wouldn't just be sending a message. I'd be sending a *location*. A signal has a source. Every operator down the line who heard me would know exactly where the signal came from, to within a few hundred meters of scree. The company had satellites, patrols, and a private security budget larger than some nations' navies.

The chest sat against the far wall, coral-painted wood gone dull with altitude, holding two hundred names and the geometry of a murder. One key-press away from free. One key-press away from hunted.

I didn't sleep so much as negotiate brief truces with unconsciousness, and in the gray hour before dawn I gave up, lit the stove again, and went back to the desk.

---

Dawn came up the color of old tin. Mira woke to the smell of heated ration tin and the sight of me hunched over the key like a penitent at an altar, which, honestly, was not far off.

"You've been sitting there all night," she said. Not a question.

"I've been *thinking* all night. The sitting was incidental."

"And?"

"And I'm going to send a test." I turned on the stool. "One transmission. Short. Coded — station identification, nothing more. No names, no data, no confession. Just enough to find out who's listening."

"They'll trace it."

"They'll try. But a test costs us nothing we haven't already decided to spend, and it buys us the one thing we can't proceed without." I held up a finger. "Certainty. I am not going to read two hundred names into a dead line. If I'm going to do this, the mountain is going to hear it happen, and so is every station from here to the sea."

Mira was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the light was strengthening, and the glacier below the saddle had turned from blue-gray to blinding, the calved route lying open down the eastern face like a road paved for us specifically.

"When I was a girl," she said finally, "my grandmother kept a radio hidden in a flour tin. Vellmarki music, news from the exile stations, names of the missing read aloud every solstice. The penalty for owning it was prison. She kept it anyway. She said a people who cannot broadcast are a people being erased in real time." She looked at the key. "Key it."

I put the headphones on. Took a breath that tasted of solder flux and pine smoke. And for the first time in twenty years, I pressed a telegraph key with intent.

*VQ HALDEN SUMMIT HALDEN SUMMIT VQ TEST TEST WHO COPY*

I released the key. The static rushed back in like water filling a footprint.

Ten seconds. Twenty. Long enough for doubt to unpack its luggage.

Then the headphones spoke.

Not words. Just a carrier shift — a station down the line opening its receiving circuit, the electrical equivalent of a door unlocking somewhere far below. And then, crisp and professional and impossibly calm, a hand I did not recognize began to send:

*STATION RECEIVING. IDENTIFY YOURSELF. YOU ARE KEYING ON A CLOSED LINE. STATE YOUR TRAFFIC.*

Mira was on her feet. I gripped the edge of the desk with both hands, because the room had tilted slightly and I needed an anchor.

*I AM THE ASHGROVE ENGINEER,* I sent back, because apparently I'd decided honesty was the theme of the day. *I HAVE SURVEY DOCUMENTS CONCERNING HALDEN PASS. I REQUIRE CONFIRMATION THIS LINE REACHES BRASSENFORD EXCHANGE.*

The pause this time was longer. Long enough for me to count four avalanches' worth of heartbeat.

Then:

*LINE TO BRASSENFORD IS INTACT AND MONITORED. ASHGROVE ENGINEER — WE HAVE BEEN WAITING TWENTY YEARS FOR THAT NAME. HOLD YOUR TRAFFIC. REPEAT, HOLD. THERE IS COMPANY TRAFFIC ON THE WIRE SEARCHING FOR YOU NOW. THEY ARE USING YOUR REAL NAME.*

My blood went from glacial to boiler in the space of a sentence.

*WHO IS THIS,* I keyed.

The answer came back steady as a level bubble:

*THE NIGHT OPERATOR AT KESSWICK JUNCTION. MY FATHER HELD THIS SHIFT BEFORE ME. HE TOLD ME: SOMEDAY THE WIRE WILL SPEAK FROM THE MOUNTAIN, AND WHEN IT DOES, YOU SEND EVERYTHING DOWN THE LINE AND YOU DO NOT STOP FOR ANYONE.*

I looked at Mira. She was crying without any of the usual embarrassment, tears freezing on her cheekbones, and neither of us said a word, because there was nothing to add to that.

Then the sounder chattered again, and the calm professional voice had an edge in it now:

*ASHGROVE ENGINEER — BE ADVISED. COMPANY OPERATOR JUST JOINED THE CIRCUIT FROM THE WESTERN LOOP. THEY ARE REQUESTING ALL STATIONS REPORT ANY UNAUTHORIZED KEYING. THEY KNOW SOMEONE IS ON THE MOUNTAIN.*

*HOW LONG DO I HAVE?*

The reply came without hesitation, without mercy, and with the unmistakable rhythm of a hand that had waited its whole life to send it:

*LESS THAN AN HOUR. START SENDING.*
