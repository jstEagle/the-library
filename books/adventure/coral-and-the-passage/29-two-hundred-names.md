## 29. Two Hundred Names

The wire station sat on the shoulder of the mountain like a bird that had died mid-flight and been left there out of respect. Its roof was half gone, its windows were either shattered or furred with frost-lichen, and its mast leaned at an angle that would have gotten any engineer I'd ever worked with fired on the spot. But the line came up the valley on the far side, crossed the col on porcelain insulators that some long-dead lineman had set with more care than the company had ever shown its workers, and dropped into the station through a hole in the wall like a snake returning to a den it had outgrown.

Mira held the lantern while I checked the line.

"Live?" she asked.

I pressed the back of my hand to the wire where it entered the binding post — old habit, and a stupid one, because a live telegraph wire doesn't always announce itself politely. It hummed. Not current you could feel so much as current you could *hear*, a faint insect whine in the iron, the sound of a circuit closed somewhere far below in the dark and waiting.

"It's live," I said. "Somebody's keeping this circuit fed from the valley."

"Why would they? The station's been dead twenty years."

"Because copper is expensive and companies are cheap," I said, "and because nobody ever sent the order to cut it. That's the thing about the railway, Mira. It never forgets a line. It just stops looking at them." I touched the brass of the key, cold enough to burn. "It's still listening."

She set the lantern on the operator's desk, which was a miracle in itself — oak, scarred, one leg shimmed with a folded page of what looked like a freight manifest from before the war. The chair was gone. We stood. There was something fitting about that. Nobody should get to sit down for what came next.

The chest sat between us on the floor, coral-painted, flood-stained, smelling of river and years. I knelt and opened it for maybe the hundredth time since the water gave it up, and for the hundredth time the smell of it hit me — wet paper and brass oil and something older underneath, something like a cellar, like a place things were put to be forgotten.

"Say it again," Mira said. "Slowly. What you're going to send."

"The survey plates first. Elevations, charge placements, the timing records. All of it keyed out as figures — numbers don't need translation, and no relay clerk can claim he misheard arithmetic." I pulled the plates free and stacked them on the desk, edge to edge, like bread. "Then the cover letter. Then—"

"Then the names."

"Then the names."

She nodded once and turned to face the door, which faced east, which faced the pass, which faced the direction a man named Renner would come from if he came at all tonight. She'd insisted on taking the watch. I hadn't argued. My hands needed to be free for the key, and hers needed to be free for the knife, and neither of us said that out loud because saying it out loud would have made it true in a way we weren't ready for.

I grounded myself. That's what my mentor called it — Halden Voss, whose initials I'd found scratched into a wall two days' walk below us beside two hundred names, who taught me that before you touch a key or a throttle or a charge wire, you put your feet flat and your breath slow and you remember what you are. I am an engineer. I put my feet flat on the frozen floorboards. I breathed.

Then I took the key in my hand and opened the circuit, and the mountain began to talk.

* * *

You have to understand what keying is, if you've never done it. It isn't typing. It isn't writing. It's *singing* with your hand — every letter a rhythm, every rhythm a small act of muscle memory laid down over ten thousand hours of practice until the wire stops being a machine and becomes a voice, your voice, thrown two hundred miles down a copper throat.

I started with the header. Every station on the closed lines had a call sign, and I'd spent fifteen years mapping those dead calls the way other men map graves. I used the oldest one I knew — HALDEN SUMMIT, a station that officially did not exist, on a line that officially carried nothing, calling down the wire into the dark:

**HALDEN SUMMIT TO ALL STATIONS ALL STATIONS ALL STATIONS. PRIORITY TRAFFIC. RELAY WITHOUT ALTERATION.**

Then I paused, because the next part required a decision I'd already made but wanted to feel the weight of one more time.

Somewhere below — I pictured Brassenford, the yard office, the night clerk with his collar too tight and his tea going cold — someone would be reading this. A company man, probably. Maybe Renner himself, if he'd thought to put an operator on the wire when he hired his guides. He was thorough that way. Thorough men make the best monsters.

Let him read it. Let him read every figure.

I keyed the survey data.

Elevation of the crown pillar: keyed. Charge placement, east gallery, stations fourteen through thirty-one: keyed. The timing sheets — and God, the timing sheets, Halden's neat engineer's hand marching across the plates like a metronome counting down to murder — keyed, all of them, every interval between the fuse order and the collapse, every entry showing the shift roster still underground when the order went through. Two hundred and six names on the roster. Six had been above ground fetching mules. The arithmetic of survival, done in pencil, by a man who knew exactly what he was writing and wrote it anyway.

My fist ached around the key. Not from the work. From holding back what the work made me want to say.

Numbers went down the wire clean and cold, and somewhere in the valley a relay clerk began, I hoped, to copy them, and somewhere behind that clerk a telephone rang, and somewhere behind that telephone a man who thought he owned the mountains felt the first cold draft of the morning coming for him.

* * *

Mira watched me work for an hour before she spoke.

"You're crying," she said.

"I'm concentrating."

"On the semicolons?"

"Engineers cry at semicolons. It's a professional hazard." I wiped my face with my sleeve and kept sending. The truth was simpler and worse: every figure I keyed was a sentence being served by proxy. Halden couldn't stand trial. The two hundred couldn't testify. So the numbers went instead, each one carrying its little weight of dead men's hours, and I was only the courier, and couriers are allowed to cry, whatever the manual says.

When the last plate was copied — I could tell because the faint relay echo changed character, the distant stations' repeaters falling silent in a way that meant *message complete, awaiting further* — I set the plates aside and took out the final document. The list. Halden had written it in the margin of the timing sheet, two hundred and six names, and six struck through with a single line and a note: *above ground, spared.* Two hundred remained.

Two hundred names.

"I can't read them all," I said. "Not fast. It'll take hours."

"We have until dawn," Mira said. "And after dawn, we have however long it takes a man with a rifle to climb a col." She came and stood beside me at the desk, close enough that I could see her breath silvering in the lantern light. "Read them slowly. My grandmother used to say a name read quickly is a name half-heard. These people have been half-heard for twenty years."

So I read them slowly.

* * *

Here is what happens when you key two hundred names into a telegraph wire at four o'clock in the morning, alone, in a ruined station above a murdered tunnel.

First, nothing. The wire takes each name and carries it away into the dark and gives nothing back, and you begin to wonder if anyone is copying at all, if the whole exercise is a man talking to copper, and there is a particular loneliness in that which I will not try to describe because I would do it badly.

Then, forty names in, the wire answers.

It comes as a break — a firm, professional double-tap on the circuit from somewhere south, the telegrapher's equivalent of clearing a throat. Then:

**BRASSENFORD RELAY. COPY YOUR TRAFFIC. WHO IS SENDING.**

And I sat with my hand hovering over the key, and I thought about all the answers I could give. *Nobody. No one. A ghost. The mountain.* And then I thought about Halden Voss, who spent his last free years teaching me that an engineer signs her work, always, because unsigned work is how buildings fall down and tunnels kill people.

I keyed:

**ASHGROVE. FORMERLY CHIEF SURVEYOR, CORREN DIVISION. THE DOCUMENTS ARE AUTHENTIC. VERIFY AGAINST COMPANY ARCHIVES IF YOU DARE. CONTINUE RELAY.**

A pause. Long enough for a man in Brassenford to swallow twice and look over his shoulder. Then:

**COPY ASHGROVE. RELAYING.**

And the wire woke up.

* * *

I wish I could tell you what it sounded like. I've tried, in the years since, and I've never managed it. The nearest I can come: imagine a congregation. Imagine two hundred miles of dark country, station after station along the dead and living lines — Brassenford, Corren Mills, the junction at Lowmarsh, the little flag-stop shacks where a lone operator kept a stove and a cat and a copybook — imagine all of them waking one by one to a name coming down the wire, and repeating it, and passing it on.

Because that's what they did. That's what operators do. A priority relay without alteration means you copy what comes and you send it forward, and these were company men, most of them, men who'd spent careers learning not to ask questions — and they asked none. They heard a name, and they keyed it onward, and the name went south, and another name followed it.

**VOSKARAN, PETRA.**

Relayed.

**VOSKARAN, DANILO.**

Relayed.

**IMRE, KATALIN. IMRE, JOZEF. IMRE, ANNA-MARIE.**

An entire family. I stopped after Anna-Marie, because my eyes had blurred past the point of usefulness, and Mira put her hand flat on the desk beside the plate — not on my shoulder, she wasn't sentimental that way, but near me, the way you'd put a lamp near someone working — and said, "Keep going. They're listening. I can hear them listening."

She couldn't, of course. But she was right anyway.

Down the line, the relays were changing. I could hear it in the rhythm — new fists joining the circuit, different hands, different habits, the particular stutter of an operator who learned on a rural spur versus the smooth roll of a mainline man. Word was spreading down the wire faster than the message itself: *something is happening on the Halden circuit, something old, listen in.* By name sixty there were stations copying that had no business being awake. By name ninety I could hear a relay echo I didn't recognize at all — a heavy, deliberate fist, careful, almost reverent — and when I broke to ask, the answer came back:

**VELLMARKI MUTUAL AID SOCIETY, EASTON YARDS. WE HAVE WAITED TWENTY YEARS FOR THIS TRAFFIC. SEND.**

Mira made a sound I had never heard her make. Small. Like something unlocking.

"That's my uncle's society," she said. "That's — Ashgrove, that's *my people's* key clicking right now. In Easton. They're hearing the names." She laughed, or sobbed, or both, in the way people do when the body can't decide. "They're hearing them in a room with candles lit."

* * *

Name one hundred and twelve was a boy. Fifteen years old, listed as *mule driver, apprentice*. His name was Tomaš Brek. I know I'll remember it until I die, the way you remember the first stone that hits you in an avalanche.

I keyed it slowly. **BREK, TOMAŠ. AGE FIFTEEN. MULE DRIVER, APPRENTICE.**

And the wire, for the first time that night, answered *me*.

It came from Easton, the heavy reverent fist: **ACKNOWLEDGED. BREK, TOMAŠ. HIS MOTHER'S GRANDDAUGHTER LIVES ON OUR STREET. SHE WILL BE WOKEN GENTLY.**

I put my forehead down on the desk, on the cold oak beside the lantern, and I stayed there while the circuit hummed, patient, full of light I couldn't see. Mira stood watch at the broken window with her back straight as a rail, and neither of us spoke, and the mountain outside held its snowfall close around the station like a hand cupped over a match.

Then I lifted my head, and I read the remaining eighty-eight names.

* * *

Dawn found us still at the desk. The last name went down the wire as the sky over the eastern col went the color of old pewter, and when I keyed the closing —

**END OF TRAFFIC. TWO HUNDRED NAMES SENT. TWO HUNDRED NAMES RECEIVED AND RELAYED. THE MOUNTAIN HAS TESTIFIED. ASHGROVE, HALDEN SUMMIT, CLEARING THE CIRCUIT.**

— the replies came in a flood, station after station, overlapping, breaking each other's traffic in their hurry, which is the worst sin in telegraphy and every one of them committed it gladly:

**BRASSENFORD COPY COMPLETE. GOD FORGIVE US.**

**LOWMARSH COPY COMPLETE. FORWARDING TO COUNTY PRESS.**

**CORREN MILLS COPY COMPLETE. MY FATHER DIED IN THAT TUNNEL. THANK YOU. THANK YOU.**

And from Easton, last, the heavy careful fist:

**TWO HUNDRED NAMES RECEIVED. TWO HUNDRED NAMES WILL BE READ ALOUD AT SUNDOWN IN EVERY VELLMARKI HOUSEHOLD FROM HERE TO THE COAST. ASHGROVE — WHATEVER THEY DO TO YOU AFTER THIS, KNOW THAT YOU ARE NO LONGER ALONE ON ANY LINE IN THIS COUNTRY. END OF TRAFFIC.**

Mira blew out the lantern. We didn't need it anymore; the grey light was coming up through the broken windows, laying itself across the chest, across the plates, across my hands, which had finally stopped shaking and now simply hurt, the way hands do after they've done the one thing they were made for.

"Well," I said. My voice came out scraped raw, as if I'd shouted for six hours instead of whispered into copper. "That's done."

"No," Mira said. She nodded east, toward the col, toward the funicular line where yesterday's smoke still hung in the windless air like a signature. "That's *sent*. Done is something else." She picked up her pack and slung it, and looked at me with the flat calm I'd learned to read as her version of kindness. "He'll have heard it too. Every word. His own name is going down that wire next, Ashgrove, and he knows it. A man like that doesn't wait to be arrested."

Outside, far below us on the white flank of the mountain, something moved. A dot. Then two. Then a column, small as ants, climbing.

Moving fast.

"They're on the switchback," Mira said quietly. "And they're not stopping for weather."
