## 31. The Descent Begins

The mountain closed behind us the way a door closes when someone in the house has died — slowly, politely, with a sound you feel more than hear.

I was watching it happen. We all were. Astrid stood at the lip of the pass with their hands jammed in their coat pockets, watching the snow come down the western face in that long grey curtain that doesn't fall so much as arrive, and I watched Astrid watch it, because I had learned over nine days of ice and rope and bad rations that Ashgrove's face was a better instrument than anything in that brass-stamped chest. It told you things the barometer missed.

"There it goes," Astrid said.

"There what goes?"

"The way back." A pause. "Not that there ever was one."

This was, I would learn, how Ashgrove processed catastrophe: as an engineering observation, delivered flat, with the emotional content buried somewhere below the frost line where I'd need a crew and dynamite to get at it. I was beginning to find it less infuriating than I should have. That worried me more than the snow did.

Tomas came up beside us, favoring his left leg — the one he'd nearly lost to Renner's funicular shortcut, before the avalanche, before the digging-out, before everything that had turned three hunters and two fugitives into something closer to a walking wounded expedition with a shared enemy. He looked at the closing pass for a long moment.

"My father worked that tunnel," he said. "Twenty-two years old. They told my mother it was geological subsidence." He said it without heat, the way you'd report a measurement. "She believed them for eleven years."

"And then?" I asked.

"Then she stopped believing anyone from the company about anything, including which of her children was which at funerals." He adjusted his pack strap. "It's a long walk down, isn't it."

"It's a long walk down," Astrid confirmed.

None of us said the other thing, which was that the walk down led to courts, and courts led to testimony, and testimony led to every name in that chest being spoken aloud in a room full of people paid to pretend they hadn't heard. The mountain had been simpler. The mountain only wanted to kill you honestly.

* * *

We made camp that first night in the lee of a rock ridge that Astrid examined for ten full minutes before pronouncing it structurally sound, which from Ashgrove was practically a love letter. The eastern face was kinder than the western — stepped terraces of granite and old moraine, the kind of ground a surveyor's eye could read like large print. Astrid read it constantly. I caught them doing it at every halt: eyes flicking across the slope, lips moving faintly, running some private arithmetic of grade and load and water.

"You're designing," I said, when we'd gotten a fire going small enough not to announce us to the valley.

"I'm resting."

"You're designing a railway."

Astrid fed a stick to the fire. "There's a grade line through that saddle," they said finally, pointing with their chin. "Maybe four percent if we cut the shoulder. You could bring steel up this face without a single funicular cheat." A pause. "Properly, this time."

I let that sit. *Properly, this time* was doing a lot of work in that sentence, the way a keystone does, holding up an arch of everything Ashgrove couldn't say yet — the tunnel, the two hundred names, the avalanche they'd set themselves, the guide who hadn't come out of the snow. I had spent my whole exile learning to read silences in three languages. Ashgrove's silences were the hardest and the most worth the effort.

"The elders will want the lines reopened," I said. "When Vellmark is recognized. If it's recognized."

"If?"

"If. My grandmother spent forty years saying *when*. She died saying *if*, and I decided on the glacier that I'd rather be her than convenient." I pulled my knees up. "But yes. When. And whoever surveys those lines will have to answer to us. To the families. Every cut, every fill, every tunnel mouth — signed off by people whose grandparents are under the mountain."

Astrid looked at me across the fire for a moment longer than comfort allows between people who met as adversaries.

"That's the offer, then," they said. "Co-signature or nothing."

"It's the offer. Don't look so surprised. I've been threatening to hold you accountable since the culvert."

"You held a knife to my throat at the culvert."

"I was establishing rapport."

Tomas snorted from his bedroll. "You two are going to be unbearable in a courtroom."

* * *

The second day brought the first sign that the valley knew we were coming.

We crossed a shepherd's track above a hanging valley and found a cairn that hadn't been there — I was almost sure — when the guides had come up this way years ago. On top of it, wedged under a stone, was a strip of oilcloth. Tomas unwrapped it with the caution of a man who had recently been introduced to avalanches as a professional courtesy.

Inside was bread. Still good. And a scrap of paper with a single line in a shepherd's blunt hand:

*Heard the wire. God keep you. The whole valley is listening.*

Astrid read it twice. Then they sat down on a rock, which is not like them, and held the little paper like it might evaporate.

"They're repeating it," Astrid said quietly. "Down the line. Station to station. Like I—" Their voice did something complicated and gave up. "Like I keyed it into a live wire and now it won't stop moving. Two hundred names. Every station on the line has them. Some clerk in Brassenford is probably being driven mad by towns asking him to repeat the list so their schoolchildren can copy it down."

"That was rather the point," I said.

"I know what the point was. I just—" Astrid folded the oilcloth with unnecessary precision, an engineer giving their hands honest work while the rest of them reorganized. "I spent six years making sure no one said my name. Six years being nobody, and it turns out being nobody is just being guilty with better camouflage."

I didn't say the thing I thought, which was that I understood that particular arithmetic better than anyone alive. Instead I took the bread and divided it five ways — Astrid, me, Tomas, and the two younger guides, Pell and Anselm, who had switched sides somewhere around chapter twenty-one of our acquaintance and now carried our ropes like it had always been their idea.

"Eat," I said. "You can have your crisis of identity after the scree field. Scree fields wait for no one's epiphany."

* * *

The scree field, for the record, waited for exactly no one's epiphany, and tried to kill Tomas twice, and succeeded only in teaching me that Ashgrove's rope-work had improved from *grudging competence* to *actually quite good*, which I chose to attribute to my instruction rather than to any native talent, because a woman must maintain her narrative.

On the third day we dropped below the snowline into country that smelled of pine and wet stone, and everything changed character. Above, the mountain had been a judge — austere, impartial, interested only in whether you deserved to continue. Below, the land grew witnesses. Smoke rose from homesteads in the side valleys. A dog barked at us from a distance with the ceremonial indifference of dogs everywhere. And at midday we crested a rise and saw, spread below us in the river bend, the town of Brassenford: slate roofs, a church spire, the rail yards with their smoke, and — unmistakable even at four miles — the courthouse with its steps crowded with people looking up at the mountains.

Looking up. At us. Or at least at the direction from which we would eventually, inevitably, descend.

"They've been waiting," Pell said. There was something awed and slightly horrified in his voice. He was twenty-three and had joined Renner's party for steady pay and had ended the week digging strangers out of snow because his conscience turned out to be load-bearing. "Since the wire. Three days. Look at them."

"I see them," Astrid said.

"What do you think they want?"

Astrid considered this with the seriousness they gave all structural questions.

"The same thing everyone wants," they said. "To be told the truth by someone who can't take it back."

* * *

We camped that last night within sight of the valley lights, close enough to hear the evening train — a real train, a living one, hauling freight down from the northern works — and I lay awake listening to it and thinking about my grandmother, who had died in a rented room in a country that didn't appear on maps, insisting to the end that Vellmark existed because she existed, and that was sufficient legal basis.

She would have liked the wire business. She'd have hated the waiting.

Mira Vell, ferry-woman, exile, professional keeper of grudges — I had built a life out of carrying things across water for other people and keeping my own cargo sealed. The chest had been my cargo for fifteen years, in the sense that I'd hunted its existence across three borders and two false leads and one very regrettable marriage. And now it was going to be opened in a courtroom by clerks, catalogued, stamped, entered into evidence, and the names inside it would stop belonging to the dead and start belonging to the record.

I kept turning over whether that was a theft or a rescue.

"You're doing the thing," Astrid said from the next bedroll. Not asleep, then. Nobody slept well the night before surrender; I remembered that from my own file.

"What thing?"

"The brooding thing. You go very still and your jaw does something. It's like camping next to a landslide that's deciding."

"Charming. Have you considered poetry?"

"Once. The mountains objected." A rustle of wool. Ashgrove rolled onto their side, facing me across the dead fire. In the dark their voice lost some of its engineer's flatness. "Ask me whatever it is. You'll sleep worse carrying it."

So I asked it. "Do you trust the courts? After everything? Your company bought its verdict once already. What makes you think Brassenford's judges aren't on the same ledger?"

Silence for a while. A log settled. Somewhere downslope, an owl filed its nightly complaint.

"No," Astrid said at last. "I don't trust them. Trust is for bridges, and even then you inspect annually." I heard them shift upright. "But here's what I know as an engineer. A structure doesn't have to be perfect to bear load. It has to be *inspectable*. Cracks you can see, loads you can measure, failures you can trace back to causes. The company survived twenty years on an uninspectable structure — no records, no witnesses, no names. We just made it inspectable. Two hundred names on every wire from here to the coast. Ledgers Tomas can testify to. A chest with a seal on it that half the old men in these valleys remember from their fathers' hats." A breath. "Courts fail when they're dark. So we turn on every light we can find, and we stand there in the light, and we make them do it in front of everyone. Including me. *Especially* me — I set an avalanche that killed a man, Mira. Whatever happens to Renner, I'm not walking out of this clean, and I don't intend to try."

I stared up at the stars and felt something in my chest unclench that had been clenched since I was nineteen years old and watching my country's name get inked out of an atlas.

"You know," I said, "for a disgraced fugitive, you have a suspicious amount of civic faith."

"I have no faith whatsoever. I have redundancy." A ghost of a smile in the voice. "Go to sleep, Vell. Tomorrow we surrender."

* * *

Morning came up clear and cold, the kind of light that makes everything look like a decision already made.

We broke camp without hurry. There was no hurry anymore; that was the strange mercy of it. Nine days of flight compressed into muscle and habit, and now the habit had nowhere to run, and underneath the tension I found something I hadn't felt since before Vellmark fell: the specific quiet of a person who has stopped fleeing and started arriving.

We dressed. We packed. Astrid took the chest from my pack — my pack, because somewhere on the glacier the custody of it had passed to me without either of us marking the moment — and strapped it across their own shoulders, seal outward, coral paint showing through the mud like a wound that had finally stopped bleeding.

"One condition," I said, as we formed up at the edge of the trees.

Astrid raised an eyebrow.

"When they ask who recovered the documents, you tell them. All of it. The chest came out of the flood, but it was hunted home by a Vellmarki. My people spent twenty years being a rumor. I'm done being a rumor."

"Mira." Astrid's voice went gentle in a way that would have offended me if I hadn't spent a week learning their gentleness was never pity. "When they ask, I'm going to introduce you as the co-author of everything that happens next. Starting with the railways." They patted the chest. "And ending with whatever we build over that tunnel. Which will have both our signatures on the survey plates, or it doesn't get built."

Below us, the church bell in Brassenford began to ring — not the hour, something longer, looser, the way bells ring when the ringers have decided the occasion outranks the schedule.

Tomas squinted down at the town. "They know," he said. "Somehow, they already know."

"Of course they know," I said, and started down through the trees toward the valley, toward the courts, toward the rest of my life. "The whole valley is listening. It has been for three days."

Behind us, the pass stayed shut, white and final, holding its dead and its secrets in equal custody. Ahead, the bell went on ringing, and I walked toward it with my chin up, an exile no longer, though not yet anything else — and beside me, Astrid Ashgrove, engineer, murderer, witness, carried two hundred names down off the mountain like a man carrying fire through wind: carefully, openly, and without once looking back.

What we didn't know — what none of us could have known until the courthouse doors swung open and the clerk called the first name — was that we weren't the only ones who had come down off the mountain that morning.
