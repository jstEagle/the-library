## 32. The Inquiry at Brassenford

Brassenford smelled of coal smoke and wet wool and, underneath it all, the particular staleness of a town that had spent twenty years pretending nothing had happened. I noticed it the moment we came down off the eastern face — that civic odor of willful forgetting, like a house where someone has died and the family keeps all the windows shut.

The descent had taken nine days. Nine days of Mira setting a pace just short of cruel, of Tomas walking beside me in silence because he'd said everything worth saying on the mountain, of the other guide — Hallen, the one who'd lived — refusing to look at me until the third morning, when he'd finally said, "You came down to dig. That's more than most would've." It wasn't absolution. It wasn't meant to be. But it was enough to keep my legs moving.

Now there was a courtroom.

Well. Not a courtroom, exactly. The inquiry convened in the Brassenford drill hall, which meant rows of folding chairs facing a raised platform where three commissioners sat behind a table draped in green felt, with a company lawyer positioned to their right like a tumor they'd decided not to have removed. The walls still bore faded stencils of the municipal rifle club's targets. Someone had hung bunting. Bunting, for an inquiry into two hundred deaths. The town wanted this to feel like a fair, and I suppose in Brassenford's defense, it was the closest thing to a festival they'd had in a decade.

Mira sat beside me in the second row, close enough that our shoulders touched. She wore her good coat — the dark one with the Vellmarki stitching hidden under the collar, turned inward now, visible only if you knew to look. Half the hall was Vellmarki descendants who'd come down from the exile camps: quiet people in patched wool, holding photographs wrapped in handkerchiefs. The other half was railway men, and they looked at the Vellmarkis the way you look at weather you'd hoped would pass.

"You're doing the thing," Mira murmured.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you rehearse your own hanging so thoroughly you forget to breathe."

"I'm an engineer. We check our work."

"Your work," she said, "is going to be fine. Sit up."

I sat up. Old habits from mentors long dead or long disgraced, depending on which of us you asked.

* * *

The chest went into evidence before I did.

They'd set it on a trestle table at the front of the hall, and I watched the clerk unseal it with the reverence of a man opening a reliquary, which, in fairness, is what it was. The brass instruments caught the light. The survey plates lay in their oilcloth sleeves, each one annotated in my mentor Edvard Kellin's small, furious hand — the hand I'd known for fifteen years, the hand whose letters I'd once been ordered to burn and hadn't, the hand that had taught me that a survey doesn't lie but a man signing it can.

Commissioner Aldous Vay — the senior one, gray as slate, with the patient face of a man who'd spent his career listening to liars — asked me to state my name for the record.

"Astrid Ashgrove," I said. "Formerly chief assistant engineer, Corren Division, Consolidated Rail. Currently unemployed by choice."

A ripple of something through the hall — not quite laughter, not quite disapproval. Vay didn't smile. "And you are surrendering these documents voluntarily?"

"I am surrendering them because they belong to the dead, and the dead have waited twenty years for someone to deliver them."

The company lawyer — a sleek, unhurried man named Corvess, whose voice sounded like money being counted — rose to object on grounds of chain of custody, provenance, admissibility. He spoke for four minutes. I timed him against the station clock out of professional habit. Four minutes exactly, which told me he'd rehearsed, which told me the company had known what was coming since the wire stations started repeating names down the valley line three weeks ago.

Vay overruled him without looking at him. I began to suspect the commissioners were not entirely company men, and then I remembered that nobody was entirely anything anymore, and that hope is just a hypothesis you haven't tested yet.

Then Vay said, "Madam Ashgrove — or Mister Ashgrove; the record should reflect however you wish to be addressed—"

"Ashgrove is fine. Titles are how companies keep track of what they own."

"—the inquiry has received testimony regarding an avalanche on the western approach, during your ascent, which resulted in one death. Do you wish to address that matter now?"

There it was. The room shifted. I felt it the way you feel a rail joint under a train — a small hard knock traveling through everything, everyone straightening slightly. Mira's hand found my wrist under the bench. Tomas, three seats down, closed his eyes.

I had rehearsed this too, though not the way Mira thought. Not the hanging. The wording.

"Yes," I said, and stood, because some things you don't say sitting down. "On the twenty-third day of the crossing, I deliberately triggered a snow release above the old funicular track to stop a pursuit party from reaching the summit wire station before we could broadcast the Halden surveys. I used knowledge of slope loading and cornice structure that I learned in the employ of Consolidated Rail. Two guides were buried. One of them, Daven Ostrick, did not survive. His death is mine. I chose the method knowing its history — knowing that a man once used explosives and engineered collapse in those same mountains to bury two hundred workers alive. I told myself the scale made it different. It did not make it different. It made it smaller, which is worse. I am not asking this inquiry for understanding. I am asking it to write down what I did, in full, next to what was done to them, so that no one can ever use one to excuse the other."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Somewhere in the back a woman began to cry, quietly, the way people cry when crying is permitted for the first time in years.

Corvess leaned over to whisper to his junior counsel, and I watched him do the arithmetic — a confession freely given, on the record, unprompted. You could see him deciding whether to pounce on it as evidence of general lawlessness or let it stand as proof the fugitives weren't monsters. He chose, eventually, to adjust his cufflinks. Even predators know when the meat is poisoned.

Vay wrote for a long time. Then: "The inquiry accepts your statement. Madam Ashgrove, you understand you remain liable, and that this inquiry's findings may be referred for criminal proceedings?"

"I crossed a mountain range to put myself in front of this table. I'm not going to be surprised by it now."

* * *

They broke for the midday recess, and the hall emptied into a square where vendors had materialized as if summoned by the smell of grief — meat pies, roasted chestnuts, a man selling cheap tin compasses that wouldn't have found north in a barrel of magnets. Mira bought two pies and refused to tell me what she'd paid, which was her way of saying she'd overpaid and didn't care.

"You realize what you did in there," she said.

"I confessed to manslaughter."

"You gave them a story they can't cut apart. Before today, they could've painted you as a fugitive who happened to carry papers. Now you're a witness who happened to break a law and walked in the front door to say so." She bit into her pie and chewed with the efficiency of a woman who'd grown up eating in shifts. "My grandmother had a word for that kind of move. It translates roughly as 'turning the knife into a hinge.'"

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have run the railway better than the railway does."

"She ran a ferry. Same principle. Everything is about reading the current." She looked out at the square, at the Vellmarki families clustered near the fountain, at the children who'd never seen the country stitched inside their collars. "Tomas testifies tomorrow."

"I know."

"He's frightened. Renner's name carries weight in the valley towns. Men he drank with, men who owe him. And half of them are in that hall every day, taking notes for somebody."

"Then he's braver than I was. I hid for six years."

"You hid for six years and built a map of every dead line in the Correns while you did it. Don't confuse hiding with surrendering." She finished the pie, wiped her hands on her coat, and fixed me with the look she used on glaciers — the one that assessed load-bearing capacity. "One more thing. Corvess requested the inquiry admit company maintenance records for Halden Pass. Sealed records. He thinks they'll show the tunnel was sound and the collapse was geological."

"And are they?"

"They're forged. Badly. The ink batch dates are wrong — I checked against the camp records my mother kept. But the commissioners won't know that unless someone tells them, and the person who can prove it—" she tapped her own collar, where the hidden stitching lay, "—has spent twenty years being told her people's paper isn't real paper."

I looked at her for a while. "You're going to testify."

"I'm going to testify," she agreed. "Tomorrow. After Tomas. And Ashgrove — whatever happens in there, when I'm done, they're going to come for me the way they came for you. Slower. With paperwork instead of guns. I need to know you'll still be standing next to me when it's boring."

"That's the only way I know how to stand next to anyone."

* * *

The afternoon session belonged to the plates.

They brought in a projection lantern — an antique, borrowed from a schoolhouse — and put the survey overlays onto a bedsheet strung between two poles, and for two hours the drill hall watched the mountain confess. The tunnel cross-sections. The charge placements, marked in Kellin's hand with dates and initials. The blast schedule signed by a supervisor whose signature matched, stroke for stroke, the signature on Ivo Renner's employment file, which Corvess had entered into evidence himself that morning in what I can only assume was an act of confidence bordering on religious faith.

I watched the room watch the sheets. An old man in the third row — a rail widow, I'd learn later, married forty days before Halden — kept her eyes closed through most of it, as if she could hear the drawings better that way. A young company clerk took notes until he stopped taking notes and just stared. That's how it goes, when the truth finally gets projected at scale. First people argue with it. Then they take notes on it. Then they stare at it, because arguing requires believing there's another side, and there isn't. There's just the mountain, and what was done inside it.

Kellin's initials appeared on the final plate — E.K., countersigned, dated eleven days before the collapse. Eleven days. Long enough to file objections. Long enough to evacuate. Long enough, if a man had chosen, to die trying to stop it instead of living to document it.

He'd chosen documentation. Everyone had called it cowardice, afterward, including me, for a long time, from inside my own exile. Sitting in that hall, watching his handwriting taller than a person on a bedsheet, I understood it differently. He hadn't failed to act. He'd built the case and carried it into the flood, and the river had held it for twenty years until it washed up against the door of the one engineer stubborn enough to dredge it out.

The dead give you time, Mira had said once, on the glacier. They're patient in a way the living can't afford to be.

* * *

That evening, in the boarding house where the inquiry had billeted us — two narrow rooms, a stove that smoked on principle — Tomas knocked on my door.

He stood in the hallway holding his hat, turning it, a big weathered man reduced to fidgeting like a boy outside a schoolroom. "Couldn't sleep," he said. "Thought I'd look at the chest again. If that's allowed."

"It's evidence. It's locked in the clerk's office."

"Right. Yes. Of course." He didn't leave. He looked at the floor, the wall, the smoking stove through my open door. Then: "Twenty years I worked for that company. Twenty years, and I guided for Renner because the pay was steady and I told myself the past was the past." He turned the hat again. "On the mountain, when they dug me out — I saw his face before I blacked out. Not angry. Calculating. Deciding whether I was worth the labor of digging. I've seen that face before, Ashgrove. I saw it in a photograph in my father's things, taken at the Halden worksite, spring of the last year. A young supervisor. Everyone always said the fuse-man died in the collapse, that it was an accident that took even its own. But fuse-men don't survive collapses. Supervisors who step back early do."

"Tomas—"

"Tomorrow I'm going to say his name in front of everyone," he said, "and I needed to practice saying it to a person first, so it doesn't come out wrong in front of a room." He swallowed. "Ivo Renner. Ivo Renner. There. Twice. Once more for luck." He put his hat on, crooked, and managed something that was almost a smile. "Get some sleep, engineer. Tomorrow the mountain finishes testifying."

He went down the stairs heavily, each step deliberate, a man pacing himself for a long walk.

I stood in the doorway a while after he'd gone. Down the hall, through Mira's half-open door, I could see her at the window, awake, watching the street below where — I followed her gaze — a figure stood under the gas lamp across the way, not moving, not leaving, taking notes by lamplight for somebody.

Mira caught me looking. She didn't wave. She just reached up and turned her collar outward, letting the Vellmarki stitching show to the empty street, and left the lamp burning.

Tomorrow, then. The knife into the hinge.
