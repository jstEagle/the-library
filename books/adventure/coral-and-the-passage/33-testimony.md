## 33. Testimony

I have been in courtrooms exactly twice in my life. The first time, I was nineteen, and I was fined three crowns for riding the freight cars without a ticket, which even then struck me as a strange economy — the railway fined me for using the railway, like charging a man for breathing his own air. The second time is now, and there are no fines here. There are two hundred names on a board behind the magistrate's bench, chalked up in Astrid Ashgrove's own hand, and every morning when the inquiry opens, the clerk dusts them off as if they might smudge.

My name is Tomas Brandt. For twenty years it has been nothing — a name on no ledger, attached to no property, owed to no one. I dug coal, I laid track, I kept my mouth shut. A man can build an entire life out of keeping his mouth shut. I built a very good one.

Then Renner's avalanche came down on us at the switchback, and Ashgrove climbed down into the snow to dig us out, and somewhere under all that white I decided my mouth-keeping days were over.

* * *

The inquiry sits in Brassenford's old customs hall, which the town has scrubbed and hung with bunting for the occasion, the way you'd dress up a barn for a wedding. It holds perhaps four hundred people. There are four hundred people in it by half past seven every morning, and another hundred outside who listen through the open windows because the weather has turned mild and the magistrates, to their credit, have not ordered them shut.

The Vellmarki sit together on the left side, maybe sixty of them, wearing the colors of a country that does not exist. Blue and rust-red sashes. Some of the older ones still carry the accent like a stone in the shoe — you can hear it when they stand to swear their oaths, that soft rolling of the r's, and every time it happens the room goes quiet in a way that isn't hostile. It's the quiet of people listening to a language come back from the dead.

I testify on the fourth day. They seat me in the witness box and the clerk reads my name into the record — *Tomas Brandt, formerly of Halden Pass, laborer, grade two* — and something about hearing "formerly" said out loud makes my hands shake on the rail. Twenty years of formerly.

The lead counsel is a thin woman named Ostrava with spectacles she cleans when she wants you to wait. She asks me where I was born.

"Vellmark," I say. "A town called Dren. You won't find it."

"Why won't we find it?"

"Because your maps were redrawn." I nod toward the wall of names. "Because everything was redrawn."

She lets that sit. Then: "Mr. Brandt, tell the inquiry what you did before the collapse."

"I drilled charge holes in the east gallery of the Halden tunnel. Two hundred and eleven of us worked the pass shift. We were told the mountain was unstable above the bore, that the company had ordered a controlled venting of a gas pocket. Standard work. Dangerous, but standard."

"And what actually happened?"

And here is the thing nobody tells you about testimony: you rehearse it a thousand times in the dark, and when the moment comes, none of the rehearsal helps. What comes out instead is the truth in its work clothes.

"A man came down the gallery at the end of shift. He wasn't one of ours. He wore a company coat with the collar up, and he carried a detonator case, and he walked the line of charges like a farmer walking a fence. Checking his posts. Then he went back up the ventilation shaft, and twenty minutes later the mountain came down on us."

"Did you see this man's face?"

"No, ma'am. But I saw his boots. Company issue, size thirteen, with a repaired seam on the left toe. And I saw him again, twenty years later, standing over me in the snow at the switchback while I couldn't breathe, and the seam was still there."

You could have heard a pin drop in that hall, except that in Brassenford they don't drop pins, they drop hammers, and what you heard instead was four hundred people not saying anything at all, which is louder.

Renner's counsel objected, of course. Objected to everything. He was a soft-bellied man from the capital with a voice like wet paper, and he spent most of the week rising to his feet to say *inflammatory, speculative, prejudicial*, as though the words themselves were sandbags he could stack against the flood. The magistrates let him stack. The flood came anyway.

* * *

They brought the ledgers in on the sixth day, and I want to describe those ledgers properly, because the newspapers got it wrong. The papers called them *a trove*. They were not a trove. They were worse than a trove. They were boring.

Eleven volumes of company accounts covering the years around the collapse, seized from the district office after Ashgrove's broadcast went down the wire and half the valley's clerks suddenly remembered they had consciences. Ostrava walked the inquiry through them page by page, and the room learned what I already knew in my bones, which is that evil keeps excellent books.

Here is how it unravels, layer by layer, and I watched it happen from the public benches with my heart going like a trip-hammer:

First layer. The payroll. In the quarter of the collapse, the Halden Pass workforce is listed at two hundred eleven men. In the following quarter, the same men appear on a different ledger — transferred, the entries say, to the Corren South extension. Except the Corren South extension employed forty men that year, and its payroll names are all accounted for elsewhere. Two hundred and eleven men had been moved onto a page that led nowhere. The company hadn't even buried them properly in its own arithmetic. It had just stopped writing them down.

Second layer. The explosives. Requisition orders for blasting powder at Halden run triple the engineering estimates for the entire quarter — enough charge, Ostrava demonstrated with a surveyor's calm that made Ashgrove look like an amateur by comparison, to bring down not a gas pocket but a gallery, deliberately, from a shaft only accessible to management. Beside one requisition, in the margin, in a small precise hand: *approved — I.R.*

Third layer. The insurance. This was the one that made the workingmen in the gallery start swearing under their breath. Six weeks before the collapse, the company had taken out a policy against "geological failure of the Halden bore," insuring the tunnel works for a sum that exceeded the cost of the entire pass project. After the collapse, the claim was paid. The mountain was unstable; the mountain collapsed; the company collected. The workers inside were listed in the claim documentation as *equipment losses, non-recoverable*.

Equipment losses. Non-recoverable. My brother Joren was equipment. My cousin Alek was equipment. The boy who used to sing Vellmarki harvest songs in the dark until the foreman told him to pipe down — equipment, non-recoverable.

An old woman in the front row of the exile section stood up during that part of the reading, and nobody asked her to sit down, and she stood for the better part of an hour with her hand pressed flat against her chest like she was holding a door shut.

Fourth layer. The letters. Private correspondence between the district director and the capital, subpoenaed late in the week, discussing "the Vellmarki question at Halden" — meaning, in the company's dialect, that too many of the pass workers were Vellmarki nationals, that Vellmark was about to be erased from the maps, and that a disaster at the pass would be *politically convenient*. One letter, dated nine days before the collapse, contains a sentence I will carry to my grave: *"Should the mountain object, the timing could scarcely be improved."*

The mountain objected. The timing was improved. And a man with a repaired seam on his left boot lit the fuse.

* * *

Renner sat through all of it.

I want to be honest about that, because I expected him to be a monster, and monsters are easy, and he was not easy. He sat in the dock in a good grey suit with his hands folded, and he listened to the ledgers being read the way an engineer listens to a bridge report — professionally, attentively, occasionally nodding as though confirming a figure he'd already verified himself. When the insurance entry was read aloud, he inclined his head slightly, and I swear on my brother's name it looked like pride.

He spoke only once that week, in his own defense, and it lasted four minutes, and I have thought about those four minutes every night since.

He did not deny the ledgers. He did not deny the fuse. He stood, adjusted his cuffs, and addressed the magistrates in the tone of a man correcting a minor error in a timetable.

"Gentlemen. The pass was a liability. The workforce was a liability. The nation those workers belonged to was, by treaty, a liability scheduled for dissolution. I resolved a liability. Every figure in those books balances. You may convict me of the method, but do not insult this inquiry by pretending the outcome was not desired by everyone in this room who ever rode a train through those mountains and never once asked what held it up."

Then he sat down, and the wet-paper counsel gathered his papers, and the room was so quiet I could hear the chalk dust settling on the two hundred names.

That was the moment I understood him, finally. He wasn't ashamed. He wasn't even afraid, not really. He believed the books balanced, and in his religion a balanced book is innocence itself. Ashgrove had beaten him on the wire and buried his funicular and dragged his name down the line station by station, but none of it had touched him, because in his arithmetic witnesses were just figures awaiting reconciliation.

The magistrates were going to need more than figures to reconcile him. They were going to need the one thing he didn't keep books on.

* * *

The exiles testified on the eighth and ninth days, and I sat with them, because Mira Vell had put my name forward and the elders had voted — actual votes, counted aloud, a thing I hadn't seen since Dren — to let a half-blooded track worker stand with them.

They testified to the erasure. That was the word everyone used, *erasure*, and it took me two days to stop flinching at it. A schoolteacher from the eastern camps described the morning the maps changed and the border posts came down and her father burned the family papers so the census men wouldn't find them. A ferryman — one of Mira's river kin — described the decree that struck Vellmarki names from shipping registries, so that boats their grandparents had built became ownerless overnight. An old surveyor, bent as a question mark, produced a pre-collapse map of the Corren Range with the word VELLMARK printed across the high country in faded gold leaf, and laid it beside the modern edition, where the same mountains bear a company logo.

"The mountains did not move," he said. "Only the ink."

And then, on the ninth day, they read the names.

Not the two hundred eleven — those had been read into the telegraph record weeks ago, and the inquiry had formally entered the transcript. These were the others. The families scattered. The ones who died in the camps and the road gangs afterward, in the years when having a Vellmarki name was itself a kind of sentence. The clerk had ruled that these names fell outside the inquiry's scope, and the chief magistrate — a dry stick of a man named Halloran who surprised everyone that week, including, I think, himself — overruled his own clerk from the bench.

"The scope of this inquiry," Halloran said, "is what happened at Halden Pass. What happened at Halden Pass cannot be understood without what happened to Vellmark. Read the names."

So they read them. It took most of the day. People fainted. People sang. At one point the whole left side of the hall was humming an old lament in unison, low, barely a sound at all, and the stenographer gave up trying to transcribe it and simply wrote in the margin: *the room sang.*

I did not sing. I am not good at belonging, even when it's handed to me. But I wrote my brother's name in the air with my finger, the way our mother taught us, so it would go somewhere even if no book recorded it.

* * *

On the tenth day, Ostrava recalled me.

"Mr. Brandt. You were buried in the avalanche at the switchback, alongside two of Renner's hired guides. You survived. One guide survived. One did not. Do you know why you were on that mountain?"

"Because I recognized Ivo Renner," I said. "And someone in his camp decided I was a loose end."

"You understand that this inquiry cannot try that act. It falls under criminal assize."

"I understand it, ma'am. I'm not here for justice on that. I'm here because Ashgrove climbed down into the snow to dig out the men sent to kill them, and one of those men died anyway, and Ashgrove carries it like a stone. I've seen what carrying stones does to engineers." I looked at the dock. "Somebody in this room should set one down, and it isn't going to be them."

Renner looked at me then — the first time all week he'd looked at anyone directly — and there was nothing in his face. That was the frightening part. Not hatred, not contempt. Inventory. He was counting me, the way he'd once counted charges in a gallery, deciding which ones to light.

Halloran adjourned early that day. As the hall emptied, the old woman with her hand on her chest passed my bench, and she touched my shoulder without a word, and I found I was crying, quietly, like a leak in a pipe, and I let it run, because the hall was emptying and the mountains outside were turning gold with evening and for the first time in twenty years I was crying in a room where the doors were open.

* * *

The summations came on the twelfth day. The wet-paper counsel spoke for two hours and proved that a man can drown in his own adjectives. Ostrava spoke for forty minutes and needed thirty-five of them only because she read the insurance clause aloud twice.

*"Equipment losses, non-recoverable,"* she said. *"Two hundred and eleven human beings, entered as equipment. The defense tells you the books balance. He is right. They balance perfectly. That is precisely the horror. Somewhere in this company there was always a column for the dead, and it was always in credit."*

She turned to the magistrates last.

"You have heard the surveys. You have heard the ledgers. You have heard the wire transcript that ran from station to station down the whole closed line, repeated by clerks who were children when this crime was committed. The mountain has testified. The paper has testified. The living have testified. All that remains is for the law to decide whether it is also a witness — or whether it, too, keeps a column."

Halloran took off his spectacles. Rubbed the bridge of his nose. Looked for a long moment at the chalked names behind him, all two hundred and eleven — the count had been corrected upward by the ledgers, three men recovered from the payroll who'd been missed by every list for twenty years — and then he said the words I had waited two decades to hear:

"This inquiry finds sufficient grounds. Ivo Renner will stand trial for the murders at Halden Pass. Bail is denied. Court will reconvene for sentencing arguments in—"

And that was as far as he got, because the hall erupted — the exiles on their feet, the singing starting again, hats thrown, the window-listeners outside cheering loud enough to scatter pigeons off the customs house roof — and under all of it, in the dock, Ivo Renner sat perfectly still with his hands folded, doing sums.

I know, because I watched him. And I saw the exact moment the arithmetic failed — the moment his eyes flicked to the door, where the bailiffs were coming, and then to the magistrates, and then, briefly, unbearably, to me — and I saw him understand that some columns don't reconcile, that some debts get called in full, in public, in front of everyone.

But I also saw something else, in the last second before they took him down, and it's the reason I haven't slept properly since.

He smiled. Just slightly. Just at the corner of his mouth. The smile of a man who has been counting this whole time — the trial, the witnesses, the ledgers entered into evidence, every name now permanently on the public record — and who has realized, too late for anyone's comfort, that a public record cuts both ways.

Sentencing arguments reconvene Monday. And Monday morning, before the court opens, a clerk from the company's capital office arrived in Brassenford on the night train, carrying a sealed briefcase, asking — very politely, very quietly — to see the presiding magistrate.

Whatever is in that briefcase, it was heavy enough that he carried it in both arms.
