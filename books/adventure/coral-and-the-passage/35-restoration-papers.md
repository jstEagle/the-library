## 35. Restoration Papers

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were the day my mother had done laundry and my father had done nothing in particular, and if a nation is going to come back from the dead, it ought to do so on a day associated with ordinary chores. Nothing about Vellmark had ever been dramatic when it was alive. It was only its death that had theatrical flair.

The letter came by post rider to the shrine camp — an actual post rider, on an actual horse, carrying actual paper with a wax seal that made three of the elders weep before anyone had broken it. The seal was the double mountain and the river fork, pressed in blue wax instead of the old gold, because gold was for a country that could afford vanity and we were, for the moment, a country that could afford only hope.

I read it standing at the edge of the camp, where the shrine of names caught the morning light. Two hundred and eleven names now. Astrid's broadcast had added eleven that nobody in exile had known for certain — men written off as deserters, their families told they'd run south with company money. Eleven families who had spent twenty years ashamed of sons and fathers who had died loyal, buried under Halden Pass.

I want you to understand what that did to us. Not the recognition process — that was paperwork, committees, a border commission from three neighboring states who all suddenly remembered Vellmark existed once the railway scandal broke. Paperwork I could handle. But those eleven names, read out over the wire by a disgraced engineer with mud still under their fingernails — that was the first time in two decades the mountains had spoken our dead aloud.

The letter said, in formal Old Vellmarki script that half the camp could no longer read:

*To the Assembly of the Exiled, greetings from the Provisional Commission for the Restoration of Vellmark. Be advised that the process of international recognition has commenced. Be further advised that the Commission requires a Surveyor-General of the Historic Lines: one versed in the old alignments, the closed corridors, and the passes. Nominations close at the autumn assembly.*

Nominations. As though this were a parish council seat.

* * *

"They mean you," said Aunt Halva, not looking up from her mending. Halva was ninety-one and had survived three deportations and one marriage, and she ranked them in that order of difficulty.

"I assumed they meant someone with credentials," I said.

"You have credentials. You've spent your whole life memorizing a country that doesn't exist. That's either madness or qualification, and governments have never been able to tell the difference."

She bit her thread and squinted at me over her spectacles.

"Besides," she added, "who else would they ask? Petra? Petra gets lost walking to the well."

Petra, from her tent ten feet away: "I heard that."

"It was meant to be heard."

I sat down on the shrine bench, the one Astrid and I had dragged up from the ruined station two summers ago, and looked at the names. My grandmother's name was there, third column, carved shallow because the carver had been crying. She hadn't died in the tunnel. She'd died in the winter after, in a resettlement camp, of what the company doctor recorded as "heart failure" and what everyone else recorded as grief. But her name was on the wall because the wall didn't distinguish between ways of being killed. That had always seemed right to me.

"Say something useful, Halva," I said.

"Take it," she said. "Then refuse half of it."

"That's not advice, that's a riddle."

"Good advice usually is." She threaded a new needle without looking. "They'll want a Surveyor-General who says yes to everything. Signs where they point. Draws the lines where the money wants them. You know who does that? Men like the one who lit the fuse under Halden. A surveyor-general who can't say no is just a fuse with better handwriting."

I thought about Renner then — his ledgers, his calm, the way he'd spoken of justice as a column to be balanced. He was awaiting trial in Brassenford, and the papers called him "the accused" with the delicate horror of newspapers discovering a monster who wore a good coat. I thought about how many yes-men it had taken to make him possible. Whole departments of them. A civilization of nodding heads.

"And the other half?" I asked.

"The other half you refuse is whatever they offer you to make you quiet. There will be something. There's always something. A house. A pension. A nice office in the capital with a view of nothing important." She snapped the thread. "Refuse it loudly enough that everyone hears you refusing."

* * *

Astrid came up from the valley the following week, riding the new spur line as far as it went and walking the rest, because Astrid Ashgrove had opinions about riding horses that I'd heard twice and did not need a third time.

They looked different than they had on the glacier. Fed, mostly. Sleeping, apparently. They'd taken the suspended sentence with a grace that fooled the newspapers but not me; I'd seen them at the inquiry, hands flat on the table, describing the avalanche they'd set and the guide they hadn't reached in time, in the same level voice they used for gradient calculations. Tomas had testified the day after. Renner's lawyers had objected eleven times. The commission had sustained none of them.

"You got the letter," Astrid said, dropping onto the bench beside me. Not a question. News travels faster than post riders.

"Everyone got the letter. It was read aloud at dinner like scripture."

"And?"

"And I'm thinking."

Astrid nodded slowly, watching the shrine. We'd fallen into an ease together that still surprised me — two people who had met as adversaries over a stolen chest, who had cut an ice bridge anchor between them and a hunter, who had shared a fire through a whiteout across a stone wall neither trusted. Somewhere in all that, calculation had worn down into something plainer. Not friendship exactly. Friendship implies leisure. What we had was more like two survivors of the same shipwreck who keep finding each other on different rafts.

"For what it's worth," Astrid said, "the old lines are a mess. I've seen the company's restoration surveys — the ones they're submitting to the border commission. They've redrawn half the alignments to avoid the tunnel. They're going to route around Halden Pass entirely, call it a geologic hazard, bury the whole history under a detour."

"They wouldn't."

"They would. It's cheaper. And every year the pass goes unused, the easier it is to pretend it never happened. Maps are how countries forget, Mira. I know because I drew the forgetting myself, once." They turned a stone over in their fingers, an old habit. "If someone who remembers takes the Surveyor-General's chair, the lines stay honest. If someone convenient takes it—"

"—we get a monument instead of a memory."

"A plaque," Astrid agreed grimly. "Plaques are how the living apologize to the dead without changing anything."

We sat with that. Below us, the camp children were playing a game that involved shouting the station names of the old northern line in order — Brassenford, Kelv, Ostramoor, Halden — and shrieking when someone got one wrong. Two years ago most of them couldn't have named five stations. Now they raced each other through the whole network. Halva had taught them. Halva taught everything; she considered dying before finishing the curriculum a personal failure.

"My mother wanted me to be a ferry-woman," I said. "Like her. Like her mother. Three generations of Vell women moving people across water, asking no questions, keeping no records. 'Records get people killed,' she said. She wasn't wrong. But she also wasn't right." I watched the children shout *Ostramoor*. "When Vellmark fell, the ferries kept running. Nobody asked whose flag was on the far bank. We moved refugees, smugglers, company men, all the same fare. I used to think that was integrity. Neutrality as a kind of honor." I shook my head. "It was just fear with good posture."

Astrid said nothing, which was one of the things I'd learned to value about them. They listened the way good engineers listen to a bridge — waiting for the sound underneath the sound.

"If I take it," I said, "I take it my way. Every alignment surveyed in the open. Every closure documented. The Halden route rebuilt or formally consecrated — one or the other, but not quietly abandoned. And the names on the new station walls. All of them. That's not negotiable, and if the Commission thinks it is, they can nominate Petra."

From her tent: "I accept."

* * *

The autumn assembly met in the old customs house at the river fork — a building that had spent twenty years as a granary and smelled like it, dust and barley and the ghost of officialdom. Delegates came from every settlement: the valley exiles, the mountain clans, a contingent from across the eastern border who'd kept the language alive in secret schools, and one ancient man who claimed to be the last employee of the Vellmarki postal service and had brought, as evidence of his continuing authority, a rubber stamp.

The nomination went the way Halva predicted. A lawyer from the eastern settlements — polished, reasonable, fluent in three bureaucracies — proposed himself with a speech about "pragmatic integration of historic infrastructure into modern regional frameworks," which I translated, for the benefit of the room, as "let's not make the neighbors uncomfortable."

I stood up to decline the nomination. That had been my plan on the walk over: decline it, name someone else, go back to the ferry crossing and my maps and my small incorruptible life.

What came out of my mouth instead was different.

"I'll take it," I said, "on conditions."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms do when someone who was expected to say no starts making demands.

"One. Every survey conducted in public. Published, posted, argued over. No closed sessions, no sealed annexes. The last time these lines were drawn in secret, two hundred people died inside them."

Murmurs. Heads nodding. The lawyer's smile developing a crack.

"Two. The Halden corridor will be surveyed, documented, and either rebuilt or consecrated as burial ground — decided by the families, not by the Commission, and certainly not by cost estimates. There will be no detour around it. We do not route around our dead."

Silence now. Real silence. Somewhere a child outside dropped something and was hushed.

"Three. I will not hold property, pension, or residence from the Commission. I keep my ferry. I keep my camp. If the Restoration wants a Surveyor-General, it gets one who can't be bought because she owns nothing worth buying." I looked at the lawyer. "And four. Anyone who voted to keep the surveys buried at the split counsel last spring recuses themselves from the oversight committee. We've already had one vote on whether truth was convenient. Truth won. I don't intend to re-litigate it annually."

An elder from the eastern delegation — old Soren, who'd walked forty miles on legs that hated him — began clapping before he finished standing up, which meant he clapped and rose simultaneously, like a slow-motion miracle. Then the room followed.

Halva, beside me, leaned over. "You refused the other half," she murmured. "Loudly. Good girl."

"I haven't refused anything yet."

"You will. They'll offer you the capital office within the month. Watch."

* * *

She was right, of course. She was ninety-one; being right was less a skill than a habit by then. The Commission offered me a residence in the provisional capital, a stipend, and a title with more syllables than duties. I declined all three in a letter that was read aloud at the next assembly, to general satisfaction and the lawyer's visible indigestion.

But here is the part I didn't expect, the part I'm still learning to sit with.

On the last evening of the assembly, they asked me to sign the first document of the restored state — not the declaration, that came later, but the survey charter, the thing that would govern how every line was measured and every measurement published. They brought it to me on a plain table under a lamp, and Soren handed me a pen, and the whole hall waited.

I looked at the signature line for a long time.

My mother had signed nothing in her life but ferry manifests. Her mother had signed nothing at all — couldn't write, marked with an X that some clerk had circled with contempt. A hundred years of Vell women, moving people across water, keeping no records, because records got people killed and signatures got people erased.

And here was a piece of paper that wanted my name on it precisely so that things could *not* be erased.

I signed it *Mira Vell*, and beneath it, because the form asked for a place of origin, I wrote the name of a village that has no ferry anymore, no houses, no church — a village that exists now only in the mouths of people who remember it. Writing it felt like setting a bone. It hurt in the exact way that means the healing has started.

Halva squeezed my arm hard enough to bruise. "There," she said. "Belonging. Terrible inconvenience. You'll get used to it."

* * *

Winter came early that year, and with it, a letter forwarded from the valley post — Astrid's handwriting, cramped and hurried, the handwriting of someone who writes the way they talk, too fast for the page.

*Mira —*

*The Commission's preliminary alignment drawings crossed my desk (don't ask how; Tomas owes me). The southern approach looks wrong. Gradient's fine, drainage's fine, but they're cutting through the old Vellmarki boundary markers, and I don't think it's accident. Someone wants the border question settled by bulldozer before the recognition vote.*

*I could be paranoid. I've earned a little paranoia. But you should see it yourself before the ground freezes.*

*Come south. Bring your instruments and your stubbornness. I'll bring the coffee and the bad news.*

*A.*

I read it twice by the stove, then folded it into the chest pocket of my coat — the coat with the faded crest on the cuff, the one that started everything, which I had never gotten around to replacing and now never would.

Outside, snow was starting again, fat and unhurried, filling in the tracks between the tents. The children had gone quiet. On the shrine wall, two hundred and eleven names held the firelight.

I got my instruments down from the shelf and started packing.

Some habits, it turns out, are load-bearing.
