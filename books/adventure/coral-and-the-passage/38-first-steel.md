## 38. First Steel

I have carried many things across the Corren Range — a chest of dead men's testimony, a grudge the size of a nation, a rope I trusted more than any person for fifteen years. But I had never carried a rail joint. They weigh more than you'd think. Everything worth carrying does.

The morning they laid the first steel above Halden Pass, I was up before the sun, which surprised no one who knew me and annoyed everyone who didn't. The camp was already stirring below me — canvas and cook-smoke and the coughing of an engine being persuaded to care about its work. Astrid's engine. Their baby, if you could call four hundred tons of iron and stubbornness a baby, and knowing Astrid Ashgrove, you absolutely could.

I stood at the edge of the work plateau with my hands in my pockets and watched the light come up over the eastern face, the one we'd descended two winters ago with snow closing behind us like a door. From up here you couldn't see the old tunnel mouth. The mountain had folded it away under scree and new grass, the way mountains do — not forgiving, just tidying.

"You're up early," said a voice behind me, and there was Astrid, two mugs in hand, steam rising off both. They handed me one without asking what I drank. Nobody asks anymore. That's what three years of shared surveys does to a person.

"Somebody has to watch them do it wrong," I said.

"They won't do it wrong."

"They'll do it *your* way, which is worse, because then I can't even complain honestly."

Astrid smiled into their mug. Three years ago that smile would have cost something. Now it came free, most mornings, and I had learned — slowly, the way I learn everything, by nearly dying about it — to stop counting the price.

Below us, the gang was assembling the first length of rail on fresh-cut bed. There's a ceremony to it that engineers pretend isn't a ceremony. The plates, the bolts, the gauge checked twice because Astrid had personally threatened to check it a third time and everyone knew she would. I watched a young Vellmarki worker — Danya, seventeen, all elbows and certainty — kneel at the joint with a wrench bigger than her forearm and set the first bolt home.

The sound it made was very small. A click, really. Metal finding metal.

You would not believe how loud a small sound can be, if you've waited your whole life to hear it.

* * *

Vellmark was erased from every map twenty years before I was old enough to understand what maps were for. I grew up in exile camps where our language survived in kitchens and lullabies and nowhere official, where children learned geography from memory because the borders we remembered had been declared a clerical error. When I tell people this, they nod sympathetically, and then they ask when we're "moving on." As though grief were a ferry crossing and you simply wait for the next boat.

Mira Vell does not move on. Mira Vell keeps ledgers.

But here is what nobody tells you about keeping ledgers: eventually the numbers start to mean something other than debt. The morning of the first steel, I stood on that plateau doing arithmetic I never thought I'd do. Two hundred names carved into the new station wall, finished last autumn, each letter cut by hand because Astrid refused the machine — "the machine didn't know them," she said, "we did, or we should have." Two hundred names, and beneath them, in smaller letters, the words *THE MOUNTAIN TESTIFIED*. And now: rail. Actual rail, going through Halden Pass properly, with drainage and grade calculations and rock bolts and every safety provision that twenty years ago would have been called expensive and was now called standard, because standards are just graves that got counted.

Danya set the second bolt. Then the third. The gang moved down the rail like water finding its level, and I found I had to look away, briefly, at the mountains, because my eyes were doing something undignified.

"Wind," I said.

"It's a still morning," Astrid said.

"It's a windy morning."

"Mira."

"I will push you off this plateau."

Astrid laughed — actually laughed, out loud, in front of the whole crew, who pretended very hard not to notice because they were fond of her and also afraid of me, in roughly equal measure. And I let the wind have my eyes for another moment, and then I went down to work, because mourning folded into forward motion is still motion, and motion is the only prayer I've ever trusted.

* * *

The minute of silence happened at noon, at the old tunnel mouth.

Astrid had planned it the way she plans everything — obsessively, secretly, and with a contingency for weather. The whole crew walked up from the plateau along the old survey path, the one Mira cut three years ago with a machete and a bad attitude, and gathered before the collapsed portal. The mountain had half-swallowed it. What remained was a mouth of broken concrete and twisted steel ribs, furred with lichen now, a dark seam where the light gave out. Two hundred workers had gone in through that seam. None of them came out. The company said it was the mountain's fault. The mountain, as it turned out, kept excellent records.

Astrid stood at the front with Tomas — yes, that Tomas, the guide they dug out of the avalanche, the one who put his hand on Renner's shoulder at the inquiry and said, *I know your face; you lit the fuse* — and beside him old Sefa from the exile camp, who is older than the erasure itself and walks like the ground owes her money.

Nobody made a speech. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger heaven keeps for things done right: nobody made a speech. Astrid had drafted one — I saw the pages in her coat pocket for a week, folded and refolded until they went soft as cloth — and at the last minute she left them folded.

She took out her father's brass compass instead, the one that rode the console of the inaugural train, and held it up so the crew could see it catch the light.

"This pointed true," she said, "even when everything else didn't. One minute. For the two hundred."

And then silence.

I have stood in silences before. The silence after verdicts. The silence of the glacier calving, which is less a silence than a held breath before thunder. The silence of the exile camp at night, two hundred families breathing in the dark, keeping count of themselves. This one was different. This one had steel in it. Two hundred living workers standing before a grave, and behind them, faint but unmistakable, the smell of hot iron and creosote and the new century coming through the pass whether anyone liked it or not.

Sefa broke the silence, as she breaks everything, by singing. Just four bars, the old mourning verse, the one that starts *the river gives up its dead* — which, I will note for the record, Astrid stole shamelessly for her memoir title, and which Sefa forgave precisely once, in exchange for permanent first-class passage on any train through the pass forever. The crew didn't know the words. They hummed anyway. You don't need the words. That was always the point of Vellmark: you don't need the words, you need the people willing to stand in the cold and hum.

Then Astrid said, "Back to work," in exactly the voice she used to say it during the crossing, and everybody laughed, and the minute ended, and we went forward. Mourning folded into forward motion. Fold it small enough and you can carry it anywhere.

* * *

That evening I found Danya at the station wall.

The wall runs along the platform's north face, granite hauled down from the ridge, and the names go letter by letter in Astrid's own hand-guided chisel work. Danya was tracing one of them with her finger — *HALVARSEN, E.* — the way you'd touch a scar to check it's real.

"My grandmother's brother," she said, without looking at me. She knew it was me; everyone knows my footsteps by now, I walk like I'm still checking ice. "He went into the tunnel the day it closed. She talked about him like he'd stepped out to buy bread and gotten lost for fifty years."

"He did, more or less," I said. "History lost him. Different kind of lost, same kind of gone."

"She cried when the verdict came. Not happy crying. Angry crying. She said, 'Now they admit it, and admitting it doesn't dig him out.'" Danya turned to look at me. She has her grandmother's eyes, flat grey, unimpressed by the world. "Does it? Dig him out?"

"No," I said. "Nothing digs anyone out. We're not in the digging-out business. We're in the naming business. It's smaller and it lasts longer."

She considered that with the seriousness of the young, who haven't yet learned which questions are rhetorical. "Is that why you hunted the chest your whole life? For the naming?"

"I hunted it because it was mine to hunt," I said. "The chest didn't care whose grief it served. That's the thing about truth — it's the only property that gets heavier the more people carry it, and lighter too. Both at once. Don't ask me how. Ask an engineer."

"Astrid says you ask an engineer and they say 'it depends' and then bill you."

"Astrid," I said, "bills correctly."

Danya almost smiled. Then she looked back at the name, and her voice dropped to the register the young use when they're saying something they've decided to mean. "When I'm old, I'm going to tell children I helped lay the first rail through Halden Pass. And I'm going to tell them about the two hundred first. Every time. Names first, then trains."

"That," I told her, "is the entire religion. Congratulations on your ordination."

* * *

Astrid found me later at the plateau's edge, where I go to do my ledger-keeping, which these days consists mostly of looking at mountains and pretending I'm not proud of anything.

"The offer came through again today," they said, settling beside me on the cold stone. "Brassenford office wants a dedication plaque for the tunnel bypass. They've asked what it should say."

"What did you tell them?"

"'The mountain testified.' Their exact words back were, 'Isn't that a bit dramatic?'"

"What did you say?"

"I said, 'You buried two hundred men and blamed a ghost for twenty years. I'll allow myself one dramatic plaque.'"

I laughed despite myself. Below us, the last rail of the day sat gleaming in the work lights, running straight toward the pass like a sentence finally finished. Somewhere beyond it, the glacier waited, calving on its own schedule, indifferent to plaques and inquiries and the small ambitions of the living. Good. Some things should stay indifferent. It keeps the rest of us honest.

"There's a letter," Astrid said, after a while. Quieter now. "From the ministry. About the surveyor-general post. For you. They've asked formally. Again."

"I know. I read it."

"And?"

"And I'm keeping the ledger open a little longer," I said. "There are lines on the old maps that were drawn by exiles from memory, and memory drifts. Somebody has to walk them and make the paper honest. It might as well be somebody who's already fallen into one crevasse on this range and resents it."

"So that's a yes?"

"That's a 'the chest goes in the museum next month, and when it does, I want to be the one who carries it in.' One last carry. After that—" I shrugged, and the shrug meant more than the words around it, and Astrid, who reads me better than any telegraph line ever carried signal, nodded.

"One last carry," they agreed.

We sat there while the work lights burned and the stars came out over the pass, hard and clean, the same stars that watched the collapse and the cover-up and the crossing and the verdict, and that will watch, if we do this right, a hundred years of trains going through with their windows full of ordinary people who will never know how much it cost to make their journey boring. That's the goal, really. Boring. Let the drama belong to the plaque.

Tomorrow they spike the second rail. Tomorrow after that, the third. The pass fills with the sound of hammers, which is, if you listen correctly, the same sound as two hundred names being spoken all at once, in a language the mountain finally agreed to learn.

And far below, in the valley, the telegraph line hummed its evening traffic — schedules, prices, weather — and then, brief and deliberate, a single repeated word passed down the wire from station to station, as it does every year on this date now, all the way to the sea:

*Remembered. Remembered. Remembered.*

The mountain keeps score. These days, so do we — and ours is a kinder arithmetic.
