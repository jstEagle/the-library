## 39. The Train Through

The compass sat on the console where I'd put it the night before, and in the morning light through the cab windows it looked smaller than I remembered. That's the thing about objects that carry weight — they don't get heavier with the years. They get lighter, because you keep setting them down and picking them up again until your arms learn the shape of them. The brass case was dented on one side from a fall I took off the Halden incline when I was twenty-three, and my mentor had laughed at me for it, said *an engineer who can't hold onto her instruments deserves to be lost.* He'd been wrong about a lot of things by the end, but not that.

"She'll take the grade at eight," Mira said beside me, hands folded, watching the track like she was daring it to misbehave.

"She'll take it at ten if I ask nicely."

"You won't ask nicely."

"I never do."

The locomotive breathed under us — a new machine, electric, quiet as a held breath compared to the coal-burners I'd learned on. There was something almost insulting about how smoothly it ran. Twenty years ago this line had been a rumor and a graveyard. Now the rails sang over the ties at forty miles an hour, and somewhere behind us, in nine passenger cars, two hundred families were riding toward the names of their dead.

* * *

I should back up. It's a habit of mine, backing up — surveyors do it constantly. You shoot a line forward, then you walk back and shoot it again from the other end, because a measurement you've only taken once is a guess wearing a uniform.

Three years since the summit station. Three years since I sat at that telegraph key with frost in my beard and keyed out the survey plates one figure at a time while stations down the whole length of the Corren line repeated me, station after station after station, until the words *Halden Pass, two hundred souls, deliberate demolition* had traveled further than any train ever had. Three years since Renner stood in that doorway with his gun and discovered that power is just a story people agree to believe, and that morning, four hundred miles of wire had stopped believing his.

Two years since the inquiry at Brassenford, where I surrendered the chest and my own confession in the same breath — the avalanche, Tomas's brother Hallvard buried under the snow I'd brought down, the line I'd sworn I wouldn't cross and crossed anyway. The tribunal gave me a suspended sentence and something worse: they gave me back my name. *Engineer Astrid Ashgrove.* Printed it in the proceedings like it was a fact that had always been true. I sat there listening to them restore me and thought about the man who didn't get restored, and decided that absolution was a currency I'd have to earn in installments for the rest of my life.

Eighteen months since the railway came to me with the offer. Chief engineer of the reopening. Build the line through Halden Pass properly this time.

I told them yes, but only with conditions, and I meant every one of them as a wall between me and the version of myself who had once believed that a schedule was worth more than a life. Mira co-signs every survey. Every cut, every fill, every tunnel alignment — her signature next to mine or the work doesn't move. And the names go on the wall. All two hundred of them, carved into the stone of the new Halden Station, where every passenger and every worker will walk past them forever.

They agreed too fast, honestly. Companies agree fast when agreeing costs them nothing yet. But then the carving started, and the stonemasons wanted to charge extra for diacritical marks, and Mira spent a week in their office explaining Vellmarki orthography with the patience of a woman describing, slowly, exactly how she would dismantle them, and eventually the marks went in. All of them. Čaradžija. Šumavets. Names that only exist properly in a language the maps once pretended wasn't there.

* * *

Now here we were, three years on, riding the inaugural train through the pass we'd rebuilt, and I was discovering that arrival has its own kind of vertigo.

Mira stood at my shoulder in the cab, and I watched her watch the mountain. She'd taken the surveyor-general post eighteen months ago — the exiles had asked, and this time she'd said yes on her own terms, which is the only way she says anything. She wore her old ferry coat still, patched at the cuffs where the Vellmark crest used to be stitched. She'd unpicked the crest herself, years ago, when erasure seemed like safety. Last winter she'd sewn it back on. New thread, slightly wrong color, deliberately visible.

"There," she said suddenly, pointing left. "Do you see it?"

I looked. Below us, maybe six hundred feet down the mountainside, the old collapsed tunnel mouth showed as a scar in the rockface — the original bore, the one that had been blown with two hundred workers inside, the one we'd routed the new line around rather than through. We could have blasted it open and reused it; it would have saved eleven months and a fortune. I'd stood at its mouth with the plans spread on a rock and made the call myself: no train runs through that hole. Not mine. The new alignment swung wide, a longer gentler arc through sounder rock, costing more and meaning more.

"The scar," Mira said. "From up here it looks small."

"Everything looks small from above. That's the engineer's disease." I checked the ammeter, mostly to give my hands something honest to do. "You spend enough years looking down at the world from a cab and you start believing people are distances."

"And what are they today?"

"Passengers," I said. "Two hundred families' worth. Which is a sentence I never expected to say without flinching."

* * *

We'd debated the inaugural run for months, Mira and I, in the particular way we debate everything — which is to say she states her position as though it were geology and I argue as though arguing might move the mountain, and usually the mountain wins, which is how we both save face.

My position had been: no ceremony. Cut a ribbon if you must, quietly, at dawn, with the work crews, and let the first train be a freight run like any other. Trains aren't miracles. Trains are maintenance schedules and ballast depth and a hundred small decisions made correctly, and dressing one up in bunting felt like lying about what the work actually was.

Mira's position had been: the families ride first. Before freight, before officials, before anyone from the company so much as breathes on a window. Two hundred families — the descendants, the widows now grown old, the children of children who had grown up hearing that their grandparents died in a cave-in, an accident, an act of God, and who had learned only three years ago that it was none of those things. They ride first. They see the wall. The company pays for everything and stands in the back and keeps its mouth shut.

I lost, obviously. I lose most arguments that matter, and I've come to understand that this is what partnership is: losing gracefully to someone whose judgment you trust more than your pride.

So the inaugural train carried no freight at all. Just people. Nine cars of them, and the company standing in the back with its mouth shut, more or less — the chairman had managed to give one speech at the platform, and Mira had stood directly in front of him the entire time with her arms crossed, and I'm told the speech was short.

* * *

The grade steepened past the old water tower and I eased the throttle back, feeling for the traction curve the way you'd feel for a pulse. New line, new motors, but mountains don't care about your equipment. Mountains care about weight and weather and the angle of repose, and they have been keeping score far longer than any ledger of ours.

"Talk to me," Mira said. It was our ritual. On the survey trips, in the bad months, whenever the silence in the cab got heavy enough to have a shape, one of us would say *talk to me,* and the other would narrate — the grade, the drainage, the memory attached to this curve or that cutting. It kept us both anchored. It kept the ghosts in the seats behind us instead of at the controls.

"Approaching marker forty-one," I said. "Six percent grade for the next mile and a half. Ballast was renewed in September — I walked it myself, all of it, because I don't trust a report I haven't put my boots on. There's a spring seep under the east embankment that we haven't fully solved; I've got a drain field planned for summer and I check the gauge pins weekly. If the track shifts even a finger's width, I'll know before the geometry car does."

"And beyond the marker?"

"Beyond the marker is the long curve where the old spur used to branch off toward the brake-house." I paused. "Where you and I slept, that first night up the dead lines. Rotten floorboards. You claimed the dry corner and pretended you'd offered it to me out of generosity."

"I did offer it out of generosity."

"You claimed it like a cat claiming a stove."

"That is also generosity. The generous thing was letting you watch me enjoy it."

Despite everything — despite the day, despite the wall waiting at the top of this grade — I laughed. That's another thing about grief nobody tells you: it doesn't leave. It just makes room. There's space in me now for both the laughing and the two hundred names, side by side, and neither crowds the other out anymore. I think that's what peace actually is. Not the absence of the dead. Room enough to hold them.

* * *

We stopped at the halfway water point, though the new engines barely needed water — the stop was scheduled, ceremonial, a nod to the old steam days. I stepped down onto the platform to stretch my legs, and a woman approached me. Seventy, maybe older, dressed in black wool with the particular severity of someone who has worn mourning so long it became tailoring.

"Engineer Ashgrove." She said it carefully, testing the name. "My father was a mason on the original tunnel. Jozef Čaradžija."

I knew the name. Of course I knew the name. I'd read it aloud into a telegraph key at the top of the world, and I'd carved its letters into wet clay for the casting mold, and I could probably draw its diacritics in my sleep.

"He was thirty-four," she said. "I was nine. They told my mother he owed the company money at the end, that they'd deduct it from — " She stopped. Recalibrated. This woman had rehearsed a speech her whole life and was now discovering that the real moment refused the script. "I don't know why I'm telling you this. Everyone on this train knows the story now."

"They know the facts," I said. "Facts and stories live in different cars."

That surprised a sound out of her — half laugh, half sob, quickly mastered. Then she reached into her bag and brought out something wrapped in cloth, and unwrapped it, and held it out to me.

A mason's trowel. Old, rust-spotted, the handle worn smooth and dark. "His. My mother kept it. She said if the line ever ran true again, someone should finish his work with it." Her chin trembled. "I don't know what that means, exactly. But it means something. I've carried it sixty years waiting to find out what."

I took it. What else does one do? My hands shook, and I let them shake in front of her, because pretending steadiness felt like one more kind of lie and I had retired from that trade.

"It means the wall gets one more tool laid against it," I said. "At the dedication. His name's already on the stone. Today his iron touches it too."

She nodded, pressed her lips together, and walked back toward the passenger cars with her spine very straight, the way people walk when they're carrying something heavier than they're showing.

I stood on that platform holding a dead man's trowel and understood, finally, what the whole cursed journey had been for. Not justice — justice happened in Brassenford, in a courtroom, imperfectly, and it was done. Not vindication — I'd stopped wanting that around the time I realized I couldn't want it without wanting my old life back, and my old life was the problem. It was this. A woman handing a stranger her father's tool across sixty years of silence, because the silence had finally been broken by something solid enough to build on.

* * *

The last climb. The pass opened ahead of us like a book finally turned to its hardest chapter, and I drove the train up through it at a respectful crawl, because some grades deserve reverence.

Mira had gone quiet, which for Mira meant full engagement. She stood at the cab window with the wind pulling at her coat, watching the rock walls rise on either side, and I saw her lips moving — counting, I realized. She was reading the mile markers, or the old blast scars, or perhaps just counting in the way her people counted, which I'd learned was never merely arithmetic.

The tunnel approach came into view. The new bore — clean concrete portal, the date cast into the lintel, and above it, worked into the stone itself at my insistence, a single line of text in both languages:

*THE MOUNTAIN REMEMBERS WHAT THE MAPS FORGOT.*

And there, at the portal, waiting for us, the entire work crew. Two hundred of them — we'd hired deliberately, half of them Vellmarki descendants, and the hiring hadn't been charity; it had been the best labor pool in the range, men and women who knew this rock in their bones because their grandparents had died teaching it to them the hard way. They lined the approach in their high-visibility vests, and they took off their hard hats as we passed, and I brought the train through at walking pace with my throat closed and my hands steady because the train required it, whatever the rest of me was doing.

Then the tunnel swallowed us, and inside, lit by the work lamps along the crown, the wall began.

* * *

They'd finished it in March. I had inspected it alone, at night, because I didn't want my face doing anything in front of witnesses. Two hundred names in Vellmarki and the common tongue, carved deep, letter by letter, running the length of a hundred meters of tunnel wall. Not a plaque. Not a monument you glance at. A wall you pass alongside, name after name after name, at the speed of a slow train, so that reading them takes exactly as long as it takes — long enough to feel the number stop being a number.

The train slowed to that speed now, as designed, as specified, as co-signed. And in the passenger cars behind us, I heard it begin — not weeping, or not only weeping. Voices. Families finding their names, calling them out to each other, touching the glass. Somewhere behind me a child was reciting one over and over, learning it, the way children learn anything: by repetition, by ownership, by love.

Mira's hand found my shoulder. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. Twenty years ago she'd pulled a drowned nation's grief out of a river and carried it up a mountain, and now the mountain carried it back down, distributed, shared, two hundred times lighter per person than it had ever been on either of us alone.

I looked at the console. The compass sat there in the vibration-light, needle trembling, pointing the only direction it knew.

My mentor had worn that compass through these same mountains. He'd taught me to read grade and bearing and the angle of repose, and he'd died — disappeared, blamed, erased — carrying the truth of this pass in a sealed chest, and for twenty years the current had held it, and then the flood gave it up, and then a disgraced engineer who wanted nothing but invisibility had dragged it ashore and been unable, in the end, to look away.

The needle settled. North. Forward. Through.

The tunnel ended and the eastern face opened below us — the long descent toward Brassenford, toward the valley, toward the courts and the schoolrooms and the ordinary future where trains are just trains and engineers are just engineers, whatever that turns out to mean now. Sunlight filled the cab. Behind us, two hundred families rode through the passage their grandparents never got to finish, and ahead of us lay the rest of the line, and the rest of the work, and the rest of our lives, which had somehow become the same thing.

"Arrival," Mira said quietly, watching the valley open. "It doesn't feel the way I imagined."

"No?"

"I imagined it would feel like an ending." She smiled without turning her head. "It feels like a departure."

I took the throttle and drove us down into the light.
