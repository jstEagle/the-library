## 1. The Weight of Miles

There are four hundred and eleven switchbacks between the Greywater trailhead and the saddle at Miller's Notch. I know this the way other people know their children's birthdays, which is to say: I have never once counted them on purpose, and I have never once gotten them wrong.

I was counting them again that morning because it was easier than counting anything else. Twenty years since the avalanche on the Kestrel face. Twenty years since I stopped celebrating birthdays and started measuring my life in safe miles instead — vertical feet gained without incident, clients delivered home with all ten fingers, seasons passed without my name appearing in a newspaper. By that arithmetic I was rich beyond reason and exactly as old as the mountain wanted me to be.

"Guide Nystrom." The voice belonged to Dale Priddy, a retired dentist from Coeurville who had booked the Greywater Traverse twice before and treated each booking like a pilgrimage. He'd caught up to me at the third switchback, breathing like a man being chased by his own cholesterol. "You ever get tired of these?"

"Tired of what?"

"The mountains." He waved a hand at all of it — the granite shoulders, the larches going gold at the treeline, the Serpent River flashing silver far below us where it came down out of country no permit allowed us to enter. "Same rocks every year."

I let three steps go by before I answered, which for me is a speech. "Rocks change," I said. "Slowly. You have to pay attention."

Dale laughed like I'd said something charming instead of something true, and behind him the rest of the party strung out along the switchbacks — six clients, mid-level fitness, high-level enthusiasm, the kind of people who book a guided trek because they want to feel competent somewhere in their lives and have figured out that a mountain will rent them that feeling by the day. I don't mean that as an insult. It's honest work, renting out competence. It's just not the work I trained for, back when my name meant something on a permit application.

We climbed. I counted.

* * *

By noon we'd made the notch and dropped our packs in the lee of a wind-carved boulder shaped vaguely like a kneeling man, if you squinted and had recently been deprived of human company, which I hadn't been, technically, but some days the distinction felt academic. I got water going, handed out electrolyte tabs, performed the small theater of competence that twenty years of practice had worn smooth as river stone. Check the boots. Check the blisters. Check the sky, always the sky, because weather in the Greywaters doesn't knock, it just lets itself in.

It was Priya Raghavan who asked the question. She was new this season, a software architect from the coast, thirty-four years old, fit as a whip and curious as one too. She'd been watching me all morning with the particular attention of someone who does research for a living and had clearly done some on me.

"So how long have you been guiding?" she asked, casual, sipping her tea.

"Twenty-two years."

"And before that? I read you used to run bigger routes. Technical stuff. Alpine starts." She smiled. "There's not much online about you. It's like you appeared fully formed in 2004."

The kettle hissed. Somewhere above us a pika squeaked its tiny alarm, and I envied it the simplicity of its concerns.

"I took some time off," I said.

"For what?"

"Dale," I called across the camp, "your boot's coming untied."

It wasn't. But Dale looked down anyway, bless him, and Priya watched me do it and understood, whatever it was she understood, and didn't push. That's the thing about clients. They pay you to keep them safe, and most of them can smell it when the guide needs keeping safe herself, and the good ones pretend they don't notice. Priya was a good one. She went back to her tea, and I went back to counting switchbacks in my head, except now I was counting something else underneath them, the way you always are, the way the miles sit on top of the years like snowpack over rock.

Here is what she would have found if the internet had been honest with her, which it mostly isn't, because money talks to search results the way it talks to everything else:

In 2003 I was the youngest lead guide licensed in the northern ranges. In 2004 there was an accident on the Kestrel face — an avalanche, two climbers roped together, one survivor. The survivor was me. My partner, Wren Calloway, was not. The inquiry lasted nine months and concluded, officially, with a finding of "inconclusive circumstances." Unofficially, the licensing board declined to renew my certification, citing — and I quote the letter I still keep in a drawer — "concerns regarding decision-making under adverse conditions." Three companies blacklisted me within the year. The fourth offered me a job contingent on my signing a statement I wouldn't sign, so I didn't, and after that I guided unlicensed routes, private parties, the grey-market trails where nobody checks papers and everybody pays cash.

What Priya couldn't have found anywhere, because only two living people knew it and one of them was me: the company whose equipment failed on that route — Halvard Alpine Supply, as it was then; Halvard Extraction, as it became once it decided mountains were better mined than climbed — had offered me a settlement. Silence about the faulty anchors in exchange for a quiet exit from the profession. I refused. And then, somehow, mysteriously, tragically, the blacklist appeared, and my testimony became the testimony of a disgraced guide with a grudge, and nobody prints the grievances of the disgraced.

That was twenty years ago. I've been walking uphill ever since.

* * *

We made camp at Miller's Notch an hour before dark, and I set the clients to their tasks — tents, water, bear hang — with the brisk economy that passes, among strangers, for warmth. Dale told his story about the root canal that made him faint in his own chair. Priya asked intelligent questions about glacial moraine. A teenager named Marcus, dragged along by his outdoorsy father, discovered that if you held your phone at the exact right angle on the saddle you could get one bar of signal, and spent the evening sending messages into the void like a man feeding coins to a wishing well.

I cooked. I checked the sky. I counted the tents twice, which is a habit, and then counted them a third time, which is a tell.

"You always do that?" Priya nodded at my head, mid-count. "Count things?"

"Keeps the numbers honest."

"What numbers aren't honest?"

I gave her the look I give clients who are about to get a lecture on hydration, and she grinned and raised her hands in surrender, and I turned back to the stove, and the truth — the number I wasn't counting, the one under all the others — sat there in the firelight with us like a fourth client nobody had booked.

One tent for every person. One rope for every pair. One partner for every climb. The arithmetic of the mountains is simple until it isn't, and then it's the only math that matters, and I had been getting it wrong for twenty years by exactly one.

* * *

On the last morning, coming down, I sent the clients ahead through the meadow section — easy walking, impossible to lose — and cut off the trail onto a sheep track I've never marked on any map I've shown anyone. It climbs fifteen minutes to a granite shelf above the tree line, wind-scoured, anonymous, and there stands a cairn.

I built it the first spring after. Wren Calloway, 1971–2004. No body to bury — the mountain kept her, the way mountains do, and I've never decided whether that's greed or mercy — so I stacked stones instead, the oldest technology of grief there is. Every season since, I've come back and added one. Just one. You don't build monuments all at once. You build them the way you build a life: one stone, chosen carefully, placed where it won't fall.

This year's stone was a flat gray slab the size of a dinner plate, wedged loose from the shelf below the cairn. I carried it up, set it down, and did the thing I do, which is stand there like an idiot talking to a pile of rocks.

"Twenty years," I said. "Dale asked if I ever get tired of the mountains. Can you believe that guy."

Wind moved through the pass. Somewhere below, faintly, the clients' laughter came up the meadow, bright and oblivious, and I thought: that's the sound of the life I guide other people through. I know the route. I just don't live there.

"I'm still here," I told the cairn. "Still counting."

I placed the stone. I pressed it down with both hands, the way you check a hold before you trust it, and for a moment — the way it happens maybe once a season, maybe less — the grief didn't weigh anything at all. It was just a fact, like elevation. Like weather. Something you plan around, not something that stops you.

Then I heard voices on the track below. Not my clients — theirs were fading toward the trailhead, a half mile gone. These were different. Two men, by the sound, moving fast for the altitude, and talking low, and one of them said a word that came up the slope clear as a bell in the thin air.

"Halvard."

I stood very still on the shelf beside Wren's cairn, twenty years of safe miles under my boots, and listened to the past come up the mountain looking for me.
