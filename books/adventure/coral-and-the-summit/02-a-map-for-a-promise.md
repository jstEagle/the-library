## 2. A Map for a Promise

The saloon in Kettle Gap was called the Last Chance, which was either optimism or an apology, depending on how much you'd had to drink. I'd had one beer, so I was still reading it as architecture.

I'd stopped there because the road stopped there, and because my knees had filed a formal complaint about the eleven miles I'd put on them since dawn. The Greywater trek had ended two days early — a client with altitude sickness and a husband who thought ibuprofen was cheating — and now I had three days of dead time and no plan, which is the most dangerous condition a guide can be in. Idle hands, my mother used to say, find mountains.

The place smelled like pine varnish and old fryer oil, and it was nearly empty at four in the afternoon: a bartender polishing glasses that didn't need it, two truckers arguing about snow chains over their phones, and an old man in the corner booth who looked like he'd been carved out of the same material as the booths themselves and left out in weather for sixty years.

I took the bar stool farthest from everyone, because that's my seat everywhere. I've gotten good at it. Twenty years of practice will do that — you learn to occupy space without taking any up. You learn to be furniture that can read a topo map.

"Beer's cold," the bartender offered, by way of conversation.

"That's all I ask of anything," I said, and he laughed like I'd said something funnier than I had, and went back to his glasses.

The old man in the corner watched me. I could feel it the way you feel sun through a window — indirect but persistent. I kept my eyes on the window instead, where the Serpent River valley opened up to the north, a green seam between gray shoulders of mountain, the river itself just visible as a bright thread stitched down the middle of it. Tomorrow I'd drive up the access road as far as they let civilians go, park at the gate, and walk the legal miles along the legal side of the fence, counting switchbacks, keeping my head down, being nobody.

That's the whole job now. Being nobody, professionally.

"You're Nystrom."

The voice came from the corner booth, gravel over gravel. I turned. The old man had shifted forward in his seat like a man leaning into wind, both hands flat on the table as if the table might otherwise leave without him.

"I'm sorry?" I said, though I'd heard him fine. I always hear it fine. That's the problem with a name that got printed in three newspapers and one licensing board ruling — it sticks in people's memories the way a splinter sticks in skin, invisible until pressure.

"Hazel Nystrom." He said it like he was checking a specimen against a drawing. "Greywater Range. You ran the north face routes before they pulled your ticket." His eyes were pale, almost colorless, the blue boiled out of them, and they didn't waver. Old men get to stop wavering; it's one of the few benefits. "I watched you work once. '09. The Corrie Traverse. You turned a party back off the cornice when every other guide in the county would've pushed for the summit photo."

"I think you have me confused with someone else," I said, which is what I say, which is what I always say, and I picked up my beer to make the sentence load-bearing.

"I don't." He didn't move. "Come sit down, Nystrom. My legs are done pretending and my voice won't carry twice."

Everything sensible in me said stay on the stool. Sensible has been running my life for twenty years and it keeps me fed and unphotographed and alive. But there's a thing that happens when someone says your real name out loud in a room full of strangers — something in your chest unlocks a half-turn, like a door that's been painted shut and doesn't open anymore but remembers how.

I sat down across from him. Up close he was worse and better than he'd looked from the bar — worse in the yellow of his skin, better in the steadiness of his hands, which were enormous, knuckled like tree roots, and completely still.

"Eben Voss," he said. "You won't know the name. Nobody does anymore. That was the point of it."

"Voss," I repeated, testing it. Nothing rang. The valley country had a thousand names like that — families that had been somewhere until suddenly they weren't, bought out or flooded out or legislated out, their names surviving only on defunct mine claims and the occasional historical plaque nobody read.

He nodded slowly, satisfied, like I'd passed a test I hadn't known I was taking. Then he reached into the canvas satchel beside him on the bench and set something on the table between us.

It was a map. Hand-drawn, on paper gone soft and tea-colored with age, folded so many times the creases had begun to fur, and when he smoothed it open with those root-knuckled hands I saw ink lines fine as spiderwork — contour after contour, elevation marks in a cramped engineer's hand, and a river running down the whole length of it like a spine.

The Serpent River. And above the last named landmark — above the ranger gates, above the survey markers, above everything with a legal existence — the line kept going, up into the high glacial country where the maps in the public record simply stopped, the way old maps used to write *here be dragons* and modern ones write *restricted watershed* and mean the same thing.

"You drew this," I said. Not a question. You don't fake linework like that. Every contour on it had been earned by somebody's boots.

"In '78. '79. The springs run under the glacier, up past the gorge. There's a cavern where the water comes up through the ice — blue as church glass, if you ever see it, and almost nobody has." His voice had gone quiet, the way men's voices go when they're talking about the only thing they ever loved properly. "I spent four seasons mapping it. Four seasons, Nystrom. Best work I ever did, and I buried it, because that's what I was paid to do."

I looked up from the map. "Paid by whom?"

His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "There's the question. There's always the question with you survey types." He tapped the table once, a dry click like a lock seating. "Here's what I want, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide, because I don't have the time to say it twice and I don't have the strength to beg."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"No," he agreed. "But you're listening, and you came and sat down, and that's more than I expected, so we're ahead."

I should have stood up then. I want that on the record, wherever records go — I should have stood up, finished my beer, paid the man, and driven north to walk my legal miles. Instead I sat in a roadside saloon in Kettle Gap and listened to a dying prospector tell me about a valley that no longer existed, and I told myself I was only being polite, which is the lie I use for curiosity when I want it to pass inspection.

"The Voss family homesteaded the upper Serpent in 1911," he said. "Before the dam, before the reservoir, before the government decided that valley made a better lake than a home. We were exiled for it. Compensation on paper, nothing in fact. My father died in a boarding house in Millbrook counting the money they gave him and calling it theft, which it was." His thumb moved along the river line on the map, following it upstream, an unconscious gesture, a man walking home in his head. "I'm the last one who remembers the valley from the ground. And I'm dying, Nystrom, in case the jaundice hasn't made that plain. Liver. Six weeks the doctor gave me, and doctors are optimists about other people's livers."

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it, and he waved it off like smoke.

"What I want is simple, which is not the same as easy. I want this map carried up the Serpent to the source. To the cavern. There's a cache there — steel drum, sealed, my mark on it. What's in it stays sealed until it reaches daylight on its own terms. I don't need you to open it. I need you to *carry* it out. Bring it down to the valley mouth where the old homestead road meets the water, and leave word at the Voss plot in the Kettle Gap cemetery. That's it. That's the whole errand."

"And why me?"

"Because you're the best mountaineer this state has produced in thirty years, and they took your license for telling the truth, and a woman like that—" he paused, choosing, "—a woman like that knows what it costs to carry something heavy."

The beer turned to tin in my mouth. I set it down.

"I don't take clients," I said. "And I don't take errands. And I especially don't take errands into restricted watershed, which is a federal offense, in case your four seasons up there didn't include the signage."

"It included the signage." He gathered the map back toward himself, unhurried, folding it along its furred creases. "I had to ask. Vosses ask once." He looked at me then with those bleached-out eyes, and what was in them wasn't anger or disappointment. It was worse. It was recognition. "You turned a party back off a cornice in '09 because you knew the mountain better than their wanting. I figure a person like that knows the difference between a promise she can keep and one she can't. So I'll not waste a promise on you that you can't keep. No harm done, Nystrom. Buy your own beer."

He raised one of those enormous hands — two fingers, casual, a man signaling a bartender he'd known for years — and settled back against the booth like the whole exchange had cost him less than it had cost me, which I doubted, and began, of all things, to hum. Something old. Hymn-adjacent. A tune that had probably been played at his parents' wedding in a valley that was now forty feet underwater.

I went back to my stool. I paid. I told myself the strange lightness in my chest was relief.

It was not relief. It was the specific weightlessness of guilt arriving ahead of consent — the feeling of having already said yes somewhere inside myself and simply not informed the management. I knew the feeling. I'd felt it standing under the cornice in '09, feeling the answer form in my body a full ten seconds before my mouth caught up and shouted *turn around*. The body decides first. It always decides first. The rest of us just do the paperwork.

I was doing the paperwork — stirring the last of my beer, planning tomorrow's legal miles — when the humming stopped.

It didn't fade. It stopped, mid-phrase, the way a clock stops, and the silence it left was louder than the tune had been. I turned on the stool.

Eben Voss was sliding sideways out of the booth. Not falling — sliding, slow and terrible, like a man sinking through ice, one hand scrabbling at the table edge and finding nothing to hold. The map slid with him, off the table, and I was moving before I'd decided to move, crossing the floor in four strides and catching him under the arms as he went down, and God, he weighed nothing, he weighed *nothing*, this man built like a root system, and his head lolled against my shoulder and his breath came in shallow, rattling pulls like wind through a cracked window.

"Bartender!" My voice came out in my guide voice, the one that carries across a valley. "Call nine-one-one. Now."

The truckers were on their feet. The bartender was already reaching for the phone behind the register. Somebody knocked a chair over. None of it registered as more than weather, because I was on my knees on the floor of the Last Chance Saloon holding a dying stranger's head in my lap, and he was looking up at me with those pale eyes that had gone very clear and very focused, the way eyes do at the end when everything else has been edited out.

"Nystrom," he said. Each word cost him visibly, coins paid out one at a time from a purse nearly empty. "The map. Floor. Under—"

"I've got it." I had it — it had landed half under his hip and I'd pinned it under my knee without thinking, the way you catch a rope without thinking. "Don't talk. Help's coming."

"My grandson." A breath, wet and long. "Tomas. He'll come for it. He thinks—" Another breath, longer, the pauses stretching like thinning ice. "He thinks it's a lie. Let him think it. Carry it anyway."

"I never said I'd carry it."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. The memory of one. "No," he whispered. "You didn't."

The sirens came up the valley road, faint at first, then filling the windows. His hand found my wrist — those root-knuckled fingers closing around it with a grip that had no business being that strong, that had everything to do with a man spending his last currency all at once.

"Blue as church glass," he said. "You'll see."

Then his eyes fixed on something above me, past the ceiling, past the roof, up the river and the gorge and the furred creases of a hand-drawn map, and stayed there, and the grip on my wrist eased by degrees, like a tide going out, until the paramedics arrived and had to ask me, twice, to let go.

* * *

They took him to the county hospital in Millbrook, thirty miles south, and I drove behind the ambulance without deciding to, my truck full of questions and the map folded in my jacket pocket, because when they'd lifted him onto the gurney his hand had found mine one more time and squeezed — once, deliberate, a signature on a contract neither of us had signed — and the bartender had pressed the map into my hands at the door saying, *he'd want you to have it, he showed it to nobody else in six weeks of drinking here,* and what was I supposed to do, refuse a dying man's paperwork?

I sat in the hospital parking lot for an hour while the sun went orange behind the Millbrook water tower, holding the map on my steering wheel like a steering wheel of its own. In the sodium light the ink lines looked almost fresh. The river ran down its spine, patient as rivers are, all the way up past the fences and the gates and the signs, into the high white country where the water came up through the ice blue as church glass.

Restricted watershed. Federal offense. Banned guide caught trespassing — the newspapers would eat it in one bite, and there'd be no coming back from a second fall, not that there was a first one to come back *to*. Every sensible thing I owned argued for driving to the nearest ranger station and handing it in. Found property, unknown deceased, let the government do what governments do with things, which is file them.

But I kept seeing his hand close on my wrist. *Carry it anyway.* And underneath that, quieter, the thing he'd actually said, the thing that had slipped under my ribs and lodged there like a splinter under a nail: *they took your license for telling the truth, and a woman like that knows what it costs to carry something heavy.*

He'd known. That was the worst of it. Some old man I'd never met had looked across a saloon table and seen straight through twenty years of safe miles and small treks and being furniture, and had handed me the one thing I couldn't put down.

My phone buzzed. Unknown number, local prefix. I answered because answering was easier than thinking.

"Miss Nystrom?" A young voice, careful, official. "This is the nursing station at Millbrook County. You were listed on Mr. Voss's effects as next of kin contact. There's a family member here asking after his belongings. A grandson. He's asking specifically about a map."

I looked down at the paper in my lap, at the river running through it like a spine, at the high country where the lines kept going after the world stopped.

"He's asking," the voice repeated, when I didn't answer, "if you have it."

Through the windshield, north past the water tower, the Serpent valley had gone dark, but I could feel it there the way you feel a storm building behind a ridge — the whole hidden length of it, the gorge, the source, the steel drum waiting under the ice, and somewhere downstream a grandson already angry about a thing he hadn't seen yet.

"Yes," I heard myself say. "Tell him I have it."

The line clicked dead, and I sat in the dark with the map and the guilt, which had arrived, as usual, well ahead of my consent — and this time, I suspected, it wasn't going to wait for the paperwork.
