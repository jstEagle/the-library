## 3. The Grandson Who Says Burn It

The phone call came at 6:14 on a Tuesday morning, which should have been my first clue that the universe had stopped pretending to be fair.

"Tomas Voss?" A woman's voice, flat with the particular exhaustion of someone who works in a hospital and has stopped apologizing for it.

"Who's asking?"

"St. Alder Regional. You're listed as next of kin for Eben Voss."

"I'm listed as *what*?"

"Next of kin," she repeated, with the patience of a woman who had said this sentence four thousand times and would say it four thousand more before her shift ended. "He gave your name at intake. He's asking for you."

"He's been asking for me for eleven years," I said. "What changed?"

"His kidneys, mostly."

I want to be clear about something: I did not go because I forgave him. I went because there is a specific kind of cowardice that dresses itself up as closure, and I have never once managed to resist it. I told myself it was a two-hour drive, that I'd sit by the bed for twenty minutes, that he'd probably be unconscious anyway, and that afterward I could tell myself I'd done the decent thing and get back to the life where Eben Voss was a name I'd scratched off my emergency contacts twice and he'd written himself back in three times.

That's the thing about my grandfather. He was a man who treated other people's boundaries as suggestions from a committee he hadn't appointed.

* * *

St. Alder Regional smelled like industrial lemon attempting to drown something older and sadder. The nurse at the desk — the same voice from the phone, now attached to a face that looked like it had personally witnessed the decline of several civilizations — directed me to room 214 without looking up.

I stood in the doorway for a long moment before going in.

Eben Voss had always been built like something the mountains had tried to keep and partially succeeded: big hands, a spine curved from decades of leaning into wind, a face weathered down to its load-bearing features. In the hospital bed he looked deflated, like a tent after the poles are pulled. His skin had gone the color of weak tea. There was a machine beside him doing his breathing's paperwork, and an IV line running into a hand that used to be able to crack walnuts between finger and thumb, a trick he'd performed at every family dinner until the dinners stopped.

His eyes were open. They found me the way they always had — immediately, hungrily, as if I were the interesting part of whatever room we were in.

"There he is," he said. His voice was gravel run through a sieve. "Tomas. Look at you. You got old."

"You got dead-ish."

"That's just Tuesday." He tried to laugh and it turned into a cough that bent him nearly double, and I found myself — traitor body — stepping forward and adjusting the angle of the bed until the coughing eased. My hands remembered how to do that. Eleven years of not speaking to a man and my hands still knew his maintenance schedule.

"Don't fuss," he wheezed.

"I'm not fussing. I'm performing routine equipment checks." I sat down in the vinyl chair because standing felt too much like staying. "Why am I here, old man? And don't say kidneys. Kidneys don't call people."

"They do when they're mine." He studied me with those pale eyes that had missed nothing in seventy-nine years except, apparently, the concept of enough. "You look tired."

"I look *employed*. There's a difference."

"When's the last time you slept?"

"When's the last time *you* apologized?"

Something moved across his face — quick, tectonic, gone. "Ah," he said softly. "So we're starting there."

"We're not starting anywhere. I came because a stranger called me, and I'm leaving when she kicks me out. Say what you called me here to say so we can both get back to our regularly scheduled silence."

For a moment I thought he'd actually fight me on it. He had a lifetime of practice at fighting people on things, most of them things nobody else wanted. But then his gaze slid sideways, to the nightstand, and I followed it.

There was a canvas roll on the nightstand. Oilcloth, actually, tied with a length of paracord gone soft and gray from handling. It was about the size of a hardcover book, and it sat there with the particular density of an object that has been carried a long way by someone who believed in it.

"What is that?" I asked, though some animal part of me already knew, the way you know a storm is coming from the way birds go quiet.

"That," said my grandfather, "is why you're here."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Eben Voss, the official version, the version I'd constructed out of childhood fragments and adult silences like a man rebuilding a wrecked ship in a bottle:

He had been a prospector. He had left my grandmother alone for months at a time chasing claims through ranges whose names I only half-learned. He had come back from one of those trips — the last big one, the Serpent River country, upstream of everything — with money he wouldn't explain and a silence he couldn't break, and within two years my father had packed us all out of the valley town where the Voss family had lived for four generations, and we had become people who lived in cities and didn't talk about where we came from.

My father died without explaining any of it. That was the family inheritance, I'd come to believe: land we couldn't return to, and explanations we couldn't have.

And now here was the explanation, sitting on a hospital nightstand in oilcloth, and my grandfather watching me look at it with an expression I recognized from my childhood — the expression he wore when he'd decided something was true and the rest of the world just hadn't caught up.

"No," I said.

"You haven't even heard—"

"No. Whatever it is. Whatever speech you've been rehearsing since the nurse called me. No."

"It's a map, Tomas."

"I know it's a map. I know exactly what kind of map, because you've been drunk at three family funerals telling anyone who'd listen about the source of the Serpent, and everyone always assumed it was the drink talking, and then you'd sober up and deny every word, and we'd all pretend the whole thing hadn't happened. That map has ruined Thanksgiving *twice*."

"This time I'm not drunk." He lifted his chin toward the IV bag. "They've got me on the good stuff and none of it's alcohol. This time it's just true."

"The truth doesn't need to wait until you're dying to come out."

"Doesn't it?" And there was something in his voice then that stopped me — not defiance, for once, but a genuine question, asked by a man who had apparently spent fifty years wondering the same thing. "You'd think that. I thought that. I was thirty-four years old and I told myself I'd speak up when it mattered, and then it mattered, and I opened my mouth, and I learned what money sounds like when it talks to a family that's already lost everything once." His hand crept across the blanket toward the oilcloth roll but didn't touch it. "Silence isn't a decision you make once, boy. It's a decision you make every morning. And one morning you wake up and you're eighty years old and the mornings are numbered, and the silence weighs more than the mountain does."

I should have softened. Any reasonable person would have softened. Instead I heard myself say, in a voice cold enough to surprise me:

"So you sat on it. For fifty years. While Dad grew up ashamed of a name he didn't understand. While Mom fielded questions about why we never went back. While I built an entire life on top of a hole shaped like a valley." I leaned forward. "You want credit for finally unpacking it on your deathbed? You want a medal for honesty with a countdown clock on it?"

"Yes," he said simply. "I want the medal. I'll take the medal wherever they're handing them out, even if it's posthumous and even if you're the one who has to pin it on." He coughed again, wetter this time. "But first I need you to take the map."

"Why me? Why not burn it yourself? You've had fifty years of matches."

"Because burning it doesn't unburn what happened." His eyes fixed on mine and held, and for the first time since I'd walked in, he wasn't performing. He was begging, and he knew it, and he did it anyway. "Because there are people in that valley who were owed something, and I'm the last one who knows the debt exists, and when I stop breathing, the debt dies with me unless it's in your hands."

"What people? What debt? Grandfather—" The word came out before I could stop it, the childhood word, and I hated that it came out, and I saw him hear it, and I saw him decide not to gloat about it, which was somehow worse.

"There's a woman," he said. "Coming to see me. Today, maybe tomorrow. Hazel Nystrom. She's a guide — banned, blacklisted, but she's the only one alive who's walked the upper Serpent and can read the country well enough to do what needs doing. I've got a promise out to her. She carries the map to the valley mouth. That's all. Just to the valley. Deliver it home."

"And then what? We hold a ceremony? The valley opens up and says thank you?"

"And then whoever's left decides." He sagged back against the pillows, and I watched the effort of the conversation drain out of him in real time, his breath going shallow, the machines ticking their disapproval. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I ran the numbers on that a long time ago and they don't come out. I'm asking you to be the last Voss who knows where the bodies are buried. Literally, in this case."

"Grandpa—"

"Eben," he corrected, gently. "You haven't called me that since you were nine. Let's not ruin a good day."

* * *

She arrived while he was sleeping.

I was in the corridor, drinking vending-machine coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during a previous administration, when she came down the hall — and I knew her instantly, though I'd never seen her before, because she walked like the floor was a rumor. Compact, gray-streaked hair cut practical, a canvas jacket with the particular faded dignity of gear that has outlived several owners. Her eyes did a thing I'd later learn was habitual: they swept the corridor once, catalogued exits and weights and sightlines, and then settled on me with polite, professional blankness.

"You're here for 214," she said. Not a question.

"You're Hazel Nystrom."

Something flickered behind the blankness. "He talked about me?"

"He talked about a lot of things. Most of them I wish he hadn't."

"Yeah," she said. "That tracks." And she moved past me toward the door with the unhurried certainty of someone entering a room she'd already decided the shape of.

I should have let her walk in alone. I should have gone home. Every rational thread of my life was pulling toward the parking lot, toward the highway, toward the city where Eben Voss was a closed file and a blocked number.

Instead I followed her in, because I was beginning to understand that this was how my family worked — we didn't choose the current, we just discovered we'd been swimming in it.

* * *

He woke up for her. Of course he did. He'd been drifting in and out all afternoon, surfacing just long enough to check whether I'd touched the oilcloth roll (I hadn't) and sinking again, but when Hazel Nystrom pulled the vinyl chair up to his bedside, his eyes opened like a shopkeeper hearing the bell over the door.

"You came," he rasped.

"You collapsed in a saloon in front of me," she said. "It seemed rude not to follow up."

"Still the politest person in the Greywaters." He fumbled for the oilcloth roll and pushed it toward her with a hand that shook like a leaf in weather. "You remember our arrangement."

"I remember you made an offer. I remember I didn't accept it."

"You will." He untied the paracord with fingers that had forgotten how, and she reached over — gently, without being asked — and finished the knot for him, and something passed between them in that small gesture that I did not like and could not name. Unrolled, the map was a sheet of survey paper gone amber with age, covered in his cramped, obsessive handwriting: elevations, dates, water marks, and along one margin, in red pencil, a route drawn with the confidence of a man who had walked it.

Hazel Nystrom looked at that map for a long time. Long enough that the machines filled the silence with their small bureaucratic noises. When she looked up, her face had gone carefully, professionally empty, and I recognized the expression, because I was wearing its cousin.

"I told you I'd think about it," she said.

"You've thought about it for two days."

"I've thought about *you* for two days. Different problem."

"Hazel." He said her name like a man setting down something heavy. "I'm dying on a schedule now. The doctors were very clear, and I was very rude to them about it, but the numbers are the numbers. I need to hear yes or no."

And that was when I broke.

"Say no," I said.

Both of them turned. I was on my feet without remembering standing, and my voice came out with eleven years of interest compounded on it.

"She should say no. Everyone should say no. Do you know what that map is? It's the reason my father drank. It's the reason my mother stopped smiling sometime around my eighth birthday. It's the reason the name Voss is a punchline in three towns and a shame in a fourth. Fifty years this family has paid the bill on whatever he found up there, and now he wants to send a stranger — no offense—"

"Some taken," said Hazel Nystrom, dryly.

"—to carry it back into the valley like it's an heirloom instead of what it actually is, which is evidence of whatever rotten deal gutted us in the first place." I pointed at the map, at him, at the whole apparatus of his dying sincerity. "Burn it. Burn it and die clean, old man. It's the only apology left that means anything."

The room went very quiet.

Eben Voss looked at me for a long moment, and I braced for the fight, the bluster, the stubbornness that had outlasted avalanches and claim jumpers and my grandmother's patience. Instead his face did something worse than any of those. It crumpled — briefly, terribly, the way a mountain face crumples, all at once and from somewhere deep — and then reassembled itself into something almost gentle.

"You think the map disgraced us," he said quietly.

"I *know* it did."

"No, boy." He laid his palm flat on the amber paper, covering the red route like a man warming his hands over a fire. "The map is the only honest thing this family owns. Everything else — the money, the move, the silence, the shame — all of that was bought and paid for by people who are still alive and still comfortable, and they bought it precisely so that no one would ever ask what happened at the source of the Serpent." His eyes came up to mine. "You want to bury the family's sins? Fine. But make sure you're burying the right ones. Because the ones you're carrying aren't mine."

"That's easy to say from a deathbed."

"It's the hardest thing I've ever said from anywhere," he said, "and I once confessed to your grandmother that I'd sold her piano to fund a claim." A ghost of the old grin. "Ask Hazel. She'll vouch for the terrain ahead. Or she will, if she says yes."

Everyone looked at Hazel Nystrom.

She had not moved through any of it. She sat with her forearms on her knees, watching my grandfather with the expression of a woman reading a river — reading the surface water for what it told her about what lay underneath. And I understood, in that moment, that she had already decided; she was just letting him have the dignity of thinking the decision was still open.

"I'll deliver it," she said. "To the valley mouth. That's the promise. Nothing past the mouth unless I choose it."

"Nothing past the mouth," he agreed, "unless you choose it."

"And I'm not promising to agree with what's in the margins. If this map says what I think it might say, there are people who'll want it buried deeper than the valley ever was. I've met those people. They've got budgets."

"They've got more than budgets," Eben said, and the lightness dropped out of his voice entirely, and the temperature of the room dropped with it. "Halvard Extraction has survey crews staking the upper Serpent right now. They've waited fifty years for the last witness to die. I intend to inconvenience them."

"Halvard," Hazel repeated, and I watched something shut behind her eyes, a door closing on a room I couldn't see into. "Of course it's Halvard."

"You know them?"

"I've got history." She rolled the map back up with precise, economical movements and tied the cord, and the object disappeared into her jacket like it had always belonged there. "I'll start upstream as soon as the passes clear. Calving season gives me maybe three weeks before the ice gets stupid."

"Three weeks," Eben whispered. His hand searched the blanket, found hers, gripped it with whatever strength remained, which was more than I expected and less than he wanted. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me if I come back down."

Then he turned to me, and his grip on her hand loosened, and his eyes — bright, terrible, hopeful — found mine across the dying light of the afternoon.

"Tomas. Come with her."

"No."

"Come with her, or follow her, or fight her the whole way up the river — I know you, boy, you'll do one of the three, because you're a Voss and we have never once in four generations let a thing go." His breath caught, held, released. "Just don't let the map reach the valley without a Voss beside it. Promise me that much. Promise me the family walks it home."

"I'm not promising you anything. You don't get to spend fifty years hollowing us out and then draft me into your redemption on the way out."

"Then don't do it for me." His voice was failing now, fraying at the edges, and the machines were getting louder, or maybe the room was getting quieter. "Do it for your father. Do it for the name. Do it because—" and here he lifted his head off the pillow, gathering himself for one last sentence, one last argument, the summation of seventy-nine years, and his eyes locked onto mine with the full desperate weight of everything he'd never said—

—and the sentence never landed.

The monitor's tone changed. Flatlined into its long, indifferent note. Hazel Nystrom was moving before I'd processed the sound, her hands finding his wrist, then his sternum, her voice calling for the nurse in a register I hadn't heard her use yet — sharp, commanding, the voice of a woman who had given orders on mountains — and then the room filled with people in scrubs and I was pressed back against the wall watching strangers work on the man who had raised me and ruined me in roughly equal measure, watching them work with competence and urgency and finally, horribly, with the particular gentleness of people who have stopped trying.

It took them four minutes to call it. I counted. Counting is what my family does instead of feeling.

Afterward, the room emptied, and it was just me and Hazel Nystrom and the machines ticking down to silence, and the space on the nightstand where the map had been.

"He died mid-sentence," I said. My voice sounded like it belonged to someone else. "Fifty years of silence and he died mid-sentence."

"They usually do," she said quietly. "The important ones, anyway. The rest gets carried by whoever's left." She shrugged her jacket straight and looked at me, and there was no pity in her face, which I appreciated more than I can explain. Pity would have broken me. Assessment I could survive.

"I'm not coming with you," I told her.

"I know."

"I mean it. Whatever he thinks, whatever the great Voss destiny requires — I'm not walking into that valley. I'm going home, and I'm going to figure out how to grieve a man I hate, and that map is your problem now."

"I know," she said again, and picked up her pack, and paused at the door. "Get some sleep, Tomas. You look like a man about to make a long drive."

"I'm making a short one."

"Sure," she said, in a tone that suggested she'd guided enough stubborn clients to know the difference between what they said at the trailhead and what they did at mile six.

I drove home that night. Two hours of dark highway, the wipers keeping time against a rain that couldn't decide whether to commit. I made it to my apartment. I made it inside. I sat down on the edge of my bed with my boots still on, in the apartment where Eben Voss was a blocked number and a closed file, and I stared at the wall, and I listened to the rain, and I told myself the story I'd brought with me: that the map was a liar's fantasy, that my grandfather had died the same way he'd lived, making promises with other people's legs.

And then I got up, and dug my father's old box out of the closet — the one with the photographs, the valley photographs, the ones nobody was allowed to ask about — and I sat on the floor at three in the morning holding a picture of a gorge I'd never seen, and in the background of the shot, half-hidden behind my grandmother's shoulder, was a rock formation I'd know anywhere now, because I'd spent forty minutes that afternoon studying a hand-drawn map while a dying man begged me to look.

The gorge matched. To the contour line. To the bend of the river.

He hadn't been lying. That was the unbearable part. Fifty years of shame, and the old man hadn't been lying about a single contour.

I was on the road by dawn, driving back the way I'd come, telling myself it was to retrieve the map and burn it myself — that this was about ending it, not continuing it. The lie lasted approximately as far as the first sign for the Serpent River drainage, at which point I admitted what Hazel Nystrom had known at the trailhead: some rivers you don't choose. You just find out you've been swimming in them the whole time.

I caught up with her at a gas station outside Greywater Junction, loading fuel cans into a truck older than both of us combined. She looked at me over the roof of the vehicle, and she didn't smile, and she didn't say *I told you so*, which was its own kind of mercy.

"Changed your mind?" she asked.

"I'm going to intercept you," I said. "At the valley mouth. Before you do anything with that map. We're going to settle this — what it says, what happens to it, all of it. Family business."

"Family business," she repeated, neutrally, and slammed the fuel cap. "Well. Then I guess you'd better keep up."
