## 4. Two Debts, One River

I left the funeral home before the coffee went cold, which is to say I left quickly and without apologizing to anyone.

The plan was simple, the way all my plans are simple: honor the promise, keep the company minimal. One woman, one map, one river. I'd carried Eben Voss's paper out of that deathbed room like it was a live grenade, and the only responsible thing to do with a grenade was get it somewhere remote before it went off in a crowd.

Tomas Voss had other ideas, but Tomas Voss was twenty-six years old and grieving, and grief makes people certain of things they have no business being certain of. He wanted the map burned. He'd said so at the bedside while his grandfather's chest was still rising, and he said it again at the reception while relatives I'd never met picked at a cheese tray, and by the time I slipped out the back door into the parking lot, I had no doubt at all that if I handed him a match he'd use it on me first and the map second.

So: alone. The way I liked it. The way I'd arranged my whole life, actually, one safe mile stacked on another, nobody close enough to drown when the avalanche came.

I told myself that as I loaded the truck at dawn two days later, in the gravel lot behind the Greywater Motel, where the neon VACANCY sign buzzed like a fly trapped in glass. I told myself the promise didn't require witnesses. A promise is between you and the person you made it to, and Eben Voss was ash now, so technically I could've mailed the thing to the valley's last surviving Voss and been done with it.

But promises made to dying men don't work that way. They're not transactions. They're debts, and debts follow you upstream.

* * *

The Serpent River starts, officially, at a place called Headwater Bridge, forty miles north of town, where the water comes out of the canyon country already moving like it has somewhere to be. I'd run sections of it back in my licensed days — commercial trips, eight clients apiece, everyone wearing matching helmets and matching smiles. That was another life, one with a laminated card in my wallet that some board in the capital had revoked, and I tried not to think about how the river looked different when you weren't being paid to love it.

It looked better, honestly. That was the bitter joke of it.

I put in at the bridge at seven in the morning, alone, which any sane person would tell you was stupid. Solo runs through canyon water are how guides end up as cautionary tales told around permit-office desks. But the Serpent's lower reaches were class three at worst this time of year, and I knew every eddy from here to the Narrows, and more to the point, I wanted the miles to myself. I wanted to read Voss's map in peace, spread across my dry bag, and figure out what exactly I'd sworn to deliver.

The map itself was a marvel and a mess. Eben Voss had drawn it on the back of a mining claim form sometime in what I guessed was the late seventies, in pencil gone soft with age, and his hand shook in a way that suggested either age or excitement or both. The Serpent wound up the page in blue ink, annotated in a cramped script: *false channel*, *scree chute — stay right*, *the gorge narrows — listen for the falls before you see them*. And then, past a bend labeled only with a small, furious X, a valley. No name. Just a triangle of peaks and, inside it, a spring marked with three concentric circles, like a target, or an eye.

*The source*, he'd written underneath. *What they flooded to hide.*

I'd stared at those words for a long time the night before, in a motel room that smelled like industrial lavender, and I still couldn't make them mean anything. Valleys got drowned for reservoirs all over this state; it was practically a civic hobby. What they hid was usually just somebody's farmstead and somebody's grievance. But Voss hadn't drawn a reservoir. He'd drawn a source, high and dry above any dam line, and he'd died with his hand on my wrist making me swear.

*Carry it home,* he'd said. *Not to them. Home.*

I didn't know who *them* was yet. I had a bad feeling I'd find out.

* * *

I made good time the first day. Twenty-two miles of river, the canyon walls going from rust to rose as the sun dropped, bighorn watching me from ledges with the bored contempt of animals who've never once had to fill out paperwork. I camped on a gravel bar below a bend the map called the Kettle, ate freeze-dried stroganoff that tasted like salted cardboard, and slept the dreamless sleep of a body that's earned its exhaustion.

Which is why I almost missed him.

I smelled the fire first — woodsmoke where there shouldn't be woodsmoke — and came up out of the tent with my knife already open, because twenty years of running rivers solo teaches you that smoke means either rescue or trouble, and I'd stopped believing in rescue.

He was sitting on a rock fifty yards downstream, feeding a fire made mostly of sagebrush, looking like a man who'd lost an argument with a washing machine. His jacket was soaked to the elbows. His boots were worse. When he saw me, he stood up too fast and nearly went into the coals.

"You," he said.

"Me," I agreed.

"You were supposed to—" He stopped, jaw working, recalibrating. "You left."

"I did. It's a thing I'm known for." I sheathed the knife, mostly because holding it felt theatrical. "How did you find me?"

"I followed your truck to the bridge. Then I rented a kayak from the outfitter in Millbrook and asked them where a solo boater would camp the first night." He said it flatly, like a confession extracted under duress. "The guy at the outfitter thought I was insane. He might be right."

"He is. You are." I walked closer, keeping the fire between us, and took stock. Kid had a rental kayak strapped with gear he clearly didn't know how to pack — dry bags cinched wrong, PFD buckled loose enough to slip off in any kind of water. Twenty-six years old, city hands, and he'd put in alone on the Serpent to chase down a stranger. "You could have died today. Do you understand that? The Kettle Bend has a hole in it at this flow that eats kayaks for breakfast. There's a man buried under the cairn at the takeout who found out the hard way."

"I portaged it," he said, with a flicker of something that wasn't quite pride. "I read about it. I'm not an idiot."

"You put in alone on moving water with rented equipment. Yes, you are. You're precisely an idiot. The question is what flavor." I crouched by his fire and held my hands over it despite myself. The temperature was falling fast, and the sky over the canyon rim had gone the color of a bruise. Weather coming. Not tonight, maybe, but soon. "You want the map burned. Fine. Here's your chance — I'll hand it to you right now, you can burn it in this fire, and we both go home."

Something crossed his face then that I recognized, because I'd worn it myself for twenty years. It was the look of a person standing in front of the thing they say they want and discovering they can't pick it up.

"She said you'd say that," he muttered.

"Who said?"

"My grandfather. Before—" He stopped again. Grief kept interrupting him like a bad phone connection. "At the end. He said, *she won't give it to you, boy, she keeps her word even when it costs her.* He said it like it was a character flaw."

"It is."

"So you won't give it to me."

"Nope."

"And I can't take it from you."

"Not without drowning, and you'd lose the map anyway. Paper floats badly." I stood up. "So here's where we are, Tomas. You followed me eighty miles down a river to intercept a promise, and you've intercepted nothing, and tomorrow I put back on the water and you put back on a bus. Go home. Grieve properly. Let the old man rest."

"The valley isn't real," he said, and his voice cracked on it, and I understood suddenly that this whole mad pursuit wasn't about a map at all. "Don't you get it? My family doesn't talk about him. Ever. My mother changes the subject when his name comes up. Whatever he thinks is up there — whatever he drew on that paper — it's the lie that broke us. If there's a valley, then he had a reason to stay away for fifty years, and if there's no valley, then he sent a stranger to die chasing his fantasy. Either way—" He kicked a stone into the fire. Sparks went up like startled birds. "Either way I lose him twice."

I should have told him the truth, which was that losing someone twice is the standard rate, and anyone who tells you different is selling something. Instead I heard a sound upcanyon that turned my blood to ice water.

Rain. On the rim.

* * *

Flash floods in canyon country don't announce themselves. There's no polite knock. What there is, is rain falling ten miles away on ground too hard to drink it, and gravity doing the rest, and a dry wash becoming a brown freight train in the space between one heartbeat and the next. Every guide learns to hear it before seeing it — a low grinding roar, like the earth clearing its throat.

I heard it at two in the morning.

"Tomas!" I was out of the tent and moving before the word finished leaving my mouth. "Side channel — grab your bag — MOVE!"

He came out of his bivy in his underwear, which under the circumstances I chose not to comment on, and we ran. The roar was behind us and below us, which meant the flood was coming down the main stem from the tributary drainage two miles up, and our camp — the beautiful, flat, gravel-bar camp I'd chosen for its view — was about to become part of the riverbed.

We gained the bench thirty seconds before the water took the bar. I will not pretend I counted them calmly. I counted them screaming internally, hauling the kid by his strap because he'd stopped dead to watch the wall come through, a head-high brown slab carrying whole cottonwoods and half a hillside, and I got my hand in his PFD — he'd slept in it, thank God, thank God, some instinct in him had been smarter than his brain — and dragged him the last ten feet as the first wave slapped the rocks where we'd been standing.

Then it was over, sort of. Flash floods peak fast and fall slow, and for the next hour we sat on the bench above the churning brown and watched the Serpent briefly forget it was a river and remember it was an avalanche made of water. My boat was gone. His kayak was gone. Everything we owned except what we'd grabbed was either downstream or buried.

Tomas was shaking. So was I, though I kept it in my hands where he couldn't see it.

"We could have died," he said finally. His teeth were chattering. "In our sleep."

"Yes."

"You knew. You heard it coming."

"That's the job."

"What job? You don't have a job. They took your—"

"Tomas." I said it quietly, and he stopped. Rain started then, real rain, a fine cold needle-fall, and we huddled against a boulder with the one dry sleeping bag between us and the fire long dead, and I decided something. I decided it the way you decide to jump — not because the landing looks soft, but because staying on the ledge is its own kind of falling.

"Here's the bargain," I said. "You clearly aren't going home. You've proven you're stupid enough to follow me and lucky enough to survive it, and I can't in good conscience leave you out here to die on my conscience, which is already crowded. So you come with me. You do exactly what I say, when I say it, no debates, no heroics. You learn to read water, because I am not carrying you. And when we reach wherever this valley is — if it exists —"

"If?"

"*If.* Then we decide together what happens to the map. You and me. Equal votes. Burn it, bury it, deliver it — whatever we choose, we choose as partners. That's the deal. Take it or swim home."

He was quiet for a long time. The flood muttered below us, dropping inch by inch, revealing the bones of our camp scattered along the new waterline.

"Why?" he said at last. "Why would you want me along? You've been trying to shake me since the funeral home."

Because your grandfather died holding my wrist and I owe him more than a delivery, I thought. Because you look at that map the way I used to look at mountains I was afraid of. Because I have spent twenty years measuring my life in safe miles and I am so tired, kid, so bone-tired of safe.

"Because a promise witnessed is a promise kept," I said, which was true, and easier than the rest.

He stuck out his hand. It was freezing and cut up from the scree, and I shook it, and that was that. Two debts, one river, neither of us happy about the arithmetic.

"We'll need gear," he said. "Our boats are gone."

"There's a supply cache at the old ranger station twelve miles up. We walk the bank." I looked up at the rain, then east, where the sky was starting to bruise toward gray. "And Tomas — when we get to the Narrows, there's a ranger checkpoint. You let me do the talking."

"Why? What are you not telling me?"

I pulled the sleeping bag tighter around my shoulders and watched the flood carry the last of my old life downstream.

"Chapter and verse," I said. "Now try to sleep."

Above the canyon rim, far to the north, thunder rolled — or maybe it was something else, something with engines in it. In the dark, I couldn't tell the difference, and I suspected that before this was over, neither would the river.
