## 6. The Company's Shadow

The town was called Meridian Fork, which struck me as optimistic naming for a place that existed mostly to sell rope, diesel, and lies about how far the next town was. It had one street wide enough for a truck to make a U-turn it would regret, a general store whose porch sagged like a tired man on a barstool, and a saloon that had been three different businesses in my lifetime and was always, somehow, still the same saloon.

Tomas hated it on sight.

"This is where you're taking me?" He stood in the middle of the street with his pack slung over one shoulder like it had personally offended him, squinting at the store's window display of canned peaches and shotgun shells arranged with the aesthetic sensibility of a ransom note.

"This is where we resupply," I said. "Unless you've been hiding a grocery store in that attitude of yours."

"I'm just saying, my grandfather's map didn't mention—" He stopped himself. He'd been stopping himself a lot lately, mid-sentence, whenever the map came up, like touching a bruise to check if it still hurt. It did. I could tell by the way his jaw worked.

"Your grandfather's map," I said, "doesn't mention anything except contour lines and a river. That's what makes it credible."

We'd been on the Serpent for four days now, and something had shifted between us that neither of us wanted to name out loud. He still thought the map was fantasy or fraud — he said so every morning, with the regularity of a rooster with a grudge — but he'd stopped trying to take it from my pack. And I'd stopped pretending I wouldn't notice if he tried.

The resupply itself went the way resupply does: unglamorous and necessary. Dried beans, fuel canisters, a fresh spool of repair tape for Tomas's boot sole, which had developed opinions about the terrain. The store's owner, a woman named Petra with forearms like dock line and a memory like a ledger, rang us up while telling me in exhaustive detail about her son's failed attempt to keep bees above six thousand feet.

"Bears got them," she concluded, sliding the cans across. "Bears or the government. Up here those are often the same thing."

I laughed, because it was expected, and then I saw what was pinned to the corkboard behind her, half-buried under missing-dog flyers and a notice about a church potluck.

A work posting. *Halvard Extraction — Seasonal Survey Personnel. Upper Serpent corridor. Competitive wages. All inquiries confidential.*

I stopped laughing.

"You know these people?" Petra asked, following my eyes.

"No," I said, which was true in the way that mattered least. "Just reading."

*Halvard Extraction.* The name sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. Twenty years since I'd seen it last, printed in crisp corporate serif across the top of a letter informing me that my mountain guide certification was suspended pending review, pending review becoming revoked pending appeal, pending appeal becoming a door closing so quietly I hadn't even heard it shut until years later, when I finally understood why no outfitter in three ranges would return my calls. The letter had come on good paper. Companies that ruin you always use good paper.

"Hazel." Tomas was at the door, impatient, holding the door open with his hip. "The light's going."

"The light's always going," I said. "That's its whole personality."

But I bought a disposable camera at the register — Petra raised an eyebrow, because who buys film cameras anymore, and I said *for the trip*, which was even sort of true — and walked out into the late afternoon with the word Halvard ringing in my skull like a struck bell that refused to dampen.

* * *

Here is what I told myself while we ate beans at the campsite behind the store: coincidence. Halvard was a big company. Big companies have long fingers and short memories, and they stick them into every watershed from here to the coast. A survey crew on the upper Serpent meant nothing. It meant jobs for local boys and a line item in some vice president's quarterly report. It did not mean me. It did not mean Voss. It did not mean a hand-inked map folded into oilcloth in the bottom of my pack like a confession I hadn't made yet.

I am very good at telling myself things. Twenty years of practice. If self-deception were a certifiable skill, I'd never have been blacklisted in the first place.

Tomas turned in early, which he'd taken to doing with the exaggerated innocence of a teenager establishing an alibi. I waited until his breathing went slow and even, and then I took the camera and walked back toward town under a sky doing its usual high-country performance — stars flung around like someone had spilled them and couldn't be bothered to clean up.

Because here was the thing I hadn't told myself yet, the thing that had been itching under my skin since the corkboard: the posting said *upper Serpent corridor*. Not the lower river, not the valley approaches, not any of the dozen drainages a mining concern might reasonably poke at. The upper Serpent. The corridor that led, according to a dying man's ink-steady hand, to a source that wasn't supposed to exist on any map the public could buy.

And Eben Voss had died eleven days ago. In a roadside saloon. After asking for me by reputation, after pressing a map into my hands, after making me swear a promise I still wasn't sure I'd agreed to so much as failed to escape.

If I were a company that had paid a man to stay quiet thirty years ago, and that man had just died, and his deathbed visitor log showed he'd spent his last afternoon talking to a disgraced mountain guide —

Well. I'd want to know what she was carrying too.

* * *

Meridian Fork after dark was a different animal. The saloon threw yellow light onto the street like butter on hot bread, and past it, at the far end of town where the road turned to gravel and then to suggestion, there was a yard I hadn't noticed in daylight: chain-link, floodlit, full of equipment. Helicopter drums. Cases of what looked like geophone cable stacked like cordwood. Two white trucks with the logo stenciled on their doors — a stylized H splitting a mountain peak down the middle, as if the company were proud of the bisecting.

And men. Three of them, maybe four, moving between the trucks with the unhurried purpose of people loading for an early start. One of them carried a wooden crate, and even at forty yards I knew the weight distribution of a man carrying dynamite. You don't forget. It's the way they hold it slightly away from their bodies, the way they set it down like it's sleeping.

I crouched behind a stack of pallets at the yard's edge and raised the camera.

Click. The trucks. Click. The crates being loaded into the bed of the nearer truck, and — this was the detail that made my mouth go dry — stenciled on the crate sides, small but legible through the viewfinder: *SEISMIC CHARGE — CLASS 1.1D*. Seismic charges. For a survey. Sure. Some surveys you thump the ground and listen. Others you thump it hard enough that nothing ever answers again.

Click. A clipboard on a truck hood, too far to read but close enough to see the header block, the little grid of boxes where permits and coordinates live. Click. The logo again, because documentation is a religion and I was feeling devout.

I worked methodically, the way Wren taught me — God, Wren, whose name I hadn't let myself think properly in weeks, whose cairn I'd left unvisited this spring for the first time in twenty years because the miles had started to feel like counting instead of mourning. Frame, check edges, frame again. Don't rush. Rushing is how people die on mountains and get caught in towns.

Which is when I noticed the fourth man.

He wasn't loading anything. He was leaning against the far fence post with a cigarette, and he wasn't looking at the trucks either. He was looking at the dark beyond the yard, sweeping it in slow, patient arcs, the way a man looks when looking is his job. A spotter. Every operation like this has one — the guy who isn't on the crew roster, who drinks alone, who watches the road both ways.

I froze behind the pallets, camera against my chest, and did the math I should have done before I ever left camp. Floodlights at my back meant I was silhouetted to anyone inside the yard. My shadow stretched out ahead of me across the gravel like a long accusation.

The cigarette tip glowed. The man pushed off the fence post.

I did the only smart thing available, which was to become boring. I stood up, stepped out from behind the pallets, and walked toward the saloon at the pace of a woman who had simply stepped out for air and possibly a cigarette of her own. Boring is camouflage. Panic is a spotlight. I'd learned that lesson at altitude, at cost.

Behind me, faintly, I heard the click of a camera shutter.

Not mine.

I didn't turn around. Turning around confirms interest; interest confirms identity; identity confirms everything else. I walked into the saloon's yellow light, ordered a beer I had no intention of finishing, and sat with my back to the wall where I could watch the door and the window both, heart going like a jackhammer in a rock face.

Ten minutes later the spotter walked past the saloon window without looking in, unhurried, phone held to his ear. His other hand was in his jacket pocket. I'd bet the camera in my pocket that the camera in his pocket had my face in it.

* * *

Tomas was awake when I got back to camp, sitting up in his bag with the lantern lit, and whatever he saw in my face made him skip the sarcasm entirely, which frightened me more than the spotter had.

"You went back into town," he said.

"I went back into town."

"At night. With a camera you bought specifically so Petra would remember you buying it."

Petra would absolutely remember. That was the problem with small towns; they remembered everything except the things you needed them to. I sat down heavily on my pack and told him. The corkboard. The posting. The yard, the trucks, the seismic charges, the man with the cigarette and the shutter click.

When I finished, Tomas was quiet for a long moment, turning his grandfather's lighter over and over in his fingers — he'd taken to carrying it, though he claimed it was only because he kept forgetting to give it back to nobody.

"Halvard," he said finally. "That's the name from your paperwork. From your — the blacklist."

"Same name. Same serif font, probably. They're sentimental that way."

"So either it's a coincidence—"

"It's not a coincidence."

"—or they know about the map." He said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "They know Voss died. They know who visited him. And they've got a crew heading up the exact corridor your map describes, carrying enough explosive to rearrange the landscape." He looked up at me, and in the lantern light his face had gone from angry-young-man to something older and colder. "Hazel. What if the map isn't the treasure? What if it's the evidence?"

I had been circling that thought all evening the way you circle a crevasse field, probing, refusing to step on it. Tomas walked straight out onto it.

"Voss didn't strike me as a gold-chaser," I said carefully. "Struck me as a man paying off a debt."

"To who?"

"That's the question, isn't it." I pulled the map from the oilcloth and spread it on the ground between us, weighting the corners with stones. In the lantern light the ink lines looked almost alive, the river writhing north toward a blank space at the top of the sheet where Voss had drawn nothing at all — just a small symbol I'd taken for a surveyor's mark, and four words in his cramped hand along the margin.

Tomas leaned over it. His breath fogged in the cold.

"What does it say?"

I read it aloud, and hearing it in my own voice made it worse, the way saying a diagnosis out loud makes it real:

"*They paid me to forget. I didn't.*"

The wind moved down off the ridge and rattled the lantern, and somewhere south of us, back down the valley, I could have sworn I heard a truck engine turning over in the dark — early, eager, headed upstream.

Tomas looked at me across the map, and for the first time since the deathbed, he and I wanted exactly the same thing.

"We decode the margins," he said. "Tonight."
