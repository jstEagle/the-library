## 7. What Voss Actually Found

My grandfather's handwriting was a crime scene.

I don't mean that figuratively. I mean that whoever taught Eben Voss to write letters had clearly given up on him around the age of nine and left the rest to chance. His capitals leaned like fence posts in a windstorm. His numbers were worse — a 3 could be an 8 if you squinted, or if you were tired, or if you wanted it to be. For three days on the river I'd been telling myself the map was simple: a line of blue ink for the Serpent, a jagged scrawl for the gorge, an X where Xs go. Simple things don't need decoding. Simple things don't hide.

It was Hazel who gave me the idea without meaning to. We'd made camp above a bend where the river went quiet and glassy, and she was doing her evening ritual with the map — holding it up to the last light at an angle, the way she checked everything, like the paper might lie to her if she looked at it straight on. She did it absently, the way you'd scratch an old scar. Then she grunted, tilted it further, and said, "Huh."

"Huh" is not a word that inspires confidence coming from a professional guide. I asked what.

"Watermark," she said. "Or something under the ink. Hold it toward the sun."

I took it from her, careful, the way you take something from someone who trusts you more than you've earned. And there it was — faint as breath on cold glass. Lines beneath lines. My grandfather hadn't drawn one map. He'd drawn two, one on top of the other, and pressed so hard with his pen that the lower map had bled through into the upper one like a bruise showing through skin.

I didn't say anything to Hazel right then. I want that on the record. There was a moment — maybe ten seconds, maybe twenty — when I could have handed it back and said *nothing there, just the grain of the paper*, and she would have believed me, because she was busy with the fire and because trust is a thing people extend to you until you teach them not to.

Instead I folded the map into my jacket and told her I wanted first watch.

* * *

Here's what you should know about my family, since apparently I'm the only one left to tell it: we were never liars. That was the whole tragedy. The Vosses were terrible liars and proud farmers and, after the valley flooded, proud farmers with nowhere to be proud. The story I grew up on went like this: Eben Voss found gold in the high country above the drowned valley, got greedy, got strange, abandoned his wife and my mother to chase it, and died rich in secrets and poor in everything else. Every Christmas some aunt would drink too much and say the word *gold* like it was a diagnosis. My mother never said it at all. She just went quiet in a way that made the whole room go quiet with her, and I learned early that the map — if it existed — was the family's tumor. You didn't look at it. You certainly didn't touch it. You hoped it wasn't malignant.

So when he showed up dying in that roadside saloon and pressed the thing into Hazel Nystrom's hands instead of mine, I read it as the final insult. The last selfish act of a selfish man. Burn it, I told her. Bury it with him. I meant it the way you mean things when grief has hold of your throat — completely, and without thinking.

Now I sat alone by a fire I'd let burn low, with the map flat on a rock, and I held it toward the flames' light at the angle Hazel used, and I began to read the map underneath the map.

The top layer was the treasure hunt. Blue river, black gorge, an X in a cirque below the glacier's snout, and marginal notes in that ruined hand: *third bench*, *under the serac*, *cold even in July*. Gold-country language. Prospector's shorthand. The kind of notes a man writes when he's thinking about weight and yield and how many mules it takes to carry a fortune out.

The bottom layer was different. It was surveyor's work — precise, ruled, almost bureaucratic, which was so unlike my grandfather that it took me a while to accept it was his pen at all. But it was. I knew the little hooked tail he put on his 7s; they were all over the under-map. He'd traced something. Copied it. And what he'd copied was a grid.

Not a claim grid. I've seen claim grids; half the men in my hometown had framed ones over their toilets, aspirational wallpaper. This was a contour survey of the valley itself — the valley, the drowned one, rendered as it had been before the water came. Benchmarks. Elevations. And along the eastern rim, a series of marks I couldn't read at first because they weren't letters. They were symbols. Little triangles with tails. Circles with cross-hatching.

I sat with them for an hour, feeding the fire sticks and telling myself I was being ridiculous, that a dying prospector's doodles didn't constitute anything, that I was tired and grieving and looking for patterns the way lonely people find faces in wallpaper.

Then I remembered my uncle's garage.

This is the part I've never told anyone, and I suppose this is as good a place as any to lose my grip on it. When I was fourteen, my uncle Rune — my grandfather's son, the one who never forgave anybody — kept a locked steel box in his garage, and one summer afternoon I found it unlocked. Inside were papers. Old ones, brittle, stamped with the letterhead of a company I didn't recognize except that the name Halvard was in it somewhere, big and confident, the way names are when they can afford lawyers. I didn't read them. That's the coward's version and it's true: I heard my uncle's truck in the drive, and I closed the box, and I never went back, and by the time I was old enough to ask questions my uncle was dead and the box had vanished into whatever void swallows the belongings of angry men.

But I remembered the stamps. Triangles with tails. Circles with cross-hatching. Contamination markers — I know that now because I spent one very long night in a university library, years later, researching the flooding for a term paper I never turned in, and the sample reports from the valley's last water tests used exactly those symbols before the testing stopped. Before the valley stopped existing as a place anyone tested.

The under-map marked those same symbols along the eastern rim. Eleven of them. And beside each one, in my grandfather's ruined hand, small enough that he'd clearly hoped no one would ever read them, were dates and figures. Dates spanning six years. Figures with dollar signs.

He wasn't paid once. He was paid eleven times.

* * *

I want to describe what that does to a person, but the honest description is boring: I sat still. That's it. I sat still by a dead fire while the river talked to itself in the dark, and somewhere across the camp Hazel breathed in the rhythm of a woman who could sleep on a knife edge, and I sat still for what the stars told me was two hours and what felt like the rest of my life.

Because here's the arithmetic my brain refused to do and then did anyway, cruelly, all at once:

If the under-map was a contamination survey — and it was, the symbols matched, I'd stake my life on less — then my grandfather hadn't found gold above the valley. He'd found proof of what poisoned it. And if he'd been paid eleven times over six years, then the flooding hadn't been the accident our family lore wrapped us in like a blanket. Accidents don't come with installments. The valley hadn't drowned. It had been drowned. Deliberately. By a company that needed the evidence at its headwaters to sit under two hundred feet of water forever, and by my grandfather, who had taken their money and walked away and let his own family carry the shame of his absence like a coat that never fit.

Every Christmas. Every drunk aunt saying *gold*. My mother going silent in doorways. We thought we were carrying the story of a man who chose wealth over us. We were carrying the story of a man who chose money over truth, and the truth was that the water we'd been exiled from had been poison, and the exile had been the point, and he'd signed off on all of it in a hand so ugly it must have been ashamed of itself.

I laughed. I'll admit that. One short, ugly laugh out there in the dark, and Hazel stirred in her bag and I shut my mouth fast, and the laugh came back out through my nose in little steam-ghosts and wouldn't stop for a while.

Truth as betrayal's twin. That's the phrase that came to me, sitting there, and I've never improved on it. Because that's what I was holding: not a revelation, which is clean, but a betrayal wearing the revelation's clothes. The map wasn't a treasure. It was a confession he'd been too cowardly to make out loud, encoded in a format only greed would bother to decode. He'd known that. Maybe that was the joke he played on everyone, including himself — hide the truth inside the lie, so that only someone hunting gold would ever find the poison. Or maybe he just couldn't help himself. Dying men get honest. It's the one law of nature nobody escapes.

And the worst part — I keep digging for a worse part and finding the same one, so fine, here it is again: he gave it to Hazel. Not to me. Not to the family whose name he'd sold. To a stranger. A blacklisted guide he'd recognized from twenty years ago. Which meant either he trusted no one with his blood in them, or he'd watched what his silence had done to the people with his blood in them and decided we were compromised goods.

Both readings were insults. Both readings were probably true.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and unhurried, the way it does in canyon country, filling the gorge from the bottom like water rising. Hazel was up before it finished, boiling water, moving with that economy she had — no gesture wasted, no motion that didn't earn its keep. Twenty years of measuring miles had pared her down to pure function. I used to find it cold. That morning I envied it. Function doesn't have to decide what to do with a map.

"You were on watch," she said. "You're supposed to wake me at two."

"I lost track."

She looked at me over the stove flame. Hazel Nystrom has a way of looking at you that makes you aware of every lie currently residing in your body, like customs officers checking luggage. "You lost track," she repeated. "For four hours."

"The stars moved."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. She let it go and handed me coffee, and I took it, and we stood there drinking in silence while the river ran silver below us, and I understood that she knew. Not what — she couldn't know what — but that something had happened to me in the dark. Guides read people the way they read rivers. She'd seen the eddy where the current changed.

"We should make distance today," she said. "Gorge narrows ahead. If the map's honest, we hit the bench formations by tomorrow afternoon."

The map's honest. I nearly dropped the cup.

"And if it isn't?" I asked. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"Then we walk back out and I've wasted a season and you've wasted a grudge." She kicked dirt over the stove coals. "Either way the river goes the same direction."

We broke camp. I carried the map in my jacket, against my chest, where I could feel it with every step like a second heartbeat, and I spent the morning's miles composing speeches. That's what I do — you may have noticed. When I can't act, I compose. *Hazel, we need to talk about the margins.* *Hazel, my grandfather was paid to bury a valley.* *Hazel, the thing you're carrying for a dying man isn't a promise, it's evidence.* Each speech died before it reached my mouth, because each one required an admission I wasn't ready to make: that I'd gone through the map alone, at night, after she'd shown me the trick of the light. That my first instinct had been concealment. That the Voss talent for secrecy was hereditary and I'd caught it inside of twelve hours.

By midday the canyon walls had risen around us, red rock going to gray, the river compressing from a broad lazy animal into something quicker and more opinionated. Hazel moved through it like she was reading a book she'd loved as a child. I moved through it like a man being followed by his own handwriting.

At a rest stop — she called them "breathers," which I found unbearably patronizing until I noticed she took them herself and needed them — she sat on a boulder and unslung her pack and said, casually, to the middle distance: "You know what's funny about prospectors?"

"No."

"They never mark the gold. Not really. Oh, they draw the X, they write the benches and the seracs, but the real note, the important one, is always about the water." She flexed her fingers, working warmth into them. "Every old-timer's map I've ever handled, the margins come back to water. Where it runs. Where it pools. What it tastes like." She glanced at me then, brief and level. "Gold you can carry out. Water stays. Water remembers."

I said nothing. The river said plenty.

That afternoon we passed the third bench formation — I checked it against the top map, the gold map, the lie — and it matched perfectly, which somehow made everything worse. A liar's map shouldn't be accurate. Accuracy was for the under-map, the survey, the eleven payments. My grandfather had built his false front with the same care as his confession, and both were true, and neither was, and I walked between them like a man walking between mirrors.

Toward evening, Hazel stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into her pack. She crouched at the edge of the trail, and I followed her gaze down to the gravel: boot prints. Fresh. Two sets, heavy-treaded, moving upstream — the same direction we were going.

"Somebody's ahead of us," she said quietly.

"Other trekkers?"

"Not in a gorge with no exit and no permit station." She rose, scanning the rims above us with narrowed eyes, and I saw her do the thing she did — the counting, the calculating, the whole silent arithmetic of danger. "Company boots," she said. "Halvard treads. I saw the pattern on their supply crates in town."

Halvard. The name went through me like a fishhook. I thought of steel boxes and letterheads and triangles with tails, and of my uncle's garage, and of the fact that the company in my grandfather's margins and the company staking the upper Serpent were, I was suddenly certain, the same company, and that certainty arrived not as a thought but as a physical event, a cold hand closing around the back of my neck.

"Tomas." Hazel was watching me now, fully, all the customs-officer attention aimed at once. "What did you find in the margins?"

The gorge was quiet. The river ran. Somewhere upstream, men in company boots were walking toward the source my grandfather had bought and paid for and hidden, and I stood there with his confession against my chest and my family's whole rotten inheritance finally legible, and I heard myself say:

"I think we need to talk about the water."
