## 8. The Cold Camp

The storm came in like a debt collector — no warning, no negotiation, just a wall of white rolling off the western peaks at two in the afternoon while we were still an hour below the pass.

I'd been reading the sky wrong all day. That's the honest version. The flattering version is that the sky lied to me, which skies are legally allowed to do in the Greywater Range, but I've been doing this long enough that I should have known better. The wind had gone quiet around noon, that held-breath quiet that greenhorns mistake for mercy and guides mistake for nothing good. The temperature had dropped without dropping, if that makes sense — a cold that wasn't in the thermometer yet but was already in your bones, waiting for its paperwork to clear.

"Storm?" Tomas asked, when I stopped and looked up at the ridgeline.

"Storm."

"How bad?"

"On a scale of one to my ex-husband? Worse."

He didn't laugh. He never laughed at that one, which was starting to feel less like a failure of humor and more like a policy. We were four days out from the last supply town, six days into following the Serpent toward whatever Voss's map promised, and we had settled into something I hesitated to call companionship because it sounded too warm for what it was. It was more like two people carrying separate loads who had discovered their straps could be adjusted to share weight. Barely. Grudgingly. With frequent re-tightening.

The problem was elevation. The map's route climbed here — Voss had drawn a dotted line straight over the shoulder of the mountain instead of around it, because Eben Voss had apparently never met a shortcut he didn't trust more than his own knees. Below treeline we could have waited out weather in the pines, built a fire, been miserable in comfort. Above treeline there was nothing but rock, wind, and the kind of honesty you can't argue with.

"We make the saddle or we don't," I said. "If we don't, we dig in behind that moraine and wait it out."

"And if it waits us out?"

"Then we get very well acquainted."

We made the saddle with twenty minutes to spare, which sounds like luck but was actually arithmetic — I'd been counting our pace against the cloud line since noon, shaving breaks, lying about how much farther each push was. A guide's first tool is her legs. Her second is her mouth.

Behind the moraine — a great ugly rampart of rock the glacier had coughed up centuries ago — I dug us in. Snow cave, technically, though "cave" flatters what was essentially a hole we'd be living in. Tomas worked beside me without being asked twice, which by then counted as fluent cooperation. By dark we had a hollow big enough for two people and one pack, a sleeping platform, and a vestibule for the stove. Outside, the world ended. Inside, the world was four feet wide and smelled of butane and wet wool.

"Well," Tomas said, looking at the walls of our new home with the expression of a man touring real estate he hopes to flip quickly. "Cozy."

"Don't touch the ceiling. It'll snow on you."

"It's already snowing on me. It's snowing on everything."

"Not *in* here it isn't. That's the whole point of civilization."

He almost smiled. I saw it happen and then watched him decide against it, which was becoming its own kind of ritual between us. There was a version of this trip where we liked each other. We kept almost finding the door to it and then remembering we were supposed to be adversaries.

*

Here is what nobody tells you about storm camps: the storm is not the hard part. The hard part is the third day.

Day one is novelty. You melt snow, you eat, you sleep twelve hours and call it recovery. Day two is maintenance. You check the anchor points, you dig out the vestibule, you play cards with a deck missing the sevens through nines because Tomas had lost them somewhere in chapter five of our acquaintance and refused to explain how. Day three is when the walls start talking.

Not literally. Mostly.

By the morning of the third day the wind had dropped to a moan and the snowfall had thinned to that fine dry sift that means the system is dying but not ready to admit it. Visibility: maybe fifty feet of gray. Not enough to travel. Plenty enough to think, which was the problem, because thinking was the one activity I'd built my entire adult life to avoid.

Tomas was reading the map again. He read it the way other people pray — same pages, same hope of an answer, same suspicion that the answer would be a demand. His grandfather's handwriting crawled around the margins in pencil, annotations in a shorthand we'd partially cracked back in the supply town. Elevations. Dates. And in one corner, circled twice, a phrase that had been sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone ever since: *they paid for the water to stay down.*

"You knew him," Tomas said, on the third day, not looking up. "My grandfather. Before."

"I met him once. Twenty years ago. At a guide exchange in Corridor City." I fed another pinch of snow into the pot. "He gave a talk on high-altitude prospecting. Half the room thought he was a crank. The other half thought he was a genius. Both halves were right."

"He never mentioned knowing anyone." A pause with an edge on it. "He never mentioned much of anything. That was sort of his signature move."

"Some people's histories don't fit through doorways. They have to be carried through sideways, or not at all."

"That's a very elegant way of saying he was a coward."

"It's a very accurate way of saying he was afraid. Those aren't the same thing, but they wear the same coat."

He looked up at that. In the stove light his face had the rawness of someone who hadn't slept properly in a week, which was true of both of us; storm camps don't care whose grief is heavier. "You talk like you know."

"I know some things."

"Then say one."

The pot began to mutter. Outside, the wind found a new note, lower, almost conversational, like the mountain leaning down to eavesdrop. I have spent twenty years constructing a person out of competence — the guide who counts switchbacks, who reads rivers, who leaves cairns for the dead and calls it closure. That person has one load-bearing wall, and Tomas was standing next to it with a hammer he didn't even know he was holding.

"The avalanche," I said. "The one that got me blacklisted. You've heard the official version."

"Rope failure. Your partner fell. You survived. The company said your anchors were bad and pulled your certification."

"That's the version they printed." I turned the pot handle so it faced me, which is the kind of pointless thing hands do when the rest of you is busy falling. "Here's the version that happened."

And then — because there was no way down off that ledge except through — I told him.

*

His name was Wren Calloway. We'd been partners four years. Not lovers — everyone always assumes lovers, as if two people can't choose each other for colder, better reasons — but partners in the way that matters more. She read ice the way I read water. We finished each other's rope lengths. On the North Face of Mount Aster we once spent nine hours in a hanging belay in a lightning storm and invented a card game to stay sane, and she won, and I never let her forget it, and she never let me forget that I let her win.

We were guiding a two-client expedition on the Serpent's headwall — before the headwall was closed, before any of it was forbidden, back when the upper river was just hard country and not haunted country. Spring cornice season. Everyone knew the risk profile. Everyone signed the forms.

What the forms didn't cover was the client. A Halvard Extraction executive — I didn't know the name then, wouldn't learn it for years, learned it eventually in a way I'll tell you about in a minute — a man who wanted the summit photo for reasons involving a merger and a rival, and who treated our turnaround time as a negotiating position. We hit the deadline. He offered a bonus that would have covered my mother's medical bills for a year. Wren said no. I said no. He said he'd already radioed base that we were continuing, so the decision had been made for us, and wouldn't it be easier if we just agreed?

We turned around anyway. That part I did right. Remember that, later, when the rest of it comes out.

The cornice let go on the descent. Not on the steep pitch — on the flat, the cruel joke of avalanches, where you relax. It took Wren and one of the clients. The rope went taut. I was anchored. They were falling past me into the white, and physics was holding a vote, and every vote was going to end with all three of us in the debris field.

There was a knife on my harness. Guides carry them for exactly this. For exactly this, and you spend your whole career hoping the blade stays ceremonial.

I cut the rope.

I cut the rope and Wren went into the white without me, and I lived, and the client lived — barely, half-buried, dug out screaming — and Wren did not live, and I stood on the mountain with a severed rope in my hand and understood that I had just become the kind of story other guides tell each other in the dark.

The inquiry lasted eleven days. The Halvard lawyers attended every session. The verdict was rope failure — equipment defect, manufacturer liability, no fault assigned to the surviving guide. They gave me that. A clean verdict, no negligence, no charges. And then a man from Halvard's legal office bought me dinner and explained, in the gentle tone of someone discussing a weather system, that my testimony had been noted, appreciated, and concluded — and that in exchange for my continued agreement with the official record, certain professional considerations would be extended. My certification would remain suspended pending review. Indefinitely. The review required documentation. The documentation required the case to remain closed. It was all very circular and very airtight, the way cages built by lawyers are.

"They blacklisted you for staying quiet," Tomas said.

"I blacklisted myself. They just handled the paperwork. My testimony was the only thing that could have unraveled their version — that the executive pressured the team, that the turnaround protocol was ignored, that the whole thing was a man buying a photograph with other people's gravity. If I'd testified fully, Halvard would have eaten a wrongful death suit that would have gutted the upper-river operation. So they bought my silence with the thing I loved most, and called it severance." I laughed, and it came out wrong, the way laughs do when they've been stored too long. "Twenty years. Do you know how many miles I've walked trying to outrun a knife stroke that took half a second?"

Tomas didn't say anything for a long time. The stove hissed. The wind outside worked at the moraine like an old woman worrying a hem.

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because day three is when the walls start talking, and I'd rather it be me than them." I nodded at the map in his lap. "And because your grandfather knew. About the verdict, about the deal. He was at the inquiry — I remembered his face halfway up this mountain and it scared me half to death. He was in the back row, the crank prospector nobody listened to, and he watched them hand me a clean verdict and a dirty cage, and I think—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I let it. "I think that's why he picked me. Why he handed a stranger his life's work in a saloon. Because he'd seen what they did, and he'd taken their money himself, and he wanted — at the end — to put something back on the right side of the ledger. Starting with me."

Tomas set the map down carefully, the way you set down something that might still go off.

"You cut the rope to save yourself," he said. Not a question. Not an accusation either. Just a man laying the fact on the table between us and looking at it.

"Yes."

"And you've been paying for it ever since."

"Every mile."

He was quiet a moment longer. Then he said the thing I had not prepared for, because I had prepared for horror, for disgust, for the slow polite withdrawal of a man rearranging his tent assignments — anything but this:

"My grandfather took money to bury what he found in that valley. Twenty years of my family calling him a liar, a disgrace, a man who abandoned us for greed. And maybe he was a coward too. But he was *our* coward, and he spent his last months trying to climb out of it, and now you're telling me you spent twenty years doing the same thing in the opposite direction." He shook his head slowly. "You cut a rope to live. He signed papers to protect us. Neither of those is the sin people say it is. The sin is the ones who made the choice necessary."

"Tomas—"

"Don't." But gently. "Don't take it back. You've carried it alone long enough that you've forgotten it isn't shaped like guilt. It's shaped like survival. Those look similar from the inside. I'm telling you how it looks from out here." He picked the map back up, and his voice steadied into something almost practical, which from him was practically tenderness. "Get some sleep, Nystrom. Tomorrow the sky clears, and we've got a valley to find, and apparently a company to ruin. You can't ruin a company tired."

I lay back against the pack and stared at the low dome of blue-dark snow above us and felt something in my chest unclench by a single degree — the first degree in twenty years. Confession, it turns out, is not absolution. It's just setting the weight down long enough to remember you have arms.

I slept better than I had any right to.

*

The fourth dawn came clean and hard, the sky scoured to a blue so deep it looked structural. We broke camp in an hour, bodies moving on muscle memory and mutual silence, and I stood a moment at the mouth of the cave looking down the far side of the saddle at the country beyond — and stopped.

Because the snow told stories after a storm, and this one was telling one I didn't like.

Tracks along the eastern ridge. Two sets, maybe three, boot-soled, deliberate, spaced like men who knew they were being a message. They'd come up during the storm's tail, sheltered somewhere on the far side, and moved on before first light. Nobody hikes a storm ridge for pleasure.

Tomas came up beside me and followed my gaze. "Those ours?"

"Our tracks are behind us. Those are pointed at us."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "How long have they been watching?"

I checked the drift patterns, the fill-in at the edges, and did the math I didn't want to do. "Since before the storm. Maybe since the supply town." I shouldered my pack and turned up the ridge, toward the dotted line and whatever waited at the end of it. "Walk fast, Tomas. And stay where I can see you."

Behind us, on the eastern ridge, something glinted — glass, or a lens, catching the new sun.

Then it was gone.
