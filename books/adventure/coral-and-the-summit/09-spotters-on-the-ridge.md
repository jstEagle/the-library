## 9. Spotters on the Ridge

I learned to spot my grandfather's lies by the way he polished his glasses when he told them. I learned to spot Halvard's men the same way — by the small things they couldn't help doing wrong.

It started with the cairn.

We'd come up out of the gorge country onto a long gray shoulder of ridge where the pines gave out and the world turned to stone and lichen. Hazel had stopped at a pile of rocks someone had stacked at the trail's edge — not one of hers, she said, too sloppy, no reverence in it — and while she was frowning at it I noticed the second thing: boot prints. Two sets. Fresh enough that the edges hadn't crumbled. One set heavy on the heels, the other light, almost dancing, like a man who walked for a living and enjoyed it.

"Company boots," Hazel said, before I even asked. She crouched and touched the print without touching it, hovering her fingers over it like it might bite. "Vibram soles, new. Rangers wear theirs down. These boys bought their gear last month."

"Maybe hikers."

"Hikers don't walk heel-first through scree." She stood and scanned the ridgeline the way she did everything — methodically, left to right, like reading a page she didn't trust. "And hikers don't leave a cairn where there's no route to mark."

The cairn wasn't marking anything. It sat beside the trail like a headstone for nobody. Which meant it was marking *us*. A signpost for whoever came behind: *they passed here.*

That was three days ago. Since then I've gotten very good at looking at rocks.

* * *

You should understand something about me before this goes any further: I am not an outdoorsman. I'm an archivist. I spent six years cataloguing municipal water records in a basement in Meridian City, and the most dangerous thing I ever encountered down there was a box of 1970s asbestos tiles that the facilities department kept promising to remove. My hands are soft in a way that used to embarrass my grandfather. He'd take one look at them across the dinner table — the two dinners a year we shared, both of us armed and armored — and say nothing, which from Eben Voss was a sermon.

So when Hazel Nystrom says *we're being followed* and my stomach drops through the scree into the mantle of the earth, that's not cowardice. That's accurate self-assessment. I know exactly what I'm built for, and it isn't this.

But here's the other thing about me: I'm stubborn in the specific way of people who grew up around a liar. You spend your childhood being told the flood was an act of God, that Grandfather's silence was grief, that the family name is clean if you just don't dig — eventually you develop a allergy to unfinished sentences. And everything since that deathbed has been one long unfinished sentence. The map. The valley. The word my grandfather died trying to say, his mouth still shaping it when the breath went out of him.

I wasn't going to let two men in new Vibrams finish it for him.

"They know we're coming," I told Hazel that first night, in the wind shadow of a boulder the size of a chapel. She was boiling snow over a stove the size of a coffee can, and she didn't look up.

"They've known since Meridian. Maybe since the visitor log."

"The what?"

She looked up then. "Hospitals keep logs. Who visited the dying man, how long they stayed. Somebody at Halvard reads those. Or somebody sold it to them." She handed me the cup, which was mostly hot air with ambitions. "Your name's in a book somewhere, Tomas. So's mine. Probably underlined."

I thought about that all night, the way you think about a splinter. Somewhere in a fluorescent office a man with a laminated badge had read *T. VOSS — GRANDSON — 40 MIN* and made a phone call, and now there were boots on our trail, and the whole apparatus of a billion-dollar company had tilted toward us like a searchlight. All because a dying man drew a map on butcher paper and I couldn't stop being angry at him long enough to burn it.

Hazel slept in shifts after that. She tried to give me the first watch, saw my face, and revised her estimate of me downward without comment. I took the second watch instead, midnight to four, lying in the dark listening to the mountain tick and settle, learning its sounds the way you learn a house at night. Wind in the couloir. Rockfall somewhere far off, a dry rattle like dice. And once — I'm nearly sure — footsteps that stopped when I stopped breathing.

* * *

By the second day they'd stopped pretending.

We were traversing a scree field below a band of red cliffs — Hazel called it "the talus highway," which is guide-speak for *the ground is actively trying to kill you but efficiently* — when I heard it: a single sharp whistle, three notes, descending. It echoed off the cliffs and came back wrong, doubled, so I couldn't tell where it came from. Hazel froze mid-stride with one foot on a slab the size of a door, and for a moment she was absolutely still in a way that made the mountains look restless by comparison.

"Don't look," she said quietly. "Keep walking. Natural pace."

"My natural pace is a dead crawl."

"Then die slowly and evenly."

We crossed the field like that, her calling holds to me in a low flat voice — *left foot, the gray one, weight back* — while my neck crawled with the certainty of eyes. When we finally dropped behind a rib of solid rock into the next drainage, she pulled me down against it and we waited. Ten minutes. Twenty. Then, high on the ridge above us, a figure moved between two boulders — just a suggestion of a man, tan jacket, easy gait — and stopped. He knew he'd been seen. He raised one hand, not waving. More like a conductor acknowledging the orchestra.

"He wants us to know," I whispered.

"He wants us to turn around." Hazel's jaw was working. "This is the polite part. This is them being *nice*."

"What's the impolite part?"

She didn't answer, which was the answer.

That night we didn't risk a fire, and we ate cold — oatmeal bars and the smoked fish we'd bought in the supply town, the town where I'd photographed survey crews staking the upper Serpent and, as it turned out, been photographed myself. Hazel checked the perimeter twice. I lay with the map inside my jacket, against my chest, because it had become the kind of object you guard with your body. Butcher paper and pencil lines and my grandfather's cramped annotations, worth more per square inch than anything I'd ever touched.

"Tomas." Hazel's voice out of the dark, low. "If they come tonight, we split. You go down-drainage, hard, all night if you have to. You don't wait for me."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You're not *leaving* me. You're carrying the map away from me. That's different." A pause. "One person runs faster than two. Two people run slower than one. It's the oldest math there is."

"I've done that math," I said. "My family's been doing that math for sixty years. Somebody always stays behind and somebody always gets paid to forget them."

The dark was quiet a long time. Then, softer: "Okay. New plan. Nobody splits."

* * *

They came on the third day, and they were not polite.

We'd climbed into a bowl of broken rock below the ridge crest, following a line Hazel had picked from the map — a shelf system that switchbacked up through the cliffs toward the high route. The sky was the flat white of a page not yet written on. I remember thinking the light was bad for seeing distance, and then remembering I'd learned to think things like that, and feeling briefly proud, and then the first rock came down.

Not a rockfall. A *thrown* rock, the size of a fist, cracking off the slab ten feet ahead of us with a report like a rifle. Then another, wider. Then a voice, amplified off the cliffs so it seemed to come from everywhere:

"That's far enough."

Hazel had me behind a boulder before the echo finished. She had her ice axe out — not for climbing. I'd never seen anyone hold an ice axe like that, like an argument.

"We're just passing through!" she called back. "Public land!"

"So is the morgue." The voice was cheerful, which was worse. "Ma'am, we counted your camps. We know your pace. We know you're carrying something old man Voss drew, because he drew it for everybody who'd listen the last twenty years, and every one of those maps ends in the same hole in the ice. There's nothing up there but a spring that'll rot your teeth and a cave full of his crazy. Turn around. Go home. Write your book or whatever it is you do."

I put my eye to the crack between boulder and rock. Two figures on the ridge crest above, maybe three hundred meters up-slope. The tall one with the easy gait, and a shorter man standing very still, and — my stomach went cold — a third, farther along the crest, positioned exactly where we'd have gone if we ran down-drainage. They'd herded us into the bowl overnight. While I slept my watch and felt proud of myself for learning the mountain's sounds, they'd been doing arithmetic.

"That's Renn's crew," Hazel breathed. Almost to herself. Something complicated crossed her face and was gone.

"Who's Renn?"

"Nobody. Later." She raised her voice. "We're going up. You want to stand in our way, that's your paperwork, not mine."

"Here's *our* paperwork," the cheerful voice said, and the tall man bent, and this time it wasn't a rock.

It was our packs.

They'd taken them off the ledge where we'd cached them an hour earlier — cached, hidden, *my idea*, I'd read about caching in a book — and the tall man held mine up over the drop like a trophy. Everything. The tent. The stove. The rope. The food. Six days of the upper route in a fifty-liter bag, held out over three hundred feet of scree by a man in a new jacket.

"Turn back," the voice said, "or be buried. Your call. We're patient people. Company's got deep pockets and longer winters than you do."

I want to tell you I did something brave. What I did was grab Hazel's arm, because the map was in *my* jacket and the packs were everything else, and I watched a stranger dangle my whole life over a cliff. But Hazel had gone very quiet, and quiet Hazel, I was learning, was the dangerous one.

"Drop them," she called, "and I'll tell you a story about a climb on Mount Corren, eleven years ago, and a man named Aldous Renn, and a brother he hasn't heard from since."

Silence on the ridge. Long enough that the wind filled it.

Then the packs came sailing down — both of them, tumbling, slamming into the scree in bursts of gear and grit — and the three figures withdrew along the crest, unhurried, the tall one last, pausing once to touch two fingers to his forehead. Not a salute. A receipt.

* * *

We salvaged what we could. The tent had a torn fly. The stove survived. Half the food was gone — scattered, buried in scree, or simply taken; they'd rifled the bags and left a message in the arrangement, our things laid out neat on a flat slab like a display case: *we can reach anything you have.* Under the stove, folded, a page torn from a Halvard survey notebook. On it, printed block letters:

**LAST WARNING. THE MOUNTAIN IS UNSTABLE. WE WOULD HATE TO SEE AN ACCIDENT.**

"'An accident,'" I read aloud. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which I've decided is the family talent. Not courage. Delivery.

Hazel repacked in silence, fast and economical, her hands doing the work while her eyes stayed on the ridgeline. When she finished she sat back on her heels and looked at the torn page for a long moment.

"They're scared," she said finally.

"They threw our food off a cliff."

"That's what scared looks like, when it has a budget." She stood, shouldered the wounded pack, and squinted up at the route — the shelf system switchbacking into the white sky, the high country beyond it where the map's inked line ended in my grandfather's final annotation, the one word he'd underlined twice: *SOURCE.* "They had three days to just report us and let the permits do their work. Instead they burned a day playing theater on a ridge. Companies only get theatrical when the lawyers have stopped answering emails."

"So we keep going."

She looked at me then, really looked, the way she did when she was deciding whether to say a true thing. "There's another way up. Faster. It saves us two days, maybe three, and right now days are the only currency we've got." A pause, and I watched her pay for the next sentence in advance. "It's called the Kestrel Traverse."

Something in the way she said it. The way she'd said *Renn* earlier. Like a word with a hook in it.

"And?" I said.

"And it's the route I haven't climbed since the avalanche." She turned up-slope before I could answer, before I could even arrange my face. "Eat while we walk. We've lost the luxury of camp."

I stood a moment longer among our scattered, salvaged lives, looking back down the valley we'd climbed out of — the long gray miles, the little cairn that had started it, the place where three men in new boots were probably already radioing that the trespassers hadn't turned back.

Then I followed her up, toward the route she feared, with a dead man's map against my chest and a warning in my pocket that promised to bury us both.

The mountain, I was beginning to understand, had only been warming up.
