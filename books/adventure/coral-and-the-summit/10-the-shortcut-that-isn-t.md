## 10. The Shortcut That Isn't

The map lied to us on the third day past the gorge mouth, and I should have known it would. Voss drew that thing with a dying man's optimism and a prospector's contempt for scale. What he'd marked as "half a day's walk" turned out to be two days of riverbed choked with boulders the size of sleeping cattle, and what he'd marked as "easy going" was a talus slope that shed rock every time the sun warmed it, like the mountain had dandruff and no shame about it.

We lost the calving window by inches. Then we lost it by feet.

By the evening of the fourth day, sitting on a ledge above the Serpent while it hissed through a slot canyon below us, I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. The long way around — the sane way, the way any licensed guide would route a client — was four more days. Four days we didn't have. Halvard's dynamite crates were already stacked at the valley mouth like firewood someone meant to burn before winter, and every hour we spent picking our way through this geological junkyard was an hour closer to Renn's detonator plans becoming a crater and a press release.

There was another way. There had always been another way. It hung in my head like a tooth that needed pulling, and I'd been tonguing it for days.

"The Kestrel Traverse," I said.

Tomas looked up from the stove he was coaxing to life. He'd gotten good at the stove. Two weeks of bad weather will teach anyone to feed a flame. "That a place or a threat?"

"Both, maybe." I pulled out my old topo — not Voss's map, but mine, the one from before, creased along lines I'd worn soft with my thumbs over twenty years of not looking at them. I spread it between us on the flat of a boulder. "It cuts the corner. Two days instead of six. High route, above treeline, across the shoulder of Mount Kestrel itself." I tapped the line. "It's also banned."

"Banned how? Like your-guide-license banned, or like actually-illegal banned?"

"There's a difference?"

He almost smiled. He'd been doing that more lately, and I kept catching myself cataloguing it, the way you notice weather turning.

"It was closed after an accident," I said. "Twenty years ago."

Tomas went still in that particular way of his, the stillness of a man doing math he doesn't want to finish. "Your accident."

"My accident."

* * *

Here is what I had not told him yet, though he'd earned pieces of it on the cold camp and never asked for the rest: the Kestrel Traverse wasn't just where the avalanche happened. It was where the avalanche happened *because of me*. Because I pushed the schedule. Because the snowpack had been screaming at us for two days — whumpfing under our skis, that hollow drumbeat sound that every mountaineer knows means the mountain is lying to you about being solid — and I wanted the traverse done before a client deadline, because I was twenty-six and hungry and my partner Dana was the kind of person who trusted her guide completely, which is its own kind of hazard.

I cut the rope. That part I told him already, on the storm night, in the dark where confessions are cheaper. But I hadn't told him the rest: that after the company bought my silence and the board took my license, the Kestrel Traverse got closed too — quietly, unofficially, folded into some land-use restriction Halvard helped draft. My ruin and the route's ruin came from the same pen. They closed the mountain the way you'd close a wound you didn't want examined.

So when I said *banned*, I meant all of it. Banned by regulation. Banned by grief. Banned by me, most thoroughly, in the private country where I kept my laws.

"We're taking it," Tomas said.

I laughed, short and ugly. "You don't get a vote on this one."

"I'm not voting. I'm telling you." He set down the stove handle and faced me, and I saw something in him I hadn't seen before — not the angry grandson from the deathbed, not the kid who lied to a ranger checkpoint with his heart hammering so loud I could hear it. Something settled. "Hazel. You've been circling this route since the supply town. Every time the map gets hard, your eyes go up." He pointed at the shoulder of Kestrel, blue and white and indifferent in the last light. "You want to take it. You've wanted to take it since before I met you. You just need someone to make it not your idea."

"That's manipulation."

"That's what family does." He said it flatly, without drama, and then seemed surprised at himself, as if the word *family* had come out of his mouth without clearance from headquarters. He looked away first. "My grandfather spent twenty years not talking about this valley. I know what avoidance looks like from the inside. It looks exactly like a woman doing extra miles she doesn't have to do."

I wanted to argue. God, I wanted to argue. But the truth was I'd been counting switchbacks again — the old habit, measuring my life in safe increments, safe miles, safe detours — and somewhere in the last two weeks the counting had started to feel less like discipline and more like a cage I'd built and called it prudence.

"One condition," I said.

"Name it."

"You carry the rope."

* * *

We started at four in the morning, because glacier routes belong to the dark. The approach was honest work — moraine, then snowfield, then the long grunt up the shoulder where the wind came off the ice cap with opinions. Tomas moved well. Better than well; he'd gone from a man who complained about his pack to a man who adjusted it without breaking stride, and I caught myself grading him the way I used to grade clients, and then stopped, because he wasn't a client. Clients don't learn your silences.

The crux came at midmorning: a rib of rock and ice, maybe three hundred meters, angling across the face below a cornice that hung there like a held breath. Twenty years ago I'd led this pitch with Dana laughing behind me, saying I climbed like I argued — stubbornly and slightly too fast.

I stood at the base and my hands knew things my head hadn't authorized. They remembered the cold. They remembered the specific weight of a rope coming taut wrong.

"Hazel." Tomas's voice, level. "Talk to me. Where are you?"

"Twenty years ago," I said.

"No. You're here. It's Tuesday, it's freezing, and I'm about to follow you up a banned mountainside, so one of us needs to be competent." He clipped into the anchor I'd built, checked it twice the way I'd taught him, and looked at me. "You told me once that the mountain doesn't care about your story. So stop telling it one."

"Were you always this annoying or is it recent?"

"Recent. It's your influence."

I laughed — actually laughed, at the base of the Kestrel Traverse, which felt like blasphemy and medicine at the same time — and I racked my gear, and I started up.

And here is the thing I will tell you plainly, because it took me twenty years and one stubborn grandson to learn it: the fear doesn't leave. It comes with you. It rode every meter of that pitch, sat on my shoulders like an old climbing partner, whispered its familiar arithmetic — *ice at forty degrees, cornice overhead, you are fifty-one years old, Hazel Nystrom, what are you proving* — and I climbed anyway. Not through the fear. With it. Letting it ride along like weather, checking my placements a little more carefully than pride required, moving slower than twenty-six-year-old me would have, and faster than scared me would have believed possible.

Halfway up, the wind gusted and the whole face went silent in that eerie way wind does when it holds its breath, and for one second I was back in the white roar, the rope, the cut, the falling —

— and then Tomas's voice came up from below, steady as a metronome: "Slack's good. Move when ready."

Not a rescue. Just a man holding a rope, doing his job, trusting me to do mine.

I moved when ready.

At the top of the rib, I built the anchor, brought him up, and we stood together on the shoulder of Kestrel with the whole Serpent watershed spilled out below us — the canyon we'd crawled through, the distant glint of the valley mouth, and beyond it, high and white and terrible, the source itself, a smudge of blue on the horizon like a bruise seen through skin.

My hands weren't shaking.

I checked. Twice. I stood there in the wind with my palms open like an idiot, examining them, and Tomas watched me do it and understood, because by now he understood most things about me that I hadn't said out loud.

"Well?" he said.

"Well what?"

"Do you feel different?"

I thought about lying. I thought about giving him the movie answer, the triumphant-answer, the one where the ghosts stand down and the music swells. Instead I said: "No. Same. Everything's the same." And then, because he deserved better than the movie version: "But I'm the same too. That's the point. I crossed it and I'm still here and nothing changed except the miles." I looked at the route behind us, the clean line of it, mine again. "Turns out the shortcut was never the mountain. It was me getting out of my own way."

Tomas nodded slowly, and then, because he was still young enough to be honest: "You're crying."

"I'm sweating from my eyes. It's a mountaineering thing."

"Sure it is."

* * *

We made the far side by late afternoon and dropped into a hanging basin where the snow lay deep and quiet, and we camped early for the first time in a week — not because we had to, but because for once neither of us could find a reason to push. Tomas melted snow and I rigged the shelter and we ate rehydrated something-or-other that tasted like victory anyway, and I felt something in my chest unclench that I hadn't known was clenched. Twenty years of measuring life in safe miles, and it turns out the safest mile I ever walked was the one everyone told me never to walk again.

Tomas was quiet for a long time, poking the stove. Then: "Dana. Was she the one who —"

"The cairn. Yeah." I fed a twig to the flame. "Every trek, every year. One stone. You've seen me do it."

"I thought it was a survey habit."

"It's a promise habit. I'll show you where hers stands, someday. If we get through what's coming."

"When," he said. Not *if*. When.

I let him have it. Hope is cheap currency out here and I wasn't going to devalue his.

We turned in early, and I slept the sleep of the newly unburdened, which is to say deeply and without the usual audit of regrets running its nightly ledger. Somewhere past midnight, a sound woke me — not the wind, not settling snow. A light. Far off, down on the flank of the mountain we'd just crossed, a pinprick of white sweeping slowly across the scree fields below the traverse. Methodical. Deliberate. The kind of sweep that isn't looking for a lost hiker.

A floodlamp. Or a spotlight from a machine that had no business being on this mountain.

I lay very still and watched it crawl, and counted the seconds between passes, and did the only arithmetic that mattered anymore: they knew we'd taken the Kestrel. Which meant they knew where we were going.

Which meant we weren't racing the calendar anymore.

We were racing them.
