## 11. A Valley That Answers

The river told us before the map did.

That's the thing nobody tells you about water — it's a worse liar than people. People will smile at you and say everything's fine while their eyes are doing arithmetic on your throat. A river just narrows its voice when it's being squeezed, and if you're listening, you can hear the walls closing in before you ever see them.

We'd been walking since before dawn, after two days of moving only at night, hugging the shadow side of the ridge like criminals, which — technically — we were. Hazel had a system for it. She always has a system for everything: sleep in shifts, eat cold, no fires above the snowline, tape over anything that glints. She'd taped my belt buckle the first night without asking, the way you'd muzzle a dog that barks at the wrong moment. I hadn't decided yet whether to be insulted.

"Listen," she said, and stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into her pack.

I listened. The Serpent had gone quiet. Not quiet like a sleeping thing — quiet like a held breath. The wide brown chatter we'd followed for six days had pulled itself into something narrow and intent, a single low note threading between stone.

"The gorge," she said. "It should be close."

She didn't take out the map. That was how I knew she didn't need to. Somewhere in the last week my grandfather's hand-drawn lines had migrated from the oilskin packet into her head, the way routes do with people who've spent twenty years measuring mountains instead of living in them. She walked like she was reading something written under the ground.

The canyon mouth opened in front of us an hour later, and I'll be honest about what I felt, because I've spent this whole trip lying to myself about less important things.

It was relief. Enormous, stupid, childlike relief.

Because the map was right.

* * *

You have to understand what that meant to me. For most of my life, Eben Voss's map had been the family's ghost story — the thing he'd supposedly drawn in some fevered year, the fantasy that made him unmarriageable at reunions and unmentionable at funerals. My mother called it "the paper." Not *the map*. The paper, like it was a bill that never got paid. When I was ten, I found a corner of a copy in his old footlocker and my father burned it in the sink and said, gently, the way you say things to children you don't want to scare: *Some men would rather be right than be loved.*

So there I stood, at the mouth of a gorge that matched the map line for line — the twin spires Voss had drawn as broken teeth, the shelf of white scree he'd marked with a little skull he probably thought was charming, the exact place where the river bent left around a rib of black rock — and every one of those lines was true, and the man who drew them had died in a rented room smelling of camphor and pennies, alone except for me and a stranger he'd trusted more than his own blood.

"He was right," I said. It came out smaller than I meant it to.

Hazel didn't gloat. She just looked at the gorge the way she looks at everything — like she's pricing it against what it might cost her — and said, "Being right is the easy part. Come on."

The gorge swallowed us by midmorning. The walls rose sheer and striped, red and grey and a green-black lichen that looked like the mountain had been tattooed by someone with poor impulse control. The river ran fast and glass-green between them, and the sound came off the stone from all directions at once, so that you couldn't tell where the water was, only that it was everywhere and it wanted through.

Hazel moved through it like a woman returning to a house she'd been evicted from. Touching the rock as she passed. Reading the flood debris caught in cracks eight feet over our heads and muttering numbers. At one point she stopped, put her palm flat against the wall, and stood there for so long I started to worry.

"What are you doing?"

"Listening." She said it without embarrassment, which is a skill I'm still working on. "Rock talks. This section's talking fine. The far wall's quieter." She took her hand away. "Quieter is not better."

I wrote that down in my head, next to everything else she'd taught me without meaning to. How to read a bend in the water for depth. How to tell fresh rockfall from old by the color of the fracture. How to walk on scree so it doesn't announce you. Two weeks ago I'd been a man who thought mountains were scenery. Now I caught myself checking the angle of a slope out of pure habit, and I don't know whether that's education or infection.

We camped that night in a hollow above the waterline, cold camp, no fire, sharing a stove flame cupped behind her back like contraband. And that was when I saw it — or rather, heard it. Underneath the river's note, underneath the wind worrying at the gorge rim, there was another sound. Deep. Irregular. Like something enormous turning over in bed.

Hazel heard it too. I watched her face do a thing I'd only seen once before, on the Kestrel Traverse, when she'd looked at a slope and named, out loud, the exact way it would fail.

"That's ice," she said. "High up. Moving."

"It's June."

"It's June," she agreed. "And it's calving season anyway, and warm springs come early now. The glacier's shedding." She turned toward the dark end of the gorge, upstream, where the walls pinched together into blackness. "Which means the source is awake. Which means Halvard knows exactly how much time they have, same as us."

I lay on the cold stone listening to the glacier groan and thought about calendars. About how my whole life I'd treated time like an infinite resource because I'd never wanted any particular day to arrive. Now every hour had a price tag and I could hear the meter running in the dark.

* * *

We reached the mouth of the valley at noon the next day, and this is the part I keep trying to write down honestly and keep failing, because the honest version sounds like poetry and I am not a poetic man.

The gorge opened. That's all. The walls stepped apart like hands unclenching, and there it was — the valley. Wide and green-grey along the floor, the river braiding itself into slow silver channels, the slopes rising on either side into country that had clearly never been logged, never been grazed, never been anything but itself. And at the head of it, filling the sky from wall to wall, the glacier: blue-white, fissured, ancient, hanging over the valley like a judge about to read a verdict.

Voss had drawn it small, in pencil, in the corner of a page. It was not small. It was the largest living thing I had ever seen, and it was dying, and it was beautiful, and both of those facts arrived in my chest at the same moment and refused to sort themselves out.

"There," Hazel said quietly, and pointed.

At the base of the glacier, maybe three miles up the valley floor, the ice dipped into a fold of rock, and beneath that fold — invisible from here, but present, certain, the way a heartbeat is present through a wall — was the source. The spring. The poisoned vein my grandfather had found and been paid to forget.

The valley was real. My grandfather had been right. I stood there letting that land, letting it settle into all the hollow places in me that the word *fantasy* had carved over thirty years, and I felt something I can only describe as grief and vindication having a fistfight.

Then Hazel grabbed my arm and pulled me down behind a boulder, and the fistfight ended, because hope is a luxury you can only afford when nothing is trying to kill you.

"Dynamite," she said.

I looked. Past the last stand of pines, where the valley floor narrowed to a track barely wide enough for a vehicle, there were crates. Maybe forty of them, stacked in a rough pyramid under tarps, and beside them a piece of equipment I recognized from news footage of mining disasters: a bore rig. Fresh tire ruts stitched the mud. A generator hummed somewhere out of sight, that flat industrial drone that sounds wrong in wild country the way a scream sounds wrong in a church.

And staked into the ground beside the crates, flapping in the valley's first clean breeze, a flag. White field, blue mark. Halvard Extraction.

"They're already here," I said. Stupidly. As if saying it would make it untrue.

"They've been here a while." Hazel was scanning the tree line with those flat guide's eyes, cataloguing. "Those ruts are rain-softened. Week, maybe more. They set up before the melt even finished." She crouched lower. "They weren't waiting to find the source, Tomas. They already know where it is. They're waiting for the calendar."

"For calving season."

"For calving season." She said it the way a doctor says a diagnosis they've given a hundred times. "Glacier lets go, the valley floods, half the mountain comes down, and whatever happens to that spring gets written up as an act of God. Nobody investigates a glacier. Glaciers don't testify."

I thought about the margins of the map. The numbers Voss had hidden in plain sight for thirty years — contamination readings, dates, initials of men who'd signed things they shouldn't have. All of it sitting in a steel drum somewhere under that blue ice, and all of it scheduled to become rubble and meltwater within the month.

My grandfather had spent three decades carrying that evidence like a coal in his chest, burning a hole in him slow enough that he mistook it for a life. And these men had driven up in trucks and stacked it in crates and put a flag on it, and they planned to be home by August.

"We go up tonight," I said. "While it's light—"

"No."

"—while they're—"

"No." Hazel's voice wasn't harsh. It was worse. It was patient. "There's a camp past that tree line. I counted four structures and at least one antenna. There'll be watches, radios, probably a spotter on the rim — there's always a spotter on the rim, it's the first thing I'd do and I assume they pay people to think like me." She finally turned and looked at me straight on. "We get one approach. One. If we go up there tonight, tired, in daylight habits, we die stupidly and the drum stays buried and Voss burned for nothing. We do this the way you do anything on a mountain. Slowly. Boringly. Alive."

"I hate that answer."

"I know. It's still correct."

We withdrew the way we'd come, back into the gorge's throat, and made cold camp in a hollow she chose for reasons she explained in full and I understood maybe half of. And then we did the worst thing there is to do on a mountain, which is nothing. We watched. Through the afternoon, through the long gold slide of evening, we lay in the rocks and watched the valley we'd crossed a country to reach, and it sat there in the light, gorgeous and doomed, wearing a company flag like a name tag.

Somewhere in the middle of the watch, the glacier spoke again — that deep irregular groan, longer this time, rolling down the valley like a clearing throat. In the camp below, tiny with distance, figures paused, looked up, and went back to work. They weren't afraid of it. They were counting on it.

"Tomas." Hazel's voice, low, near my ear. "Look at the track. Left of the crates."

I looked. A figure was walking down from the camp toward the valley mouth, alone, unhurried, carrying a survey case. He stopped at the edge of the mud, took out instruments, began taking readings with the bored competence of a man who has done this four hundred times. He was fifty yards from the tree line. Fifty yards from us. No escort, no radio raised to his face, nothing between him and the shadows but his own routine.

"One man," Hazel murmured. "Alone. If we ever want to know their schedule, their blast plan, their watch rotation — that's the door, and it's open right now, and it won't be open tomorrow."

"And if he doesn't want to talk?"

"Then we'll have a problem." A pause. Her profile in the failing light was unreadable, which from Hazel was practically a confession. "But people talk, Tomas. Especially people who've started wondering whose math they're doing."

The figure closed his instrument case. Started walking back. Fifty yards of open ground between him and safety, and between him and us, and the glacier groaned overhead like a clock striking.

Hazel's hand found my shoulder in the dark.

"Now or never," she said.
