## 12. The Man in the Crate Road

The thing nobody tells you about working for Halvard Extraction is that the pay is excellent and the dreams are not.

My name is Renn Calloway, and for eleven days I had been walking the upper Serpent with a surveyor's transit, a crate of dynamite I wasn't supposed to think too hard about, and a set of orders so clean they squeaked: stake the blast line, log the readings, don't ask what's under the ice. I'm good at not asking. It's a family trait. My brother Deke is good at falling off mountains instead, which is how we ended up even, more or less — he lived because somebody up there didn't ask questions either, just did her job and hauled him off a cornice with two broken ribs and frostbite turning his fingers into something out of a butcher shop.

He talks about her sometimes. The guide. Says she never once made him feel like cargo.

I thought about that a lot on the crate road, which is what we called the dozer-cut track running up the gorge floor where the old trail used to be. Halvard doesn't do trails. Trails imply you want people to come back.

* * *

It was raining sideways when they took me, which is my excuse and I'm sticking to it.

I'd left the main crew at Camp Two to run a solo line up the east bank — standard practice, one man, one instrument, one radio that worked about half the time. The gorge pinched down there into a slot of wet granite, and the crate road hugged the wall like it was afraid of the river. Good ambush country. If I'd been thinking like a guide instead of a company man, I'd have seen it coming from half a mile out.

What I saw instead was a cairn.

That's what stopped me. A small one, knee-high, built at the mouth of a side gully with the particular carelessness of someone who builds them all the time — stones fitted, not stacked. Nobody in a Halvard hard hat builds cairns. We build stakes, flags, orange plastic. Cairns are a courtesy to strangers, and Halvard does not believe in strangers.

I was still looking at it when the voice came from above and behind me, level as a spirit bubble.

"Radio on the ground. Then hands where I can see them."

I'll be honest: my first instinct was to go for the flare pistol on my hip. My second instinct was arithmetic. One of me, wet, tired, carrying forty pounds of instrument. Whoever it was had the high ground, the patience to let me walk into it, and the discipline to say *radio first*, which meant they knew the radio was the only weapon that mattered out here.

I set the radio down.

"Turn around slow."

I turned around slow.

She stood on a shelf of rock maybe eight feet up, and I knew her before my brain finished assembling the face, because Deke had a photograph — crumpled, coffee-ringed, kept in his truck visor like other men keep pictures of their kids. Hazel Nystrom. Blacklisted, according to the file I'd skimmed in the camp office, though the file spent more words explaining why her name shouldn't come up than why she'd been struck off. Files that defensive are usually lying.

"You're a long way from your permit," she said.

"I could say the same."

"You could. You'd be wrong." She came down the rock without seeming to decide to, the way water comes down. Behind her, half-hidden in the gully shadow, stood a younger man with Voss's jaw — I'd seen the grandfather's picture in the personnel annex, next to a land deed from thirty years ago that our lawyers got very quiet about. The grandson held a river pole across his body like he hadn't decided yet whether it was a tool or a club.

"Nystrom," I said. "Hazel Nystrom."

Something moved behind her eyes. People who've been blacklisted flinch when strangers know their name; she didn't flinch, but she went very still, the way you do when you hear a slope settle.

"And you're the man who's been staking my river."

"Not my choice of—"

"Don't." She said it mildly. That was the worst part. "You've been driving stakes along the east bank for six days. Orange flags every forty meters, sight lines to the glacier face. I've watched you do it. So before you tell me it's just a survey, explain to me why a survey needs a blast pattern."

The rain ticked on my hood. Somewhere below us the Serpent ran high and brown with melt, talking to itself.

I could have lied. I'd rehearsed lies; we all had. *Seismic study. Ice-safety assessment. Standard pre-season protocol.* But she'd said *blast pattern*, which meant she'd read the spacing, which meant she understood what the spacing was for better than half my own crew did, and lying to a person like that felt like lying to a mountain. Mountains always find out.

"It's not a survey," I said.

Tomas — I learned his name later — shifted forward. Hazel put out one hand without looking, a small gesture, hold.

"Then what is it?"

And here is the thing I have turned over maybe ten thousand times since. I didn't tell her because she threatened me. She hadn't, really. I told her because of the photograph in the visor, and because my brother breathes a little wrong on cold mornings now, and because some part of me had been waiting eleven days for an excuse to stop not-asking.

"The source cavern," I said. "Under the glacier snout. There's a chamber in there — big, stable, been stable since the last advance. Our charges are set to bring it down."

"Why?" Tomas asked. His voice cracked on it, young and furious.

"Because of what's in it." I looked at Hazel. "You already know, don't you. That's why you're here. Voss's map."

Neither of them confirmed it. They didn't need to.

"When the valley flooded," I said, "it wasn't the dam project that did it. Not alone. The company diverted the melt season into the basin to bury the tailings seepage — arsenic, heavy metals, the whole catalog. Voss found the spring. Found the contamination plume moving through the glacial aquifer toward three towns downstream. They paid him to keep quiet and exiled his family to make sure he stayed quiet. And now—" I heard my own voice go flat, the way it goes when I read numbers off a sheet, because flat was safer. "Now the numbers are starting to show up downstream anyway. Fish kills. Wells. Somebody filed a water-quality petition in the spring. So the plan is to collapse the cavern during calving season, when the glacier's making noise anyway, and call it a natural event. No cavern, no spring, no evidence. Case closed by geology."

The rain filled the silence.

"They're going to blame it on the calving," Hazel said quietly. Not a question.

"Calving season starts in nine days. The charges are timed to the window. Nobody will ever prove different, because there won't be anything left to test." I finally looked away from her, down at the cairn, because I couldn't keep looking at her while I said the rest. "I staked half that pattern myself."

There's a moment on every mountain when the route stops being a line on a map and becomes a fact under your boots. I'd been living on facts for twenty years — safe miles, counted switchbacks, the long arithmetic of staying alive. And now a wet company man with my dead partner's gratitude in his family somewhere was handing me another fact, and this one had a timer on it.

Nine days.

I made myself look at him properly. Mid-thirties, competent hands, the particular exhaustion of a man who has been carrying a secret the way you carry a pack that's packed wrong — doable, but it eats you. And underneath the exhaustion, something else. Recognition, yes, but more than recognition. Hesitation. He'd had chances to lie and hadn't taken them. In my experience that made him either the bravest man on the Serpent or the most dangerous, and often those were the same person on different days.

"You know my name," I said. "How."

His jaw worked. "My brother. Deke Calloway. Fourteen years ago, the Cornice Route on Mount Alder. He fell through a snow bridge on descent. Broken ribs, hypothermic, pinned against a serac." He swallowed. "The guide who got him out — she was supposed to be done for the day. Her client was already down. She went back up alone."

I remembered. God help me, I remembered everything about that day except, until this moment, his face. The boy — man — who'd cried into his own frozen sleeves and apologized for it. I'd told him crying was just the body doing its job.

"She lost her license over it, later," Renn went on. "Everyone said she cut corners. Deke says she cut nothing. Deke says she saved his life twice — once on the mountain and once after, when she testified for him so the inquiry wouldn't hang it on the client." He met my eyes. "They took her name anyway."

"They did," I said.

"So." He spread his hands, and the rain ran off them. "Ask me whatever you're going to ask."

What I was going to ask was: *why are you telling me this.* What I actually heard myself ask was worse, because it was the question I'd been avoiding since the crate road, since the flags, since the word *blast*.

"How many people are upstream of us right now?"

"Eleven at Camp Two. Six more at the portal camp past the narrows. Plus air support on standby for the shot window." He paused. "And a spotter who photographed you in Meridian. Your face went into the file the same night. They know you're on the river, Nystrom. They've known for a week."

Tomas swore beside me, low and inventive. I let him. Somebody should.

"And if they find you talking to us," I said to Renn, "what happens to you?"

"Nothing good." He said it simply, no drama in it. "But here's what happens to everybody else if I don't. Three towns drink that water. The Voss family spends another thirty years as the crazy branch of the tree. And a woman who once carried my brother down a mountain on her back gets buried in an 'accident' the same week the evidence does." He looked at the cairn again. "I've been building those little stone piles since I got here. Couldn't tell you why until about four minutes ago."

I thought about Eben Voss dying mid-sentence with his grandson's hand in his. I thought about a rope, twenty years ago, and the weight of it. I thought about the difference between loyalty and silence, and how for two decades I'd called the second one the first.

"Here's what's going to happen," I said. "You're going to walk back to Camp Two and report that you found nothing on the east bank. Old trail, no traffic, bear sign. You're going to be boring. Boring men get forgotten." I stepped closer. "And in nine days, when your bosses start their countdown, you're going to be the slowest, most careful, most obstructive blaster they have ever employed. Every reading double-checked. Every fuse tested twice. You lose them hours, Renn. Hours are lives up here."

"And you?" he asked. "What are you going to be doing?"

"Getting to the source before they do." I glanced at Tomas, whose face had gone through fury and arrived somewhere harder and cleaner. "We're going to need the layout. Perimeter schedules, charge positions, the shot-window timing. Everything you can carry out in your head."

"That's treason," Renn said. Almost smiled. "Deke will be insufferable about it."

"One condition." I held his gaze. "When this breaks — and it will break — you testify. On the record. Under your own name. I've spent twenty years watching men stay silent for good reasons. The reasons are always good. The silence is always the same."

The rain eased, the way it does all at once, like a decision. Down in the gorge the Serpent rose a note, impatient.

Renn Calloway picked up his radio, weighed it in his hand, and then — deliberately, watching me watch him — pulled the battery and dropped both pieces into the river.

"There," he said. "Radio malfunction. Terrible luck. Cost the company a whole afternoon." He shouldered his pack and turned uphill toward Camp Two, then stopped. "Nystrom. The shot window — they moved it up. It's not nine days anymore. Weather's accelerating the melt. As of this morning's briefing—"

He looked back at me, and I watched a man hand over the last piece of himself that belonged to Halvard Extraction.

"—it's five."

Five days. Five days to run a gorge, beat a perimeter, and reach a cathedral of poisoned ice before the company turned it into rubble and called it weather.

Tomas was already tightening his pack straps.

"Then we'd better stop standing around getting rained on," he said, and pointed upstream, where the river narrowed into dark, and the map in my jacket seemed suddenly to weigh what it had cost.
