## 13. Loyalty's Arithmetic

My grandfather died owing debts, and I had spent the whole trip upstream assuming I was one of them. Turns out I was an asset. There's a difference, and the difference has a weight, and that weight was currently sitting in my pack in the form of a waterproof map tube with Renn's detonator plans rolled inside it.

We'd taken them off him at dawn — not "taken" exactly; Renn had pressed them into Hazel's hands like a man passing a hot coal he was tired of holding, along with a schematic of the blast perimeter and a schedule written in pencil on the back of a ration wrapper. *Charges set by Thursday. Detonation window opens with calving. Nobody will question it.* Then he'd walked back down toward the crate road whistling, which is either the bravest or the most broken thing I've ever watched a man do.

Now we sat on a granite shelf above the gorge, the Serpent roaring green and furious below us, and I did the arithmetic out loud because Hazel Nystrom only respects numbers.

"Two days," I said. "Maybe two and a half if the pass is soft. The signal town at Meridian Fork. There's a wire office there, a newspaper stringer who files from the mining camps, and a train. We take Renn's plans to the press, and Halvard can't seal anything, because sealing things requires nobody knowing, and newspapers are very good at knowing."

Hazel didn't look up from the map. She has this way of reading terrain like other people read letters from lawyers — carefully, expecting bad news, underlining nothing.

"Tomas."

"One word. That's not even an argument. That's a syllable with opinions."

She looked up then. Her face did the thing it does where all the weather goes behind glass. "The promise comes first."

"The promise is to a dead man about a piece of paper." I heard myself say it and flinched, because two weeks ago I'd have given a month of my life to hear someone call Voss's map a piece of paper, and now here I was defending it against the woman who'd sworn to carry it. Grief rearranges your furniture. You wake up and the chair you hated is the one you sit in.

"The promise is to a dying man," she said, "which is worse, because you can't argue with him anymore. And the paper isn't a piece of paper. It's a valley."

"It's evidence." I pulled the tube out of my pack and held it between us like a verdict. "It's contamination ledgers and water samples and photographs of crews that flooded a valley on purpose, and every hour we spend walking upstream is an hour Halvard spends setting charges in the dark. If we go to the source first and something happens — a storm, a patrol, a wrong step on the icefall — the drum stays buried under a glacier and the story dies with it. Again. That's how they won last time, Hazel. They just waited for the mountain to eat the paperwork."

"That's how they'll win this time too, if we're not standing there when it matters."

"Standing there doing what? Admiring it?"

"Delivering it." She said it quietly, which is how you know Hazel is angry. Loud anger is a luxury she doesn't allow herself. "Voss didn't ask me to start a newspaper war. He asked me to carry his map to his family's valley. That's the whole job. Everything else — the press, the boards, the reckoning — that comes after, and it comes after because after is the only place it can come from. You don't get to skip to the ending. I've tried. The ending skips you back."

I stood up. My knees cracked, twenty-six years old and already sounding like my grandfather. "Two days. That's all I'm asking. Two days to make sure none of this was for nothing."

"And if the calving starts early? If Halvard fires those charges while we're filing copy in Meridian Fork?" She finally closed the map. "You want to do the arithmetic? Fine. Here's mine. Two days to the signal town. Two days back, if the weather holds, which it won't, because it's the Serpent and it never holds. Four days round trip against a detonation window that opens with the calving season, which the ice has been telling us for three days is early. You can hear it at night. Don't tell me you haven't heard it."

I had heard it. Deep under everything, like a door being tested in its frame. I'd been telling myself it was the river.

"So we lose," I said. "That's your math. We walk four days, the valley gets blown, and we arrive in time to watch the dust settle on the second-worst thing this company ever buried."

"No. We walk two days upstream, we reach the source before the charges, and we take the drum out ourselves. Evidence in hand beats evidence rumored. Then we go to the press with the actual documents instead of a dead man's margins and a prospector's schedule scrawled on a ration wrapper." She paused, and something moved behind the glass. "Tomas. I know what you're really asking."

"Do you." It wasn't a question. It was a wall.

"You're asking me to choose the world over the promise. And I understand why. God help me, I do. But I've spent twenty years choosing the smart move, the safe mile, the version of events that keeps everybody breathing, and here's what it bought me: a blacklist, a cairn, and a life measured in distances instead of days. I am done making trades where I'm the only one who pays." She got up too, and we were close enough that I could see the windburn crack her lower lip. "Eben Voss traded me a map for a promise on the worst night of his life, and I said yes, and I have been saying yes ever since, every switchback, every cold camp, every mile. You don't get to renegotiate my oath for me. Not even for a good reason. Especially not for a good reason. Good reasons are how mountains kill people. They convince you the shortcut is sound."

"This isn't a shortcut. This is the long way around to actually winning."

"Then win it after." Her voice didn't rise but it sharpened, the way ice does. "After the drum is out. After the promise is kept. After I stand in that valley and put his map down on the ground his family was exiled from. Then burn Halvard to the ground with my blessing. I'll hold the match."

"And if there is no after? If the calving eats the window?"

"Then I keep the promise anyway, and I live with it, because that's what a promise is. It's not a contract with good exit clauses. It's the thing you do when doing it costs more than not doing it."

I laughed. It came out ugly. "That's not loyalty, that's stubbornness wearing loyalty's coat."

"Yes," she said simply. "It usually is. The coat fits most of us badly and we wear it anyway."

* * *

We broke camp without speaking, which in Hazel's language is a paragraph and in mine is a scream. The gorge narrowed as we climbed, the Serpent compressing itself into white fury between walls the color of old bone, and we moved through it in single file with ten feet of silence stretched between us like rope.

I kept doing the arithmetic. I couldn't stop. It's the family disease — Voss counted ounces of ore, my mother counted years of exile, and I count hours until deadlines, and all of us pretending the counting is a virtue instead of a way of not feeling anything. Two days to Meridian Fork. A wire to the capital papers. A stringer named Okafor who'd filed three stories on Halvard's water rights and gotten sued twice, which meant he was either corrupt or brave, and the lawsuits argued for brave. Two days. One roll of film. One chance to make the story too big to bury.

Against that: the deep testing knock of the glacier at night. The dynamite crates at the gorge mouth. Renn's pencil schedule, *Thursday*, and today was Tuesday, and Tuesday was burning down into Wednesday while I walked away from the only wire office within a hundred miles.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about loving someone: it makes their judgment look like a choice about you. Every mile Hazel refused to detour felt like a mile she refused me. I knew that was unfair. Knowing it changed nothing. The unfair thing sat in my chest and did its own arithmetic, and its sums always came out the same: she trusts a dead man's promise more than a living man's plan.

At midday we stopped at a seep where the water ran orange with iron, and Hazel knelt and filled a sample vial without being asked, because she's been sampling everything since chapter whatever-this-is, building her own little archive of the crime. I watched her label it in her precise surveyor's hand — *Serpent headwaters, iron seep, date* — and something in me went quiet and cold.

"You're collecting evidence," I said.

She capped the vial. "Always."

"Then collect it faster. Or admit that's what the detour is for. We could gather for two days and still file. Okafor would wait for a package with this much in it—"

"He'd wait, and Halvard's spotters would find the package, or find us, or find the drum, and we'd have traded the one thing Voss asked for for a maybe." She stood, pocketed the vial. "I've seen what happens to maybes on this river."

"What happened to you?" It came out before I could stop it, and once it was out I couldn't take it back, so I made it worse. "Twenty years ago. Whatever they bought your silence with. Is that what this is? Is the promise just the shape your own deal takes now?"

The silence afterward lasted longer than the seep ran. Somewhere below us the river hit a choke point and roared like something wounded.

"You don't want to know what happened to me," she said at last, evenly. "And you especially don't want to know it during a fight, because you'll use it, and I'll let you, and then neither of us will be able to take it back. Ask me on a good day. Assuming we get one."

"There are no good days on the Serpent."

"No," she agreed. "There are only days you survive. Pick your battles accordingly."

* * *

We made the high bench by late afternoon, the place where the gorge opened just enough to show us the next day's route: a shelf of gray ice angling up toward the saddle below the source, blue-white and beautiful and absolutely riddled with fresh fracture lines. Even from a mile away I could see them — the glacier's handwriting, getting larger, less patient.

Hazel studied it through her binoculars for a long time. When she lowered them her face had gone careful again, all weather behind glass, but the glass was fogging.

"We cross at first light," she said. "Before the sun softens the bridges. Six hours to the source if the ice holds."

"If."

"If." She handed me the binoculars. "Look at the left lateral. Tell me what you see."

I looked. Fractures, crevasse shadows, the whole rotten architecture of a glacier getting ready to shed its skin. And near the left lateral, small as punctuation marks, a line of canvas and crates and the glint of a floodlamp rig not yet lit. Halvard's forward cache. They weren't waiting for calving season. They were already there.

"They're on the ice," I said.

"They're on the ice," she confirmed. "Which means our window isn't Thursday. Our window is whenever they decide the weather gives them cover. Which means the arithmetic you keep doing—" she turned and looked at me straight on, and for once there was no glass at all, just tired, stubborn, honest fire "—is running the wrong direction. Every hour we spend going sideways is an hour they spend wiring. I know you think I'm choosing a dead man over you. I'm not. I'm choosing the only route where both of us get to be right. Drum first. Press second. Both, Tomas. I'm promising you both."

"But you're sequencing it."

"I'm sequencing it."

"And if the sequence breaks?"

"Then it breaks, and we live in the wreckage, and we keep going, because that's the whole skill. That's mountaineering. That's grief. That's everything." She shouldered her pack. "I need you tomorrow. On that ice I need your eyes and your stubbornness pointed uphill, not at me. Can you give me that?"

And here is the truth I've been circling since the saloon, since the deathbed, since the flash flood taught me that Hazel Nystrom ties a knot like she means it: I couldn't. Not because she was wrong — God, maybe she wasn't even wrong, maybe the promise-first route is the only one that works — but because I am my grandfather's grandson, and the Voss inheritance turns out to be this: we love in absolutes, and absolutes don't sequence.

"I can't," I said. "Not tomorrow. Maybe not ever, if we do it your way. So I'm going to do it mine."

Her jaw tightened. "Tomas—"

"Meridian Fork. Two days. I'll file everything — Renn's plans, the photos, the margins, the seep samples if you give them to me. Then I'll come back up the gorge and meet you at the source, or meet your cairn, whichever the mountain leaves me." I held out my hand for the map tube, and after a long moment — a moment I will be unpacking for the rest of my life — she gave it to me. All of it. The plans, the schedule, the whole combustible inheritance.

"You'll die on that road," she said quietly. "Renn's people will be watching it. They traced Voss from a visitor log; they'll trace a lone man on a supply trail in half a day."

"Then I'll be fast."

"That's not a plan."

"It's the only kind of plan I've got left." I cinched the tube to my pack, top and bottom, the way she'd taught me, and hated that my hands remembered her lessons so well. "You keep the promise. I'll keep the world. Between us we might cover it."

She looked at me for a long time across ten feet of granite and twenty years of her own ghosts, and I watched her decide — deliberately, expensively — not to argue. "The Kestrel Traverse cuts two days off your return," she said finally. "If you come back. Stay left of the seracs and don't trust any bridge wider than your boot."

"You're giving me route advice while we break up."

"We're not breaking up." Something crossed her face, fast, gone. "We're splitting the work. There's a difference. Ask me on a good day."

I walked. Down the bench, west toward the low country, toward the wire office and the stringer and the train, the tube riding my spine like a second backbone. At the edge of the bench I stopped and looked back once. She was already climbing, small and steady against the blue-white wall, moving toward the source alone, and the sight of it did something to me that no argument had managed: it scared me. Not for her. For the arithmetic. Because I suddenly understood that if the glacier spoke before I got back, the last thing I'd have said to Hazel Nystrom was *I can't*.

Behind me, faint under the wind, the ice knocked twice — patient, testing, like a door.

I turned west and started to run.
