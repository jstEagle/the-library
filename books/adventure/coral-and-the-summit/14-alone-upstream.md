## 14. Alone Upstream

The gorge took Tomas's absence personally. That's how it felt, anyway — every echo came back wrong, one voice short of a conversation. I'd spent twenty years perfecting the art of being alone in loud places, and I'd forgotten how much noise a second person makes even when he's sulking: the scrape of his boots, his breathing, the way he narrated his own resentment under his breath like a man reading scripture he disagreed with.

Now there was just me and the river, and the river had opinions.

I made good time. That was the problem with good time — it always felt like a lie told by someone about to be caught. The Serpent narrowed through the gorge the way Voss's map promised, walls closing to a stone's throw, water running green-black and fast over polished rock. I moved the way you move when you're trying to outrun something that isn't behind you. I counted switchbacks out of habit and then stopped, because counting was for people measuring their lives in safe miles, and safe miles were not what this was.

This was the kind of ground where you don't get to be careful. You just get to be lucky.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about climbing alone after someone dies on you: your body remembers the arithmetic. Every decision gets run twice. *Is this hold good? Is this hold good enough for two?* Except there's no two anymore, so the question curdles into *is this hold good enough to die on alone*, which is a different question entirely, and worse.

The last time I climbed solo — properly solo, no client, no rope team, just me and my own judgment — Wren was still alive. That's the part I can't make peace with. People assume the haunting starts after the avalanche. It doesn't. It started three days before it, when she asked if I wanted company on the descent and I said I'd rather go light and fast. I'd rather go light and fast. Six words. I've turned them over so many times they've worn smooth as river stones, and they still cut.

So when the gorge pinched down at midday into a section where the only line was a wet ledge above the water, I stood there longer than the ledge deserved. Long enough for a dipper — one of those fat little gray birds that walks underwater like it's nothing — to land on a rock, look at me, and leave. Even the birds had somewhere better to be.

I crossed it fine. Of course I crossed it fine. My hands didn't shake until afterward, when there was nothing to shake about, which is the body's cruelest joke: it saves its fear for the quiet parts.

I ate standing up. I didn't build a cairn. There was no one to leave one for who wasn't already carrying theirs.

* * *

By late afternoon the walls opened just enough to show me what I'd been refusing to look at all day: the crate road. Halvard's crate road — a rough track blasted along the eastern bank, stacked with fresh dynamite boxes under tarps, spools of det cord coiled like sleeping snakes. Renn's intel, before he'd walked off into custody or whatever passed for it in a company that didn't believe in consequences. Collapse the source cavern. Blame the calving. Erase forty years of drowned valley in one very photogenic act of geology.

And between me and the source: the perimeter. Floodlamps, motion sensors, at least four men Renn had counted, probably more now that their surveyor had gone missing. A blast perimeter is a strange thing to sneak through. It's not built to keep people out, exactly. It's built so that anything inside it when the charges fire becomes an unsolved problem instead of a witness.

I could go around. The map showed a high route, a shoulder of rotten rock above the north wall — two days, exposed, and in my current condition "exposed" meant "honest about how tired I am," which is the most dangerous condition there is. Or I could go through, tonight, fast and low, and bet everything on Halvard's men being bored.

I sat on a boulder and did the math the way I do all math now, twice, expecting both answers to be bad.

That's when I heard the whistle.

Three notes, descending. Not a bird. Birds don't whistle in minor keys.

I had the ice axe off my pack before my brain finished the thought, which tells you where my head was at. He stepped out from behind a boulder with his hands up and open — Renn, the surveyor, the man whose brother I'd pulled off Mount Ceres in a whiteout eleven years ago. Out of uniform. No radio clipped to his shoulder, no company jacket, just a wool shirt and a face that looked like it hadn't slept since he'd watched me walk away with his detonator plans.

"You're supposed to be answering questions somewhere," I said.

"I am. Just not theirs." He lowered his hands slowly, the way you move around a spooked animal, which was generous of him given that I was the one holding the axe. "They let me go this morning. Walked me to the trailhead and told me to forget what I saw in the gorge." He almost smiled. "I'm bad at forgetting. Occupational hazard of surveying — you spend your whole career writing down exactly where things are."

"So you quit."

"So I'm off the clock." He said it carefully, like the distinction mattered more than it sounded. "There's a difference between quitting and being off the clock. Quitting means I decided. Off the clock means right now, this hour, I'm just a guy in the mountains who happens to know where every sensor on that perimeter is buried."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow I might be a guy who testifies. Or a guy who disappears. Haven't done the math yet." He looked past me, up the gorge, toward the source neither of us could see but both of us could feel, the way you feel weather coming. "You're going through the perimeter tonight."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it.

"You'll trip the east sensors," he said. "They repositioned after we lost the plans. They're expecting you low and slow, near the water. Which means—" he crouched, drew in the gravel with a stick, and I hated how much my body leaned in, hungry for exactly this, "—you go high. Along the old haul line. It's decommissioned, but the lamps don't cover it because Halvard's crew never walks anywhere they can't drive."

"Why are you telling me this?"

He kept drawing. "Because my brother would want the math to come out even."

"That's not math. That's debt."

"Same thing, if you carry it long enough." He stood, brushed the gravel off his knees, and looked at me straight. "I'm coming with you as far as the perimeter. Not into it — if they catch two of us, that's a conspiracy, and conspiracies get buried deeper than evidence. But I can walk you to the edge of the dark and tell you where the dark ends."

I should have said no. Every instinct I'd built in twenty years of surviving my own decisions said no. You don't take gifts from men who work for the people trying to bury you; you don't hand your route to anyone whose loyalty has a shift schedule.

But Tomas was a day behind me and a letter away from maybe turning around, and the calving cracks had started — I'd heard the first one at dawn, a deep groan up-valley like the mountain clearing its throat — and grace, I've learned, rarely arrives wearing a trustworthy face. It arrives wearing whatever face is standing next to you when you need one.

"One condition," I said.

"Name it."

"When this is done, you testify. On the record. Your name attached."

He flinched. Actually flinched, a small involuntary thing, and I understood it — I'd spent two decades watching my own name get taken apart, so I knew exactly what I was asking him to lend out.

"My brother's name is on the safety report from Ceres," he said quietly. "Did you know that? He wrote it. Said the fixed lines were rotten. Nobody read it until you pulled him off the mountain and made them read it." He picked up his stick, put it down again. "One name on a page. That's all it ever takes. Fine. On the record."

* * *

We walked the last mile in silence, which suited us both. Renn moved well for a man who lived in trucks and trailers — sure-footed, quiet, with the particular humility of someone who knows the mountains professionally but loves them privately and keeps the two separated like church and state.

At the base of the north wall he stopped and pointed with his chin. "Haul line. Switchbacks at the scree, then a bench cut into the wall. Stay on the bench forty minutes, don't trust the third cable anchor — it's decorative. The perimeter fence ends at a cairn with red paint. Past that, you're outside their map." He paused. "Their old map. The new one they're keeping off the books."

"The one Voss drew."

"The one Voss drew." He looked at me then, really looked, the way you look at someone you've decided something about. "He came into our camp once. Months ago. Before any of this. Asked to see our survey permits, polite as anything, and then he stood there reading them and I swear the man was grieving. Like he was reading his own obituary written in somebody else's handwriting." He shook his head. "I didn't understand it then."

"And now?"

"Now I think some people spend their whole lives walking toward a place they got paid to betray." He stuck out his hand. I took it. His grip was dry and steady and human, and I held on maybe half a second longer than a handshake requires, because some debts you settle in contact.

"For nothing," he said. "Remember that. If anyone asks — and someone will ask — I guided you for nothing, and I'd do it again, and that's the whole of my testimony."

"It's a good line."

"It's the only honest one I've got left." He turned back down the trail, then stopped, not looking at me. "Nystrom. The third cable anchor. I mean it. Everything else on that bench will hold your weight and your ghosts. That one won't hold either."

Then he was gone, down into the dusk, a man walking toward his own reckoning at a pace that suggested he'd finally stopped dreading it.

* * *

The bench was exactly where he said, cut into the wall by hands long gone, wide enough for one tired woman and all her arithmetic. The gorge dropped away green and roaring on my left. Above me the first stars came out, indifferent as always, and ahead — maybe two hours, maybe less — the floodlamps of the perimeter threw their pale cones across the water like searchlights looking for a crime that hadn't happened yet.

Forty minutes, Renn had said. I checked my watch at thirty-five and kept moving, because the watch was mine and the mountains have never once respected it.

At minute fifty-one I reached the third cable anchor.

It was rusted through at the base, a brown bloom spreading into the rock like a stain, and I stood there in the dark with my hand hovering over it and thought about Wren, about light and fast, about six words worn smooth, and I did the thing I have spent twenty years learning to do, the thing that is harder than any pitch I've ever climbed:

I went around.

A long, ugly, unglamorous detour, up and over loose rock with my heart slamming, arriving back on the bench ten minutes later with scraped palms and no dignity and both feet still attached to my body. Progress, of a kind. The kind they don't put on permits.

And then, below me — far below, at the mouth of the gorge where the crate road began — a light blinked twice. Paused. Blinked twice again.

Not a floodlamp pattern. Floodlamps don't count.

Somebody down there was signaling. And unless Halvard had started hiring people who counted in twos, it could only mean one thing: Tomas had come back.

Or it was a trap, and I was about to find out which, at the edge of the dark, with the glacier groaning up-valley like a door being forced open.
