## 16. The Perimeter

Renn found me at the mouth of the gorge an hour after full dark, and I'll be honest — part of me had hoped he wouldn't.

Not because I didn't want the help. Because a man who shows up twice is either a fool or a friend, and I'd stopped trusting myself to tell the difference somewhere around mile four hundred of my life. He came out of the shadow of the rock wall without a headlamp, which told me he'd been moving in Halvard's territory long enough to learn its habits, and he was carrying two coils of rope, a battered thermos, and the expression of a man who had already lost the argument with himself.

"You're off shift," I said. Not a question. His coveralls were gone, replaced by the kind of nondescript wool that mountain people wear when they don't want to be anyone in particular.

"Off the clock. Off the payroll, if this goes wrong." He set the thermos between us like a peace offering or a bribe. "Coffee's terrible. Drink it anyway."

I drank it anyway. It was terrible. It was also hot, and there is a category of gift — small, warm, badly made — that hits harder than anything expensive ever will.

"Walk me through it," I said.

He crouched and drew in the dirt with a stick, quick and sure, the way men draw when they've drawn the same thing fifty times for crews who weren't listening. "The perimeter runs here to here. Two towers, floodlamps on a sweep cycle — ninety seconds dark, forty seconds lit, staggered so there's no true blind spot on the flat ground. That's the trick. They designed it for the flat ground." The stick tapped a jagged line above the perimeter. "Nobody designs for the icefall."

"The icefall's a death trap."

"The icefall's a *schedule*," Renn said. "The lamps can't reach it. The search teams won't enter it after dark because last spring a man from crew three went into a crevasse and it took them nine hours to get him out, and the site supervisor hates overtime more than he hates trespassers. You go up through the icefall at night, you come down behind the source ridge, and you're inside their perimeter before they've finished their morning coffee."

"And you?"

"I'm the boring part." He straightened, brushed the dirt off his knees. "I walk the flat ground. Slow. Visible. A surveyor who's had too much coffee and lost his radio, wandering around inside his own perimeter looking embarrassed. Every lamp, every pair of eyes, every man with cold hands and a thermos of his own — all of them looking at me."

"And while they're looking at you—"

"You're a rumor on the ice." He looked at me then, and in the starlight his face was younger than it had any right to be, given what he was proposing. "You said you ran the Kestrel Traverse. That you crossed it clean."

"I did."

"Then you can run an icefall in the dark. It's the same grammar. Different punctuation."

It wasn't, quite, and we both knew it. But I'd spent twenty years learning that courage isn't the absence of the lie; it's agreeing to say it out loud anyway.

"One condition," I said.

"Name it."

"When this is done — whatever happens — you testify. The drum, the ledgers, the water samples. None of it means anything from my mouth. It means everything from yours."

Renn was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere up-valley, a generator changed pitch, and the first sweep of a floodlamp painted the canyon wall across from us a pale, dead white, then let it go.

"My brother," he said finally, "climbed with you. Before."

"He climbed with everyone. He was friendly that way."

"He told me once that mountains keep honest books." He picked up the stick again, turned it over in his fingers. "That every debt gets written down eventually, and the only question is whether you're holding the pen when it does." He tucked the stick behind his ear like a carpenter's pencil, absurd and practical at once. "My brother would want the math to come out even. So would I."

We shook on it. His hand was cold and steady as rebar.

* * *

We moved at 2 a.m., when the generators hummed lowest and the night was at its flattest, darkest hour — the hour I have always thought of as the world holding its breath.

The plan worked exactly as plans do, which is to say it worked until it didn't.

The approach went clean. Renn peeled off at the split in the trail with a nod that managed to be both casual and final, and I watched his shape amble down toward the compound lights, hands in pockets, radiating the specific innocence of a man looking for a lost radio. Within two minutes the nearest tower swung its beam toward him. Within five, a voice crackled over a radio I could hear faintly on the wind — annoyed, bored, human. All of it pointed at him. None of it pointed at me.

I went up.

The icefall at night is not a place. It's a negotiation. The glacier moves all day, every day, shrugging tons of ice downslope by fractions of inches, and by nightfall nothing is where the daylight left it. Seracs lean at angles that were merely suspicious at noon. Crevasse lips crumble under snow bridges that were load-bearing that morning. You move by sound as much as sight — the hollow knock of a bridge that will hold, the wet sigh of one that won't — and you move slowly, and you do not think about the fact that the whole structure is a machine built for falling down.

I had a headlamp with a red filter, which showed me just enough ice to distrust it properly. I had Renn's route notes, transcribed in blocky engineer's handwriting onto waterproof paper: *left of the leaning block, stay off the white bridge, count eleven paces past the blue serac, DO NOT trust the ramp.* He'd drawn it from memory of daytime surveys, and every line of it carried the quiet apology of a man describing a room he'd only seen in photographs.

Left of the leaning block. I counted. Eleven paces past the blue serac. The ramp groaned under my boot and I stepped off it fast, heart slamming, and stood on solid ice listening to my own breath come back at me in clouds.

So far, so good. So far, exactly as promised.

I was two-thirds through — maybe forty minutes from the high shoulder where the icefall gave way to the source ridge — when the world turned white.

Not gradually. Not the slow bloom of dawn. One instant the glacier was a cathedral of shadows, and the next it was lit like an operating theater, every crevasse suddenly visible and every shadow suddenly a place where something could be hiding, including me.

A floodlamp. Mounted somewhere I hadn't scouted, on the ridge itself — Renn couldn't have known; it wasn't in his survey. Someone had decided the icefall needed watching after all, and tonight of all nights, someone had decided to look.

I dropped flat against the ice, cheek to the surface, and did the only thing there was to do: nothing. Below and behind me, I could hear the compound waking up. Doors. Voices. The particular rhythm of boots on gravel that means someone has spotted something and wants confirmation before they commit.

The lamp swept. Held. Swept back.

And there, down on the flat ground, a light came on — a headlamp, bobbing, unmistakable, a man walking openly across open ground toward the compound gate with his hands raised in cheerful surrender.

Renn.

He was supposed to be caught near the fence, plausibly, deniably, a wanderer. Instead he walked straight down the middle of everything, lamp high, and I heard his voice carry across the cold air, easy and loud: "Yeah, yeah, it's me! Lost my radio, fell in a creek, somebody get me a coffee before I file a complaint!" And the floodlamp on the ridge — the one pinning me to the ice like a specimen — hesitated. Swung. Followed the easier story down to the gate, because that's what lamps do, that's what eyes do, that's what people do: they look at the man waving instead of the shadow holding still.

I ran.

Not carefully. Not with the grammar and the punctuation and the counted paces. I ran the way I hadn't run since the avalanche — flat-out, arms pumping, across bridges that cracked and held, around seracs that hummed in the sudden temperature drop, up the shoulder of the ridge with my lungs full of knives. Behind me the voices at the gate resolved into laughter — they knew him, some of them, the way crews know each other — and then into something else, sharper, as somebody asked the question that would unravel it: *what was he doing on the south side?*

I didn't wait to hear the answer. I topped the shoulder, dropped below the ridgeline out of the light, and knelt on the ice with my hands shaking so hard I had to press them flat against the glacier to make them stop.

Below me, the compound noise changed key. Not laughter anymore. Radios. Whistles. The organized alarm of men who have realized the decoy was a decoy.

They'd take him. They'd question him. He knew it when he walked out there, and he walked out there anyway, and the last thing I'd heard him say was a joke about coffee, and I would carry that joke for the rest of my life alongside everything else I carried, and it would weigh almost nothing and everything at once.

*My brother would want the math to come out even.*

I got up. There was nothing else the night required of me except that: getting up, and going on, and making his arithmetic true.

The source ridge lay ahead, black against the stars, and beyond it — if Voss's map told the truth, if the old man's dying obsession had been a compass and not a curse — the cathedral of blue ice, and the drum, and the truth, and the calving season's first cracks already talking to each other somewhere deep in the dark.

Behind me, down in the valley, a vehicle engine started.

Ahead of me, the glacier groaned — long, low, patient, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

I ran.
