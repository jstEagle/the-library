## 17. The Source

The gorge stopped pretending to be a canyon and became a throat.

I'd been climbing since before dawn, following the Serpent as it narrowed from a river to a rumor, the walls closing in until I could touch both sides of the water with my elbows. Renn had left me at the blast perimeter's edge with a hand-drawn sketch of the sensor lines and a look on his face like a man paying off a debt he didn't owe me. "The icefall starts where the blue begins," he'd said. "You'll know it when you see it. Everyone does."

He was right. You do know it.

The blue began an hour past the last dynamite crate — Halvard's crates, stacked in neat military rows at the valley mouth like a company that believed in tidiness even in vandalism. One moment the ice was the ordinary white-gray of high glacier, dirty with scree and old snowfall. The next it was blue. Not sky-blue, not lake-blue. A blue that seemed lit from inside, the way deep water is lit, the way a bruise is lit. The whole face of the glacier above the gorge glowed with it, hundreds of feet of compressed ancient light, and threading through it, veins of even deeper blue where meltwater had carved channels over centuries and then frozen shut again.

I stood there longer than I should have. I'll admit that. Twenty years of measuring my life in safe miles, and here was a place that made miles feel like the wrong unit entirely. Some things you measure in breaths.

Then I remembered what Renn had said about the charges, and started moving again.

* * *

The climb up to the source was the kind of climbing I used to do without thinking, back when thinking was the dangerous part. Steep ice, forty degrees, maybe fifty in the steeper chimneys. My tools bit clean. The cold was the honest kind — dry, stable, no wind to speak of. A day made for this work, if any day could be trusted.

I didn't trust it. I'd stopped trusting beautiful days twenty years ago, on a mountain whose name I still couldn't say out loud without tasting copper. Beautiful days were how mountains lied to you. They got you relaxed. They got you feeling competent. And then they introduced you to their friends.

So I climbed ugly — deliberate, checking every placement twice, counting my protection points like a miser counts coins. It cost me time. Time was the one thing I didn't have, because somewhere below me, men were wiring a cavern full of evidence with explosives and calling it geology.

Twice I heard engines. Once, distantly, a shout — two voices, maybe three, carried up the gorge on that treacherous still air. Halvard's crews, working the lower perimeter. Renn's diversion would buy me hours, not days. I climbed faster and hated myself for it, because fast is what killed Wren.

Wren. There. I said her name in my own head for the first time in chapters of silence. Not *my partner*. Not *the person who died*. Wren Calloway, who laughed at bad jokes and tied a bowline wrong her entire career and refused to learn right because she thought it was funny that it held anyway.

I cut the rope. That was the truth I'd finally said out loud in a storm camp above treeline, to a boy who didn't flinch, and saying it hadn't killed me, which had been the fear all along — that confession was a structural failure, that if I admitted what I'd done the whole edifice of Hazel Nystrom, Competent Professional, would come down. It hadn't come down. It had, if anything, gotten lighter. Guilt you carry secretly weighs more than guilt you carry openly. Nobody tells you that. Everybody should.

I cut the rope because the choice was both of us or one of us, and my hands chose before my heart finished voting. The company knew. The company had always known — the incident report, the sealed testimony, the blacklist that wasn't punishment but purchase. My silence had a price and Halvard Extraction paid it, and I'd spent twenty years telling myself I'd been punished when really I'd been *bought*, which is worse, because punishment ends.

All of that went through my head on the ice, pitch by pitch, the way it does — grief doesn't wait for camp, it climbs with you, it takes a turn at the tools.

* * *

Near noon I came around a shoulder of blue ice and stopped breathing.

Not figuratively. Actually stopped — the air went out of me and forgot to come back, because ahead, at the head of the gorge, the glacier opened into a chamber the size of a cathedral, and I mean that with all the irreverence of a woman who hasn't been inside a church since her grandmother died. Vaulted walls of translucent ice fifty feet high. Light entering through a crevasse far above and scattering through the blue until the whole space glowed like the inside of a lamp. And at the center of the floor, fed by some artery deep in the mountain, a spring — welling up clear and then running *wrong*, running orange-stained, iron-bright, leaving mineral rust down the channel it had cut toward the Serpent's headwaters.

Poison, dressed as beauty. The spring was gorgeous. That was the obscenity of it. The water came up glowing amber against the blue ice, and if you didn't know, you'd think it was holy.

This was what they flooded the valley for. This was what Eben Voss found, and kept quiet about, and carried alone for thirty years until his body gave out in a roadside saloon and he pressed a grease-pencil map into the hands of a stranger because strangers were all he had left.

"Okay, old man," I said aloud. My voice went up into the vaulting and came back small. "Show me."

The map put the cache behind the spring, in a recess where two ice walls met — Voss had drawn it as a triangle with a dot, and in the margins, in handwriting gone shaky but determined: *steel drum, green, painted over once, still green underneath.*

It took me forty minutes to find it. The drum was buried under a fall of newer ice, half-melted and refrozen, and I dug it out with the care of an archaeologist and the urgency of a thief, which is roughly what I was. Green paint under gray paint, just like he said. The lid was crusted with frost but the seal held. When I broke it free, the sound rang through the cathedral like a bell.

Inside:

Water samples first — dozens of glass vials packed in straw, each labeled in Voss's blocky hand with dates going back three decades. *Upper Serpent, spring vent, iron content visible.* The earliest ones were dated years before the flood. He'd been sampling before the valley drowned. He'd known what was coming up out of the ground and he'd documented it while the company paid him to forget it.

Ledgers next. Payments, coded but legible once you saw the pattern — monthly sums from a Halvard precursor firm, and beside them, in the margins, Voss's own arithmetic: what the payments totaled against what the contamination cleanup would have cost. He'd done the math. The math said the company chose poison over responsibility, and drowned a valley to hide the receipt.

And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, photographs. Black-and-white, curling with age. Flooding crews at the dam site. Detonation sequences. And one image I had to set down and pick up again because my hands were shaking: a survey team posed at the valley mouth, and standing third from the left, young and whole and already bought — a man whose name appeared on my blacklist paperwork, whose signature had ended my career before it properly began.

Eben Voss had photographed his own sinners. Including himself — the last photo was him, younger than Tomas was now, holding a sample vial up to the light with an expression I recognized because I'd seen it in my own mirror for twenty years.

*They pay you to be silent,* the expression said, *and then you pay forever.*

* * *

I was repacking the drum when I heard it.

A crack. Not the small talk of settling ice — I know that language, the ticks and pops of thermal shift, the glacier clearing its throat. This was deeper. A groan that started below the floor of the chamber and rolled up through the walls, and the blue light flickered as something shifted far inside the ice.

Calving season. The first word of it, spoken in the only language glaciers use.

I checked my watch and did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do. The calving window Renn described, the schedule Halvard was racing — we weren't early. We weren't even on time. We were *last*. The charges at the valley mouth were meant to bring the source cavern down and dress it up as natural collapse, and nature was about to hand them the perfect alibi by collapsing things on its own schedule. Everything Voss had hidden here — thirty years of samples, ledgers, photographs, the whole anatomy of a crime — sat in a room with a demolition date that had just moved up.

Another crack, longer this time. Somewhere above, a serac the size of a house adjusted its opinion of gravity.

I got the drum closed. Lashed it into my pack frame, which turned sixty pounds of steel and truth into sixty pounds of steel and truth I now had to get down an icefall while a glacier came apart. The descent routes I'd noted on the way up suddenly looked optimistic. The gorge below looked narrow. The air, which had been so honest all morning, had gone heavy and expectant, the way it does before mountains stop lying and start acting.

I took one last look at the spring, still welling its beautiful poison into the blue light, and made it a promise in the family tradition I'd apparently inherited from a dying prospector: *you're coming out of here. All of you. Even the parts that smell like rust.*

Then the glacier spoke again — a triple crack, rapid, conversational, like something counting down — and far above, at the mouth of the vaulted chamber, a seam of white opened across the blue.

And out of the shadow at the chamber's edge, half-frozen, staggering, impossibly, gloriously alive, came Tomas.
