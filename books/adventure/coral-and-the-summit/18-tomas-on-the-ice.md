## 18. Tomas on the Ice

I want it on the record that I did not plan to cross an icefall at dawn with no rope, no partner, and a grandfather's letter crumpled in my jacket like a confession of my own. Planning is for people whose grandfathers die quietly and leave them watches.

What I had instead was forty-one hours without sleep, a thermos of slush that had once been coffee, and the letter.

I'd read it eleven times since turning back. That's not an exaggeration; I counted, because counting was the only thing keeping me from thinking about what I was walking toward. The first time, I read it standing in the middle of the signal town road with my pack half-unpacked, watching the bus that would have carried me away from all of this pull out in a cloud of diesel and good sense. The eleventh time, I read it by headlamp in a drainage culvert while Halvard's search teams swept the valley mouth with floodlamps, looking for a woman who wasn't there anymore — because she was already upstream, alone, doing exactly what I'd told her was suicide.

The letter said, in part: *I did not stay silent because I was ashamed. I stayed silent because Halvard knew where my daughter lived, and what her husband earned, and how easy it is for a bridge to fail.*

My mother. The bridge line was about my father. I sat in that culvert and understood, with the slow sick clarity of a man reading his own family's autopsy, that everything I'd been angry at Eben Voss for — the silence, the exile, the map nobody wanted — had been a wall he built around us, brick by brick, with his own cowardice as mortar. He hadn't disgraced the family. He'd paid for it. In the only currency he had left.

And then he'd spent his last months trying to give the money back.

So: the icefall.

* * *

Renn had drawn me a sketch before he went off the clock, back when he was still technically my kidnapper and already behaving like something else. "The icefall moves," he'd said, tapping his pencil against the napkin. "Anything I draw today is a rumor by tomorrow. But the spine holds. Big serac on the east flank, blue and clean — if you can see blue, you're on the safe side of it. When the route kinks left toward the rock, don't follow the kink. That's last week's route. It's in the drink now."

"And if I fall in the drink?"

"Then you'll be the second person this month," he said, and didn't smile, which was how I learned Renn's jokes were indistinguishable from his weather reports.

The search teams were the real problem. Halvard had men at the gorge mouth, men on the flanks, men — I was fairly sure — in a helicopter that made passes every ninety minutes like a vulture with a budget. They were looking for Hazel. Nobody was looking for me, which was either an insult or an advantage, and I'd decided to spend it as an advantage.

I moved at night and hid by day, which sounds dramatic until you learn that hiding by day means lying in wet scree listening to your own heartbeat and trying to convince it to shut up. On the second night I crossed a blast road — graded gravel, fresh crates stenciled with Halvard's logo, the whole obscene infrastructure of erasure laid out under the stars like someone's very tidy plan to murder a landscape. I counted the crates. Forty-six. Then I stopped counting, because some numbers you carry and some numbers carry you.

By the third dawn I could see the source.

I'm going to try to describe it and I'm going to fail, but here goes. The glacier came down off the summit massif in a long white tongue, and where the tongue met the valley floor there was an icefall — a staircase of broken ice the size of office buildings, tilted, shattered, refrozen, glowing from within like something lit from underneath. And behind it, half-buried in the headwall, a cavern mouth laced with veins of blue so deep they looked less like ice than like stained glass in a church nobody had finished building.

Voss's map had called it *the throat*. Hazel had called it, when she thought I wasn't listening, *the most beautiful thing I've ever been afraid of*.

Between me and it lay four hundred meters of moving ice, and somewhere up there, presumably, a woman who thought she was finishing this alone.

* * *

The icefall made its own weather of sound. Groans, cracks, the occasional deep boom like a door slamming in a house the size of a mountain. Every noise reorganized my priorities instantly and completely. I learned quickly that the trick was not courage — courage is a story people tell afterward — the trick was smallness. Small steps. Small breaths. Be a man the glacier couldn't be bothered to notice.

Halfway across, the route kinked left toward the rock, just like Renn said it would, and just like Renn said, the kink ended in a gap full of meltwater the color of old bottle glass. I went right instead, along the base of the big blue serac, and found myself in a corridor of ice with walls higher than my house growing up, and light coming through them green and cold, and my breath hanging around me like I was smoking a hundred cigarettes a minute.

That's where I heard the voice.

"Tomas." Faint. Ragged. From above and to the right, from a shelf of ice near the cavern mouth. "Tomas, if that's you and not a hallucination, say something, because I've been arguing with a hallucination of you for two days and losing."

"If you're hallucinating me," I called back, "you should know I'm offended by whatever I've been saying."

Silence. Then laughter — cracked, disbelieving, half sob. Hazel Nystrom stood up on the shelf above me, roped to a steel drum, her face wind-scoured and her eyes red-rimmed, and she looked at me the way people look at a sunrise they'd stopped believing in.

"You absolute idiot," she said. "You came back."

"I read the letter."

"I know. You're crying."

"I'm not — it's the wind. Glacial winds are emotionally complicated."

"Come up, you idiot," she said, "before the glacier decides it has opinions about you."

* * *

Getting up to the shelf took another hour and most of what remained of my dignity. She anchored me with a rope she'd salvaged from somewhere — I didn't ask where, and she didn't offer, and later I would understand that the rope had come off Renn's pack and that its provenance was one more debt we were both carrying now.

The drum sat wedged against the cavern mouth, steel, rusted at the seams, stenciled in fading paint: *E.V. — PROPERTY OF NO ONE.* Voss's handwriting even in stencil, somehow. The man had labeled his own ghost.

Hazel had already worked two of the lid clamps loose. Her hands were shaking — from cold, from exhaustion, from three days alone above the calving line, and I suspected from something older than all of those. I put my hands over hers on the third clamp.

"Together," I said. "He'd have wanted — I don't actually know what he'd have wanted. But together feels right."

"Together," she agreed.

The lid came free with a groan, and the smell of old steel and colder air rose out of it, and we looked down into Eben Voss's fifty-year secret.

Water samples first — dozens of glass vials packed in straw, each labeled in a cramped engineer's hand with dates going back decades. Even frozen in time, some of them had gone strange colors, orange and rust and one that was a green I have no honest name for. Ledgers beneath them, oilcloth-wrapped, dry as bones. And photographs — Hazel lifted them out one at a time, and neither of us spoke for a long while.

Crews at work on the valley's old dam. Men in hard hats smiling for the camera. A shot of the flooding itself, water rising through somebody's orchard, somebody's fence posts standing in what would soon be thirty feet of lake. And in the corner of several photographs, circled in pencil by Voss's hand, the same figure: a man in a suit who did not belong among the hard hats, directing, pointing, present at every decision like a signature.

"Halvard," Hazel said quietly. "That's Aldous Renner-Halvard himself. Standing in a valley he was about to drown, checking his watch."

"There's paper at the bottom," I said. "One sheet. Sealed in wax."

She looked at me. Whatever she saw in my face made her go still. "You read the letter," she said again, softer. "You know what this might be."

"I know what it is," I said. "I just think you should be the one."

Because I'd done the arithmetic in the culvert, in the dark, eleven readings deep. The letter had told me what Voss's silence bought. This document, I understood, would tell us who set the price. And one of the things the price had bought — one of the items on the invoice, buried in the ledger of my grandfather's shame — was a young guide who'd survived an avalanche she shouldn't have, and testified anyway, and then vanished from every licensing roll in the country.

The wax cracked under Hazel's thumb like thin ice.

It was a confession. Signed, dated, witnessed by a notary in a town I'd never heard of. Voss's handwriting, steady at first and deteriorating toward the end, the way a man writes when he knows the pen is outrunning his heart. It named the flooding operation. It named the contamination — tailings seepage, heavy metals, the spring at the source poisoned years before the valley ever drowned, and the drowning designed to bury the proof under a hundred feet of scenic water. It named the payments. It named the threats.

And in the fourth paragraph, in a sentence Voss had underlined twice, it named the executive who had handled the problem of Hazel Nystrom — who had taken her testimony, thanked her for it, and then purchased her blacklist from three boards and two agencies in a single season, to make sure the only witness to the spring never got close enough to the water to matter.

Aldous Renner-Halvard. The man in the photographs. Checking his watch.

Hazel read it twice. I watched her do it — watched twenty years land on her at once, the avalanche and the rope and the hearings and the blacklisting and every safe mile she'd measured her life in so she'd never have to measure it in anything else. Her face didn't collapse. It did something worse. It went calm, the way a river goes calm right before it learns its name.

"They didn't blacklist me because I cut the rope," she said finally. Her voice was almost conversational, which is how I knew we were near the center of the blast radius. "They blacklisted me because I was *there*. Because I saw the spring, before the flooding, before any of it — I filed a report when I was twenty-four years old and they buried it and then they buried me with it. The avalanche was just the tool they reached for when the report wouldn't stay buried." She folded the confession along Voss's creases, precisely, like handling something alive. "I spent twenty years believing I was the crime. I was the witness."

"Tomas." She looked up at me, and the calm cracked, just once, down the middle. "Your grandfather carried this so I wouldn't have to. All this time. He carried my name in a drum on a mountain."

"He carried everyone's name," I said. "That was the whole job. That was the whole miserable, heroic job."

Above us, deep in the headwall, the glacier spoke — not a groan this time but a long tearing exhalation, the sound of something vast shifting its weight. We both went still. Down in the icefall, a serac the size of a barn leaned, considered, and settled. The calving season wasn't coming.

It was arriving.

Hazel slung the drum strap over her shoulder and looked up the slope toward the summit rim, then back at me, and there was something new in her face — not peace, nothing so finished as peace, but direction.

"We need to be off this ice before it finishes waking up," she said. "And then, Tomas Voss, you and I are going to introduce the world to your grandfather."

The glacier answered before I could. Somewhere below us, muffled by a hundred meters of ice, came a sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard — a deep, rhythmic thudding, mechanical and wrong, charges being set in the throat of the source by men who believed the evidence was still safely fictional.

The calving had competition.
