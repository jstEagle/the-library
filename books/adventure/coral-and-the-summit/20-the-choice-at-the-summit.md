## 20. The Choice at the Summit

The drum weighed forty pounds empty of meaning and about four hundred with it. I know because I carried it up the last thousand feet of the valley rim myself, refusing Tomas's help twice and accepting it once, on the pitch where the scree moved like a rumor underfoot. That's the arithmetic of trust: you let someone take the weight only when the mountain insists.

We came out of the gorge at dusk, the two of us and a steel drum wrapped in Renn's jacket, which was now more duct tape than jacket, and stood on the rim looking down into the valley Voss had died to give back. The calving had done its work below us. The glacier's face was a raw white scar, the icefall a field of shattered blue teeth, and somewhere under all that fresh rubble lay the source cavern — sealed, collapsed, exactly as Halvard had planned, except for one detail their plan hadn't accounted for.

The evidence was standing on the ridge behind me, breathing hard, asking if I wanted water.

"Here," Tomas said, handing me the bottle. "You look like a woman who's been arguing with a glacier."

"I won," I said.

"You always say that."

"It's always true. That's what makes it funny."

He almost smiled. His lips were cracked from the cold and his left hand was still wrapped in the improvised bandage from the icefall, but he was upright, he was here, and three days ago I'd watched him cross that collapsing river of ice half-frozen and furious and absolutely unwilling to quit. Voss's blood ran stubborn in him. It occurred to me, not for the first time, that the old man had probably known exactly what he was doing when he put that map in my hands. Dying men don't gamble. They invest.

Below us, the valley caught the last of the light. Even wrecked, even dynamited and scarred and littered with the debris of Halvard's ambition, it was beautiful — a long green throat of land between granite walls, the Serpent running through it thin and bright as wire. Somewhere down there were the foundations of the homesteads they'd flooded fifty years ago. Somewhere down there was the poisoned spring, laced through with blue ice like veins in marble, holding its contamination in frozen custody until somebody with a drum full of ledgers came to set it free.

I sat down on a boulder. My legs made the decision for me; they'd been making all my decisions since the icefall.

Tomas sat beside me. For a while neither of us said anything, which is its own kind of conversation, and we'd gotten fluent in it over these weeks. You can't share a storm camp above treeline with someone without learning their silences. His meant thinking. Mine usually meant lying to myself, but I was working on that.

It was Tomas who broke it, because it was Tomas who'd been carrying the question longer than I had.

"So," he said. "What happens now?"

"Now? Now we descend the east shoulder, make the Kestrel basin by tomorrow night, and reach the signal town the day after. Then—"

"That's the route, Hazel. I'm asking about the choice."

I looked at him. He was watching the valley, not me, which I appreciated. Some conversations go better when nobody has to be looked at during them.

Because there it was. The thing I'd been not-thinking about since the source cavern, since I'd pried the lid off that steel drum and seen what Voss had kept cold and dry for fifty years. Water samples dated and labeled in a careful prospector's hand. Ledgers showing payments from a Halvard predecessor company, monthly, punctual, shameful. Photographs of the flooding crews — and of the engineers who'd ordered the valves opened early, who'd drowned a valley not for a reservoir but for a burial. And underneath it all, signed and witnessed and folded twice, the confession naming names.

One of those names was mine.

Not *Hazel Nystrom, blacklisted guide*. The other kind of mine. *Hazel Nystrom, witness.* The climber who survived the Corrie Glacier avalanche twenty years ago, whose testimony about the commercial route's ignored warnings would have cost Halvard's alpine division a licensing renewal — if her testimony had ever been heard. Instead it had been bought. My silence, purchased with a blacklist dressed up as a safety ruling, and I'd spent two decades believing the lie because the lie was kinder than the truth. The lie said I'd failed. The truth said I'd been erased, and then had gone along with being erased because fighting it meant testifying, and testifying meant saying out loud what happened on the rope that day.

The drum held both truths. That was the elegant cruelty of it. Voss's confession cleared my name and damned my silence in the same paragraph.

"There's a man in Meridian," I said finally. "Aldous Wrenn. He runs the licensing board's appeals office. He owes me — or thinks he does, which is better. If I bring him this drum privately, before anyone else sees it, he can broker a deal. Halvard settles quietly, my blacklist is rescinded, my license comes back. Twenty years of exile, undone. I guide again. Legal, sanctioned, real." I turned the water bottle in my hands. "And the valley stays buried. The contamination stays a rumor. The Voss family stays exiled from land that was stolen from them twice — once by floodwater, once by paperwork."

"And the other option?"

"You know the other option."

"I want to hear you say it."

Of course he did. Tomas had learned somewhere — maybe from his grandfather's deathbed, maybe from me, maybe from the river itself — that a person will agree to anything in the abstract and fight it in the specific, so you make them say the specific out loud. Make it real. Make it cost.

"We publish," I said. "Everything. The samples, the ledgers, the photographs, the confession. We send copies to every paper, every regulator, every lawyer who's ever sued Halvard and lost. The cover-up dies in daylight. And my name—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "My name is cleared in the story, sure. *Retaliation exposed, whistleblower vindicated.* Lovely headline. But the licensing board doesn't move fast for people who embarrass their funders. I stay blacklisted. Formally. Indefinitely. Maybe forever. No permit, no legal guiding, no reinstatement hearing, nothing. I traded my name back once already, in a way. Turns out you can only spend it once."

The wind came up the valley and pushed against us like it had an opinion. Below, the Serpent caught the last light and threw it back.

Tomas was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "Can I tell you something my grandfather told me? The one good thing. The night before he died."

"He talked that much?"

"He talked exactly that much. One sentence, rationed out like morphine." Tomas turned the bandage on his hand, not looking at me. "He said, *The family didn't lose the valley because we were weak. We lost it because we were quiet.* And then he asked me to burn his map, and I said yes, because I thought the map was the shame. But it wasn't. The map was the receipt." He looked at me then. "You're doing the thing where you decide for both of us again."

"I'm doing the thing where I decide for me. It's my name on the line."

"It's my grandfather's confession in your pack."

"It's my promise in your grandfather's grave."

"Yes," he said. "Both. That's the point. This isn't yours alone, Hazel. It stopped being yours alone the day I followed you up the Serpent instead of going home." He paused. "But okay. Your name. Your call. So let me tell you what I see, and then I'll shut up, and whatever you choose, I'm standing there when you choose it. Both options. I mean it. You pick the deal, I'll carry the drum down the mountain myself and hate every step and never once say I told you so."

"That's a lie and we both know it."

"It's a generous lie. Take it." He shifted on the boulder to face the valley. "Here's what I see. Option one, you get your license back. You get to lead treks through the Greywater Range for tourists who ask careless questions about your blacklist, except now the answer is *actually, I was vindicated,* and they nod, and it changes nothing, because the valley is still sealed and the company that buried you is still burying things. You get your miles back. Counted ones. Safe ones. The kind you've been measuring your whole life with because counting them meant you never had to measure anything else."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It's worse than unfair. It's accurate." He let that sit. "Option two, you lose the license. Formally, on paper, in the eyes of every board and bureau in the range. And you gain—" he gestured, at the valley, at the drum, at me, at the whole ridiculous ruined gorgeous world below us "—this. A valley that gets given back. A family that gets to come home. A spring that stops poisoning a watershed because somebody refused to trade it away. And a life that isn't measured in safe miles anymore, because you'll have spent your last safe mile buying something that mattered."

"You've been practicing that speech."

"Since the icefall. I had a lot of time. It's very boring hanging off a fixed line waiting for the world to stop ending."

I laughed — properly this time, ugly and relieved — and then I did the thing I'd been avoiding, which was actually think. Not strategize. Not run the angles the way I ran switchbacks, counting them so I wouldn't have to feel them. Think.

Here's what I knew about myself, twenty years of knowing it: I had built my entire life around a single transaction. I gave Halvard my silence; they gave me exile; and I called the exile penance, because penance was easier than theft. Every cairn I stacked for Mara, every safe mile I logged, every client I ferried up routine routes with flawless competence — all of it was interest paid on a debt I'd agreed to in a hospital corridor with a company lawyer's card in my hand. Loyalty, I'd called it. Loyalty to the dead. But Mara hadn't asked for my loyalty. Mara had asked, once, in the tent the night before she died, whether I ever got tired of being careful. And I'd said no, because I was a liar even then, especially then.

The blacklist wasn't the price of my survival. It was the price of my silence, and I'd been paying it voluntarily for twenty years, calling it grief.

If I took the deal, I'd keep paying it. Nicer cell. Same walls.

"Tomas," I said.

"Yeah."

"If I do this — if we do this — I need you to understand what you're signing up for. Because you said you'd stand by either choice, and I believe you, but this one has weather. There's no permit in my future. There's maybe a lawsuit. There's definitely a stretch where the money runs out and the papers move on and we're just two people in a valley with a reputation and no income. I'm forty-six years old and I don't know how to do anything but walk uphill behind strangers."

"First of all, you also know how to read rivers, fix ropes, argue with glaciers, and win. Second—" he stopped, and I watched him decide to say the true thing instead of the easy one, which is the hardest climb there is. "Second, I've been reading my grandfather's ledgers for three weeks. Do you know what I found in the margins? Not numbers. Routes. He surveyed this whole range on foot, alone, unlicensed, off every map that mattered. Fifty years of it. Everyone called it obsession. It wasn't. It was a man walking toward something everyone told him didn't exist." He looked at me. "I come from a long line of people who walk toward things. I'd like to keep the tradition. Preferably with company."

The sun went down behind the western wall and the valley filled up with blue shadow, and somewhere far below, the new glacier groaned — the deep settling sound of ice finding its shape after catastrophe. I'd heard that sound my whole life. I'd always heard it as warning. Sitting there, with the drum between my boots and Tomas beside me, I heard it as something else. Ice doesn't settle because it's giving up. It settles because it's becoming solid. Becoming what it's going to be.

I thought about Mara's cairn, the one I'd built at the trailhead every season for twenty years, one stone for every year she'd been gone. I'd told myself it was memorial. It was hostage-taking. As long as I kept counting, I never had to stop.

I put my hand flat on the drum. Cold steel, warm evening, the whole weight of two families' worth of buried truth under my palm.

"Okay," I said. "Okay. Here's how it goes. We make three copies of everything tonight — paper's precious, so we copy the confession first, then the ledger pages that matter, then the photographs. One set stays with you. One set goes in the drum. One set—" I tapped my chest pocket, where the map rode, Voss's map, soft as cloth now from handling. "One set travels separately. If anything happens to either of us between here and the signal town, the truth survives us. That's non-negotiable."

"Agreed."

"Then we go down the east shoulder, and we don't stop until the Kestrel basin, and we send it all out on the radio relay, every outlet, every regulator, Wrenn included — because if he's going to broker anything, he can broker it in daylight like everyone else. No private deals. No quiet settlements. Sunlight or nothing."

"Sunlight or nothing," Tomas repeated, and stuck out his hand, formal as a treaty, ridiculous as a treaty, and I shook it, and that was that. Twenty years of careful miles, dissolved by a handshake on a boulder above a drowned valley. It should have felt like losing something. It felt like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was wearing.

"One more thing," I said, as we started gathering gear in the failing light. "When this breaks — and it will break big, Halvard will come apart like the glacier did — someone is going to offer me my license back. Publicly. A board member wanting a photo op, a minister wanting redemption points. It'll be tempting. It'll be *exactly* tempting. So I want you to remember this moment, right here, and remind me what I said."

"What did you say?"

I looked down at the valley one more time. The first stars were out over the eastern wall, and the Serpent ran silver through the blue, and somewhere beneath the rubble the source waited, sealed but not silenced, because we were its voice now.

"I said some things aren't for sale at any price," I told him. "And I said it sober, on a mountaintop, with witnesses. Well. Witness."

"One witness," Tomas agreed, shouldering his pack. "Best ratio you've ever had."

We started down the east shoulder in the dark, two headlamps bobbing on the scree, the drum lashed between us like a third climber. Behind us the glacier settled into its new shape with a long, grinding sigh. Ahead of us lay two days of descent, a signal town, a press release that would detonate louder than any charge Halvard ever set, and a life I couldn't yet imagine — unlicensed, unmapped, uncounted.

For the first time in twenty years, I wasn't measuring the miles.

I was just walking them.
