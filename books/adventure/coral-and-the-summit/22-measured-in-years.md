## 22. Measured in Years

A year is a long time to learn how to stop counting.

I used to measure everything. Switchbacks on the Greywater approach — forty-one if you cut the last one, which I never did anymore, because cutting switchbacks was the kind of arithmetic that got people killed. Miles to the next water. Days since the avalanche. Years since the rope. I kept a ledger in my head so complete that some nights I'd lie awake auditing it, running totals that always came out the same: one partner dead, one name ruined, zero forgiven.

These days my ledger has exactly three entries, and none of them are numbers.

The first entry: four clients, not six, because six was what the old me would have promised and the new me knows better than to promise anything a mountain hasn't co-signed.

The second entry: Tomas Voss, walking point, arguing with a mule.

The third entry: open.

* * *

The trek had started three days back at the trailhead above Kestrel Junction, where the sign still read SERPENT RIVER — CLOSED BY ORDER, except somebody — and I had my suspicions, and those suspicions wore my jacket — had taken a grease pencil to it and added *not anymore* underneath.

"You know," said Marisol Okafor, our botanist-for-the-week, squinting up at the defaced sign while she adjusted her pack straps, "in the city they told us this whole drainage was cursed."

"Everything worth walking into is cursed," I said. "That's how you know it's worth walking into."

"That's either wisdom or a liability waiver."

"It's both. Sign here."

She laughed. They always laugh at first. By day two they've stopped laughing and started listening, which is the actual job — not guiding feet, guiding attention. Anyone can walk uphill. Not everyone can hear a river change its mind about you from fifty yards off.

Tomas heard it before I did, that morning. Of course he did. He'd been hearing rivers for a year now, learning their grammar the way his grandfather must have learned it, alone, with a pencil stub and a grudge against the world. He held up a fist without turning around — the old trail signal, which I'd taught him and which he'd adopted with the solemnity of a man being knighted — and we all stopped, and thirty seconds later the Serpent shrugged a boulder loose somewhere upstream and sent a brown tongue of water licking over the bank where Marisol had been about to step.

"River-reading," Tomas announced to the group, in the tone of a man who has discovered religion and intends to share it whether you want it or not, "is just listening to someone argue with gravity. The river always loses eventually. Your job is to not be standing where it loses."

"Where do you get this stuff?" asked Dev, the retired schoolteacher who'd signed on because his doctor said walking or die and he'd chosen walking out of spite, which I respected enormously.

"My grandfather," Tomas said. And then, quieter, almost to himself: "Turns out he wrote most of it down after all."

That's the thing about Eben Voss. The man spent sixty years being a liar by profession and then, in the last month of his life, became the most honest corpse in the territory. The steel drum came out of the calving zone dented but sealed, the evidence inside dry as scripture, and when the story broke — Halvard, the engineered flood, the contamination, the bought blacklist with my name sitting in the file like a receipt — it broke everywhere at once. Front pages. Hearings. Renn Okafor — yes, Marisol's brother; the valley has a sense of humor — testifying in a suit that didn't fit him, saying things under oath that he'd first said to me in a crate road with dynamite dust on his boots.

Halvard's executives fell like rockfall. Slow at first, then all at once, the way everything falls.

And me? My name got cleared in every paper in the country. The licensing board, meanwhile, discovered the ancient bureaucratic art of the stall. Fourteen months of "under review." I've stopped checking the mail for it. Not because I've given up — I don't give up, ask anyone, ask especially the licensing board — but because I finally understood that the permit was never the thing. The thing was the miles. And the miles were mine the whole time. Nobody can blacklist a woman from her own legs.

So: unlicensed. Small. Honest. Four clients instead of fourteen. Word-of-mouth only. I charge enough to eat and not enough to be greedy, and I tell every client on day one that their guide is technically operating outside the law, and every one of them so far has looked at the mountains, looked at me, and decided the law could catch up whenever it felt like it.

It's the freest I've ever been inside a system designed to cage me.

* * *

We made the valley mouth on the fourth afternoon, and I let Tomas bring them through the gorge.

This was deliberate. A year ago I'd have white-knuckled every bend of that narrows myself, checking the map against the stone like the map might lie to me again. But the gorge doesn't scare me anymore. It matches itself. Every landmark Voss drew — the leaning pillar, the twin seeps, the shelf he called the Preacher because it looked like a man mid-sermon — sits exactly where his pencil said it would, and there's a strange peace in that. A dead man's honesty, load-bearing.

Tomas walked them through it narrating. He's good at it now, better than he thinks. He tells the family stories as we go — the great-grandparents exiled when the valley drowned, the grandmother who kept a jar of valley water on her windowsill for forty years like a relic, Eben coming back alone year after year with survey equipment and a secret eating him alive. The clients love it. Dev took notes. Marisol asked questions Tomas couldn't answer, and he turned to me and said, "She'll know," meaning me, meaning the woman who dug his grandfather's truth out of a glacier, and I answered what I could and admitted what I couldn't, and that admission — *I don't know* — felt like the sturdiest handhold on the whole route.

Then the gorge opened, and there it was.

The valley.

I've been back eleven times since the calving. You'd think it would dull. It doesn't. The walls still rise sheer and gold-veined where the afternoon hits them, the river still runs green-bright over gravel bars that haven't seen footprints in sixty years, and the glacier at the head of it — scarred now, its face a raw amphitheater of blue where it calved, where we ran, where the world came down — still catches the light like something that forgives you for looking.

The access ruling came through in the spring. The Voss descendants — scattered across three states, most of them strangers to each other until the lawyers introduced them — were granted right of return. Some came. Some stood at the mouth and wept and drove home. One of them, an elderly woman named Ingrid who looks so much like Eben it startles me every time, comes up twice a season to sit by the river with a thermos and say nothing. She says the nothing is the point.

"They're going to ask about the cairns," Tomas murmured to me as we dropped toward the flats. "Dev already asked me on the switchbacks. I told him to wait and ask you."

"Coward."

"Strategist. There's a difference and it's mostly delegation."

* * *

The cairns stand on the bench above the river, where the ground is stable and the view runs the length of the valley — the spot I chose a year ago, hauling stones with bleeding hands while the echo of the calving still rolled around the walls like slow thunder.

Two cairns, side by side.

The left one is Eben Voss's. Ingrid brings a fresh stone every visit, so it grows a little each time, a monument that accumulates the way grief should — slowly, by hand, in weather. The right one is Wren Calloway's. My partner. Twenty-two years dead this autumn, though I stopped counting the years somewhere around the time counting stopped being survival and started being a cage.

For twenty years I built Wren a cairn alone, wherever I happened to be, small anonymous stacks on anonymous ridges, because I couldn't go back to the real place and because I couldn't say the real words. *I cut the rope.* Three words that cost more than any pitch I've ever climbed. I'd left them unsaid so long they'd fossilized into something worse than guilt — a shape, a posture, a way of holding my shoulders through every conversation I never had.

Then a storm above treeline, a boy wrapped in a sleeping bag pretending not to listen, and I said them out loud for the first time. And Tomas — twenty-six years old, furious, grieving, armed with every reason to judge me — didn't flinch. He handed me the thermos. That was all. The thermos, and the silence of a man deciding that the truth, even ugly, even aimed at him, was safer company than another person's careful lie.

I've thought about that thermos more times than I can count. It might be the single kindest object anyone has ever handed me.

Wren's cairn here isn't anonymous. It has a plaque — Ingrid insisted, brass, small, funded by whatever obscure line item the restitution settlement allowed. It reads: *WREN CALLOWAY, CLIMBER. THE MOUNTAIN KEPT HER. WE KEEP HER STORIES.* We keep her stories. That was Tomas's phrasing. He fought the lawyer who wanted *loved and remembered*, said it sounded like a headstone, and this wasn't a death, it was a handing-down. The lawyer gave in. Everyone gives in to Tomas eventually. It's his best quality and, occasionally, the thing I threaten to leave him behind for.

Because here's the part I still can't say lightly, the part that makes Dev raise his eyebrows and Marisol pretend very hard to study lichen:

Tomas stayed.

Not dramatically. No grand declaration at the summit, no sweeping gesture. He walked down out of the hills with me, sat through the press circus with his jaw set like a man biting a bullet, testified when they needed him, buried his grandfather properly — twice, once in the cemetery and once in the family story, which was the burial that mattered — and then one morning in the shoulder season he appeared at my cabin door with a duffel bag and a look on his face that dared me to make it weird.

"I need to learn the range," he said. "Properly. From someone who isn't dead."

"You're hiring me? I'm blacklisted."

"You're *good*. The paperwork will sort itself." A pause. "Also I can't cook and you apparently can, and I've done the math on my life expectancy accordingly."

So he apprenticed. Unofficially, illegally, gloriously. He learned knots and river-reading and how to read a client's blisters from ten paces. He learned when to push and when to shut up — mostly. He learned the Kestrel Traverse with me in June, both of us quiet on the bad stretch, neither of us needing to say anything about it, which is its own kind of fluency. And somewhere in there the apprenticeship quietly dissolved into partnership, and the partnership quietly dissolved into whatever this is, which we have declined to label because labels are for jars and we are not preserving anything, we are using it, daily, in all weather.

People ask. Clients ask, delicately. Dev asked with the subtlety of a falling piano. I tell them the truth: he argues with me about route-finding, he makes the best coffee in the drainage, and when I wake up at 3 a.m. with the old dream — the rope, the white, the weight going light — he doesn't ask what it was. He just puts the coffee on. The smell reaches the bedroom before I'm fully back in my body, and by the time I come out to the kitchen, the dream has nowhere to sit down.

Measured in years. That's what I have now. Not miles. Not safe distances between me and the past. Years — plural, expected, planned-for, argued-over. We fight about whether to expand to five clients next season (he's for it, I'm against it, we'll probably compromise at five because he's right and I hate it). We fight about whether the licensing appeal is worth another round (it is, he filed the paperwork himself, the absolute menace). We plan winters. We plan summers. We plan like people who believe in the future, which is a skill I had to learn at forty-nine, and let me tell you, learning to believe in the future is harder than ice climbing and considerably colder at the start.

* * *

That evening, after camp was struck and the clients fed — Dev telling his third story about the school district, Marisol cataloguing something by headlamp, Tomas doing dishes with the concentration of a man defusing ordnance — I climbed the bench alone to the cairns.

Ingrid had been up that morning. Eben's pile had grown by one flat grey stone, placed with her particular precision, and beside it she'd left the jar. Empty now. She'd poured the valley water out onto the ground at the base, forty years of carrying it ended in one slow pour, and told me afterward, "He can have it back. I only kept it so I wouldn't forget what we lost." She'd looked out at the river, bright and loud and alive. "Now I can't forget. It won't shut up." Best eulogy anyone's given in this valley, including mine, and I've given two.

I sat between the cairns with my back against the evening-warm stone and talked to them both, the way I do. To Wren: the season's news, the clients, the traverse clean in June, no shaking. To Eben: your grandson is insufferable and magnificent, your valley has footprints in it again, and the water — I tested it myself, third-party lab, because some ironies deserve documentation — runs clean.

Then I did the thing I'd been carrying the stone for.

I'd picked it up two days back, down in the gorge, off the gravel bar below the Preacher — a round river stone, pale, veined with a thin green seam like a thread of the water itself. It had been riding in my pack pocket, warming against my hip, waiting for me to find the sentence to go with it.

Here's the sentence: For twenty years I measured my life in miles because miles could be counted safely from a distance, and distance was the only thing I trusted to keep me alive. Then a dying man traded me a map for a promise, and the promise dragged me upstream into the one place distance couldn't protect me, and it turns out that was the cure. You can't heal at a remove. Nobody ever came back to life by staying far away from the wound.

So I built the third cairn small — knee-high, three courses, the stone with the green vein on top — and I put no name on it. No plaque. No date. Nothing to maintain, nothing to grieve, nothing to count.

It's the mile I no longer need. The distance I closed. The ledger line marked *paid in full* and torn out.

Below me, camp glowed — stove-light, headlamps, Dev's laugh rolling up the bench, Tomas's silhouette moving among the tents with an ease he didn't have a year ago, checking guylines against a wind I hadn't even noticed yet. He'd notice it before me tomorrow. He always does now. We take turns being the one who hears the river first, which is, I've decided, the entire definition of a life together.

I pressed my palm flat on the nameless cairn once, the way you seal a letter.

"Hazel!" Tomas's voice carried up the bench, half irritation, half the other thing. "Your protégé is drowning the lentils and your client wants to know if the glacier is haunted!"

"It's not haunted," I called back, standing, brushing off my knees. "It's just talkative!"

"That's what haunted means!"

"Come up here and I'll show you the difference!"

A pause. I knew he'd come. He always comes — up the scree, into the wind, toward whatever I'm pointing at. That was the deal we never said out loud: I show him the range, he shows me the years, and neither of us counts anything anymore except what matters.

His headlamp bobbed up the slope toward me, and behind it the valley went on talking to itself in the dark — water over stone, stone under water, the oldest argument there is, still going, still losing, still beautiful.

I turned to meet him halfway.
