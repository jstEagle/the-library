## 1. The Weight of Miles

The Empty Reach earned its name honestly. Silas Oyelaran had crossed it eleven times in seven years, and every crossing had taught him the same lesson: the desert did not hate you, and it did not want you. It simply declined to notice you, the way a man declines to notice an ant on his boot.

He rode north out of Perdition Wells at first light with a contract folded in his breast pocket and three days' water strapped behind his saddle. The contract was simple enough to fit on one page. A mining concern called the Meridian Concession had lost a ledger—stolen, they claimed, by a clerk named Tomas Vey—and they wanted it back before its contents could reach a rival's hands. Five hundred dollars on delivery. Silas didn't care what was in the ledger. He cared that Vey had gone north, alone, on foot, into country where alone and on foot were usually the last two decisions a man made.

He tracked the way other men prayed: without thinking about it, without believing it would save him. Vey's prints were narrow-heeled, city boots, and they told a story Silas could read like a letter from home. The man had started out striding. By the second day the stride shortened. By the third there were rest stops every mile, long ones, the kind where a man sat down and argued with himself about turning back.

Silas measured his life in miles. It was a habit he'd built deliberately, the way a man builds a wall. Miles could be counted, logged, closed out. Years couldn't. Years accumulated things—a name spoken too often, a face remembered in a mirror, debts that weren't money. Seven years ago he had stopped being someone, and the miles since then had been the interest paid on that erasure. He kept them moving so they'd never come due.

On the fourth morning, the trail bent east toward a dry wash the maps called Sutter's Cut, and there the story changed. The boot prints broke into a run. Then something else crossed them—drag marks, a wide furrow, as if a body had been pulled. And over everything, faint but unmistakable on the wind, the smell of blood.

Silas loosened the rifle in its scabbard and followed the smell down into the cut.

* * *

The prospector lay in the shadow of an overturned ore cart, and by all rights he should have been dead already. He was old past guessing—white beard gone yellow at the mouth, hands like cured leather—and there was a hole in him below the ribs that bled in slow pulses onto the sand. Two mules stood hobbled nearby, patient as furniture. Beside the cart, scattered in the dirt, were survey stakes. New ones. Painted with the mark of the Meridian Concession.

Silas swung down and knelt, keeping himself in the old man's line of sight, hands open. "Easy," he said. "I'm not the one who did this."

"Didn't think you were." The voice was gravel and wind. The prospector's eyes found him, pale and unafraid. "You're the ranger. Heard you were working the Reach again."

"I'm not a ranger anymore."

"No." A wet laugh cost the old man something. "Nobody out here is anything anymore. That's the whole point of the Reach."

Silas pulled his canteen and held it to the man's lips. He drank a little, then pushed it away with surprising firmness. "Save it. I know arithmetic when I see it." He nodded at the stakes. "You know what those are?"

"Came to pull them up. They're claiming water rights on land that hasn't got any water. That's the trick of it, boy. You file a claim on a dry riverbed, nobody contests it, because who fights over dust?" His breath whistled. "Then one day the river comes back. Rivers do that. They always come back. And the company owns it."

"You're saying there's water under here."

"I'm saying there's a river under half the Reach, and it's alive, and it runs to a settlement called Marrow Bend that's been dying of thirst for ten years while men in offices argue about whose paper says what." The old man fumbled at his shirt, and Silas saw the blood on his fingers wasn't all his own—he'd been clutching something against the wound. "They shot me for pulling up six sticks of wood. Six."

Silas glanced at the tracks around the cart. Boot prints, several sets, heavy-soled, heading northeast. The same direction Vey's trail ran. He filed that away and turned back.

"What's your name?"

"Hollis. Amos Hollis." The prospector pressed the bundle against his chest once more, then held it out with both hands, the way a man hands over a child. "Map. I drew it myself, thirty years of walking put into it. Every sink, every seam, every place the stone gets thin. It'll take you to the river."

"I'm not a water man. I'm tracking a thief."

"Then track him northeast." Hollis smiled, and there was blood in his teeth. "Because the man who shot me went northeast too, and he works for the company that wants that ledger back, and I'd wager my last tooth your thief and my shooters are headed to the same place. Nothing out here is separate, ranger. Everything runs downhill, even sins."

Silas took the bundle. Inside the oilcloth was a map drawn in faded ink on sailcloth, dense with annotations in a small, patient hand. It was beautiful work. It was also, he understood immediately, worth killing for—which meant holding it made him a target, which meant the miles ahead had just changed character entirely.

"I can't carry this for you," Silas said. "I can get you to Perdition Wells. Maybe."

Hollis shook his head slowly. "You can carry it home. That's what I need. Not me—*it*. Marrow Bend. Before the permits expire at season's end and the whole basin goes to the concession on paper." He gripped Silas's wrist with a strength that startled him. "One promise, ranger. That's the price of the map, and it's the only thing I own. Carry it home. Don't let them own the river."

"I don't make promises."

"Everybody makes promises. Most just make them quietly, to themselves, and then spend their lives pretending otherwise." Hollis's grip loosened. His eyes drifted toward the rim of the wash, where the sky was going white with morning. "I've been out here thirty years looking for that river. Found it two years ago. Kept it secret because I thought secret was safe." Another wet laugh. "Secret is just lonely with better manners."

He died twenty minutes later, between one breath and the next, mid-sentence, talking about the taste of the water in the deep seams—sweet, he said, sweeter than anything on top of the ground. Silas sat with him until the desert finished noticing neither of them, then dug a grave in the floor of the wash with the ore cart's broken handle. It took two hours. He marked it with one of the concession's own stakes, driven in upside down, points to the sky.

Before he filled it in, he said the only words he had: "I'll get it there."

He hadn't meant to say it aloud. Saying it aloud made it real, and real things came due.

* * *

He rode northeast through the heat of the day, the map riding against his spine like a second heartbeat. At a seep called the Widow's Tit he watered the horse and studied the map by the light through the canvas flap, and what he saw settled the matter of direction beyond any argument. The route Hollis had drawn—the only viable route, the one that threaded the box canyons and avoided the salt flats—ran northeast. Exactly northeast. Toward the heavy boot prints. Toward Vey's shortening stride. Toward whatever waited where the thief, the shooters, and the buried river all converged.

Five hundred dollars for a ledger. One promise for a river.

Silas rolled the map closed and looked north, where the Reach went on being empty and indifferent, and felt—for the first time in seven years—the strange, unwelcome sensation of carrying something that mattered more than his own invisibility. He'd spent seven years being no one, and no one could not keep promises. No one could just ride away.

But the map needed someone. And somewhere ahead, a thief was walking toward the same water.

He mounted up. The miles began again, and this time they counted toward something.
