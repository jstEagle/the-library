## 2. Water Under Stone

Silas found the hydrologist in a jail cell in Coriander Flats, of all places, and the mule driver at a card table losing money he didn't have, and the cartographer nowhere at all until she found *him*.

The hydrologist was named Dr. Amara Sesay, and she greeted him through the bars with the flat courtesy of a woman who had stopped expecting good news. "Let me guess," she said. "You need someone who can read water."

"I need someone who can find it where it isn't supposed to be."

Her eyes changed then—something waking behind the exhaustion. "That's the only kind worth finding." She stood, brushed dust from a coat that had clearly been slept in, and said, "I was stripped of my license for insisting the Reach sat on an aquifer the concession surveys conveniently never mapped. They called me a fraud. I called them liars. Guess who had lawyers."

"Can you follow a map drawn by a dying man?"

"I've followed worse from living ones."

The mule driver was Tomas Reyes, and he owed money to three separate men in Coriander Flats, which was why he agreed before Silas finished the sentence. He had six mules, a bad knee, and a laugh that came too easily for a man in his position. "Where are we going?" he asked, already loading.

"Into the Reach."

"Course we are." He spat, grinning. "Everyone who owes me money goes into the Reach eventually."

The cartographer was another matter. Silas didn't recruit her; she recruited herself. He found her waiting beside his pack mule at first light on the morning they were meant to depart—a young woman with ink-stained fingers and a leather satchel of instruments, standing in the cold like she'd been there all night.

"You're Oyelaran," she said.

"No one's called me that in years."

"Well, someone has now. Wren Ashby. I draw maps." She held up a rolled sheet, and Silas unrolled it despite himself. It was the Reach—the whole basin, rendered in fine confident linework, every wash and rim and dry canyon labeled in a small precise hand. And in the center, where the prospector's map had marked its buried river, she had drawn a question mark.

"How did you—" He stopped. "You copied the old man's map."

"I saw it. In the assay office, before they burned his claim papers. I have a memory like wet clay—everything presses in and stays." She took the sheet back, careful as a surgeon. "I know you didn't ask for me. But you're taking a dead man's promise into country nobody's surveyed properly in forty years, and you're doing it without a cartographer. That's not brave. That's just slow."

Tomas laughed from atop the lead mule. "I like her."

Silas looked at the girl—at the ink stains, the stubborn set of her shoulders—and felt something in his chest shift, some door he'd braced shut swinging an inch on its hinge. Seven years he had traveled alone because alone was safe. Alone couldn't be lied to. Alone couldn't be lost.

"Keep up," he said, and swung into the saddle.

* * *

Wren had drawn maps of places she'd never seen her whole life, and she had learned to expect the disappointment of arrival—the real country always thinner than the imagined one, the lines always lying a little.

The Reach did not disappoint. The Reach was worse than anything she'd drawn.

They went down into the canyon country on the third day, following the dead man's bearings, and the walls rose around them like the ribs of something enormous and sleeping. The stone was red going to black, banded with iron, and the heat pooled in the narrows so thickly that Tomas's mules walked with their heads low and their ears flat. Nothing grew but saltbush and the occasional twisted juniper clinging to impossible ledges. The maps she'd memorized called this section the Hollows, and the surveyors' notes said what the notes always said: *no water, no value.*

"It should be here," Dr. Sesay said on the fifth day, crouched over a seam of greenish clay in the canyon floor. She touched it the way other people touched relics. "This is spring deposit. This is water that *was* here, leaving its signature." She looked up at Silas, and Wren saw the hunger in her face, ten years of professional disgrace burning off like morning fog. "It hasn't been gone long."

"How long?" Silas asked.

"Years. Not centuries." She stood, knees cracking. "Something redirected it. Or something buried it."

Wren was sketching as fast as her fingers would move—not the map anymore, but the canyon itself, the way the light fell at noon like poured brass, the way Dr. Sesay knelt in the dirt with her hands full of ancient water. She caught Silas watching her work and looked away quickly, embarrassed, but not before she saw the expression cross his face: something startled and soft, quickly shuttered, like a man catching himself enjoying a meal he believed he hadn't earned.

He did that a lot, she was learning. Let himself be part of the crew for an hour, two hours—arguing route logic with her over supper, teaching Tomas how to read the stars for bearing, listening to Dr. Sesay lecture about recharge rates with genuine attention—and then some internal bell would ring and he'd go quiet and apart, tending his gear, walking the perimeter, being a stranger again.

On the sixth night, camped beneath an overhang streaked with desert varnish, Wren worked up the nerve to ask him.

"Who were you? Before."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, a coyote made its thin remark and got no answer.

"A man who made mistakes," Silas said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I'm giving." But he said it gently, and after a moment he added, "Ask me again when we get where we're going. Maybe."

She decided that was a door left open rather than shut, and she could live with that. For now.

* * *

Silas heard the water before anyone else—because he wasn't listening for water at all, only for trouble, and trouble had taught him to hear everything.

A sound under the wind. A hush inside the hush.

They had come down into a slot canyon so narrow the mules had to be walked through single file, their packs scraping both walls, when Silas raised a fist and brought the column to a halt. Ahead, the canyon floor changed—cracked mud giving way to smooth black stone, and out of a fissure no wider than a coffin, air moved. Cool. Damp. Smelling of mineral and green.

"Dr. Sesay," he said quietly.

She pushed past him, pressed her palm flat against the fissure's lip, and Wren watched the woman's face do something complicated—joy and grief arriving together, like twins.

"It's here," Sesay whispered. "Under our feet this whole time. The river didn't die. It went down. It's running under the stone, following the fault line, and it comes up—" she pointed down-canyon, where the walls began to widen toward a distant pale glow, "—there. Where the canyon opens. There'll be a spring. Maybe a whole reach of open water."

They walked the last mile in silence, and then the walls fell away and the world opened, and there it was: a ribbon of living water unfurling across the canyon floor, fringed with cottonwood and willow, bright as a struck match in all that red. Birds Wren couldn't name lifted from the reeds. Tomas took off his hat, which Wren had not yet seen him do for anything.

Nobody spoke for a long time. Then Tomas said, hoarsely, "We should name it," and Dr. Sesay said, "It has a name, it's had a name for ten thousand years, we just forgot it," and Wren wrote in her field book, *Day 6. Found it alive.*

Silas stood apart, as he did, looking not at the water but at the ground.

Because the ground told a different story than wonder. Stakes—survey stakes, steel-tipped, driven in a measured line along the eastern bank. Wire, cut and coiled, the bright ends still fresh. Boot prints in the river mud, machine-soled, days old at most, a whole patrol's worth of them. And nailed to the largest cottonwood, a placard in official blue:

**MERIDIAN CONCESSION COMPANY — PENDING ALLOCATION — SURVEY IN PROGRESS**

"They know," Wren said behind him.

"They've known longer than us," Silas said. His voice had gone flat and cold, and when Wren looked at his face she saw not the quiet campfire man but someone older and harder, someone who had done this before—read sign, counted enemies, calculated odds. "Fresh prints. At least eight men, moving heavy. They'll have a supply cache within a day's walk, maybe less." He turned, and his eyes swept each of them in turn. "And they took the old man's map off his body, or they're about to. Which means they know exactly where this water comes up."

"So what do we do?" Tomas asked.

Silas was quiet a moment. Out in the shallows, the river made its patient sound, the oldest sound in the world.

"We get to the outlet before they do," he said. "And then—" He stopped. Wren watched him wrestle with whatever came next, watched him decide not to say it. "Then we'll see."

But that night, while the others slept, Wren woke to find him standing watch alone, and she pretended to sleep and listened, and she heard him say one word to the dark, so softly it barely disturbed the fire:

"Dae-Ho."

She didn't know the name. But she knew the way he said it—the way you say the name of a debt you've been carrying so long it's grown into the bone—and she lay awake a long time after, staring at the stars above the canyon rim, wondering what kind of past could make a man flinch at a single syllable.

Two days later, on the morning they broke camp for the outlet, she got her answer. It came riding up the canyon with the dust, wearing company blue.
