## 3. What the Company Burns

Wren smelled the smoke before she heard the engines.

She'd been up early, as always, sketching the canyon walls in the gray half-light, her pencil tracing the strata like a physician reading a pulse. The camp behind her was still mostly asleep—Silas at the fire edge with his back to the dark, the hydrologist Odile muttering calculations into her cold tea, Bram's mules stamping somewhere down the wash. Seven days out from the last settlement. Seven days of following a dead man's map through stone that sang when the wind moved through it.

Then the wind changed, and it carried diesel and burning canvas.

"Silas." Her voice came out flat and strange. "Silas, wake up."

He was already on his feet. That was the thing about him she'd never been able to draw right—the way he moved before sound reached him, like his body lived a half-second ahead of the world. He looked east, toward the trail they'd come in on, and Wren watched something cross his face that she had never seen there before.

Not fear. Recognition.

"How many?" he asked.

"At least two vehicles. Maybe three."

"Get them up. All of them. Now."

* * *

They came out of the dawn like insects, black and gleaming—three concession company haulers with bull bars and mounted floodlights still cutting yellow holes in the morning. A dozen enforcers in dust-gray fatigues fanned out across the camp perimeter with the bored efficiency of men who had done this a hundred times. Behind them, the expedition's supply tent was already alight, the map case's hiding place beneath Wren's drafting table exposed like a wound.

Wren stood between Odile and Bram near the cold fire ring, her hands shaking not from cold but from the specific fury of watching seven days of work burn. The water samples. The survey notes. Her original tracings of the buried river's course, inked in her own hand, the first maps anyone had ever made of water running under the Empty Reach.

A man stepped out ahead of the others. Tall, broad-shouldered, moving with an ease that didn't match the rifle slung across his chest. His hair was cropped short and going gray at the temples, and when he stopped ten paces from Silas, he smiled the way old friends smile at funerals.

"Seven years," the man said. "You look terrible, Oyelaran."

The name went through the camp like a bullet through glass.

Wren felt it land in her own chest. *Oyelaran.* Not Silas-the-tracker who'd hired them off a tavern board with honest coin and honest silence. Not the man who'd sat up with her three nights running while she redrew the river's course, saying nothing, just keeping the fire fed. *Oyelaran.*

"You know this man?" Odile asked quietly.

"I know exactly who this is," the enforcer said, answering for him. "Dae-Ho Min, resource enforcement, Meridian Concession Group. And this—" he gestured at Silas with two fingers, almost fond, "—is Silas Oyelaran. Formerly senior tracker, Meridian survey division. Formerly my partner. Formerly the man who walked out of a company town with a payroll wagon and forty thousand in scrip and vanished so clean we filed him under dead." He tilted his head. "You want to tell them the rest, or should I?"

"There isn't a rest," Silas said.

"There's always a rest." Dae-Ho turned to the crew, spreading his arms like a showman. "The rest is that everything he's told you is true except himself. There is a river under the Reach. It's real, it's mapped, and as of six o'clock this morning, it belongs to us. Your friend here led you straight to it. Best prospecting work I've ever seen. Pity about the permits."

Two enforcers pulled the map case from the ashes of the tent. Dae-Ho opened it, checked the contents against some memory, and nodded once.

"The rest of you are free to walk," he said. "Company transport will even drop you at Kettle Bend. Mr. Oyelaran stays. We have paperwork."

"We have a contract," Bram said. His voice was low, gravel over iron. "Signed. Witnessed."

"And I have a writ of seizure covering every claim staked in this basin, signed by a magistrate whose daughter works for our legal department." Dae-Ho smiled again. "Contracts are weather, friend. This is climate."

* * *

They were gone within the hour, taking the map, the seized supplies they didn't burn, and the trail south toward the outlet canyon. They left the crew a single water barrel and their own feet.

Wren didn't speak to Silas for four hours. She inventoried what remained instead—her hands doing arithmetic while her chest did something worse—and it wasn't until the sun stood straight overhead and the heat made lying still its own kind of pain that she finally turned on him.

He was sitting where he'd been sitting all morning, at the fire ring, facing the direction the convoy had gone. Watching nothing.

"Say it," he said, without turning.

"Which part? You've got a lot of names. I don't know which one you answer to."

"All of them, eventually."

"Odile says the payroll story is real." She crouched in front of him, forcing him to look at her or look away. "That Meridian never recovered that money. That it funded three years of land claims appeals in the northern basins—farmers, well cooperatives, people fighting concessions just like this one. Is that true?"

His jaw worked. "It's true."

"So you didn't steal it."

"I stole it. From them. Which is still stealing, depending on who's counting." He looked at her then, and his eyes were the flattest she'd ever seen them, the eyes of a man standing in the ruins of the only shelter he'd built in seven years. "I ran because I couldn't be that person anymore. The one who surveyed aquifers and then watched the company drain them and called it development. So I buried the name and took contracts tracking thieves, because thieves are simple. Things get taken, things get brought back. No geology required."

"And us?" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Were we simple? Was any of it real, or were we just the next wagon you needed driven?"

For a long moment he said nothing. Behind them, Bram was repacking the surviving gear with the deliberate calm of a man listening to every word, and Odile had stopped pretending to work.

"You drew that river," Silas said quietly. "Three nights, no sleep, by lantern light, because nobody else could read that prospector's hand. Nobody made you. And when we found the water under the stone, you cried, and you tried to hide it, and I pretended I didn't see." He shook his head slowly. "I've spent seven years being no one to everyone. You're the first people who made me want to be someone again. That's not something I know how to say better than that."

Wren stood. Her legs felt unsteady, but her voice, when it came, held.

"Dawn tomorrow," she said. "That's when the permits finalize. After that, Meridian can lawfully drain the outlet and every farm downstream of Kettle Bend goes dry inside a season."

"That's the shape of it."

"Then we have maybe eighteen hours, one barrel of water, and no map." She looked around the circle—at Odile, already pulling her instruments from the wreckage; at Bram, who had silently begun saddling the mules. "But I memorized the river's course. Every bend. Every fault line. In *my* head, where he can't seize it."

Silas stared at her. "Wren—"

"You said you wanted to bring it home." She slung her satchel across her shoulder. "So bring me. I'm the map now."

Bram swung up onto the lead mule and looked down at Silas with something that was almost, but not quite, a smile. "Well, Oyelaran? You coming, or do we leave you here with your paperwork?"

Odile was already walking. Silas rose slowly, like a man surfacing from deep water, and looked at each of them in turn—as if checking whether they were real, whether they'd still be real in another hour, another day.

"The outlet canyon," he said at last, and his voice was rough. "There's a route Dae-Ho won't expect. It runs through country I haven't set foot in since—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "Since before."

"Before what?" Wren asked.

"Before I was no one." He picked up the last water skin and slung it over his shoulder. "We move now. If we make the high pass by nightfall, we reach the outlet before his convoy does."

"And if we don't?"

Silas looked south, toward the canyon where a river waited under stone, and where a company waited above it.

"Then by this time tomorrow, I'll have lost two names instead of one," he said. "Let's not find out."

They moved out into the white heat of noon, five shadows and three mules, racing a deadline written in someone else's ink. Behind them, the smoke of their burned camp rose straight up into a sky with no clouds in it anywhere.

Ahead, Wren thought, there was water. And eighteen hours.

And a name that hadn't been spoken aloud in seven years—one Silas would have to say again, soon, in front of witnesses, if any of this was going to mean anything.

She just hoped he'd survive saying it.
