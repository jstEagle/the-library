## 4. The River Stays

They ran the last twelve miles in the dark, and the desert let them.

Silas had known the Empty Reach long enough to distrust its generosity, but tonight it gave them what it gave no one: a sky scoured clean of dust, a moon like a struck coin, and a wind at their backs that smelled faintly—impossibly—of wet stone. Wren rode beside him with the copied map spread across her saddle skirt, her fingers tracing the contour lines by feel more than sight. She hadn't slept since the camp burned. None of them had. But she'd redrawn the map from memory before they broke camp, every line of it, and when Silas had asked her why she'd bothered, she'd said, *Because he took the paper, not the river,* and something in his chest had unclenched for the first time in seven years.

Behind them came the mules, and behind the mules came Okonkwo's wagon with its cisterns empty and rattling, and behind the wagon came Marisol on her lame-legged bay, humming an old song under her breath like a woman going to a wedding instead of a war.

Dawn was the deadline. The concession permits expired when the sun cleared the eastern rim, and after that, the land belonged to whoever could prove claim fastest—which meant the company's surveyors would be at the outlet canyon by first light with their stakes and their wire and their lawyers' signatures already inked.

Which meant Silas had to be there first.

* * *

The outlet canyon opened before them like a throat.

He reined up on the last ridge while the sky was still the color of iron, and looked down at the place where the buried river finally surfaced—a seep at first, then a runnel, then a narrow green vein threading through red rock toward the flats where Harrow's Crossing sat dying forty miles downstream. The water was there. It had always been there, running patient under stone while men above it measured everything but depth.

And Dae-Ho was there too.

Six vehicles strung across the canyon mouth, headlights off, engines idling. Men moving between them with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told no. And at the center, standing in the gray pre-dawn with his hands in his pockets, watching the ridge line as if he'd been expecting this all along—

Silas knew that posture. He'd eaten beside that posture for eleven years, slept back-to-back with it through three sand seasons, bled next to it when the Kessel raid went wrong. Dae-Ho didn't look surprised. He looked tired.

"He's waiting," Wren said quietly.

"He's stalling." Silas swung down. "Permits die at sunrise. Every minute he keeps us talking is a minute his surveyors stake the outlet."

"Then why are we talking?"

Because some debts don't get paid in miles. Silas checked the old revolver at his hip—heavier than it used to be, or maybe his hand had gotten lighter—and started down the slope.

* * *

The canyon floor was cold and smelled of creosote and, underneath it, that green mineral breath of living water. Dae-Ho met him halfway, alone, waving his men back to the vehicles. Up close he looked older than seven years should have made him. Gray at the temples now. A scar along his jaw Silas didn't recognize.

"You look terrible," Dae-Ho said.

"You should see the other guy."

"I did. I burned his camp." No apology in it. No gloating either. Just fact, laid out flat between them like a body on a table. "Give me the map, Silas. Or whatever you're calling yourself these days."

"I gave my word to a dying man."

"The man's dead. The word died with him."

"No," Silas said. "That's the whole point of a word."

Dae-Ho exhaled through his nose—the old sound, the one that meant *you're being impossible*, the one Silas had heard across a hundred campfires in another life. "The company pays me to secure this outlet. If I come back without it, I'm done. If I let you walk through, I'm worse than done. You understand what you're asking."

"I'm not asking."

Behind him, Silas heard the crew coming down the slope—Okonkwo's heavy tread, Marisol's uneven gait, Wren's quick light steps. They fanned out behind him without being told, without a word, and something about the simple geometry of it—four people who owed him nothing standing where he stood—made his throat tighten.

Dae-Ho saw it too. His eyes moved across each face, and then back to Silas, and something in them shifted. "Seven years," he said. "You vanish off the face of the earth. No word. Not even to me. And now you crawl out of a hole in the ground for a river nobody knew existed, with a cartographer and a mule driver and—" he squinted at Marisol, "—is that a washboard?"

"It's a zither," Marisol said.

"It's a washboard."

"Focus," Silas said.

But the absurdity cracked something open, the way it always had between them, and for one suspended moment they were just two tired men in a cold canyon with a job to do and only one way to do it. Then Dae-Ho's hand drifted toward his sidearm—not fast, not threatening, just settling—and Silas stepped forward into the space between them and did the thing he had spent seven years refusing to do.

He said his name.

"My name is Silas Oyelaran," he said, loud enough to carry to the vehicles, to the men leaning out of windows, to the ridgeline and the sky and anyone listening. "Ranger, second district, badge number 4471. I never resigned. I never surrendered my commission. Which means every acre this company has surveyed since the Reach annexation falls inside a protected watershed, and I am formally testifying to that fact, right here, right now, in front of witnesses." He turned slowly, letting them see his face. "Check the rolls. Check the seal. I'll wait."

The canyon went very quiet.

Wren made a small sound beside him—not shock; she'd known since the burning—but grief and pride braided together, and her hand found his sleeve and stayed there.

Dae-Ho stared at him. "You're insane. You know what claiming that name means? Hearings. Depositions. Your face on every notice board from here to the coast. You spent seven years burying yourself."

"I know."

"And you're doing it anyway. For a river."

"For them." Silas nodded back at the crew, at the wagon, at the forty miles of dying settlement downstream. "For a man who traded his last breath for a promise. For water that doesn't belong to any of us." He held Dae-Ho's gaze. "You can shoot me. But you'd be shooting a sworn ranger testifying on the record, in front of six of your own men, and the company would burn you alive to save itself. Or you can stand aside, and when the inquiry comes—and it will come—you tell them exactly what you saw: that you found the permit expired and the ranger already there."

The eastern rim was turning gold. Somewhere below the stone, the river ran on, indifferent to all of it.

Dae-Ho looked at him for a long time. Then he laughed—one short, broken note, half grief, half relief—and took his hand off the gun.

"Badge number 4471," he said. "You always were a sentimental bastard." He turned and walked back toward the vehicles, and over his shoulder: "Pull the stakes! We're leaving!" And then, quieter, almost lost in the engine noise: "Don't disappear again, Silas. Not without saying goodbye this time."

The convoy ground away up the far trail, taillights fading in the growing light, and Silas Oyelaran stood in the middle of the outlet canyon and felt seven years fall off him like a coat he'd forgotten he was wearing.

* * *

They opened the channel at noon.

It took Okonkwo's crowbar, two sticks of Marisol's blasting putty—she'd been carrying them the whole time, "for emergencies, or music"—and Wren reading the old prospector's notes aloud in a voice that shook only once. When the last stones came free, the river rose through the crack like something waking, spilled over the lip of the outlet, and ran silver and reckless down the flats toward Harrow's Crossing.

Nobody spoke for a while. There wasn't anything to say that the water wasn't already saying.

Marisol played the washboard. Okonkwe wept openly and denied it. Wren stood at the water's edge with her boots off and her notebook closed, and when Silas came to stand beside her, she didn't look at him.

"Silas Oyelaran," she said, trying the name out.

"That's me."

"It suits you better than the other one."

"The other one wasn't mine."

She nodded, and leaned her shoulder against his arm, and they watched the water go.

By evening the first riders had reached them from the Crossing—children, actually, sent ahead because children run fastest—screaming about the river, the *river*, actual water in the dry bed, and behind them the whole settlement would be packing wagons by lantern light. The inquiry would come, and the hearings, and the company's lawyers with their knives out. Let them come. He had testimony, witnesses, a map drawn twice over, and a crew that had seen him at his worst and followed him anyway.

That night they camped on the bank of a river that belonged to no one, and Silas wrote his real name in the ledger Wren kept, at the top of the page, and beneath it the words: *Carried home.*

He was still awake when Dae-Ho's message arrived—an old ranger's signal code, flashed once from the far ridge with a hand lantern, three short and one long, the sign they'd used in another life to mean *all clear, brother, go home.*

Silas watched the dark where the light had been for a long moment. Then he lay back against his saddle, under stars he no longer needed to hide from, and listened to the river run.
