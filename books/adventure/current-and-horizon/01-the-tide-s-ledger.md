## 1. The Tide's Ledger

The third letter that week came on pink paper, which Odile Okafor took as a personal insult. Pink was a color for birthday cards and bakery boxes, not for the words *FINAL NOTICE* set in a typeface designed by someone who hated joy.

They read it standing in the doorway of their rented room, one boot on, one boot still by the bed, because the envelope had been wedged under the door at an angle that suggested the courier had also once been a debt collector and knew exactly how to make an entrance.

"Twelve thousand," Odile said to the room. The room, which contained a camp stove, a cot, and a framed photograph of a man they were trying very hard not to think about, said nothing back. Rooms were good like that.

Twelve thousand in arrears across four creditors, plus the survey equipment they'd pawned and would need to buy back, plus the interest accruing on all of it with the cheerful relentlessness of mold in a wet season. Odile did the math again, hoping it had changed overnight. It hadn't. Math was loyal that way — the only thing in their life that was.

The photograph on the wall was of Dr. Emeka Okafor, standing on a barge in front of a trench that had made him famous and then infamous in roughly equal measure. *Fraud,* the journals had said. *Fabricated provenance,* the committees had said. *Dad,* Odile had said, age nineteen, and gotten no answer worth having. He'd died four years later with his reputation in ruins and his daughter's tuition unpaid, which was, Odile reflected, the most complete thing he'd ever done.

Odile put on the second boot. There was work. There was always work, if you were willing to do work nobody respectable wanted.

* * *

The concession office smelled of diesel and old coffee, and behind the desk sat a woman named Mrs. Adeyemi who processed salvage permits the way other people processed grief — thoroughly, without warmth, and with occasional flashes of unexpected tenderness.

"Okafor," she said, and let the name sit there like a fish on a scale.

"That's me."

"Any relation to—"

"No."

She looked at Odile over her glasses for a long moment, then stamped the contract anyway. "Routine survey and debris clearance, grid seven through twelve. The leaseholder wants the seabed mapped before the dredging company moves in. Pay is three hundred a day plus hazard supplement." She slid the paperwork across. "You'll be working under Veyra Maritime Holdings. Sign there, there, and — read that clause before you sign it, everyone always skips that clause."

Odile read the clause. It gave Veyra Holdings first claim on anything of "commercial or historical value" recovered from the concession, compensation to be determined "at the sole discretion of the leaseholder." Odile signed it anyway, because discretion was a luxury for people whose creditors wrote letters on white paper.

Grid seven through twelve. Odile knew those grids. Everyone in coastal archaeology knew those grids, the way everyone in a certain family knows which chair at the table nobody sits in anymore. It was the stretch of water where Emeka Okafor had claimed to have found a Bronze Age harbor — a claim so spectacular, so career-making, that when it collapsed it took him and eleven colleagues' trust down with it. The sea there had been surveyed six times since. It had produced nothing but sand, rusted anchor chain, and one very confused octopus.

Which was why the contract paid hazard rates. Nobody expected to find anything. The job was to make the seabed look officially empty so the dredgers could make it actually empty.

Fine. Odile could do empty. Empty was honest work.

* * *

The tide began going out on a Tuesday morning and simply — kept going.

Odile noticed it first as a wrongness in the light. They'd been running transect lines off the survey skiff since dawn, and around nine o'clock the shallows near the cliff base started doing something shallows weren't supposed to do: receding. Not the gentle breathing of normal tides but a long, steady withdrawal, like the sea taking a step back to look at something.

By noon, the fishing village up the coast was on the radio in three languages at once. By one, Odile had beached the skiff on the shingle below the cliffs and was walking out over seabed that hadn't seen air in living memory — maybe in any memory. Tide pools lay stranded and steaming. A wreck of some nineteenth-century coaster sat exposed to its keel, and Odile walked past it without slowing, because past it, at the base of the cliff where grid nine met grid ten, the retreating water had uncovered something else.

A cave mouth.

It was arched, deliberate, and sealed — or it had been. The low tide had dragged away the shingle bank that had choked it for centuries, and now a dark opening gaped in the rock, its rim dressed with fitted stones that no wave and no accident had ever placed. Someone had built this. Someone had wanted what was inside kept from the sea, and then, apparently, changed their mind about wanting it found, because the entrance had been filled and packed and forgotten.

Odile stood at the threshold for a long time, doing the arithmetic of fear. Tide tables said they had perhaps four hours before the water turned, and a record tide like this one would come back hungry, taking back everything it had lent. Inside that arch was either nothing — collapsed rubble, silt, disappointment — or something, and *something*, in Odile's experience, was the more dangerous of the two. Something had ruined their father. Something had a way of ruining whoever touched it next.

Their boots crunched on shingle. Odile told themselves they were just going to look. Just a quick assessment, for the survey report. *Anomaly noted at grid nine-ten, recommend further study.* Bureaucratic language. Safe language. Language that couldn't ruin anyone.

The passage went back maybe thirty meters, dry-floored and cold, and then opened into a chamber where Odile's headlamp found the walls and stopped understanding them.

Bronze.

Panels of it, riveted to the living rock, covering every surface the lamp touched — and not plain panels, not sheet metal hammered flat by some ancient plumber. Carved. Dense with figures: ships with prows like bird beaks, processions of people whose clothing belonged to no culture Odile could name, and lines of script running between them in characters that looked like someone had taken three different writing systems, put them in a bag, and shaken.

Odile's father had spent his whole career claiming a Bronze Age harbor here. The journals had laughed him into his grave. And the whole time, forty meters from his survey markers, the proof had been sitting behind a wall of shingle, waiting for the sea to change its mind.

Odile sat down on the cold floor of the chamber and laughed until it turned into something else, and then sat with that too, because there wasn't time for much of either.

Because here was the ledger, and Odile Okafor had learned to read ledgers young:

Asset: one bronze-lined chamber, contents beyond price.
Liability: one signed contract giving Veyra Holdings "sole discretion" over anything of value.
Liability: four hours until the tide returned.
Liability: the name Okafor, attached to any claim from these grids, guaranteed to draw every fraud-hunter and rival academic within radio range like blood in water.

And underneath all of it, in small handwriting, the entry Odile kept trying not to see:

Asset: possibly, finally, a way out.

The lamp flickered. Somewhere above, gulls were screaming the way gulls scream when the water starts coming home. Odile photographed everything — every panel, every line of script, wide shots, detail shots, the entrance, the approach — hands steadier than they had any right to be. Then they climbed back out into the gray afternoon light and stood watching the sea crawl toward the cave mouth like a landlord coming to inspect.

Four hours had become two. Two had become less.

Odile got on the radio and called the only person they knew who could build anything, fix anything, and keep his mouth shut about all of it. Tomas picked up on the fourth ring, mid-yawn.

"Tomas. How do you feel about a salvage job?"

A pause. "Define 'salvage.'"

"The kind where we might have to move something heavy, quickly, quietly, and possibly lie about it afterward."

Another pause, longer. Then, in the tone of a man who had decided years ago that Odile Okafor was going to be the death of him and had made peace with it: "I'll bring the winch."

Odile cut the call and looked back at the water rising over the stones their father had died twenty meters from never finding. Twelve thousand in debt. One impossible discovery. One corrupt leaseholder with first claim on everything.

The tide came in, patient and total, and closed the door.

Odile turned toward town, already composing the lie they'd tell the crew, and hating themselves for how good at it they were getting.
