## 2. Concession

The trick to hiring people when you have no money, Odile had learned, was to be very specific about the work and very vague about everything else.

"Routine bathymetric survey," they told Ines Marchetti, who had run dive boats out of three different countries and been politely asked to leave two of them. "Six weeks. Hazard pay if the weather turns."

Ines looked at the contract, then at Odile, then back at the contract, the way a cat looks at a closed door it knows contains fish. "Your father's old concession," she said.

"The same rocks."

"The same rocks that ended his career."

"Different tide, though."

Ines signed. That was the thing about the broken ones — they didn't ask enough questions, because they recognized the shape of someone else's desperation and preferred not to look at it directly. Odile counted on that, and hated themselves for counting on it, and did it anyway.

Tomas Reyes came next, an engineer with salt-cured hands and a laugh like a diesel engine turning over. He'd built salvage rigs in water too deep for the insurance companies to bother with. He asked exactly one question: "Is anything down there going to try to kill us?"

"Tides," Odile said. "Rocks. Possibly my family's reputation."

"So, nothing new." He shook hands like he meant it.

The rest filled in over four days — Priya on sonar, a pair of brothers named Okonkwo who could fix anything short of a broken heart, and a cook called Mabel whose coffee was widely considered a controlled substance. Nine souls, one leased boat, six weeks of permits, and a lie sitting in the middle of it all like ballast nobody had asked for.

* * *

**TOMAS**

Tomas had worked for men who lied before, and he'd learned to read the weather of it. Liars got precise. They over-explained. They flinched at maps.

Odile Okafor did none of these things, which worried Tomas more than lying would have. Odile simply went quiet whenever anyone mentioned the cave mouth the low tide had opened on the north face of the concession — quiet in the way of a person holding something very carefully, very still, so it doesn't spill.

He'd seen them stand at the rail the first evening, looking north, and he'd seen their hand move — just slightly — toward the pocket where they kept whatever it was they'd brought up from inside. A rubbing? A fragment? Tomas didn't ask. You didn't ask a captain about the thing in their pocket. You waited for the sea to tell you instead, because the sea always told you eventually, usually at the worst possible moment.

What he did do was check the survey equipment twice as often as necessary, because if Odile wasn't telling them something, then the something was worth protecting, and Tomas protected things. It was the whole of his personality, more or less. Ask his ex-wife.

* * *

Veyra arrived on the ninth day, which was eight days sooner than Odile had hoped and precisely as bad as they'd feared.

She came by helicopter, which felt like showing off, and stepped onto the deck of the *Meridian's Fancy* in shoes that had never once been wet. Her company's name was on the concession paperwork in letters small enough to deny and large enough to own.

"Okafor." She said the surname like she was checking it against an invoice. "You've assembled quite the crew for a geological survey."

"We're thorough."

"Mm." She walked the deck without being invited, trailing one finger along the rail, and stopped at the sonar station where Priya sat very still. "Show me your grid."

Priya looked at Odile. Odile nodded. Veyra studied the overlay for a long moment — the careful, boring lattice of a legitimate survey — and then her eyes came up, and they were not bored at all.

"You've excluded the north quadrant."

"Unstable rock. Rockfall risk from the low-tide event. I pulled it from the survey area pending assessment."

"Pending *your* assessment."

"I'm the one holding the permit."

Something moved behind Veyra's face — not anger; appetite, briefly unhidden, like a fish flashing under grey water. "My lawyers will want the assessment expedited. I'll send a team to assist. Consider it a courtesy."

"Consider it declined."

"Ms. Okafor." She smiled, and it was almost warm, which was the frightening part. "I own the ground under your feet, the paper in your hand, and the bank that owns your debts. The last one is my favorite. Do you know what happens to a concession lease when the lessee defaults? It reverts. Quickly. Quietly. With all findings intact." She turned for the gangway, then paused. "Your father sold out for less than you owe. I'd hate to see the family tradition interrupted."

Then she was gone, thumping away in her dry shoes, and the whole crew stood there pretending they hadn't heard, which is its own kind of loyalty, or its own kind of dread. Odile couldn't tell which yet.

* * *

That night the lie curdled.

It happened at dinner, which was how the worst things happened on the *Fancy*. Mabel had made stew, and the Okonkwo brothers were arguing about football, and Ines was telling a story about a port official in Valletta that could not possibly be true, and everyone was laughing, and Odile sat in the middle of it feeling the lie press against their ribs like a second heart.

Because here was the arithmetic Veyra had laid bare: the find in the cave was worth enough to clear every debt Odile had, with change left over. And the find belonged — legally, technically, in the eyes of every document Veyra's lawyers could produce — to the woman who owned the ground. Selling it to her meant the crew got paid and Odile walked free. Keeping it from her meant fighting a war Odile could not afford, with people who had signed on for rock samples and were now, unknowingly, soldiers.

"Oi." Ines kicked Odile's boot under the table. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're doing math instead of eating. Bad math. I can tell by the jaw."

Laughter around the table. Mabel refilled Odile's bowl without asking. Tomas caught Odile's eye across the lamplight and held it a half-second longer than comfortable — not accusatory, just *present*, the way good engineers are present, noticing the vibration before the machine fails.

"North quadrant tomorrow," Odile said, and heard their own voice go precise, over-explaining, flinching. "Full assessment. Tide window's at dawn, so we'll want eyes on the cave mouth before the water comes back."

"Cave mouth?" Priya said. "There's a cave?"

"Sea-cut fissure. Probably nothing." Probably bronze, thought Odile. Probably history. Probably the end of me either way.

Tomas said nothing at all, which from Tomas was a paragraph.

* * *

**TOMAS**

Later, in the engine room, Tomas found Odile sitting on an ammo crate next to the generator, looking like a person who had been carrying furniture up stairs all day.

"You lied to Priya tonight," Tomas said. No accusation in it. Just calibration.

Odile didn't deny it. That was the thing about them — they were a terrible liar in person and a magnificent one on paper, and the gap between those two facts was where all their suffering lived.

"There's something in that cave," Odile said finally. "And if I tell you what it is, you're all in danger of wanting it. Or of being wanted for it. And I don't—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "I've never had a crew before. Not really. I've had employees. This is different and I don't know how to do it and I'm afraid I'll ruin it by telling the truth and ruin it worse by lying."

Tomas wiped his hands on a rag for a long time, buying himself the silence.

"Here's what I know," he said. "Whatever's in your pocket, you've been carrying it since day one and it hasn't gotten lighter. Second: Veyra's people will come whether we know what we're protecting or not. So the only question that matters is whether we're protecting it *with* you or *despite* you." He stood, cracked his neck. "Third: I fixed the number-three pump. It was making a noise."

"That's it?"

"For tonight." At the hatch he paused. "Dawn's early, Captain. Whatever you're deciding, decide it before the water decides for you."

* * *

Odile stood the midnight watch alone, which was against every rule Ines had ever stated and several she hadn't. The north face of the concession was a black line under a blacker sky, and somewhere behind that line, in the dark the sea had kept sealed for longer than any nation had existed, the bronze mechanism waited — patient as debt, older than greed.

They took out the rubbing they'd made that first day: the curve of carved metal, the script no one living could read, pressed into paper by a shaking hand. Their father had spent thirty years chasing this coast and died owing money to it. Odile had spent thirty years running from the chase and arrived owing more.

The tide was coming back in. By morning the cave mouth would be underwater again, accessible only on the rarest lows, guarded by schedule and luck.

By dawn, Veyra's "assessment team" would be arriving by barge.

Odile folded the rubbing away, picked up the radio, and woke the whole crew — every bunk, every hammock, Mabel mid-snore — because some truths you couldn't ration out anymore.

"All hands," they said. "Change of plans. What we're really here for—"

And out of the north, across the black water, came the sound of engines that were not theirs.
