## 3. What the Water Keeps

Tomas had fixed engines in war zones, monasteries, and one memorable week aboard a floating casino run by ex-nuns, so he liked to think he was hard to rattle. But when the black inflatable came around the headland at four in the morning — no running lights, no hail, just the low growl of a big outboard being deliberately rude about it — he set down his coffee and said, to nobody, "Ah. There it is."

He'd known for three days. You couldn't spend twenty years listening to machines without learning to listen to people too, and Odile Okafor lied the way a bad gasket leaks: not all at once, but constantly, and worse under pressure. The survey reports that weren't surveys. The dive gear ordered in quantities no geological survey had ever needed. The way Odile went quiet whenever anyone asked what exactly they were looking for.

The inflatable grounded on the shingle below the camp with a sound like a dropped toolbox. Six men got out. They wore matching jackets with the Veyra Maritime crest on the breast, which Tomas found almost touching — mercenaries who dressed for the annual report.

"Morning," said their leader, a broad man with the cheerful face of someone who enjoyed his work. "Ms. Veyra sends her regards. She'd like to see the site."

"It's four in the morning," Tomas said.

"She's an early riser."

Odile came out of the survey tent pulling a jacket over sleep-rumpled clothes, and Tomas watched them do the arithmetic — six men, one cave, forty minutes until the crew woke — and arrive at the wrong answer.

"Of course," Odile said, smooth as poured concrete. "Let me show you around."

* * *

The trouble was that the cave didn't care whose morning it was.

The record tide had gone out two nights ago and hadn't fully come back — some pressure thing offshore, the kind of anomaly that made Tomas's instruments sulk — and the sea cave sat open like a held breath. Inside, past the throat of wet rock, was the chamber. And in the chamber, half-drowned in water so still it looked solid, stood the mechanism: bronze rings within rings, taller than a man, crusted with fourteen centuries of sea life except where Odile had already begun to clean it, revealing metalwork of a precision that made Tomas's hands itch and his stomach drop in equal measure. Nothing from that era should have existed here. Nothing from *any* era, arguably. It was the kind of find that ended careers or started religions, depending on who got to it first.

Veyra's men reached it in eleven minutes, because of course they'd brought lights, because of course Veyra had known exactly where to look.

"There she is," the cheerful man breathed, sweeping his lamp across the bronze. Rings caught the light and threw it back green-gold, and for a moment even the mercenaries went quiet, the way people do in churches and engine rooms.

Then somebody's boot found the wrong stone.

Tomas heard it before he understood it — a deep, structural groan from the back of the chamber, the sound of a wall that had been holding back the sea for a very long time deciding, on reflection, to stop. A crack ran up the far side of the chamber. Water began coming through it not in a trickle but in a *decision*, a fat rope of grey-green pressure that hit the pool and sent the whole surface shivering.

"The inner seal," Odile said, white-faced. "It breached. That chamber's been pressurized against the outer rock for—"

"Congratulations," said the cheerful man, and then he ruined the poetry of the moment by keying his radio. "Ma'am? We've got it. We've got it exposed."

And that was when Tomas understood the real problem, the one worse than armed men and collapsing caves: the air. Fourteen centuries underwater, sealed away from oxygen, and now the bronze stood in open air, weeping salt, the corrosion blooming across its surface like frost forming in fast-forward. He could see it happening. Green eating gold. Every minute out of the water was a minute the thing died a little.

"Get your people off my site," Odile said, and there was something in their voice Tomas had never heard before — not the broker's charm, not the debtor's flinch. Something older and harder, dredged up from wherever Odile kept the parts of themselves they didn't spend.

"Your site," the cheerful man said, amused. "That's generous, coming from someone leasing it."

The wall gave another groan. Somewhere above, rock shifted.

* * *

They retreated to the camp, both sides, because the cave was actively trying to kill everyone equally and even mercenaries respect a neutral party. Veyra's men posted themselves at the cave mouth with the patience of people being paid by the hour. Odile stood at the edge of camp looking at the sea, and Tomas walked over and stood next to them, because someone had to say it and he'd apparently volunteered.

"You lied to us," Tomas said. "To me. Three weeks of 'routine survey.' I calibrated equipment for you. I told Priya this was safe work. I told *myself* this was safe work."

"I know."

"You were going to sell it." Not a question. "Pay off the debt, disappear, send postcards."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. The eastern sky was going the color of old pewter. "Yes," they said finally. "That was the plan. It was a good plan. It was a plan with numbers in it." They turned, and Tomas saw that whatever Odile had been bracing for — anger, departure, the whole familiar choreography of being left — was already happening behind their eyes, rehearsed and expected. "I'm sorry. I'll sign whatever separation paperwork you want. Nobody has to be part of what comes next."

"What comes next," Tomas said slowly, "being Veyra hauling your discovery out in pieces while the sea eats the rest?"

"Being me figuring out how to stop that."

"With what? Good intentions?" Tomas jerked his chin toward the cave mouth, where the guards waited. "Listen to me, Okafor, because I'm only going to say this once, and I want you to hear how stupid it is before I say I'm doing it anyway. I have worked for people who saw me as a line item my entire career. You included, apparently, for three weeks. But three weeks ago you also learned my daughter's name. You remembered it. You asked about her recital. And I have been waiting my whole adult life for someone to betray me *and* apologize for it in the same breath, and you're the first one who managed both." He stuck out his hand. "So. What comes next?"

Odile stared at the hand like it might be another lie, this one aimed at them. Then they took it, and something in their shoulders came down half an inch, like a load finally distributed where it belonged.

"We flood it," Odile said. "Properly. Controlled demolition of the outer seal — the whole chamber goes under, stays under, corrosion stops, and Veyra gets a hole full of ocean instead of a museum wing."

"You're destroying the find of the century."

"I'm keeping it." Odile's jaw set. "There's a difference. It keeps better down there than it ever will up here, and it keeps better down there than it would in her vault, and if the only way it survives is where nobody can touch it — then that's the price. I spent my whole life watching my father dig things up and call it saving them while everything around him fell apart. I'm not doing the dig. I'm doing the keeping."

Tomas nodded slowly. "I'm going to need explosives, which I don't have, and time, which we also don't have."

"No," said a voice behind them, "but you've got me, and I've been rigging charges since before you two met."

Priya stood there in her coat, arms crossed, and behind her, yawning and unimpressed, the rest of the crew — every one of them, drawn by the shouting, every one of them staying.

"How long have you all been listening?" Odile asked.

"Long enough," said Priya. "Move. We've got maybe two hours before Veyra's barge arrives, and I'd like to be done lying to each other by breakfast."

* * *

What followed was, Tomas would later insist, the finest hour of his professional life, conducted mostly underwater, mostly illegally, and entirely too fast. While Priya talked paperwork at the guards — permits, jurisdiction, a forged injunction she produced from somewhere with genuine flourish — Tomas and Odile swam the flooded approach passage with charges and line, placing them along the weakened outer seal with the tenderness of people tucking something into bed.

At the chamber itself, Odile paused, one hand flat against the bronze, the corrosion creeping green under their fingers.

"Fourteen hundred years," they said quietly. "And everyone who finds it wants to own it."

"Everyone but us," Tomas said.

"Yeah." Odile pushed off the metal, and their smile in the lamplight was the first honest one Tomas had seen from them. "Everyone but us."

They swam out together as the first grey light came up — and with it, on the horizon, the running lights of Veyra's salvage barge, coming fast.

"Charges are set," Tomas said, treading water. "One question. When she asks why there's nothing left to salvage — what do we tell her?"

Odile looked at the barge, then at the cave, then at the ragged line of their crew gathered on the shingle above, waiting.

"The truth," they said. "For once. Just — after the explosion."
