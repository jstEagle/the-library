## 4. Horizon Line

The tide came back at 3:47 in the morning, and Odile Okafor was there to meet it.

Not with a shovel. Not with a winch or a crate or the bronze heart of a civilization that had waited three thousand years under the rock to be found. With a clipboard.

"Read it again," said Tomas, who was standing hip-deep in the surf holding the flood line like a man holding a leash on something enormous and mostly asleep. "I want to hear you say it out loud one more time. It's my favorite thing anyone's ever said."

Odile held the paper up to the work light, though they had memorized it days ago. "Concession 114-B, marine salvage survey, permit expires at first civil dawn. All recovered materials must be removed from site by that time. Materials remaining after expiration revert to—" they paused for effect, because Tomas liked the pause— "the sea."

"The sea," Tomas repeated reverently. "Beautifully written. Whoever drafted that had poetry in them."

"Veyra's lawyers drafted it."

"Then Veyra's lawyers have poetry in them, and I stand by what I said."

Behind them, up on the shingle, the rest of the crew was doing the last of it: Priya coiling hose, Maren logging every valve turn in a hand so neat it looked typeset, old Ferro sitting on an overturned dinghy with his bad knee propped on a crate, supervising the way old men supervise, which is to say by existing loudly in the vicinity. The pumps had been running since midnight. The inner chamber was filling. By now the water would be climbing the walls of the antechamber, finding the seams the ancients had sealed with such patience, settling over the mechanism like a blanket drawn up to its chin.

Three thousand years dry, then wet again, forever. It felt less like destruction than like tucking something in.

* * *

The barge arrived at four.

They saw its lights first, out past the point — a string of white lamps strung across the black water like a necklace someone had dropped. Then the low diesel growl. Then the searchlight, swinging across the cave mouth and pinning them all like moths.

A voice came over a loudhailer, tinny and furious. "This is the *Meridian Star*, operating under charter to Corvane Maritime Holdings. You are obstructing a licensed recovery operation. Stand away from the site."

Odile waded forward until the water was at their thighs and cupped their hands around their mouth.

"You're welcome to check our permits!" they called back. "We're very legal!"

There was a silence on the water. Somewhere behind Odile, Ferro snorted loud enough to startle gulls.

"We know what's in that cave," the loudhailer said.

"No," Odile said, pleasantly, "you don't. Nobody does. That's rather the point." They lifted the clipboard. "But I'll tell you what *is* in that cave, as of forty minutes ago: seawater. Rising at approximately nine centimeters per hour through channels your divers will never find in the dark. Your permit application lists 'recovery of antiquities' as its purpose. There are no antiquities. There is a flooded karst formation of no archaeological significance whatsoever, which we will be documenting in a report so boring that two people have already fallen asleep reading the draft."

"That's true," Priya called from shore. "I've read it twice. Both times against my will."

"I'm asking you formally," the voice said, and now Odile could hear the strain under it, the sound of a man whose bonus was evaporating with the tide, "to stand down."

"And I'm asking *you* formally," Odile said, "to note the time. First civil dawn is at 5:12. Our permit expires then. Yours doesn't begin until the regional office opens at nine — and I checked, because I've spent the last week becoming the world's leading expert on paperwork I despise. Your authorization requires daylight operations and a marine archaeologist on board. Is Dr. Ilves aboard? Because if she is, she'll tell you exactly what I told her on the phone Tuesday night, which is that any intervention before the chamber equalizes risks a collapse that would bury your divers and your reputation together."

Another silence. Longer this time. The searchlight wavered, found Odile's face, held there.

Odile did not flinch. This was, they reflected, possibly the first time in their adult life they had not flinched, and it was remarkable how easy it was once you stopped having anything left to lose. The debt was settled — that was the other thing, the quiet miracle of it, the money wired at midnight from the sale of the survey equipment and the boat and every scrap of Odile's father's collection except the brass compass, which Odile kept, because some inheritances were worth more as ballast than as currency. The number had been ugly. The account balance afterward had been zero. Zero, Odile had discovered, was the most restful number there was. Nothing below you. Nothing owed. Just the water and the crew and the coming light.

"Your move," Odile said, not loudly. The sea carried it anyway.

The searchlight clicked off. The barge sat on the horizon for another twenty minutes, lights burning, engines idling, a monument to a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. Then, at 4:51, it turned, slow and graceless, and began the long sulky trip back around the point.

Maren started clapping. Then everyone did, even Ferro, who clapped the way he did everything, grudgingly and slightly out of rhythm, and Tomas grabbed Odile around the shoulders and shook them until their teeth rattled.

"You held off a salvage barge," Tomas said, "with a clipboard."

"With a *correct* clipboard," Odile said. "There's a difference."

* * *

At 5:12 the sun came up the color of hammered copper, and the tide finished what the pumps had started.

They stood on the shingle and watched it. There wasn't much to see — the cave mouth swallowed the water without ceremony, a dark seam drinking the bright morning sea, and within the hour even the seam was only a shadow, and then not even that. The waves went about their business. A cormorant landed on the rocks above where the entrance had been and began, with tremendous indifference, to dry its wings.

"It's still down there," Priya said quietly. "All of it. Whole."

"That's the part I keep thinking about," Maren said. "It's not gone. It's just *nobody's*. Three thousand years nobody's, starting today."

Odile thought about their father, who had spent his whole life clawing things out of the ground and calling it truth, and who had died owing everyone and owning nothing worth keeping. Odile had spent years terrified of becoming him — of the same hunger wearing a different face. And here was the strange medicine of it: the way out of the family curse hadn't been to dig harder or hold tighter. It had been to let the water come.

* * *

The report published in October. *Sedimentology and Karst Hydrology of Concession 114-B: A Preliminary Survey.* Forty-one pages. Odile had worked hard on making it dull — had sanded every interesting edge off it personally, had invented a limestone deposit so aggressively unremarkable that a reviewer in the field journal described it as "thorough" and meant it as an insult. Two citations in eighteen months. One was Odile's own.

Tomas framed the review and hung it in the galley of the new boat, which was smaller than the old one and better in every way that mattered, because it belonged to five people instead of a bank.

"So," Tomas said one evening, leaning in the galley doorway while Odile pretended to study charts, "Veyra made an offer last week. Through intermediaries. Very discreet. She wants to fund a proper expedition. Full permits, real equipment, your name leading it." He paused. "She used the word 'legacy.'"

Odile didn't look up from the chart. "What did you tell her?"

"I told her I'd pass it along." Tomas grinned. "I also told her you'd say no, and I put twenty dollars on it with Ferro."

"You bet on me?"

"Ferro bet you'd say yes. He says hunger like yours doesn't quit, it just changes address." Tomas fished a crumpled bill from his pocket and slapped it on the chart table. "I said hunger's got nothing to do with it anymore. I said you already found the thing people spend their whole lives digging for, and it doesn't fit in a museum."

Odile looked at the twenty dollars for a long moment. Outside, the harbor lights were coming on, one by one, and the tide was turning, patient and enormous, keeping everything it had ever been given.

"Tell Ferro he's half right," Odile said finally, and reached for the pen. "The hunger doesn't quit. But it eats different now."

Below the chart, taped to the bulkhead where the whole crew could see it, was the only photograph taken during the entire season: five muddy people on a gray beach, squinting into a sunrise, guarding absolutely nothing at all.
