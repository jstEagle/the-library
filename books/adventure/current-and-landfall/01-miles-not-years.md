## 1. Miles, Not Years

Three hundred and eleven miles since the last time Wren Sallow had gotten the better of him, and Casper Nakamura counted every one of them the way other men counted years.

He'd learned the habit from his mentor, back when he was still a cartographer's apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a head full of the Republic's borrowed grandeur. *Distance is honest*, old Master Corvel used to say, tapping a surveyor's chain against the drafting table. *Time lies to you. Distance never does.* It had sounded like wisdom then. Now it was just arithmetic, and arithmetic was easier than thinking about what else he'd lost.

The flats south of the Karst River were the color of old bone that morning, cracked clay stretching to a horizon so flat it looked drawn with a rule. Casper rode a dun mare named Tally who had opinions about everything and expressed them mostly through her ears. Her left ear swiveled back at him now, which meant she thought they were wasting time.

"We're not wasting time," he told her. "We're being thorough."

Tally flicked the ear again. Disagreement. Three years of riding alone and he'd started holding conversations with his horse, which was either a sign of deep self-sufficiency or deep trouble, depending on which of the settlement women you asked. Most of them asked why he didn't stay put somewhere. He never had a good answer that wasn't also a true one: because staying put meant unpacking, and unpacking meant admitting he intended to remain in a world that had already taken everything he'd packed for.

He tracked Wren Sallow the way he did everything now—methodically, at a distance, without hope of catching her and without quite wanting to.

It had started fourteen months ago as a job. A way-station keeper in Dry Fork had offered twenty silver for the recovery of a stolen strongbox, and Casper had taken it because twenty silver bought feed and flour and another month of not having to decide what he was doing with his life. The thief's trail had been easy to read even then. She traveled light, slept cold, and left sign like a woman writing letters she knew someone would find—a boot print too clean here, a campfire ring built from stones arranged just slightly too neatly there. Sloppy on purpose, or careless by nature. He hadn't decided which yet.

Then the trail had led somewhere interesting, and interesting was dangerous, and Casper had kept following anyway.

She stole from people who deserved it. That was the whole of it. Tax collectors who padded their levies. Way-station keepers who sold water at famine prices. A caravan master in Salt Hollow who'd been running indentured girls east under bills of lading marked "dry goods." Casper had watched from a ridge while she emptied that man's lockbox and left a chalk drawing on his wagon door—a small bird, wings spread, done in four strokes. She'd been gone an hour before the man found it and went purple shouting at men who were as guilty as he was.

Casper should have turned back then. Twenty silver didn't cover whatever came next. But he'd sat on the ridge with his canteen warm against his hip and realized he couldn't remember the last time he'd wanted anything, and here, suddenly, was a want: to know who drew that bird.

So he followed. Through the salt pans where the ground sang at night when the temperature dropped. Through the ghost orchards of the old irrigation colonies, dead trees still standing in their dead rows like congregations waiting for a god who'd been recalled. Across the Karst at the shallows, where he'd paid a ferryman three days' food to take them both over and spent the crossing watching the water for signs of the flash floods the season promised.

And always, always, counting miles. Two hundred. Two hundred fifty. Three hundred and eleven.

The spring was ahead, or had been once. His map said so—the good map, the one he'd drawn himself in the first year after Veyra fell, when mapping the borderlands had been the only prayer he knew how to say. He'd called it Coldwater Spring on his own draft because the official name, *Veyran Relief Station Nine*, had made something twist in his chest. The Republic had built relief stations all through these flats in its last confident decade, wells and cisterns and shade walls for travelers, each one stamped with the seal: the river-serpent coiled around a sheaf of grain. Plenty to eat, plenty to drink, plenty of Veyra in every drop.

The Republic was three years dead. Its water stations died slower.

Tally smelled it before he saw it—ears forward now, nostrils working—and picked up her pace without being asked, which for Tally was practically enthusiasm. The station rose out of the flats a quarter mile on: a low stone structure with its shade wall half-collapsed, the serpent-and-grain seal above the arch worn to a suggestion, a face seen through fog. The cistern beside it stood open to the sky.

Dry. Of course dry. But the mud at its bottom would hold water longer than the surface showed, and animals knew it, and a thief traveling light through the flats in high summer would know it too.

Casper reined in at the edge of the station's shadow and sat there a moment, letting the silence settle. This was the part he was honest about liking—the approach, the reading of a place before he entered it. Out here nothing lied to him. Sign was sign. Water was water or it wasn't. A man could be competent in the desert in a way he could never be competent among people, and competence, Casper had decided, was the closest thing to peace a man like him got to have.

He dismounted and walked the station's perimeter first, the way Master Corvel had drilled into him. *Read the edges. The middle will tell you about itself.*

The edges told him plenty.

Boot prints in the clay pan east of the wall—small, narrow, a stride shorter than his own. A day old, maybe less; the wind hadn't yet softened their edges into suggestion. She'd come in from the northeast, which was wrong. Anyone sensible crossed the flats along the Karst terraces where the ground held occasional seep water. Coming in from the northeast meant coming from the direction of the old military road, which meant she'd been near the road, which meant—

He stopped himself. One thing at a time. Reading a trail was like reading a map: if you stared at the whole thing at once you saw only noise.

At the cistern he found more. She'd climbed down inside—scuff marks on the lip, handprints in the dried silt on the inner wall—and at the bottom, where the last damp clay held impressions the way wax holds a seal, a knee print and the clear arc of a cup or tin dipped twice. She'd found water. Not much. Enough.

Beside the prints, pressed into the clay near the cistern's lip where the sun hadn't reached, something pale. He crouched. A scrap of paper, torn small, deliberate—torn, not dropped, the edges knife-straight. On it, in a quick sure hand, a fragment of a line:

*...counted in miles, not years...*

Casper sat back on his heels.

Fourteen months of following this woman and she had never once acknowledged him. No note, no gesture, no chalk bird left where he'd find it. He'd told himself she didn't know he was there, though some nights, lying in his bedroll under more stars than seemed fair, he'd admitted the truth: nobody traveled that clean by accident. She knew. She'd always known. And she had chosen, until today, to say nothing.

Now she'd left him a sentence.

He turned the scrap over. Blank. He held it up to the light as if the rest of the words might be hiding in the fibers. Nothing. Just the fragment, in ink gone brown at the edges, torn from something longer—a letter, a journal, a page of a book. Something she'd carried long enough to tear a piece off and leave behind like a coin for a ferryman.

*Counted in miles, not years.*

His own phrase. Or close enough to it that his neck prickled. He'd said it aloud exactly once in three years, to a way-station keeper in Dry Fork, the night he took the recovery job. *I don't count days anymore,* he'd told the man. *I count miles. Miles are honest.* He'd been drunk on two cups of terrible date wine and talking to hear a human voice answer, and he'd never thought of it again.

Until now.

Tally stamped and blew air through her lips. He ignored her. He walked the station again, slower, and this time he found what he'd missed: at the base of the shade wall, tucked into the shadow of a fallen block, a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Not hidden. Placed. Set where a man entering the station's arch would walk within arm's reach of it.

He crouched and unwrapped it with the care of a man defusing something.

Inside: a strip of dried apricots. A twist of paper holding salt. And a folded page, written on both sides in that same quick sure hand.

*Casper Nakamura,* it began, and his own name in a stranger's handwriting did something strange to the morning.

*You've followed me 311 miles, give or take, and I figure we're past the point where pretending is useful. I know who you are. I've known since Dry Fork—you talk in your sleep, did you know that? You said a name. Corvel. You said it like an apology.*

*I'm leaving you this because tomorrow I cross the Karst lowlands and I won't be easy to follow, and because there's a thing in my possession I can't carry alone much longer. If you want your twenty silver, forget it. If you want to know why a tax collector in Marrow Bend paid me forty times that to steal a sealed chest and deliver it to a colonel named Ashgrave—and why I read the manifest first and ran—meet me at the drowned bell tower, two days north, where the old flood channel meets the river. Come alone. Come armed. I don't trust anyone, but I trust you to be exactly what you are.*

*P.S. The apricots are from the Salt Hollow caravan. Consider them interest on your twenty silver.*

Casper read it three times. The wind moved across the flats and made the dead station hum faintly, a sound like a held breath. Somewhere north, past the horizon's ruled line, the Karst River was gathering itself for the seasonal floods, and a woman who signed nothing and trusted no one was waiting for him at a ruin he'd mapped and never visited.

*A thing in my possession I can't carry alone.*

He should ride south. That was the sane arithmetic. Twenty silver, one thief, no contract worth the paper it wasn't written on, and a colonel's name sitting in the middle of it like a stone in a shoe. Ashgrave. He didn't know the name, and not knowing names was how a man stayed alive out here.

But he looked down at the page, at his own name written in a stranger's hand, and felt the thing he'd spent three years outrunning turn and face him at last: not danger. Company. Someone had watched him sleep and heard him say a dead man's name and had not run away from him. She'd left him fruit and salt, which was, he understood dimly, the oldest language of the flats—the language of travelers feeding travelers, older than any republic, older than any law.

Tally nudged his shoulder with her nose, then pointedly looked north.

"Yeah," Casper said. His voice came out rusty. "I see it."

He folded the letter into his coat, next to the map he'd drawn himself, and swung up into the saddle. Behind him the dead station hummed its held breath, and the seal above the arch—the serpent, the grain—watched him go with its worn stone eyes.

Two days north. He gave himself the distance instead of the hours, because miles were honest, and began to count.

He was nine miles out when he saw the river, far off across the flats, running brown and fast and wrong for the season—swollen early, carrying things.

Carrying, he would learn by nightfall, a chest.
