## 2. What the River Keeps

The rain started an hour before dawn and Casper Nakamura knew what it meant before he was fully awake: the river would come up angry.

He'd been camped in the bend country below the old ferry crossing, three days off his trail and hating every one of them. Wren Sallow's sign had gone cold at a dead spring two nights back—her mare's shoes cut deep, then nothing, as if she'd grown wings—and he'd swung south toward water on the logic that even thieves drank eventually. It was good logic. It was also the kind of logic that put you in a river valley when the sky split open over the Serpent Range, forty miles upstream, and sent everything it held down your throat at once.

He broke camp in the dark by feel, rolling his bedroll with hands that had done it ten thousand times, and led his horse, Pell, up out of the wash to the shelf of rock where he'd cached his gear. The rain came sideways, warm and gritty, smelling of wet stone and distant lightning. He counted the flashes between thunder the way his mentor had taught him to count everything—miles, days, seconds of distance between himself and trouble.

Four miles. Then three. Then the thunder stopped bothering to wait for the lightning at all.

By full light the river was a brown animal the width of a street, then the width of a field, tearing cottonwoods loose whole and sending them cartwheeling downstream like drowned men waving. Casper sat on his shelf with his knees drawn up and watched it, because there was nothing else to do, and because watching floods was a form of patience and patience was the only currency he'd never run short of.

*Day nine hundred and forty-one,* he thought, and then corrected himself, because that was the count from Veyra, from the night of the fires, and he wasn't supposed to keep that count anymore. He kept the other one instead. *Two hundred and six miles since the last town. Fourteen miles since Wren Sallow's trail went cold.*

Miles, not years. That was the rule. Years were how you measured a life you were living. Miles were how you measured one you were running through.

The flood crested near noon and began, slowly, grudgingly, to die.

* * *

It took until the following afternoon for the river to give back enough land to walk on. What it gave back was unrecognizable—a new country laid down overnight, silt and wreckage and the bones of the old banks showing through like ribs. Casper walked it with his boots tied around his neck and his trousers rolled to the knee, feeling the mud suck at him with each step, reading the ground the way he'd once read maps.

That was the thing nobody understood about being a cartographer's apprentice. You didn't stop reading terrain when you stopped drawing it. Every cut bank told him where the channel had been. Every drift line told him where the water had stood. And every object left behind told him a story about the night it arrived.

Most of the stories were ordinary. A dead ewe, bloated and pitiful. Half a corral fence from some ranch twenty miles upstream, its posts still holding the shape of somebody's labor. A child's doll, face-down in the silt, which he turned over and set upright against a stone without letting himself think about why.

Then, half-buried at the base of a shattered willow, wedged so deep it had clearly been anchored somewhere solid before the river tore it free: an iron-bound chest.

He saw the seal before he saw anything else.

Veyra. The double sunburst of the Republic, stamped in black wax under a lead disc, pressed into a document sleeve lashed to the chest's lid—the kind of official wrapping they used for courier freight, the kind that meant *this belongs to the state and the state is watching*. Except the Republic hadn't existed for three years. Except the wax was fresh. Except someone, somewhere, was still stamping things with the seal of a dead nation, and the river had just carried one of those things to Casper Nakamura's feet.

He stood in the mud for a long moment, water seeping cold into his rolled cuffs, and felt the routine he'd built around himself—nine hundred and forty-one days of it, mile after mile after mile—develop a crack straight down the middle.

Pell shifted behind him, blowing through her nostrils. The river muttered over its new gravel bars. Somewhere a killdeer cried its two-note cry, absurdly calm.

Casper knelt and dug.

* * *

The chest weighed as much as a man. He got it onto higher ground with his back screaming and his boots filling with silt, and then he sat beside it in the last of the daylight and made himself be slow about it, because slow was safe and fast was what got people killed, and also because some part of him already knew what was inside and wanted a few more minutes of not knowing.

The lock was a courier lock, Veyran military pattern, the kind that opened to a key or a strong arm. He had neither a key nor a crowbar, but he had a knife and three years of stubbornness, and the hasp gave way on the fourth try with a sound like a tooth coming out.

Inside, packed in oilcloth and straw:

A ledger, bound in grey leather, corners reinforced with brass.

A second document, folded twice, sealed with the same black sunburst.

And beneath them, wrapped separately, a map. His map. He knew it before he touched it—knew the weight of the paper, the particular blue-black of the ink he'd mixed himself in Master Oviden's workroom, the little compass rose he drew with a flaw in the northeast point because he'd always been careless with northeast. He'd drawn this. Three years ago, in another life, with clean hands and a full belly and no idea what the drawing would cost.

His mouth had gone dry. He made himself look at the manifest first.

The ledger opened to a page headed in a clerk's precise hand: *RECEIPT OF ASSETS — TREASURY OF THE REPUBLIC OF VEYRA — CONTINGENCY DISPOSITION.* Below it, columns. Gold specie, quantities estimated. Sealed bonds. Plate. And then a section titled *CUSTODIAL MANIFEST*, and beneath that, names.

Nine names. He read them the way you read a list of the dead, one at a time, braced.

Oviden, Maren. Cartographer-General.

Tessaly, Rho. Pass Warden, Serpent Range.

Odumane, Bekka. Sergeant, Third Rifles.

Sallow, Wren. [no station listed]

Three more he didn't recognize. And then, seventh down, in the same clerk's hand, no different from any other entry, no asterisk, no mark of shame or honor:

Nakamura, Casper. Apprentice, Office of Maps.

The world did not end. The river kept moving. The killdeer kept crying. But something in Casper's chest went very quiet and very cold, the way air goes before snow, and he understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own name on a grave marker, that the list was right about all the others too—that these were survivors, scattered survivors, people the Republic's death had missed somehow, and that someone had gone to the trouble of finding them all and writing them down.

Someone knew he was alive.

For three years, *no one knowing* had been the whole architecture of his life. No name on any roll. No trace past the border stations. He had burned his apprentice's credentials in a cookfire outside Veyra while the city glowed behind him, and he had spent every day since being nobody, and being nobody had hurt, but it had kept him breathing, and he had told himself the hurt was peace. Loneliness mistaken for peace—he could see that now, seeing it from the outside, the way you only see the shape of a wall when something knocks a hole in it.

Because this ledger was a hole. Through it he could see the thing he'd refused to look at: that *nobody* was a costume, and costumes come off, and somewhere in the world there was a hand that had reached across three years and a drowned river and pulled his name back into the light.

He unfolded the sealed document. The wax cracked under his thumb.

*To the custodians named herein:* it began, in the same clerk's hand. *If the Republic falls, the treasury shall be moved to the coordinates marked on the accompanying survey, there to be held in trust until such time as legitimate authority is restored. Each custodian named holds one-ninth claim and one-ninth duty. This instrument is the proof of both.*

Legitimate authority restored. He read the phrase twice and heard, underneath the clerk's neutral ink, the sound it would make in certain mouths. He'd heard men talk like that in the refugee camps in his first winter—men who talked about restoring things, reclaiming things, men whose eyes went bright and wrong when they said the Republic's name. A dead nation's treasury was just gold. But gold plus a list of legitimate claimants plus a seal and a document—

Gold plus legitimacy was a country. Or the seed of one. And seeds like that didn't sprout on their own. Somebody planted them.

He looked at the map again, really looked, and his stomach dropped through the mud beneath him, because he remembered drawing it. Remembered the day. Master Oviden had called him in, unusual in itself—she did her own surveys, guarded her own drafts—and she'd had him plot a route into the high country above the Serpent Range, to a basin she described from memory, and she'd watched him draw every line with an expression he'd been too young and too tired to read. He'd assumed it was a supply cache. He'd assumed a lot of things, that year, right up until the night the fires started and he ran.

The coordinates on the map were in a basin he knew. And now, three years later, with a thief's cold trail and a colonel he'd never heard of and a chest that had traveled by river into his exact path, he looked at his own careful lines and felt the first thin edge of a question he didn't want to ask:

Had Oviden given him true coordinates? Or had the finest cartographer in the Republic used her own apprentice to draw a lie?

* * *

He built a small fire in the lee of the willow root, mostly for the light, and sat with the ledger open on his knees while the stars came out over the wet country. Pell dozed behind him. The river ran lower and quieter, already forgetting what it had done.

Nine names. He turned the thought over like a stone, looking at its underside. Nine custodians, scattered across three years of ruin. Which meant eight other people out there had either received chests like this one or were supposed to. Which meant the chest in front of him was one of nine—or it was the only one, and the other eight names were decoration, or bait, or—

Or the chest had been *sent*. That was the thought that finally made him stand up and pace, mud sucking at his bare feet. Rivers didn't deliver things to specific people. But couriers did. And a courier carrying a chest marked with a dead republic's seal, traveling the river roads through country where a man named Casper Nakamura was known to ride patrol—

Wren Sallow. Her trail had gone cold at a dead spring fourteen miles north. She was a thief; he'd chased her across four counties for a string of jobs he'd never bothered to itemize. What if the job she'd been running wasn't theft at all? What if she was the delivery?

He laughed, once, without much humor in it. Nine hundred and forty-one days of being nobody, and the universe had waited for him to pull a chest out of a flood to inform him that he was somebody after all—somebody with a claim, a duty, a one-ninth share in a ghost. Somebody hunted, probably, though by whom he couldn't yet say. Somebody whose name was written down somewhere in a clerk's neat hand, waiting for the wrong eyes to find it.

He should burn it. That was the sensible move. Burn the ledger, burn the letter, burn the map, scatter the ash in the river, and go back to being no one. He'd gotten good at it. Three years of practice.

He crouched at the fire with the ledger in his hands and held a corner of the cover near the flame long enough to smell the leather begin to scorch.

Then he thought of the names again. Oviden, Maren. If she was alive—if any of them were alive—then this ledger was the only proof anywhere that they'd survived, that they existed, that the Republic's people were still owed something by the world. Burning it wouldn't erase him. It would just erase the evidence that anyone had ever tried to keep faith.

He pulled the ledger back from the fire and sat there shaking slightly, annoyed at himself, certain he was making a mistake, and unable to do otherwise.

Below him, far off in the dark to the north, a light flickered. Once. Twice. Deliberate—not a campfire's idle wavering but a signal, spaced and repeated, the kind of light that meant *we are here* to someone who knew the code.

Casper pinched out his fire and stood motionless in the dark with the chest at his feet, listening to the river and the killdeer and his own heart, doing the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since noon.

Someone had put this chest in the water. Someone was signaling from the direction of Wren Sallow's cold trail. Someone, somewhere, had written his name in a book and was now checking it off.

He loaded Pell in the dark, by touch, fast. Whatever came next, he would not be standing in the open when it arrived.

Fourteen miles north, the light blinked again—closer now, or maybe just clearer—and Casper Nakamura turned his horse toward it, because the trail he'd been following for nine days had just become the only road out of the trap he was standing in, and because some part of him, the part that had never stopped being an apprentice, needed to know who else was still alive to answer to their name.
