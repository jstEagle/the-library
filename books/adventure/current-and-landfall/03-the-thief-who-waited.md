## 3. The Thief Who Waited

Wren Sallow had been waiting in the rocks above the dead spring for two hours when the ranger finally came for her.

She'd watched him work the trail since dawn—read him the way she read any mark. He moved like a man who'd been alone so long he'd forgotten how to be anything else. Careful feet, patient hands, a habit of stopping to look back at nothing. She knew that look. It wasn't checking for pursuit. It was checking whether the world was still there.

He was good. Better than the last three the colonel had sent after her, and better than she'd expected from a desert ranger with dust in his collar and a week of stubble on his jaw. But he was tired, and tired men looked at water before they looked at high ground, and that was all she needed.

When he crouched at the spring's dry basin and ran his fingers through the silt, she stepped out of the shadow of the rocks with his own knife drawn from his bedroll—taken an hour ago while he slept, which still stung her pride more than it should have—and said, "You're sitting on my chest."

He didn't startle. That surprised her. He just turned, slow, and looked at the knife, then at her face, and something crossed his expression that she couldn't name until later. Recognition. Not of her. Of the situation. The particular shape of two people meeting at the end of a long chase neither of them wanted anymore.

"You're Wren Sallow," he said.

"And you're the man who's been following me across forty miles of nothing on half a canteen." She tipped the knife toward the saddlebags behind him. "The chest. Hand it over slow."

"It's not yours."

"Neither is it yours, ranger, but you've been carrying it like it's your firstborn, so let's not pretend either of us has clean hands here."

He stood. Unhurried. His hand didn't go for the weapon she hadn't taken, which told her he'd already noticed it missing and had decided, for reasons she'd spend days trying to understand, not to fight about it. "Casper Nakamura," he said. "Since we're doing names."

"I know who you are." She said it flat, and watched it land wrong—not the way a threat lands, but the way bad news lands. "Your name's in the chest, Nakamura. Page three. I read it."

That did move him. A small thing—a tightening around the eyes—but she'd made her living off small things, and she saw it plain as a footprint.

"You opened it."

"I open everything I steal. Occupational habit." She kept the knife up, mostly out of honesty; they both knew she wasn't going to use it. "Manifest. Sealed orders. And a list of names longer than my arm, written in a clerk's hand, with yours sitting in the middle of it like it belongs there. *Nakamura, Casper. Cartographic apprentice, second class.* So before you decide I'm the villain of this piece, maybe ask yourself why a Republic that burned three years ago is mailing treasure chests with your name on them down a river."

The wind moved through the dry wash, carrying grit and the smell of stone baking. He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him do the arithmetic—the same arithmetic she'd done four nights ago, crouched over the chest in a rented room in Perdition Flats with a candle burning down and her employer's seal staring up at her from the manifest's foot.

*Deliver to Colonel Ashgrave. Contents unexamined. Payment on receipt.*

She'd examined them anyway. Thieves who didn't look died ignorant, and ignorant thieves died often.

"The colonel hired you," Casper said.

"Hired me to steal it. From a courier caravan on the Salt Road, night of the flood moon. Clean job, good pay, no questions." She smiled without warmth. "I took the job because I don't ask questions. Then I opened the chest, and every question I never asked came due at once."

"What changed your mind?"

She could have lied. She was good at lying; it was most of her trade. But there was something about this man standing in the dirt with his own knife pointed at him and his name in a stranger's box that made lying feel like a waste of a rare thing—an honest audience.

"There was a girl on page eleven," Wren said. "Maret Oduya. Age six. Listed under *survivors, eastern districts*, next to her mother and grandmother. I know that name, Nakamura. Maret Oduya grew up. She's seventeen now, and she pulls barges on the Veyra cut, and she thinks everyone she loved died in the purge because nobody ever told her different. Somebody wrote her name in that chest so that someday somebody could tell her." She lowered the knife a few degrees. "And Colonel Ashgrave paid me to hand that to him sealed. You tell me what a man wants with a list of survivors that he can't afford to have it read first."

Casper didn't answer right away. He walked to the saddlebag, unbuckled it, and lifted the chest out with both hands—the same careful grip he'd used pulling it from the river mud, she'd learn later, though she only had his word for that part. He set it on a flat rock between them like a man laying cards on a table.

"Open it again," he said. "Show me."

"You haven't seen inside?"

"I pulled it out of a flood. I read the manifest through the lid gap when the seal cracked. That's all." He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the lid, where the Veyran seal sat stamped in black wax, intact except for one corner. "Three years I've run from everything with that seal on it. I wasn't in a hurry to open a box wearing it."

Wren understood that more than she wanted to. She'd spent the same three years running from nothing in particular, which amounted to the same thing.

She knelt and worked the wax loose with her thumbnail, the way she had in Perdition Flats, and lifted the lid.

Inside: oilcloth, wrapped tight. Under the oilcloth, three bundles. The manifest, written in a clerk's even hand, columns of names and districts and dates. A letter bearing the counterseal of the Office of Records—Veyra's last functioning bureaucracy, or its ghost. And a map, folded into eighths, drawn on survey vellum that made Casper go very still when he saw it.

"That's mine," he said quietly. "Or—it's copied from mine. Look at the margin notes. That hatching pattern in the northeast quadrant, the way the contour lines break around the scarp. That's Halven's school. My master's hand taught that hand."

"You drew a map to a treasury?"

"I drew a map. Three years ago, before the fall. Halven and I surveyed the old imperial storehouses in the Serpent Range for the Records Office. We were told it was inventory work." His voice went flat in a way she recognized—the voice people used when they were hearing their own past rearrange itself. "I never saw what they filed it under."

Wren watched him read the manifest. Watched his finger stop on his own name. Watched him sit with it, there in the dirt, a man finding himself listed among the possessions of a dead country.

"Why am I in here?" he asked, not really to her.

"Because somebody knew you lived." She said it plainly. "That's the whole point of a survivor roll, ranger. Somebody, somewhere, spent the last three years collecting proof that the people Ashgrave's war erased are still real. Names, districts, dates. That's not treasure. That's a census. That's—" she paused, hunting for the word, and landed on it with a strange reluctance, "—that's a country in a box. Or the bones of one."

"And Ashgrave wants it sealed and delivered."

"Ashgrave wants a lot of things. Legitimacy. An army that fights for a flag instead of a paycheck. A resurrected Veyra with him holding the seal." She'd pieced that together from fragments—the way he talked to his captains, the mercenary companies already sniffing north, the forged provenance papers she'd glimpsed once in his field tent before she was supposed to see them. "A survivor census is exactly what a man like that needs. Proof of continuity. *The Republic lives, and here is its roster, and here is its treasury, and here is its rightful heir, and by the way the rightful heir is me.*"

Casper looked at her. Really looked, for the first time—past the knife, past the chase, past whatever story he'd built about the thief he'd tracked for a hundred miles.

"You could have delivered it," he said. "Taken the pay. Been gone before anyone knew."

"Yeah." She closed the lid. "I keep asking myself why I didn't."

"And?"

"And I don't have an answer that survives daylight." She stood, brushing grit off her knees, uncomfortable in her own skin in a way she hadn't been since she was young enough to still believe in reasons. "Maybe it's the girl on page eleven. Maybe it's that Ashgrave's kind of money spends dirty. Maybe I'm just tired of being useful to men who burn things." She shrugged, and hated herself a little for shrugging. "Take your pick. They're all true enough."

*

They sat on opposite sides of the flat rock after that, the chest between them like a border, sharing water from his canteen because hers had gone dry two days ago and pride was a luxury she'd stopped affording somewhere around the second canyon.

It was the strangest negotiation she'd ever conducted, because neither of them named terms. They just talked, and the talking became the terms.

He told her about tracking her—how long, how far, and finally, when she pressed, why. Not for the chest. He'd picked up her trail before the theft, chasing a string of burglaries through the river settlements, a professional obligation worn thin as everything else in his life. The chest had been an accident of the road. Fate, if you believed in that. She didn't, but she let him have it.

She told him less. But she told him true things, which for her was nearly the same generosity.

"You followed me for a hundred miles," she said, "and you never called the rangers in. No posse, no warrants. Just you."

"Rangers don't come out this far anymore. Budgets." He almost smiled. It was a rusty motion, like a door unused. "Also, I wanted to know what you were stealing. There hasn't been much worth stealing out here since the fall. When a thief works this hard, she's usually after something specific."

"And now you know."

"Now I know." He turned the canteen in his hands. "Here's my problem, Sallow. Everything I've done for three years has been aimed at staying away from Veyra. The seal, the war, the purge, the politics—all of it. I crossed a desert to get away from that box, and the desert handed it to me."

"Funny thing about rivers," Wren said. "Everything upstream ends up downstream eventually. Chests. Corpses. Countries."

"So I can't walk away from it. Which means I'm in this. And if I'm in this, I need to know whose side you're on—not Ashgrave's, fine, I believe that. But yours. What does Wren Sallow want out of this?"

It was a fair question and a brutal one, and she made him wait while she decided how honest to be. The honest answer was ugly: she didn't know. She'd spent so long wanting nothing larger than the next job, the next meal, the next town behind her, that the question of want itself had rusted shut.

"I want Ashgrave to not get it," she said finally. "That's the only clean thing in me right now. Start there."

"All right." He nodded slowly. "Start there."

"But understand what you're starting," she added, because fairness cut both ways. "If we're doing this, we're doing it against a colonel with a company of outriders, mercenary contacts in three territories, and no rules whatsoever. He hired me because I'm good. He'll send someone better. Someone who doesn't sit down and share water." She met his eyes. "I've run from men like him my whole career. Running works, most times. But running with cargo—that's different. Cargo slows you. Cargo makes you a target with a shape."

"Then we don't run with it."

"Oh?" She raised an eyebrow. "Enlighten me, cartographer. What do we do with a box full of a dead republic?"

"We take it where it goes," Casper said. "East. Over the Serpent Range, before the snows close the passes. There are settlements out there—survivor camps, river towns, places the rolls name. If that census is what you think it is, it doesn't belong in a vault or a colonel's war chest. It belongs to the people in it."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. It was, she had to admit, either the stupidest plan she'd ever heard or the first honest one, and she genuinely could not tell which, and the fact that she couldn't tell bothered her more than the knife still tucked in her belt bothered him.

"That's a thousand miles," she said. "Through a range that eats caravans. Ahead of winter. With a colonel's whole company behind us."

"Yes."

"And your grand contribution to this expedition is a map you drew for the government that tried to erase you, and a canteen that's half empty."

"And a tracker who found you twice," he said. "And apparently a thief good enough that a colonel trusts her with his secrets."

She snorted. "Flattery. From a lawman."

"Former cartographer. Current ranger. Lawman is a strong word out here." He held out his hand across the chest, formal as a treaty. "Partners. Provisionally. Either of us can walk at the next town."

Wren looked at the hand. She thought about every partnership she'd ever entered, and how each one had ended—with betrayal, arrest, or a window at midnight—and how none of those endings had ever surprised her, because she'd always known they were coming, because she'd always chosen partners she could predict, and predictable partners were just betrayals with a delay on them.

This one she couldn't predict. This dusty, stubborn, half-starved ranger with his dead republic in a box was a complete unknown, and unknowns were the one thing her trade had taught her to fear above all else.

She shook his hand anyway.

"Provisionally," she said. "But I keep the knife."

"It's my knife."

"It's my knife now. Finders keepers. Ask anyone in my profession."

Something happened to his face then—something that took her a moment to identify because she'd seen it so rarely, directed at her, that it barely registered as human at first. He laughed. Short, rough, real. And she felt the sound land somewhere under her ribs and stay there, warm and unwelcome, like a coal she'd have to carry.

*

They broke camp at dusk, moving east along the wash while the light lasted, because Wren insisted on putting miles between themselves and the road Ashgrave's couriers would ride looking for a thief who'd gone quiet.

It was on the third mile, in the blue hour when the desert gives up its heat and starts giving up its secrets, that Casper stopped walking.

"Wren." His voice had changed. She turned and found him looking back the way they'd come, west, toward the low line of the Salt Road and the darkening flats beyond it.

"What?"

"Lights," he said. "Half a league back. Lantern signal. Three short, one long."

She knew the pattern. Every thief on the Salt Road knew the pattern. It was a company sign—a mercenary unit signaling contact made.

"They found your camp," she said quietly.

"No," Casper said, and his hand found her shoulder, turning her fully around, and his face in the last light was grim as a closed border. "They found *us*. Those aren't trackers, Wren. Trackers travel quiet. That's a picket line. That's a man who already knows where we're going."

She looked west at the little lights winking in the dark, patient as stars, and felt the provisional partnership become permanent in the space of a single breath.

"Ashgrave," she said.

"Ashgrave," he agreed. "So. How fast can you move with cargo?"

She tightened her grip on the chest's handle and started to run.
