## 5. Three Days to the Range

The Serpent Range showed itself on the second morning, a blue-gray spine on the northern horizon like something sleeping under ash. Casper measured it the way he'd been taught—by thumb-widths at arm's length. Three widths. At their pace, three days, if the weather held and if nothing behind them got faster.

He didn't say any of this to Wren. He'd learned in Veyra that a mapmaker who narrates his own uncertainty invites everyone else's.

They rode out of the dead spring before full light, the chest lashed behind his saddle where it rode against his spine like a second heart. Wren had argued for leaving it buried. He'd argued for burning it. In the end they'd done neither, because neither of them could quite make their hands do it, and that fact sat between them now, unspoken, heavier than the chest itself.

"You're doing it again," Wren said.

"Doing what."

"Counting." She rode with her hood down despite the wind, dark hair whipping, and she sat her borrowed mule like she'd stolen it, which she had. "You get this look. Like you're adding up the distance and subtracting us from it."

"The look is called navigation."

"The look is called *dreading*." She let the mule drift closer to his horse. "My mother did sums like that. Every coin accounted for and somehow never enough. You know what happened to her? Nothing dramatic. She just got smaller every year until one day there wasn't enough of her left to bury."

Casper kept his eyes north. "Then I'll try to stay large."

"Don't strain yourself."

The flats gave way to broken country by midmorning—washes and low mesas, rock the color of old rust—and Casper called the first halt in the shadow of a tilted shelf of sandstone. Not because they needed rest. Because he needed to teach her.

"Dismount," he said. "We're stopping here an hour."

"We've been riding two hours."

"And we'll stop again before noon, and again after. You don't cross open country in one long bleed of distance. You cross it in pieces, and you rest in the pieces, so when the bad piece comes you've still got legs." He was already moving as he talked, checking the shade line, the wind, the ground under the overhang. "Also, we stop where the ground can't see us. Ridge lines are for people who want to be silhouettes. Washes are for people who want to be found face-down after a flash flood. Under rock, off the skyline, with your animals downhill of you so their smell doesn't carry."

Wren slid off the mule and looked around with the expression of someone appraising a house she intended to rob. "Who taught you all this?"

"A cartographer named Odile Vess. She said a map is a promise, and most promises are lies, so learn the ground itself." He caught himself saying the name aloud for the first time in three years and had to stand very still for a moment, the way you do when a door you'd nailed shut rattles on its hinges.

Wren noticed. She was annoyingly good at noticing. But she only said, "Vess. That's a Veyran name."

"It is."

"And you're going to tell me eventually what a Republic cartographer's apprentice is doing running cargo through the flats for a living."

"I'm going to tell you eventually a lot of things."

"Eventually is a terrible currency," Wren said. "It always devalues."

But she learned. That was the thing about her that unsettled him most—not the thieving, not the mouth, but how fast she took hold of whatever he put in her hands. By the time they remounted she could read the difference between a wash that drained a wide basin and one that drained a single slope, and she'd worked out without being told why he'd chosen the high shoulder of the mesa instead of the tempting flat below it.

"Because the flat floods," she said.

"Because the flat floods."

"And the flood comes from—" she squinted south, at a horizon that held nothing but heat shimmer, "—there. Where it isn't raining yet."

"That's the whole trick of desert country," Casper said. "The danger is always somewhere it isn't yet. You navigate by where the water will be, not where it is."

She was quiet for half a mile. Then: "That's also how my kind work, you know. Thieves. We don't watch the purse. We watch where the hand is going to be."

* * *

The outriders found them at dusk—or rather, Casper found the outriders, which was better but not by much.

He'd made camp in a fold of hills above a dry creek bed, no fire, cold rations, and he was lying awake doing the arithmetic he pretended not to do when he saw it: a spark of light on a mesa six miles south, there and gone. A heliograph. Or a lantern flashed off polished metal. Either way, a signal, and signals meant organization, and organization meant Ashgrave wasn't sending lone riders anymore.

He woke Wren with two fingers on her shoulder and nearly lost the hand for it—she came up out of sleep with a knife already moving—but she checked the blade a finger's width from his ribs, which he supposed counted as progress.

"Riders," he breathed. "South mesa. Six miles, maybe less. They signaled somebody."

"How many somebodies?"

"If they're signaling, more than us."

Wren lay back and stared at the stars, thinking. He watched her think. It was like watching a lock being picked from the inside.

"Options," she said finally. "One: we run all night and arrive at the Range with dead animals and no water. Two: we stand and fight, which is insane. Three—"

"Three, we stop being interesting."

Her head turned. "Meaning?"

"Meaning six riders chasing two riders is a hunt. Six riders chasing nobody is a waste of a night." He was already pulling the saddlebags apart. "How are you at making things disappear?"

"I'm a professional."

"Good. Because tonight, so am I."

It took them two hours in the dark, working by feel and by the occasional shielded match, and it was the strangest collaboration of Casper's life. They dragged brush across their trail for the last mile into the hills. They cut a second set of tracks heading east toward the salt flats, shod differently—Wren produced, from somewhere in her kit, a pair of horseshoes that were simply wrong, and nailed them onto a spare set of pads she strapped to the mule's feet for a quarter mile of convincing false trail. They scattered the remains of their cold camp in a pattern that suggested panic: a dropped ration tin, a torn strap, a boot print deep and hurried.

"They'll read it as a split," Casper murmured, standing back from their work. "One party east to the flats, one party north. And the east tracks are fresher-looking, so—"

"So they take the bait east," Wren finished, "and we go north slow and quiet while they gallop at salt." She grinned at him in the starlight, and it was the first time he'd seen her grin and it did something unfair to his chest. "Odile Vess taught you this?"

"Vess taught me the ground. The lying I learned from watching the Republic do it professionally for twenty years."

They moved north that night, walking the horses, no talking, and near dawn Casper did something he hadn't done since Veyra fell.

"Wake me at moonset," he said. "I'll take the next shift."

Wren stopped walking. "You're offering to sleep."

"I'm offering to *try* to sleep."

"You haven't slept a real shift since I met you. You catnap sitting up like a suspicious bird. Don't think I haven't noticed."

"I've noticed you noticing. It's very tiring." He handed her the lead rope. "Here's the truth of it. After Veyra, sleeping felt like dying. So I stopped. Three years of four hours a night, and I told myself it was discipline." He heard his own voice go flat and factual, the voice of a man reading coordinates. "It wasn't discipline. It was fear with good posture."

Wren took the rope. For once she didn't have a joke ready, and he understood that too—recognized it, actually, the way you recognize weather. She'd waited for him at that spring. She'd read names on a manifest and chosen to become hunted rather than hand them over. Whatever she'd survived before he met her, it had taught her the same lesson from the other direction: that trust wasn't a feeling you had, it was a skill you practiced, badly at first, in small increments, with your knife close.

"Moonset's in three hours," she said. "Sleep. If anyone comes, I'll wake you before they're close enough to matter. If I run, I'll leave the mule, because the mule hates me and deserves better than to die for my habits."

"That's the least reassuring vow of loyalty I've ever heard."

"It's the only honest kind." She settled against a rock, knife across her knees, eyes on the dark. "Go on, Nakamura. Trust is just a chore you do daily. Like watering stock."

He slept. It was dreamless and black and brief, and when her boot nudged him at moonset he woke clear-headed for the first time in years, and hated how much he liked it.

* * *

The second day they climbed. The flats fell away behind them and the land began to gather itself into folds and shoulders, sage and juniper replacing bare rock, the air thinning and cooling by degrees. The Serpent filled half the sky now, its passes visible as pale notches, and Casper didn't like the look of the highest ones—the white on them was too low, too early, the snowline coming down like a lid.

"Three days," he said, then corrected himself. "Two and a half, if the horses hold."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we walk, and it's four days, and we find out what Ashgrave's men know about winter."

At the noon halt, in a hollow ringed with juniper, Wren asked the question he'd been waiting three days for her to ask.

"Your name was on the manifest," she said. She said it plainly, no blade in it, which was somehow worse. "Eleven names. Ten of them mean nothing to me. Yours I've been sleeping next to. When do I get to know why?"

Casper fed a handful of grain to his horse and took longer about it than the task required.

"There's a map inside that chest," he said finally. "A treasury map. Old Republic seal, official survey marks. And the reason it's official—" he heard the sentence coming like a flood from a wash miles south, the danger somewhere it wasn't yet, "—is that I drew part of it. The base survey. The routes, the water, the approaches. My masterwork, Odile used to call it, when she was being cruel. I drew it the year before the Republic burned."

Wren went very still. "So the colonel isn't just chasing a box. He's chasing the box *and* the man who made finding it possible."

"He doesn't know that. Nobody knows that. It's in the manifest as a credit line—'survey by C. Nakamura, apprentice grade.' Bureaucrats sign everything. It's how they prove they existed." He almost laughed and it came out wrong. "Three years ago I would have paid anything for someone to remember I existed. Now a dead government's paperwork remembers for me, and it's trying to get me killed."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then she said, "Ten other names on that list. What happens to them if Ashgrave gets the chest?"

"I don't know."

"Yes, you do. You've known since the river. You just haven't said it out loud, because saying it makes it a plan instead of a worry." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Say it out loud, Casper."

He looked north at the pass, at the white creeping down it, and said it out loud.

"Names on a Republic manifest are either assets or evidence. To Ashgrave, whoever's alive on that list is either a tool or a loose end. And loose ends get cut." He closed the grain bag. "So we get to the Range first, and we get through the pass first, and we figure out what we're actually carrying before he figures out who he's actually hunting."

* * *

They made nine miles that evening before Casper called it, in a canyon whose walls funneled the wind and hid their fire—a small one, the first in two days, because cold was becoming a more immediate enemy than pursuit. Wren cooked; he took first watch; and it was during that watch, in the last gray light, that he saw the dust.

Not a signal this time. A column of it, far south, hanging in the windless air like smoke that had changed its mind. Too much dust for six riders. Too much for twelve.

He woke Wren without touching her this time—he'd learned; you said her name twice, low, and gave her a second to surface before you said anything that mattered.

"How many?" she asked, before her eyes were fully open.

"Company strength. Maybe more. Moving fast."

"Then the false trail bought us a day, not a week."

"It bought us what it cost. That's all anything buys." He crouched beside her at the canyon mouth, and together they watched the dust hang on the southern sky like a debt coming due. "There's a way-station town tomorrow. Redgate. Half-dead, mostly empty. We need water, and Ila—"

He stopped. Wren looked at him. "You were about to say a name like you already know her."

"I know *of* her. Stonemason's daughter, father killed this spring for repairing a Veyran milestone. The townsfolk keep her at arm's length—bad luck, Republic blood. If we're passing through Redgate anyway..." He trailed off, hearing how it sounded. Rescuing a child while a company of soldiers closed behind them. Adding weight to a flight that was already too slow.

Wren stood, brushing grit from her knees, and looked down at him with an expression he was beginning to recognize as the one she wore when she'd already decided and was letting him catch up.

"You're counting again," she said. "Distance minus us. But here's the thing about ledgers, Nakamura—I've robbed enough of them to know. The entries you skip are always the ones that come due." She nodded south, at the dust, at the company marching in Ashgrave's name toward whatever he'd promised them. "He's collecting an army out of other men's debts. Maybe it's time we started collecting people."

Below them, in the canyon, the fire ticked and settled. North, the Serpent's passes wore their early white like a closing fist, and somewhere between here and there, in a half-dead town called Redgate, a stonemason's daughter was about to stop being a rumor.

Casper banked the fire and took out the map of his own making, and by its light began, for the first time, to truly study it—the routes he'd drawn, the numbers he'd trusted, the coordinates he'd memorized at nineteen and never once questioned.

And that was when he saw it.
