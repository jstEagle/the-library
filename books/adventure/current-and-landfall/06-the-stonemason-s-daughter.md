## 6. The Stonemason's Daughter

Wren Sallow had stolen from forty-one towns in her life, and she could tell you the exact moment each one died. It wasn't the shuttered windows or the empty wells. It was the sound. A living town had a hum to it—goats, argument, somebody's hammer going at all hours. A dead town had wind and nothing else.

Salt Hollow had a hammer.

They heard it from half a mile out, a thin metallic tapping riding the flat air, and Casper reined up with his hand shading his eyes like he expected an army behind every adobe wall. Three days of outrider dust on their backs had made him careful in a way Wren found almost funny. Almost.

"One person," he said. "Maybe two."

"Or a trap with good rhythm," Wren said. She'd learned that about Casper on the road: he counted everything—miles, water, hours of sleep—and trusted none of his own counts when fear got loud enough. "Colonel Ashgrave doesn't need many men to kill two people in a box canyon."

Casper didn't answer right away. He was looking at the town the way he looked at maps, which Wren suspected was how he looked at everything: for what used to be there. "Salt Hollow was a way-station," he said finally. "Veyran milestone road ran through it. Before."

"Before," Wren repeated. The Republic's favorite word. Everything was before now.

They went in slow, horses walking, Casper's rifle across his saddle bow and Wren's knife loose in her sleeve where it lived. The main street was a gutter of cracked flagstone with weeds coming up through it like the ground was trying to swallow the road back. Doors stood open on dark rooms. A well with a rope but no bucket. And from somewhere past the third building, that tapping—patient, steady, unhurried. The tap of someone who had nowhere better to be and nothing else to do until they were done.

The hammering stopped as they turned the corner.

A girl stood in the middle of the street, chisel in one hand, mallet in the other, wearing a man's leather apron cut down and cinched twice at the waist. Twelve years old, maybe thirteen, sunburned, dark-haired, with stone dust white in her eyebrows and along her jaw like she'd been powdered for burial. At her feet lay a slab of granite the size of a coffin lid, and on its face, half-finished, were carved letters.

Wren read them upside down before she could stop herself. *MILE XXVII — VEYRA FREE ROAD.* Below that, the seal: the coiled serpent of the old Republic, worked deep and clean.

"You're a long way from anywhere," the girl said. Her voice was flat and adult, the voice of a child who'd run out of people to talk to and had stopped performing for anyone. "You can keep riding."

"We heard your hammer," Casper said. He dismounted slowly, hands visible, the way you did with anyone who might be jumpy. "Thought we should see who was working."

"It needs doing." The girl looked back down at her stone like they were boring her. "Someone has to do it."

Wren stayed mounted. That was policy. You didn't get off your horse in a dead town for anybody, because getting off your horse meant deciding this place mattered, and places that mattered got you killed. But she looked at the milestone—the real one, the original, three feet to the girl's left—and understood what she was seeing. The old marker had been smashed. Not weathered, not fallen. Smashed. Someone had taken a sledge to the Veyran seal and broken it into five pieces, and the girl had gathered the pieces, fitted them together like a puzzle, and was cutting a new face for it. Cutting it by hand. Alone.

"How long have you been out here?" Wren asked.

"Since spring." The girl set her chisel against the granite and didn't look up. "My father was a stonemason. I'm finishing his work."

* * *

His name had been Dario Vess, and he had been, by every account the town's last three survivors gave, the best stone-cutter between the river and the range. He'd come to Salt Hollow twenty years ago with the road crews and stayed when the road crews left, married a local woman, raised his daughter on chisels and levels the way other fathers raised daughters on prayers. When the Republic fell and the new powers came through smashing everything with a serpent on it, Dario Vess had done the single most dangerous thing a man in his trade could do.

He'd kept repairing the milestones.

Not secretly, either. That was the part the survivors kept coming back to, the part that made the old woman who told the story grip Wren's wrist with fingers like dry roots. He did it in daylight. He'd load his cart with fresh-cut faces, drive out to whatever marker the smashers had broken, and set it right again, and when they came for him he'd say the same thing every time: *the road is the road. It goes where it goes no matter whose name is on the stones.*

The fourth time he said it, they made an example of him. In the street. In front of the girl.

"They said if anyone touched the stones again, they'd burn what was left of the town," the old woman told them that evening, in a room lit by one stub of tallow candle, while the girl sat apart in the corner sharpening chisels with terrible concentration. "Half of us already gone. No water worth fighting over. So we told her to leave it. We told her the stones don't matter." The woman's hand shook around her cup. "She went out the next morning and started cutting a new face for Mile Twenty-Seven. What were we supposed to do? Tie her up?"

"Why didn't you?" Wren asked, not unkindly.

The old woman looked at her for a long moment. "Because when she works the stone, she sounds like him. And none of us could stand to be in the quiet after."

* * *

That night, camped in the ruined caravanserai at the edge of town because the girl refused to sleep under any roof in the settlement—"their roofs," she said, meaning the neighbors who'd told her to quit—Wren took stock of the situation the way she took stock of anything: as a problem with a cost attached.

The problem was named Ila Vess, and she was not leaving the milestone unfinished, and the milestone needed another four days' work, and Ashgrave's outriders were somewhere behind them closing distance like a hand closing into a fist, and every hour they spent in Salt Hollow was an hour the fist gained.

"She's a child," Casper said. They stood in the courtyard under a sky salted with stars, voices low. Inside the colonnade, Ila slept curled against her father's tool roll, one hand still wrapped around a chisel even in sleep. "We can't take her into the Serpents. There's snow coming early—I can feel it in my knee, and my knee hasn't lied to me yet. There are men hunting us who burn towns over stones."

"So we leave her here."

"In a town with three people in it and no water worth the name, with soldiers who already killed her father possibly coming back." Casper rubbed his face. "Yes. Obviously. That's the sane thing."

"But."

"But she'll follow us." He said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd report weather. "I watched her watch our horses. She's already measured how fast we ride. She's twelve and she's been alone since spring and she has decided something, and I have been a twelve-year-old who decided something, and I am telling you: she will follow us. On foot. Into the range."

Wren leaned against a pillar and thought about that. Thought about herself at twelve, following a caravan out of a city that had stopped wanting her, and nobody in that caravan having the decency to notice her for six days, and what it had done to her, that noticing-nothing, and how long it had taken her to believe she could want things.

"We could wait the four days," she said. "Let her finish the stone."

"And the outriders?"

"Could lose us instead. This country's full of dead roads. You're the map-man. Lose us."

Casper was quiet a while. Then he laughed, short and without much humor. "You know what my mentor used to say? A map is a promise. Every line on it says *someone will come this way after me.*" He looked toward the colonnade, at the small sleeping shape. "Somebody drew the line through Salt Hollow once. Somebody promised this road would carry people."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

In the morning, Wren went to settle it herself, because Casper was too kind to do it and she wasn't, or told herself she wasn't, which amounted to the same tool with a different handle.

Ila was already at the stone, dawn light catching the dust around her, mallet swinging in that same patient rhythm. Wren crouched at the edge of the slab, out of swing range, and laid it out plain.

"We're riding north today. To the mountains. Soldiers are looking for us, bad ones, and there's snow coming, and what we're doing will probably get everyone near it hurt. You stay here. You finish your stone. You live."

Ila stopped hammering. She looked at Wren with those dust-white eyebrows and eyes older than the granite she was cutting, and she said, "You're the thief."

"I'm the—yes. Fine. Yes."

"Casper Nakamura and the thief." She said it like reading off a manifest. "Everyone in the river towns knows. The chest with the seal. The colonel who wants it." She turned back to her stone. "You stole from the Republic's grave. My father died for a rock with the same mark on it. So don't tell me what's dangerous."

Wren felt that land somewhere under her ribs and sit there.

"The stone matters to you," Wren said carefully. "I understand that. But it's a rock."

Ila set down her mallet. She put both hands flat on the finished half of the new face, over the carved serpent, and when she spoke her voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it worse.

"When they broke the milestones, they weren't breaking rocks. They were saying nobody ever lived here. Nobody built the road. Nobody's name goes anywhere." Her hands pressed whiter against the stone. "My father's name isn't written anywhere. Not on a stone, not on paper, not in any book. They didn't even write it down when they killed him. If I stop cutting, then it's true. Then nobody ever lived here." She picked the mallet back up. "So. Ride north. I have four days of work."

Wren stood there in the cold morning with nothing to say, which was, for her, a genuinely novel experience. She walked back to the caravanserai where Casper was saddling the horses, and he looked at her face and said, "Yeah?"

"Yeah," Wren said. "We're waiting four days."

* * *

They waited four days, and it went like this:

Day one, Casper rode out at first light and didn't come back till dusk, and when he returned he led two extra horses—bought, he said, from the old woman for water rights and a favor to be named later—and Ila pretended not to watch him from her stone and failed completely.

Day two, Wren caught herself teaching the girl how to check a horse's hooves for heat, purely because the girl was standing there watching with those eyes, and told herself it was so the animal wouldn't go lame under incompetent handling later. Ila learned the frog-pick in ten minutes and had opinions about hoof angles by noon.

Day three, Casper spread his maps on the caravanserai floor to plan the range crossing, and Ila stood over them and pointed at a canyon and said, "Flash flood season's wrong for it now, but the walls funnel. My father cut drainage stone for the way-stations. He said never camp in a funnel." Casper stared at her, then at the canyon, then crossed out his planned camp and moved it two miles east. Wren watched him do it and thought: *well. That's that, then.*

Day four, near dusk, Ila set her mallet down for the last time. The new face stood clean and whole on the old broken marker, the serpent seal sharp-edged, the letters deep enough to outlast weather and men. She ran her palms over it once, the way you'd close a dead man's eyes, and then she walked to the horses without ceremony and stood waiting beside the second extra mount with her father's tool roll strapped across her back.

Nobody had discussed it. That was the strange part. Somewhere across those four days the discussion had happened without words, in hoof-checks and map-margins and the sound of a hammer keeping time, and by the time it needed saying it was already true.

"You understand," Wren said, mounting up, "this is the stupidest thing any of us has ever done, and I once stole a bishop's hat off a bishop."

"I know," Ila said.

"There's snow in those mountains. Real snow. Men die up there comfortable and prepared."

"I know."

"Ashgrave's riders may find us. Your father's story ends badly enough already."

Something flickered across the girl's face then—not tears, something harder, something being forged. "Then it should end next to people," she said, "instead of in a town that wouldn't watch."

Wren looked at Casper. Casper looked at the sky, at the first thin smudge of cloud sitting on the northern horizon like a held breath, and said, "We ride tonight. Cold camp, no fire. If we push hard we make the foothills in five days."

They made the outskirts of Salt Hollow as the light died, and that was when Wren saw it—the dust plume to the south, low and wide and moving fast. Too organized for weather. Too big for travelers.

Casper saw it a heartbeat after she did. His jaw tightened.

"That's not outriders," he said quietly. "Outriders travel thin. That's a column." He wheeled his horse north. "Ashgrave's not sending hunters anymore. He's bringing the whole ledger to collect."

Behind them, faint on the evening wind, Wren could have sworn she heard it: a hammer, tapping. Or maybe just her own heart, learning a new rhythm.
