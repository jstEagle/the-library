## 7. Salt and Ash

The fire was small on purpose. Small fires ask fewer questions of the dark.

Casper fed it a single twist of greasewood and watched the flame take hold, blue at the base, orange at the lip, the way fire always looked like it was deciding something. Three days out from the dead spring, two days from the foothills, and the Serpent Range had begun to show itself on the northern horizon — a jagged gray line like a broken promise, its peaks already wearing their first white.

Wren sat across the flames, cross-legged, cleaning her knives with a rag that had stopped being white sometime before he'd met her. She worked without looking at her hands. She did most things without looking at them, which he'd come to understand was not carelessness but economy.

"You keep doing that," she said.

"Doing what."

"Counting." She nodded at his fingers, which had been moving against his knee without his permission. "You tap out numbers when you think nobody's watching. You did it all day on the ride. Twelve taps, then four, then nine again."

He looked down at his hand as if it belonged to someone else.

"Miles," he said. "I count miles. It's a habit."

"From Veyra?"

"From before Veyra fell."

She set down the knife. That was the thing about Wren Sallow — she could go very still in a way that wasn't peace, it was attention, a hunter's stillness borrowed for conversation. "You've been circling it for three days," she said. "Why your name was on that manifest. I figured you'd tell me or I figured you wouldn't, but either way I'm tired of watching you chew on it."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the flats, wind moved through the saltbush with a sound like paper turning pages.

"I drew the map," Casper said.

* * *

Three years ago, and eleven days before the end of everything, Casper Nakamura had stood in the Map Room of the Cartographic Bureau with ink to his elbows and the best work of his life drying under glass.

He was twenty-two. He had been an apprentice for six years, which made him senior among the apprentices, which meant he got the commissions nobody else wanted: the drainage surveys, the boundary disputes, the long dull slogs through marsh country measuring things that would never matter. His mentor — Master Odile Ferran, cartographer of the third rank, a woman built like a folded ruler and twice as sharp at the joints — had promised him that patience was the whole trade. *Anyone can draw a coast, Casper. The map is in what you leave off.*

Then the commission came down from the Ministry of Holdings, and it was not dull at all.

"The treasury," Odile said, laying the sealed orders on the drafting table between them. "The Republic's contingency reserve. They want it surveyed, mapped, and the map lodged in three copies: one for the Ministry, one for the Bureau vault, one for—" she paused, reading further, and her mouth did something complicated— "one for private custody. A named holder."

"Who names a treasury map's holder?"

"Afraid people do, near the end." She said it lightly, but she didn't look up from the orders, and Casper learned later that she had read the political weather better than any of them. The Republic was rotting from the inside — ministries eating ministries, colonels growing fatter than generals, the census bureaus quietly burning records of towns that had stopped paying taxes because those towns no longer existed. Everyone in the Bureau knew. Nobody said it. Maps are drawn by people who need the world to have edges, and theirs were dissolving.

The site itself was a marvel. Deep in the Serpent Range, past the high traverse, there was an old Imperial mine — worked out and abandoned two centuries, its adits collapsed, its shafts flooded — and beneath it, cut into bedrock where no flood could reach, the Republic had hollowed a vault. Casper spent nineteen days surveying it. He measured the galleries by lamplight, triangulated the approach routes, charted the snowmelt channels that fed the cisterns, and drew the finest map of his young career: every corridor, every cache chamber, every bearing fixed to bedrock so precisely that a stranger with his sheets could walk to the vault door blindfolded in a blizzard.

Three copies. He drew all three himself, because Odile said a map is only as honest as the fewest hands it passes through.

On the last night, sealing the tubes with wax, Odile watched him work and said, "You know what you've actually made."

"A treasure map."

"A list." She tapped the tube. "Everything in that vault is worth nothing. Gold is a rumor until someone agrees it's real. What you've made is proof of where the Republic kept its agreement. Whoever holds your map holds the argument." She smiled, and it was the tired smile she wore when she thought he wasn't ready for a truth yet. "Good thing it'll sleep in a vault, then. Some maps should."

Eleven days later, the purge came.

* * *

"That's why the manifest had my name," Casper told Wren. The fire had burned down to a red eye and he fed it another stick rather than look at her while he said the rest. "Named holder. Odile put me on the private copy. Not the Ministry's, not the Bureau's — the third one, the one that went into private custody. She named me. Me, an apprentice. And I never asked her why, because eleven days after I sealed those tubes, soldiers came to the Bureau district with lists of their own."

Wren hadn't moved. "Whose list?"

"Colonel Ashgrave's, though I didn't know the name then. He ran the internal security battalions in the capital. The story was sedition — cartographers plotting with foreign powers, mapping military roads for the enemy. It was nonsense. It was also, conveniently, a way to seize the Bureau's archives, and the archives held every census roll, every land grant, every record of who owned what in the Republic. Ashgrave didn't want our maps of mountains. He wanted our maps of *people*."

"And you ran."

The words landed the way a dropped stone lands in a well — a long time falling, then a sound deeper than its size.

"I ran," Casper said.

* * *

The night of the purge, he had been sleeping in the Bureau loft above the drafting hall, as apprentices did, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of linseed and cold stone. He woke to orange light under the door and boots on the stairs — many boots, in step, which civilians never managed — and he did the arithmetic of survival in about four seconds.

There was a back stair. There was a coal chute. There was a window over the canal that he'd used a dozen times to slip out to the night markets. Any of them might have led him down into the hall where Odile's lamp was already lit, where she would be standing in front of the map vault with her body between it and the soldiers, because he knew — he knew with the certainty of six years of mornings — that she would not move aside.

He took the window.

He told himself, swimming the freezing canal, hauling himself up the far bank into the reeds, that he was going for help. That he'd find the city watch, find a magistrate, find anyone with more authority than a twenty-two-year-old apprentice and come back. He told himself this while the Bureau district burned behind him, while the light turned the canal water the color of hammered copper, while something inside him counted — one, two, three — the windows of the drafting hall going dark or going bright, he couldn't tell which was worse.

He never went back. By morning he was on the south road with a forged travel pass and the clothes he stood in, and by winter he was in the borderlands learning to track men instead of rivers, because tracking was the only part of cartography that traveled well. Reading sign. Following disturbance. Finding where someone had been by what they'd left behind.

He had gotten very good at finding people who didn't want to be found.

It had taken him three years to admit he'd started with himself.

* * *

"So she died for it," Wren said. Not a question. "Your Odile. For the vault."

"For the archives, maybe. Or just because she was there and she was Veyran and the lists needed filling." Casper stared into the fire. "I don't know. That's the worst of it. I've spent three years not knowing, because knowing would mean going back, and going back means—" He stopped. Started again. "The manifest in that chest. Her handwriting was on it. Named holder: C. Nakamura, apprentice, Cartographic Bureau. She wrote my name on a map to the Republic's last treasure and I was busy climbing out a window."

Wren was quiet for a while. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge, which somehow made it worse. "My mother kept a bakery in Corvess. After the fall, when the tax collectors came through taking 'reclamation fees,' she baked bread for the men who burned the quarter, because she thought if they ate her bread they'd remember she was human." She turned her knife over in her lap. "They remembered anyway. People like Ashgrave count on us believing we can feed our way out. So when I read those names in your chest — forty-some names, living holders, witnesses — I understood exactly what I was carrying. Not gold. A list of everyone whose testimony could hang him."

"And you kept it."

"I kept it." She looked up, and the firelight caught the scar through her eyebrow, the one she'd never explained. "Same reason you're dragging it toward the mountains instead of selling it and disappearing, which I know you've thought about, because I've thought about it too and I'm the professional."

He almost laughed. It came out as breath. "What reason is that?"

"Because somebody wrote our names down," she said simply. "Somebody decided we were worth keeping track of. In a world where the other side keeps lists too — lists of who to erase — I figure the least we can do is make sure the right list wins."

The wind shifted. Casper pulled his coat tighter and found his eyes stinging, and he chose to blame the smoke.

"There's something else," he said. "About the map. Something's been bothering me since the dead spring, and tonight I finally let myself look at it straight." He reached into his coat and drew out the rolled sheet — his own work, three years old, the lines still crisp under the grime. "I drew this. I know every stroke of it. And the bearing to the vault entrance is wrong."

Wren went still again. "Wrong how?"

"Wrong by design. My design." He flattened the sheet against his knee, weighting the corners with stones. "Look. The approach route runs through the lower gallery, here, past the cistern. But the cistern was dry when I surveyed it — I noted it myself, see, in the margin. Except it couldn't have been. Snowmelt feeds it year-round; I charted the channel myself, upstream. Someone drained it between my survey and the sealing of the copies. Someone altered the route so anyone following this map has to pass through a gallery that can be flooded from above." He met her eyes. "This isn't a treasure map, Wren. It's a trap with a legend on it. Ashgrave isn't chasing a chest. He's chasing a chest into a kill room — or he knows it's a trap and he's using whoever carries this map as his key tester."

"Or both," Wren said. "He sent me to steal it. He expected me to open it, read it, run. He expected you to follow me." Her voice had gone flat and fast, the way it did when she was thinking in exits. "We weren't the hunters, Casper. We were the survey party. He's been using us to confirm the route works."

"Then we give him a route that doesn't." Casper rolled the map slowly, feeling the weight of it, the weight of everything Odile had folded into it — her caution, her foresight, her faith in an apprentice who had repaid it with a window and a swim. "There's a dead mine two valleys east of the true site. Worked out, collapsed, on none of my sheets. If Ashgrave wants coordinates, we can teach him some."

Wren studied him across the small fire, and whatever she saw in his face seemed to settle something in hers.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Tonight, sleep. I'll take first watch — and before you argue, yes, I noticed you haven't really slept since the river. You count miles so you don't have to count anything else."

He lay back on his bedroll and looked up at the wheeling stars, and for once he didn't tap out numbers against his knee. He let the silence be silent.

He was nearly asleep when Wren's hand closed on his shoulder — hard, awake, certain.

"Casper. Lights. Down the slope, by the river bend." A pause. "That's not a campfire. That's a picket line."

Outriders. Ahead of them, between them and the foothills, lanterns strung along the ford like beads on a wire — Ashgrave's men, closing the door.

Casper sat up, and the old habit rose unbidden, his fingers tapping against his knee: the distance to the ford, the distance to the pass, the distance they no longer had.
