## 8. Outriders at the Ford

The river announced itself before we saw it—a green smear of cottonwoods stitched along the flats like someone had dropped a thread from the sky. Three days of hard riding behind us, Casper's map-reading and my map-fudging getting us over the ridgeline ahead of the dust plume that had dogged our heels since the way-station. Ila rode pillion behind Casper with her arms locked around his waist and her chin tucked into his shoulder, asleep more often than not, which was a skill I envied.

"Ford's a mile," Casper said, standing in his stirrups to squint at the trees. "We cross, we're in the badlands by dark. Ashgrave's outriders won't follow past the water."

"You're sure about that?"

"No."

I liked him better for saying it. Most men would've dressed the guess up in confidence and let me ride on it. Casper just handed you the truth raw and let you decide what to do with your fear.

We came down through the cottonwoods single file, the horses blowing at the smell of water, and that was when Ila woke up and said, very quietly, "There are men on the far bank."

Of course there were. There had been men on the far bank since the day I'd stolen Colonel Ashgrave's chest instead of delivering it, and every mile since had been me spending down a debt I couldn't count yet.

* * *

Casper took off his hat and turned in the saddle, slow, reading the far side the way he read everything—like terrain, like something that could be mapped if you looked long enough. Six riders, maybe seven, spread out along the shallows where the gravel bar made crossing easy. They weren't moving. That was the part that made my stomach drop. Men who wanted to chase you moved. Men who wanted to *wait* for you didn't have to.

"They've been here a while," Casper said. "Horses are hobbled, grazing. They knew the ford was the only crossing inside two days' ride."

"So we don't cross."

"We cross or we starve on this side." He said it without drama, the way you'd note the weather. "Question is how."

Ila, behind him, had gone very still in the way children go still when they've learned that stillness is safer than noise. I hated that she knew that trick. I hated that I recognized it.

"There's a second crossing," Casper said. "Half a day north. Deeper water, faster current. We could try it with three horses and a child."

"And lose all three horses and the child," I said.

"That's the risk assessment, yes."

Below us, one of the riders on the far bank mounted up and walked his horse out onto the gravel bar, alone, unhurried. He stopped at the waterline and raised an open hand. Not a wave. A parley gesture. Old army grammar; even I knew it, and I'd learned my soldiering from stealing from soldiers.

"He wants to talk," Casper said.

"People who want to kill you also want to talk. It's the ones who don't talk you should worry about."

He almost smiled. "That's why I keep you around."

"You keep me around because I'm the only person alive who knows where the chest is hidden."

"I keep you around for both reasons."

We left Ila with the horses in the trees—over her furious objection, conducted entirely in glares—and walked down to the water with our hands visible and empty. The rider across the ford dismounted and did the same. He was a big man built like a door, sunburned to leather, wearing Veyran cavalry gray gone brown at the seams. When he got close enough I saw the sergeant's chevrons on his sleeve, and under the dust, the faded crest of the Republic.

"Sergeant Bek Odumane," he said. Her voice caught me sideways—I'd read the uniform and filed her as *he*, and the correction landed like a misstep on stairs. "Third Lancers, what's left of them. You're Nakamura."

"Casper Nakamura."

"The cartographer's boy." She looked at me. "And you'd be the thief. Sallow. The colonel has a price on you that would buy this whole valley."

"Flattered."

"Don't be. It's a working number, not a compliment." She crouched and put her palm flat on the water, testing the cold, then stood again. "Here's how today goes. My orders are to take you both alive and the girl if convenient. Dead is acceptable for you, Sallow. The colonel wants Nakamura breathing—he has questions only you can answer."

"What questions?" Casper asked, mild as milk, but I heard the wire under it.

"You drew the treasury map. He wants to know what else you drew, and who else has copies." Odumane shrugged. "I don't care about any of that. I'm telling you the shape of the trap so you understand I know it's a trap too."

I looked at her. Really looked. "You're offering us something."

"I'm telling you the weather before it turns. That's all." But she wasn't looking at Casper when she said it. She was looking at the tree line where Ila sat hunched between the horses, small as a stone. Something moved behind her face, some arithmetic done in a currency I recognized—the same one I'd used the night I read the manifest and saw forty names and thought: *these are people, these are people, and I was going to hand them to a man who counts people like coin.*

"How many of yours feel the way you do?" Casper asked quietly.

Odumane was silent long enough that the river filled the silence twice over. "Two," she said finally. "Out of six. The other three are Ashgrave's to the bone. Promised land grants when the Republic rises again. They'll shoot the girl without losing sleep." She straightened. "You have until dusk to decide. After that I stop being able to help you, because after that I stop being able to look at myself in the morning either way."

She turned and waded back. At the gravel bar she paused, half-turned. "Nakamura. Your mentor—Corvane. He taught my officer's class, years back. Before." Then she mounted and rode off, and the word *before* hung over the water like smoke.

* * *

Back in the trees, we argued in whispers while Ila pretended not to listen, which meant she listened to every word.

"She's baiting us," I said. "It's a stall. Keep us talking until the light dies and then they take us in the dark."

"Maybe."

"Maybe? That's your contribution? Maybe?"

"My other contribution is that she gave us information for free. Real information. Numbers, names, orders. Bait doesn't come itemized." Casper sat with his back against his horse's flank, hat over his eyes, doing that thing where he went so still he might've been asleep except his mouth kept working. "Three loyal to Ashgrave. Two sympathetic. One unaccounted for. If she moves, she moves at dusk, when the watch changes. That's when a defection happens—when the man next to you is someone new."

"You've thought about defection a lot."

"I've thought about running a lot. Same muscle."

Ila spoke up from where she was braiding grass into her horse's mane. "If the sergeant helps us, she can't go home."

Casper lifted the hat brim and looked at her. "No. She can't."

"Then helping us costs her everything." Ila said it flatly, the way children state enormous things. "So she's not lying. People don't lie for something that costs everything. They lie for things that are cheap."

I stared at her. Twelve years old, and she'd just dismantled my entire objection in two sentences. Casper had the decency not to grin at me, but only barely.

"Dusk, then," he said. "We get the horses saddled and ready. And Wren—if it goes wrong, you take Ila north along the bank. Don't argue. Don't wait for me. The chest matters more than either of us, and she knows where to find you."

"It doesn't matter more than you."

"To Ashgrave it does. So plan accordingly."

* * *

Dusk came down purple and slow, the way it does in the flats, no mountains to hurry it. We watched the far camp through the cottonwood trunks—watched the fires go up, watched the sentries walk their posts, watched Odumane move among her men with a mess tin, playing the good sergeant, laughing at something a corporal said. It was good acting. Or it was a woman saying goodbye to the last hours of being the person she'd been.

Full dark. The fires burned low. And then, at the edge of the firelight, a horse stepped out of the picket line without a sound, and a second followed it, and a figure on foot slipped away from the sentry post like a shadow peeling off a wall.

Gunfire. Two shots, three, wild and high—warning shots, or panicked ones. The camp lit up like a kicked anthill. And Odumane came across the ford at a gallop with two riders behind her and bullets snapping the cottonwood bark over our heads, and I stopped thinking and started doing, which is the only kind of courage I've ever actually owned.

"Horses!" Casper was already swinging Ila up. "North bank, now, go!"

We went. The river took the sound of the gunfire and broke it apart. Behind us I heard shouting resolve itself into pursuit—one voice, then two, then the terrible organized quiet of soldiers doing their work. Odumane pulled alongside us, bent low over her horse's neck, and shouted the thing I'd remember for the rest of my life:

"They'll run us down in the open! The cutbank—there's a slot canyon half a mile north—single file—we hold it there!"

"Hold it with what?" I screamed back.

"With me!"

* * *

The slot canyon was exactly what she said: a crack in the cutbank wide enough for one horse, twisting back into the mesa like a throat. We stuffed the animals in deep, all five of us crammed into stone and darkness, and Odumane climbed to a ledge at the mouth with her rifle and her bedroll and the calm of a woman setting a table.

Her two defectors were a wiry kid called Fen with shaking hands and a scarred veteran named Doss who said nothing at all. They took positions below her. I took position next to Casper, who had put Ila in the deepest crevice with instructions to stay down and stay silent, and who then stood in the open like a fool looking at Odumane's back.

"Sergeant. You could still ride north. Lose yourself in the badlands. Nobody would—"

"Ashgrave doesn't lose people. He spends them." She checked her rifle's action without looking at it. "Besides. Three years ago I fired on a village full of people whose only crime was being on a list. I told myself I was following orders, and the orders were legal, and legality kept me fed." She settled her cheek against the stock. "Tonight the list is four people and a child hiding in a canyon, and I'm on the wrong end of the rifle, and it turns out that's where I should have been all along. Go sit down, cartographer. This part isn't yours."

The pursuers came an hour later. Four of them—so the fifth had stayed with the horses, or turned back, or deserted in his own quieter way. They had lanterns, and they swept the bank in a patient line, and when they found the slot they did the smart thing, which was nothing. They set a fire at the mouth and settled in to wait us out, because they had rations and we had a bedroll and pride.

At midnight, Odumane shot their lantern out.

Not their men. Their lantern. One round, dead center, and the dark rushed in, and in the dark she started talking to them. I couldn't hear what she said—low, steady, carrying just far enough. But I heard the answers. First angry. Then uncertain. Then, near dawn, one voice asking a question with a crack in the middle of it, a question I didn't need words for because I'd asked versions of it my whole life: *what happens to us, what happens to the people we came from, who says we belong to him.*

They left before first light. All four. Whether they deserted or went back to report, I couldn't tell you, and neither could Odumane, and she sat on her ledge watching the empty fire ring with her rifle across her knees and her face wet, and nobody said anything about it, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't have been smaller than the silence.

* * *

We rode north through the badlands as the sun came up red behind us. Odumane fell in beside Casper without ceremony, the way soldiers do, as if she'd always been there.

"You understand you can't go back," Casper said. "Whatever happens with us—you're done. Ashgrave will hang you if he takes you, and the settlements won't trust the uniform for years."

"I did the math at the ford." She rolled her shoulders like a woman setting down weight. "Everything I had was already spent. I was just deciding who to spend the rest on."

Ila, riding behind me now, leaned forward. "Sergeant? Why did you shoot the lantern instead of the men?"

Odumane was quiet for a long moment. "Because I've shot enough men who were only following orders," she said. "I wanted to see what they'd do with no orders at all."

Ahead of us the badlands opened into a plain of broken rock running toward a blue wall on the horizon—the Serpent Range, snow-streaked already, closer than I wanted it to be. Casper reined up and took out the map, and I watched his face change as he studied it, watched something cold move through his eyes like a fish under ice.

"Wren," he said slowly. "These coordinates. The treasury cache."

"What about them?"

"I drew this map. I know every line on it." His finger tapped the marked spot, twice, hard. "And this isn't where I put anything. Which means either I've forgotten my own work—or somebody wanted Ashgrave riding for this exact spot."

The wind came down off the range, cold and early, smelling of snow.

"And if it's the second thing," I said, "then whatever's really out there—"

"—is somewhere else," Casper finished. "And we're the only ones alive who might know where."
