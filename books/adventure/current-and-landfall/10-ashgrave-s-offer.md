## 10. Ashgrave's Offer

The messenger was a boy. That was the first thing Ashgrave noticed, and the last thing that mattered.

He came down the river trail at dusk with a courier's satchel and a limp he was trying to hide, and the outriders took him at the ford without a shot fired—two of them wading out from the willows while a third walked up the bank whistling like a friend. The boy ran for maybe forty yards before the ground betrayed him, soft river mud sucking at his boots, and then it was over. They brought him to Ashgrave's fire with his hands bound and his chin up, which Ashgrave noted with something adjacent to respect. Courage in the young was cheap and common. It was what they did with it afterward that told you who they'd be.

"Name," Ashgrave said.

The boy looked at him. He was perhaps sixteen, Veyran by the cut of his coat—the old blue, pre-collapse, patched at the elbows. A survivor settlement somewhere south, then. The rolls would have his family in them, if the rolls still existed. If anyone had ever bothered to write them down.

"Name," Ashgrave said again, patiently, the way you'd speak to a horse you didn't want spooked.

"Pell."

"And who sent you, Pell?"

"No one sent me." Which was a lie so immediate and total that Ashgrave almost smiled. "I'm going to see my aunt in Harrow Bend."

Ashgrave crouched by the fire and opened the satchel himself. He did not delegate this. His men were good at taking things; they were worse at knowing what they'd taken. Inside: bread gone hard, a water skin, a folded change of clothes, and beneath the clothes, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, a letter sealed with plain wax and no mark at all—which meant it was important, because only people with nothing to hide marked their letters.

He broke the seal and read it twice.

It was addressed to *the ranger Nakamura, or whoever carries his trust*, and it was from a woman named Odumane. Sergeant Bek Odumane, formerly of the Ninth Veyran Rifles, currently a deserter traveling with the very man Ashgrave had spent three months trying to corner. The letter was a warning, of all things. *He knows where you'll cross. He has bought the wardens. Do not take the low pass even if it looks open.*

Ashgrave read it a third time, slower, and felt the particular pleasure of a plan confirming itself. Tessaly had taken his money and kept her mouth shut, exactly as paid. But Odumane had known her, had guessed the shape of the betrayal without being told, and had tried to warn Nakamura through channels—and the channels had failed, because the channels were a sixteen-year-old boy with a limp crossing country that belonged to Ashgrave now.

"You know what this is?" he asked the boy.

Pell said nothing. His eyes flicked to the letter and away, and that was answer enough. He hadn't read it. He'd been paid in bread and a story about an aunt.

"Here's what happens now," Ashgrave said, settling onto his camp stool across the fire, arranging himself the way he arranged everything—deliberately, visibly, so that men watching learned to read his stillness as confidence. "You have two choices. I can turn you loose tomorrow with your bread and your limp and my compliments, and you can go find your aunt in Harrow Bend, if she exists. Or you can carry a message for me, deliver it truly, and come back rich enough that your aunt won't recognize you."

"I'm not carrying anything for you."

"You haven't heard the message yet."

"It doesn't matter. You're the one hunting them. Everyone on the river knows it. You burned the Tessel farms."

Ah. So the reputation had arrived ahead of him. Ashgrave considered this. Fear traveled fast and worked cheap, but it had a ceiling; fear alone made men run, and running men were hard to buy. What he needed from Nakamura was not flight. It was negotiation. And negotiation required a man who believed he had something to trade.

"Tessel farms," Ashgrave repeated. "Yes. They were harboring a man who murdered two of my soldiers. I don't expect you to believe that. I expect you to believe that I could have done worse and didn't." He let that sit. "Here is my message, Pell, and you may repeat it word for word, because I will write it down as well, and because a boy who can recite a colonel's terms under pressure is worth more than a boy who can't."

* * *

He wrote it that night by lamplight, three drafts, burning the first two. Words were maps, his old instructor used to say—they were never the territory, but a good one got you there alive. Ashgrave had been drafting documents since before the Republic fell. Requisitions. After-action reports. The lists. He was good at words that looked like mercy and functioned as leverage, and he did not pretend otherwise to himself, because self-deception was a luxury for men who planned to lose.

*Nakamura.*

*I will be brief, because you were trained to value brevity, and because flattery is wasted on a cartographer.*

*You have something I want. I have several things you want, though you may not have admitted the second part to yourself yet. Let me enumerate.*

*One. Amnesty. Every name traveling with you—Sallow, Odumane, the child—is a dead name under standing writ. Sallow robbed a paymaster. Odumane deserted in wartime. These are not opinions; they are sentences waiting for a signature. I hold the signatures. I can also unhold them.*

*Two. A commission. You drew the treasury map; I know this because the manifest says so, and manifests do not lie to me. A man who can make a document like that is wasted chasing thieves across salt flats. I am building something, Nakamura. Not a banditry. A state. Small at first—honest at first, if honesty can be grown like a crop. It needs surveyors. It needs men who understand that borders are drawn twice, once on paper and once in blood, and that the second drawing goes better when the first was done well.*

*Three. A home. This is the item you will pretend not to care about, so I will put it plainly. You have been walking for three years. I have watched you walk—I have men who are good at watching. You sleep in hollows. You eat cold. You count your days in miles because miles end and years don't. I am offering you walls, Nakamura. Walls and a door that locks from the inside, which is the only kind of door that means anything.*

*In exchange: the map, delivered whole, and the chest, unopened by any hand but mine. You keep whatever you believe the chest contains—I have no interest in sentiment. Bring it to the high traverse within six days. Come armed. Come all of you, if you like; I will feed you before I take delivery.*

*Refuse, and I will take the chest at whatever cost, and the cost will be measured in the people you've collected, because you've done the one thing I couldn't do—you've made them care whether you live. That is a lever, Nakamura. I would be a poor engineer not to use it.*

*Consider what you're protecting. A dead republic's paperwork? Names on lists? I knew the men who wrote those lists. They wrote them in warm rooms and then ran when the rooms got cold. Whatever you think you're carrying east, it isn't worth a single one of your four lives. Whereas what I'm offering is the only version of Veyra that survives winter: mine.*

*You were an apprentice once. Take instruction. — Col. D. Ashgrave, Ninth Veyran Rifles (loyal elements).*

He read it aloud once to check the music of it, then sealed it and handed it to the boy along with a week's bread and a silver bit he didn't need.

"Word for word," Ashgrave said. "And tell him the last line is true. Tell him I said: *I was an apprentice too.*"

* * *

They released Pell at dawn, unbound, pointed east. Ashgrave watched him go until the willows took him, and then he sat with his coffee and did the arithmetic he always did after sending terms.

Six days. Tessaly would hold the pass open until the snow closed it—she'd sworn it, and her price had been paid in advance, which made her reliable the way purchased things are reliable: completely, right up until someone offers more. The high traverse was three days' climb in good weather. If Nakamura moved tonight, he could make the traverse with a day to spare. If he hesitated—if he weighed the offer, argued with his crew, lost a day to hope the way idealists always did—he'd arrive with Ashgrave's vanguard already dug in above him.

Either way, Ashgrave won. That was the design. Acceptance gave him the map and, better, the man. Refusal gave him the moral high ground, which in his experience was simply terrain you hadn't fortified yet, and cost nothing to abandon later.

What he had not accounted for—what nagged at him like a stone in a boot—was the fourth possibility. Not acceptance, not refusal, but conversion. Terms received and turned inside out. He'd seen it happen once before, years ago, to a governor who offered amnesty to a rebel town and watched them frame the letter on their meetinghouse wall as proof of what they'd refused. Documents had a way of meaning more than their authors intended. He'd written enough of them to know.

He crushed the thought with the rest of his doubts and gave the order to move.

* * *

Casper read the letter aloud because Wren told him to.

"Read it out," she said. "Lies sound different in the air. On paper they can hide."

They were camped in the lee of a rock shelf two days shy of the foothills, the Serpent Range standing up against the sky ahead of them like the spine of something sleeping. Bek sat sharpening her knife with the patience of a woman who expected to use it soon. Ila sat closest to the fire, close as she was allowed, mending a strap with small fierce stitches.

Casper unfolded the pages and read.

He read it flat, without performance, the way his mentor had taught him to read coordinates—let the numbers speak. Amnesty. Commission. Home. He watched the words land on each of them. On Wren, *amnesty* landed like a coin she was pretending not to look at. On Bek, *desertion* landed like a diagnosis she'd already made herself. On Ila—nothing. She was twelve. She cared about straps and fires and being included.

"*Walls and a door that locks from the inside,*" Casper read, and stopped, because his voice caught on it, and he hated that it caught.

"That's the hook," Bek said quietly. "That's the true sentence in the trap. Everything else is bait around it."

"He's not wrong, though." Wren was looking at the fire. "About the walking. About counting miles." She glanced up, and her look dared him to be offended. "Three years, ranger. You sleep like a man listening for hooves. Don't tell me a locked door doesn't sound like heaven to you."

"It sounds like heaven to everyone," Casper said. "That's how doors work. The question is whose key hangs on the other side of it."

"And his answer is his own belt," Bek said. "A commission. A state with his face on it. I served under him. I've stood in the warm rooms he's talking about. The lists get written there." She said *lists* the way you'd say a wound. "Whatever we're carrying east, he wants it because it's the only thing in the world that can make his Veyra real. Paper does that. Ask any government."

"It's not just paper," Casper said, and then stopped, because he wasn't ready to say what else it might be, because he wasn't sure yet himself. The wrongness of his own coordinates had been sitting in him for two days like a swallowed coal. *Deliberate. I made it deliberate. Why would I—*

He folded the letter.

"So we refuse," Ila said. It wasn't a question, but it was close to one, and her voice had the careful brightness of a child who has learned that adults' decisions are weather that happens to you.

"We refuse," Casper said.

"But he said six days," Ila said. "And the pass—"

"The pass is bought," Bek said. "Tessaly sold us. I warned you."

"You warned a boy with a limp," Wren said. "Who got caught. Which means Ashgrave knows we know, which means the low pass is a mouth and the high traverse is—" she paused, working it, and Casper watched her mind move the way he loved watching it move, quick and crooked and honest underneath the crookedness, "—a throat. He's dug in somewhere on it. He let the messenger go because the letter was the ambush. Not the words. The *clock*. Six days. He wants us spending them deciding instead of climbing."

Silence, except the fire.

"So we don't spend them deciding," Casper said slowly. Something was assembling itself in him, the way a map assembles—not lines first, but the sense of a shape under the lines. "We spend them making him decide. Bek. When Ashgrave writes terms, does he leave room for counteroffers?"

Bek's knife stopped moving. "He leaves room for anything he thinks he's winning."

"Good." Casper pulled out his old map, the one with his own young handwriting on it, the one that lied. "Then tomorrow we send him coordinates."

Wren started to grin. Ila looked between them, sensing the weather changing into something she might actually like. And far behind them, downriver, a boy with a limp was telling a woman in Harrow Bend about his aunt, and a colonel was counting days he believed he owned.

Above the rock shelf, the Serpent Range wore its first white, thin as breath on glass.
