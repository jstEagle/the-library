## 11. Ila's Milestone

Ila counted the days the way her father had taught her to count stones: not by how many there were, but by how they fit together.

Day one of the climb out of the flats: a way-station with a leaking roof and a well that tasted of iron. Day two: scrubland, and Wren teaching her which lizards were food and which were lies. Day three: the first gray shoulders of the Serpent Range rising ahead like something sleeping under a blanket of haze.

And on day three, in the shadow of an old road that nobody used anymore, she found the milestone.

She almost rode past it. That was the thing about broken things — people's eyes slid off them like water off waxed canvas. Casper's eyes especially. He was always looking at the horizon, at the passes, at the sky measuring snow. Wren looked behind them, at the dust for outriders. Bek looked at everything, all the time, like the whole world might be a unit of soldiers waiting for orders.

Nobody looked down.

But Ila looked down, because that was where her father had always told her to look. *The ground tells you what people valued,* he'd said, kneeling in the dust of their yard with his chisel worn to a stub. *Foundations, Ila. Everything true starts below the knee.*

The milestone lay in three pieces half-buried in the sand, and it was Veyran work. She knew before she'd even brushed it clean. She knew from the cut of the stone, the fine chisel-toothed finish along the edges that only Republic masons bothered with anymore, the little beveled lip at the base that meant it had been set to stand two hundred years. And when she dug the largest piece free and turned it over, there it was, faint as breath on glass:

The seal. The seven-pointed star inside the circle, the old mark of Veyra, worn nearly smooth but still there, still saying *I was made by hands that believed in something.*

Ila sat down in the sand next to it and did not move for a long time.

* * *

Her father had died over a milestone too. Not this one — one back east, near the salt flats, a marker he'd been hired to repair by a woman who paid him in flour and didn't ask questions. It was illegal work now. Everyone knew that. The new authorities in the river towns had spent three years chiseling the star off every stone they could find, grinding the Republic out of the roads the way you'd grind out a stain. Her father said yes to the woman because they needed the flour, and because — Ila understood this later, in the nights after, turning it over and over like a stone in her hand — because he thought some things shouldn't be erased just because erasing them was fashionable.

Four men came. They didn't argue with him. They didn't even really hurt him, not at first. They made him watch while they smashed the milestone he'd just finished, and then they asked him if he understood why, and he said — Ila had been hiding behind the water barrel, small enough to fit in the gap between the staves — he said, "Because you're afraid of a rock."

They killed him in the yard. Ila had run. She'd run the way children run, without thinking, without plan, legs doing all the deciding, and she'd kept running for two days until she collapsed at the edge of the dying way-station town where Casper and Wren found her, filthy and furious and twelve years old, biting the hand of the first adult who tried to help her.

That had been eleven days ago. She hadn't bitten anyone since. But she hadn't forgotten either. She didn't think she was going to forget. Some things went into you like a chisel into stone, and after that they were just part of what you were made of.

She knelt beside the broken milestone and put her palm flat against the seal.

"Hi," she whispered. It felt stupid, talking to a rock. She did it anyway. "I know somebody who can fix you."

* * *

She waited until the camp was settled. That was important. Casper got twitchy after sundown, checking the horses, checking the sky, checking the dark line of the range like it might have moved since the last time he checked. Bek slept light and woke lighter. Wren — Wren was the problem. Wren noticed things. Wren had noticed within a day that Ila hoarded string, and within two days that she counted her steps, and within three days that she talked in her sleep, though mercifully Wren had never said what about.

So Ila waited for the deep part of evening, when Casper sat hunched over his maps by the fire and Wren took first watch and Bek walked the perimeter with her rifle slung loose, humming something low and tuneless that Ila suspected was a soldier's song with the words worn off.

She took her father's chisel from the bottom of her bag. She'd carried it through everything — the running, the town, the river crossing where she'd nearly lost the bag and screamed loud enough that Bek dove in after it without hesitating. She took the mallet too, and the little tin of pitch she'd traded two of Wren's ration bars for at the last town, and she slipped out past the firelight into the dark.

The moon was up, more than half, enough to work by. Masons' moon, her father called it. *Any fool can carve in daylight, Ila. The good ones work when nobody's watching.*

The milestone came apart into three pieces: the cap with the seal, the shaft with the distance numbers, the base. Two of the breaks were clean — old breaks, frost breaks, the kind that went with the grain. The third was ugly, smashed, deliberate. Somebody had hit this stone hard, more than once, with something heavy. Somebody had wanted it dead.

Ila fitted the pieces together dry first, the way her father taught her. No mortar until you know the joint. The cap seated onto the shaft with a sound like a held breath being let go. The base was harder; the smash had chewed the tenon, and she had to chip away the crushed stone to get to solid rock underneath, working by moonlight, tapping gentle, gentle, the way you'd tap a sleeping animal.

She was so focused that she didn't hear the footsteps until they stopped right behind her.

"What are you doing."

Wren's voice. Not angry. Worse — curious.

Ila didn't turn around. "Go away."

"You snuck out of camp." Wren crouched beside her, and Ila saw her eyes go to the seal on the cap, and saw the exact moment Wren understood. Thieves were fast like that. "You're fixing it."

"I'm *repairing* it. Fixing is what you do to fences."

"It's a Veyran milestone."

"I know what it is."

"It's illegal. You know it's illegal. There are probably towns within thirty miles where they'd—" Wren stopped herself. Recalibrated. Wren was always recalibrating; Ila could practically hear the little gears. "You want to get us all killed over a rock?"

"It's not a rock." Ila finally turned. She was surprised to find her eyes hot. She hadn't planned on crying. Crying was a waste of water and she'd promised herself she wouldn't do it anymore. "It has names on it. Distances. Towns that don't exist anymore except on this stone. This is the only place left that says they were real."

"That's not—"

"My father died for one of these." The words came out flat and hard, like dropped tools. "So don't tell me what it is. You weren't there."

For once — for maybe the first time since Ila had known her — Wren Sallow had nothing to say. She stood there in the moonlight with her thief's face gone carefully blank, and then, slowly, she crouched back down.

"Show me the joint," she said quietly.

* * *

Of course Casper woke up. Of course he did. He came out of the dark with his bedroll still around his shoulders like a cloak, and Bek a step behind him with her rifle unslung, and both of them stopped dead at the sight of it: the milestone standing upright again in the sand, two-thirds assembled, its seal catching the moon.

Casper stared at it for a long time. Long enough that Ila started counting — she counted when she was nervous, another thing from her father — and she got to forty-one before he spoke.

"Ila." His voice was careful, the voice adults used when they were walking on ice. "Do you understand what we're doing out here? What's chasing us?"

"Yes."

"Ashgrave would burn this stone. He'd burn every stone like it in the world, and everyone who ever touched one. He's already trying to kill us, and you've just made us easier to find. A repaired milestone is a signpost. It says *Veyran survivors passed here.*"

"I know."

"And you did it anyway."

"She did it anyway," Wren said, from her spot by the base, arms crossed. "I helped. Just so we're clear on who's getting executed."

"This isn't funny, Wren."

"I'm not laughing."

Bek hadn't said anything. She'd walked a slow circle around the milestone, and now she stopped in front of it, and Ila watched the soldier's face do something complicated. Bek had served the Republic before she'd served Ashgrave before she'd served no one. She'd told them that much, one night, in the flat voice she used for the worst facts. Now her fingers came up and touched the seal, tracing the seven points of the star, and when she spoke her voice was strange.

"There was one of these outside my mother's village. Kilometer marker, 214 to the river crossing. I used to sit on it as a girl and pretend it was a throne." She looked at Ila. "They knocked it down the week after the purge. Took four men most of a morning. Stone doesn't give up easy."

"Neither do I," Ila said.

Casper rubbed his face with both hands. Ila watched him do the math she'd already done — the risk, the exposure, the miles still between them and the pass — and she watched him arrive at the answer she'd arrived at, and she watched him hate it.

"The break on the base is bad," he said finally. "The tenon's shattered. You'll never get a load-bearing repair with pitch alone. It'll topple in the first wind."

"So we pack it with rubble and set it in a hole," Ila said. "Like a fence post. My father did it that way when the footing was rotten. It holds."

"It holds for a season. Maybe two."

"Two seasons is two seasons longer than it's been standing."

Something moved across Casper's face then — something old and tired and, Ila thought, a little bit grateful, like a man who'd been carrying a heavy pack alone and had just been offered someone to share the strap. He crouched down next to the base and ran his thumb along the shattered tenon, reading the stone the way she read it, and she realized, watching him, that he wasn't just a ranger. There were mason's habits in his hands. Cartographer's apprentice, he'd said once. Maps were drawn by men who understood stone.

"All right," he said. "All right. Here's how we do it so it actually lasts. Bek — dig the hole, wide as your shoulders, deep as your elbow. Wren — there's a slab of granite twenty paces north, I saw it coming in. We'll crush it for fill. Ila—"

He looked at her, and in the moonlight his eyes were steady.

"—you set the stone. Your father's trade. We're just the labor."

Ila's throat went tight. She nodded, because nodding was all that would come out.

* * *

They worked until the moon went down and the stars took over, and it was the best night Ila could remember since before the yard, since before the running. Bek dug like digging was a military operation, efficient and silent, throwing the spoil in a neat windrow. Wren came back with the granite slab over her shoulder like it weighed nothing — *don't ask how*, she said, grinning — and they broke it with the mallet's flat and filled the hole in courses, tamping each layer the way her father had taught her, *pack it like you mean it, Ila, lazy packing is a lie you tell the future.*

Casper held the shaft plumb while she worked the cap home. The joint closed with a whisper. She packed pitch into the seam and smoothed it with her thumb, and stepped back, and there it stood: a Veyran milestone, upright on the old road, its seal facing the dawn side of the sky where the light would find it first.

"Distances are wrong now," Casper murmured, reading the carved numbers. "Towns on here are ash. Roads are gone."

"Then it's a grave marker," Ila said. "Grave markers don't need the roads to be real. They just need the names."

Nobody argued. The fire had burned low behind them, and the Serpent Range stood black against the stars, closer than yesterday, closer than tomorrow would allow. Bek shouldered her rifle and said, quietly, that she'd take second watch, and Wren said she'd take third, and somehow the watches had rearranged themselves around the work without anyone deciding it, the way a family arranges itself around a table.

Before they turned in, Ila did one last thing. She took her father's chisel and, on the base, in the small neat letters he'd drilled into her hands over a hundred evenings, she cut four words into fresh stone:

*MENDED BY THE LIVING.*

Not her name. Not her father's. Names could be hunted. But the fact itself — that someone had come, and cared, and refused — that could stand.

Casper read it upside down as she finished. He didn't say anything. But he put his hand briefly on top of her head, the way her father used to, and left it there for exactly three heartbeats, and Ila decided that was enough.

* * *

They broke camp at gray light, and it was Bek, on the ridge with her spyglass, who went rigid and whistled them up short.

Behind them, far back across the flats, dust. Not weather-dust. Moving dust. Organized. A column, small but disciplined, crawling along the line of the old road — the same road, Ila realized with a cold drop in her stomach, that ran straight past the milestone. Straight past the signpost she'd raised in the dark.

"How long do we have?" Casper asked, already pulling the map.

"At their pace?" Bek lowered the glass. Her jaw was tight. "Two days. Maybe less."

"And the pass?"

Bek turned north, toward the mountains, and Ila followed her gaze and felt her breath catch — because above the high gray shoulders of the Serpent Range, where the sky should have been clean, a white veil had begun to fall. Thin. Far away. Falling anyway.

First snow.

"Everyone mount up," Casper said. "Now. We race it or we bury each other in it."
