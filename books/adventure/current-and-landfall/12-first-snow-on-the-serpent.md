## 12. First Snow on the Serpent

The Serpent Range announced itself three days before we could see it, the way a lie announces itself. First the river went cold and quick, running off stone instead of sand. Then the air changed—thinner, cleaner, with an edge to it that made Wren pull her collar up and Ila ask, twice, whether mountains could smell like that or if it was something else.

"Snow," Bek said, when Ila asked her the second time. "That's snow you're smelling. Somewhere above us, it's already falling."

Ila looked up at me with the particular gravity she'd developed since the milestone—the look of a girl who had decided the adults around her were custodians of things she cared about more than they did. "Is it early?"

"It's early," I said.

"How early?"

"Early enough that we stop talking about it and start walking faster."

We walked faster. The horses felt it too; they climbed with their heads low and their ears back, reading the mountain the way old soldiers read a skyline. By midday the foothills rose around us in folds of gray granite and wind-bent pine, and by late afternoon the first flakes came down—not the fat, lazy flakes of a storm's opening but small, dry grains, the kind that fall when the sky has been waiting all season and has no patience left for ceremony.

Wren caught one on her glove and studied it like a jeweler assessing a stone. "It's not even trying yet."

"That's what worries me." I reined up where the trail bent along a shelf above the valley, and there it was: the low pass, the Kettle Gap, the door every map I'd ever drawn marked as the way through. From this distance it looked like a notch bitten out of the ridgeline, and above the notch hung a cloud that wasn't moving. It sat on the pass the way a hand sits on a mouth.

Bek rode up beside me and followed my gaze. She was quiet a long moment. Then she said, in the flat voice she used for facts she wished weren't facts, "That's closed."

"You can't know that from here."

"I can know that from here." She pointed with her chin. "See the cloud? It's not weather, Casper. Weather moves. That's accumulation. Wind's packing it into the gap and it's not letting go. By tomorrow there'll be ten feet of it. By the day after, the cornices start calving onto the switchbacks." She turned to look at me, and her breath came out white between us. "Nobody's going through the Kettle Gap this year. Nobody goes through it some years even without the snow starting early."

Ila had stopped her pony close enough to hear. She didn't say anything. She just looked at the pass, then at me, and I watched a twelve-year-old do the arithmetic that adults do badly: *if the door is shut, what are we standing here for?*

"There's another way," I said.

Wren snorted. "There's always another way. That's how people die."

"The high traverse." I pointed higher, to where the ridge above the gap climbed in a long gray stair toward a saddle that even from here looked less like a path than a rumor. "Three days' climb. It stays above the treeline for most of it. No shelter after the second morning. But it doesn't funnel the snow the way the gap does—it scours. Wind takes the snow off the traverse almost as fast as it lands. It's exposed, it's cold, and it will kill you if you make a mistake. But it's open."

"Open," Bek repeated. "You've been over it?"

"Once. Drawing it." I kept my eyes on the saddle. "My mentor wanted the high country mapped because nobody else would bother. He said maps are written for the people who come after, and the people who come after always come in worse weather than you did."

"Poetic," Wren said. "Was he right?"

"He was right about everything except the parts that mattered."

Nobody laughed. The flakes kept coming down, small and dry and patient, ticking against our coats like sand.

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of an outcrop, a fire small enough to hide and hot enough to matter. Ila slept against Bek's side, wrapped in both their cloaks, her hands still black-rimmed with milestone dust no amount of washing had fully lifted. Wren sat across the fire from me, sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen, which was how she thought.

"Say the traverse kills us," she said finally. "Lay it out. What's the actual ledger?"

"Cold. Exposure. A fall on the second day, where the trail crosses the scree. If the storm catches us above the treeline, we dig in and wait it out, and waiting is its own kind of dying if we haven't got wood."

"And if we go back?"

"We don't go back."

She set the knife down. "I know we don't go back. I'm asking what 'back' costs, so I can weigh it against 'forward.' Ashgrave's outriders are behind us—I counted four signposts of them at the last ford, and Bek counted five. The way-station towns are burned or bought. Every road south funnels toward his garrison. So 'back' isn't a direction, it's a debt." She looked at me across the fire. "I'm a thief, Casper. I think in ledgers. Give me the numbers."

I gave her the numbers as honestly as I could. Three days up. One day's margin before the storm system Bek could read in the clouds arrived in earnest. Two chances out of three, maybe, if the weather held and nobody fell and the wardens—

I stopped on *wardens* and heard myself stop.

"What?" Wren said. "What about the wardens?"

The high traverse had wardens. Every pass in the Serpents did—an inheritance from the Republic, men and women paid in salt and grain to keep the trails flagged, the cairns built, the shelters stocked. Most of them had stayed on after the fall, because a warden's job outlives the government that names him. I hadn't thought about them in three years, because I hadn't let myself think about anything from Veyra longer than it took to walk away from it.

And now, sitting in the firelight with snow beginning in earnest, I remembered something else. Something Bek had said once, half-asleep, feverish from her frostbite, words she probably didn't know she'd spent.

"Bek," I said quietly.

She opened her eyes. She'd been sleeping light since the ford—sleeping the way soldiers sleep, in shallow water, ready to stand.

"The wardens on the traverse," I said. "Tessaly's people. Are they still honest?"

The fire cracked. Ila stirred against her side and settled again.

Bek sat up slowly, and I watched her decide, in real time, how much truth the night could hold. "Casper," she said, "when Ashgrave bought the Kettle Gap—and he bought it, that's why the gap's closure didn't worry him, why his outriders ride like men with a schedule instead of men chasing us—he didn't just buy the weather luck. He bought the wardens. All of them. Tessaly included, far as I know. Salt, grain, hard coin, and a promise of rank in whatever Veyra he's planning to resurrect. The wardens report movement on every pass. They report to him."

The cold came into the circle of firelight and sat down among us like a guest.

"So the traverse isn't a door," Wren said. "It's a doorway with a man behind it getting paid to count who walks through."

"Yes," Bek said.

"And he'll sell us," Ila said, awake now, her voice thin and furious. "Like everyone sells everyone."

"Not necessarily," Bek said. Her voice changed when she spoke to Ila—lower, slower, the voice of someone handling something breakable. "Wardens aren't soldiers. They took Ashgrave's coin because the alternative was starving, most of them. Coin buys silence easier than it buys betrayal. There may be a difference between a man who reports you passed and a man who hunts you down himself."

"And if there isn't?" Wren asked.

"Then we learn it on the mountain," Bek said, "and we learn it too late."

* * *

I took first watch, because I wanted it. I wanted the cold and the dark and the job of looking south, where the outriders would come from if they came. Watching is a kind of prayer for people who don't pray.

The snow thickened after midnight. Not a storm yet—a promise of one, the sky lowering itself over the peaks like a lid finding its pot. Below us, the Kettle Gap had disappeared entirely under its cloud. Above us, the traverse ran up into darkness, and somewhere up in that darkness were cairns I had built with my own hands at nineteen, stacking stones while my mentor called out bearings, arguing cheerfully about whether a map should tell the truth or merely be useful.

He'd have known what to do about the wardens. He'd have known Tessaly—did know Tessaly, drank with her, argued cartography with her until dawn. And I understood, sitting there with the snow ticking on my shoulders, that the mountain was going to make me pay for that knowledge. That the price of the traverse might not be measured in miles at all.

Behind me, I heard Wren turn over in her bedroll and say, without lifting her head, "Whatever you're deciding, decide it walking. We move at first light."

Ahead of us: three days of climb, a warden who knew my mentor's name, and a colonel's coin.

Behind us: Ashgrave, patient as winter, certain that every door was already bought.

Above us: the snow, coming down now in earnest, erasing the trail one flake at a time, as if the mountain had opinions about who should follow.

I woke Ila gently when it was time to change watches, and she blinked up at me and asked the question I'd been carrying all night like a stone in my boot.

"Casper. If the wardens are bought—how do we buy them back?"

I didn't have an answer. But I knew, watching her small face go serious in the firelight, that whatever the answer was, it wouldn't be coin.
