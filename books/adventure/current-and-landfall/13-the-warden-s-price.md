## 13. The Warden's Price

The pass warden's station sat in the last fold of the foothills like a tooth left in an old jaw—stone, squat, and stubborn, with a chimney smoking against a sky gone the color of dirty wool. Wren saw it first because she was riding point, which Casper had started letting her do somewhere around the fourth day out of the way-station, a promotion she suspected had less to do with trust than with the fact that Ila talked less when Wren was ahead of her.

"Smoke," Wren said, and held up a hand.

Bek Odumane reined in beside her, rifle across her saddle, eyes doing that soldier thing where they catalogued everything and committed to nothing. "One chimney. No sentries on the ridge. Either it's abandoned or whoever lives there isn't afraid of us."

"Or doesn't know we're coming."

"Somebody always knows we're coming." Bek said it without drama, the way you'd note the weather. She'd been saying things like that since the ford, since the morning she'd turned her rifle on her own unit and walked away from eleven years of uniform. Wren had decided she liked her, mostly, in the way you like a knife that has never once been pointed at you.

Casper came up with Ila riding pillion behind him, the girl wrapped in a blanket two sizes too big, her chin tucked so only her eyes showed. Three days of climbing had put a gray cast under his skin. He looked at the station for a long moment, and something moved through his face that Wren had learned to read by now—the cartographer reading ground, except this time the ground was memory.

"Tessaly," he said.

"You know her?" Wren asked.

"She was my mentor's closest friend. Odile Tessaly. She held the western approaches for Veyra for thirty years." He said it flatly, but his hand had gone to the map case at his chest, the unconscious gesture he made whenever the Republic got too close. "She'll have heard we're coming. She hears everything on this mountain."

"Then let's not keep a lady waiting," Wren said.

* * *

The warden met them in the yard, not the door, which told Wren everything about how the next hour would go.

She was older than Wren expected—not old, exactly, but weathered down to essentials, the way driftwood is. Gray hair cut short as a soldier's, hands bare despite the cold, and a long rifle cradled in the crook of her arm with the ease of forty years' habit. Behind her, the station door stood open and warm light spilled out, and Wren understood: the warmth was deliberate. An invitation and a test in the same gesture.

"Casper Nakamura." Tessaly said the name the way you'd set a stone on a scale. Her eyes went down the rest of them—a defected sergeant, a thief, a child—and Wren watched her decide not to be surprised by any of it. "You look like him."

"Him?"

"Marek. Your mentor." She said the name and Casper flinched, actually flinched, a small mechanical jerk like a gear catching. "You have his way of looking at country. Like you're already drawing it."

"I need the high traverse," Casper said. "The low pass is closed. Snow's coming in behind us and Ashgrave's outriders are coming in behind that."

"I know who's coming." Tessaly didn't move. The rifle stayed where it was, which was nowhere in particular and everywhere. "I've known for six days. I know about the chest, the manifest, the man who wants to buy himself a republic with other people's names. I know what you're carrying, boy, or I know enough." She tilted her head toward the open door. "And I know why you stopped at the tree line this morning and looked back south for a full minute before you'd come on. So. Come inside. All of you. We'll talk about price."

Wren caught Casper's eye. *Price.* Not toll, not fee. Price.

They went inside.

* * *

The station's single room was small and fiercely warm, dominated by a stove, a table scarred by decades of maps being unrolled and rolled again, and walls hung with instruments Wren half recognized—sextants, calipers, a brass alidade gone green at the joints. On one wall, framed behind glass gone cloudy with age, hung a survey chart of the Serpent Range drawn in a fine, patient hand. Every contour line looked loved.

Marek's hand, Wren realized. The mentor. Dead three years, or missing, or whatever word people used when a republic fell on top of them.

Tessaly put water on to boil without asking anyone anything, which was its own kind of authority. Ila gravitated immediately to the chart on the wall, nose almost touching the glass, and Tessaly watched her do it with an expression Wren couldn't parse—something between pain and recognition.

"You can read a survey?" Tessaly asked her.

"My da was a stonemason. He taught me to read buildings. This is like that, only sideways." Ila traced a line without touching the glass. "This is the traverse. The high one."

"It is."

"They killed him for fixing a milestone," Ila said, still looking at the chart. "A Veyran one. With the seal on it. They said he was making a statement." Her voice didn't waver; it had gone past wavering into something harder. "He was just fixing a road marker. Roads are supposed to tell you where you are."

Nobody answered that, because there wasn't an answer. Bek sat with her back to the wall and her rifle within reach and said nothing at all, which was how Bek prayed.

Tessaly poured four cups of tea and pushed them across the table, then stood with her own cup untouched, both hands around it like it might go somewhere.

"Now," she said. "Here is how it will be. The traverse is mine. I've held this station since before your Republic ate itself, and I'll hold it after whatever comes next eats itself too. Wardens don't take sides; wardens keep the door. That's the whole religion." She looked at Casper. "But doors cost. And yours costs more than most, because you're not just asking me to open a pass. You're asking me to help carry something over my mountain that half the armed men in three provinces want buried. So the price is this, Nakamura, and it's not negotiable, and it's not coin."

She set the cup down.

"Tell me what happened to Marek Solenne. The truth. All of it. Because three years ago he wrote to me that his apprentice had gone south ahead of him and he'd follow in spring. And then no letter came in spring, or ever again, and I have spent three years not knowing whether my oldest friend died in the purge or fled east or—" her voice did something very small and quickly corrected itself, "—or walked into the salt flats because he couldn't live in a world without his maps. You were the last person with him. So you'll tell me. And if I believe you, the door opens. If I don't, you can climb the scree face on the north side and let the mountain decide, and I'll watch you try from right here with tea."

The room went quiet except for the stove ticking.

Wren looked at Casper. In the weeks since the river, since the chest, since all of it, she had seen him tired, scared, furious, and once—briefly, at the ford, when Bek turned her rifle—almost radiant. But she had never seen him look like this. Like a man standing at the edge of a bridge he'd been avoiding for three years.

"There were soldiers at the archive annex," he said finally. "The night of the purge. Ashgrave's men, though I didn't know his name then. They'd come for the master rolls—all the records, every registry, every map archive. Marek had the annex keys. He could have locked it and walked away. Nobody was hunting cartographers yet; it was the first week, they were still mostly burning paper instead of people." His hands were flat on the table, pressing down as if the table might lift off. "Instead he sent me out the back with a satchel of the primary surveys. The originals. Everything else—the copies, the duplicates, the work of twenty years—he stayed to burn it himself so Ashgrave's men wouldn't get a clean set."

"He was destroying his life's work," Tessaly said quietly.

"He was keeping it out of their hands. There's a difference, and he knew it, and I—" Casper stopped. Started again. "There was a side door and a service stair and a route along the aqueduct that I knew better than anyone alive, because I'd surveyed it. I could hear them coming up through the lower floors. And Marek looked at me and said, *Go now, Casper. Someone has to remember where things were.*"

"And you went," Tessaly said.

"I ran." It came out of him like something torn rather than spoken. "I didn't hesitate—I want to say I hesitated, I've spent three years telling myself I hesitated, weighing it, choosing the greater duty. I didn't. I heard boots on the stairs and my legs decided before I did. I went down the aqueduct and I didn't look back, and the annex burned that night with him in it, and I have been walking north ever since counting miles because miles are easier to count than—" His breath caught. "Than what I owe."

Wren found she was holding her teacup hard enough to hurt her fingers. She made herself set it down.

"The manifest," Tessaly said. "Your name was on it."

"Because I drew the treasury map. The false one. Marek and I built it together—it was a contingency, a decoy, a shell game for exactly the kind of man Ashgrave turned out to be. My name on the manifest meant I was a keeper of the secret. Which means someone out there has always known I survived." He laughed, and it was the worst sound Wren had ever heard him make. "Three years I thought I'd gotten away clean. I hadn't gotten away at all. I'd just been carried, unwrapped, waiting for someone to come collect me."

Silence. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind had begun to work at the eaves, the first real breath of the storm that was chasing them up the mountain.

Tessaly picked up her tea and drank it, slowly, all the way down, and Wren realized the woman had needed the time as much as Casper had.

"He burned the annex," Tessaly said at last. "Himself. Deliberately. Knowing they were coming."

"Yes."

"And he sent you out with the originals because a dead archivist protects nothing, but a living one—" She set the cup down with a click. "That's not a man being abandoned, boy. That's a man spending himself. Cartographers think in deposits. He put you somewhere safe and called it a map."

"He deserved better than being spent."

"Of course he did. Everyone does. That's not the question." Tessaly came around the table, and up close Wren could see she was crying, had maybe been crying for some portion of the last hour, tears that froze halfway down a face too weathered to hide much. "The question is whether you've spent these three years running, or carrying. And I've watched you since you rode into my yard. You came back north, into the teeth of it, with a thief and a deserter and a child, hauling the last honest records of a dead country over my mountain in a snow season. That's not running, Casper Nakamura. That's the heaviest carrying I've ever seen anybody do."

She turned to the wall, took down a ring of iron keys, and pulled one free—an old one, black with handling.

"The traverse gate is two miles up, past the cairns. It locks against the weather, not against travelers; any warden worth her salt leaves it passable. But the wardens below—" she paused, and her jaw tightened, "—Tessaly keeps the door, but Tessaly is also the last warden on this stretch who hasn't taken Ashgrave's silver. The others sold the passes in October. Which means once you're through my gate, you're not done paying. You're just done paying me."

"What did it cost them?" Bek asked, her first words in an hour. "To buy the wardens."

"Legitimacy," Tessaly said. "He doesn't pay in coin alone. He pays in futures. Promises of rank in the new Veyra, land grants, pardons. Cheap things, to a man printing his own country." She handed the key to Casper, closing his fingers over it with both of her hands. "For Marek. Tell the mountain I kept the door."

"I will," Casper said, and his voice broke on the second word, and nobody pretended not to notice, and nobody minded.

* * *

They saddled in the blue dark before dawn. Tessaly stood in the yard with a lantern, and when Ila hugged her—quickly, fiercely, the way children hug when they've learned hugs can be the last kind—the old warden held on a moment longer than she probably meant to.

"Girl," she said. "You fix milestones. You understand that's not a small thing. A country is just a road that tells you where you are. Keep fixing the road."

Then they were climbing, switchbacking up through the cairns with the storm shouldering down from the summit like something with intent, and Wren rode beside Casper and watched him hold the black key in his fist the whole first mile, not pocketing it, just holding it.

"You okay?" she asked, which was inadequate, and she knew it.

"No," he said. "But lighter. Is that strange? I gave away the worst thing I had and I feel lighter and worse at the same time."

"That's not strange. That's just how weight works."

Ahead, the traverse gate showed as a dark seam in the rock face, and beyond it the high country waited, white already at its shoulders. Wren squinted against the wind and saw what she'd been afraid she'd see: fresh tracks in the new snow above the gate. Boot prints. Many. Descending.

Somebody had come down the traverse in the night.

"Bek," she called. "Tracks."

Bek was already dismounting, rifle out, kneeling over the prints with snow gathering on her shoulders. She studied them a long moment, then looked up, and her face told Wren everything before her mouth did.

"These aren't three days old," Bek said. "These came down *last night*. Heading south." She paused. "Regular army tread. Ashgrave's issue. Somebody from the top just went down to report that we're here."

Above them, the storm swallowed the summit whole.
