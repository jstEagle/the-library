## 14. Whiteout

The storm came down the Serpent like something with intent.

One hour we were climbing through scree the color of old bone, Ila riding double on Bek's packhorse with her hood cinched so tight only her nose showed, and the next hour the sky simply closed. No wind at first—just a whiteness above us that thickened until it swallowed the peaks, and then the wind arrived all at once, sideways, carrying ice in it that stung like flung gravel.

"Off the trail!" Bek shouted, and her voice was torn apart before it finished.

I couldn't see ten feet. Wren's gray mare was a shadow beside mine, then a rumor, then nothing. I hauled my horse around by feel and followed the sound of hooves on stone, trusting that Bek knew this traverse better than any of us, because the alternative was standing still and letting the mountain bury us where we stood.

She found the cave by accident, or maybe not by accident—I learned later she'd marked it on her first patrol through the pass, three years ago, back when she still wore Veyran gray. A fissure in the cliff face, deep enough to walk into, high enough to stand at the mouth but nowhere else. We crowded the horses against the inner wall, stripped their tack, and stood listening to the storm scream past the entrance like a freight of knives.

Nobody said anything for a long time. There was nothing to say that the wind wouldn't have said better.

* * *

We inventoried by candlelight, because counting what you have is the first thing you do when you're trapped, and the last thing you want to do.

The number came out ugly. Two sacks of barley meal, half a slab of salt pork, a bag of dried apricots Ila had been nursing since the way-station town, tea enough for four days if we stretched it, and the hardtack nobody liked. Five people, four horses, and a storm that showed no sign of being anything other than exactly what Tessaly had warned us it would be when she opened the pass: *the early one comes mean or it doesn't come at all.*

"We don't eat tomorrow," Bek said. "Or the day after. Horses get hay—we brought enough for six days if we ration."

"And after that?" Wren asked.

"After that we're eating the horses, and after that we're a story someone tells in the spring." She said it flat, without drama, the way she said everything. "So let's not be here after that."

Ila had gone quiet in the corner where the horses' warmth pooled, and I caught Wren watching her with an expression I was starting to recognize—the thief doing arithmetic on other people's suffering, except the arithmetic always came out wrong for her now. Three weeks ago Wren Sallow would have calculated her own odds and cut them loose. I'd watched her do less kind things for less reason. Now she sat down next to the girl and split an apricot in half without being asked.

Bek's hands were the other problem. She'd taken off her gloves at the ford—that was the day she turned her rifle on Captain Merrick's squad, the day she stopped being Sergeant Odumane of the 4th Outriders—and the cold had gotten into two fingers of her right hand sometime during the climb and stayed there. They were white as tallow past the second knuckle, waxy, wrong. She kept them tucked under her arms and pretended they weren't.

"Bek," I said.

"It's fine."

"Let me see."

"It's *fine*, Nakamura."

Wren looked over. Ila looked over. The whole cave looked over, which was the worst thing that could have happened, because Bek Odumane could survive a mutiny and a blizzard and a colonel's betrayal, but she could not survive being pitied by a twelve-year-old.

Ila crawled over and took Bek's hand before anyone could stop her, the way children take things, with total confidence in their right to. She turned it toward the candlelight and frowned at the fingers like a stonemason's daughter inspecting bad mortar.

"My da fixed frostbite once," she said. "A quarry man. You warm it slow. Not fast. Fast kills it." She looked up. "You have to let somebody help. That's the part he hated too."

Something went through Bek's face—fast, gone, like a fish under ice. Then she laughed, one short bark, and let Ila hold her hand against the small warmth between them.

"All right," Bek said. "Slow. Fine. But if you tell anyone the 4th Outriders got nursed by a mason's brat, I'll deny it to my grave."

"You'll deny it anyway," Wren said. "You're a deserter."

"I'm a *defector*. There's paperwork."

And just like that the cave got easier to breathe in. Not warmer—the cold sat in the stone and would sit there for days—but easier. I've thought since about how that works, how a group of strangers becomes a unit, and I think it happens in moments exactly that small. Not the big sacrifices. The apricot split in half. The joke about paperwork. Somebody letting a child hold their ruined hand and not pulling away.

* * *

Sleep was the enemy. We all knew it. Cold plus exhaustion plus thin air equals sleep you don't wake from, and Bek said so in her flat sergeant's voice, and so we made the watch rotation and swore to it, and then the storm did what storms do, which is make time stop meaning anything.

I took the middle watch, which meant I sat at the cave mouth with a blanket around my shoulders and watched the whiteout churn beyond the rock like milk boiling, and every twenty minutes I got up and walked the line of sleepers and put my hand near their mouths to feel breath.

Wren slept like a cat, curled tight, one hand open near her knife even unconscious. Bek slept like a soldier, flat on her back, breathing slow, ready. Ila slept like a child finally somewhere safe, sprawled and boneless with her mouth open, and every time I checked on her I felt something in my chest I didn't have a name for yet. I'd carried guilt for three years the way other men carry a stone in the boot—familiar, almost comfortable. This was a different weight. Lighter and heavier at once.

On my third round, Wren's eyes were open.

"Can't sleep?" I asked.

"I can always sleep. It's my best skill after locks." She sat up, keeping the blanket around her shoulders. "You're limping worse on your left. Don't argue, I watch feet, it's a professional habit. Sit down before your watch ends and let me look at it."

"There's nothing to look at. Old injury."

"From Veyra?"

She asked it lightly, but I heard the real question underneath, the one she'd been circling since the dead spring where I first caught her trail: *who are you, actually, Casper Nakamura, name-on-the-manifest?*

I sat down. My left ankle ached the way it always ached when the temperature dropped, the souvenir of a night three years ago that I would come, in this cave, to tell almost in full.

"From the night I left," I said.

The storm filled the pause for me. Outside, the world had been erased. There was no pass, no range, no Ashgrave waiting somewhere below with his bribed wardens and his mercenary ledger. There was only the cave and its five heartbeats, and I found, sitting there in the dark with a thief who'd once stolen from me, that the erasure was almost a kindness. It's easier to say things to a blank wall.

"You know I drew the map," I said. "The treasury map. I told you that much."

"You told me that much."

"What I didn't tell you—" I worked the bootlace loose. Underneath, the old scar ran white across the tendon. "The night the purges started, my master, Aldous Ferren—he was the Republic's chief cartographer, and more than that, he was the one who hid the census rolls in the first place, though I didn't understand that then—he sent me out the north postern with the map tube and told me not to come back. And I didn't. I ran. I heard the fires behind me and I ran faster."

"You were nineteen."

"Nineteen. And he was sixty-eight, and he'd taught me every star and every contour I know, and I let him stay behind because he told me to." I flexed the ankle. The ache flared, honest and simple. "I broke this jumping the aqueduct wall. Landed wrong in the dark. And I lay in a drainage ditch for an hour with soldiers going past close enough to touch, and I remember thinking, very clearly: *he would want me to keep running.* And I've never known if that was true or if it was just the thing a coward tells himself in a ditch."

Wren was quiet a moment. When she spoke, her voice had none of its usual edge.

"You know what I did the night the Veyran garrison burned the ferry quarter? Where I grew up?"

"No."

"Took a purse off a dying man. Because it was there, and taking things is what I do." She shrugged inside the blanket. "Everybody ran from something. The ones who act like they didn't are the ones I don't trust."

"That's a terrible moral system."

"It's kept me alive."

Above us, the storm screamed. Below us, somewhere down the black throat of the pass, Ashgrave was moving—or stalled, or furious, or patient; we couldn't know. But in the cave, in the candle stub's little island of light, something had shifted again, the way it shifts and shifts in a group until one morning you realize the shifting has stopped and what's left is a shape that holds weight. Wren knew my worst night. I'd given it to her like a toll paid, and she hadn't flinched, and neither of us had died of it.

"Sleep," I told her. "Your watch is next."

"Yes, *ranger*," she said, and for once it wasn't mockery.

* * *

By the second full day, the food arithmetic had gotten uglier, and Bek's fingers had gotten worse despite Ila's slow-warming regimen, and the storm had not gotten one bit better. The wind changed pitch sometimes—a lower moan, a higher shriek—but it never stopped, never weakened, as if the mountain had decided to make an example of us.

It was Ila who saved us from the silence, and she did it the way children do things: without asking permission.

"Tell the one about the cartographer's apprentice," she demanded of me, mid-afternoon of the second day, when the candle had burned to a thumbnail and nobody had spoken for an hour. "You said you'd tell stories to keep awake. You promised."

"I promised nothing of the sort."

"You said, and I quote, 'someone talk or I'll start singing,' and Bek said she'd rather freeze, and I said fine, then *you* talk." She folded her arms. Twelve-year-olds keep records like magistrates.

So I talked. I told them about Aldous Ferren teaching me to read terrain the way you read a face—how a ridge wears its history in its shoulders, how water always tells you the truth eventually if you follow it far enough downhill. I told them about the survey trip where our mule ate the only copy of a boundary chart and Aldous spent a week redrawing it from memory and got one river wrong on purpose, just to see if the Ministry ever actually checked his work. They never did. He laughed about it for years.

Then Ila told one. About her father, and the milestone.

"He repaired the old marker on the south road. The one with the seal. Soldiers knocked it down and he set it up again and recut the letters, and he said, *names are load-bearing,* which I didn't understand then." Her voice did the thing children's voices do when they've rehearsed grief into a story to make it carryable. "He was right, though. Look at us. We're all carrying names."

Nobody spoke after that. Bek stared at the candle. Wren picked at the fringe of her blanket. And I understood that the orphaned daughter of a stonemason had just explained the entire war in three words, and that Ashgrave—with his ledgers and his mercenaries and his resurrected nation—had never understood it at all. Names aren't decoration. You knock them down, and something structural fails.

Bek told a story next, unprompted, which surprised everyone including possibly herself. About her first posting, seventeen years old, guarding a granary in a town so small the whole garrison played cards with the locals. About an old woman who fed her stew every market day and called her "sergeant" before she was one, "because you will be, girl, I can see it." She'd gone back after the purge. The town was ash. The woman's name was Maret Ovella, and Bek said it slowly, like setting down something heavy, and I remembered the census rolls wrapped in oilcloth in my pack, and I wondered—not for the first time—if Maret Ovella's name was written there.

When it was Wren's turn she claimed she didn't tell stories, and Ila informed her that this was cowardice, and Wren, outmaneuvered by a child, gave us the one about the lockmaker of Corvane who built a door that could only be opened by someone who didn't want what was behind it. She told it well. She told it, I thought, about herself.

That's how we passed the second night: trading stories against the dark, each of us paying a little truth into the common pot, the candle down to a blue bead of flame, the storm outside grinding the world to powder. Somewhere in the small hours Ila fell asleep against Bek's side, and Bek—who had once ordered artillery fire on a bridge with civilians on it, who had defected rather than do it twice—sat perfectly still for four hours so as not to wake her.

I counted breaths instead of miles. It occurred to me, dimly, that I had stopped counting miles altogether somewhere on this mountain, and that I couldn't remember when.

* * *

Dawn of the third day came gray and impossible and quiet.

Not stillness—the wind still muttered at the cave mouth—but the shriek had dropped away in the night, and when I crawled to the entrance and looked out, the world had been remade. White to every horizon, smooth and featureless, the pass buried under drifts that would take a week to firm. But the sky above was clean, hard blue, and the sun came off the snow so bright it hurt.

"Storm's broken," I said, and the word ran down the cave like current through wire.

We moved stiffly, all of us, five people who had become one organism in the dark. We ate the last of the porridge—real portions, a decision Bek made with visible pain—and repacked the horses, and Ila chattered about sunlight while Wren checked cinches and Bek flexed her bandaged hand and winced.

Then Bek, who had gone out to read the trail, came back inside with a different posture entirely. I'd learned her body language by then. This was the stance she took before delivering casualty reports.

"Two things," she said. "Good news and bad news."

"Give us the good one first for once," Wren said.

"The drifts below the traverse have slumped. There's a line down the east chute—steep, ugly, but passable today, before the sun crusts it over tonight. We can be off this mountain by dark."

"And the bad news?"

Bek held up her bandaged hand. Two fingertips, black now at the tips, the waxy white gone dark overnight.

"These are past warming. Past saving. If I don't get to a surgeon's saw in the next two days, the rot goes up the hand." She said it the way she reported everything, flat and complete. "And the second piece of bad news is that the surgeon I trust is in the valley settlement on the far side—which means the east chute, which means we move *now*—and the third piece of bad news, since apparently we're doing all of them—"

She reached into her coat and pulled out a scrap of paper, wind-scoured but legible, that she'd found pegged to a cairn fifty yards downslope. Fresh. Someone had climbed through the tail of the storm to leave it.

I took it. The handwriting was precise, unhurried, the hand of a man who had never once doubted his own authority.

*The wardens tell me you have three days' start and no more. I have patience, Nakamura, and provisions, and forty men. Come down the east chute and bring what's mine. Do this, and the girl walks free of any debt she owes me, and the child wants for nothing. Refuse, and I will meet you at the bottom regardless—you are, after all, descending into my hands either way.*

"—it's signed," Bek finished unnecessarily.

Ashgrave knew the chute. Ashgrave was below us, somewhere in the white, patient as winter itself.

I looked at Wren. Wren looked at me. Behind her, Ila had gone very still, the way she did when adults forgot she was listening.

"Well," Wren said, with the ghost of her old thief's grin, thin as the candle we'd burned to nothing. "Anyone else suddenly motivated to move fast?"
