## 15. What Bek Carried

The fever broke on the second night in the cave, and by the third morning Bek Odumane could sit up without the world tilting sideways.

She'd lost two toes to the cold on her left foot—maybe three; the bandages were too thick and her courage too thin to check. Wren had cut the boots off her with a knife meant for worse work, and Ila had held her hand through the worst of it, a twelve-year-old girl steadying a soldier twice her size, and Bek had cried like a recruit at basic training and nobody had said one word about it afterward.

That was the thing about these people. They didn't say words about things.

Now she sat propped against the cave wall where the fire's heat could reach her, wrapped in every blanket they owned, watching Casper Nakamura study his own map by lamplight for what had to be the hundredth time. He had the look of a man arguing with a ghost. His finger traced the same contour lines over and over, stopping each time at the coordinates inked in his own younger hand, and each time his jaw tightened a little more, as if the map were lying to him and he couldn't yet prove it.

Wren was asleep, curled near the fire with her back to the rock and her knife hand free even in sleep—a habit Bek recognized, because it was hers too. Ila was awake, of course. The girl never seemed to need sleep the way other people did. She was mending a sock that didn't belong to anyone in particular, working the needle with the patience of someone who'd learned early that hands needed jobs or they started shaking.

"You're staring," Ila said without looking up.

"I'm resting my eyes."

"You're staring at Casper."

Bek huffed. "Girl, you're twelve. Mind your stitches."

"I'm thirteen next month." Ila bit her thread. "And you're not staring because you're resting your eyes. You're staring because you know something about that map and you haven't said it yet. You've been doing that face since the ford."

Bek looked at her. Really looked. Small, sharp-eyed, orphaned by a man who'd died for the crime of repairing a stone marker with an old republic's seal on it—and still reading soldiers like weather.

"Your father taught you that?" she asked quietly. "Reading people?"

"He taught me stone. Stone teaches you the rest." She tied off the thread. "Cracks go where the weight is."

* * *

Casper looked up from the map then, and Bek knew the moment had arrived, the way she'd known it would eventually. Three days in a snowbound cave does something to silence. It wears it down until there's nothing left between people but the true things.

"Bek," he said. "You served under Ashgrave."

It wasn't a question. He'd earned the right to ask it anyway, dragging her down out of the whiteout with Wren hauling her other arm while the storm tried to take all three of them. He'd earned more than asking. But he asked gently, which was somehow harder to face.

"I did." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Six years. Quartermaster corps first, then field intelligence under Colonel Ashgrave directly. I was at Veyra City when it fell."

Ila's needle stopped.

Casper set the map down. "Tell me."

"You want to know if I burned anything." She met his eyes. "That's what you're really asking. Everyone who hears I was there wants to know if I lit a torch or just stood in the line and watched."

"Were you in the line?"

"I was the line." She pulled the blanket tighter, though the fever-heat was gone now and only shame kept her warm. "Ashgrave ran the census bureau's security detail in the last year. You know what the census bureau was, Nakamura? Everyone thinks it was clerks counting grain and goats. It was that. It was also the most complete record of human beings ever assembled in this part of the world. Every name. Every household. Every child registered at birth, every family relocated during the border wars, every debt, every title, every bloodline going back four generations. The Republic didn't trust its people, so it counted them. Obsessively. Beautifully. Like a jeweler cataloguing stones before selling them."

"And when the Republic fell—"

"The order came down to destroy the rolls." Bek closed her eyes. "Officially: deny the successor states any administrative inheritance. Unofficially—" She opened them again. "Unofficially, Ashgrave saw it differently. A list of every survivor, cross-referenced with property records and family ties, isn't a census anymore. It's leverage. It's an army roster of everyone with a grievance and a claim. It's a nation in a box, waiting for someone ambitious enough to open it."

The fire popped. Wind pressed against the cave mouth and gave up.

"So he didn't burn them," Casper said slowly.

"He burned *copies*. Cartloads. Made sure the smoke was visible for miles, made sure every witness went home telling the story of loyal Ashgrave destroying the old regime's dirty paperwork. And meanwhile the master rolls went into sealed chests under military seal, moved north in ones and twos, scattered across way-stations and garrison stores under manifest codes that read like supply shipments." She laughed, and it came out bitter. "Grain. Rope. Boot leather. Nobody questions a chest marked boot leather."

Casper had gone very still. "The chest in the river."

"Wasn't the treasury manifest, was it?" Bek nodded toward the map. "Look at your own document again, cartographer. You drew that map three years ago. What did they tell you it was?"

"They told me it was a contingency reserve. Gold, specie, state papers—the Republic's emergency fund, cached against exile." His voice had dropped. "I drew the route. I never saw the contents. The chests were already sealed when I surveyed the cache sites."

"How many sites?"

"Seven. Along the river corridor, spaced for wagon access." He stopped. Bek watched him arrive at it, watched the color leave his face the way it leaves a man who's found the crack in his own foundation. "Seven sites. Seven chests. And the manifest in the river chest listed names—not account holders, not signatories. *Names.* Mine among them. Names of people with clearance, people who'd worked on the caches, people who'd have to be silenced or co-opted once the contents were recovered."

"Not account holders," Bek agreed softly. "*Registrants.* People whose names appear in the master rolls. Ashgrave's manifest wasn't a list of who owned the treasure. It was a sample of what the treasure *is*. He put your name on it because your name is in the rolls—you're documented, Nakamura. You're inventory. You've been inventory since the day you registered at age six."

Ila had stopped sewing entirely. "So there's no gold."

"There might be gold. Ashgrave would want some, for paying mercenaries." Bek shifted her bandaged foot, winced, kept going. "But gold is the wrapping paper. The cargo is the rolls themselves. Every cache site holds a segment of the master census. Seven segments, seven regions. Whoever collects all seven holds the documented identity of every surviving Veyran citizen—which families lived, which fled, which children were born in exile and registered nowhere else. Proof of existence. Proof of *belonging*, written in the Republic's own hand."

"That's why he wants it before snowmelt," Wren said.

Everyone turned. She was awake—had been, probably, for some time, eyes shut, listening the way thieves listen.

Wren sat up and pushed her hair out of her face. "He doesn't just want a treasury. Anyone can rob a treasury. He wants to walk into the settlements along this river—the ones full of people who ran, who've got no papers, no legal existence, nothing but their memories—and he wants to hold the only document that says those people were ever real. Either they follow him, or they're ghosts forever. That's not buying legitimacy." Her voice hardened. "That's owning it."

"Now you understand him," Bek said. "I did six years ago, and I told myself understanding wasn't the same as agreeing. I told myself a soldier's job is the mission, not the meaning." She looked at her wrapped foot, at the place where her body had paid its first installment on that lie. "Then he ordered the clearing of the eastern districts. Not evacuation—*clearing*. Because unregistered populations were administratively inconvenient, and a clean ledger was worth more than the people on it. I signed the movement orders. My signature is on paper that sent four hundred people into the Salt Waste with three days' water."

Nobody spoke. Ila had set down the sock. She crawled across the blankets and sat down against Bek's side, small and warm and entirely unafraid, and Bek let her, because refusing would have cost more than accepting.

"Why tell us now?" Casper asked. Not cruelly. Carefully, the way a man walks ground he knows might be mined.

"Because you keep looking at that map like it's lying to you, and it's not. It's doing exactly what it was built to do." Bek nodded at it. "Show me the coordinates again. The primary cache."

He smoothed the map flat and turned the lamp toward her. Bek leaned forward, ignoring the pull of frostbitten flesh, and put her finger on the marked site.

"This is the Kettle Gorge mine. Abandoned copper works, collapsed in the quake year. I know it because Ashgrave requisitioned it as a storage depot eighteen months ago—officially for ordnance. I inventoried the crates myself." She tapped the spot twice. "There's no census segment there, Nakamura. There's ammunition, salt pork, and enough rifles for a battalion. It's a garrison. It's a *trap with a roof on it*."

Casper stared at the map. "Then the real site—"

"Is somewhere your survey was never meant to point. Think about it. They used you to draw the access routes, not the destinations. The destinations were chosen by the census bureau's own people, and your beautiful map was given false coordinates so that no single person ever held the whole picture. Standard compartmentalization. I ran the same system for him." She sat back. "Which means somewhere out there is a second document—or a memory, or a key—that corrects what you drew. And Ashgrave has been following your map this whole time, which means either he doesn't know it's false, or—"

"Or he knows exactly what it is," Wren finished, "and he's been letting us lead him to whatever we find, because we're cheaper than scouts and deniable besides."

The wind rose outside, shrieking through the pass above them, and for a long moment the only sound in the cave was the fire and the storm arguing over who owned the mountain.

"The rolls are real," Ila said at last. It wasn't a question either. This family asked very few questions. "My papa's name would be in them. Him and Mama. It would say we were real."

"It says you're real, little one." Bek put her arm around the girl's shoulders. "That's the whole point. That's what Ashgrave understands and what the Republic understood before him: a name on an official page is worth more than a name shouted in a square. He doesn't want gold. He wants to be the only man holding the proof that any of us existed." She looked across the fire at Casper, at Wren, at the small warm weight against her side. "And we're sitting in a hole in a mountain, half-starved, one soldier short of walking, holding a map to a lie."

"Then we stop holding it," Casper said.

Bek studied him. Three years ago, by her own service record, this man had been a fugitive cartographer's apprentice with a price on his head and no allies. Now he sat in a blizzard with a thief, a defector, and a child, talking about the master census of a dead republic like it was a problem with a solution.

"You have a plan," she said.

"I have the beginning of one." His finger moved on the map, away from the Kettle Gorge, tracing the high traverse toward the secondary cache markers. "But it needs a soldier who knows how Ashgrave thinks. And it needs to move fast, because the storm won't hold him any longer than it held us."

Bek looked down at her foot. Two toes, maybe three. She'd marched on worse, though never for a reason she believed in.

"Then start talking, cartographer," she said. "Because the minute this sky clears, that man is coming up this mountain—and everything he's carrying is aimed at us."

* * *

Outside, the wind shifted, and for one clear hour before dawn the clouds tore open above the Serpent Range.

From the cave mouth, wrapped in blankets, Bek saw it: the high traverse, white and silent, three days of climbing laid bare under a hard wash of stars.

And far below, tiny as sparks, lanterns moving up the valley road.

Not one column. Two.

She didn't wake the others. There was no version of waking them that helped before morning. She just sat with Ila asleep against her shoulder and watched the lights climb, and did the arithmetic she'd learned in six years of Ashgrave's service, and hated how easily the numbers came.

Two columns meant he'd split his force. Splitting meant confidence. Confidence meant he already knew where they were going.

Somebody had told him.
