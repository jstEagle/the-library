## 16. The False Summit

Wren counted the switchbacks the way she used to count locks: by feel, by rhythm, by the small betrayals of the body that told her when something was wrong.

Forty-one since dawn. The traverse had narrowed to a shelf of broken granite barely wide enough for two mules abreast, with the Serpent's north face dropping away in a haze of blue shadow and, below that, the river they'd followed for three hundred miles looking like a vein of solder. Snow came in gusts now—not falling so much as thrown, horizontal, needling her cheeks through the wool wrap. Bek had rewrapped her frostbitten fingers twice since morning, and each time the bandages came away spotted pink.

"Stop counting," Casper said beside her. He'd learned her tells. Three weeks on the road together and he'd learned them all—the way her eyes flicked left when she was lying, right when she was planning, down when she was afraid. She hated it. She'd never been read before; reading people was supposed to be her trade.

"I'm not counting."

"You're counting."

"Fine. Forty-two." She nodded up the trail. "That's what's bothering me. We should've hit the midpoint cairn by now. Casper said forty switchbacks to the saddle, and Tessaly confirmed it last night at the warden station. Forty to the cairn, then the long scree field, then the high col."

Casper shaded his eyes against the wind-driven glare. Behind them, Ila rode double on the pack mule with her arms locked around Bek's waist, her chin tucked into the sergeant's shoulder against the cold. The girl hadn't complained once in two days of climbing, which worried Wren more than complaining would have. Twelve-year-olds who went silent were twelve-year-olds conserving something.

Bek's old comrades—Maret and Doss, the two soldiers who'd walked off Ashgrave's line at the ford and never looked back—took point as they always did, moving with the easy economy of people who'd spent their lives on mountains like this. Maret had a rifle slung across her back and a habit of humming Veyran marching songs under her breath, half-remembered, wrong in places. Doss carried most of the ammunition and all of the jokes.

The wind gusted. Somewhere above them, loose stone clattered—a sound Wren had heard a hundred times on this climb and dismissed a hundred times. Rockfall was rockfall. Mountains shed their skin constantly.

But this time the clatter didn't fade. It stopped. Cut clean, the way a sound stops when someone catches it.

Wren's hand found Casper's sleeve. "There."

He'd already gone still. That was the thing about the cartographer—his stillness was total, a held breath, and once you'd seen it you understood he hadn't survived three years alone in the flats by being slow.

"Maret," he breathed, not loudly enough to carry. "Hold."

Maret raised a fist without turning. Twenty years of drill in one gesture. The column froze, mules stamping, breath steaming.

And then the mountain spoke.

Not rockfall. Rifles. A volley from the ridge above the trail—three, four shots, cracking flat echoes off the walls—and the scree slope fifty yards ahead erupted with men who'd been lying under snow-cloaks so white they might have been part of the mountain itself. Eight of them. Ten. More spilling from a cut in the rock that Wren realized, with a lurch, had been shored up with timber. Dug in. They hadn't stumbled onto an ambush. They'd walked into a position that had been built.

"No," Bek said behind her, and the word came out of her like something torn.

Because she knew them. Wren saw it happen—the moment Bek's soldier's eye sorted the figures ahead into shapes she recognized. The big man on the left with the bandaged jaw. The woman working the repeater rifle at the timbered cut, bracing it on a stone she'd clearly carried up here herself. Bek had drilled with these people. Eaten with them. Pulled them out of ravines.

"Tessaly," Casper said. Flat. Terrible. "She sold us. The timing, the route—all of it."

* * *

Later, Wren would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and find only fragments.

Maret turning, calm as arithmetic, and pushing Ila and the mules back around the switchback with one arm while unslinging her rifle with the other. Doss laughing—that awful, gallows laugh of his—and saying something Wren couldn't hear over the wind, something that made Maret snort even now. Bek screaming orders in a voice Wren had never heard from her, parade-ground voice, voice of a woman who'd commanded a platoon at twenty-five: *Down, get down, wall side, wall side—*

The second volley came from higher up. Not the dug-in position—a second team, on the rocks above, and Wren understood with sick clarity that Ashgrave hadn't just bought the wardens. He'd bought the whole pass. This wasn't an ambush laid in haste. This was a trap that had been waiting for them since before they entered the foothills, since maybe before they ever pulled the chest from the river.

Which meant the colonel had known about the traverse from the beginning. Which meant—

No time. A bullet sparked off the granite a hand's width from Casper's head and he pulled her down behind a boulder the size of a cart, and they were pressed together in the snow-dust and the ringing air, and Wren did what she always did when the world came apart: she looked for the exits.

One: back down the switchbacks. Sealed—she could hear hooves and shouting below, riders coming up the trail they'd climbed. Two: over the top, straight up the face. Suicide in this wind with two mules and a child. Three: through. Through the ambush position, through the timbered cut, onto the scree beyond, and pray the high col was closer than the bullets.

Three, then. It was always three.

"Doss is hit," Bek shouted. Wren craned around the boulder. Doss was down at the trail's edge, one leg folded wrong beneath him, propped against a stone and firing upward at the second team with the unhurried accuracy of a man who'd decided how this ended and intended to make it expensive. Maret had reached him. Was dragging him by the collar toward cover, bullets kicking white scars off the rock around them both.

"Bek!" Wren grabbed the sergeant's arm. "The cut—the timbered position—if we take it we take their cover—"

Bek looked at her, and for one heartbeat Wren watched the war happen behind the woman's eyes: the soldier who knew exactly what assaulting a fortified position cost, and the defector who understood that the men in it had once been hers.

Then Bek nodded, once, and started giving orders like the mountain belonged to her.

* * *

It almost worked. That was the cruelty of it. It almost worked.

Maret got Doss to the boulder line. Casper put three shots into the timbered cut fast enough to keep the repeater's crew ducking. Wren and Bek scrambled along the wall-side of the trail, low, using the dead ground, and Wren felt the strange clarity she'd felt once before, years ago, going over a wall in Veyra with someone else's seal ring in her teeth—the feeling of being exactly, precisely where she was supposed to be, doing the one thing she was built for.

They were ten feet from the cut when the third team opened up.

From below. From the switchbacks they'd already climbed past. Men who had let the whole column go by and lain quiet in the snow until the shooting started, closing the door behind them.

And Maret and Doss were caught between—out on the open trail with no wall at their backs, the exact place Bek had ordered everyone else away from, because they'd gone back for Doss's dropped ammunition bandolier, because Doss had said *can't leave it, we'll need it at the col,* because they were still soldiers, God help them, still thinking in terms of the next fight instead of this one.

Wren saw Maret understand it first. Saw her look at the timbered cut, at the new muzzle flashes below, and do the same arithmetic Wren had just done. One exit. None, really.

Maret shoved Doss's bandolier over the rocks toward cover—toward *them*, the useless, stupid, magnificent gesture of it—and turned to face downhill with her rifle up.

"No—" Bek was moving, breaking cover, and Wren grabbed her and missed and grabbed her again, hauling her down as a fusillade chewed the trail where she'd stood. "Bek. *Bek.* You can't."

"They're mine." Bek's voice broke open on it. "Wren, they're *mine*, they followed me out, they—"

"And they know it." Wren had her wrists now, both of them, pinning the sergeant against the stone while the world hammered and echoed. "They know it, and they chose it, and if you die on this trail too then their choice buys nothing. *Nothing.* Do you hear me? Stay alive and it means something. Get up there and finish this and it means something."

Above them, Doss was laughing again. Firing. Laughing. Then not laughing.

Then, after a long minute, not firing either.

* * *

They took the cut in the end. Of course they took it—Casper came up the blind side through the drifts with Ila's slingshot of all things whistling stones to keep heads down, and Wren put a knife into the repeater gunner's shoulder before the woman finished turning, and Bek moved through the timbered position like a verdict, disarming, breaking, not killing where she could avoid it, which was nearly everywhere, because these were still her people even now, even after this.

Eight prisoners. Two dead of theirs. And outside the cut, on the open trail where no one dared go yet because the lower team still held the switchbacks, Maret and Doss lay in the snow.

They buried them shallow, because the ground was iron and the light was failing. Stones. Casper cut a marker into a slab of granite with his cartographer's chisel, quick and sure, two names and a line beneath them. Wren read it upside down while he worked:

*MARET VEY-SOLAN · DOSS AMBERLIN*
*THEY CLIMBED TOWARD HOME*

"That's not true," Bek said quietly. She stood apart, hands wrapped, frostbite-white fingers curled into fists. "They weren't climbing toward home. They were climbing because I told them there was something worth reaching. I don't even know anymore if there is. A census. Papers. Names on lists." Her laugh had nothing in it. "I got them killed for paperwork."

"You got them killed because a warden sold a road," Wren said. "Put it on the right grave."

But Bek wasn't listening. She was looking down the mountain, toward the switchbacks, where the lower team's fires had begun to glow in the blue dusk—patient fires, siege fires. They weren't pressing the attack. They didn't need to. Ashgrave's men had the door closed and all the time in the world, and every hour they waited, the snow deepened and the supplies shrank and Bek's fingers blackened a little further.

Casper crouched at the mouth of the cut, studying the captured soldiers' gear by the last light. He'd gone very quiet, the held-breath quiet, and when Wren knelt beside him he handed her a leather dispatch case, sealed with black wax.

Ashgrave's seal. Addressed not to the vanguard commander but to *the officer holding the traverse.*

"It was in the commander's pack," Casper said. "Orders. Wren—they weren't told to stop us."

She cracked the wax. Read it. Read it again, because the words refused to arrange themselves into sense.

*"Hold the false summit until the quarry reaches the midpoint. Then permit passage to the col. The map must reach the cache. Let Nakamura lead you to it."*

Wren looked up. The wind screamed through the pass. Below, the fires burned, and above, the high col waited in the dark like a held door.

"He doesn't want to catch us," she said slowly. "He wants us to show him where the rolls are hidden. The ambush, Tessaly, all of it—it's a funnel. We're not the quarry, Casper. We're the hounds."
