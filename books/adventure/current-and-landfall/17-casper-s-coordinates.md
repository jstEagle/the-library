## 17. Casper's Coordinates

The map had been lying to him for three years.

Casper sat with it spread across his knees in the lee of a granite shoulder, the fire banked low behind him so its glow wouldn't carry. Above them the Serpent Range rose black against the stars, the high traverse a pale scar of switchbacks visible even at night where snow already clung to the upper pitches. Two of Bek's people lay wrapped in canvas fifty feet downslope, dead since the false summit. Nobody had spoken about it in an hour. Nobody needed to.

He turned the map over. Turned it back. Ran his thumb along the coordinate marks he'd inked himself at nineteen years old, in his mentor's workshop, under a lamp that smelled of tallow and hot iron.

*Grid seven-forty. The old Delve mine.*

It was wrong. It had always been wrong, and he'd known it the way you know a lie you told so long ago it feels like someone else's mouth said it — distantly, uncomfortably, and only when forced to look.

"I can hear you thinking from here," Wren said. She dropped beside him without asking, which was how she did everything now. "That's either good news or very bad news."

"The treasury isn't there."

She went still. Even Ila, curled against Bek's side ten feet away pretending to sleep, went still.

"Say that again," Wren said, "but slower, and with better news attached."

Casper smoothed the map flat between them. "I drew this when I was an apprentice. Ashgrave thinks I copied coordinates from the Republic's survey archive. He thinks the treasury is real because the map is real, because *I'm* real — he's been hunting me for three years on the strength of my name." He tapped the grid mark. "But I never saw the archive. My mentor did. What I saw was what she wanted me to see: a decoy set of coordinates, drawn into a working copy, so that if the copy ever left her hands it would lead whoever took it somewhere harmless."

"And the real one?"

"There is no real one." He heard himself say it and felt the strange lightness of a weight shifting rather than lifting. "Not gold. Not coin, not plate, not bullion. The chest we pulled from the river — the manifest, the seal, the whole performance of it — that was the message. The cargo is the census rolls. Every name the Republic recorded before it fell. Every survivor registered in the last count. Proof that these people existed, that they owned things, that they had standing under law. Ashgrave doesn't want treasure. He wants the rolls destroyed or owned, because a list of names is the only thing standing between the survivors and his version of history, where Veyra died clean and nobody's owed anything."

Bek had come awake. Of course she had; she'd been a soldier for twenty years, and soldiers slept like knives. She sat up slowly, favoring the frostbitten hand she kept wrapped in wool. "You're certain."

"I'm certain about the map. The rest is the shape the certainty makes." Casper looked at her. "You served under him. You knew what he kept close."

Bek was quiet long enough that the wind filled the silence, worrying at the canvas shrouds below. "At the purge," she said finally, "there were clerks. Not soldiers — clerks. Wagons of ledgers moving out of the capital two days before the order came down. We were told it was pay records." Her jaw worked. "I burned a village that week. I've spent three years telling myself it was war. If there was a list — if there was a *count* — then it wasn't war. It was bookkeeping."

Ila opened her eyes. Twelve years old, and she'd learned to listen like the adults around her, which was maybe the saddest thing Casper had seen since the river gave up its chest.

"So the mine," Wren said, pulling them back. "The Delve. That's where the map says the treasury is. That's where Ashgrave will go if he believes the map."

"Yes."

"And it's empty."

"It's a dead mine, Wren. Collapsed galleries, flooded lower shafts, nothing in it but bad air and rock. It's been dead forty years."

Wren smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile. It was the smile she'd worn the first time he'd caught her trail at the dead spring, all teeth and arithmetic. "Then we should absolutely tell him about it."

* * *

They talked until the stars wheeled past the notch of the ridge and the east went the color of dirty pewter. By the end of it the plan had the shape of all desperate plans: simple enough to say in a breath, fragile as ice across a ford.

Ashgrave believed the map was genuine because Casper had made it genuine — aged paper, apprentice errors, the mentor's notation habits faithfully copied. But Ashgrave also knew Casper had the map, and knew Casper had escaped the ambush at the traverse midpoint, and would now assume Casper understood the game. A man like Ashgrave didn't chase a map. He chased the man who could authenticate it.

So they would give him both.

"He needs me to confirm the coordinates," Casper said. "Or he needs someone he believes has my confidence. Someone plausible. Someone with a reason to turn."

Wren raised her hand like a schoolgirl, which was somehow worse than if she'd just said it.

"No," Casper said.

"You haven't heard the pitch."

"I don't need the pitch. The pitch ends with you in a room with Ashgrave."

"That's the part I like best." She leaned forward, firelight catching the sharp planes of her face. "Think it through, cartographer. Who does he believe? Not you — you'd say anything to save your own skin, and he knows it. Not Bek — she shot her own unit at the ford; he'll have her hanged before she finishes surrendering. Me? I stole the chest in the first place. I was *hired* to bring it to him and I kept it. From his chair, I'm already a traitor twice over — once to my employer, once to you. All I have to do is make the second betrayal look like it finally caught up with me. Greed, fear, a knife at my ribs, whatever story sells. I walk into his camp with the map and a confession, and I give him the Delve."

"And when he finds out it's empty—"

"It won't matter if it's too late." She held up fingers, counting. "One: the Delve is three days' hard ride south of here, downrange, while the true cache — wherever your mentor stashed the rolls — is north, over the traverse. He commits to the mine, he commits his outriders, his vanguard, his wagons. Two: he takes me along as insurance, which means every soldier he owns is looking at me instead of the pass. Three—" she paused, and for the first time her voice lost some of its polish, "—three, I keep him talking. I'm good at that. You've noticed."

"You'd be alone with him," Casper said. "If he sees through it halfway—"

"Then halfway is more than most people manage." She shrugged. "Casper. Look at what we're carrying. Names. Thousands of them. People who are currently nothing — no papers, no standing, no proof they ever lived anywhere — and Ashgrave wants to erase even the memory of the ledger. If we run, we might get the rolls over the pass. Might. And then what? They sit in a cave until someone else comes hunting. This way, we take his army off the board *and* deliver the names." She looked around the circle: Bek, arms crossed, frostbitten hand tucked against her ribs; Ila, sitting up straight now, eyes enormous. "Everyone gets a role. Isn't that what you said?"

"I said everyone gets a role," Casper admitted. "I was picturing less martyrdom."

"Oh, I'm not planning to die. Dying's easy. Lying to a colonel for four days in his own camp — that's craft." She grinned. "Besides, you owe me. I taught you to sleep in shifts. Consider this the advanced lesson."

* * *

Bek laid out the route on a scrap of hide, using charcoal stubs for markers. Her hands were steady even though the right one wasn't fully hers anymore; the cold had taken something from two fingers that the hot springs at the way-station hadn't given back.

"The cache," she said. "Where?"

Casper had spent the night turning that over too, and the answer had been sitting under his nose the entire climb, the way answers do. "The milestone." He looked at Ila. "The one your father repaired. The one he was killed for."

Ila's chin came up. "What about it?"

"Milestones aren't just markers. In the old Republic surveys, they were reference points — fixed elevations, fixed bearings. My mentor used public works as anchors because nobody questions a road marker. If she hid the rolls, she anchored them to something permanent, something a mason would be trusted near." He paused. "Your father wasn't killed for repairing a Veyran milestone, Ila. He was killed because someone guessed what a Veyran mason might have been asked to do *inside* one."

The girl was quiet. Then, in the flat practical voice that always undid Casper a little: "The base course. There's a hollow in the base course. Papa called it the weep channel. He laughed about it — said the Republic built its secrets the same way it built its roads, with room for water to escape." She looked up. "I put the seal stone back on it. At the crossing. Before you found me."

"You re-set the capstone," Casper said slowly.

"With the seal. Like it was supposed to be." Her eyes narrowed. "Is that bad?"

"That," Wren said, "is either very good or extremely funny, and I genuinely can't tell yet."

"It means the cache may already be sealed against us," Casper said. "Or it means your father finished the work and the rolls are exactly where he left them, waiting for someone with the sense to look inside a rock." He met Ila's gaze. "We'll need you at the milestone. Nobody else knows the hollow."

"She's twelve," Bek said.

"I'm twelve and a half," Ila said, "and I'm coming, and if anyone tries to leave me at a way-station again I will follow you across the snow, and I will complain the entire time, and none of you will sleep."

Nobody argued. There was nothing to argue with.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin. They broke camp in the economy of people who had buried friends the day before: no wasted motion, no words above a murmur. Casper stood apart with the map, doing the last thing the plan required, the hardest thing.

He forged himself.

Not the paper — the paper was already a forgery, three years old and perfect. He forged the *hand*. He added a notation in the margin, in his own current script, older and rougher than the apprentice's line but recognizably the same man: a confirmation of the Delve coordinates, a note on access through the flooded adit, a flourish of desperation — *this is the only entry, the rest is buried, trust the grid.* Enough to satisfy a colonel who wanted certainty more than he wanted truth. Enough to hang a betrayal on.

Wren watched him work. "You're good at that," she said. "Making true-looking things."

"My mentor used to say a map is a promise." He blew on the ink. "I've spent three years learning how promises break. Seems fair to spend one morning breaking one on purpose."

"If he hurts you—" she started, and stopped, because the sentence had come out wrong, backwards, hers instead of his.

"If he hurts *you*," Casper said, "I will feed him his own ledger page by page. That's not comfort, Wren. That's logistics. So don't get hurt. It would ruin my supply chain."

She laughed — short, surprised, real — and took the map from his hands, rolling it with a thief's tenderness. Then her face settled into something else, the mask she'd wear for four days in a murderer's camp, and Casper understood that he was watching her leave already, standing right in front of him.

"One thing," she said quietly, so the others wouldn't hear. "The manifest. My name was on it too. Sallow, W. — courier, retained. Whoever packed that chest knew I'd read it. Knew I'd keep it." She looked at the eastern sky, where the pass waited like a held breath. "Whoever packed that chest planned all of this. Maybe even you finding it. So when we're done playing traitor — find out who our ghost is. Because I want to know whether I was hired or *chosen*."

* * *

They split at first light, where the trail forked below the tree line: Bek and Ila and the last of Bek's defectors swinging west toward the hidden crossing and the milestone, Casper holding the high route with two rifles and a head start on the traverse. Wren rode south alone, the forged map against her ribs, toward the smoke of Ashgrave's camp.

Casper watched her go until the trail bent and took her.

Then he turned uphill, into the snow, and began to count — not miles, for the first time in three years, but hours. How many hours until Ashgrave marched on an empty mine. How many hours until a twelve-year-old's small hands found a hollow in a dead man's stone.

Behind him, far to the south, a bugle sounded thin and clear across the flats.

Ashgrave was moving.
