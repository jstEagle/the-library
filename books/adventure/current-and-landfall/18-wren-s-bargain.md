## 18. Wren's Bargain

Wren had stolen a lot of things in her life—coins, horses, a bishop's ring off his finger while he slept, once an entire wagon of salt cod by simply driving it away—but she had never before stolen a lie and handed it over as truth. It felt different. Heavier. A stolen thing you kept was yours; a lie you delivered belonged to the man who believed it, until he didn't.

She rode down out of the rocks at midmorning with her hands empty and her face arranged into something between exhaustion and surrender. The false map was sewn into the lining of her coat, against her left ribs, where Casper had stitched it himself with a sailmaker's needle by firelight while she memorized every wrong coordinate twice.

The mine was called Graywater on no map that mattered. Ashgrave's camp sat below it like a wound: black tents, picketed horses, cook fires smoking thin in the cold air of the high valley. She counted outriders as she came down the slope. Thirty-one visible. Probably more in the trees. Bek would have counted forty.

A sentry stopped her two hundred yards out, rifle level, face raw with cold.

"Name."

"Wren Sallow."

He spat to the side. "The thief. The colonel said you'd come." He looked at her hands, her coat, her empty saddlebags. "Where's the rest of it?"

"Rest of what?"

"The map. The chest. Whatever you people have been hauling across my range for three weeks."

"Only the map," Wren said, and let her voice crack just enough. "The chest is buried. Nakamura wouldn't tell me where. He said I'd get it back when he got me back."

That part was true, or true enough. Casper had insisted on it. *If Ashgrave thinks you're his only key to the cache, he keeps you breathing.* She'd hated the logic and then, riding alone through three days of scree and frozen grass, had come to love it. A key got carried carefully. A prisoner got dragged.

The sentry bound her wrists with wire, not rope, which told her everything about how much Ashgrave trusted his own men not to be bought. Then he walked her down into camp.

* * *

Ashgrave was not what the stories made him. The stories gave him a scarred face and a voice like gravel; he was instead a neat, gray-templed man with clean hands and the patient eyes of someone who had spent thirty years watching other people make mistakes. He sat outside his command tent in a folding chair, drinking tea from a tin cup, as if he were a farmer reviewing a field rather than a colonel with no army left to command.

"Wren Sallow," he said. "Sit."

There was one stool. She sat, wrists still wired, and noticed he hadn't ordered them cut. A test, then. Fine. She could be comfortable in wire. She'd been uncomfortable in better places.

"You were hired to steal a chest," Ashgrave said. "You kept it. You ran. You fell in with Nakamura, who I understand you were originally running *from*. And now you've come to me carrying—" he paused delicately "—what?"

"A way to end this," Wren said. "Nakamura's tired. He won't say it, but he is. Three years of running and now he's dragging a child and a defector up a mountain in the first snow of the year. For what? Names on paper?" She shrugged with her whole shoulders, a thief's shrug, careless and cheap. "I told him there was another way. He didn't believe me. So I took the map and I came."

"And why would you bring it to me rather than sell it in some free city?"

"Because free cities don't exist anymore," Wren said. "And because you pay in futures, not coin. I read your courier's letters when I robbed him last spring. You're not building a bandit company, Colonel. You're building a country." She leaned forward, wire biting. "I want to be a citizen of something again. Even if the founding father is a butcher."

Silence. Somewhere behind the tents a horse complained. Ashgrave turned his cup slowly on its saucer, and Wren watched him watch her, and understood that everything now depended on whether he was a man who believed people changed sides for ambition or only for fear. She had bet on ambition. Thieves always did. It was the sin they recognized.

"You read his letters," Ashgrave said at last. "Which courier?"

"Tall fellow. Red hair. Rode like his saddle was on fire."

"Merrick." Something flickered behind the patience. "He reported you robbed him near Salt Hollow. He also reported you searched his papers and burned them."

"Burning them was the point," Wren said. "I didn't want anyone knowing what I knew."

Another silence, longer. Then Ashgrave smiled—not warmly, but with genuine professional appreciation, the way one craftsman admires another's joinery—and nodded to a soldier standing at the tent flap. "Cut her hands loose. Get her tea. Not the good tea."

* * *

They fed her, questioned her for two hours, and fed her again. She gave them Casper's route in careful pieces—the pass Tessaly had opened, the cave above the tree line, the traverse—everything they already knew or could guess, wrapped around the one thing that mattered: that the chest was buried at coordinates Casper had never shared with anyone, and that the map in her coat lining showed the burial site, and that she alone had watched him dig.

"He trusts you that much?" Ashgrave asked. "After you stole from him?"

"He doesn't trust me," Wren said. "He trusts that I'm too smart to bury myself with a treasure. If anything happens to him, the map dies with me unless I've sold it first. He knows that. It's why he let me go." She sipped the bad tea and grimaced honestly. "It's also why he'll kill me if I'm lying. So consider the source of my honesty."

Ashgrave laughed at that—a short, real laugh—and Wren felt something cold slide through her stomach, because she had made him laugh, and men like Ashgrave killed the people who amused them too, eventually, once amusement wore out.

It happened at dusk, on the second day.

* * *

She'd been held in a supply tent under light guard, fed, watered, even allowed to walk the perimeter with an escort, all of it theater to keep her calm and cooperative. On the second evening Ashgrave summoned her to his tent and had the map spread on his table under a lamp.

He didn't look at it. He looked at her.

"Tell me about the digging," he said.

"I told you. I watched him—"

"You told me you watched him dig. Describe the ground."

Wren's mind moved fast and found nothing to hold. "Frozen ground. Scree. There was a—"

"There was a what?"

"A marker. A cairn."

"Casper Nakamura," Ashgrave said mildly, "was apprenticed to the finest cartographer the Republic ever produced. I have seen his work. He does not mark caches with cairns. Cairns are moved. Cairns are *found*. He marks with bearings off permanent features—cliff faces, springs, fault lines. His master taught him that. His master wrote a monograph on it." Ashgrave tapped the map once, lightly, with one clean finger. "This map has a cairn on it. This map is drawn in Nakamura's hand—I recognize the lettering, the way he crosses his sevens. Which means either Nakamura has forgotten everything he ever learned, or you are standing in my tent holding a lie you stitched into your own coat."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the wind moved through the valley like something large turning over in its sleep.

Wren did the arithmetic of survival in about a second and a half. Deny, and he'd put her to the question, and she'd break, and everyone would die anyway. Run, and she'd take a bullet at fifty paces in open ground. Or—

Or become more useful than the truth.

"You're right," she said. "It's false."

Ashgrave's eyebrows rose a fraction. Of all the responses he'd prepared for, honesty was evidently not among them.

"It's false," Wren repeated, "because Casper knew I'd run. He's known since the ford. He let me see him draw it. He wanted you to have it, Colonel, because the coordinates on that map aren't a treasury—they're a dead mine with a rotten timber roof and a hundred feet of flooded shaft, and he wanted you standing under it when the snowmelt comes off the Serpents in six weeks." She let that land. "I'm telling you because I don't want to die in your trap. I've been his bait for a month. I'd rather be yours."

For a long moment Ashgrave said nothing. Then he began, very quietly, to laugh again, and this time there was nothing appreciative in it at all.

"Do you know," he said, "in thirty years of soldiering, I have been lied to by generals, ministers, kings-in-exile, and my own mother. And the lies that nearly killed me were never the clever ones. They were the ones that arrived wearing honesty." He stood. "Take her coat. Search it thoroughly. Then bind her properly and post four guards. She comes with us to the mine."

"Colonel—"

"You're insurance now, Sallow. Nakamura wants you alive; that makes you worth more than any map, true or false. We march at first light." He rolled up the false map and set it aside almost gently, the way a man sets aside a letter from a dead friend. "And if it comforts you—if you're going to pray to anything—pray that Nakamura loves you enough to trade. Because I have decided I no longer need coordinates. I have something better."

He looked at her the way a cartographer looks at blank space.

"I have leverage."

* * *

They marched her at the front of the column, roped to a lead rider, so that anyone watching from the heights would see her clearly. That was the point, she realized. Ashgrave wasn't hiding his approach; he was advertising it. *Come and see what I hold.*

The trail climbed through pine gone black with cold, then above the tree line onto bare rock furred with old snow. Wren's hands went numb inside the rope. Her boots were wrong for this ground—thief's boots, soft-soled, made for rooftops and riverbanks—and by the second day her feet were two blocks of pain she learned to carry separately from herself. She catalogued the column as she marched. Twenty-two men now; nine had peeled off somewhere behind, toward the low passes, and she filed that away without knowing yet what it meant. She noted the pack mules, the lack of siege equipment, the fact that Ashgrave's men checked their rifles constantly against the wet, and that their sergeant—a heavy, gray-faced man named Corvus whom the others obeyed without looking at him—never once checked his own.

On the third night, huddled against a rock wall with a guard blanket that smelled of mule, she got her chance to talk.

The youngest guard, a boy of maybe seventeen with chapped lips and a Veyran button worn smooth from touching, crouched next to her to check her ropes. Up close she saw the name stitched inside his collar: DANN. A Republic name. A census-roll name, probably, though he'd never know it.

"Your colonel says he's rebuilding Veyra," Wren said, conversational, half-frozen, harmless.

"Shut up."

"He's rebuilding it for himself. You've read his orders. Did any of them mention what happens to the people in the valleys? The survivors? The ones the rolls name?" The boy's jaw tightened. She pressed, soft as snowfall. "My family farmed near Veyra City. I was eight when it fell. I don't remember much—my mother's hands, mostly. Flour on them." She watched the boy's eyes flicker. Everyone had a mother's hands somewhere. "Ashgrave talks about legitimacy. Do you know what legitimacy means, Dann? It means somebody writes down your name and says *you existed, you mattered, you belong to something.*" She let the silence do its work. "Ask him whose names are in the chest he's climbing this mountain for. Ask him why he needs soldiers to fetch paper."

"Shut *up*," the boy said again, but his voice shook, and he checked her ropes twice that night, and both times his hands were gentle.

Wren lay back against the stone and stared at the stars and did not sleep. Courage, she had learned long ago, was not a thing you had. It was a thing you spent, like coin, and hers was running low, and the mine was one day away, and somewhere behind her in the white dark Casper was coming—with a plan she didn't know, a split she couldn't coordinate, and odds she'd have called insane if she'd heard them aloud.

Good, she thought. Insane plans were the only kind that worked. Honest ones got you caught in tents by neat-handed colonels.

At dawn they reached the mine.

It crouched in the mountainside like a skull: rotted timbers, a collapsed ore chute, the black mouth of the main adit breathing cold air. Ashgrave dismounted, studied it with his clean hands clasped behind his back, and smiled at the dead shaft as if greeting an old colleague.

"Flooded, she says," he remarked to Sergeant Corvus. "Six weeks of snowmelt. How industrious of Master Nakamura." He turned and looked at Wren, roped and frostbitten and swaying on her feet, and his voice was almost kind. "Set up the signal fire on the ridge. Big and visible. Let's give our cartographer something to navigate by."

Then, to her, quietly, as the soldiers moved:

"When he comes—and he will come, because men like him always do—you will stand at the mouth of that mine and smile at him. And whatever happens after, Sallow, remember that you chose this road yourself. I find that helps people. At the end."

The smoke went up from the ridge, straight and black against the morning sky, a line drawn across the mountains like a signature.

Wren looked at it and thought: *Come on, Casper. Come on. Bring the whole mad family.*

Far below in the valley, so small it might have been a bird, something moved along the trail.
