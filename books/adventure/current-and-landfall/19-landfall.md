## 19. Landfall

The mine sat in a fold of the Serpent Range like a wound that had never learned to close. Casper saw it first as a scar on the white—timber framing gone gray, a spoil heap furred with snow, the adit's mouth a black rectangle punched into rock the color of old bone. Three days of false coordinates, three days of Wren riding into Ashgrave's camp with a lie folded against her ribs, and now here they were: the dead mine Ashgrave believed held the treasury of a nation.

Casper crouched at the ridgeline above it, breath fogging, and counted what he had.

One rifle. Bek, beside him, her frostbitten hand wrapped in Ila's spare scarf, her face the color of the snow. Ila behind them both, pressed flat against a granite shelf because she'd refused every order to stay below. And somewhere down there, inside that black mouth or one of the shafts beyond it, Wren Sallow—alive an hour ago when Bek's glass caught sunlight flashing off a sentry's buckle and then caught Wren walking between two soldiers, hands free, head up, playing her part so well that Casper's chest had hurt watching it.

"You don't have to do this," Bek said. She'd said it four times since dawn. This was the fifth.

"Yes, I do."

"It's not a trade he'll honor."

"He will if I make it worth more than breaking his word." Casper checked the rifle out of habit, though he'd never fired it well and everyone knew it. "He wants legitimacy. A man who keeps his bargains is worth more to him than a dead cartographer."

"A man who keeps his bargains doesn't burn republics."

"He didn't burn this one." Casper settled the map case against his back—the real one, the rolls, everything they'd bled for—and stood. His knees cracked in the cold like green wood in a fire. "He just wants to own the ashes. Come on."

They went down through the pines single file, boots punching crust, the mine growing until Casper could see the details he'd once drawn from a surveyor's notes twenty years ago: the ore chute, the collapsed tramway, the hoist house with its great wheel frozen mid-turn. He had drawn this place into his false treasury map precisely because it was forgettable. A hole men dug silver out of and abandoned. He'd never imagined standing in front of it with his heart going like a drum in a parade.

The sentries spotted them at fifty yards. Rifles came up. Casper stopped, raised both hands, and called out in the clear Veyran parade-ground voice he hadn't used in three years:

"Casper Nakamura! Tell Colonel Ashgrave his cartographer has come to collect his commission!"

* * *

They took the rifle and walked him between them, and the strange thing—the thing Casper would remember all his life—was how young they were. Eighteen, maybe nineteen, cheeks chapped raw, uniforms cut for bigger men. Children dressed in a colonel's ambition. One of them kept glancing at Casper's face and away again, the way you look at a name you've heard your mother say.

Inside the hoist house, a stove burned and the air smelled of coal and wet wool. Ashgrave stood at a table spread with papers, and Wren sat across from him, wrists bound in front of her, looking bored. Only Casper, who had spent weeks learning the geography of her face, saw the tension in her jaw, the way her eyes flicked to him and did the arithmetic—*you came, you idiot, thank you, you idiot.*

"Cartographer." Ashgrave didn't look up from his papers. He was a compact man, gray at the temples, with the neat hands of someone who had never dug anything. "You cost me two outriders at the ford and a week of my life. I hope your commission is worth it."

"You read my terms?"

"I read them." Now he looked up. His eyes were pale and patient, and that was the worst thing about him—not cruelty, just patience, the patience of a man balancing a ledger. "You want the girl released. You want your companions given passage south. In exchange, the map, the chest, and yourself."

"And in exchange you get the treasury of Veyra," Casper said. "Enough gold to buy ten thousand mercenaries, a fleet, a flag. Enough to be a nation instead of a bandit with a title."

Something moved at the corner of Ashgrave's mouth. Not quite a smile. An appreciation. "You understand me better than most."

"I drew the map, Colonel. I understand exactly what you think is buried here." Casper let the pause stretch. "But we both know gold was never the point. Gold is a means. What you're buying is *legitimacy*. The right to say Veyra lives, and that you are Veyra."

Ashgrave set down his pen. Around the walls of the hoist house, soldiers had gone very still. Even the stove seemed quieter.

"So here is my counteroffer," Casper said, and his voice only shook a little. "You don't need the gold. You need the *rolls*. The census. Every name the Republic ever recorded—every family, every holding, every birthright. Proof of who survived the purge and who they belonged to. That's legitimacy, Colonel. That's a nation you can hold in your hand. And I have it."

He shrugged the map case off his shoulder and set it on the table, and felt Wren's eyes on him like a brand. *Not yet,* her look said. *Not all of it.* But there was no other way, and they both knew it. You couldn't buy a man like Ashgrave with a fraction. You had to show him the whole fortune before you could cheat him.

Ashgrave looked at the case for a long moment. Then he untied the flap and lifted out the top sheaf of papers, and Casper watched the colonel's neat hands tremble—actually tremble—as he read names.

"There it is," Casper said softly. "Ten thousand families. Some of them are yours, aren't they? Your soldiers'. Men from Veyra's provinces, conscripted after the fall, fighting for wages because their towns are ash and their names are on no list but yours." He turned slowly, addressing the room, addressing the young faces along the walls. "Sergeant? The tall one by the door. Where were you born?"

Silence.

"Answer him," Ashgrave said quietly.

"Dul Marek," the soldier said. His voice cracked on it.

"Dul Marek," Casper repeated. "Page six hundred and eleven of those rolls. Forty-one households. I mapped the district myself when I was sixteen years old. If the Republic still existed, Sergeant, your name would mean something. Your mother's name. Your street." He looked back at Ashgrave. "That's what I'm offering you. Not gold. *Them.*"

Ashgrave closed the case. His hands had steadied. That frightened Casper more than the trembling had.

"You mistake me for a sentimentalist," the colonel said. "These lists are a means too. Better than gold, I'll grant you—a census is a claim of ownership no coin can match. With this, every survivor of Veyra belongs to whoever holds the paper. I don't intend to free them, cartographer. I intend to *enroll* them."

"That's not a nation," Wren said from her chair. Her voice was flat and hard. "That's a ledger with a flag."

"Girl, you stole for me once. Don't lecture me on ownership."

"I stole the chest *from* you," Wren said. "Read the manifest sometime. My name's on it too. So is his." She nodded at Casper. "So are half the people in this room, if any of them ever lived under the Republic. You're not buying soldiers, Colonel. You're buying children of a country you murdered, and asking them to salute."

"Enough." Ashgrave raised one finger, and it was such a small gesture, such a tidy gesture, that it took Casper a heartbeat to understand why the temperature of the room changed. Two soldiers stepped forward and took Wren's chair, turning it to face the door. Another took the map case from the table.

"The trade stands," Ashgrave said. "The girl walks. The case stays. And you—" his pale eyes settled on Casper with something almost like kindness, which was the cruelest thing yet—"you'll draw for me, cartographer. New maps. Accurate ones. A nation needs a surveyor, and I find I'd rather have a willing one than a broken one. We'll see how long the willingness lasts."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the girl walks out of here and my sharpshooters walk her down before she reaches the treeline. And your friends on the ridge watch it happen." He said it without heat, without malice, checking figures in a ledger. "I keep my bargains, Nakamura. I told you. It's the only currency I have left."

* * *

They marched him to the adit to wait while a runner went down to the ridge with terms for Bek. Casper went with his hands bound, flanked by the boy from Dul Marek and another soldier whose jaw worked like he was chewing something bitter.

At the mine mouth, the boy stopped. Snow ticked down through the pines. Somewhere below, a crow argued with the wind.

"My sister," the boy said suddenly, not looking at Casper. "She was in Veyra when it fell. She'd have been nineteen now. Nobody knows. There's nobody to ask. No record of anything." His throat moved. "Is she in there? In that case? Is there a list that would say?"

Casper thought of page six hundred and eleven. Of forty-one households in Dul Marek. Of ten thousand families reduced to ink, and one ink-stained apprentice who had run from the burning city with nothing but a satchel of maps while his mentor stayed behind to guard the archives.

"If she survived," Casper said, "she's in there. And if she's in there, then she was never erased. Whatever anyone tells you. Whatever flag flies over her. Somebody wrote her name down, son, and that means she counted."

The boy stared at him. Then at the map case, slung over the other soldier's shoulder. Then back toward the hoist house, where Ashgrave's silhouette stood framed in the doorway, patient as winter.

"She counted," the boy repeated, like a man testing whether ice would hold.

And that was when the shooting started.

* * *

It came from the treeline—two shots, then three, then Bek's rifle cracking in its distinctive slow rhythm, deliberate as a metronome, and Casper understood in one cold flash: she'd never intended to negotiate. While he'd been trading himself, she'd been moving Ila down through the trees, and Ashgrave's pickets had seen them.

"Get down!" The soldier shoved Casper behind the spoil heap, which was the last thing Casper expected a man to do, and then the boy from Dul Marek turned and fired three rounds past Casper's ear—not at Bek, at the hoist house, at the two sharpshooters scrambling onto its roof with orders to walk Wren down.

Everything happened fast after that, the way disasters do, each piece simple and terrible. The sharpshooters fell or fled. Soldiers poured from the hoist house—and stopped, because Ashgrave stood in the doorway shouting for order, and the order he shouted was for the treeline, for Bek, for the child, and the soldiers heard it and did not move.

"Your families are in that case!" Casper was on his feet, screaming over the gunfire, hands still bound, standing in the open like a fool because there was no other card left to play. "Every one of you! Dul Marek! Corvel! The river towns! He's not carrying your country—he's carrying *you*, and he means to spend you like coin!"

"Shoot the cartographer!" Ashgrave's voice, still level, still patient, and that patience finally curdled into something else as his own line wavered. "Shoot him *now*!"

A dozen rifles. A dozen young faces. And not one of them rose.

Because the tall sergeant had taken the map case off the other soldier's shoulder, and opened it, and was reading. Casper watched the man's lips move over the names. Watched him turn pages with fingers gone suddenly gentle, the way you touch a thing that might be holy. Watched him stop, and go white, and hold the paper out wordlessly to the boy from Dul Marek.

The boy read. The boy made a sound Casper had heard once before, at a mass grave outside Veyra, and would hear again in dreams for the rest of his life.

"No one fires," the sergeant said. He didn't shout it. He said it the way you state a fact about weather. "Colonel, no one fires."

"They are under my command—"

"They're under your *pay*," the sergeant said. "There's a difference. There always was." He looked down at the rolls in his arms, then at Casper, and something passed between them that needed no words: the understanding that a country is not a flag or a treasury or a man with a title. It is a list of names, and the people willing to answer to them.

Ashgrave stood alone in the doorway of the hoist house. For a moment—one long moment—Casper saw him truly see it: the rifles lowered, his soldiers reading their dead and living kin in the snow, his currency spent, his nation evaporating around him like breath off warm iron. His hand went to his pistol.

Bek's shot took it out of his grip before it cleared the holster. She stepped from the treeline with Ila behind her, small and snow-dusted and glaring at the colonel like he was a wall she intended to break.

"It's over," Casper said. Not gloating. Almost gentle. "Walk away, Colonel. There's nothing here to buy."

Ashgrave looked at the map case. At the soldiers. At Casper. And Casper saw the ledger close behind those pale eyes, saw the final entry written, and understood—with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold—that a man like this did not surrender. He liquidated.

"You're right," Ashgrave said quietly. "There's nothing here to buy. But there is something left to *burn*."

He moved faster than a man his age should have—straight for the fire barrel beside the hoist house door, where the coals of the stove had been raked that morning, where embers glowed orange under ash. The sergeant lunged. Too slow. Ashgrave snatched the nearest sheaf of rolls from the open case, pages fanning, names scattering like startled birds—

—and Casper hit him at the waist.

They went down together into the snow, and the papers flew, and Casper scrambled on his knees through the drifts snatching them back one by one while Ashgrave laughed—a short, awful sound, half breath, half arithmetic. Soldiers closed in. Bek's rifle covered everything. And Wren, wrists still bound, walked out of the hoist house calm as Sunday, bent down, and picked up the last scattered page from under the colonel's boot.

She read it. She went still.

"Casper," she said. "It's your mentor. Halven. Page one. He's listed among the dead of the archive fire—but there's a note. A correction, in the warden's hand." She looked up, and her eyes were shining and terrified at once. "*Survived. Last seen north pass, autumn of the fall year.*"

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere far off, the crow again.

"He went north," Casper said. The words came out of him like something that had been lodged in his chest for three years, finally working loose. "Tessaly's pass. He went *north*."

Behind him, Ashgrave got to his feet, brushed the snow from his coat with two precise strokes, and looked at the circle of armed men who had been his army and were no longer. He did not rage. He straightened his collar.

"This isn't finished, cartographer," he said. "Nations are patient. So am I." And he turned and walked into the treeline, alone, unhurried, a man already drafting his next ledger—and every rifle in the company tracked him, and not one fired, because even now, even beaten, he kept his bargains, and so, bitterly, did they.

Wren put the page in Casper's hands. Halven's name. *Survived.*

"We have to go back up the mountain," Casper said.
