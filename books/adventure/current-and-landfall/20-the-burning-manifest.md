## 20. The Burning Manifest

Colonel Edran Ashgrave had commanded men through the fall of a nation, and he had learned one truth above all others: power was not taken. It was *held*. It was held in ledgers and seals and signatures, in the paper that told ten thousand scattered people who they were and where they belonged. Paper burned easily. That was why you kept it close.

He was thinking about this as his mercenaries lowered their rifles, one by one, in the snow outside the dead mine at Kell's Hollow.

It began with the woman on the left flank—a broad-shouldered sergeant named Voss, whose file he knew by heart because he had written half of it himself. Voss had a brother listed on page forty-one of the census rolls. Ashgrave knew this too. He had known it for years, and he had never once considered it a problem, because men like Voss did not read manifests. Men like Voss followed orders.

But Casper Nakamura had spread the pages out on the frozen ground like a merchant laying out cloth, and the wind had done the rest.

"Voss." Ashgrave kept his voice level. He had practiced level. "Pick up your rifle."

Voss looked down at the page in her hands. Her lips moved over the names. Then she looked up, and her eyes were not angry, which was the worst of it. They were simply finished.

"No, sir."

The word went through the line like frost through mortar. No. No. A rifle butt set down in the snow. Another. Ashgrave counted them falling silent the way he had once counted companies: fourteen, then twenty-two, then thirty-one of his forty picked men, standing in the white with their hands loose at their sides, reading the names of their own dead.

He had bought these men with the promise of a country. It occurred to him now—late, always late—that he had promised them a country the way a counterfeiter promises coin. Weight without substance. Shine without gold.

Nakamura stood ten paces away, between him and Wren Sallow, who was bleeding from the mouth and grinning anyway. The cartographer's apprentice. Three years Ashgrave had chased the ghost of that name through manifests and border reports, and here he was at last: thin, sunburnt, unremarkable, holding nothing but a chest key and a look in his eyes that Ashgrave recognized and despised, because it was the look of a man who had already won.

"The rolls are mine," Ashgrave said. "By right of conquest, if nothing else."

"You didn't conquer anything," Nakamura said. "You stole a map and called it a birthright."

"I am the last officer of the Republic still under arms." His voice cracked on *officer*, and he hated himself for it. "Without me there is no Veyra. Without me, those people—" he gestured at the pages scattering across the snow, at the mercenaries bending to pick them up, "—are refugees forever. I was going to give them back their *nation*."

"You were going to give yourself an army," said the thief, spitting red into the snow. "We read your ledger, Colonel. Page by page. You're very thorough when you think no one's looking."

Ashgrave turned to her because turning to Nakamura hurt more than he could allow. "And what do you have, Sallow? A stolen chest and a child's faith in strangers?"

"A crew," she said simply. "Which is more than you've got standing behind you right now."

He looked. He shouldn't have looked, but he did.

Behind him, Sergeant Bek Odumane—the traitor, the defector, the woman whose rifle he had trusted at three fords and two passes—stood with her weapon slung and her frostbitten hand wrapped in dirty cloth, watching him with something that was almost pity. Pity. From Odumane. He remembered pinning a commendation medal on her uniform four years ago, in the plaza at Veyra City, with the banners still hanging and the crowds still cheering. He remembered thinking: *this one will follow me anywhere.*

She had. Just not where he'd intended.

"Last chance," Ashgrave said, and heard how it sounded—how it had always sounded, maybe, to everyone but him. "Give me the rolls and walk away rich. Every one of you. Amnesty, land, a place in the new Republic. I keep my word. Ask anyone."

"We did ask," Nakamura said quietly. "We asked the people at the river settlements. We asked the pass wardens. Nobody remembers you keeping your word, Colonel. They remember the purge."

The purge. There it was. The word he had spent three years refusing to say aloud, as if leaving it unnamed would leave it undone. Two hundred souls in the cartographers' quarter, because a clerk had falsified a census and someone had to be blamed, and blame was cheaper than investigation. He had signed the order with a steady hand. He had told himself it was triage. Nations were built on worse.

Nations were also built on better. That was the thought arriving now, in the snow, too late, wearing Nakamura's face.

* * *

They took his sidearm first, then his sword, then the dispatch case chained to his belt. Nakamura did it personally, gently even, the way you disarm a man you don't consider dangerous anymore, and that gentleness was the deepest cut of the day. Ashgrave stood in the trampled snow of Kell's Hollow and let himself be searched like a smuggler while his army read its family histories off the ground.

The chest itself sat open against the mine's timber frame. Not the original chest—Sallow had lost that one in the river, or claimed to—but a copy, iron-banded, seal-stamped, filled to the brim with the thing that mattered. The census rolls. Eleven years of the Republic's bookkeeping: every name, every district, every surviving household recorded before the fall. Legitimacy, bound in ledger leather. The difference between a warlord and a government.

Odumane stood beside it, guarding it from him specifically. She had positioned herself there without being asked. He noticed such things. He had always noticed such things; it was the one talent that had never failed him, and it had never once saved him.

"You understand what you're doing," he said to her. Not a question. He was past questions.

"I do."

"You'll scatter the rolls to the winds. Every settlement, every camp along the river. And in five years there will be nothing left of Veyra but stories. Is that what they died for? To become *stories*?"

Odumane considered him for a long moment. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she didn't brush it off.

"My mother's name is on page sixty-three," she said. "District Nine. I haven't seen her since the purge. Do you know what I would have given, these three years, for a piece of paper that said she existed? That said somebody official, somebody with a seal, had looked at her face and written her down?" She shook her head slowly. "You wanted to own that paper, Colonel. They just want to read it. Those aren't the same thing. They never were."

"They could be the same thing," he said, and heard the beggar in his own voice. "Under me, they could be—"

"Under you, they're collateral." She stepped back from the chest. "That's all anything ever was. Go home, Edran. If you can find one."

* * *

They let him keep his coat. He supposed that was mercy, or indifference, or simply the practical arithmetic of men who had no stomach for shooting a disarmed officer in front of witnesses. Nakamura walked him to the edge of the hollow, past the mine's collapsed adit, past the milestone Ila—the stonemason's girl, twelve years old and fearless—had repaired with fresh mortar and Veyra's seal, absurd and stubborn as a flower in a crack.

"Why?" Ashgrave asked him. It was the only question left. "You drew the map. You could have kept the treasury. Real gold, real power, a life no one could touch. Instead you burn your own inheritance for a box of paperwork. Explain it to me, because I have been building toward this for three years and I cannot see the flaw in my logic."

Nakamura looked at him with something worse than hatred. Recognition.

"There was never any gold," he said. "I drew the coordinates wrong on purpose, three years ago, so the treasury story would die with everything else. You've been chasing a lie I invented to protect the names." He paused. "That's the flaw, Colonel. You thought the Republic was its gold. It was always its people. Everything else was just the box we kept them in."

"And now the box is broken."

"Now the box is *open*," Nakamura said. "There's a difference. You should think about it, on the walk down."

Then he turned and went back to his people—his thief, his soldier, his child—and Ashgrave stood alone at the edge of the hollow with the wind coming down off the Serpent Range like a debt collector.

* * *

They marched away at dusk, down the eastern trail, toward the river settlements, carrying the chest between them like pallbearers. Some of his former mercenaries went with them. Voss went. Two others. The rest dispersed into the snowfield in ones and twos, heading wherever men go when the thing they were promised turns out to have been smoke.

Ashgrave could have gone south. There were garrisons in the lowlands that still answered to his name, still saluted the eagle on his collar. He could have raised another company inside a month. He had done it before. He was very good at it.

Instead he found himself walking back to the mine.

The chest was gone—they had taken it, of course—but the manifest lay where Nakamura had spread it, half-buried in drifting snow, pages frozen together in ragged clumps. The master index. The top sheet. Ashgrave knelt and peeled it free with numb fingers.

His own name was not on it. He had checked, years ago, through intermediaries, and confirmed what he had already suspected: the census clerks had struck him from the rolls the week after the purge. *Edran Ashgrave, Colonel, First Veyran Rifles — record sealed by order of the Provisional Authority.* Erased. Unpersoned. He had told himself it was a bureaucratic insult, a wound to his pride and nothing more. He had built three years of ambition on top of that wound like a man building a tower on a grave.

But kneeling in the snow with the master index shaking in his hands, he finally understood the shape of it. They hadn't erased him because he was dangerous. They had erased him because he was *empty*. Because somewhere in the clerks' quiet, merciless accounting, someone had weighed Edran Ashgrave—his service, his signature on the purge order, his hunger—and found nothing underneath worth recording. No family. No district. No name that meant anything to anyone except as a threat.

A nation needed people. He had never been one. He had only ever been an officer, which was a costume, and costumes burn.

He laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle, and the sound of it frightened him more than the rifles had.

* * *

He built the fire small, in the lee of the mine's timber frame, the way he'd taught a hundred recruits to build fires in a hundred frozen bivouacs. His hands worked on their own. That was the soldier's curse and gift: the body keeps its competence long after the purpose dies.

When the flames caught, he fed them the master index, page by page.

It was not the real census. He knew that—Nakamura had the real one, and by morning it would be flowing downriver in copied bundles, name by name, into hands Ashgrave would never see. What burned here was a copy of a copy, worthless to anyone but him. That was the point. That was the whole point, and he understood it with a clarity that felt almost like peace, though he knew it wasn't.

Because legitimacy could not be surrendered. That was the final lesson of the day, the one the snow had been teaching him since noon. If he handed the rolls back—if he signed anything, renounced anything, begged—he would remain what he had always been: a claimant. A man with a case pending. As long as the possibility of Veyra-under-Ashgrave existed, it would rot him from the inside, one negotiation at a time, one compromise, one more order signed with a steady hand.

So he burned it. All of it. The forged commissions. The mercenary contracts bearing his seal. The letters patent he'd had drawn up in a border town, naming him Protector of the Restored Republic, in Latin and everything, as if ink could raise the dead. He fed the fire until his arms ached and the snow around him melted in a black ring, and the smoke went straight up into the darkening sky like a signal nobody was watching.

The eagle buckle on his belt he considered for a long time. Then he unbuckled it and dropped it into the coals, and watched the gilding blister and blacken until it was just metal, just a lump, just weight.

"There," he said aloud, to no one. His voice sounded strange in the empty hollow. "There it is."

What remained, when the fire died, was ash and a man. Forty-six years old. No nation. No army. No name in any ledger anywhere, unless someone had written *villain* beside his, which perhaps they had, and perhaps he had earned.

He stood. He brushed the snow from his knees. And he found—to his genuine surprise—that he was not afraid. Grief, yes. A grief so large it had no edges, the grief of a man who has torn down the only wall he had and discovered there was no house behind it. But not fear. Fear required a future worth defending, and he had just burned his.

The high pass was closed; everyone said so. The low trails were days away and crawling with men who used to obey him. It didn't matter. Walking was the one thing left that required no permission, no signature, no seal. One boot, then the other, into the blue shadow of evening, north along the range's shoulder where the snow lay untouched and unmarked and would take no impression of him longer than the next fall.

Let the rolls scatter. Let the refugees read their names. Let Nakamura build his little way-station at the ford and call it a country, if that was what a country was. Ashgrave was done making things. He had made one thing in his life that mattered, and it had been an order, and two hundred people had died of it, and no fire would ever be hot enough for that.

The snow thickened. The mine disappeared behind him. Somewhere below, faint on the wind, he thought he heard voices—many voices, rising from the river valley, the sound of a settlement at supper—and he walked the other way, into the white, alone, carrying nothing at all.

For the first time in three years, Colonel Edran Ashgrave was entirely free.

It weighed nothing. It held nothing. It was the lightest thing he had ever owned, and he carried it into the mountains until the dark took even that.
