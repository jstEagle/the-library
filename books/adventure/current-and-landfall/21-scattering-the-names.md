## 21. Scattering the Names

Spring came up the river valley like a rumor, then like a fact.

Casper felt it first in the ford—the ice going rotten at the edges, the water running brown and loud, carrying sticks and last year's leaves and one improbable boot past the way-station every morning. Then the willows along the bank greened overnight, the way they always did, as if they'd been waiting for permission. By the time the mud dried enough to hold a wagon, they had been at the ford for five weeks, and the chest that had started everything sat open on the table in the main room, no longer a treasure, no longer even a secret.

Just paper. Eleven hundred pages of it, give or take. Names.

"We can't copy all of this by hand," Wren said for the fourth time that morning, fanning herself with a blank sheet like she was trying to summon a breeze out of sheer irritation. "Not before summer. Not with two pens."

"Then we get more hands," Casper said.

He had learned something on the road, in the whiteout and after, in the long cold weeks of Bek's recovery: problems that looked like arithmetic were usually people problems wearing a disguise. Two pens was an arithmetic problem. The answer was a town.

* * *

The town was called Lowbridge, four miles downstream, and it was less a town than a scar where a town used to be—half its buildings burned in the purge years, half the rest collapsed from neglect, maybe forty people scratching a living out of the river silt and their own stubbornness. Casper rode down with Ila riding double behind him, her legs swinging, because she had declared at breakfast that she was coming and nobody had the energy to argue with a girl who had once repaired a milestone under threat of a rifle.

The man who ran Lowbridge's surviving well was named Odo Ferrin. He was sixty, missing two fingers to frostbite, and when Casper laid the first page of the census rolls on his table—the page with LOWBRIDGE DISTRICT inked across the top in a dead clerk's careful hand—Odo looked at it the way a starving man looks at bread he doesn't trust.

"That's my wife's name," he said finally. "Second column. Marisol Ferrin. That's her writing under it—she signed the household count herself." His voice did something complicated. "They told us the records burned. They stood in the square and told us everything burned, so there was nothing to go back to, nothing to prove we were ever—"

"I know," Casper said. "I was there when they said it."

"You were?" Odo squinted at him. "You're young for it."

"I'm older than I look. We all are, from there."

It took three hours to convince Lowbridge. It took one hour to equip them—Wren turned out to be very good at explaining how to make ink from soot and tallow, and Ila appointed herself instructor in letterforms, standing on a chair, drawing block capitals on a slate with the gravity of a general. By dusk, six households in Lowbridge had copies of their own pages, and a boy of fifteen named Tam had volunteered to ride upstream with the master rolls and carry copies to the settlements beyond the bend.

"They'll want to know why," Tam said, cinching his saddle. "Why now. Why us."

Casper thought about it. He had spent three years not answering that question himself.

"Because someone wrote your names down before anyone decided you didn't exist," he said. "And the writing survived. That's all. That's enough."

Tam nodded slowly, the way people nod when they've been handed something heavier than they expected but not unwelcome. Then he rode north along the river, and Casper watched him go until the willows took him.

* * *

They fell into a rhythm over the following weeks, the way water finds a channel.

Bek handled security, which mostly meant sitting on the way-station porch with her rifle across her knees and her frostbitten hand wrapped in clean linen, radiating the kind of calm that made trouble decide to happen somewhere else. She also turned out to know half the survivor settlements by name—she had helped burn some of them, in another life, and she carried those names like stones in a pack she never set down. She went to each one herself. She knocked. She said, "I was there. I'm sorry. Here is proof you existed." Some doors stayed shut. Most didn't.

Wren ran logistics, which meant she stole—openly now, cheerfully, from Ashgrave's abandoned supply depots and from anyone who had ever profited off displaced Veyrans. Paper, ink, lamp oil, a printing press from a dead-town chandler's back room that she hauled forty miles on a cart and presented to Casper like a cat presenting a bird. "You're welcome," she said. "I expect gratitude and also dinner."

And Casper copied. Every morning, first light, before the visitors came, he sat at the table with the chest open beside him and wrote. Household by household, district by district. The Republic had kept meticulous records right up until it decided records were dangerous; the census clerks had been the last honest bureaucracy in Veyra, and their work outlived them. Casper had been apprenticed to a mapmaker, not a census-taker, but he understood the discipline—it was the same discipline. You put what was true onto the surface in front of you, exactly, without flinching, because someday someone would need to find their way by it.

Some days the copying was just work. Some days it wasn't.

The day he reached the Kestrel District rolls, it wasn't.

* * *

Kestrel District. The cartographer's quarter. His mentor's street.

He knew the page before he read it—he knew the shape of it, the density of names, the little marginal notes the clerks had made about households that had taken in neighbors after the first burnings. And there, third entry from the top, in the clerk's hand and then in a second hand he would have known anywhere, blindfolded, underwater:

**Aldous Merrin, cartographer, Royal Survey Office (dissolved). Residence: 9 Lantern Row.**

And beneath it, in Aldous's own slanted script, added when the clerk came door to door in the last autumn: *Household includes apprentice, C. Nakamura, age 19.*

His name. Written down by the man who taught him to draw coastlines, three months before the purge, three months before the night Casper went out the back window while Aldous barred the front door and told him, in the voice he used for correcting bad survey work, *Run straight, boy. Don't look back until the river.*

Casper hadn't run straight. He'd run in circles for a year, and then in lines for two more, counting days in distance, telling himself the flat horizon was peace.

He sat with the page for a long time. The lamp burned. Outside, Ila was teaching Wren's mule to shake hands, which the mule resented and Wren found hilarious, and their noise came through the window thin and warm.

When Bek came in at midday with dispatches from the northern settlements, she found him still there.

She didn't ask. She poured two cups of the terrible coffee Wren traded for, set one by his elbow, and read the page upside down across the table, because Bek Odumane did not believe in pretending not to see things.

"He signed for you," she said.

"He lied for me, more likely. Told the clerk I was household staff so I wouldn't be listed as kin. Kin got relocated. Staff got ignored." Casper turned the page toward himself. "This is the only place in the world where the two of us are written on the same line."

Bek drank her coffee. She was a woman who let silences do work.

"You kept everyone else's page for them," she said at last. "Copied it, delivered it, watched them cry over it. This one's yours."

"It belongs to the district."

"The district has its copy. This is the original, and you're the executor of a dead man's estate whether you like it or not." She stood, collected the dispatches, and paused at the door. "My sergeant's name is in here too. Third district. I copied it myself, last month. I keep it in my breast pocket." A shrug that wasn't a shrug. "People say you should let the dead go. Nobody who says it has ever lost anyone worth keeping."

She left him alone with it, which was its own kindness.

That night, Casper did not file the page with the others. He cut it free along the margin—cleanly, with the bone folder Aldous had given him at fourteen, the one tool he'd carried out of Veyra—and he folded it into quarters, and he put it in the breast pocket of his coat, over the left side, where a person keeps things.

* * *

By early summer the river settlements had a name for what they were doing, though Casper hadn't given them one. They called it the scattering. Word traveled faster than Tam's horse: *the ford station has the rolls, the ford station will copy your page, the ford station asks nothing.* Which wasn't quite true—they asked one thing, and it was the same thing everywhere: *tell us if our people survived.* So the way-station became a relay point, letters flowing up and down the river like the current itself, reuniting sisters and cousins and old neighbors who had each believed the other erased. Wren rigged a signal post on the bluff—a mirror by day, a lantern by night—and took enormous pride in the fact that she could flash a message eight miles to the bend settlement faster than any rider.

"You built a communications network out of salvage and spite," Casper told her.

"I built it out of competence," Wren said. "The spite is just fuel."

Ila, meanwhile, had grown two inches and acquired a following. Children from three settlements came to the ford on market days to learn her letterforms, and she drilled them mercilessly on the proper drawing of the Veyran seal—not as a flag, she explained to anyone who asked, with the fierce precision of a twelve-year-old who had thought about it more than most adults, but as a signature. "A seal says *this was real,*" she told Casper once, chalk dust on her nose. "Milestones, documents, doesn't matter. It's how the dead sign their names."

He had no argument. He'd stopped having arguments with Ila around the time she out-argued Bek, which was a feat previously considered impossible.

One evening in high summer, a woman arrived at the ford on foot, dusty, sixty-ish, with a surveyor's transit strapped to her back. She introduced herself as Pell, late of the Royal Survey Office, and asked if the stories were true that Nakamura the mapmaker's boy was keeping the rolls.

"He was my junior clerk for one season," she said, when Casper confirmed it. "Before Merrin poached you. He always said you'd be better than him." She looked around the yard—at Wren's signal post, at Ila's school bench, at Bek's rifle rack, at the chest itself, open and unguarded on the table inside, because guarding it had stopped making sense months ago. "He'd have liked this. He always said a map was just a promise that a place exists. You've gone and promised a lot of places."

After she left, Casper walked down to the ford alone, as he did most evenings now, and stood where the water ran loud over the stones.

Three years ago he had crossed a river like this one, alone, with a stolen horse and a head full of coordinates, and he had measured his life in miles since—miles put between himself and Veyra, miles of flats and ranges, miles as a substitute for the thing he refused to name. He had pulled a chest out of a flood and thought it was a treasure, then a trap, then a burden. It had turned out to be a ledger of debts, and he had spent a year paying them down, page by page, name by name, and the strange truth—the truth he could feel now, ankle-deep in cold water under a sky going amber—was that the paying had paid *him*. Every page delivered was a mile he didn't need anymore.

Aldous had told him to run straight. It had taken him three years and a thief, a soldier, and a stonemason's daughter to understand the instruction. Straight wasn't a direction. It was a line between two points, and both points had to be real. He'd finally found the second one.

Behind him, up the path, the way-station lamps were lighting one by one. Wren's voice carried down the slope, arguing with Bek about something; Ila's laugh cut through it like a blade through rope. Tomorrow there would be more pages to copy, more riders, more names moving up and down the river like fish returning to a cleaned stream. Somewhere south, in whatever country Colonel Ashgrave had failed to buy himself, there would be other men with ledgers and ambitions—but that was tomorrow's weather, and tonight the water ran clear.

Casper touched the folded page in his breast pocket, once, the way you touch a milestone to confirm the distance.

Then he turned back up the path, toward the light, toward the voices that knew his name and used it, and went home.
