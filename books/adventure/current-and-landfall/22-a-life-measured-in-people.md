## 22. A Life Measured in People

The river broke up in the third week of thaw, and the sound of it carried all the way to the ridge where Casper stood with a hammer in his hand and a nail between his teeth.

He'd forgotten, in three years of flats and dead springs and counting, that water could be loud. The Serpent Range still wore its snow like a white cloak thrown over one shoulder, but down here the ford had opened, the ice going out in groaning plates that rode the current and ground themselves to nothing against the shallows. The willows along the bank had gone the color of green fire. Somewhere below, Ila was shouting at Bek about fence posts.

Casper took the nail out of his teeth, set it against the beam, and drove it home with one swing.

The way-station was not much yet. A long hall with a stone hearth at one end, built on the bones of the old ford crossing where Bek had once turned her rifle on her own unit. A stable with a sagging roof that Wren swore she'd fix and had not fixed. Two bunkrooms, a kitchen, a porch facing the water. They'd raised the frame in the gray tail of winter when the ground was hard enough to haul timber, and all through the cold months people had come — a few at first, then more — drawn by word passed settlement to settlement along the river, the way word always traveled in the empty country: slowly, and mostly true.

*The ford,* the word said. *There's a place at the ford. Ask for Nakamura.*

He still wasn't sure how he felt about that. Being asked for. Being a name that meant something other than a line on a dead republic's manifest.

He drove the last nail, tested the rail, and looked out at the water.

* * *

They had scattered the rolls in the autumn, after the mine, after Ashgrave walked into the snow and did not come back. Casper had thought that would be the end of it — the copying, the riding, the long cold circuit of survivor camps with a satchel full of names. He had imagined handing over the pages and riding on, the way he'd ridden on from everything for three years. Deliver the cargo. Leave before anyone learned your face.

It hadn't worked that way.

The first settlement had been a cluster of tents in the lee of a burned granary, forty-some people who had fled Veyra's collapse and been living as squatters in a country that wanted them gone. When Casper read out the names — his own careful cartographer's hand, the census rolls he'd once believed were treasure — an old woman had stood up in the middle of it and said, *Say mine again. Slower.* And when he did, she had wept, and her neighbors had touched her shoulders, and Casper had understood that he was not delivering cargo. He was returning something that had been stolen so long people had stopped believing it existed: the fact of themselves, written down, witnessed, real.

The old woman's name had been Sefa. She had fed them lamb and flat bread and refused payment, and when they left she had pressed a copper coin into Ila's hand — a Veyran coin, worn smooth — and said, *So you remember where the names came from.*

They'd copied the rolls four times before winter closed in. Bek's handwriting was a soldier's — square, merciless, legible. Wren forged the seal at the top of each page so well that Casper had laughed until his ribs hurt, and then had felt strange about laughing. Ila had insisted on drawing the milestone seal on every cover, her tongue between her teeth, her father's chisel-work reproduced in ink.

*The dead deserve their names kept,* she'd said, and none of them had argued. Nobody argued with Ila about names.

* * *

"You're brooding on the porch again."

Wren came up the steps with a coil of rope over one shoulder and straw in her hair, which was the default condition of Wren's hair. She'd cut it short at midwinter and it was already shagging back into its old disorder, dark and wind-tangled, and she had a smudge of pitch on her jaw and the particular walk of a person who had been arguing with a horse.

"I'm not brooding," Casper said. "I'm inspecting the rail."

"You've been inspecting the rail for an hour." She dropped the rope on the porch boards and sat beside him, close, the way she sat now — no careful distance, no thief's spacing between her body and the nearest exit. "Bek wants to know if you're going to help with the fence or if you're going to stand there being a landmark."

"Landmarks are useful."

"Landmarks don't complain about the food."

"I've never once complained about the food."

"You complained about the food on Tuesday."

"That was a *question* about the food. I asked what was in the stew."

"And then you made a face."

Casper looked at her. She was looking at the river with the expression she wore when she was pretending not to be happy, which was the only expression she had for happiness — a kind of narrowed-eyed suspicion, as though joy were a mark she was casing for a job. Three years of chasing her across the flats, and what he remembered most now was not the pursuit. It was the dead spring, the first time he'd caught her trail, and the way she'd waited there — because she'd read the names, because she'd known he would come, because some part of her had already decided that the man on her trail was the only other person alive who understood what the chest meant.

"You know what I was thinking about," he said, "before you came out here being insulting."

"My stew."

"The spring." He turned the hammer over in his hands. "Where I caught your trail. I used to count days in miles out there. Twenty miles to water. Sixty to the pass. I had the whole desert in my head as distances. It was — I told myself it was peaceful."

"And now?"

He considered the honest answer, which was that he had not counted a single mile since the thaw began. He measured things in other units now. How many bunks were made. How many names were on the slate by the kitchen door — the slate where arrivals wrote themselves in, because Ila had decided that anyone who slept under this roof deserved to be written down. Forty-one names as of last night. Forty-one, on a slate Ila had framed in scrap timber with her father's chisel.

"Now I count other things," he said.

Wren nodded slowly, still watching the water. "Forty-two by tonight, probably. Bek said the Kellin family's coming up from the delta. Three of them. The ones from page sixty."

Page sixty. Casper knew it without looking. He knew most of the pages now, the way he'd once known the desert — not as distance but as people. The Vell family, page twelve, who had arrived in the first snow and whose eldest son now did most of the smithing. Old Sefa, page three, who had come at the thaw with eleven others from her camp and who had taken over the kitchen with the gentle tyranny of a woman who had fed forty people on nothing for two years. The Odumane cousins, page thirty-one, who had walked three hundred miles because Bek's name was in the rolls and they wanted to see with their own eyes what a defector built when she stopped defecting and started staying.

"You know what's strange," Wren said. "I spent my whole life making sure no ledger had my name in it. Names were how they caught you. Names were how they found your family, your debts, your—" She stopped, flicked a bit of straw off her knee. "And now I inked a seal on four hundred copies of one, and it felt like the first honest work I ever did."

Casper said nothing, because there was nothing to say that she didn't already know. That was the thing about the road, he was learning. It taught you people the way the desert taught him water — where it hid, how it moved, what it looked like when it was close. He'd found Wren at a dead spring because he could read a trail. He'd kept her because she'd read him back.

Below them, Ila's voice rose over the willows — *Bek, that post is crooked, my father would have had you flogged* — and Bek's laugh, deep and unbothered, and the ring of a maul on wood. Ila had appointed herself foreman of the fence in the first week and nobody had contested it. She was thirteen now, or near enough; they'd decided her birthday fell at the thaw, because she couldn't remember the true date and she'd chosen the day the river broke up, and that was as good a day as any to have been born. She wore her father's smallest chisel on a cord around her neck. She had repaired the milestone at the ford crossing in March, the one with Veyra's seal, and this time nobody had argued, and this time nobody had died for it.

That was the whole point, Casper thought. That was what they'd bought at the pass, in the whiteout, at the mine, with the two graves on the traverse that Bek still visited when the weather allowed. The right to repair a milestone without it being a death sentence. The right to a name in a ledger without it being a trap.

* * *

He went down to help with the fence in the afternoon, because landmarks should be useful, and the work was good in the way work was good when your hands knew it was for something. Bek set posts with the economy of a woman who had built fortifications in worse country. Ila supervised. Casper dug holes and tamped earth and let the rhythm of it empty his mind down to the clean grain of the task.

Bek's hand was healed, mostly. The frostbite had taken the tip of her little finger and part of the one beside it, and she'd joked at the time — *good, one less trigger finger to think about* — but she didn't joke about it anymore, and neither did they. She wore gloves now, even in spring. Some mornings she stood at the river's edge for a long time, looking at the far bank, and Casper had learned not to interrupt. He knew what she was looking at. Not the enemy. The version of herself who had stood at this ford with a rifle and made, in one breath, the choice that had unmade her old life. He understood that look. He'd worn it for three years, looking back at Veyra.

"You're staring," Bek said, without turning.

"Checking your fence line."

"My fence line is straighter than your porch rail."

"Nothing is straighter than my porch rail. I built that rail."

"Then the porch is crooked." She drove the post home with two blows, stood, and finally looked at him, and there was something in her face that had been there for a week, some letter she was composing. "Rider on the delta road," she said. "Wren saw him at noon. Alone. Veyran saddle rig, army remount brand filed off. He'll be here by dark."

Casper felt the old reflexes wake — the map of escape routes laying itself out behind his eyes, the count of rifles in the hall, the arithmetic of distance. He let it rise and then, deliberately, let it settle. "One rider."

"One rider."

"Armed?"

"Sidearm. Openly carried, which out here means he wants to be seen as honest or as stupid."

"Which is it?"

Bek almost smiled. "Wren says honest. I say we let him eat first and find out."

They let him eat first. That was the house rule, Ila's rule, chalked on the slate by the door in her careful thirteen-year-old hand: *Nobody is turned away before supper. Decisions after supper.* It had been tested twice — once by a horse thief whom Wren recognized from her old life, who ate, was fed, and left at dawn with his horse and a warning and, everyone agreed, a changed expression; and once by a half-starved ex-warden's clerk who turned out to have been one of Tessaly's people at the pass, and who had wept into his stew and been given a bunk and a job hauling water, and who had turned out, in the spring, to be not the worst hand with a hammer.

The rider came in at last light. He was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, with the bowed legs of a career cavalryman and the careful manners of a man entering a place he'd heard about and half disbelieved. He stood in the doorway of the hall with his hat in his hands while forty people ate, and his eyes went around the room — the slate with its names, the milestone seal carved over the hearth, the Veyran copper coin Ila kept on the mantel — and something in his face came apart, quietly, the way ice comes apart in the spring.

"I'm looking for Nakamura," he said. "Casper Nakamura."

Casper set down his spoon. Around the hall, the room had gone still, but it was not the old stillness of danger. It was the stillness of a household watching its own threshold.

"You found him," Casper said. "Supper's on. Decisions after."

The man sat. He ate like a soldier, fast and grateful, and afterward, when the bowls were cleared and the fire was down to a red heart, he took a folded paper from inside his coat and set it on the table between them.

"There's a settlement on the upper Veyra," he said. "Two hundred people, maybe more, what's left of the river garrisons and their families. They heard about the rolls. They heard there's copies. They sent me because—" He faltered, and looked down at the paper, and Casper saw it was a petition, signed, in dozens of hands, some of them shaking. "Because they want their names read out. Properly. All of them. And they want — they're asking if there's a place. A place that takes people whose names are in a dead republic's book. They heard there was one at the ford."

Casper looked at the paper. The signatures went on and on. He recognized, near the bottom, a name from page nineteen of the rolls — a garrison clerk, a woman, whose entry he had copied four times in his own careful hand.

He thought about the man he had been three years ago, at a dead spring, counting miles. Twenty to water. Sixty to the pass. A whole country measured in the distance between himself and everything.

He slid the petition across the table to Ila, who had appeared at his elbow the way she always did when something important was happening, and who read it twice with her lips moving, and then looked up at him.

"Page nineteen," she said. "We have room."

"We have room," Casper said.

The hall exhaled — not silence but the opposite, the low warm noise of a house deciding to grow. Wren was already reaching for the slate. Bek was already calculating timber. And Casper sat in the firelight with the hammer-calloused hand of a man who no longer counted miles, and thought: this is what I built. Not found. Not inherited. Built, post by post, name by name, at a ford where a soldier once chose a new loyalty in a single breath.

Outside, the river ran loud with the thaw, carrying the last of the winter's ice down toward the sea, and the first stars came out over the Serpent Range, and the slate by the door waited for its forty-third name.
