## 1. The Weight of Brass

The debt collector came on a Tuesday, which Felix Sterling would later consider the last ordinary day of his life.

He knew it was a Tuesday because Tuesdays were when Mrs. Okonkwo from the flat below climbed her stairs slowly enough that Felix could hear every one of her forty-three steps, and he had counted thirty-nine of them when the knock came at his door. Nobody knocked at his door. The landlord slid notices under it. His mother's few remaining friends had stopped calling after the funeral. And creditors, as a rule, preferred letters — they were cheaper, and they didn't have to watch your face while they told you what you already knew.

Felix opened the door to find a broad man in a gray suit holding a leather folder the way a doctor holds an X-ray: with professional sympathy and no good news inside.

"Mr. Sterling? Felix Sterling?"

"That depends on how much I owe."

The man smiled the way people smile at jokes they've heard four hundred times. "I'm afraid we need to discuss the estate of Aldous Sterling. There are certain... irregularities."

*Irregularities.* That was the word that had been following Felix around for eleven months now, ever since the lawyers finished going through his father's papers and discovered that the great Aldous Sterling — chief engineer, bridge-builder, the man whose photograph hung in two separate railway museums — had died owing more money than most towns saw in a fiscal year. Not gambling debts. Not debts of vanity. Debts of *silence*. Payments made to people who did not exist on paper, to accounts in places that did not appear on maps, stretching back nearly three decades like a river running under a house.

"Irregularities," Felix repeated. "Is that the technical term."

"It's the polite one." The man held out the folder. "There's a final notice regarding the survey contract. Your father's name is still attached to certain obligations, and there's a party interested in—"

"No." Felix said it before he'd decided to say it, which was how he said most true things. "Whatever my father signed, whatever deal he was chasing at the end, I'm not finishing it. I'm not paying for it either, but mostly I'm not finishing it."

The collector looked at him for a long moment, and something almost human crossed his face — pity, maybe, or its well-dressed cousin. "Mr. Sterling, nobody finishes their parents' deals. They just inherit the interest."

He left the folder on the step. Felix let it sit there overnight, through rain, because he was petty in small ways even when he was broke in large ones.

---

In the morning the folder was warped but legible, and Felix sat at his kitchen table with cold coffee and read it twice.

It was a survey contract. That much was straightforward: a private commission to assess the condition of closed rail lines in the Kessarin River territory — lines shut down twenty-six years ago and left to rust into the jungle, officially because of "economic nonviability," unofficially because nobody official would say why at all. The client was listed as a land management concern with a name so bland it had to be fake: Territorial Holdings Group. The pay was obscene. Three months' work, and it would clear the principal on the estate, keep the family house out of receivership, and leave enough over for Felix to do something he hadn't done since he was nineteen, which was buy a train ticket without checking his balance first.

The part that wasn't straightforward was the letter clipped behind the contract. It was written in his father's handwriting.

Not addressed to anyone. Just a page, folded into the file years ago by some clerk who hadn't known what they were holding, dated eleven days before Aldous Sterling's death:

*Kessarin is not what the maps say it is. If anyone comes asking about the closed lines, be careful who you answer. Some doors are locked from the outside. Some are locked from within. Check which kind before you open it.*

*A.*

Felix read it four times. Then he put it face-down on the table, went to the window, and watched the city go about its morning — trams shrieking, gulls arguing over the fish market, a thousand people walking to work at jobs that paid them honestly or dishonestly but at least predictably.

His father had been dead eleven months, and this was the first time Felix had heard his voice since the funeral. It sounded exactly as it always had: tired, wry, and absolutely certain that whatever trouble was coming, it could be handled with preparation and a steady hand. Aldous Sterling had believed in preparation the way other men believed in God.

The trouble was that preparation had not saved him. Whatever he'd been preparing for, it had caught up with him anyway, somewhere between the third mortgage and the final payment to an account that didn't exist.

Felix picked up the letter again. *Be careful who you answer.*

"Well," he said aloud, to the kitchen, to the letter, to the ghost of a man who had never once in his life given straight advice when cryptic advice was available, "that's not ominous at all."

---

He should have refused. He wants it noted, in whatever account eventually gets written of his life, that he understood perfectly well he should have refused. The money was too good, the territory too remote, the letter too strange. Every instinct he'd inherited from his mother — the sensible half of the family, the half that balanced checkbooks and read contracts — said walk away.

But then there was the other inheritance. The brass compass.

It lived in the bottom drawer of his desk, wrapped in a chamois cloth, and he took it out that evening the way he took it out maybe twice a year: to hold it, turn it over, run his thumb across the engraving on the back. *A.S. → F.S., 1897.* He'd been nine when his father gave it to him, standing on the platform at Corvel Junction with the evening express breathing steam behind them.

"A compass doesn't tell you where to go," his father had said, closing Felix's fingers around it. "It tells you where you're facing. Those are different problems, and men who confuse them end up lost in interesting places."

"What if I don't want to be lost anywhere?"

His father had laughed — that big, unguarded laugh that seemed impossible now, knowing what it must have cost him to produce it all those years while the debts piled up in the dark. "Then you're not paying attention. Everybody's lost somewhere, Felix. The trick is being lost toward something."

Felix set the compass on the table beside the contract and watched the needle settle. It pointed north, as compasses do, but sitting next to his father's handwriting it felt less like an instrument and more like a question.

Three months' work. Enough to erase the debt, save the house, start over. A door opening after eleven months of doors closing — foreclosure notices, the auction of his father's tools, the museum quietly removing the photograph from its lobby when the scandal broke. Felix had spent a year learning to live smaller, and he'd gotten good at it, the way you get good at anything through practice, and he hated it.

And underneath the money, underneath even the fear, was the thing he couldn't say out loud yet: *the closed lines were his father's lines.* Aldous Sterling had worked the Kessarin territory early in his career — Felix had grown up on stories about it, the gorge country, the river that ran backward in flood season, the junction town where the men drank pepper tea and argued about bridge tolerances until dawn. His father had loved that valley. Then he'd spent thirty years secretly paying for something connected to it, and died with a warning about it folded into his files.

If Felix took the contract, he wouldn't just be earning money. He'd be walking into the one blank space in his father's life, carrying the only map marked in the old man's hand.

That map was upstairs. It had come with the estate papers — a survey chart of the Kessarin lines, decades old, annotated in pencil in margins that got denser toward the upper reaches of the river. Most of the notes were engineering shorthand: load ratings, span measurements, a recurring notation — *gate status?* — penciled beside a feature near the top of the map that the official legend didn't label at all. Just a blank space where a station should have been, and in his father's tight hand, over and over across the years: *gate status? gate status? gate status?*

Felix spread it out under the lamp and traced the blank space with one finger.

"Gate status," he murmured. "What gate, old man? What were you checking on for thirty years?"

The compass needle trembled — a truck passing outside, probably, or the building settling, or nothing at all. Felix looked at it anyway. Being lost toward something. He'd been lost toward nothing for a year now, and nothing was a direction too; it just took longer to arrive.

---

He signed the contract on Thursday. The agent from Territorial Holdings — a brisk woman named Ms. Ferro who smelled faintly of engine oil under expensive perfume — met him at a café near the freight yards and watched him sign with an expression he couldn't quite read. Relief, maybe. Or the particular tension of someone watching a piece move into place on a board she hadn't designed.

"You'll join the expedition at Lowmarsh," she said, gathering the papers. "River transport the whole way — the lines can't be reached by road. Survey team's already assembled. You'll report to the expedition lead, Odile Vann."

"Vann." Felix turned the name over. It snagged on something, some memory of his father's stories, but wouldn't come loose. "I don't know the name."

Something flickered across Ms. Ferro's face — quick, gone before he could name it. "She comes highly recommended. Former military survey corps. Very thorough." She stood, shook his hand with a grip like a coupling pin, and paused at the edge of the table. "One thing, Mr. Sterling. The Kessarin territory is... off the grid. No telegraph past the gorge, no reliable post. Once you're upriver, you're committed."

"I've read the terms."

"The terms aren't what I mean." She met his eyes, and for a moment the brisk professionalism dropped and he saw something underneath it that looked almost like regret. "Your father worked that territory. You know that."

"He did."

"And you're going anyway."

Felix thought about lying, decided against it, and settled on the truth in its smallest possible form. "I'm going anyway."

Ms. Ferro nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed something she'd suspected. "Then pack light, Mr. Sterling. And—" she hesitated, and he would think about that hesitation for weeks afterward, turning it over like a stone looking for what was under it, "—pack for longer than three months."

She left coins on the table for coffees neither of them had drunk, and was gone into the yard noise.

---

He packed that night, and it took twenty minutes, which told him everything about how much of a life he had left to pack.

Clothes, instruments, drafting tools, the waterproof document case. His mother's photograph, though he'd have denied it under oath. A tin of the pepper tea his father used to drink, bought from a shop that still stocked it, because some superstitions you keep even when you don't believe in them.

Last, the compass and the map. He wrapped the compass in its chamois and put it in his breast pocket instead of his bag, where he could feel its small weight against his chest like a second heartbeat. The map he rolled tight and sleeved in oilcloth. At the door he stopped, went back, and added his father's field notebook — mostly blank, the last third filled with the dense, increasingly agitated entries from the final years. If he was going to walk into his father's blank spaces, he wanted the old man's voice along.

At the window, the city glittered its indifferent glitter. Somewhere north and east, up a river he'd never seen, a valley was waiting with a locked door and a question his father had asked in pencil for thirty years.

Felix Sterling was twenty-eight years old, insolvent, orphaned, and — for the first time in eleven months — pointed somewhere.

"Toward something," he told the compass, and it spun lazily and settled north, agreeing with him or humoring him, which with instruments as with fathers was often the same thing.

He slept badly and woke early, and took the morning freight east toward the coast and the rusting river port called Lowmarsh, where a boat and a crew and an expedition leader named Vann were waiting.

He did not know yet that Odile Vann would flinch at the sound of his surname. He did not know about the radio, or the storm, or the boy in the engine hold, or the lamplight moving in the hills above a drowned station. He knew only that a door had opened after a very long winter of doors closing, and that his father's last words on paper had been a warning, and that he had walked through anyway.

Some doors are locked from the outside, his father had written. Some from within.

Felix intended to find out which kind this one was — and, if it came to it, to pick the lock himself.
