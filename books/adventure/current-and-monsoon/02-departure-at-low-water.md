## 2. Departure at Low Water

The river port of Callow's Bend had once been the kind of place where steamboats queued three deep and dockhands sang work songs in four languages. Now it was a rusting comma at the end of a sentence nobody was writing anymore. Felix Sterling stepped off the mail coach with his duffel over one shoulder and his father's map rolled tight in a tin tube, and he stood for a moment on the cracked apron of concrete, letting the smell of it settle into him—diesel, river mud, rust, and underneath it all, that green water-smell that every river town wore like a faded coat.

A heron stood on a bollard, regarding him with the flat judgment of a creditor.

"I know," Felix told it. "I'm here about money too."

The contract had come by post six days ago, typed on good paper with a letterhead he'd had to look up: *Vann River Survey & Cartographic Services*. The terms were generous to the point of insult—generous enough that Felix had read them four times looking for the catch. Survey the closed rail lines of the Kestrel Territory. Three months' work. Payment on completion, half up front. The half up front had already paid off the worst of the debt collectors, the ones who knocked instead of just sending letters, and there was a particular kind of silence in an apartment when the knocking stops that Felix could have wept over.

His father had worked these lines. That was the part Felix kept turning over like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. Aldous Sterling had been a railroad engineer for thirty years, and for the last decade of his life he'd been a broke one, and when he died he left his son nothing but debts, a brass compass worn smooth from a lifetime of thumbs, and a map of the upper river marked in pencil in handwriting Felix knew better than his own. *Do not survey what is not lost,* one note read, underlined twice. Felix had never figured out what it meant. He'd packed the map anyway. Some things you carry not because they're useful but because putting them down would mean admitting what they were.

The expedition boat sat low against the dock at the far end of the port—a river steamer gone gray with age, sixty feet of working hull called the *Marrow*, which Felix decided was either honest or ominous and probably both. She had a patched funnel, a wheelhouse with one window fogged white, and cargo netting strung along her rails hung with drying tarpaulins. Men were loading crates down her gangway with the unhurried rhythm of people who had done this many times and expected to do it exactly as many more.

Felix checked the letter again, though he knew the words by heart. *Report to O. Vann, berth seven, Callow's Bend, on or before the 14th.* Today was the 13th. He was early. He'd been early to everything since the debts started, as if punctuality were a form of penance.

He found Odile Vann on the boat's aft deck, going through a crate of instruments with a jeweler's focus. She was perhaps fifty, built like a mooring post, with iron-gray hair cut short and hands that looked like they'd argue with machinery and win. She didn't look up when he came aboard.

"You're the engineer," she said. Not a question.

"Felix Sterling. You have my—"

"I know what I have." She looked up then, and her eyes did something strange. They went to his face first, the way anyone's would, and then dropped to the duffel strap in his hand, and then somewhere past his shoulder, out over the brown water, and stayed there a beat too long. "Sterling," she said, quieter, as if testing the weight of it.

"That's me."

"Yes." She set down the sextant she'd been holding with exaggerated care, the way you put down something when your hands aren't entirely steady. "Welcome aboard the *Marrow*, Mr. Sterling."

And that was the whole of it. No flinch, no startle—nothing so obvious. Just a stillness that lasted two seconds too long, and a woman suddenly very interested in her instruments, and the particular quality of attention a person pays when they're trying not to pay attention. Felix had spent five years being audited, assessed, and appraised by people deciding how much of him was worth taking. He knew what calculation looked like from the outside.

This hadn't looked like calculation. It had looked like recognition.

* * *

The crew assembled on deck that evening for introductions, such as they were. There were six of them besides Vann, and Felix made it his business to learn names the way he learned anything—methodically, with notes.

There was Dov, the cook, a wide soft-spoken man whose galley smelled of cardamom and whose opinion of everyone seemed to be filed under *pending*. There were the Halloran twins, Pim and Ross, deckhands who finished each other's sentences mostly incorrectly. There was a surveyor named Enkhuizen, a Dutchman with a beard like a storm front, who shook Felix's hand with genuine warmth and immediately tried to recruit him into an argument about load tolerances on truss bridges, which was the fastest any stranger had ever made Felix feel at home.

And there was Amara Osei.

She arrived last, coming down the dock with a canvas bag over her shoulder and the walk of someone who owned whatever ground she crossed but wasn't sentimental about it. She was compact and quick-eyed, maybe thirty-five, with close-cropped hair going silver at the temples and a way of looking at the *Marrow*'s hull that Felix recognized—it was the look of a person reading a document for fraud.

"Who patched the seam on the port side?" she demanded, before hello.

"Previous owner," said Vann.

"Previous owner should be arrested." Amara came up the gangway, nodded once at the assembled company as if confirming a suspicion, and stuck out her hand at Felix. "Amara Osei. I pilot this barge."

"Engineer," Felix said. "Passenger, technically."

"Nobody on a riverboat is technically anything. Everybody's crew the moment the water gets ideas." Her grip was brief and strong. "You've run the Kestrel before?"

"Never been north of Callow's Bend."

Something flickered across her face too—not quite the flinch Vann had given him, but a cousin of it. "Then you're in for an education." She said it lightly, but her eyes went to Vann, quick as a fish surfacing, and Vann was suddenly very busy with a lantern.

Felix let it go. He was getting a lot of practice letting things go lately; it was practically his only exercise.

Dinner was served on deck because the cabin below was full of crates, and it was good—rice and smoked fish and some dark stewed greens that Dov refused to name—and for an hour the evening almost managed to be pleasant. The twins argued about whether a heron could beat a cormorant in a fight. Enkhuizen held forth on the tensile properties of wrought iron until even he got bored of himself. The river slid past black and slow, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

It was during the second helping that Felix made his mistake, if it was a mistake. Enkhuizen asked, casually, what had brought him to closed-line survey work, and Felix said, "My father ran those lines, years back. Aldous Sterling."

The table went quiet.

Not dramatically. Nobody dropped a fork. But the conversation thinned out the way a river shallows over a sandbar, and Dov became intensely interested in the rice pot, and the twins stopped talking mid-argument, and Amara looked at her plate, and Odile Vann said, "Did he," in a voice as flat and careful as a level bubble finding center.

"He died three years ago," Felix added, because the silence needed filling and he'd never learned when to stop. "Left me his debts and his compass. I figure surveying his old lines is as good a use for either as anything."

"Well," said Vann, standing, "we make way at dawn. Get some sleep, all of you."

She left the table. The evening sort of folded itself up after that, everyone drifting to their berths with the polite haste of guests at a party where something has broken in the next room. Felix helped Dov stack plates, and the big man said, quietly, without looking at him, "You shouldn't say names at dinner."

"Whose names?"

"Anybody's." And he carried the pot below.

* * *

Felix didn't sleep. He told himself it was the unfamiliar bunk, the river sounds, the general strangeness of a first night aboard—but that was a lie with good posture, and he knew it. He lay in his narrow berth listening to the hull tick and breathe, and he turned the evening over the way he'd turn a seized bearing, looking for where the friction was.

Odile Vann flinched at his surname. There was no gentler word for it. A woman who hired a stranger by post, who signed generous contracts, who ran her expedition with crisp competence—that woman had heard the name *Sterling* and gone still as a snapped wire. And Amara had known something. And Dov had warned him about names. And nobody, in the whole course of a long dinner, had asked Felix a single question about himself except Enkhuizen, bless him, who apparently hadn't gotten the memo or hadn't cared.

He got up, lit the little lamp, and unrolled his father's map on the desk bolted to the wall. The upper river spread out under the light, drawn in Aldous Sterling's confident hand—the same hand that had written *do not survey what is not lost*. Felix traced the lines north of Callow's Bend. Rail spurs, mile markers, a junction labeled in pencil and then, strangely, erased—not scribbled out but carefully rubbed away, the paper thinned where an eraser had worked patiently, as though the name had mattered too much to leave and too much to destroy outright.

Under the lamplight, holding the page at an angle, Felix could just make out the ghost of what had been erased. Two letters' worth of shadow. Something starting with *J*.

"Can't sleep either?"

Amara stood in the doorway, a mug in each hand. She handed him one without asking—coffee, black, strong enough to stand a spoon in—and leaned against the doorframe, watching him with those quick pilot's eyes.

"Thanks." He hesitated. "Ms. Osei, can I ask you something?"

"You can ask. Whether you get an answer depends on the question."

"What am I missing?"

She smiled, and it was a real smile, tired around the edges. "That's a better question than most." She drank her coffee. Outside, somewhere downriver, thunder muttered like an old man disagreeing with the news. "You're missing the fact that Odile Vann picked you personally. Out of forty applicants—I saw the file drawer. Forty applicants, and she hires the son of a man she goes quiet about. You don't find that interesting?"

"I find it terrifying. Interesting is what I'll call it when I've been paid."

"Honest. Good." She pushed off the doorframe. "One more thing, engineer. When the radio starts acting strange—and it will—you tell me first. Not Vann. Promise me that."

"Why?"

"Because I used to run boats for people who thought rivers belonged to them," Amara said, "and I know what it looks like when an expedition isn't really an expedition." She paused at the threshold, silhouetted against the passage lamp. "Get some sleep, Sterling. Dawn comes whether you trust it or not."

* * *

They cast off at first light, into a morning the color of pewter. The *Marrow* backed off berth seven with a shudder and a complaint of old timbers, and Callow's Bend began to slide away astern—first the docks, then the leaning warehouses, then the heron, which watched them go with the same flat judgment it had offered yesterday, having apparently concluded that Felix remained insufficiently solvent.

Felix stood at the stern rail with his coffee and his father's map tube and watched the last of the town dissolve into mist. Ahead the river narrowed, and the hills came down closer to the water, green and dripping and older-looking than hills had any right to be. Somewhere up there were closed rail lines and an erased junction and whatever it was that made a competent woman flinch at a dead man's name.

He was walking into something. He'd known it since the dinner table went quiet, and he boarded anyway, because the alternative was going back to the apartment where the knocking had stopped and waiting for it to start again. Hope, Felix was learning, felt almost identical to dread from the inside. Both of them kept you moving.

At the wheelhouse, Odile Vann rang for full ahead. Through the fogged glass, Felix watched her check the compass, then glance north upriver at the gathering clouds, and he noticed—for the first time, though it would matter later—that she held a folded paper in her free hand, creased soft from much handling, and that she put it away quickly when she saw him looking.

Three days later, the radio would die mid-sentence. But that morning, at low water, with the mist burning off and the engine settling into its long steady heartbeat, it was still possible to believe he'd simply been hired for a job.

The river, patient as a creditor, carried them north.
