## 3. Radio Silence

Amara Osei had a rule about rivers: they never lie, but they never tell you everything either. A river shows you its surface—brown water folding over itself, the occasional log spinning slow as a drunk thinking better of his evening—and keeps the rest, the snags and sandbars and drowned fences, for you to discover at speed.

She'd been reading this one for three days now, and it was lying by omission more than usual.

"Sandbar, port side, forty meters," she called over her shoulder, hands light on the wheel of the *Kestrel's Promise*, which was neither a kestrel nor, in her professional opinion, much of a promise. "We'll swing wide."

Behind her, Felix Sterling looked up from the charts spread across the chart table and made a note. He did that a lot—made notes, checked figures, ran his thumb along contour lines like he was reading braille. Three days aboard and she still hadn't decided what to make of him. He had the look of a man who'd been handed a bill so large he'd stopped flinching at individual line items. But his hands were steady on the sounding line when it was his turn to throw it, and he hadn't once complained about the coffee, which Amara considered the truest test of character a river could offer.

The coffee, for the record, was criminal.

"How far to the next marker?" Odile Vann asked from the bow, where she spent most of her daylight hours with binoculars, scanning the banks like the answer to something might step out of the ferns wearing a name tag.

Amara consulted the river in front of her rather than the map. The map said one thing; the river, lately, seemed to be saying another. "By the chart? Eleven kilometers. By my eyes?" She shrugged. "The chart hasn't met this river. Water's down half a meter from where the survey says it should be. Dry season running long upstream, or somebody's pulling more out than they're putting back."

Odile lowered the binoculars. For just a moment—a moment Amara caught only because she'd spent fifteen years catching moments exactly like that—the expedition leader's face did something complicated and then filed it away. "Noted," Odile said. "Log it."

*Noted. Log it.* Two days ago it would have been *are you sure?* followed by an actual conversation. Something had changed since yesterday morning, and Amara didn't need a radio to pick up that frequency.

She swung wide around the sandbar anyway. The *Kestrel's Promise* leaned into the turn with all the grace of a filing cabinet, and somewhere below decks Jun—who was not officially aboard, because officially there was no sixteen-year-old stowaway mechanic in the engine hold, and everyone had agreed to maintain this fiction with the seriousness of diplomats—could be heard arguing lovingly with the port engine.

"You're not a bad old girl," Jun's voice floated up through the deck plates, muffled and cheerful, "you're just *misunderstood*, and also leaking slightly, and we're going to fix both—"

"That child talks to engines," Amara murmured to Felix.

"I heard that!" Jun called up. "And I'm not a child, I'm a *professional*."

"The professional is grounded until her hands heal," Odile said without turning around.

"They're barely burned!"

"Barely is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

Amara smiled despite herself. It was strange how quickly a boat could become a household. Strange and, given everything, dangerous. Households were things you could lose.

***

The radio died at 4:17 in the afternoon, on the third day, mid-word.

They'd had contact with the advance team twice daily since departure—morning check-in, evening check-in, brief and crackly but regular as church. Rennick's voice, dry as the paperwork he loved, reporting positions, water levels, nothing remarkable. The advance team had gone ahead two weeks earlier to set base camp at the upper gorge and begin preliminary readings on the closed lines. Boring work. Reliable work. Rennick was a boring, reliable man, and Amara had come to find his evening reports oddly comforting, the way you find a neighbor's porch light comforting even if you've never spoken to him.

At 4:15, the radio had crackled alive with something that wasn't scheduled.

"*Kestrel, Kestrel, do you copy—we've got—*" Static, long and grinding, like gravel poured into a tin cup. Then Rennick's voice again, faster now, stripped of all its dryness: "*—not on any chart, Odile, I'm telling you it's not—there's a station here, there's a whole—*" A sound Amara couldn't identify. Not wind. Not water. "*—someone's cut the—*"

Then nothing. Not static, even. Nothing, the particular dead nothing of a carrier wave simply ceasing, which was different from a signal fading, and Amara knew the difference the way you know the difference between someone hanging up and someone dropping the phone.

She sat very still with her hand on the dial.

Felix looked up from the charts. Odile turned from the bow. Nobody spoke for perhaps five seconds, which on a small boat is a geological age.

"Try them again," Odile said.

Amara tried them again. She cycled through every frequency they'd agreed on, then the emergency ones, then, on general principle, the ones nobody used except smugglers and priests. She adjusted the antenna. She thumped the set once, gently, the way you'd pat a horse—not because it helps but because your hands want to do something.

Nothing.

"It's not the set," she said finally. "Set's fine. I checked it this morning, and it was fine, and it's fine now." She kept her voice level, pilot-flat, the voice she used for reporting weather that was about to kill someone. "Their signal didn't fade. It stopped. Mid-transmission. That's not distance, that's not weather, and that's not equipment failure. Equipment failure sounds like something. This sounded like someone turning it off."

"Or their transmitter failing," Felix offered. His voice was careful. She noticed he'd gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with seasickness.

"Transmitters fail loud or fail quiet, but they don't fail *mid-sentence* with a man saying *someone's cut the*—" She stopped herself. Took a breath. "I've run boats on this river for fifteen years, Mr. Sterling. I know what sabotage sounds like. I've heard it before." More than once. From the inside. She didn't say that part.

The sky had been building all afternoon—clouds stacking themselves up over the western ridgeline in great grey towers, the kind of clouds that start as scenery and end as biography. The light had gone the color of old brass. Downriver behind them, the world was still bright. Ahead, under those clouds, the river narrowed between walls of green stone, and the air smelled like wet iron and something green and growing too fast, the smell of a valley holding its breath.

"Turn us around?" Felix asked. It wasn't quite a question and wasn't quite a suggestion. It was the thing a careful man says when he wants to see who agrees.

Odile stood at the bow rail with her back to them for a long moment. When she turned, her face was the professional mask again, fitted snug, no gaps—but Amara had already seen the gaps, two days' worth of them, the flinch at Felix's surname on the dock, the way she went quiet whenever the advance team's route came up, the chart she kept folded to a page nobody else was allowed to annotate.

"No," Odile said. "We go on."

"Rennick's signal died saying *someone's cut the*—" Amara began.

"And we are eleven hours behind them at best, further at worst, and if someone has cut something, then every hour we spend debating is an hour they spend alone." Odile's voice was calm, reasonable, and absolutely sealed. "We continue. Full watch tonight. Amara, I want us through the narrows before dark if the water allows."

"The water doesn't allow," Amara said flatly. "Narrows after dark in falling water with a storm coming is how you write 'lost with all hands' on a memorial plaque. We anchor above the narrows and take them at first light."

A muscle worked in Odile's jaw. For a heartbeat Amara thought she'd win the argument, and then Odile nodded once, sharp, and said, "First light, then. Anchor above the narrows." And walked aft to her cabin, and closed the door, and did not come out.

Amara looked at Felix. Felix looked at Amara.

"You believe her?" he asked quietly. "That it's urgency?"

"I believe she believes it," Amara said. "That's not the same animal." She turned back to the wheel and began the slow business of finding an anchorage, eyes on the water, mind on the dead carrier wave. "Your father's partner, huh?"

"He knew my father. I don't know if they were partners. I don't know anything yet." Felix gathered the charts together with hands that wanted to shake and mostly didn't. "You've heard sabotage on this river before. You said it like a man saying he's seen a ghost."

"A woman," Amara corrected. "And I haven't seen a ghost. I *was* one, for a while." She found the eddy behind the rock spur she wanted, brought the boat around into it gentle as setting down a sleeping baby, and killed the engines. Silence rushed in, huge, filled immediately with river-noise: water worrying the hull, wind starting to talk in the treetops, thunder clearing its throat somewhere beyond the ridge. "Ask me someday when there's more rum aboard. Tonight we've got a boat to anchor and a watch to stand."

From below came a knock-knock-knock against the engine room floor—Jun's signal, three taps, meaning *I heard everything, is it bad?*

Amara knocked back twice: *bad enough.*

Three taps again: *I can fix things.*

Two knocks: *not this.*

A pause. Then, faintly, one tap. *Okay.*

"Yeah," Amara said softly, to the engine hold, to the river, to nobody. "Okay."

***

They anchored as the first rain arrived, fat drops the size of coins hitting the deck like applause from an audience that had decided against you. Amara rigged double anchors fore and aft, checked them twice, and stood her watch from eight to midnight while the storm worked itself up to a proper fury. Lightning stitched the gorge walls white. In the flashes she could see the narrows downstream, a quarter kilometer of river compressed between stone, water standing up in the middle of it in gray ridges even from here, even in the dark, even in the rain.

It was going to be a bastard at first light. Falling water plus storm surge equals rapids that rearrange themselves hourly. She'd thread it. It was what she was for, the one thing she was still for, after everything. But her hands on the wheel rail were cold, and not from the wind.

Felix relieved her at midnight, moving quiet, carrying two cups of the criminal coffee like penance. He stood beside her a moment before taking the wheel, looking downriver into the dark.

"My father used to say," he offered, apropos of nothing, "that a river's just a road that changes its mind."

"Your father," Amara said, "sounds like he understood rivers better than whoever drew these charts."

"He understood a lot of things better than he let on, I think." Felix's jaw tightened. "Everyone on this boat seems to have known him better than I did."

The rain hammered. Somewhere below, Jun slept in the engine hold among her tools, having formally requested—and been formally denied—permission to stand a watch, and having informally fallen asleep with a wrench in her hand anyway. Odile's cabin light burned past midnight, then past one, a yellow rectangle in the dark that Amara watched longer than she meant to.

At 2 a.m., half-asleep in the pilot's chair, Amara heard it.

The radio clicked.

Not on—she'd have heard the warm-up hum. Just a click, a single soft mechanical sound from the radio shack, like a switch being thrown, or a connection being made. She was out of the chair and across the cabin in four steps, heart going like the pistons below, and threw open the shack door.

The set sat dark and innocent on its shelf. The dial untouched. The power lead connected, connected to nothing but the battery bank, which read full.

But the antenna wire, the long copper one running up the mast, was humming. Very faintly. Not carrying a signal out. Carrying one *in*—or trying to. A whisper of modulation buried under the storm static, rhythmic, deliberate. Three pulses. Pause. Three pulses. Pause.

Amara stood in the doorway with rain-wet hair and listened to the river talk in code she almost recognized, and the dread that had been walking beside her for three days finally stepped out of the trees and showed its face.

Someone was signaling. And they were close enough that the signal didn't need a radio to be heard.

Above the narrows, on the black shoulder of the ridge, a light blinked once—twice—and went dark, and the storm swallowed the rest.
