## 4. The Stowaway

The engine hold of the *Kestrel's Wake* smelled like hot iron and old diesel, and Felix had been lying awake in his bunk for an hour listening to it breathe.

That was the thing about boats—unlike trains, they never went quiet. A locomotive at rest was a dead thing, cooling and ticking toward stillness. But a boat on anchor was alive whether you wanted it to be or not. The hull flexed. The bilge pump coughed every eleven minutes like a man clearing his throat before saying something unpleasant. And somewhere below the deck plates, there was a rhythm that didn't belong.

Felix had noticed it the second night out and told himself he was imagining it. A loose bearing. Water slapping in a void tank. By the third night, he'd stopped lying to himself, because the rhythm had a *shape*. It wasn't mechanical. Mechanical sounds repeated exactly; this one hesitated, shifted, caught itself—like someone trying very hard to be a machine and not quite managing it.

He got up, pulled on his boots, and took the lantern.

The corridor to the engine hold was narrow enough that his shoulders brushed both walls. Amara had the midnight watch on the wheelhouse above, which meant if he was wrong about this—if he woke the whole boat over a loose bearing—he'd never live it down. She'd already given him two nicknames in three days, both unflattering, both delivered with the flat calm of a woman who'd piloted rapids that would make a grown man weep and considered Felix Sterling mildly amusing cargo.

The hatch to the hold was latched from the outside. That was the first confirmation. Nobody latches an engine room from outside unless they don't want what's inside getting out—or don't want anyone going in.

Felix lifted the latch slowly, easing the hinge so it wouldn't sing.

Below, in the orange glow of the running lamp, the engine idled down for the night, and wedged into the gap between the coolant reservoir and the hull plating—folded up like a pocketknife someone had tried to close too fast—was a kid.

Sixteen, maybe. Thin as a rail spike, with grease worked so deep into their knuckles it looked like tattooing. Dark hair cut short and practical, a canvas jacket two sizes too big with the sleeves rolled six times, and a socket wrench clutched against their chest like a talisman. Their eyes were open. They'd been watching him come down the ladder the whole way.

"Don't," the kid said.

Felix stood on the last rung and considered this. "Don't what?"

"Yell. Don't yell. I'll go over the side if you yell." The voice cracked in the middle of the sentence, which settled the question of age better than any document could have. "I mean it. I've been swimming since I was four."

"It's the middle of a river in monsoon season."

"I know how long I can hold my breath."

"That's not—" Felix pinched the bridge of his nose. "That is not how drowning works."

"How would you know? You're a track man." The kid said *track man* the way other people said *parasite*.

"I'm a surveyor."

"You're a Meridian contract. I read the manifest when you boarded. Felix Sterling, consulting engineer, fee payable through Meridian Regional Holdings." The kid recited it flatly, from memory, and Felix felt the words land somewhere under his ribs. "So whatever you're about to do—drag me up, hand me to the captain, put me off at the next landing—I want you to know I'll fight, and I bite, and I've already loosened four bolts on your fuel line, so maybe think about who you want angry at you."

Felix looked at the fuel line. Sure enough, the bolts sat proud of their seats, backed off a quarter turn each. Not enough to leak. Enough to prove a point.

"That's actually good work," he heard himself say. "Even torque pattern. Most people just crank the nearest one."

The kid blinked. Whatever script they'd rehearsed for this moment—and clearly they'd rehearsed several—it hadn't included a review.

"You're not supposed to compliment the sabotage," they said.

"Well, it's distracting me from the part where I decide what to do with you, so thank you for that." Felix came down off the ladder and lowered himself onto an overturned crate, keeping his hands visible, keeping his voice level. Up close, the kid looked worse than thin. There was a bruise fading along the jaw, yellow-green, a week old at least, and the jacket sleeves hid wrists he was willing to bet weren't hiding nothing. "What's your name?"

A long pause. The engine ticked. Somewhere above them, Amara's boots crossed the wheelhouse deck, unhurried.

"Jun," the kid said finally.

"Just Jun?"

"Just Jun works fine."

"All right, Just Jun Works Fine." Something flickered across the kid's face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one might eventually be built. "How long have you been aboard?"

"Since Port Calloway. Two days before you came."

Felix did the math. "You've been living in my engine hold for five days."

"Somebody has to keep her running. Your pilot runs her hard. Third cylinder's running hot and nobody's noticed because she hasn't seized yet." Jun's chin came up, defiant, daring him to dispute it. "I've been bleeding her down at night. You're welcome."

And here was the strange thing—the thing Felix would turn over later, in the dark, trying to understand when it had happened: some door in his chest that had been rusted shut for three years swung open half an inch, all on its own, at the sight of a greasy teenager defending an engine that didn't belong to either of them.

His father used to find stowaways. That was the memory, arriving uninvited. Freight yards outside Marrow Junction, a boy of nine trailing behind a tall man in a conductor's coat, and his father crouching down to share bread with some runaway curled behind a coupling, talking soft, never once raising his voice, walking the kid to the yard office after like delivering a parcel he intended to be treated gently. *Everybody's running from something, Felix. Doesn't mean they don't deserve a seat.*

He'd forgotten that. He'd spent three years remembering only the debts, the letters from creditors, the shame folded into every drawer of the house like mothballs. He'd built his father entirely out of ledger lines and forgotten the rest.

"Who are you running from?" Felix asked.

Jun's whole body changed. The defiance stayed, but underneath it something braced, the way a hull braces against a wave it can't dodge.

"Meridian," the kid said. "Indenture program. Fourteen-year-olds sign seven-year contracts, except nobody tells you that your guardian signs it for you and takes the signing bonus, and then you work it off in the shops and the bonus counts against your wages at a rate they invent whenever they feel like it. My uncle signed me. I did two years in the Calloway yards." The socket wrench turned over in those grease-blackened hands, once, twice. "Two more years of that and I'd have come out the other end owing them more than when I started. So. I ran."

"And stowed away on a Meridian-contracted survey expedition."

"Yeah, well." For the first time, Jun looked away. "I didn't read the whole manifest. I saw a boat going upriver, away from everything, and I got on. Then you boarded and I read the rest and I couldn't get off, because there's nothing between here and the headwaters except water and trees, and now—" The bravado finally cracked clean through, and what was underneath was sixteen years old and terrified. "Now I figure you're going to turn me in, and Meridian pays recovery bounties, and I'm sorry about the fuel line, I'll tighten the bolts, just please don't—"

"Nobody's turning you in."

Jun stopped. Stared.

"I said what I said," Felix said, and was surprised at how steady his own voice was, because inside he was anything but. He should turn the kid in. Every practical instinct said so—a stowaway was a liability, a discipline problem, a complication on an expedition that already had radio silence and a captain who flinched at his surname. Handing Jun over would buy goodwill with Odile Vann, and goodwill with Odile Vann might buy answers about his father, and answers about his father were the entire reason Felix was on this boat, drowning in debt, chasing a map marked in a dead man's handwriting.

Instead he heard himself say: "But Odile's going to find out. Probably tonight. She counts heads the way misers count coins. So we're going to go up there together, and you're going to tell her yourself, and I'm going to stand next to you while you do it. Understood?"

"Why would you stand next to me?"

Because somebody should have stood next to you two years ago, Felix thought, and didn't say, because it was too much, too soon, and because he barely understood it himself.

"Because it's harder to throw someone overboard when there's a witness," he said instead.

* * *

Odile Vann took the news the way she took everything—with a stillness that made the wheelhouse feel smaller.

She listened without interrupting, arms crossed, eyes moving between Felix and the kid dripping river water and engine grease onto her deck plates. Behind her, the rain had started again, a soft static against the glass, and the river beyond was black and getting blacker.

"A Meridian indent," she said at last. "Runaway."

"Yes," Felix said.

"On my boat. For five days. Eating my stores."

"I ate almost nothing," Jun offered. "I'm small."

"That's not the point."

"The point," Felix said carefully, "is what we do now. We're three days from anywhere. Turning back costs us four days and puts us off schedule before the weather closes. Putting Jun ashore isn't possible until the next settlement, and the next settlement is a Meridian supply depot, which means putting them ashore is handing them back."

"So we carry a fugitive deeper into territory we don't control, on a contract paid by the company hunting them." Odile's voice was dry as August. "You've thought this through."

"I've thought it through enough to know the alternatives are worse." Felix held her gaze. He was aware of Jun beside him, very still, the way small animals go still when the large ones negotiate over them. "Turn us around if you have to. But hear me first. This expedition is missing its advance team, the radio's dead, and we're heading into country that isn't on any current map. You need a mechanic more than you need a clear conscience. The kid kept our engine alive for five days without being asked. That's not a liability, Captain. That's the best hire you'll make this year, and they'll cost you nothing but a bunk."

"They," Odile repeated, with the faintest arch of an eyebrow.

"Jun," said Jun. "It's—doesn't matter. Jun's fine."

"Jun." Odile looked at the kid for a long moment, and Felix watched something move behind her eyes—something old and tired and, unexpectedly, not unkind. "You understand that if Meridian comes asking, I will deny knowing you with tremendous conviction."

"...Yes?"

"Good. You'll be convincing." She uncrossed her arms. "Bunk aft, portside, next to the spare parts locker. If you touch the engine without telling Mr. Sterling, you walk the plank, and yes, I know we don't have a plank, I'll improvise. Breakfast is at six. Don't be late."

Jun's mouth opened. Closed. Opened again. "That's—you're not turning me in?"

"I said I'd deny knowing you. Try to keep up." Odile turned back to the window, dismissing them both, but as Felix steered the kid toward the door, she added, without looking around: "Sterling. A word about your judgment later. Arguing forward for a stranger on my deck—that was either brave or stupid, and I haven't decided which."

"Often the same thing," Felix said, and got out before she could respond.

* * *

They couldn't sleep afterward, either of them, so they ended up in the engine hold anyway, because apparently that was where things happened now. Jun sat cross-legged on the deck plates with the inspection lamp pulled close, showing Felix the third cylinder—the hot one, the one nobody had noticed—and explaining, with the rapid-fire enthusiasm of someone who had just been given permission to exist, exactly how the fuel mixture was running rich.

"—so if I shim the injector a hair, just a hair, she'll run cooler and burn less, which matters, because your pilot's going to need every drop once we hit the narrows." Jun paused, wrench hovering. "Can I? Shim it? Or is that a surveyor-only job?"

Felix looked at the kid's hands—capable, careful, scarred in the specific ways of someone who'd learned machinery young and learned it the hard way—and thought about his father crouching in a freight yard, sharing bread.

"Show me how," he said. "Then we'll do it together."

Jun showed him. It took twenty minutes, most of which was Jun explaining torque tolerances with the gravity of a professor, and when the shim seated and the idle smoothed out by a fraction—audible only, Felix suspected, to the two of them and possibly the engine itself—Jun grinned.

It transformed the kid's whole face. For a second, the fear and the flintiness fell away and there was just a sixteen-year-old who had fixed something, and Felix felt that door in his chest swing another inch open, and thought: *well. Here we are, then.*

"Get some sleep," he said, standing. "Breakfast at six. Don't be late—she meant that part."

"Hey. Sterling?" Jun was looking up at him from the deck plates, lamplight catching the grease and the fading bruise. "Why'd you argue for me? You don't know me. You could've bought a lot of goodwill turning me in."

Felix thought about it honestly, because the kid deserved honesty.

"My father used to feed stowaways," he said. "I forgot that about him until tonight. Seems like the kind of thing worth remembering." He climbed two rungs, then paused. "And for what it's worth—whatever we find upriver, whoever that advance team was running from—you're crew now. Crew doesn't get handed back."

He was nearly at the top of the ladder when Jun's voice followed him, quieter, almost lost under the tick of the cooling engine:

"Sterling? The name on the manifest. Your dad wouldn't happen to be *Elias* Sterling, would he? The engineer? From the old line?"

Felix stopped.

The rain picked up against the hull, and somewhere far ahead, past the dark and the narrowing river, thunder rolled over hills that no official map bothered to name.

"Why," Felix said carefully, "do you ask?"
