## 5. Monsoon Warning

Amara Osei had spent eleven years reading water for a living and one afternoon losing that living in a single spectacular act of incompetence—or so the river board's hearing had decided—and if there was one thing she still trusted herself to do, it was to look at a sky and know what it intended.

What this sky intended was murder.

She stood at the wheel of the *Coriander*—a boat named, she suspected, by someone who had never tasted coriander or met this river—and watched the western horizon do something no honest sky should do in the dry weeks before monsoon. The clouds were building in a wall, green-black at the base, boiling upward like something being poured from an invisible jug. The air had gone thick and sweet, the way it did when the whole valley held its breath.

"Odile," she called. "Come look at this."

Odile Vann emerged from the chart room with the expression of a woman who had been hoping nobody would need her for at least another hour. She squinted westward for perhaps three seconds. "Weather."

"Weather." Amara laughed, but there was no humor in it, and Odile heard that. "That's not weather. That's the monsoon arriving early and angry. That front is going to be on top of us inside two days, maybe less. And after it comes the rest of it. The real season. Weeks of this, off and on, until the river climbs ten meters and forgets where its banks are."

"Your forecast said we had three weeks."

"My forecast said we had three weeks because I wrote it before I saw that." She pointed at the wall of cloud. "Forecasts are opinions. Clouds are testimony. I'm telling you: days, not weeks. If we're going to find your advance team, we find them now, and then we get downstream or we get high, because this boat—" she patted the rail affectionately, the way you'd pat a sick dog, "—this boat is not going to survive what's coming. Neither are we, probably."

Odile studied her for a long moment. Amara had learned to read people the way she read water, and what she read in Odile was a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. Days, not weeks. A missing team somewhere upriver. A pilot whose license had been revoked but whose eyes, apparently, still worked.

"Full ahead, then," Odile said. "And thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," Amara said, and pushed the throttle forward. "Thank me if we live through Tuesday."

* * *

Felix Sterling found her at dusk on the aft deck, where she'd gone to be alone with her bad feelings, which preferred company after all.

"You were right about the sky," he said, leaning on the rail beside her. "Jun says the barometer's dropped like a stone. Jun also says, quote, 'the needle looks scared,' which I didn't know instruments could do."

"Instruments can't. People can. Jun reads gauges the way I read clouds—the machine tells the truth even when the person attached to it doesn't want to hear it." Amara glanced sideways at him. Sterling. The name had gone through the expedition like a fishhook through a net, snagging on everyone. Odile flinched. The advance team's last transmission had cut off mid-word. And now the son of the man whose handwriting was all over the closed lines was standing at her rail looking like a man who'd inherited a debt and couldn't find the creditor.

"How bad?" Felix asked. "Honestly. You've been up this river longer than any of us."

"Honestly? This stretch narrows into the gorge in forty kilometers. In normal conditions it's a hard run—shoals, sweepers, current that wants to eat you. In rising water with rain coming down so hard you can't see the far bank?" She shook her head. "I've run gorges in weather like that exactly once, and I was younger, stupider, and licensed. Two out of three of those things have changed."

"But you can do it."

"I can try it. There's a difference, and the difference is usually measured in hull planks." She looked at him more closely. He had his father's jaw and none of his father's reputation, whatever that reputation was; around him the crew spoke in careful half-sentences, as though the name Sterling were a floorboard that might give way. "Why do you care? You could turn back. Contract says survey the lines, contract doesn't say drown doing it."

Felix was quiet for a while. Downriver, behind them, the last light caught the water and turned it to hammered tin.

"When I was a kid," he said finally, "my father told me that rivers don't hide things. People hide things. Rivers just wait." He shrugged, embarrassed. "I don't know why I'm telling you that. I guess—I came up here to settle accounts. And now there's a missing team, and a dead radio, and a sixteen-year-old hiding in my engine hold, and a leader who flinches at my surname. The accounts have gotten complicated. But somebody's up that river waiting for us, and I keep thinking about how long I'd want someone to keep coming if it were me."

Amara nodded slowly. It was, she thought, the most honest thing anyone had said on this boat since they'd cast off, including everything she herself had said, and she included her own weather warnings in that assessment.

"Well," she said. "If we're going to be idiots together, we should do it properly. First watch starts at midnight. Bring coffee. Real coffee, not whatever Jun brewed this morning—that wasn't coffee, that was a threat."

The ghost of a smile crossed Felix's face, the first she'd seen from him. "Aye, Pilot."

"And Sterling? Don't call me Pilot. My license lives in a drawer in Port Meridian under a stamp that says REVOKED in letters even Jun could read from the engine hold."

"What should I call you?"

"Amara works fine. It always has." She turned back to the blackening west. "It's the only thing about me the river board never managed to take away."

* * *

The storm arrived fourteen hours early, because storms, like debts, never respect the schedule you've built your hopes around.

It came at them out of the gorge at midday, a brown-green darkness rolling over the water with the wind running ahead of it like a warning shot. Amara had the wheel, Odile on the chart table, Felix and Jun hauling the deck cargo under tarps, and for six minutes everything was merely difficult—rain like gravel, visibility down to fifty meters, the current picking up as the first surge of floodwater came down from the high country.

Then the wind swung ninety degrees without notice, the way gorge winds do, and the *Coriander* broached.

Amara felt it happen in her hands before her eyes confirmed it—the wheel going light, the stern lifting, the whole boat deciding, with the cheerful treachery of all floating objects, that sideways was the new forward. "Hang on!" she shouted, and hauled the wheel hard over, feeding in throttle to bring the bow around, and for one long moment the *Coriander* surfed down the face of a wave that hadn't existed thirty seconds earlier—

—and slammed broadside into a submerged shoal that the rising water had made out of nothing.

The sound was terrible and intimate: a crack along the starboard side like a giant knuckle cracking, felt through the deck plates and up through boot soles and into teeth. The boat lurched, righted, wallowed. Water began finding its way in, because water always does; it is patient and it is thorough and it holds grudges.

"Bilge!" Amara roared. "Somebody get eyes on that seam!"

Jun was already gone, sliding down the engine hatch ladder with the total absence of self-preservation that Amara would later learn was simply part of the boy's operating system. Felix followed. From below came the sound of voices, then shouting, then—worse—the steady mechanical gulp of the bilge pump starting up and sounding, immediately, like it was losing an argument.

"She's cracked!" Jun's voice floated up, thin over the storm. "Starboard, frame twelve! Seam's opened maybe a hand's width! I can slow it but I can't stop it—not out here!"

"Can you slow it enough?" Odile demanded, appearing at Amara's shoulder, soaked through, hair plastered flat, looking for the first time since Port Meridian like a woman whose plan had developed a leak along with the boat.

"Enough for what?"

"Enough to reach the gorge mouth. There's a shingle bar above the rapids—we beached supply barges there, years ago. High ground behind it. If we can make that, we can patch her properly instead of watching her sink slowly in the middle of nowhere."

Amara did the arithmetic. Distance: eight kilometers. Current: rising. Visibility: a joke. Hull: compromised. Crew: one disgraced pilot, one engineer with secrets, one teenager with a wrench, one commander with a past.

Odds: honestly, better than she'd expected, given the inventory.

"Eight kilometers," she said. "Keep her light, keep her bow into the chop as much as she'll allow, and pray the shoal that bit us was the only surprise between here and there." She set the wheel and felt the boat answer—sluggish, listing two degrees to starboard, but answering. "Everybody listen! We're not passengers anymore! Felix, you're on the pump—if it falls behind, you bail with anything that holds water and you sing while you do it, loud, so we know you're alive! Jun stays on the engine and tells me the moment anything changes! Odile—you're my eyes. Get forward, call the water for me, because I can't see past my own nose out there!"

"And you?" Odile asked.

"I drive." Amara bared her teeth at the rain. "It's the only part of this job they couldn't take away from me either."

* * *

They ran the eight kilometers in fifty minutes that felt like fifty years.

Later, Amara would remember it in fragments, the way you remember any night that rearranges you: Odile lashed to the foredeck rail, shouting depths and shadows through cupped hands, her voice going hoarse by kilometer three; Felix below decks, bailing with a rice pot, singing—actually singing—some mournful ballad about a miner's daughter, off-key with tremendous commitment, because she'd told him to and because, she suspected, he needed something to do with his fear besides feel it; Jun's voice reporting from the engine hold at intervals, calm as a metronome—"RPM holding, temperature good, water's gaining but slow, keep her level, Amara, keep her level"—a sixteen-year-old calling an old convict-pilot by her first name like they'd crewed together for years.

At kilometer six the river threw its best punch: a sweeper, a whole fallen tree pivoting in the current like a gate, invisible until the lightning showed it twenty meters off the bow. "Hard to port!" Odile screamed, and Amara put the wheel over and the *Coriander*, heavy with water and graceless as a drunk aunt, slid past the trunk close enough that Amara could have reached out and touched bark, and then it was behind them and nobody said anything for a full minute because there was nothing to say that the river wouldn't overhear.

At kilometer eight, the shingle bar appeared out of the rain like a promise kept by accident—a long pale shoulder of gravel backed by dark trees and, behind those, high ground. Amara brought the boat in on the back side of the bar where the current slackened, ran her bow up onto the stones with a grinding crunch that everyone aboard felt in their skeletons, and killed the engine.

Silence, except for rain. Wonderful, ordinary rain.

The four of them stood on the tilting deck and looked at each other: soaked, shaking, absurd. Jun surfaced from the engine hold wearing a grin split wide across a grease-streaked face. Felix climbed up from below carrying a rice pot half-full of river water, still humming. Odile sat down abruptly on the cargo hatch and laughed—a short, startled sound, as if it had escaped custody.

"We made it," Jun said.

"We beached," Amara corrected. "Making it comes next. That seam needs tin, rivets, and a miracle, in ascending order of availability." She looked around at the shingle, the trees, the wall of cloud still unloading itself on the gorge upstream. "But tonight, we float. Tomorrow, we work."

Felix set the rice pot down and shook river water out of his cuffs. "You know," he said, "when I signed the contract, the job description said 'surveying.'"

"The job description lies," Amara said. "Everything does. The river doesn't, though. The river only ever asks one question." She nodded at the dark water sliding past the hull, patient and thorough and keeping its grudge. "It asks whether you'll pull. Tonight, everybody pulled."

* * *

They camped that night on the high ground above the bar, under a tarp strung between trees, eating cold rice and canned peaches while the storm worked itself hoarse upstream. Jun fell asleep mid-sentence, sitting upright, a wrench still in one hand, and Felix eased the boy down onto a folded tarp with the unhurried tenderness of someone who had done it before, or wished he had.

Amara took the first watch, because old habits drown last. She sat with her back against a tree and watched the river rise, centimeter by centimeter, licking higher up the shingle bar, and she thought about the word *crew*, which she hadn't used in years without irony.

Across the fire's coals, Odile stirred and looked at her.

"You knew," Odile said quietly. "Before anyone. You looked at the sky and you knew."

"I told you. Clouds testify."

"Where did you learn that?"

Amara fed a stick to the coals and watched it catch. Somewhere upstream, deep in the gorge, thunder rolled like a door closing.

"From the same place I learned everything worth knowing," she said. "From a river that doesn't lie. And tomorrow, Captain Vann, you're going to tell me what's really up this river—because I just risked my neck on your missing team, and my neck has started asking questions."

Odile held her gaze for a long moment. Above them, the rain drummed the tarp like fingers counting down.

"All right," Odile said at last. "Tomorrow. When we're moving. Because once I start talking about Junction Nine, Amara, you're going to want to turn this boat around—and I'm going to need you to decide not to."

Amara looked out at the black water, rising and rising, already halfway up the bar.

"Junction Nine," she repeated. "Never heard of it."

"No," Odile said. "Nobody has. That was rather the point."
