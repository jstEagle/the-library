## 6. What Odile Knows

Felix found her at the stern, of course. Odile Vann had a way of standing at the back of things—of boats, of conversations, of her own life—as if she were perpetually checking whether anyone was following.

He'd rehearsed the confrontation three times in his head while pretending to check the fuel lines with Jun, who had seen through the pretense immediately and offered, helpfully, "You're going to yell at her, aren't you? Want me to hold your coat?"

"I'm not going to yell."

"Shame. I'd hold your coat anyway."

But now, standing behind her with the rain just beginning to speckle the deck plates, all that rehearsal evaporated. She was watching the water the way a person watches a door they've locked but don't trust.

"Odile."

She didn't turn. "Felix." His name in her mouth sounded like something she'd been chewing on for days.

"The radio died mid-sentence three days ago. The advance team's last transmission cut off between one word and the next. And you ordered us onward anyway." He stepped beside her, close enough that she had to acknowledge him or pretend he wasn't there. "I've been telling myself you're a professional making a professional call. But professionals turn around when their people go silent. So I'm going to ask you plainly, and I'd take it as a kindness if you answered plainly for once in this expedition."

The river narrowed ahead, the banks closing like a book being shut. Thunder muttered somewhere upstream, low and conversational, as if the storm were discussing them in another room.

"Ask," she said.

"What is Junction Nine?"

Her jaw tightened. He watched it happen—a small mechanical failure in her composure, visible only because he'd spent the last week learning to read her the way Amara read water.

"That name isn't on any map," she said carefully.

"No. It isn't. I checked every survey document my father left, every official registry, every archive I could afford to access, which, given my financial situation, means the ones that are free." He kept his voice level. "And yet the advance team went looking for it. You sent them looking for it. And when they stopped transmitting, you steered us straight toward wherever they'd gone instead of away from it. So either you're the worst expedition leader on this river, or Junction Nine matters very much to you. Which is it?"

For a long moment the only sound was the engine's laboring heartbeat below them and the first fat drops hitting the canvas awning.

"You sound like him," Odile said at last.

Felix felt the words land somewhere under his ribs. "Like who."

She turned then, finally, and looked at him properly—the first time since the dock at Port Calloway, where she'd flinched at his surname and he'd been too desperate for the contract to ask why. Her face in the grey light was older than her file said she should be, carved by weather and by whatever else.

"Elias Sterling," she said. "Your father. I knew him."

* * *

The wheelhouse smelled of diesel and old coffee, and Amara stood in the doorway with her arms crossed, having invited herself the moment she heard the word *father*. Jun appeared thirty seconds later, dripping, holding two mugs of tea nobody had asked for and distributing them like a peace offering. Nobody drank theirs. They sat on the chart table, steaming, forgotten.

"Start at the beginning," Felix said. "Not the version you gave the contract office. The real one."

Odile sat down slowly, like a woman lowering herself into cold water. "Twenty-six years ago, I was a junior surveyor for the Meridian Mining Consortium. Fresh out of the academy, full of myself, drawing lines on maps that would decide which valleys got roads and which got shafts." She wrapped her hands around nothing, an old habit of holding a mug she no longer carried. "Meridian wanted the upper gorge. There were mineral seams in those hills worth more than anything else in the territory. All we needed was rail access, and all rail access needed was a survey confirming the line was stable."

"And my father?"

"Was the chief engineer on the closure project. The *opposite* project." She almost smiled. "You have to understand what your father was, Felix. Everyone in this territory knew Elias Sterling as the man who built half the mountain branch lines. What they didn't know was that in his last years, he un-built them. Or tried to."

Jun leaned forward. "Un-built? Like, demolition?"

"Like erasure." Odile's eyes moved to the rain-streaked window. "There was a junction station upriver. A real place, with a signal tower, a water stop, a crew of forty-some people running freight and maintenance through the gorge. On paper it was called Junction Nine. Your father helped design it when he was young. And when Meridian came sniffing twenty years later, he did everything in his considerable power to make sure they never found it again."

Amara shifted in the doorway. "You can't erase a station. It's a hole in the ground with rails in it."

"You can if you're Elias Sterling." Odile counted off on her fingers, and Felix recognized the gesture with a strange lurch—it was his own gesture, the one he made when explaining load tolerances, inherited without ever knowing its source. "He pulled the registry filings. He bought out the land records through three shell companies. He redrew the regional surveys himself—he was one of the few men whose maps the government accepted without field verification, because his reputation was that good. And then he used that reputation to lie. He filed reports declaring the gorge geologically unstable. Landslide risk. Flood plain. He wrote it so convincingly that Meridian's own engineers never questioned it. The line was officially closed, the station struck from the rolls, and the valley dropped off every map that mattered."

The rain thickened against the roof. Somewhere aft, a loose tarp slapped its rhythm against the cargo crates.

"So the debts," Felix said slowly. Pieces were rearranging themselves in his chest, painfully, like furniture in a house being flooded. "The debts I've spent four years drowning in. The creditors. The liens on the house. Where did the money go?"

"Where do you think?" Odile said gently. "Shell companies cost money. Bribed clerks cost money. Buying silence from forty families so they could stay in a valley that officially didn't exist—that costs money every single year, for decades. Your father financed the disappearance of an entire town out of his own pocket, and when his pocket ran out, he borrowed. And borrowed. And borrowed."

Felix stared at his hands. They were his father's hands too, big-knuckled, scarred at the thumb where a wrench had slipped twenty years before Felix was born. Four years he had hated those debts. Had hated the man who left them, the man who'd seemed so careful with everything except the one thing that mattered—his family's solvency. He had built his whole understanding of his father out of that failure: brilliant engineer, careless man.

"He wasn't careless," Felix said aloud, testing the shape of it.

"He was the most deliberate man I ever met," Odile said. "Deliberate right up until the end. Because Meridian doesn't give up, Felix. Not ever. They waited him out. Ten years after the closure, their new surveyors started asking questions about the gorge. Fifteen years, they started buying up old claims. And your father started spending faster—lawyers, injunctions, a second round of falsified geological reports. He was fighting a war on paper with a pencil, and pencils are cheap but wars aren't."

"How did he die?" The question came out smaller than Felix intended.

Odile was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed. "Officially? Heart failure, alone in a rented room in the capital." She met his eyes. "Unofficially—he was up here. In the gorge. Three weeks before they found his body downstream. He came up to finish something. A final piece of work on the line itself. Whatever it was, he didn't finish it. And Meridian has been trying to find Junction Nine ever since, because the mineral seams are still there, and the only thing standing between them and the richest mining concession in the territory is the fact that nobody can prove the valley exists."

The wheelhouse felt suddenly small. Jun had gone very still, both hands wrapped around the untouched tea. Amara hadn't blinked in what felt like a minute.

"And you," Felix said, and his voice came out harder than he meant it to. "You worked for Meridian. You drew the maps they wanted drawn. And now you're leading an expedition up the closed line with a crew of five and a leaking boat, following the trail of a missing advance team—and you hired *me*, the son of the man who spent his life keeping people like you out of this valley. So explain that to me, Odile. Explain why I shouldn't turn this boat around right now."

"I defected," she said simply. "Nineteen years ago."

"People say that."

"I know people say that." She didn't flinch. "I was the surveyor who found the gap in your father's records. Did you know that? I'm the one who noticed that a forty-kilometer stretch of the old mountain branch had been surveyed by Elias Sterling personally, twice, with contradictory results—which no honest engineer does. I brought it to my superiors and they gave me a promotion and told me to keep digging. And I dug, and the deeper I dug the more I understood what your father had actually built—not a lie, exactly. A wall. A wall made of paperwork, with forty families living safely behind it." She exhaled. "I resigned. Meridian blacklisted me across half the industry. I've spent nineteen years piloting rivers and running small expeditions and waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For someone to fund an expedition into the gorge so I could find out if the town was still there. And then eight months ago, a funding offer landed on my desk—from a shell company I recognized, because I'd helped build its predecessor when I was twenty-three. Meridian, wearing a false moustache. They wanted the same thing I wanted: Junction Nine. So I took the money." She held up a hand before Felix could speak. "Because the alternative was letting them send their own people, with their own equipment and their own security detail and no one inside the expedition who knew what the valley actually was. I took their money to get here first, or alongside them, and to control what happens when we arrive. That's the truth. All of it. You can hate me for the method. Plenty of people do. I hate me for the method."

Silence filled the wheelhouse like water filling a hull.

It was Jun who broke it, in the blunt way of sixteen-year-olds everywhere. "So the advance team. The ones who went quiet. Were they yours or Meridian's?"

Odile closed her eyes briefly. "Two were mine. Rennick and Okafor—good people, loyal to me, told only what they needed to know. The third—" She hesitated, and that hesitation rang alarm bells through Felix's whole body. "The third was assigned by the funder. A man named Corliss. Quiet, competent, kept to himself. When the radio died, Rennick's last transmission mentioned they'd found evidence of the old station and were pushing up the tributary on foot. Corliss was with them."

"And now the radio's dead," Amara said flatly, "and you don't know which of them cut it."

"No," Odile admitted. "I don't."

* * *

Felix walked out into the rain because the wheelhouse had become too small to contain what he was feeling, and the deck, at least, was honestly wet.

His father's map was in his breast pocket, as it had been every day since Port Calloway—the old survey sheet, soft as cloth from handling, marked in Elias Sterling's cramped, precise hand. Felix had assumed the annotations were engineering notes. Now he stood under the awning and looked at them again and saw them differently. *Sound bridge, do not trust.* *Water stop—keep.* *Bao.* A name, written next to a coordinate in the middle of what the official record called uninhabited floodplain. His father had known someone up here. Had trusted someone enough to write their name on the only copy of a map that wasn't supposed to exist.

"You look like a man doing arithmetic," Amara said, materializing beside him with the uncanny quietness of a born pilot. She handed him his tea, still faintly warm. Jun's work.

"I'm trying to add up my father," Felix said. "It doesn't balance. Every year of my life I thought I knew what he was, and it turns out I had the ledger upside down. The debts weren't weakness. They were—" He stopped, because the word caught in his throat.

"Say it," Amara said.

"A promise. He mortgaged our whole life to keep a promise to strangers I've never met." Felix laughed, and it came out wrong, half grief. "I spent four years resenting a man for being irresponsible, and the whole time he was the most responsible man on this river. I just couldn't see what he was responsible *to*."

Amara sipped her tea and watched the rain stitch holes in the river. "My father was a harbor pilot," she said, after a while. "Everyone in the port thought he was a drunk, because he lost his license and never fought to get it back. Took me ten years to learn he'd taken the fall for a friend's error. Ten years thinking the worst of him because the truth was buried under a story everyone preferred." She shrugged. "People are ledgers with the important entries written in invisible ink. You find out what someone really paid for, eventually. Usually too late to thank them."

"Too late," Felix agreed. Then, because the thought arrived fully formed and refused to leave: "Unless it isn't. Odile said he came up here at the end. To finish something. Some piece of work on the line itself." He touched the pocket where the map lay. "Whatever he came to finish—maybe it's still unfinished. Maybe that's not just an inheritance of debt. Maybe it's an inheritance of *work*."

Ahead, the gorge walls rose on either side of the river like the covers of a closing book, and the light beneath the storm clouds had gone the color of tarnished brass. Amara followed his gaze and her expression changed—the easy warmth draining into something older and sharper, the face she wore when reading water.

"That sky's wrong," she said quietly. "That's not afternoon rain. That's a monsoon squall line coming down the gorge at us. We've got maybe an hour, less if the wind backs around." She was already moving toward the wheelhouse, raising her voice. "Captain! I need you on the helm and I need it *now*—and somebody tell the kid to lash down everything that isn't welded!"

Felix stayed under the awning one moment longer, his hand over the map, over his father's handwriting, over the name *Bao* written beside a blank space on the world.

Then the first gust hit, hard enough to heel the boat, and he ran for the bowline.

* * *

They took the squall broadside at the worst possible moment—at the mouth of the narrows, where the gorge pinched the river into brown, boiling rapids and there was nowhere to run except through. Amara flew the boat like a woman arguing with it, calling depths from memory and instinct while Odile worked the throttle and Felix and Jun hauled the cargo tarps tight and bailed what the river threw aboard.

Lightning split the gorge white. In that frozen instant Felix saw the far bank clearly—saw, wedged in the rocks above the flood line, a rusted iron signal mast, leaning, its lamp housing long dark.

A signal mast. Exactly where his father's map showed a coordinate and a name.

Then the wave hit, the hull cracked somewhere below the waterline with a sound like a knuckle popping, and there was no more time for seeing anything at all.
