## 7. Leaking Toward the Dark

The rain did not fall so much as arrive, all at once, like a debt collector who had finally found the right address.

Felix stood in the wheelhouse doorway and watched the river disappear. One moment there was water, banks, the green shoulders of the gorge; the next there was only gray, a moving wall of it, and the sound—God, the sound—like the sky had torn open and couldn't decide whether to be thunder or applause.

"She's coming down the narrows," Amara shouted over the din, both hands on the wheel, her braid plastered to her neck. "Felix! Get below and check the bilge. If that seam I don't like has decided it doesn't like us back, I want to know before we're swimming."

He wanted to argue that he was an engineer, not a bilge boy, but the truth was he was both, and had been since he was twelve years old, kneeling beside his father in a puddled rail yard while the old man taught him to listen to machines the way other people listened to weather. *Everything that's breaking tells you first,* his father used to say. *It's the things that break quiet you have to fear.*

The engine hold was dim and hot and smelled of diesel and wet iron, and it was full of Jun.

The boy—sixteen going on forty, all elbows and grease—had stripped off his shirt and wedged himself into the bilge space with a lantern clenched in his teeth, and the light threw his shadow huge and wavering across the hull plates. Water was coming in. Not gushing. Not yet. But seeping up through the cracked seam in a thin, insistent silver thread, the way water always did, patient as arithmetic.

"How bad?" Felix shouted.

Jun took the lantern out of his mouth. "Bad enough I stopped singing."

"You were singing?"

"Motivationally." Jun pointed at the seam. "It's opened maybe two inches. That storm knocked us around like a dice cup. I can patch it, but I need tin, and I need this boat to hold *still*, which—" he gestured vaguely upward, at the rain, at the rapids Amara was about to thread blind, "—seems unlikely."

Felix crouched at the edge of the bilge and put his palm flat against the hull, the way his father had taught him. He listened. The vibration was wrong—there was a flutter in it, a stutter, like a heartbeat with a skipped beat.

"Jun. When did you last check the engine mounts?"

"Hour ago. Why?"

"Because the starboard one's working loose, and if it goes while you're down here with your head next to the flywheel, the patch job won't matter, because there won't be enough of you left to appreciate it."

Jun looked at him. In the lantern light his face did something complicated—fear, mostly, but underneath it a flicker of something that looked almost like relief. The look of a kid who had been carrying a boat alone and had just been told, by a stranger with tired eyes, that he didn't have to anymore.

"Okay," Jun said. "Okay. You do the mount, I do the seam. Deal?"

"Deal. And Jun?"

"Yeah?"

"Sing if you want. It helps me too."

The boy grinned, wet and crooked, and went back to work.

* * *

Above them, Amara Osei was having a conversation with a river that was not listening.

She had run the Sable Narrows twice in her life, both times in daylight, both times with a spotter on the bow calling the water, and both times she had been younger and the river had been in a kinder mood. Now the rain had erased every landmark she knew, the channel markers had gone wherever lost things go, and the water beneath her hull was doing something she could *hear* but not see—a low, grinding mutter over rocks she couldn't place.

"Talk to me," she muttered, to the river, to the rain, to whatever patron saint looked after disgraced pilots. "Just one rock. Give me one rock."

The river gave her three.

The first she felt before she heard it—a long, smooth slide in the hull's voice, the boat's whole body leaning as the current grabbed her stern and swung it. She corrected with the wheel, gentle, gentle, the way you'd steady a spooked animal. The second rock announced itself with a bang from the port side that she felt in her back teeth, and from below came a muffled *yelp* and a burst of very creative profanity in two languages, which meant Jun was still alive and still patching.

The third rock she never saw at all. She only heard Felix bellow from the engine hold—"HARD STARBOARD, NOW"—and she threw the wheel over without thinking, without asking, on nothing but the voice of a man she'd known four days, and the boat heeled, and something scraped beneath them with a sound like a giant filing its teeth, and then they were through, into a wider reach, the water suddenly calmer, the rain still hammering but the river, for the moment, done with its joke.

Amara realized she was shaking.

She realized, also, that she was laughing. Just a little. The kind of laugh that comes out sideways when the alternative is screaming.

"Felix," she called down the hatch. "How did you know?"

His head appeared in the hatchway, streaming water, streaked with grease, wearing the expression of a man who has recently been very frightened and is pretending otherwise. "The vibration changed. When the current piles up against a shoal, the hull hums at a different pitch. My father taught me. He said a rail man can hear a broken rail from a moving train if he shuts up and pays attention."

"Your father," Amara said, "sounds like he was exhausting to be related to."

"He was," Felix said, and there was something raw and warm in it, and then his face changed. "Amara. The supplies."

She turned. The lashing on the cargo deck had parted—one of the lines, the old one, the one she'd said they should replace and then hadn't—and the river had already taken its tax. Crates were going over the side in ones and twos, bobbing away into the gray like little wooden coffins, and the rain made it impossible to see where they went, or to go after them.

"Rice," Felix said grimly, counting. "The fuel cans. The medical kit. Half the rope."

"The medical kit," Amara repeated, flat.

"Odile had it lashed wrong," Jun said, appearing at the hatch with a sheet of scavenged tin under one arm and a fresh cut on his forearm. "I told her. I told her the cleat was pulled half out of the deck and she said—" he did a passable impression of Odile's clipped, precise voice, "—'It will hold until we make the junction.'"

Amara closed her eyes. Opened them. The rain kept falling, indifferent, enormous.

"Well," she said. "It held until we made the narrows. Which is, technically, what she said."

Nobody laughed. Then Jun did—a short, shocked bark—and then Felix, and then, helplessly, Amara herself, the three of them standing in the rain on a crippled boat with half their supplies bound for the bottom of the gorge, laughing like idiots, because the alternative was not laughing, and the body will choose laughter sometimes just to keep the engine turning over.

"Right," Amara said, wiping her face, which needed no wiping, being already soaked. "Jun. How's the patch?"

"Sealed. Ugly, but sealed. It'll hold if we don't hit anything else."

"And the odds of not hitting anything else?"

Jun considered the river, the rain, the general state of the universe. "I'm a mechanic, not a fortune-teller."

"Then we'll take that as a maybe. Felix—come up. We need to re-lash what's left, and I want your ears on this hull for the next reach. Jun, get that arm wrapped before it goes septic. There's antiseptic in—" She stopped. The medical kit was currently negotiating rapids somewhere downstream. "There's antiseptic in the galley. First-aid tin. Small. Red. If it's gone too, we'll use whiskey and prayer."

"Whiskey first," said Jun.

"Prayer first," said Felix.

"Whiskey *and* prayer," Amara ruled. "Move."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the rain worked with them, or against them—it was hard to tell the difference, and after a while it didn't matter. Felix re-lashed the remaining cargo with new knots, the kind his father had drilled into him, knots that got tighter the harder you pulled. Jun wrapped his arm in a strip of shirt and went back below to check his patch every twenty minutes, emerging each time with a report delivered with mock formality—"The seam remains ugly but obedient"—as though he were a ship's officer filing dispatches. Amara ran the boat through reach after reach of drowned country, half by memory, half by the sound of the water, and once, memorably, by smell.

"Rocks smell different," she explained, when Felix gave her a look. "Water breaking on stone. It's got a bite to it. Smooth water smells green. Don't ask me how. I've been doing this since I was smaller than Jun and I couldn't tell you how I know my own name either."

By dusk the rain had thinned from a wall to a curtain, and by full dark it had settled into a steady, businesslike drizzle, as though the storm had finished shouting and now intended merely to sulk. Amara found a slack eddy behind a bluff and tied off to a deadfall tree, and the three of them sat in the wheelhouse eating cold rice out of a shared pot, because the cooking fuel had gone overboard along with everything else.

It was, Felix thought, the worst meal of his life, and he was strangely, unreasonably content.

Jun had fallen asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, his head tipped against the wheelhouse frame, one hand still loosely holding his spoon. His bandaged arm was cradled against his chest. He looked, asleep, about twelve years old, and Felix felt something in his chest that he didn't have a word for—something protective and fierce and slightly terrified, the way he imagined it must feel to hold something breakable and know you'd fight the whole river for it.

"He's a good kid," Amara said quietly, watching him too.

"He's a stowaway with an indenture contract on his head."

"He's a stowaway who patched our hull with a tin sheet and a lantern in his teeth while rocks tried to kill us. That's not a stowaway anymore. That's crew." She said it simply, without ceremony, the way you'd state the river's direction, and Felix understood that as far as Amara was concerned the matter was closed.

"He's sixteen," Felix said. "He should be in school. He should be—" He stopped. He'd been about to say *safe*, and the word caught in his throat, because none of them were safe, and hadn't been for a long time, and pretending otherwise would be an insult to the kid asleep three feet away who had run from the only life available to him.

"He should be a lot of things," Amara said. "So should I. So, I'd wager, should you." She nodded at the compass on the lanyard around his neck, the brass case catching the lamplight. "That was his, wasn't it. Your father's."

Felix's hand closed around it. The brass was warm from his skin. "He carried it on every survey he ever ran. He said a map tells you where things are. The compass tells you where *you* are. He said the second one mattered more."

"And which are you using right now?"

Felix looked at her. "What do you mean?"

"I mean," Amara said, turning her cup of cold rice in her hands, "that for four days you've been a man following a map. Debts, contract, survey points, the missing team, your father's name. All lines on paper. And today you stood in a bilge with a stranger and sang sea shanties—" Felix opened his mouth. "Don't deny it. Jun told me. You sang *badly*—" "—and when the boat needed saving, you didn't check the map. You listened to the hull. You told me to turn the wheel on nothing but a feeling and your father's voice in your head." She looked at him, and her eyes in the lamplight were tired and sharp and kind. "That's not a man following a map, Felix. That's a man finding out where he is."

Felix didn't answer. He didn't have one. Outside, the river murmured past the hull, and the rain ticked on the wheelhouse roof, and Jun slept on with his spoon in his fist like a man who'd fallen asleep guarding something.

* * *

It was near midnight when Odile came up from below.

Felix was on watch, wrapped in a tarp, and he heard her before he saw her—her footsteps on the deck had a particular precision, like punctuation. She stood at the wheelhouse door for a moment, looking out at the dark water, and then at him, and then at the sleeping shapes of Amara and Jun, and something crossed her face that Felix had never seen there before. It took him a moment to recognize it, because it was so ordinary, so human, against the armor she wore.

It was guilt.

"The cargo manifest," she said quietly. "Before the storm. I lashed the medical kit myself. On the bad cleat. Jun told me, and I—" She stopped. Started again. "I told him it would hold. I was wrong. I'm not often wrong, Mr. Sterling, and I find I like it less than I expected."

"Jun's fine. The arm's shallow."

"Jun is sixteen and he did a man's work today, and I treated him like ballast." Odile's jaw tightened. "I want you to know that I noticed. What he did. What all of you did." She looked out at the river. The rain had stopped entirely now, and the clouds were tearing open in the east, and through the gap Felix could see stars—cold, indifferent, beautiful.

"Odile," Felix said. "Three days ago the advance team's radio went dead mid-word. You ordered us onward anyway. You knew Junction Nine was on no map, and you knew my father's name, and you flinched the first time you heard mine. I've been too tired and too wet to push it. I'm not too tired anymore."

Odile was silent for a long moment. The river murmured. Somewhere in the hills, water fell from a height, endless, patient.

"No," she said at last. "I don't suppose you are." She turned to face him fully, and in the starlight her face looked older, and stripped, and braced. "The advance team wasn't surveying the closed lines, Felix. They were looking for Junction Nine. A station that was erased from every official map forty years ago. And the reason I flinched at your name—" her voice dropped, and steadied, the way a voice does when someone finally decides to tell the truth, "—is because I knew your father. And I know exactly how he died."

The river ran on in the dark, carrying its secrets toward the sea.

"Start at the beginning," Felix said.

Odile took a breath. "It begins with a station that doesn't exist," she said, "and a man who spent his last years making sure it stayed that way."

Above them, through the tearing clouds, the stars came out one by one, like witnesses.
