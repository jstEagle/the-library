## 8. The Drowned Station

The river stopped pretending to be a river somewhere past the third bend.

It became something narrower and more opinionated—a green corridor of water threading between walls of stone and rain-forest so dense that Felix half expected the banks to lean in and shake hands overhead. Amara stood at the wheel with her weight on her back foot, the way she'd stood for six hours straight, reading water the way other people read letters from home: quickly, hungrily, with occasional wincing.

"There," she said.

Felix followed her chin. Ahead, where the gorge widened into a drowned bowl of a valley, the mist parted just enough to show him the tops of things that had no business being tops of anything. A signal gantry, rusted orange, its lattice arms folded like a sleeping bird. The peaked roof of a water tower, its tank long since gone, standing over the flood like the ribcage of some enormous patient animal. And beneath all of it—beneath the brown-green water that lay flat and glassy as poured tea—the shapes of buildings.

Junction Nine was underwater to the second story.

Felix set down the tin cup of coffee Jun had pressed on him an hour ago—it was terrible, boiled twice, tasting faintly of engine grease—and gripped the rail instead. His father's map, unfolded across his knee despite the damp, showed this place as a careful cluster of inked rectangles and one confident star. On the official survey sheets back in the city, this entire valley was blank space. Not marked *uninhabited*. Not marked *flooded*. Just blank, the way a page is blank when someone has taken great pains to make it so.

"It's beautiful," Jun said, and then, catching themselves, "in a haunted way."

"Everything's haunted if you look at it long enough," Amara said. "Question is whether it's *occupied*."

She cut the engine to half, and the sudden quiet let in all the sounds they'd been outrunning for days: water dripping off leaves in fat deliberate drops, the creak of the hull patch Jun had installed—which held, mostly, weeping only a little at one corner like a child pretending not to cry—and underneath it all, a silence with texture to it. A silence that felt less like absence and more like listening.

Odile came forward from the stern, binoculars up before she'd finished walking. She'd barely slept since the radio died; it showed in the gray under her eyes and in the way her orders had gotten shorter, each word carrying its own small suitcase.

"Signal house is intact," she said. "Turntable too, or what's left of it. Waterline's four meters above design flood stage." She lowered the binoculars and looked at Felix, and there was something in her face he couldn't file—guilt wearing professionalism as a coat two sizes too big. "Your father's notes put the station yard above the high-water mark. The town itself wasn't supposed to flood."

"Notes?" Felix said. "You said you'd never seen his work."

"I said I hadn't seen his *maps*." Odile turned back to the glass. "Different thing."

Before Felix could decide which end of that sentence to pull, the boat drifted close enough that details resolved out of the murk. A street lamp, still standing, its glass fogged green with algae. A rowboat tied to what had once been a second-floor balcony, now serving as a dock. Laundry—actual laundry, shirts and trousers, faded but mended—hung on a line strung between the lamp post and the balcony rail.

Jun saw it first. Or rather, Jun said it first, which amounted to the same thing.

"Somebody's washing clothes."

"Could've been hung years ago," Odile said, but her voice didn't believe itself.

"In the rain? Every day?" Jun squinted. "Those are dry. Well—dry-ish. That shirt moved."

Nobody answered. The boat slid between the shoulders of the drowned street, and Felix felt the strange vertigo of traveling through a place built for feet and finding it built for keels. They passed over what must have been the main road; below them, dim through the tea-colored water, he could make out the pale seam of tram rails running straight as a ruled line into the dark. His father's handwriting floated up in his memory unbidden: *Junction Nine — yard capacity 40 cars — see note re: eastern approach.* A note Felix had never found. A note, he was beginning to suspect, that somebody had made very sure he'd never find.

Amara brought them alongside the balcony-dock with a delicacy that seemed almost apologetic, and Odile went over the rail first, boots on wet planks, pistol loose in its holster in a way that managed to be both casual and not casual at all. Felix followed. The wood groaned but held. Up close, the mended laundry told its own story—patches on patches, thread of three different colors, a child's shirt no bigger than a hand towel hanging beside the adult clothes.

"Kids," Jun said softly. There was a whole complicated weather system moving across their face—longing, alarm, and something that might have been envy.

"Or very small adults," Amara offered, climbing up last. "I've met some."

They fanned out along the balcony into what had been a hotel corridor, doors open onto rooms full of river light and silt. In one, a bed frame cradled a garden of moss. In another, someone had chalked a hopscotch grid on the floorboards, the numbers worn soft by actual use. Felix knelt and touched the chalk dust. It came away on his finger fresh.

"Days old," he said. "Maybe hours."

"Then where—" Odile started.

The kettle whistled.

It came from somewhere below and to the left, thin and domestic and utterly impossible, drifting up through the flooded building the way sound travels through water. Four people froze on a ruined hotel balcony in the middle of a blank space on every map, and a kettle whistled at them.

"That," Amara said finally, "is either the friendliest ambush in history or the worst-run haunting."

They found the source by following the smell of woodsmoke down through the building's bones—down staircases that ended in water, along corridors where the flood had turned windows into aquariums, fish drifting past doorframes with the bored air of commuters. At the building's heart, where a courtyard had been, someone had built a platform above the waterline and roofed it with salvaged tin, and around a blackened stove sat the evidence of a life: crates of vegetables growing in floating planters, nets drying, a wall of tools hung with the loving precision of a person who expected to need every single one.

The kettle's owner was gone. Three bowls on the table, still steaming. A fourth place laid and empty, waiting.

"They heard us coming," Odile said flatly. "And they left us lunch."

"That's either hospitality or a trap with excellent manners," Amara said. She picked up a bowl, sniffed it, and—over Odile's objection—tasted it. Her eyebrows performed a small revolution. "Fish stew. Good fish stew. If this is a trap, I want my last words to be a compliment."

"Don't eat—"

"Too late. Also there's bread."

Felix didn't eat. He walked the platform's edge instead, looking out over the drowned town, and the longer he looked the more he saw. A cable strung between rooftops, sagging but taut enough to carry something. A wind vane on the church spire—not decorative; wired. A garden raft moored where a market square had been, rows of green riding the floodwater like patient boats. This wasn't a ruin. It was a town that had looked at catastrophe, shrugged, and renegotiated the terms.

His father had known. His father had drawn the star on the map, written the note about the eastern approach, helped close the line—and all these years Felix had imagined abandonment, decay, maybe skeletons. Instead there was laundry and hopscotch and a kettle whistling for whoever had stepped out ten minutes ahead of strangers.

"You knew," Felix said quietly, without turning around. He heard Odile stop behind him. "When you hired me. When you flinched at my name on the dock. You knew this place was alive."

The silence stretched long enough that Jun, hovering near the stove, actually paused mid-reach for the bread.

"I knew there were stories," Odile said at last. "Stories aren't coordinates. I needed your father's maps because they're the only ones that show the approaches through the gorge, and I needed *you* because—" She stopped herself, jaw working. "Because I owed him. That's the short version. The long version needs dry socks and probably liquor."

"Save it," Felix said. Not cruelly—or he hoped not cruelly—but firmly, the way you set down something heavy before it falls on your foot. "Right now I want to know who's feeding the stove."

As if summoned—as if, Felix would think later, the whole valley ran on dramatic timing—the lamplight appeared.

It came first as a smudge of gold high on the northern slope, where the forest climbed steep and black above the flood line. Then a second light, lower, moving with purpose. Then a third, and Felix understood with a lurch that what he'd taken for stars caught in the trees were windows—shutters opening, lamps lit, a whole hillside waking up and turning its attention downhill toward four strangers standing in a dead woman's kitchen.

"Amara," Odile said, very calmly, "how many guns are on the boat?"

"Two mine, one yours, and whatever Jun's got hidden in that tool bag, which don't think I haven't noticed."

"It's a wrench," Jun said, with the specific dignity of someone whose wrench is absolutely also a weapon.

"Company," Amara murmured, nodding uphill. "Lots of it. Moving quiet, though. If they wanted us dead we'd already be dead—we've been clanking around their kitchen for twenty minutes."

She was right, and the thought settled over Felix strangely. Whoever lived here had watched them arrive, weighed them, and chosen soup over gunfire. That was either tremendous confidence or tremendous need, and he wasn't sure which frightened him more.

The lights descended. Through the trees came figures—maybe a dozen, moving with the easy footing of people who knew every root and rock, most of them carrying lamps, several of them carrying long guns held in the relaxed-but-ready way of folk who'd done this before. At their head walked an old man, small and straight-backed, with a white beard trimmed short and a lantern raised in one hand like a man greeting a train.

He stopped at the edge of the platform. Behind him, his people spread into a loose crescent, and Felix noted without pleasure that the crescent's geometry was very good—every angle covered, every exit politely closed.

The old man looked at them for a long moment. At Odile's sidearm. At Amara's river-hard hands. At Jun, who had instinctively positioned themselves between the strangers and the stove, guarding the bread with their body, which Felix suspected would earn them points later.

Then his gaze dropped to Felix's belt, to the brass compass hanging there—his father's compass, dented, the needle housing clouded with age—and the old man's whole face changed. It was like watching a locked door discover it had never needed a key.

"Well," he said, in accented but precise English, lowering the lantern. "Forty-one years, and the compass comes home on the son's hip." He stepped onto the platform, and behind him the guns did not exactly lower, but they softened, the way a dog softens when its person relaxes. "Your father stood on this exact board, boy. Right there, where you're dripping on the stew table. Come inside before the rain does. We have questions, and I suspect—" the old eyes flicked to Odile, and something hard passed through them like a fish under ice, "—so do you."

"How do you know who I am?" Felix asked.

The old man smiled, and it was a kind smile, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes.

"Son," he said, "you have his exact way of standing in a doorway asking questions instead of coming in from the rain. Bao. Signalman, retired, allegedly." He jerked his head toward the hillside, where the lamplight spilled warm from doors Felix now realized were everywhere—dozens of homes tucked into the treeline, a whole town living vertically above its own drowned twin. "Now come. All of you. Leave the guns on the hook by the door—that's not a request, but I'll say please, because your father would have wanted me to."

Felix looked back once, at the drowned street glowing green under the evening water, at the laundry line swaying, at the blank space on every map in the world that was, in fact, full to the brim with life.

Then the first true drops of the monsoon hit the tin roof like applause, and he went inside.
