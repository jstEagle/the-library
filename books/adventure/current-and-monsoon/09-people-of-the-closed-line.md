## 9. People of the Closed Line

The lamplight in the hills did not come down to meet them. That was the first thing Felix noticed, and the thing that kept his hand near the flare gun as Amara eased the boat against the drowned station's loading platform.

"They're watching us," Jun whispered from the engine hatch, one eye swollen nearly shut from three days of bilge water and bad sleep. "Have been since we rounded the bend."

"Of course they are," Amara said. She killed the engine and let the current nudge them in, her hands moving on the wheel with the unhurried grace of a woman who had decided that if she was going to be shot at, she'd rather not see it coming. "I'd watch us too. We look like a floating disaster."

It was true. The boat sat low in the water, riding half a foot heavier since the storm took their supplies overboard. Tin patches gleamed along the hull like brass buttons on a beggar's coat. Odile stood at the bow with her arms crossed, and Felix could see her doing the same arithmetic he was: whoever lived up there in those hills had watched them struggle through the rapids for two hours and had chosen, so far, to do nothing.

That was either very good news or very bad news, and Felix had learned—mostly through his father's example—that the difference usually came down to what happened next.

What happened next was a voice.

"Set your weapons on the platform," it called, from somewhere in the treeline above the station roof. It was an old voice, dry as river reeds, but steady. "All of them. Then come up slow, hands empty, and we'll talk about whether you eat tonight."

Felix looked at Odile. Odile looked at Felix. It was, he reflected, a strange inversion of every hierarchy the expedition had ever claimed to have—the contract said he was in charge, the boat said Amara was, and the last three days of shared misery had quietly established that it was actually Jun, because nobody argued with the person holding the wrench.

"Comply," Odile said finally. "They have the high ground and we have nothing worth dying over."

"You have maps," the voice observed, with something that might have been amusement. "We can hear you arguing about them at night. Sound carries on water, surveyor."

Amara snorted. Felix felt heat climb his neck. They *had* been arguing about the maps—about whether to press on to Junction Nine or turn back—and apparently the whole valley had gotten the performance free of charge.

He set the flare gun on the wet planks. Amara added a fishing spear she'd lashed together from a boathook. Jun produced, from somewhere inside her jacket, a knife so large that Felix genuinely wondered how she'd been sitting down.

"That's mine," she said defensively, to no one in particular. "I'm not eating anything I can't defend."

"Jun," Felix said.

"Fine." She slapped it down beside the flare gun like a woman surrendering a child.

The lamplight moved then—a lantern descending through the trees in careful stages, held by figures who knew the ground under their feet the way Amara knew the river. By the time the light reached the platform, Felix could count six of them, maybe seven, shapes resolving out of the dark: men and women in oilskins gone soft with age, carrying rifles with the easy, terrible competence of people who had never once needed them for show.

At their center stood a small man, old in the way of driftwood—weathered down to essentials, nothing left on him that wasn't load-bearing. He wore a signalman's coat, brass buttons polished to mirrors, and he carried the lantern up close to his face so they could see him clearly, which Felix understood was its own kind of courtesy. *Here is my face. Here is an honest old man. Now show me yours.*

"I am Bao," he said. "Signalman, Junction Nine, thirty-one years of service before the service ended. You are four travelers in a leaking boat, and you are lost, which is the only reason you found us." His eyes moved across each of them in turn, weighing. "You will tell me why you came, and you will tell me true, because I am too old to survive another lie."

Odile stepped forward. "We're looking for our advance team. Three surveyors who came upriver eleven days ago. Their radio went silent."

Something passed over Bao's face—not surprise, exactly. Recognition, perhaps, of a debt already incurred.

"Two of them are alive," he said. "In the cave past the falls, sick with fever. My people have been feeding them when the water allows." He raised a hand before the relief could become noise. "Do not thank me yet. Answer me this first: who sent you?"

"The Territorial Survey Office," Felix said. "Under contract to assess the closed lines for—" He hesitated. "For reopening."

The rifles did not move, but somehow they became more present, the way weather becomes present before a storm.

"Reopening," Bao repeated. He said the word the way other men said *landslide*.

"It's just a survey," Jun blurted. "Paperwork. Nobody's laying track through your—"

"Girl." Bao turned to her gently. "Nothing has ever been *just* paperwork in this valley. Paper is how the first ones came. Paper is how the miners came. Paper," he said, "is how your line was closed, though none of you know that yet, I think."

Felix's chest tightened. *None of you know that yet.* As if Bao were reading from a book whose later chapters Felix had never been allowed to see.

"We're not Meridian," Amara said quietly. It was the first time anyone had named the consortium aloud since the radio died, and the name landed among the armed strangers like a stone dropped into still water.

"No," Bao agreed, after a long moment. "Meridian does not hire river pilots with disgrace in their file, or children with indenture scars, or engineers drowning in debts." He smiled without much humor. "Yes, we know things here. Thirty years of watching teaches you to read a boat the way you read a timetable. Every person on that deck is running *from* something. Meridian men run *toward*."

He lifted the lantern higher, and the light fell full on Felix's face, and Bao went very still.

"Your name," he said. "Give it to me."

"Sterling," Felix said. "Felix Sterling."

Behind Bao, one of the riflemen made a sound—half breath, half word—and brought the rifle up off his shoulder. Bao stopped him with two fingers, never taking his eyes from Felix.

"Show me your right pocket," Bao said. "The heavy one. You've been guarding it since you climbed the ladder, the way a man guards a wound."

Slowly, aware of six rifle barrels and one teenage mechanic holding her breath, Felix reached into his pocket and drew out the brass compass.

His father's compass. Dented at the bezel where it had once fallen from a bridge trestle, the needle housing clouded with age, the case worn smooth by forty years of a thumb worrying it in moments of doubt—which, judging by the wear, had been frequent.

Bao looked at it for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice had changed, the dryness gone out of it, replaced by something older and more tired.

"Where did you get this?"

"My father left it to me. With his debts." Felix heard his own voice go flat. "Mostly the debts."

"And did he leave you anything else? A letter? A mark on a map?" Bao stepped closer, lantern raised, studying the compass as if it were a face he'd once known well and had dreamed about since. "Anything at all?"

"A map," Felix admitted. "Marked in his handwriting. I don't know what half the markings mean."

Bao closed his eyes. For a moment he seemed to be listening to something far away—the river, perhaps, or thirty-one years of trains that no longer ran.

"Elias Sterling," he said. "You have his chin. And his way of standing like the ground owes him money." He opened his eyes, and they were bright and hard and grieving all at once. "Your father stood on this platform, boy. Stood right there, where you're standing, the night we closed the line. He shook my hand and told me the valley would be safe now, and then he went downriver to finish the job, and the job finished *him* instead."

The rain chose that moment to arrive—a fine, needling drizzle that hissed against the lantern glass. Nobody moved. Felix became aware that his heart was going hard enough that surely everyone on the platform could hear it.

"Come inside," Bao said at last, turning away. "All of you. Dry clothes, hot food, and a roof that mostly holds." He paused, glancing back, and the suspicion returned to his face like a tide coming in. "But understand what this hospitality is. You are guests the way a fire in a hearth is a guest—it warms the house only so long as it stays where it's put. Step outside the walls we draw, and we will treat you as what you might be. Do you understand?"

"Perfectly," Odile said.

"He means don't wander around," Jun translated.

"I understood," Odile said, with dignity.

* * *

The town—if it could be called a town—clung to the hillside above the drowned station like a barnacle colony, forty-odd structures built from salvaged rail timber, boat hulls turned on their sides for roofs, anything the valley had ever given up. Lamplight showed in windows behind shutters that were, Felix noted, hinged for quick closing. Paths zigzagged between houses, and along every path, at intervals, stood posts with bells—signal posts, he realized. A whole town wired like a rail yard, ready to ring alarm from ridge to ridge in seconds.

These people hadn't just survived off the grid. They'd built a nervous system.

"They've been expecting trouble a long time," Amara murmured, walking beside him up the switchback path.

"Thirty years of it, according to Bao."

"Mm." She glanced sideways at him. "And your father built the alarm system, I'd wager. Look at the bell mounts. Railroad standard. Somebody taught these people to speak signal code."

Felix didn't answer. He was watching a boy of perhaps ten peer at them from a doorway, wide-eyed, until a woman pulled him back inside with a firmness that was less *stranger danger* than *drill practice*. Everyone here knew exactly what to do when outsiders came upriver. Everyone except, apparently, the outsiders.

The communal hall was a converted freight depot, its great doors open onto a central fire, long tables crowded with people who had stopped eating to stare. The silence as they entered was total and enormous. Felix had played concert halls smaller than that silence.

Then an old woman at the far table said something in a language Felix didn't know, and the room laughed—short, surprised, relieved—and the spell broke. Bowls appeared. Rice, river fish, greens from gardens Felix couldn't imagine surviving this altitude. Tea that tasted of smoke and something medicinal.

"Sit," said Bao, indicating the end of a table where he himself settled, flanked by two of his riflemen who did not sit and did not stop watching. "Eat. Questions after. Yours and mine both, and I promise mine are worse."

Jun needed no second invitation. She ate with the focused intensity of someone refueling machinery, and Felix saw more than one of the townsfolk soften visibly at the sight of her—the universal diplomacy of a skinny teenager demolishing a bowl of rice. An old man near the fire began, quietly, to teach her the word for *more* in the local tongue. She learned it instantly, deployed it twice, and by the third bowl had acquired a small audience.

"She'll have the town eating out of her hand by morning," Amara said.

"She'll have the town *re-engineered* by morning. Did you see her eyeing their water wheel?"

"I did. God help the water wheel."

Felix almost smiled. It was the closest thing to ease he'd felt since the radio died, and he distrusted it immediately, the way you distrust a calm stretch of river.

Bao waited until the bowls were empty. Then he dismissed the room—all but his two riflemen and a sharp-faced woman he introduced only as Sefa, who sat down with a ledger and a pencil and the air of someone about to conduct an audit.

"Now," Bao said. "Tell me everything. From the contract onward. Leave nothing out, especially the parts that shame you. Those are usually the truest parts."

So Felix told it. The debts. The contract. Odile's flinch at his name—he said it plainly, watching her take the blow without flinching again, which was its own kind of answer. The dead radio. The advance team. The storm, the leak, the maps. He told it all in the flat voice he used for engineering reports, and the room let him, and when he finished, Bao was quiet for a long while, turning the brass compass over in his small brown hands.

"Your father carried this the day he came to close the line," Bao said. "He set it on the dispatcher's desk, right there in the station below, and he said, *When the needle points home, I'll come back.*" He set it down in front of Felix, gently, the way you return something borrowed from the dead. "The needle never pointed home for him. But it has pointed here, tonight. Draw from that whatever meaning you can carry."

"What job was he finishing?" Felix asked. "Downriver. What job?"

Bao exchanged a look with Sefa. It was the look of people deciding how much truth a room could hold.

"Not tonight," he said. "Tonight you are wet and fed and half-asleep, and the rest of the story deserves better than a tired audience." He rose, joints cracking like green kindling. "Sefa will find you bunks. In the morning, I will show you the dispatcher's office, and the ledgers, and the thing your father left in the strongbox, and then you will understand why we keep our rifles close even at our own tables."

"There's a strongbox?" Jun said, instantly, scandalously awake.

"*Bed,*" said Amara.

* * *

The bunks were in the old crew quarters above the depot—narrow iron frames, wool blankets that smelled of cedar and woodsmoke. Rain worked steadily at the roof. Somewhere below, the town's bells hung silent in the dark, waiting.

Felix lay awake with the compass open on his chest, watching the needle tremble and settle, tremble and settle. Pointing, as it always pointed, north. Or pointing home. Or pointing wherever Elias Sterling had needed to go and never arrived.

Through the wall, he heard Jun's breathing finally go deep and even, the exhaustion winning its argument with curiosity. Across the room, Odile sat fully dressed in the dark, keeping a watch she didn't explain and he didn't ask about. Some trusts were rebuilt in silence or not at all.

He was nearly asleep when the bells rang.

One short strike, then another, then a pattern—three and two, three and two—rolling up the valley from the lower ridges, answered post by post until the whole hillside was speaking in brass. Doors opened along the paths. Lamplight bloomed. Voices, urgent but not panicked, moved through the rain.

Felix was at the window before he knew he'd moved. Down the switchback, Bao stood in the downpour beneath the nearest signal post, face turned toward the black gorge below the town, listening to something Felix couldn't hear over the rain.

Then Felix heard it too.

An engine. Far off, downriver, laboring against the current in the dark—more than one. Deep-throated, diesel-hearted engines built for pushing heavy loads, the kind of engines that belonged to companies, not travelers.

Beside him, Odile spoke quietly into the dark, not moving from her chair.

"Well," she said. "Now we learn what kind of guests *they* are."
