## 10. His Father's Handwriting

The rain came back at dusk, but gentler now, a soft gray hush on the tin roofs of Junction Nine that made the whole town sound like it was whispering secrets to itself. Felix sat in Bao's house—a signalman's cottage built half into the hillside, its walls papered with old timetables and diagrams of track switches—and watched the old man light a lamp with hands that shook only slightly, the way a man's hands shake when they've done the same motion ten thousand times and refuse to stop being careful about it.

"You have questions," Bao said. "You've had them since the moment you stepped off that boat. You hold them like a man holding a full cup of tea on a moving train. Very determined not to spill."

"I was told the line closed for flood damage," Felix said. "Officially. There were reports. Engineering assessments. My father—" He stopped. Started again. "There were reports."

"There were." Bao lowered himself into a chair across the small table, and the lamplight caught his face, deepening every crease into a small canyon. "I wrote some of them. Very convincing. I was always good at convincing paper of things."

Felix's cup of tea—Bao had insisted, and Felix was learning that refusing Bao's tea was like refusing the river its current—sat untouched in front of him. "You wrote false engineering reports."

"I wrote *true* engineering reports," Bao said, with the mild dignity of a man correcting a small but important point. "The line was unsafe. That was true. The question is *why* it was unsafe, and who wanted it that way, and who made sure the answer never reached the people who could have fixed it." He turned the lamp's wick down a fraction, and the shadows deepened. "Your father wrote the others. The false ones. The ones that said the bridge at Kilometer Forty-One could not bear freight traffic. The ones that said the gorge tunnel had collapsed beyond repair."

Felix felt something turn over in his chest, slow and heavy, like a barge coming about. "My father falsified safety reports."

"Your father *saved* this valley with falsified safety reports," Bao said. "It depends very much on your point of view. Engineers are like that. The same drawing can be a lie or a shield, depending on who's holding the lamp."

* * *

It took an hour, and two more cups of tea, and one interruption when a woman named Sefa brought in a bowl of stewed river fish and stayed long enough to look Felix over with frank, unembarrassed curiosity before leaving without a word. Through it all, Bao talked, and the story assembled itself piece by piece in Felix's mind like a bridge being built in the dark, each element clicking into place with the terrible satisfaction of a load-bearing joint.

Thirty-five years ago, the valley had been on every map. The line ran through it twice a week, and Junction Nine had been a real junction—water tower, freight yard, a school with a bell, forty-one families. The Meridian Mining Consortium had held surveying rights to the hills above the gorge, and their geologists had come through on the trains, polite men with polite clipboards who took core samples and said things like "preliminary" and "promising" and "we'll be in touch."

"They were in touch," Bao said. "Oh, they were in touch. They found copper in the north ridge. A great deal of copper. Enough to make an open-pit mine, which meant enough to make the town of Junction Nine an inconvenience sitting on top of their ore body. They offered to buy us out. Generously, they said. We said no. They offered again. We said no again. And then the offers stopped being offers, and the accidents started."

"What kind of accidents?"

"The boring kind. That's how you know they're deliberate. A fire in the freight warehouse. A brake failure on a company truck. A surveyor's camp washed out—nobody hurt, but the men were shaken, and Meridian's insurance men arrived very quickly to suggest that perhaps the valley's weather was not to be trusted, and perhaps the consortium's offer was looking better." Bao's mouth twisted. "Boring accidents. Patient men. They had time, and we had a school bell and forty-one families, and for a while it looked like patience would win."

"Then my father."

"Then your father." Bao reached beneath the table and brought up a wooden box, old and scarred, its lid worn smooth by handling. He opened it with the reverence of a man opening a reliquary, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a bundle of papers.

Felix knew the handwriting before Bao finished unwrapping it.

He had grown up with that handwriting. It was on the inside covers of every textbook in the house, on shopping lists, on the margins of his childhood drawings—*good line weight, Felix, but the curve is too eager*—on the last letter his father had ever sent him, three pages about a valve gauge and one sentence about being proud. A slanting, confident, slightly hurried hand that always looked like it was on its way somewhere important.

The papers were engineering drawings. Sections and elevations of the gorge tunnel. Stress calculations for the Kilometer Forty-One bridge. A schedule of "structural failures" laid out month by month with the meticulous care of a man planning a garden—except every failure was a fiction, every crack a forgery, every collapse a performance staged in ink and delivered to the rail authority's head office two hundred miles downstream.

"He came upriver in the spring of that year," Bao said. "A young man, then. Not much older than you. He'd been hired to do a condition assessment of the line—Meridian's doing, they wanted a report that would justify running heavy ore traffic. And instead of giving them what they wanted, he looked at the valley, and looked at the town, and looked at what a mine would do to the river, and he went home and started lying to his employers in the most beautiful way I have ever seen."

Felix picked up the top drawing with hands that did not feel entirely like his own. His father's initials in the corner. A date. And in the margin, in that same slanting hand: *Bridge rated for 12 tons max. Recommend permanent closure of gorge section. Regrettable. No alternative.*

*Regrettable. No alternative.* Felix almost laughed. His father had written *regrettable* the way other men wrote love letters.

"The line closed within two years," Bao went on. "Passenger service first—*unsafe conditions in the gorge tunnel*, your father's report. Then freight. Then the whole branch, condemned. Meridian fought it, of course. They hired their own engineers, who found the tunnel perfectly sound. So your father went into the tunnel himself, at night, with tools, and made certain parts of it *actually* unsound, so that the two reports would agree. He was thorough. He was always thorough." Bao's voice softened. "The town stayed. Meridian's rights lapsed—they needed rail access to make the mine profitable, and the rail access died, and their lawyers moved on to easier valleys. We took down the water tower, boarded the windows, and let the maps forget us. Forty-one families became thirty. Then twenty-six. The young ones leave, and I don't blame them. But we stayed. We stayed because a stranger drew lies on paper to give us the right to."

Felix set the drawing down. His tea had gone cold. He found he didn't care.

"He died on the river," Felix said. "The inquest said an accident. A survey trip."

"It was a survey trip." Bao looked at him steadily. "The last one. There was a section of the closure he was never satisfied with—a spur line, an old maintenance track that still appeared on one regional map, a map in a Meridian archive. He wanted it erased. Erasing a line from a map is harder than you'd think. Paper is stubborn. So he went down to do the paperwork in person, and to inspect the old spur one final time, and on the way back his boat went down at the gorge mouth in weather no sane pilot would have gone out in." Bao paused. "The pilot of that boat was a young woman named Vann."

The name landed in Felix's chest like a dropped wrench.

"Odile," he said.

"Odile," Bao agreed. "She was twenty-three. She told the inquest the weather turned. She told me, years later, that Meridian had offered her a great deal of money to make sure the boat went out that day, and that she took the money, and that she told herself the storm would do the work anyway, and that she has spent every day since trying to give the money back in a currency it can't be returned in." Bao's eyes were very old in the lamplight. "She came upriver looking for your father's work, Felix. Not to sell it. To finish it. She's been circling this valley for years like a bird that can't land. You arriving on her boat was either fate or very poor scheduling, but either way, the debt you've been carrying—" He tapped the box of drawings. "This is what your father actually left you. Not the debts. The debts were the *cover*. A man drowning in obligations is a man nobody follows, nobody questions, nobody suspects of holding the map to a valley that doesn't exist. Your father arranged his own ruin the way he arranged the tunnel collapse. Thoroughly. On purpose."

Felix stood up. He didn't decide to; his legs simply informed him. He walked to the small window, where the rain drew silver threads through the lamplight spilling from the houses below, where people moved between buildings with the easy unhurried certainty of people who were home.

All those years. All those creditors at the door, all those auctions, all those nights doing arithmetic by lamplight and hating a dead man for the hole he'd left. And the hole had been a vault. The debts had been a disguise. His father had spent Felix's childhood constructing a lie so complete that even his own son would believe it—because a son who believed it would never be followed, never be questioned, never be *used*.

"He could have told me," Felix said. His voice came out rougher than he intended.

"He could have," Bao said. "And you'd have spent your life looking over your shoulder, and Meridian would have spent its life looking at you. He chose to leave you free and angry instead of hunted and knowing. It was not a kind choice. It may not have been a wise one. But it was made by a man who loved you the way he loved everything—thoroughly, and in the margins, where nobody thought to read."

Felix pressed his forehead against the cool window glass. Somewhere below, someone was laughing—a real laugh, unhurried—and someone else was scolding a child about wet boots, and the river went on speaking its low continuous language under all of it.

*Regrettable. No alternative.*

"You said he died trying to finish the job," Felix said, without turning around. "The spur line. The map in the Meridian archive. Is it still there?"

"It is," Bao said. "As far as we know. Maps are like weeds. You pull one and find six more. Your father's work was never finished, Felix. It was *paused*. And now Meridian is sending surveyors upriver again, and gunboats, and men with clipboards, and I am an old man with a lamp and a box of beautiful lies, and the river is about to do what rivers do." Bao rose, joints cracking like small green wood, and laid one weathered hand on the box of drawings. "Your father left this valley a shield made of paper. I think he left you the hands to hold it up."

Felix turned from the window. Outside, the rain was thickening, and far off up the gorge, thunder muttered something that might have been a warning—or might have been Meridian's engines, still two days out, learning the river.

"Bao," he said. "Show me the sluice house."

The old signalman's eyebrows went up. "Your father's drawings don't mention a sluice house."

"No," Felix said, and picked the top drawing out of the box, and turned it over, and held it to the lamp. "But they mention a *flood control gate* at Kilometer Thirty-Three, rated for manual operation, marked—and I quote my father's handwriting—*in case of emergency, and I do mean the real kind*."

Bao took the paper. He read it twice. And then the old signalman began, very quietly, to smile.

"Tomorrow," he said. "At first light. Wear boots."

* * *

Felix didn't go back to the boat right away. He stood instead under the eave of Bao's cottage, watching the rain, doing the thing his father had taught him to do when a problem was too large to hold: he broke it into pieces small enough to carry.

His father had lied to him for twenty years, and the lie had been armor. Odile had betrayed a man she admired, and had been sailing toward her own redemption ever since. The debt that had broken his childhood had been a wall built around something worth protecting. Every terrible thing in his life had just been turned over like a stone, and underneath it was not rot but *purpose*, and he honestly did not know yet whether that made everything better or infinitely more complicated.

"You look like a man doing arithmetic," said a voice at his elbow.

Jun stood there in an oversized rain slicker, hair plastered flat, holding two cups of something hot and steaming that smelled aggressively of ginger.

"Where did you get tea at this hour?"

"I didn't get tea. I got *ginger water*, which is what tea becomes when you're poor and resourceful." Jun pushed a cup into Felix's hand. "Sefa's kitchen. She said I could take some if I fixed her stove damper, which I did, because I fix things, it's what I do, and because her kitchen has the only stove in this town that doesn't smoke." Jun leaned on the rail beside him and sipped. "So. Big box of old papers. Old man talking for hours. You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face my mother had when the indenture office letter came. The face where the ground moved." Jun blew on the ginger water. "You don't have to say what it is. I just thought you shouldn't stand out here alone with it."

Felix looked at the kid—sixteen, indentured, escaped, patched their hull with tin two days ago and hadn't once mentioned it since—and felt the strange new thing in his chest that had been growing since the engine hold, the thing that had no name yet but was starting to feel a lot like *mine*.

"My father," Felix said, "spent his whole life lying to a mining company to keep this valley safe, and then lied to me so I'd stay safe too, and then died before he could tell me the truth. And now I'm standing in the valley he died for, holding his lies in a box, and the company is coming back up the river."

Jun was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked on the eaves.

"My dad used to say," Jun offered, "that the best inheritance isn't money. It's instructions."

Felix huffed—almost a laugh. "His instructions are forty pages of forged bridge inspections."

"Then he left you the good kind," Jun said. "The kind you have to *figure out*." The kid drained the cup, shivered, and jerked a thumb toward the lamplit path down the hill. "Come on. Amara's got the stove going on the boat and she's pretending she isn't worried about you, which means she's very worried about you, and Odile's sitting in the bow like a woman waiting for a verdict. Which, no offense, she is."

Felix looked out once more at the dark gorge, where the thunder had gone quiet, as if the storm itself were holding its breath.

Then he picked up the box of his father's beautiful lies, tucked it under his arm, and followed the kid down into the lamplight.
