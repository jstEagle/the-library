## 11. The Advance Team

Amara Osei had been reading rivers since she was nine years old, and she had learned long ago that a river tells you everything if you're willing to shut up and listen. The water below Junction Nine was telling her plenty that morning: it ran a hand's width higher than yesterday, it carried more silt than it should this early in the season, and it smelled—she pressed her palm flat to the surface and lifted it to her nose—of far-off rain that hadn't made up its mind yet.

"Two days," she said. "Maybe three."

Bao stood beside her on the drowned platform of the old station, his hands folded behind his back like a man inspecting troops he didn't entirely trust. "You can smell that?"

"I can smell that, I can hear it in the current, and I can feel it in my knees, which have better forecasting equipment than most weather bureaus." She wrung out her sleeve. Her knees were not wrong often. It was one of the few things about getting older she'd decided to tolerate.

Behind them, Felix Sterling was arguing gently with Jun about whether a sixteen-year-old should be climbing into the flooded lower gallery of the station to check on their patched hull seam. Jun was winning, as Jun always won, by the simple strategy of already being halfway down the ladder.

"He's going to drown," Bao observed.

"He's going to be fine. That boy has the survival instincts of a cat and the self-preservation instincts of a moth." Amara shaded her eyes against the grey light. "It balances out."

But she wasn't really watching Jun. She was watching the hills.

The advance team had been missing for eleven days now. Odile Vann had finally said their names out loud last night around the cook fire—Okonkwo, Reyes-Lin, and a quiet man named Tull who'd signed on late and smiled too easily—and asked Bao's people if anyone had seen them. The answer had been no, but the *way* it was no had interested Amara. Old Sefa, the woman who kept the valley's goats and apparently its secrets, had looked at the ridge above the eastern gorge before she'd looked at Odile. Just for a second. Just the way you look at a place where something is.

Amara had spent twenty years piloting boats through country where the difference between reading a person right and reading them wrong was the difference between arriving and being buried under a rockslide. She trusted her read of Sefa the way she trusted her knees.

So at first light, while Felix slept off his night of studying his father's drawings and Jun slept wherever Jun had fallen asleep mid-task, Amara found Bao on the platform and said, "Show me the caves above the east gorge."

Bao had studied her for a long moment. "Why?"

"Because your goat woman knows something, and because if those three are alive, they've been eating cave moss for eleven days, and nobody deserves that." She'd paused. "Also because if they're dead, I'd rather we know before the river decides to make the question academic."

He'd brought four others. Now there were six of them picking their way up a goat track so narrow it could barely be called a track, with the gorge dropping away beneath them and the clouds sitting low enough to comb through the pines.

"You knew Okonkwo?" Amara asked Bao as they climbed. "The advance team leader?"

"No." Bao didn't turn around. "But I know the type. Surveyors come in two kinds—the kind who draw what's there, and the kind who draw what they were paid to find." He stepped over a fallen trunk slick with moss. "I am hoping your Mr. Okonkwo is the first kind."

"And if he's the second kind?"

"Then we will have a different conversation."

* * *

They found the cave an hour later, almost exactly where Amara had guessed—not from any skill, she'd insist later, but because Sefa's goats had been grazing near its mouth and goats always graze near shelter. A curtain of hanging roots half-hid the entrance. Inside, the air was cool and mineral and carried the unmistakable smell of human habitation: smoke, sweat, and something that might once have been a tin of fish.

Amara held up a hand. Listened.

Water dripping. Wind in the roots behind them. And then—faint, cracked, barely a voice at all—

"—told him. Told him the badges were wrong. Nobody wears the badges anymore unless they want you to see them—"

Her chest went tight. Alive. At least one of them alive.

She went in low and fast, lamp high, the way you enter anywhere that might contain either a friend or a problem. The cave opened into a chamber maybe ten meters deep, and against the back wall, wrapped in a survey tarp gone grey with damp, lay two men.

The first was skeletal, bearded past recognition, and unconscious or sleeping—it took Amara a moment to tell the difference, and even then only because his chest moved. The second was propped against the stone with a broken survey rod across his lap like a spear, and he was pointing it at her with hands that shook so badly the rod chattered against itself like teeth.

"Don't—" His voice was a ruin. "Don't come closer. I'll—I'm armed. Technically."

"It's a measuring stick," Amara said.

"It's *loaded*."

Despite everything—despite the dread sitting in her stomach like ballast—Amara felt her mouth twitch. "Sir, if you can load a measuring stick, Meridian would probably like to hire you."

The man blinked. His eyes, sunken and wild, tracked over her face, her clothes, the lamp. Something in them shifted—from animal wariness toward something almost human. "You're not them," he said. Not a question. A man checking his own arithmetic.

"No. We're the rescue that's eleven days late. My name's Amara Osei. I pilot the boat that was supposed to meet you at the confluence." She lowered herself slowly to a crouch, keeping her hands visible. "Which one are you? Okonkwo? Reyes-Lin?"

"Tull." He said it like it cost him. Then, quickly, as if the name needed defending: "I'm the second kind. The kind who draws what's there. Ask anyone. Ask—" His face crumpled. "Ask Marcus. He can't ask anything right now. Fever broke two days ago but he keeps going somewhere else. Keeps talking about badges."

Amara crossed the chamber and knelt by the unconscious man. Up close, the fever-heat came off him like a stove. His lips were moving, shaping the same words over and over, and when she leaned close she caught them:

*"...badges... they had the badges... why would they wear the badges..."*

She sat back on her heels and let the cold of the cave move through her.

"Bao," she called toward the entrance. "Get word down to the station. We need the stretcher, clean water, and every drop of medicine Bao's people are willing to part with." She paused. "And send someone for Felix Sterling."

Tull made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't rusted out of him. "Sterling. God. Is he here? Odile never told us whose son—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "Never mind. Never mind whose son."

"You knew his father?" Amara asked carefully.

"Everybody on the old lines knew Elias Sterling." Tull's eyes had gone distant, fixed on some middle distance only he could see. "Knew him, feared him, loved him, depending on which side of the maps you stood." He looked at her then, and there was something urgent gathering behind the exhaustion, a man who had been holding a message alone in the dark for eleven days and could finally put it down. "Listen to me. Listen. We found the junction. We surveyed it, all proper, all honest, Marcus drew every span of it. And then three days later, two men came up the river trail wearing Meridian consortium badges—badges, plural, worn *openly*, like they owned the air. They said they were with our expedition. They said Odile had sent them ahead." His voice dropped to a whisper, ragged and fierce. "Nobody wears those badges openly. Nobody. Unless the wearing *is* the message. Unless they wanted us to run."

"Did you run?"

"We ran." His gaze slid to the dark mouth of the cave, and Amara watched eleven days of fear pass across his face. "Marcus fell on the scree. Broke his leg in two places. We got him in here and I went back for water and when I came back—" He swallowed. "When I came back there were boot prints at the entrance. Fresh ones. They'd found the cave and left us alive in it. Do you understand? They *left* us. Like leaving a note. Like saying: we know where you are, and it doesn't matter, because soon none of this will matter."

The drip of water filled the silence.

"The third member of your team," Amara said quietly. "Reyes-Lin. Was he with you when the badge men came?"

Tull's face did something complicated. "Reyes-Lin went ahead of us. Day six. Said he'd scout the downstream approach and meet us at the junction." He shook his head slowly. "We waited three days. He never came. And then the badge men arrived from *downstream*, Amara. From the direction Reyes-Lin went." He let that sit between them, heavy as floodwater. "I'm not saying. I'm just saying what directions things came from."

Amara stood. Outside, Bao's people were already moving, voices calling down the gorge, and somewhere below, faint as a heartbeat, the river kept rising.

* * *

Felix Sterling arrived at the cave two hours later, breathing hard from the climb, with Jun two steps behind him carrying a stretcher roll nearly as big as they were and complaining about it with the cheerful relentlessness of a woodpecker.

"—and I'm just saying, next time somebody finds lost surveyors, maybe we send the tall person, because my legs end approximately *here*—" Jun stopped dead in the cave mouth, taking in the scene: the tarp-wrapped figure, the skeletal man clutching his measuring stick, Amara crouched over both. All the complaint drained out of that young face at once, replaced by something older. "Oh. Okay. Yeah. Where do you want me?"

"Stretcher down flat, slow," Amara said. "Jun, you're on Marcus's leg—splint's already set, don't touch the setting, just keep it still. Felix—"

Felix had already knelt beside Tull. Whatever he'd expected to feel, Amara watched it rearrange itself as he took in the state of the man—eleven days of cave, carved down to wire and stubbornness.

"Mr. Tull. I'm Felix Sterling."

"I know who you are." Tull managed to get the measuring stick aside and gripped Felix's forearm with both hands, and his grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "Your father drew the original closure plans for this line. Did you know that? Did Odile ever tell you that?"

Something flickered across Felix's face—grief, confirmation, the particular exhaustion of a man collecting new facts about a father he thought he'd finished grieving. "I'm learning," he said. "Lately I'm learning a lot."

"Well, learn this too." Tull pulled him closer, and his whisper was hoarse and absolute. "Meridian is in this valley. Not coming. *In.* Two of them walked past me wearing their colors, and a third one may have been standing next to me for six days sharing my coffee." His eyes went to the cave entrance, to the grey light beyond it, to the whole green hidden world spread out below. "Count your crew, Mr. Sterling. Count them twice. And count them again after dark."

Jun looked up from the stretcher, splint pins held between their teeth, and around the pins said something that sounded like *"I counted mine this morning, we're fine,"* which was either reassurance or a joke, and with Jun it was genuinely impossible to say.

Felix wasn't laughing though. Amara watched him look at his own hands for a moment—at the compass callus, at the ink stains—watching him do the arithmetic Tull had handed him. Six had come upriver. Six were accounted for. But the advance team had been three, and one of them had walked into the valley ahead of everyone, and no one had seen him since.

"Felix," Amara said quietly, and he looked up. "There's something else. When we carry them down—we take the long way. Through the station galleries. Not the open trail."

"Why?"

"Because if someone in that valley is reporting to Meridian," she said, "I'd rather they not watch us carry the only witnesses home."

Above them, through the root curtain, the clouds had begun, at last, to make up their mind.
