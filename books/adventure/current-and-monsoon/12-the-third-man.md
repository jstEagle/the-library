## 12. The Third Man

Felix didn't sleep so much as hover above it, the way a man hovers above cold water he's about to be pushed into.

The cave had changed everything. Two of the advance team alive—Hollis with his frostbitten hands and his fever-bright eyes, and Marek, who hadn't stopped shivering since they'd carried him down to Junction Nine. And one name that wouldn't stop circling Felix's head like a bird looking for a place to land.

*Where is Tomas?*

Hollis had said it twice before the delirium took him properly. *Tomas went for help. Tomas went for help.* But Felix had lain awake in Bao's signal house, staring at the lamplit ceiling beams, doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

Three men had gone upriver with Odile's advance team six weeks ago. Two were in a cave, half-starved, one of them talking to ghosts. The third had never been in the cave at all.

Which meant the third man was either dead somewhere in these hills—or he'd walked back out. And if he'd walked back out, there was only one direction to walk: downstream, toward the boat, toward the four strangers who had come blundering upriver asking questions about closed lines.

Felix sat up in the dark.

*Oh,* he thought, with the terrible clarity of three in the morning. *Oh no.*

* * *

He found Amara on the dock at first light, coiling rope with the economy of someone who'd been awake for hours and resented everyone who hadn't been. Mist lay on the water like something spilled. Behind her, Junction Nine was waking—the smell of woodsmoke, a woman beating a rug on a balcony that hung over dry air where a rail platform used to be, two children chasing a goat along what had once been the station approach.

"Amara," Felix said. "I need you to think back to the night the radio died."

She didn't look up from her coils. "I've thought about little else for a week. Congratulations on joining the club."

"The transmission cut off mid-word. You said it yourself—it wasn't weather, it wasn't distance. Someone killed it."

"I also said it could have been a power fault. Old equipment." She tied off the rope and finally looked at him, and whatever she saw in his face made her go still. "What did Hollis say?"

"He said Tomas went for help." Felix crouched beside her, lowering his voice even though the nearest person was forty yards away arguing with the goat. "Amara, help was downstream. Six weeks downstream, through country he didn't know, alone, without a boat. Nobody walks that. Not and lives."

"So he died trying. It happens on rivers."

"Or—" Felix made himself say it, made the words real by giving them air, "—or he never left the valley at all. Or he came back down to us."

Amara's hands stopped moving. He watched her run the same arithmetic he had, watched it land on her the way it had landed on him, like a dropped anchor.

"There are five of us who came upriver," she said slowly. "You, me, Jun, Odile, and Rennik. Rennik signed on in March. Rennik volunteered for every watch. Rennik—" She stopped.

"Rennik asked me last week which route we'd take back down," Felix said quietly. "Casual, over dinner. I told him. I drew it in the dirt with a stick."

The mist drifted between them. Somewhere behind them the goat won its argument.

"We don't know anything yet," Amara said, but her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a pilot reading water she doesn't trust. "A man can ask a question without being a traitor. I've asked worse ones."

"Have you ever asked them for Meridian?"

That landed hard—he saw it hit—and he regretted it instantly, because Amara's disgrace was an open wound and he'd just pressed his thumb into it. But she surprised him. She didn't flare. She just looked at him with tired eyes and said, "No. And neither has Rennik, far as I know. Which is exactly why we don't hang him on a feeling, Felix. We watch. We say nothing. If he's clean and we accuse him, we lose a pair of hands we'll need when the water rises. If he's dirty and he knows we suspect him—" She let the sentence die, but its meaning floated there between them like a body face-down.

"He could hurt Jun," Felix said. It came out before he could dress it up as strategy.

"Yeah," Amara said softly. "He could hurt any of us. That's rather the point."

* * *

They agreed on a system without ever calling it a system. Amara would keep Rennik talking—she was good at talking, good at steering conversations the way she steered boats, into currents other people didn't notice. Felix would check the survey maps against their case each evening, counting pages, noting creases. And neither of them would breathe a word to Jun, because Jun wore every feeling on her face like paint, and sixteen-year-olds were constitutionally incapable of pretending not to know a secret.

It lasted eleven hours.

The failure, when it came, was not suspicion or drama. It was soup.

They ate that evening in Bao's kitchen, all of them crammed around a table built from an old signal-box door laid across two barrels, eating a fish stew that tasted of river mint and someone's entire annual supply of salt. Rennik sat across from Felix, telling a story about a crane barge he'd worked on years ago, big hands shaping the air, his laugh easy and loud. He was, Felix thought miserably, extremely likable. That was the worst part. Traitors in stories were supposed to have shifty eyes. Rennik had kind ones.

Jun was ladling herself a second bowl when she froze, spoon halfway to her mouth, and looked at Felix with an expression of dawning horror.

"You counted the map case this morning," she said.

The table went quiet. Rennik's spoon paused.

"Jun," Felix began.

"No, wait—I saw you. By the lamp, before breakfast. You had the case open and you were turning pages and your face was doing the thing it does when you're doing sums you hate." She pointed the spoon at him, betrayed. "You're checking the maps. You think somebody took something. You think—" Her eyes went wide, and traveled, with the terrible frankness of youth, directly to Rennik. "You think it's *him*."

"Junie," Amara said gently, "sit down."

But it was already broken. Rennik set his spoon down with exaggerated care, and Felix watched the easy warmth drain out of his face and something harder settle in behind it—not guilt, Felix thought desperately, just the look of a man realizing the floor has tilted.

"Well," Rennik said. "That's a fine thing to eat supper to."

"Nobody's accused anyone," Amara started.

"You counted pages." Rennik laughed, but it was a wrong laugh, a door slamming dressed up as a chuckle. "Eleven months I've hauled gear through mud for this outfit. Eleven months, and the railroad man counts his papers like I've got sticky fingers." He stood, pushing back from the barrel-table so it rocked. "You want to know where I was while your father's friends were starving in a cave? Ask your pilot. Ask her what she was doing the year she ran her boat aground with a hold full of—"

"Careful," Amara said. One word, quiet as a knife coming off a magnet.

Rennik heard it. To his credit, some part of him flinched. He stood there breathing, jaw working, and then he grabbed his jacket off the nail by the door and walked out into the blue dusk, boots hammering down the old platform boards until the sound faded into the hills.

Silence. The stew steamed. Jun sat down very slowly, looking like she wanted to fold herself into the soup pot.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. "I didn't mean—I just *saw*—"

"You saw true," Felix said heavily. "That's the problem. I should have told you both myself instead of sneaking around. Sneaking is how you make innocent people look guilty and guilty people look innocent." He rubbed his eyes. "And now he knows we're watching."

"That," said Amara, staring at the door, "may have been the whole purpose."

* * *

Odile found them an hour later on the ridge path, where Felix had gone to be alone with his failure and Amara had followed to make sure he failed correctly. She climbed toward them with her lantern held high, and even before she reached them Felix could read trouble in the set of her shoulders—that particular rigidity she got, he was learning, when bad news and personal history had gotten tangled together again.

"Hollis is lucid," she said. "Bao's sitting with him. He wants to talk to whoever's in charge of the survey." A pause. "He asked for Sterling. By name. Said, and I quote, 'Tell the son of Elias Sterling I need to tell him about the third man.'"

The wind moved through the pines above the valley, and Felix felt it go through him too, cold finding every gap in his coat.

"He knows," Felix said. "Hollis knows what happened to Tomas."

"Or he knows what he *believes* happened," Odile said. "Delirium makes poor witnesses. Come anyway."

They descended through the terraced gardens—Bao's people grew astonishing things on the old embankments, beans and squash climbing trellises made from rail spikes and signal wire—and entered the signal house, where a fire burned low and Hollis lay propped on blankets, gaunt as a fence post, his frostbitten hands wrapped in clean linen. His eyes tracked Felix across the room with an effort that seemed physical.

"Sterling's boy," he rasped. "You've got his chin. And his habit of standing like the room owes him money."

"You knew my father?"

"Everybody on this river knew Elias Sterling. Best engineer the line ever had, and the reason we're all still breathing free air." Hollis coughed, a wet, terrible sound. "Sit down, son. This'll take the wind out of you either way."

Felix sat. Amara stayed by the door; Odile lowered herself onto a stool near the fire, and Felix noticed her hands were folded very tightly, as if holding something in.

"Tomas Vey," Hollis said. "Third man on our crew. Good climber, better talker. Signed on late—Odile picked him up in a port town, vouched for him herself." His eyes flicked to Odile, and there was something in them that wasn't quite accusation but lived next door to it. "First week out, I caught him at night with a notebook. Writing figures. Bearings, elevations, distances. I asked him what he was doing and he said he kept a log for himself, liked the neatness of it. And maybe that's true. Maybe. But then the radio died, and two days later Meridian's survey stakes showed up on the north bank where no surveyor had any business being, and Tomas said he'd go downriver for help."

"He left," Felix said. "You saw him leave?"

"I saw him walk into the trees with a pack." Hollis's mouth twisted. "What I didn't see, because I was half-dead with fever by then, was whether he walked *down*. Marek swears he heard a motorboat that same night, way down in the gorge. Swears it plain as prayer." He fixed Felix with eyes that had seen too much and would see more. "Your maps, son. The survey maps you're carrying. Who else aboard knows the return route?"

Felix's stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution.

"Everyone," he said. "We talk openly. We're a crew."

"Mm." Hollis closed his eyes. "Elias Sterling taught me something once, back when we were closing this line. He said: *a secret isn't a thing you keep, it's a thing you refuse to carry past certain doors.* You've been carrying yours through every door on this river, boy. Time you learned which doors to stop at."

The fire popped. Outside, far down the valley, a night bird called three times and got no answer.

"And the notebook?" Felix asked. "Did Tomas leave it? Do you know where—"

"He burned it," Odile said suddenly.

Every head turned. She was staring into the fire, and her face in the firelight looked ten years older than it had that morning.

"I checked his kit after he left. Before we went into the cave country. I told myself it was procedure." Her voice was steady, but her knuckles were white around each other. "The notebook was ash in his cookfire. Every page. And I let myself believe that meant he was clean—a man burning his own notes, a man going for help." She looked up, and her eyes found Felix's, and what was in them was the thing she'd been carrying since the port town, since the day she'd flinched at his surname. "Felix. When I hired your father's son onto this expedition, I told myself it was because you were the best surveyor available. That was a lie I told comfortably. The truth is I needed someone who'd understand what we might find. Someone with his blood." She exhaled. "And I may have handed Meridian the one man alive who can read everything Elias Sterling hid in this valley."

The words settled over the room like ash.

Felix thought of Rennik's easy laugh, of the return route drawn in the dirt, of Jun's horrified pointing finger, of a motorboat in the gorge six weeks ago. He thought of his father's compass in his pocket, needle steady, pointing at nothing and everything.

"Then we assume the worst," he said, and was surprised at how level his own voice came out. "We assume Meridian has our route, our numbers, and a guide who knows this valley's shape. We assume they're already moving." He stood. "And we stop carrying secrets through doors. All of us. Starting tonight."

Amara pushed off from the doorframe. "Starting with the sluice," she said quietly. "Because if they're coming upriver, Felix, then whatever your father built under this town—we'd best learn to use it before someone else learns to break it."

Above the signal house, through the window, the peaks stood black against a sky full of swollen clouds, and the river below ran high and fast and dark, carrying everything downstream except the truth.

That, they would have to bring down themselves.
