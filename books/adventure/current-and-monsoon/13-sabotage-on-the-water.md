## 13. Sabotage on the Water

Felix woke to the sound of an engine that wasn't right.

He'd spent enough years around machinery to know that engines had moods, the way people did. The *Kestrel's* old diesel had grumbled and coughed since Amara first coaxed it to life, but it had grumbled honestly, like an old man complaining about his knees while still climbing the stairs. This was different. This was a note held too long, a smoothness where there should have been texture, as if someone had fed it something sweet to keep it quiet.

He lay in his bunk under the tarp awning and listened, and the wrongness settled into him like cold water wicking up a sleeve.

Then he remembered the maps.

He was moving before his boots were fully on, ducking through the canvas flap, and the deck was empty except for Jun's bedroll — empty too — and the chart case where it had sat wedged beneath the pilot bench every night since Junction Nine.

The case was there. The case was open. The case was light as a lie.

"No," Felix said, to no one, to the river, to himself. "No, no, no."

* * *

They found Tobin Hale at the bank of the cut, fifty yards downstream, hauling the boat's tender through the reeds with the desperate rhythm of a man who knew exactly how many minutes he had. That was the thing Felix would remember afterward, the thing that would sit in his chest like a swallowed stone: Tobin had been with them for eleven days. He'd shared watch shifts with Amara, taught Jun a card game called Ruin that involved cheating, had once given up half his ration of dried fish without being asked. He'd laughed at Felix's jokes. Not politely — actually laughed.

And all that time he'd been Meridian's ears, and now Meridian's hands.

"Tobin!" Felix shouted, and the name came out cracked down the middle.

Tobin didn't turn. That told Felix everything. A man with nothing to hide turns around.

It was Jun who got there first — of course it was Jun, sixteen years old and built out of stubbornness and spare parts, who'd been sleeping light ever since the cave, who'd heard the tender's painter slip free before anyone else. Jun came out of the treeline above the bank like a thrown wrench, hit Tobin at the waterline, and took him down into the shallows.

What followed was not a fight so much as a collision — two bodies and a boat hook and a great deal of muddy water, the tender overturning, the chart case skidding across the rocks. Felix was running, Amara was running behind him shouting something about heads and skulls, and the rain that had been threatening all morning finally arrived, fat drops hammering the river flat.

Jun got a hand on the case. Tobin got a hand on Jun's arm and twisted, and Felix heard the sound — a wet, ugly pop, like green wood breaking — and heard Jun scream, high and furious rather than frightened, which somehow made it worse.

Then Amara arrived like weather.

Felix had seen Amara angry before, but never like this. She didn't swing wildly; she moved through Tobin's guard the way she moved through rapids, reading the current of him, and put him down against a boulder with three economical blows. Tobin crumpled, gasping, blood threading pink from his lip into the rainwater.

"The case," Amara said, breath ragged. "Is the case—"

Jun had it. Curled around it on the rocks, cradling it one-armed, the other hanging wrong, face white as bone under the freckles and rain. "Got it," Jun said through gritted teeth. "Got it, got it." And then, looking past Felix at Tobin with an expression far too old for sixteen: "He loosened the fuel line. I saw it this morning. I thought it was vibration wear."

Felix turned slowly.

Tobin was sitting up against the boulder, and he wasn't reaching for anything, and he wasn't apologizing either. He looked tired. He looked, horribly, like himself.

"You were going to let us run dry in the gorge," Felix said. "In monsoon season."

"I was going to let you drift somewhere survivable," Tobin said. His voice was steady, and that steadiness was its own kind of confession. "Meridian pays for results, not corpses. Mostly."

"Mostly," Amara repeated, flat.

"The advance team," Felix said. "Renn and Okafor. Was that you? Leading them to the cave, feeding them the route?"

Tobin looked at the river. "I told them there was a shortcut. What happened after that wasn't my doing." It was the closest thing to shame he offered, and it was thin as tin.

"And the maps?" Felix crouched, brought them eye to eye. Rain ran off his nose between them. "You didn't get them. Why are you still here?"

For the first time, something flickered behind Tobin's eyes — calculation, or fear, hard to say. Downstream, faint but unmistakable, came the sound none of them wanted: engines. Deep-throated, multiple, working against the current. Gunboats, coming upriver along the exact channel Tobin had spent eleven days learning.

"There's your answer," Tobin said quietly.

And then he moved — not at any of them, but sideways, rolling off the rock into the current with the practiced ease of a man who'd planned his exit before he'd planned anything else. Amara lunged; her fingers closed on cloth and lost it. The river took him the way rivers take everything, without malice or hurry, and by the time Felix reached the bank Tobin was thirty meters out, swimming hard for the far side, angling downstream toward the sound of the engines.

Amara stood hip-deep in the shallows, fists clenched, watching him go. She didn't fire the flare pistol she carried, though her hand hovered over it. "No shot," she said, mostly to herself. "Not worth revealing our position over one traitor."

"He already sold our position," Felix said.

"Yes," Amara said. "But they don't know we know."

* * *

Jun's arm was broken. Not badly — Bao's people had a splint kit better than most hospitals, salvaged from the junction's clinic days — but badly enough. Felix sat beside Jun in the signal house while old Marta wrapped the forearm with practiced, unsentimental hands, and Jun stared at the wall and refused to make a sound.

"You could've died," Felix said.

"So could you. So could anybody. Welcome to the river." But Jun's voice wobbled on the last word, and the bravado cracked just enough to show what was underneath.

"You went after him alone."

"He was getting away." Jun flexed the splinted fingers experimentally, winced, kept going. "Somebody had to."

"Somebody had to shout. Somebody had to wake me. Somebody had to do literally anything besides tackle a grown man twice your size into a river." Felix heard his own voice rising and couldn't stop it, because underneath the anger was the other thing, the cold slick thing, the image of Jun floating face-down in the cut while the rain came down. "I trusted him, Jun. I shook his hand. I told you all we should push on when Odile wanted to turn back, and I vouched for—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "This is my fault."

The room went quiet except for the rain on the tin roof.

"Felix." Jun's voice, careful now. "Look at me."

He looked. Jun was watching him with that unflinching mechanic's stare, the one that diagnosed things.

"If it's your fault that a liar lied," Jun said, "then it's my fault for sleeping, and Amara's fault for keeping watch, and Odile's fault for hiring him, and Bao's fault for building a valley worth stealing. That's not how fault works. That's just how grief dresses itself up when it doesn't want to be sad."

Felix blinked. "How old are you?"

"Sixteen."

"That's obscene. You can't be allowed to say things like that at sixteen."

Jun grinned despite everything, then hissed and cradled the arm again. "Besides," Jun added, quieter, "you want to talk about trust? I stowed away on your boat. You didn't throw me overboard. Trusting people is the whole job. The alternative is Tobin."

That landed harder than Felix expected. He nodded, because his throat had closed up, and looked out the rain-streaked window toward the river, where the engine sounds had faded downstream — taking Tobin, taking the coordinates in his head if not on paper, taking word of everything they'd found here straight to the men in the gunboats.

* * *

They gathered that evening in the signal house, the whole strange crew of them: Odile and Amara, Felix with the recovered chart case on the table between them, Jun propped on a crate with the splinted arm resting on one knee like a tool set aside mid-repair. Bao stood by the window with his arms folded, and behind him the lamplight caught the old brass fittings of the junction, polished by decades of faithful hands.

"The fuel line's patched," Jun reported. "New gasket, spare hose from the tender. We're floatable. Slow, but floatable."

"The maps?" Odile asked.

Felix opened the case. His father's originals were there — damp at the edges, one corner torn where it had scraped rock, but whole. Every line. Every elevation. Every secret the valley had paid so much to keep.

"They're intact," he said. "Because Jun broke an arm holding onto them."

"Because Jun is impossible," Amara said, and there was no humor in it at all, only fierce agreement.

Odile leaned over the drawings, and Felix watched her face do something complicated. "Tobin will tell them about the sluice house," she said. "Even without the maps, he knows enough. He watched us study them for two days."

"Then we have until their boats reach the narrows," Bao said. "Two days. Perhaps less, if the water rises fast."

Outside, the rain thickened, drumming the roof like impatient fingers. Somewhere downriver, in the dark, Meridian was coming — guided by a traitor's memory, hunting a valley that had spent forty years learning how to disappear.

Felix put his hand flat on his father's drawings and felt, for the first time, not the weight of debt but the weight of instruction.

"Then we'd better be ready," he said, "before the water does it for us."
