## 14. Meridian Comes Upriver

Odile Vann had spent thirty years learning to read a river the way other people read faces, and what she read in the gorge that morning made her want to sit down on the wet stone and refuse to get up.

She'd climbed the ridge before dawn—old habit, old guilt, the same instinct that had her checking moorings twice and counting heads three times—and she'd brought the field glasses because Bao's people had offered them and because looking at trouble from a distance had always been her one reliable talent. Trouble up close was another matter entirely. Trouble up close was how you ended up with a ruined reputation, a dead engine partner, and a name that made men flinch when they heard it spoken in river towns.

The mist sat low on the water, the way it did most mornings here, thick as wool and just about as useful for seeing through. Below her, Junction Nine went about its business in the half-light: smoke from cook fires, the clank of Bao's people reopening the signal house, someone laughing near the vegetable terraces—the laugh was Jun's, high and startled, followed by an older woman's cackle. The boy had been adopted by the entire valley in roughly four hours flat. Odile suspected there were already three grandmothers competing over his breakfast.

Then the mist moved.

Not the way mist moves—sideways, lazy, dissolving—but the way a thing *parts* when something large comes through it.

She put the glasses up.

Three boats. Grey hulls, angular superstructures, the blunt bow geometry of vessels built to push through things rather than around them. Gun mounts fore and aft, though at this distance they looked like folded wings. Running lights burning in daylight, which meant either arrogance or urgency, and Odile had known Meridian long enough to know it was usually both.

They came up the gorge in single file, hugging the eastern channel where her own charts—*her* charts, the ones she'd drawn twenty years ago for a company that had promised her the survey work would be "environmental assessment"—showed deep water. Which meant whoever was piloting them had maps. Good ones. Recent ones.

Which meant the third man hadn't wasted any time.

"Damn it, Calloway," she said quietly, to no one, to the man who wasn't there anymore, to the version of herself who'd shaken his hand at the dock and thought *this one seems honest.* "You couldn't have drowned him."

* * *

By the time she got down to the town, Felix was already awake, standing in the doorway of the signal house with his father's compass open in his palm, staring at it like it might start taking dictation. He'd been doing that a lot since Bao told them the truth about Sterling senior. Odile recognized the look. It was the look of a man reorganizing every memory he owned into new categories.

"They're coming," she said.

He didn't ask who. That was the thing about Felix Sterling—slow to anger, slow to trust, but once a fact landed in him it stayed landed. "How many?"

"Three gunboats. Maybe forty men, maybe more below decks." She jerked her chin toward the ridge. "We need eyes up there and everyone else down here deciding what they're willing to do about it."

Felix pocketed the compass. "Get Amara. I'll find Bao."

"And Jun?"

Felix hesitated, and Odile watched him wrestle with it—the same wrestling match she'd been losing for two days. The boy had patched their hull with tin scavenged from a drowned barge and then slept twelve hours sitting upright against the boiler like a cat who'd decided this engine was now his personal property. Sending him away from danger was not going to work. It was especially not going to work if you phrased it as sending him away from danger.

"Tell him everything," Odile said. "He'll find out anyway. Better he hears it from us than catches us lying."

"That's cynical."

"That's experience."

* * *

The council convened in the signal house, which Bao apparently considered the appropriate venue for all matters of civic importance, military crisis, and arguments about whose turn it was to mend the terrace fence. It was a long room dominated by an ancient brass lever assembly—the interlocking mechanism that had once controlled the junction's switches—and it smelled of oil, dust, and forty years of careful maintenance by a man who clearly believed civilization was a thing you greased weekly.

Bao stood at the lever frame with his arms crossed. Amara leaned against the wall with her hat pushed back, arms also crossed, in what Odile privately thought of as the defensive posture of river people everywhere. Jun sat on a crate, bandaged hand cradled in his lap, listening with the ferocious attention of someone who had grown up being talked *over* and intended never to be again.

"Forty men," Bao said, when Odile finished. He said it without inflection, the way you'd note the weather. "Meridian does not send forty men for a valley they believe is empty."

"No," Odile agreed. "They send forty men for a valley they've been told isn't empty."

All eyes went to her. She'd known they would. She'd been waiting for it since the gunboats came out of the mist, actually—waiting for it for years, if she were honest, carrying the confession around like ballast she kept meaning to jettison at a more convenient moment. There was never a convenient moment. That was the joke of it.

"You should hear this from me," she said, "before someone less gentle tells it to you."

Amara straightened off the wall. "Odile."

"I signed with Meridian nineteen years ago." She said it plainly, because she'd rehearsed plainness in front of a hundred mirrors of dark water. "As a pilot. Survey runs, mostly. Charting tributaries for 'resource assessment.' I was good at it. I was young and my mother needed surgery and they paid triple what the passenger lines paid, and I told myself the same story every company man tells—that the maps were neutral. That a chart doesn't choose what happens to the land it describes."

"And then?" Felix asked quietly.

"And then I charted a tributary that turned out to run through a village. A real one, with real people, who were gone within the year—bought out, moved on, scattered. And I got a bonus." Her mouth twisted. "A *bonus*, Felix. With a little card thanking me for my contribution to regional development. So I burned my remaining charts, walked off the boat at Kessel Bend, and spent the next two decades building a reputation so bad that no respectable company would hire me." She spread her hands. "Congratulations. You've all been traveling with the most unemployable pilot on the river."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

It was Jun who broke it, because of course it was. "So when the radio died," the boy said slowly, working it through, "and you said sabotage—you weren't guessing. You knew what corporate interference looked like."

"I knew what it *smelled* like," Odile said. "Like money moving quietly."

Amara crossed the room and stood beside her—not touching, but close, shoulder to shoulder, facing the others. "I've known her eleven years," Amara said. "She's the only reason I still have a license to lose. Whatever she was, she's been rowing the other direction a long time."

"She didn't tell us," Felix said. Not accusing. Weighing.

"No," Odile admitted. "I didn't. Because I've watched what happens when people learn a thing like this about someone—they stop hearing anything else you say. Every decision you make afterward gets filtered through it." She met his eyes. "I should have told you on the dock. I'm telling you now because the dock is gone and we're all standing in the same flood zone."

Felix looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something she hadn't expected: he almost smiled. "My father hid an entire valley from them for ten years and let his family drown in debt doing it. I think I can extend some professional courtesy to a defected pilot."

"Professional courtesy," Bao repeated, with the ghost of amusement. "The young man negotiates like a stationmaster."

"He's his father's son," Odile said, and saw Felix absorb the blow—or the gift; with that sentence it had always been both.

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing, and Odile learned something about fear that day: it worked better in company.

Bao's people numbered sixty-three—she counted, because counting was what she did instead of panicking—and perhaps a dozen of them had ever held a weapon with intent. But they knew the valley the way Amara knew water, every switchback and slide-path and blind ravine, and Bao organized them with the unhurried competence of a man who had spent decades preventing trains from colliding and saw no reason to change methods now.

"The ridge road," Bao said, tapping the old track bed on Felix's map. "If they come upriver to the station, they must come up the cut. One path, narrow, exposed. My father helped build that cut. He always said it was the only part of the line built properly." A dry pause. "He would be pleased it is finally useful."

Amara took two of the younger ones and went to check the boat—patched hull, half-drowned supplies, engine running rough but running. Odile caught fragments of her voice drifting up from the bank, patient and wry, teaching a girl of maybe fifteen how to read a fuel line. Everyone was teaching someone today. It seemed to be how these people metabolized dread.

Jun, meanwhile, had claimed the signal house's electrical guts—a jury-rigged hydro line off the old sluice wheel, copper and cloth wire and more ingenuity than code—and was rewiring the lamp circuit with his bandaged hand braced against his knee, swearing softly at a connector in two languages.

"You should rest," Odile said from the doorway.

"I rested in June," Jun said, not looking up. "What's the sluice wheel connected to? Actually connected?"

Odile hesitated. "Ask Felix."

"I *did* ask Felix. He got a face like a locked door and went to look at his father's drawings again." The boy glanced up, and beneath the grease smudges his eyes were sharp and scared and steady all at once. "There's something under this town, isn't there. Something big. Everyone keeps almost saying it."

Odile looked at the kid for a while. Sixteen, indentured, escaped, injured, and still the first person in this whole expedition to ask the right question directly instead of circling it like the adults did.

"Yeah," she said. "Something big. Ask him again after dark. He'll be ready by then. Some doors need a minute with the key in hand before you turn them."

Jun nodded slowly, accepting this, and returned to his wiring. Then, without looking up: "You're staying, then. Even after they know about Meridian."

It wasn't a question, but Odile answered it anyway. "I'm staying."

"Good." A pause. A spark jumped; Jun muttered at the connector. "The grandmothers were asking. They approve of you. Apparently you eat like someone who respects food."

In thirty years of river life, Odile Vann had received commendations, death threats, and one marriage proposal delivered via semaphore. None of it had landed quite like that.

* * *

Dusk came down purple and heavy, and with it came the sound.

At first Odile thought it was thunder, but the sky was clear above the ridgeline—clear and enormous, the first clean evening in days, stars already pricking through. No. The sound was rhythmic, mechanical, wrong. Engines. Many engines, running at low power, holding position.

She climbed back to the ridge overlook with Felix, Amara, and Bao, and lay flat among the rocks, and looked down into the gorge.

The gunboats had stopped at the mouth of the narrows, three grey shapes riding their anchor lights, and between them moved smaller craft—longboats, packed with men, ferrying toward the eastern bank where the old portage trail began. Lanterns strung out along the shore like a lit fuse. Somewhere a whistle sounded, sharp and clipped, and the loading quickened.

"They're landing tonight," Amara breathed. "Moving in the dark. They'll be at the cut by midmorning."

"Forty men on the water," Odile murmured, glasses up. "And I count lanterns for at least sixty on shore." She lowered them. "They're not sending a survey team. They're sending an occupation."

Beside her, Felix had gone very still. In the last light she could see him turning the brass compass over in his fingers, once, twice, and she understood suddenly that he wasn't checking it. He was asking it something.

"Bao," he said. "That lever assembly in your signal house. The interlocking. Where does its power come from?"

Bao's eyebrows rose. "The sluice wheel. Same as everything."

"And the sluice itself—" Felix's voice was careful, the voice of a man walking out onto ice he'd only read about. "Your father maintained it. Did he ever say what it was *for*? Not the wheel. The gate beneath it."

The old signalman was quiet a long moment. When he answered, his voice had changed—lower, older, like something brought up from a deep hold.

"My father said the gate was the valley's heart valve," he said. "He said the man who built it left instructions that it should never be opened in anger. Only in necessity." He looked at Felix. "He said the man's name was Sterling."

Below them in the dark, the lanterns along the eastern shore went out, one by one, all at once—as if a hand had closed around the fuse—and the gorge filled with the sound of sixty men beginning to climb.
