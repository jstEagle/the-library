## 15. The Dry Season Clock

Bao kept a clock in the signal house at Junction Nine, and it was the strangest thing Felix had seen since the lamplight moving on the hills.

It hung above the old dispatch desk where a telegraph key had once chattered out schedules to stations that no longer existed—a round brass face, green with age, its hands frozen at eleven minutes past four. But it wasn't broken. Bao wound it every morning. The hands moved. They simply didn't tell the time of day.

"They tell the time of the river," Bao said, catching Felix staring. The old signalman's voice was dry as the paper maps curling on his walls. "My grandfather built it. Dry season, the hand sits low. When the water rises, she climbs." He tapped the glass with one knuckle, gentle as tapping an egg. "Right now she's here." The hand sat at the two o'clock mark, quivering slightly, as if eager. "At peak monsoon, she touches the top. And then—"

He drew his thumb across his throat.

Felix had come to the signal house to think. He'd meant to sit alone with his father's drawings for an hour, maybe two, and instead he'd found himself pulled into Bao's orbit the way everyone in Junction Nine seemed to be—the old man had a gravity about him, quiet and total, like the river itself.

"Destroy them," Felix said. "The boats."

"The boats, the men in them if they're foolish, and everything else the water can reach." Bao lowered himself onto the dispatch stool with the care of a man whose knees had opinions. "You've seen the gorge walls, boy. Stone funnels. When the monsoon peaks, this whole valley becomes a lake for three days. Always has. That's why the railway was built on trestles forty feet high—and why your father's sluice gate matters."

Felix's stomach tightened. "You knew about the gate before I showed you the drawings."

"I helped pour its foundation." Bao said it plainly, without drama, the way he seemed to say everything. "Four of us did. Your father, me, Odile Vann's predecessor, and a mason named Suri who is buried under the fig tree by the schoolhouse. We called it the dry season clock, too, because it only works when the water is high enough to give it teeth. Open it early and you get a flood. Open it at peak—" he spread his hands, "—you get a verdict."

* * *

By evening, the whole crew had gathered in the long room above the junction's mess hall, and Bao stood at a chalkboard that hadn't seen chalk in thirty years, drawing the valley from memory: the river entering through the gorge like a thread through a needle's eye, widening past Junction Nine, splitting into channels among the terraced hills where the survivors grew rice and kept goats and, apparently, hid very well from the outside world.

"Meridian has four gunboats," Amara said. She'd claimed the windowsill and sat there with her boots on the frame, arms around her knees, watching the rain hammer the glass. "I counted from the ridge this morning. Two are armored. One has a crane mount—they mean to dredge or drill, not just look."

"And they have my father's survey maps," Felix said. The words still tasted like ash, though he'd burned nothing yet. "The stolen ones. Every channel marker, every depth reading, every safe anchorage."

"Which means they know the gorge is navigable at current levels," Amara went on. "What they don't know—" and here something flickered across her face, grim satisfaction, the first expression he'd seen her wear that wasn't guarded, "—is what happens in nine days."

"Eight," Bao corrected mildly. "Perhaps seven, if the clouds over the western range keep eating like they are."

Jun, who had been sitting cross-legged on the floor with a disassembled carburetor spread out on a cloth like a surgeon's tray, looked up. "So we've got a week to convince four gunboats full of armed men to turn around and go home. Great. Terrific. Anyone want to start with the polite letter?"

"We tried letters once," Bao said. "Nineteen years ago. Meridian sent a man named Corliss to negotiate purchase of the valley. He stayed six weeks, made notes the whole time, and left. Eight months later their survey crews arrived with the negotiation documents—which turned out to be forged." He set down the chalk. "We closed the line after that. Flooded our own station approach, pulled the rails, let the jungle take it back. It cost us everything we'd built. But it made us invisible."

"Until now," Odile said quietly.

She stood apart from the others, near the door, as she'd stood apart from nearly everything since her confession on the ridge. Felix still wasn't sure how he felt about her—hired by Meridian years ago, defected, then hired by them again under a false pretext to find Junction Nine, except somewhere along the way she'd decided to fail at her job. It was a lot to forgive. It was also, he was learning, a lot to understand.

"Until now," Bao agreed. He looked at her without malice, which somehow made it worse. "But you came back to us, Odile. The ledger balances differently than it would have."

Odile's jaw worked. "The ledger doesn't balance," she said. "It never does. You just keep writing in it anyway."

A silence followed, filled by the rain and the tick of the strange clock downstairs, audible even through the floor, patient as a heartbeat.

Felix cleared his throat. "Bao. Walk me through it. If we do nothing—if Meridian stays past peak water—what actually happens?"

The old man returned to the chalkboard and drew the gorge, narrow and deep, then the valley behind it, broad and shallow-bowled.

"At peak, the river rises perhaps fourteen meters at the gorge mouth. The whole valley floor goes under—our fields, the station, everything below the high terraces. We build for it. We move up, we tie down, we wait it out. Three days, sometimes four, and the water drops." He tapped the gorge. "But gunboats anchored in the gorge when that surge comes down? Fourteen meters of water funneled through stone?" He shook his head. "They will be driven against the walls or capsized. And whatever equipment they've landed in the valley—drills, dredges, stores—it all goes under. Meridian loses its expedition entirely."

"So the river does our work for us," Amara said.

"The river does *its* work," Bao said. "Our problem is that Meridian doesn't know the schedule. If they realize what's coming, they'll pull out in time—or worse, they'll push harder, faster, try to strip what evidence they can find before the water takes it. Evidence of what, exactly?"

"That the line was closed deliberately," Felix said slowly, working it through. "That there's a town here. That my father sabotaged their claims. If they get documents out—Bao, do you have records? Anything written?"

"Some. Enough to make trouble in courts we can't afford to stand in."

"Then we have two problems," Felix said, and heard his own voice steady in a way it hadn't in years. "One: get Meridian out before peak water, or make sure they can't leave with anything. Two: make sure that after the flood, there's nothing left for them to come back for. No maps. No records. No coordinates."

Jun raised a hand like they were in school. "Counterpoint: I'm sixteen and I've already been shot at once this month, so forgive me if this sounds naive—but couldn't we just, I don't know, sink their boats ourselves? Before the flood? Get ahead of the deadline?"

Amara snorted. "With what? Our boat has more patches than hull."

"With the sluice," Jun said, and the room went quiet. They looked at each other, and Felix saw the same thought land in every face at once, heavy as the rain.

"No," Bao said immediately.

"It floods the gorge early," Felix said. "That's what you said. Open it before peak and you get a flood."

"A flood we cannot predict," Bao snapped, and for the first time Felix saw real anger in him, sharp and old. "Boy, that gate was built as a last resort, a door to close, not a weapon to swing. At peak water, the mathematics are known—we've watched this river for sixty years; we know within hours when the surge comes. Open the gate early, at uncertain levels, and you gamble with every life in this valley. The water could backfill the low terraces before anyone reaches high ground. Children. Goats. Grandmothers who refuse to leave their kitchens." He exhaled through his nose. "Your father understood this. Ask me how I know."

"How?" Felix asked quietly.

"Because he wanted to open it nineteen years ago, when Corliss's forged papers surfaced. And I told him no. And he raged at me for a week, and then he thanked me, because we found another way." Bao's voice gentled. "There is almost always another way, if you give yourself time to look for it. Which brings us to the actual question: how much time do we have?"

He led them downstairs to the signal house, where the brass clock ticked its patient river-time, and showed them the logbooks—sixty years of handwritten readings, water heights by date, season by season, in four different hands across three generations.

"Peak water last year: the ninth of the wet moon. Year before: the eleventh. The year of the big storm: the seventh." He ran a cracked fingernail down the columns. "This year, given the rains we've had, I say the eighth. Nine days from now, plus or minus one. After peak, the river owns the gorge for three days. So our window to act—to do anything that requires being on the water, or moving freely—is eight days, and shrinking."

"Eight days," Odile repeated.

"To convince, deceive, or defeat four gunboats," Jun said, counting on ink-stained fingers, "destroy or hide sixty years of records, protect a town of—" she glanced at Bao, "—how many people, actually?"

"One hundred and twelve," Bao said. "And nine goats who believe themselves citizens."

Despite everything, someone laughed. Felix thought it might have been him.

* * *

They worked late into the night, and what emerged from the chalk dust and argument was not a plan, exactly—not yet—but the skeleton of one, joints and sinew waiting for muscle.

Amara took the river. She'd spent the evening sketching the gorge from memory, and now she laid her pencil along the entrance like a blade. "Their boats are anchored inside the mouth, but they'll have sentries upstream. What they don't have is a pilot who knows the water. I do. In eight days I can take our boat up the side channels—they won't be watching the dead water, nobody ever does—and put people wherever we need them. Reconnaissance first. Then options."

"Options meaning sabotage," Odile said.

"Options meaning I haven't decided yet," Amara said, with dignity, "because I'm a professional."

Jun claimed the boat itself. The patch job from the rapids had held, barely, and she announced her intention to rebuild half the engine and reinforce the hull seam "so it stops singing that sad song every time we hit chop." She also volunteered, in the same breath, to rig the signal house's ancient battery bank into something useful, which made Bao regard her with the expression of a man watching a familiar ghost.

"You remind me of your employer's father," he told Felix later, watching Jun work. "She talks with her hands the way he talked with his."

Felix took the maps—or rather, the question of the maps. His father's originals lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath his bunk, and every hour or so he found himself touching the bundle like a talisman, then pulling his hand away. Those drawings were his vindication. Proof that Sterling senior hadn't been a gambler and a fraud, that the debts were the price of a promise kept. Somewhere in a Meridian office, a man was probably studying the stolen copies right now, learning the valley's secrets from lines Felix's father had drawn.

"I need to see what they see," he told Bao. "The copies they stole—I marked depths, anchorages, the trestle footings. If I know exactly what they know, I know what they'll try."

"And the originals?"

Felix was silent a moment. Downstairs, the clock ticked. Outside, the rain kept up its endless work.

"When the time comes," he said finally, "I'll decide."

Bao studied him with eyes gone pale and wise as river stones. "Your father said something similar once. Right before he burned his first set of drawings." A pause. "He cried while he did it. Then he had the best sleep of his life, and told me so at breakfast."

* * *

Near midnight, Felix climbed the ridge path alone, needing air that didn't taste of chalk and lamp oil. The clouds had torn open briefly, and moonlight silvered the valley below—terraces drowned in shadow, the river a black ribbon, and far downstream, at the edge of seeing, the faint lights of the Meridian camp. Four boats. Dozens of men. Nine days of patience between them and everything this valley held.

Odile was already there, of course. She was always where the view was worst and the truth most likely.

"Can't sleep either?" she said, not turning.

"Making lists." He sat beside her on the wet rock. "Things we can do. Things we can't. The second list keeps getting longer."

"That's how you know you're being honest." She was quiet a moment. "When I worked for Meridian, I sat in rooms where men planned exactly this. They talked about the valley like a ledger entry. Assets. Extraction costs. Relocation expenses—that's what they called the town. Relocation." Her laugh had no humor in it. "I told myself the survey was harmless. Paperwork. Everyone tells themselves that. It's how empires get built—one person at a time deciding the map isn't the territory."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to help you defend a place I helped them hunt." She finally looked at him, and in the moonlight her face was stripped of everything professional. "Not for redemption. I don't believe in it. For the ledger. You balance what you can, where you can, while you can."

Below them, faint through the rain-mist, the signal house clock ticked on, marking water instead of hours, counting down a season instead of a day.

Eight days, Felix thought. Maybe seven.

Then he saw it—a light at the Meridian camp, detached from the others, moving. Not toward the boats. Toward the gorge wall. Upward. Someone climbing in the dark, with a lantern shuttered to a sliver, heading for the high ground above the narrows.

"Odile," he said softly. "Tell me that's a patrol."

She was already on her feet, shading her eyes. The light paused at a ledge Felix recognized from Amara's sketches—a vantage point overlooking the entire upper gorge. A good place to watch the river. A better place to measure it.

"That," Odile said, "is someone taking soundings by moonlight. Which means they're not waiting for the survey. Which means—" she turned to him, and the dread in her face needed no lantern, "—they've read your father's maps. All of them. Including the part about the sluice."

Down in the valley, the brass clock ticked patiently toward the top of its dial, and Felix understood, with sudden terrible clarity, that the countdown had just gotten shorter than anyone's arithmetic.
