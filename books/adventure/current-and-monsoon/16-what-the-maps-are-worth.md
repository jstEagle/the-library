## 16. What the Maps Are Worth

The signal house at Junction Nine had once been the beating heart of the valley's railway, and now it was Bao's study, his map room, his temple, and—judging by the smell—his kitchen. Felix sat at a scarred drafting table that had clearly been a dining table first and a drafting table second, surrounded by forty years of dust, lamp oil, and paper.

His father's drawings lay in front of him.

Not the survey copies Felix had carried upriver in an oilskin tube, but the originals—rolled in waxed canvas, tucked into a cavity beneath the signal house floorboards that Bao had opened with the ease of a man who had put things there himself, or watched them being put there, a long time ago.

"Your father said, if anyone ever came asking with that compass," Bao had said, tapping the brass instrument where it sat on the table between them, "show them the floor."

Felix had spent the last two hours doing exactly that, metaphorically. The floor of the drawings. The foundations.

The paper was brittle at the edges and soft in the folds, and the handwriting—his father's handwriting, that narrow, forward-leaning script that always looked like it was in a hurry to get somewhere—covered every margin, every gap, every blank space where a proper engineer would have left room for the draftsman's stamp. Felix knew that handwriting better than he knew his own. He had seen it on debt notices. On apology letters to banks. On the single birthday card Felix had received at nineteen, the last one, which said *Proud of you, kid* and nothing else, because his father had never been a man who spent words where a look would do.

Seeing it here, on drawings of a valley Felix had only known for four days, did something strange to his chest. It was like hearing a familiar voice come from an unfamiliar room.

"You've gone quiet," Jun said.

Jun was sitting cross-legged on a crate across the table, one arm still bandaged from the fight at the bank, supposedly resting, actually holding a wrench and disassembling a lamp with the focus of a surgeon and the guilt of someone who had been told three times to rest and had agreed to it in the way teenagers agree to things, which is to say, with their mouths.

"I'm reading," Felix said.

"You're staring. There's a difference. Staring is when you look at something and it doesn't tell you anything. Reading is when it does." Jun tilted their head. "Which one is it?"

Felix looked down at the drawing again. Sheet eleven of nineteen. A cross-section of the riverbed beneath Junction Nine, drawn in his father's precise, economical lines. And in the margin, in that hurrying script, a note:

*The gate holds. Tell them the gate holds.*

"Tell who?" Felix murmured. "Tell them what gate?"

* * *

Bao found them there an hour later, when the light through the signal house's cracked window had gone the color of weak tea. The old signalman moved slowly now, favoring one hip, but his eyes moved fast, and they went straight to the papers spread across the table like a bird going to a wire.

"You found sheet eleven," he said.

"Is that what it's called?"

"That's what your father called it. He had names for all of them. He'd sit where you're sitting—" Bao lowered himself onto a stool with a grunt, "—right there, and he'd talk to the paper like it was answering him. My wife thought he was mad. My wife thought most engineers were mad, which is fair."

Felix turned sheet eleven toward him. "This cross-section. Under the station. There's a chamber here—" he traced a rectangle in the rock beneath the riverbed, "—that's on no other drawing. And this line here, this shaft, it runs from the chamber up to—" he flipped sheets, found sheet seven, the station foundations, "—here. Beneath the water tower. There's a mechanism. Valves, gearing, a hand-wheel assembly. It's drawn like it's real. Like it was built."

"It was built," Bao said.

Felix looked up.

"Your father built it," Bao said. "With my hands, and four others who are buried up the hill now, and a great deal of swearing in two languages. It took us two dry seasons. He told us we were building a flood relief channel. Which was true, in the way that a lock is a door. Technically true. Useful for lying."

Jun had stopped disassembling the lamp. "A flood relief channel under a station that's already flooded half the time?"

"The valley floods at peak monsoon," Bao said. "Not every year. But in the big years—the years the old people used to name, like storms—this whole gorge fills. The river takes back everything down to the second step of the station platform. We built for it. We built *around* it. And your father built something into it."

Felix's hands had gone still on the paper. "The gate."

"The gate," Bao agreed.

* * *

It took them until well past dark to understand it, working by lamplight, with Jun abandoning all pretense of rest and Bao fetching a second lamp and a third, and Amara appearing in the doorway at some point with two cups of something bitter and hot, saying nothing, just sitting on the stairs and listening in the way river people listen, which is completely and silently.

The mechanism was simple. That was the horror of it, and the beauty. Felix's father had not built anything complicated. He had built something *inevitable*.

A sluice gate, three meters wide, set into the rock wall of a natural cavern beneath the riverbed—a cavern that the dry-season river barely touched, but that the peak monsoon turned into a pressurized throat. In ordinary years, the gate stayed shut and the cavern stayed empty and nobody below Junction Nine ever knew it was there. But the gate was not a dam. It was not meant to hold water back.

It was meant to let it *in*.

"The gorge narrows at the lower bend," Amara said from the stairs, when the shape of it had become clear. She had run that bend a hundred times; Felix had seen her face when she talked about it, the way pilots talk about water they respect. "Canyon walls, no banks, no way to beach a boat. If you opened a gate under the station at peak water—"

"You'd add the whole cavern's volume to the river in under a minute," Felix said. His voice sounded far away to him. "And the cavern fills from the upper catchment, so it's not just volume. It's *head*. Pressure. You'd raise the river through the narrows by—" he did the arithmetic on the back of sheet eleven, because his father would have, "—six meters. Maybe eight."

"Eight meters in the narrows," Amara said. "Nothing floats through that. Nothing steers through that. You'd put a wall of water across the gorge that would take any boat—" she paused, and finished it honestly, because she was Amara, "—any boat and anyone on it, and fold it in half."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the river muttered to itself in the dark, the way it had been doing all week, louder each night, the monsoon climbing toward its peak like a slow animal climbing stairs.

"He built a weapon," Jun said. There was no judgment in it. Just the flat wonder of a mechanic looking at a machine that did exactly what it was designed to do, and wishing it had been designed to do something kinder.

"He built a *choice*," Bao said. "There's a difference. A weapon you use on people. A choice you make about them." He tapped the margin of sheet eleven, on the note Felix had found hours ago. *The gate holds. Tell them the gate holds.* "He was writing to whoever came after him. He hoped it would be you, I think. He never said so. But he checked that mechanism every dry season for eleven years. Oiled it. Tested the gearing. Replaced the hand-wheel chain twice. He tended that gate the way other men tend graves."

Felix looked at his hands. They were his father's hands, or close enough—broad, scarred at the knuckles, ink-stained in the creases. Hands that had spent years signing papers that gave away everything the family owned, paying down debts that Felix had assumed were the residue of failure. Of weakness. Of a man who had bet on the wrong things and lost.

And here was sheet eleven, in the same hands' handwriting, saying otherwise.

"Tell me about the debts," Felix said. It came out rougher than he intended.

Bao was quiet a moment. The lamp flame leaned in some draft from the broken window, and his face went in and out of shadow.

"He came to us with money he didn't have," Bao said. "That's the short version. The long version is that Meridian's surveyors found the upper valley in the year your father arrived. Copper, and under the copper, rarer things. They needed a rail line to take it out. Your father was their engineer—he'd built half their mountain lines. And then he stood where we're sitting, and he looked at the valley, and he looked at the people in it, and he went back downriver and told Meridian the gorge was unrunnable. That the rail survey was a dead end. That the maps were wrong."

"And they believed him?"

"For a while. Long enough for him to close the line—officially, with paperwork, with the *right* paperwork, the kind that takes years and lawyers and money. He spent everything getting the closure certified. Every lien he could raise against himself, against the house, against his own name. Because a closed line with a certified closure order is a legal fortress. Meridian couldn't touch it without the order being overturned, and the order was built on his surveys, and his surveys said the gorge was impossible." Bao's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "He lied in ink, your father. The most permanent kind of lie."

Felix's throat was tight. "The debts. The debts were the closure. The lawyers, the fees—"

"Every coin," Bao said. "He told me once, laughing, that he was the richest poor man he knew. That he owned nothing and had bought a valley with the nothing." The almost-smile faded. "Then Meridian sent new surveyors. Ones who didn't take his word. Ones who started measuring the gorge themselves, from boats, in secret. Your father went downriver to intercept the last of them. To buy their maps, or steal them, or talk them out of it—I never learned which. The river took him at the lower bend, in high water. The same bend the gate would flood."

Felix had heard the story of his father's death a hundred times, in a hundred flat official versions: *lost to the river, accident, high water, no body recovered*. He had spent eleven years carrying the debts of that accident like stones in each pocket, believing his father had drowned in failure, in some last desperate gamble to pay what he owed.

He had drowned keeping a promise. The debts *were* the promise. Every notice on the kitchen table, every bank letter, every year of Felix's scraped and borrowed life—it had all been the price of this valley staying hidden, and Felix had paid it without ever knowing he was paying for anything at all.

He put his palm flat on sheet eleven, over the little rectangle of the gate chamber, and felt—irrationally, undeniably—like his father had put his hand on the other side.

* * *

They talked until the lamps burned low. Bao told him about the eleven years of maintenance. Amara, from the stairs, asked the questions Felix was too full to ask—practical ones, pilot's questions. How fast the cavern filled. Where the catchment fed from. What the hand-wheel needed to turn it—two people, Bao said, maybe three, at the gearing, and the chain was new enough to hold.

Jun, who had been quiet for a long time, finally set down the wrench and said, "There's a problem."

"There's always a problem," Amara said. "It's why they pay us."

"Nobody's paying us," said Jun.

"Then it's why we do it."

Jun pointed at the drawings with their chin. "The gate chamber floods the narrows, right? Kills anything on the river during peak water. But Meridian's boats aren't at the narrows yet. They're in the lower gorge, and they're coming up. And the peak is—" they looked at Amara.

"Two days," Amara said. "Maybe three. If we're lucky, and the river hasn't been lucky to us yet."

"Right. Two days. So here's the problem." Jun's voice was steady, but their bandaged hand had curled into a fist. "If we open the gate *now*, we flood the narrows and the lower gorge, and we kill Meridian's people. Not their boats. Their *people*. Pilots, deckhands—some of them probably indentured like I was, some of them probably just poor and hired. And if we wait until they're at the narrows, we have to stand at Junction Nine and watch them come, and hope we can hold them off at the ridge with—" they gestured vaguely at everything, at the whole valley, "—*this*."

The lamp hissed. Nobody answered right away, because there was nothing cheap to answer with.

"Your father knew this too," Bao said quietly. "That's why he built it as a gate and not a trap. A trap you spring. A gate you *choose*." He looked at Felix. "He never opened it. Eleven years he tended it, and never once used it, because using it meant drowning men in a canyon, and he couldn't make himself into the kind of man who does that. Even for this. Even for us." He tapped the margin note one last time. *Tell them the gate holds.* "He wasn't telling them the gate works. He was telling them they didn't have to use it. That holding it was enough. That the valley was safe as long as one person was willing to stand at the wheel and *not turn it*."

Felix looked down at the drawings, at the neat inevitable lines of the mechanism, at the margin notes in the handwriting that had paid for all of this with everything it had.

Awe came first—awe at the foresight of it, the patience, a man spending a decade oiling a machine he prayed he'd never use, the way a good pilot studies a rapids she hopes never to run. And underneath the awe, cold and rising, the terror. Because the peak was two days out, and Meridian's gunboats were in the gorge, and the choice his father had refused for eleven years was sitting in Felix's chest like swallowed river water, and he did not yet know which kind of man he was going to be.

He gathered the sheets together with hands that were not quite steady and rolled them into the waxed canvas.

"We need to see the gate," he said. "Tonight, if the rain gives us an hour. I'm not standing at that wheel in two days' time not knowing if it turns."

Bao nodded slowly and reached for his coat. "The catchment path is flooded to the knee by now."

"Then we'd better hurry," Amara said, already on her feet, "before it's flooded to the neck."

Outside, the rain had paused—the monsoon's false mercy, its held breath—and the river below Junction Nine was climbing the station steps, one step, then two, muttering in the dark like something counting.

Three more, Felix thought. Three more steps, and then the valley, and the gate, and the choice.

He took the drawings, and the compass, and went down into the wet dark with his father's handwriting against his chest.
