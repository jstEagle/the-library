## 17. Into the Gorge

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, but the river hadn't noticed. It kept rising, brown and muscular, shoving at the banks like a drunk trying to get into his own house.

Amara stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Mercy*—a name she'd painted on the hull herself, years ago, back when the boat belonged to her and not to her failures—and watched the gorge open ahead of them like a throat.

"You're sure about this," Felix said. He wasn't asking. He'd learned that about her by now. When Amara was sure, she didn't ask questions; when she wasn't, she asked them so fast you couldn't answer any of them.

"I'm sure it's the only way," she said. "That's different from sure."

"Comforting."

"I'm not in the comfort business." She checked the throttle, ran her thumb along the brass fitting the way another woman might touch a rosary. "I'm in the getting-us-there-alive business, and business is booming."

Behind them, on the engine deck, Jun was already at work. The kid had wrapped a bandage around their forearm that morning with the neatness of someone who'd done field dressings before, which raised questions Amara had decided not to ask until after they all survived. Now Jun crouched over the patched engine, one hand flat against the housing, reading its vibration like a fortune-teller reading palms.

"She's running hot," Jun called up. "But she'll hold. Probably."

"Probably," Felix repeated.

"That's the best offer on the table," Jun said. "You want certainty, take up religion."

Amara laughed despite herself—a short, sharp bark that surprised even her. That was the thing about the kid. Two days ago Jun had been bleeding on the bank while a Meridian spy swam away with their maps, and today Jun was joking about the engine that would either carry them through the gorge or scatter them across it like chaff. Either the kid had no sense of self-preservation, or—

Or courage was just fear that had decided to get to work.

She knew something about that.

* * *

They gathered at mid-morning in the cabin, because Bao had insisted on a plan before he'd let them take the boat, and Bao's insistence had the weight of a man who'd spent forty years moving trains and knew exactly what happened when things left the rails.

"The sluice house sits at the narrows," he said, spreading a hand-drawn chart on the table—the only map left in the valley now, since Felix had burned the rest. His finger traced the river's course, the lines confident, the annotations in three faded inks. "Half a day upstream if the water were kind. It isn't kind. It hasn't been kind since the rains started."

"How bad?" Felix asked.

Bao looked at him for a long moment. "When we closed the line, your father wanted the approach flooded deliberately. I told him no one could pilot those rapids under monsoon water." He tapped the chart. "He said, 'No one alive today.' He meant you, boy. He meant the people who grew up on this river."

"I didn't grow up on this river."

"No," Bao agreed. "But you're traveling with someone who did."

Every head turned to Amara. She felt the weight of it settle on her shoulders like wet canvas.

"I ran this stretch twice a season for six years," she said. "Before." Before the disgrace. Before the inquiry. Before the name Osei became a cautionary tale told in riverfront bars. "The channel's changed since then. The flood has moved every rock I used to know. But rocks don't move far. They just move."

"So you can do it," Jun said.

"So I can try," Amara corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is that one of those words doesn't get people killed."

Felix leaned over the chart. She watched him study it—not the way engineers read drawings, all angles and tolerances, but the way sons read handwriting. His father had drawn this. His father had marked the narrows with a small, careful X and written beside it, in that cramped script Amara had seen on the burned maps: *The gate remembers.*

"What does that mean?" Felix asked quietly.

"I don't know," Bao said. "Your father said a lot of things I didn't understand until later. Usually much later. Usually while standing in water."

* * *

They cast off at noon, because Odile had climbed to the ridge at first light and come back down with news: gunboats in the lower gorge, three of them, crawling upstream behind the stolen maps like wolves following a blood trail.

"They'll reach the narrows by tomorrow evening," Odile reported. Her face was gray with more than weather. "If they find the sluice house before we do—"

"They won't need to find it," Felix finished. "They'll need to keep us from using it. And they've got guns."

"And we've got Jun," Amara said.

Jun looked up from coiling rope. "I'm flattered and terrified in equal measure."

"Good balance," Amara said. "Hold onto both."

The plan, such as it was: Amara would run the boat up the drowned approach to the narrows. Felix would go ashore where the old maintenance track touched the water—a shelf of rock barely wider than the boat—and climb to the sluice house. Jun would stay with the engine, keeping the *Mercy* alive and ready for extraction. Odile and half of Bao's people would hold the ridge above Junction Nine, buying time with rifles and stubbornness if Meridian came early.

It was a terrible plan. Amara had survived thirty years on the river by recognizing terrible plans, and this one had all the hallmarks: too few people, too little time, too much water.

"We could wait," Odile offered, without conviction.

"For what?" Felix asked. "For the valley to flood? For Meridian to dig in?" He shook his head. "My father spent his whole life building a door and hiding the key. I'm not going to hand the key to the people he built the door against."

Nobody argued. There are arguments that happen out loud, and there are arguments that end before they start, and this was the second kind.

* * *

The first hour was almost gentle. The river widened, slowed, let them believe in themselves.

Then the gorge took them.

Amara felt it before she saw it—a change in the water's voice, a deepening of its growl, the way the current began pulling the bow sideways as though the river had opinions about their destination. She eased the throttle forward and put both hands on the wheel.

"Talk to me, engine," she murmured.

"She hears you," Jun called from below. "She says she's doing her best."

"Tell her her best is about to matter."

The gorge walls rose on either side, black stone streaked white with runoff, close enough that she could have thrown a bottle against either one. The channel between them boiled. What had been rapids yesterday was a full riot today—water standing in waves where no wind blew, holes opening and closing like mouths, the whole surface flexing as if something enormous breathed beneath it.

And somewhere under all of it, the rocks. The ones she'd known once, the ones that had moved.

"There!" Felix pointed. "Left side—the eddy behind that spur!"

"I see it." She didn't, quite. She saw the water behind the spur folding wrong, which meant the rock was there whether she could see it or not. She swung the wheel hard, felt the hull heel, heard cargo shift below, and threaded the gap between the standing wave and the boil with maybe a meter to spare on each side.

The *Mercy* shuddered, straightened, and drove on.

"Nice," Jun said, appearing at the companionway with grease to the elbows and a look of profound respect.

"Don't compliment me yet. Compliments make me nervous."

"Everything makes you nervous."

"Yes, but compliments make me nervous *at speed*."

* * *

The second hour was not gentle at all.

The channel narrowed until the gorge was less a canyon than a slot, and the river, having nowhere to spread, stood up instead. Waves came at them from directions waves should not come from—one lifted the stern and slewed them broadside toward a wall of black stone, and for three full seconds Amara looked at the rock face sliding closer and thought, with strange calm, *so this is how the story ends, and I never did fix the name on the transom.*

"Hard aport!" Felix shouted, useless, because she was already doing it, because her hands had been doing it since before the wave finished forming, some older self waking up in her arms and taking the wheel.

The hull kissed the rock—a kiss, not a crash, a long grinding scrape along the guard timber that Jun had insisted on bolting on two days ago with scavenged tin and pure spite—and then the current released them and they shot through into a stretch of relative calm, everyone breathing like they'd been underwater.

Jun came up the companionway two steps at a time, pale under the tan, holding a wrench like a weapon.

"Hull check," Amara ordered.

"One scrape, no breach. The guard timber took it." Jun exhaled. "I'm going to go have feelings about that privately."

"Take five. Then check the fuel line. We're not done."

Felix stayed at the rail, knuckles white, watching the water. When he spoke, he didn't look at her. "You knew that rock was there."

"I guessed."

"You steered like you knew."

"My hands knew. My hands ran this river when I was younger than Jun." She rolled her shoulders, working out the ache. "Turns out disgrace doesn't uninstall anything."

Felix almost smiled. "My father used to say something like that. About the sabotage. He said, 'They can take the line away from you, but they can't take the line out of you.'"

"He sounds like he was fun at parties."

"He was terrible at parties. He just stood near the food and talked about culverts."

"Now *that's* a legacy worth protecting."

* * *

They made the landing at four in the afternoon, and it nearly killed them.

The maintenance shelf was exactly as Bao had described it: a shelf of rock, barely wider than the boat, half-drowned, with the old track bed climbing away from it in switchbacks cut into the gorge wall. Getting alongside it in that current was like docking a horse next to a stampede.

Amara brought the *Mercy* in stern-first, using the engine against the flow, letting the river push them onto the shelf rather than fighting it. The hull ground against stone. Jun threw a line around an iron mooring ring older than anyone aboard, and for one suspended moment everything held—boat, rock, rope, people—while Felix stepped across the gap onto the shelf with a coil of rope over his shoulder and the compass in his pocket.

He turned back at the edge.

"Two hours," he said. "If I'm not back in two hours—"

"We'll come find you," Amara said.

"—you should run downstream and warn the others."

"We'll come find you," Amara repeated, in the tone that ended discussions.

Felix looked at her, then at Jun, and something moved across his face that she recognized, because she'd seen it in mirrors for years: the expression of a person who had expected to die alone and was deeply inconvenienced to discover they had reasons to live instead.

"Two hours," he agreed, and climbed.

* * *

With Felix gone, the gorge got louder. Or maybe Amara just had room to hear it now.

She tied off extra lines, double-anchored the stern, and then stood in the middle of her boat in the failing light and let her hands shake.

Not much. Just enough. Enough that she hid them in her pockets and leaned against the wheelhouse and watched the water and admitted, silently, in the privacy of her own skull, that she was terrified. Not of dying—she'd made peace with that years ago, in a hearing room, in a bar, in a hundred small surrenders. She was afraid of the other thing. The thing where you survive, and survive, and survive, and then finally, at the end, you're asked to be brave, and you find out the tank is empty.

Footsteps on the deck. Jun, settling beside her without a word, offering a thermos cap of tea. The kid's bandage had soaked through at the edge; the face above it was drawn but steady.

"Your hands are shaking," Jun observed.

"Observant."

"Felix's were too, when he climbed up. Saw them on the rope." Jun sipped tea. "Odile's probably shaking somewhere on the ridge right now. Bao's hands shook for a week after the line closed, he told me. Everybody's shake."

"Doesn't make the steering better."

"Makes it honest, though." Jun looked out at the boiling channel. "My uncle worked indenture crews. Said the company men never shook. Said that was the scariest part of them." A pause. "I'd rather be scared and on this side of the water."

Amara took the tea. It was terrible—Jun made tea like someone defusing a bomb, all haste and no patience—but it was hot, and it was offered, and she drank it.

"When this is over," she said, "I'm teaching you proper river work. Real piloting. None of this engine-grease nonsense."

Jun grinned. "Is that a job offer?"

"It's a threat. Take it or leave it."

"Oh, I'm taking it." The kid drained the thermos cap and stood, brushing off trousers. "Somebody's got to keep you honest at the wheel. You steer like a woman being chased."

"I *am* a woman being chased."

"Yeah, well." Jun headed for the engine hatch, pausing at the top. "After tomorrow, maybe we chase something back."

* * *

Night came down into the gorge like a lid.

Amara kept the single lamp lit low, enough to watch the lines and the waterline and nothing more. Up the cliff, somewhere above them, a light appeared—small, steady, moving with purpose along the old track bed. Felix, climbing. She tracked it until the gorge wall bent it out of sight.

Below them, far down the black throat of the river, another light appeared.

It didn't move with purpose. It moved with confidence—two lights, actually, riding low on the water, coming up the channel they'd come up, at a pace no honest boat would attempt at night.

Jun emerged at the sound of her breath changing. Followed her gaze. Went still.

"Gunboats," Amara said quietly. "They found the maps' shortcut. They're running the night water."

"That's insane. Nobody runs this at night."

"Somebody does," Amara said. "Somebody who thinks the river belongs to them." She felt the fear again, the empty-tank fear—but underneath it now, something else, something with edges. She put her hands on the wheel, and they were still shaking, and she let them.

"Jun," she said. "Wake the engine. Whatever happens on that cliff tonight—we're not leaving without him. And we're not leaving slow."

Above them, the small light on the track bed flickered, steadied, and climbed on. Below them, the gunboats came on through the dark, patient as debt.

Amara watched them both, hands trembling on the wheel, and held her position.
