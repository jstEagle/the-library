## 18. Peak Water

The sluice house smelled of rust and river and forty years of waiting.

Felix stood in the doorway, rain hammering the tin roof above him like a thousand impatient fingers, and let himself have exactly three seconds to feel the size of what he'd found. Three seconds. That was all the fear was going to get. He'd learned that much from Amara on the rapids: you could be terrified, but you had to be terrified on a schedule, because the river didn't wait and neither did the water.

The controls were exactly where his father's drawings said they would be. A great iron wheel, taller than Felix, bolted to a housing of concrete gone green with moss. Beside it, a smaller brass crank—brass, always brass, his father had trusted brass the way other men trusted God—and a panel of levers labeled in handwriting Felix knew better than his own. The letters were cramped and slanted left, the *S*s like little snakes, the ink faded to the color of weak tea.

*In case of emergency, turn the wheel first,* the label said. *Then pray. Then the crank.*

Felix laughed. It came out of him ragged and surprised, half sob, and the rain swallowed it.

"You old liar," he said to a man eleven years dead. "You left instructions."

* * *

Above him, through the sluice house's one window—a slot of glass so thick with grime it turned the world the color of old beer—Felix could see the ridge where Odile and Bao's people had taken their positions. Lightning walked the ridgeline in long white strides, and in the flashes he caught glimpses of them: Bao's signalmen with their ancient rifles, the women with the fish spears, Odile crouched behind a fallen signal post with a hunting rifle that looked older than she was and, knowing Odile, probably was.

The plan had been explained to him twice, once by Bao in the careful English of a man who had spent decades choosing his words, and once by Jun, who had drawn it in the mud with a stick and added explosions for emphasis that Bao had firmly crossed out.

*The Meridian boats land at the old freight dock,* Bao had said. *They must come up the switchback trail to reach the town. The trail passes under the ridge. We hold the ridge. You hold the water.*

*And if the ridge doesn't hold?* Felix had asked.

Bao had looked at him with those eyes like river stones, unbothered, bottomless. *Then the water holds longer than we do. That is why it goes last.*

* * *

The first thunder of the morning had not been thunder at all.

Felix had been in the sluice house maybe twenty minutes, learning the wheel's weight, finding the sweet spot in the crank's resistance, when the sound rolled up the gorge—deep, rhythmic, wrong. An engine. Not one engine. Three, maybe four, the sound bouncing off the canyon walls until it seemed to come from everywhere, from underground, from inside his own ribs.

He'd gone to the window and seen them coming up the gorge: gunboats, black-hulled, riding low and heavy, their searchlights cutting cones through the rain. Meridian's mark—a stylized *M* like a mountain range—was painted on each bow, and the sight of it did something to Felix's chest that he hadn't expected. Not fear. Something colder and older. His father had spent his last years running from that mark. Had died with its paperwork stacked on his desk like a verdict.

Now the boats were at the freight dock. Now men in gray rain slickers were pouring off them onto the rotted planks, and the planks held, because of course they did—his father's generation had built things to hold—and now they were forming up at the base of the switchback trail, and somewhere up there in the rain and lightning, Odile was counting them through a scope and muttering numbers into a dead radio out of pure habit.

Felix put his hand on the wheel.

And stopped.

Because the wheel was not the problem. The wheel had never been the problem. The problem was power. His father's drawings had been clear as a sermon: the sluice gate ran on electric winches, backed up by a hand crank that required—Felix had done the arithmetic twice, hoping he'd been wrong both times—somewhere between four and six hours of continuous turning to raise the gate without power. Four to six hours. Peak water was coming tonight. Meridian was coming now.

*The town has a generator,* Bao had said. *The signal house. It runs the lights at festival time. It has not run in nine years.*

*Then we'd better hope Jun's as good as she thinks she is,* Amara had said, and Jun, who was standing right there, had gone pink with fury and pride in equal measure and said, *I'm better,* which was the most Jun sentence ever constructed.

* * *

Jun was, at this moment, half a mile up the trail at the signal house, with a bandage around her forearm where the spy's knife had found her, a toolkit older than she was, and a generator that hadn't turned over since before she was born. Felix had wanted to argue—had opened his mouth to argue—and Jun had looked at him with an expression he was beginning to know very well, the one that said *I have been underestimated by every adult in my life and I have outlived all of their opinions*, and he had closed his mouth.

*Bring her back to life,* he'd told her instead. *And then bring the power down the old signal line to the sluice house. My father's drawings show a junction box. Green door. Can't miss it.*

*Everything you say is "can't miss it,"* Jun had said, shouldering her toolkit. *You know what I missed this morning? Breakfast. Because somebody said we'd eat before the boats came, and then the boats came.*

*Jun—*

*I know.* And her voice had gone quiet, and she'd touched the compass in his jacket pocket through the fabric, a quick gesture, almost shy. *I know. Bring her back to life. Go.*

* * *

Now the rain came down in sheets and the Meridian men started up the switchback trail, and Felix did the only thing left to do, which was to be where he said he'd be, doing what he said he'd do, and trust.

The trail climbed in long switchbacks below the ridge, and the ridge was where Odile waited, and Felix had seen Odile shoot the cap off a bottle at eighty yards while complaining that the bottle was too easy a target. The first shots came down like the sky cracking open—three of them, spaced, deliberate, the rhythm of a woman who did not waste bullets—and then the whole ridge lit up with muzzle flash and the Meridian formation folded in on itself like a hand closing.

They did not retreat. That was the terrible thing Felix learned in the next hour, crouched at the sluice controls with the wheel cold under his hands: they did not retreat. They spread out, they went to ground, they returned fire with the flat mechanical patience of men who were paid by the hour and had been told the prize at the top was worth every one of them. The gorge filled with the stutter and echo of gunfire until Felix couldn't tell how many guns were firing or from where, only that the ridge was still answering, still holding, and that every answer was one bullet closer to gone.

He checked the junction box again. Green door, exactly as drawn. Inside, a brass knife switch the size of his forearm, wired back along the old signal line toward the signal house, toward Jun.

*Come on, Jun,* he whispered. *Come on, come on, come on.*

* * *

On the ridge, Odile Vann had fought in three conflicts and one marriage, and she would tell you, if you bought her a drink and asked nicely, that the marriage had been worse, because at least in a war you knew whose side everyone was on.

She lay behind the fallen signal post with rain running off the brim of her hat in a steady stream and counted Meridian men the way other people counted sheep. Thirty-one at the dock. Twenty-two on the trail now. Nine still at the boats, and those nine were the ones that worried her, because the ones who stay with the boats are the ones with orders to burn everything if the assault fails.

*Hold the ridge,* Bao had said, and Bao had not asked her how, because Bao was a gentleman and also because Bao had watched her shoot and had apparently decided the question was beneath them both.

Beside her, one of Bao's signalmen—a grandmother named Sefu who handled a rifle with the tenderness of a woman rocking a cradle—fired, worked the bolt, fired again. *They keep coming,* Sefu observed, in the tone of someone remarking on the weather.

*They're paid to,* Odile said.

*You were paid to, once.*

Odile's breath caught, just slightly. Sefu fired again and did not look at her, and Odile understood that the remark was not an accusation. It was a fact, laid out flat, the way Bao's people laid everything out. You had been paid to. You were not now. The river did not ask what you were; it asked what you were doing.

*Left flank,* Odile said instead. *Three of them working up under the rock overhang. Tell the spears.*

Sefu whistled twice, low, and somewhere in the rain to their left, fish spears became something else entirely.

* * *

The power came at the worst possible moment, which Felix would later decide was the only moment it ever came at all.

He was watching the trail through the beer-colored window when the ridge line went quiet—not stopped, but changed, the gunfire thinning, and he knew with a sick lurch what that meant. Odile's people were running low, or falling back to their second line, or worse. The Meridian men were moving again, up the switchbacks, in the open now, confident, and at their head was a figure in a rain slicker that was not gray like the others but black, and even at this distance Felix knew the cut of a man who dressed like the company paid him to be better than the company.

Then the junction box hummed.

It was not a dramatic sound. It was a hum, low and fat and wonderful, like a hive of very contented bees, and the brass knife switch began to glow faintly at its contacts, and every hair on Felix's arms stood up under his wet sleeves.

*Yes,* he said, to Jun, to the rain, to nobody. *Yes!*

He threw the switch.

The great winch above him shuddered, coughed, and turned. The wheel spun on its axle with a groan like a ship waking from a long sleep, and somewhere beneath the concrete floor, down in the dark throat of the gorge, a gate that had been closed for forty years began, inch by screaming inch, to rise.

* * *

Jun, at the signal house, was not having a beautiful moment.

She was having the kind of moment mechanics have nightmares about: a generator that had sat dead for nine years, in a building with a leaking roof, with rain coming through the hole in that roof directly onto her work, and a fuel line that had turned to something resembling licorice. She had bypassed the line with a rubber hose from the fire bucket. She had cleaned the carburetor with the last of the kerosene and half her shirt sleeve. She had cranked the starting handle forty-one times, counting, because counting kept the panic in a jar where she could watch it.

On crank forty-two, the generator caught, backfired with a bang that knocked her backward into a shelf of ancient signal lamps, and roared into life with the smug, unbroken rhythm of a machine that knew it was better than everyone who had doubted it.

*Yeah, yeah,* Jun told it, getting up, cradling her bandaged arm, grinning so hard her face hurt. *You're a genius. We're all geniuses. Come on.*

She'd run the power out along the signal line—old copper, mostly sound, one break she'd found and twisted back together with numb fingers in the pouring rain—and when the knife switch down at the sluice house threw, she felt it through the wire itself, a little shiver in the copper, and she whooped loud enough that Bao's people on the ridge probably heard it over the gunfire.

Then the lights of the signal house came on.

All of them. Every lamp Bao's people had saved for festival, every bulb that still had a filament, and the old signal tower above the house lit up in sequence—red, amber, green, red, amber, green—running its ancient pattern up into the storm like a lighthouse that had found its sea again.

Jun stood in the doorway and watched it, rain streaming down her face, and for one whole second she let herself be sixteen and amazed.

Then she saw the black-slickered man on the trail stop, and look up at the signal tower, and turn to his men, and point—not at the tower.

At the sluice house.

* * *

Felix heard them coming before he saw them: boots on the gravel path, four sets, maybe five, moving fast, no longer patient. The gate was rising—through the floor grating he could hear the water beginning to move beneath the station, a deep gathering roar like the gorge clearing its throat—but it was not rising fast enough. The winch turned and turned and the river's patience was measured in minutes and Meridian's boots were measured in seconds.

He looked at the hand crank. Four to six hours.

He looked at the window. Rain, lightning, and the black slicker coming up the path with three men behind him.

Felix did the arithmetic one more time, and this time the answer was simple: there was no arithmetic. There was only the wheel and the crank and the gate, and him, and whatever he was willing to be in the next ten minutes.

He put both hands on the wheel and threw his weight into it, adding his strength to the winch, and the gate groaned a little higher, and the water beneath the floor roared a little louder, and the door of the sluice house banged open behind him.

*Step away from the controls, Sterling.*

He knew the voice. Of course he knew the voice. Hollis, the third man, the spy, the one who had eaten their food and laughed at Jun's jokes and mapped their trust the way he'd mapped the valley—for the company, for the *M* on the boats, for whatever they'd promised him. He stood in the doorway with rain pouring off him and a pistol held low and steady, and behind him the lightning lit the gorge like the flashbulb of history.

*The gate's opening either way,* Felix said. His voice sounded strange to him. Calm. His father's voice, maybe. *You can help me turn the wheel or you can watch.*

*Meridian spent two million dollars finding this valley,* Hollis said. *You think one gate—*

*It's not one gate.* Felix turned the wheel another quarter turn, and beneath them the roar deepened, doubled, and he saw Hollis's eyes flick down to the grating, and saw the first flicker of something that wasn't confidence. *It's the whole gorge. My father built it that way. You're standing on his work. We all are.*

*Last chance,* Hollis said, and raised the pistol—

—and the ridge answered.

Not with a rifle shot. With the signal tower. Jun, God bless her, had gotten into the old signal system, and the tower's lamps went from their festival pattern to a single insistent flash—red, red, red, red—the universal code Bao's people still used for one word, one warning, one truth that had kept the valley alive for forty years:

*Flood. Flood. Flood. Flood.*

And down on the river, the nine men who had stayed with the boats looked up at the tower, and then at the water, which had begun—gently, terribly, the way all of his father's work did things—to rise.

Hollis hesitated. One second. Half of one.

It was enough. Odile's rifle spoke from the ridge, once, precise, and the pistol spun out of Hollis's hand and into the dark, and Felix turned the wheel with everything he had left, and beneath the sluice house the gate came up out of its forty-year sleep, and the monsoon came down to meet it, and the gorge began to fill.

Felix stood at the controls of his father's last work, in the roar and the lightning, with the water rising and the valley's fate turning under his hands—and realized, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that the gate was not the last thing his father had built to be opened.

The drawings had shown him one more thing. A small door, at the back of the sluice house, marked with a single word in that cramped left-slanting hand.

*Burn here.*
