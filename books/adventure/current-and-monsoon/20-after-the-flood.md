## 20. After the Flood

The river took three days to say sorry.

That was how Jun put it, lying on her back on the patched deck with one arm splinted and the other throwing pebbles at a tin cup. "Rivers don't apologize," she said. "They just get embarrassed and pretend nothing happened."

Amara Osei had piloted water for twenty-two years, and she had never heard it said better. The Kessume was dropping a hand's width every morning now, the brown fury of peak water settling into something older and more patient, the way a man who has shouted all night finally sits down and rubs his face. Where the flood had torn at the banks, it left gifts: whole trees laid out like offerings, silt so rich it looked edible, and in the shallows below Junction Nine, the sad bright confetti of Meridian—a shattered barge rail, a fuel drum split open like a seed pod, one rubber boot standing upright in the mud as if waiting for its owner to come back and claim it.

Nobody was coming back for the boot.

From the pilot's chair on the *Magpie's Rest*—a name that had stopped being ironic somewhere around the third day of rain—Amara could see down the gorge to where the gunboats had been. Two of them were gone entirely, swallowed or fled; the third sat heeled over on a gravel bar two miles downstream, listing like a drunk leaning on a fence, its crew reduced to figures that moved slowly and did not wave. Every few hours a helicopter came up from the south, hovered at the mouth of the gorge as if reading a sign it didn't like, and went away again.

"They'll be back," Odile had said, the first evening after the water peaked. She'd said it without drama, the way she reported everything, which Amara had learned was either cowardice or honesty and had decided, provisionally, was honesty.

"Back for what?" Felix had asked. He'd been sitting by the sluice house door with his hands still smelling of lamp oil, though he'd scrubbed them twice. "The maps are ash."

"Back because men like that don't lose things," Odile said. "They misplace them, and then they hire someone else to look." Her eyes had flicked to Amara when she said it—not an accusation, not anymore, but a recognition between people who had both, at different times, been the someone else.

But that was three days ago, and today the sun was out, and the valley smelled like wet green growing things, and Amara had decided that doom could wait until after breakfast.

* * *

Breakfast at Junction Nine was an event that had grown since the flood. Bao's people had come down from the hills in ones and twos while the water fell, and now there were forty-some souls in the drowned station's lower yard, and Bao ran the cook fire with the same authority he'd once run a signal box, pointing his cane at pots and pronouncing them ready or not ready with finality.

Amara carried her bowl to the wall of the old station house, where the flood line showed as a dirty stripe four feet up the brick, and sat with her back against warm stone. Felix dropped down beside her a minute later, moving like a man whose body had filed a formal complaint. There was a burn wrap on his left forearm where the sluice housing had scalded him, and he kept touching it absently, the way you touch a bruise to confirm it's yours.

"You slept," she observed.

"Four hours."

"In a row?"

"There may have been a dream in the middle. Hard to say." He blew on his tea. Below them, Jun was holding court by the cook fire despite the splinted arm, demonstrating something with a spoon and a length of wire to an audience of three children from the hill families. Even from here Amara could hear the laugh—one bark, surprised out of her, followed immediately by the suspicious frown of a teenager who has been caught enjoying herself.

"She hasn't left the boat," Amara said. "Not really. She sleeps aboard. Eats fast. Comes back."

"I noticed."

"You going to talk to her about it, or are we doing this the usual way, where everybody pretends not to notice until it fixes itself?"

Felix considered his tea. "The usual way has a mixed record."

"The usual way is why we're all here instead of dead in the gorge," Amara said. "I'm not knocking it. I'm just saying somebody should say the thing out loud eventually. It's how crews become crews. Somebody says the thing."

"And what's the thing?"

Amara watched Jun hand the wire contraption to the smallest child, who received it like a knighthood. "That she's ours now. That nobody asked her and nobody's going to make her go back, and if Meridian comes looking for an indentured mechanic, they'll have to file a complaint with the entire population of this valley." She shrugged. "Something like that. I'm a pilot, not a poet."

Felix was quiet for a moment. Then: "Her indenture papers burned with the advance team's supplies. Officially, Jun Okonkwo doesn't exist anymore."

"That's cold, Sterling."

"It's practical. There's no record. There's no company with a claim. There's just—" he gestured vaguely with the mug, taking in the yard, the smoke, the children, the wrecked and stubborn boat—"us."

"Somebody should tell her that before she decides we're just tolerating her."

"Somebody should tell *her*?" Felix raised an eyebrow. "You've been teaching her river signs since day four. You let her take the wheel through the Narrows."

"She was better than me through the Narrows and we both know it."

"We don't both know it. I was below decks being seasick."

"Then take my word for it, engineer. I don't give compliments." Amara drained her bowl. "Fine. I'll say the thing. But if I do it, you have to be standing there looking supportive, because I've seen your supportive face and it needs work."

* * *

She found the moment that afternoon, because moments like that don't come to you—you have to ambush them.

Jun was in the engine hold, naturally, one-handed, re-seating a fuel line that had worked loose in the flood surge. She'd refused help twice already, which Amara respected, having once steered a barge through a cyclone rather than accept advice from a harbor master. So Amara didn't offer help. She sat down on the toolbox, out of the way, and started cleaning grit out of the throttle linkage with a rag, which was a job that needed doing and also happened to be sitting next to Jun.

For a while there was only the small music of work: the click of fittings, the drip of residual water finding its way out of the bilge, the distant sound of Bao's people hauling salvage up the bank and arguing about it in three languages.

"Your arm itching yet?" Amara asked.

"Under the cast? Constantly. It's driving me insane."

"Good. Means it's healing. When it stops itching, that's when you worry."

"Huh." Jun tightened something. "Nobody tells you stuff like that. At the company depot they just gave you the safety manual and fined you if you bled on anything."

Amara turned that over. Sixteen years old. Indentured at—what, thirteen? Fourteen? Fined for bleeding. She thought about the first time she'd ever been paid honestly for a run, and how strange the money had felt, like a letter addressed to someone else.

"Jun."

"Yeah?"

"When the water drops enough to run the gorge safe—and it'll be maybe a week—we're heading back downriver. Resupply, report, see about getting word to the families of the advance team." She kept her eyes on the throttle linkage. "All four of us. You, me, Sterling, Vann. One crew, one manifest. That's how I wrote it this morning."

The clicking stopped.

Amara let the silence sit. This was the part pilots understood that land people didn't: you don't fill the quiet. You hold the channel open and you wait for the traffic to come through.

"You wrote it," Jun said finally. "On a manifest."

"Official document. Very binding. I signed it 'A. Osei, Master,' which is a title I awarded myself during the storm and nobody has challenged."

Another silence, but a different shape. When Amara glanced over, Jun was staring at the fuel line like it contained the answer to something, and her jaw was doing the thing jaws do when their owner is losing a fight with their own face.

"I can't—" Jun started, stopped, tried again. "My contract. Technically—"

"Technically, there's no contract. It burned. Sterling checked. Twice, because he's him." Amara set down the rag. "Look, kid. I've run with a lot of crews. Good ones, bad ones, one that mutinied, one that turned out to be smugglers, one that was fine but the food was criminal. And I'll tell you the difference between a crew and a job. A job ends. A crew is the people who are still standing next to you when everything's underwater and the maps are on fire and some corporate spy has stolen the only proof you had, and they're still there in the morning asking what needs fixing." She stood up. "You fixed the engine with one hand while the gorge was trying to kill us. You're crew. That's the whole speech. I'm going to check the mooring lines."

She got two steps toward the hatch before Jun said, very quietly, "Okay."

Amara paused.

"Okay," Jun said again, louder, steadier, the way you say a thing the second time to prove the first time wasn't a fluke. "Yeah. Okay. Crew."

"Good." Amara nodded briskly, because the alternative was letting her own face do something embarrassing. "Now hand me that wrench before you strip the fitting, you're torquing it like it insulted you."

* * *

Evening came down gold through the gorge, and the whole settlement drifted toward the fire the way iron filings drift toward a magnet.

Bao held court from his chair with the good cushion, and tonight the stories were flood stories, which meant everyone talked over everyone else, correcting each other's measurements. The widow Sefu claimed the water had reached the third step of the mission house; her neighbor insisted it was the fourth and produced a witness. Someone had found a cat alive inside a floating wardrobe two miles downstream, and this fact was presented to the assembly no fewer than four times, gaining detail with each telling until the cat had allegedly ridden the wardrobe through the rapids standing up.

Felix sat across the fire from Amara, and she watched him do the thing he'd been doing for three days: watching the valley the way other men watched a fire, or a woman, or the sea. Hungry and guilty about being hungry. His father had built the sabotage into this place, had died half-finished, and Felix had pulled the lever himself, and Amara knew—from her own long acquaintance with the particular loneliness of doing the right thing—that the relief hadn't arrived yet. Relief was slow cargo. It came upriver in its own time.

Bao caught her eye across the flames and tilted his head toward Felix, a question in it: *Is he well?*

Amara spread her hands slightly: *Give him time.*

Bao nodded, satisfied, and returned to adjudicating the step dispute, ruling that the water had reached the third step but *intended* to reach the fourth, which satisfied no one and ended the argument, which is the mark of true leadership.

Later, when the fire had settled and most of the children had fallen asleep in a single intertwined pile like puppies, Odile appeared at Amara's shoulder with two cups of tea and handed her one without asking. They stood together at the edge of the light, looking down the dark throat of the gorge.

"Heard from the listening post," Odile said. "Shortwave. The gunboat crew made it to the delta. Meridian's filing it as a natural disaster. Act of God." She sipped. "There's a certain poetry in the corporation that wanted to own the valley having to admit the valley owns itself."

"They'll regroup."

"They'll regroup. But they'll regroup blind. No maps. No coordinates. And every surveyor they send next will find a valley full of people who know every game they play, because we've played them all." Odile was quiet a moment. "I spent nine years working for them, Amara. Nine years telling myself the roads I opened would bring prosperity. Do you know what I never saw, in nine years? Not one town that was better off. Not one. Just holes in the ground and men in badges counting what they'd taken." She turned the cup in her hands. "I keep waiting to feel punished for it. Instead I'm drinking decent tea next to a fire with people who fed me when they had every reason not to. I don't know what to do with that."

"Put it in the log," Amara said. "Entry reads: survived. Will assess feelings later."

Odile laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut. "You're a strange woman, Osei."

"I'm a pilot. We're all strange. It's the solitude."

* * *

It was near midnight when Amara made her last round of the boat, out of habit more than need. The *Magpie's Rest* rode easy on her new mooring lines, hull dry, engine cold, the patched seam holding like it had something to prove. In the engine hold, Jun was asleep in the hammock she'd rigged herself weeks ago and never mentioned to anyone, one arm in its splint sticking out at an angle, a manual open on her chest, rising and falling.

Amara stood in the hatchway a long moment, watching her breathe.

Then she took the manual off the girl's chest, marked the page with a pencil stub, set it on the toolbox, and draped the spare blanket over her—the good blanket, the wool one, the one Amara had been saving for a cold night that had somehow never seemed to arrive.

On her way out she paused at the little chart table where Felix had been working evenings, and saw what he'd left there under the lamp: fresh paper, and on it, in careful engineer's lettering, the beginnings of a new chart of the upper Kessume. The gorge. The Narrows. The sluice house, drawn accurately for the first time in forty years. And in the corner, where official charts carried their seals and restrictions, he'd written a note in pencil, small and plain:

*For the people who live here.*

Amara looked at it for a while.

Then she climbed out onto the deck, where the river murmured past in the dark, lower and gentler every hour, carrying the last of the flood away toward the sea, and she allowed herself—for exactly one minute, by her watch—to believe that the worst was behind them.

At fifty-nine seconds, she heard footsteps on the bank above, quick and uneven, and a voice calling down through the trees in a tone she recognized from twenty-two years on the water, a tone that meant trouble coming upriver faster than anyone wanted:

*"Boat! Boat on the water!"*

Amara was moving before the second shout finished, already reaching for the lamp, already doing the arithmetic of current and distance and dawn. Downriver, faint but unmistakable, an engine note carried up the gorge—small, single, running hard against the last of the flood.

Somebody was coming to Junction Nine.

And nobody, as far as Amara knew, was supposed to know it existed.
