## 21. Compass Points

The water went down the way a guest leaves a party it had overstayed—slowly, apologetically, leaving things behind it hadn't meant to.

For three days after the flood, Junction Nine rang with the sound of recovery. Hammers on wet timber. The creak of the old signal tower as Bao's people winched it back upright, inch by stubborn inch, like raising a drunk uncle off the floor of a tavern. Children who had spent the flood week in the upper galleries of the hill now ran shrieking down the switchback paths, chasing a goat that had, by all accounts, saved three families by headbutting them awake when the water first came. The goat had been promoted. It now wore a red ribbon and answered, allegedly, to *Commissioner*.

Felix spent those three days hauling timber, and he found that he didn't mind it. His hands, which had spent twenty years on drawings and debt ledgers, blistered and cracked and ached in a way that felt honest. There was something about work that produced a visible result—a wall mended, a beam set, a roof over the Osei family's borrowed quarters—that made the arithmetic of his old life seem like a bad joke told by a man who had never learned to laugh.

On the fourth morning, the sun came out properly for the first time since the gorge had swallowed Meridian's gunboats, and the valley steamed like a kettle left on the stove. Felix climbed the path to the signal tower, where Bao could usually be found, and found the old signalman exactly where expected: perched on the repaired gallery rail with a tin mug of something that smelled like it had been brewed from rope and regret, watching the river run clean and green below.

"You're up early," Felix said.

"I'm up late," Bao said. "Haven't been to bed. There's a difference, and my knees know it." He patted the rail beside him. "Sit. You walk like a man with something in his pocket he can't decide about."

Felix sat. Below them, Amara was on the dock directing the unloading of the *Kestrel's* surviving cargo with the crisp authority of a woman who had been given back her river and intended to enjoy every meter of it. Jun was underneath the boat's hull, upside down in a coracle of scaffolding, arguing with the propeller shaft in three languages, two of which Felix suspected Jun had invented for the occasion.

"You did a brave thing," Bao said. "In the sluice house. Opening that gate."

"I did a necessary thing."

"Mm." Bao drank. "I've found that brave and necessary are usually the same thing wearing different coats. The question is which one you were wearing when you decided."

Felix looked out at the river. The gorge, which had roared for two days like something waking from a long sleep, had settled back into its ordinary murmur. Somewhere downriver, Meridian's stranded crews were dragging their boats off the mudflats and slinking home with their tails between their corporate legs. Somewhere downriver, too, were the creditors, the ledgers, the letters with red stamps—the whole paper architecture of Felix Sterling's former life.

"I've been thinking about my father," Felix said.

"Most people do. Some of them even get it right."

"He spent his last years paying for this valley. Paying for it with money he didn't have, which is why I grew up thinking he was a fool or a criminal. Probably both. I inherited his debts and spent a decade being ashamed of him." Felix reached into his coat. "And this whole time, the only thing of his I carried was this."

He took out the brass compass.

It looked smaller than it used to. The flood had left a fine silt in the hinge, and the casing was dented where he'd fallen in the sluice house, but the needle still swung true when he opened it, settling south with the calm of a thing that had never once doubted itself. His father's initials were scratched inside the lid, half worn away. *E.S.* Beside them, so faint you'd miss it unless you knew to look, a second mark: a small nine, the way signalmen wrote their junctions.

Bao looked at it for a long time without touching it.

"He carried that when he came upriver the first time," Bao said at last. "Thirty-one years ago. He stood about where you're standing and asked me if I thought a valley could be kept secret by a man with a pencil and a compass." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "I told him no. I told him the world finds everything eventually. He said—" Bao's voice did something complicated, "—he said, *then we'll make the world work for it.*"

"He was good at that," Felix said. "Making things work for it."

"He was good at starting things he couldn't finish." Bao turned to look at him, and his eyes were old and kind and utterly without mercy. "That was his flaw, boy. Not dishonesty. Not cowardice. He simply ran out of years. The debt you inherited wasn't money. It was the last third of a job."

Felix opened his hand. "Then I'm settling it. Take it."

Bao didn't move. "Take what?"

"The compass. It belongs here. It belongs to this valley—to the people who finished what he started. I'm a surveyor with no survey, an engineer with no line, a man whose entire inheritance turned out to be a promise somebody else kept. You should have it. It's the truest thing he ever owned."

For a moment the only sound was the river and, faintly, from below, Jun's voice declaring to the propeller shaft that if it didn't cooperate there would be *consequences*.

Then Bao reached out, closed Felix's fingers back over the compass, and pressed the whole arrangement firmly against Felix's chest.

"No," Bao said.

"Bao—"

"No." He said it the way he must have said it to trains, once, from this very tower—one syllable with the weight of a signal behind it. "You misunderstand what this is. You think it's a relic. It's not. It's a tool. And a tool belongs in the hand of the one who'll use it." He leaned back against the rail. "Your father gave it to me to hold for thirty years. I held it. Now I'm giving it back to a Sterling, which is where it wanted to be all along. It points where you're needed, boy. Not where you're from."

Felix looked down at the compass in his palm. The needle trembled, then steadied, pointing—of all directions—north, upriver, toward the drowned station and the dry hills and the switchback paths where children were chasing a goat in a ribbon.

"I don't know what I'm needed for," he said. "The line's closed. The maps are ash. There's nothing left to survey."

"Wrong on all three counts." Bao held up fingers. "One: closed lines need keeping, and keeping is a job. Two: the maps aren't ash in the way you think—your father drew three sets. The originals burned, but the *knowledge* is in your head now, and heads don't burn. Three: this valley is about to have more visitors than it's had in thirty years, and not all of them will come with guns. Some will come with contracts." His mouth went dry and thin. "Contracts are worse."

"Meridian will try again."

"Meridian always tries again. That's the difference between a river and a company—the river knows when to stop." Bao drained his mug and stood, joints crackling like green firewood. "The valley will need an engineer. Someone who knows the sluice, the line, the water. Someone whose name makes old men like me trust the ground under their feet." He clapped Felix on the shoulder, and it hurt in a friendly way. "Think about it. But not too long. Around here, thinking too long is how you end up talking to propeller shafts."

* * *

Felix thought about it for exactly as long as it took him to walk down the switchback path, which was to say: he thought about it the whole way and arrived at the dock already decided, which was, he was learning, how the important decisions actually worked. You didn't make them. You noticed them, already made, sitting in your chest like a stone you'd swallowed without tasting.

The dock was chaos of the productive kind. Amara had the *Kestrel* half unloaded, half repaired, and fully commandeered as the center of a planning argument involving Bao's daughter Sefa, two of the recovered advance team, and a fisherman who kept insisting the river would behave for at least a month and being loudly doubted by everyone.

"—all I'm saying," Amara was saying, "is that if we're going to run supplies, we run them on *my* schedule, and my schedule says we don't fight the current in the afternoon like idiots. Morning water. Every run. Morning water or no run."

"Morning water," Sefa agreed.

"Morning water," said the fisherman, vindicated.

Felix stood at the edge of the argument for a moment, compass in his pocket, and watched them. Amara had a new scar along her forearm from the spy's knife and carried it like a decoration. Jun emerged from under the hull with grease to the elbows and a look of triumph that suggested the propeller shaft had, at last, accepted the terms of surrender.

"Good news," Jun announced, spitting on one palm and offering it to Felix, who shook it anyway, because some rituals you didn't fight. "She'll run. Not pretty. But she'll run. Also I found a leak you didn't know about, fixed it, and would like it noted in the record that I am the best mechanic on this river."

"Noted," Felix said. "You're also the only mechanic on this river."

"Which makes me the best. Math, Felix. It's not hard."

Amara looked over at him, and something in his face must have shown, because she set down the crate she was holding and came to stand beside him, shoulder to shoulder, facing the water the way they'd faced it together in the storm.

"You've decided something," she said.

"How can you tell?"

"Because you've got the look. The one Odile used to get." She said the name carefully now, without the old flint in it. Odile had left two days ago, walking downriver with the surviving advance team and a letter of testimony she intended to deliver to every regulator, journalist, and inconvenient committee she could find. She'd shaken Felix's hand at the dock and said, "Your father would have hated how long it took you to figure out. He'd have been proud anyway." It was the most honest thing anyone had said to Felix in years.

"I'm staying," Felix said. "Through the rebuilding. Through whatever comes after. If the valley needs an engineer for the line—the closed line, the one that stays closed—I'm it. If they'll have me."

Amara was quiet a moment. Then: "You know Bao already told everyone at breakfast."

Felix stared at her. "What?"

"Yesterday. Told Sefa, told the council, told the goat. The goat approved." She was fighting a smile and losing. "You're the last person in this valley to find out you're staying. It's been decided since the flood. There's talk of building you a house on the bluff. Sefa's already arguing about the roof pitch."

"That's—" Felix searched for the word and found, to his surprise, that it was there, waiting. "That's presumptuous."

"That's family," Amara said. "Same thing, worse manners." She picked her crate back up. "Come on, engineer. Morning water's wasted on standing around. We've got a run to plan."

* * *

That evening, the valley did what it did every evening now that it could afford evenings again: it lit its lamps.

From the bluff where his house would one day stand—Sefa had won the roof-pitch argument, Felix had been informed, in absentia and by unanimous vote—Felix could see them all: the gallery windows in the hill, the dock lanterns, the small brave light of the signal tower where Bao was already back on watch, because some people kept a line the way other people breathed. Down at the water, the *Kestrel* rode at anchor, patched, scarred, and seaworthy, her hull holding Jun's tin patches the way the valley held its people: imperfectly, stubbornly, well enough.

Felix took out the compass and opened it one more time.

The needle swung, wavered, and settled. North. Toward the gorge. Toward the sluice house he'd have to inspect, the gate he'd have to maintain, the whole hidden machinery of a promise thirty-one years in the keeping. His father's initials caught the lamplight inside the lid.

"I get it now," Felix said quietly, to a man twenty years gone. "It was never about the debt."

The needle held steady, pointing where he was needed.

Below, Jun's voice floated up the bluff—*"Felix! Amara says if you're going to stand around being poetic, do it faster, dinner's getting cold!"*—and Felix laughed, pocketed the compass, and started down the path toward the lamplight, toward the table, toward the people who had, without asking, already decided he belonged.

Behind him, the signal tower blinked once, twice—*all clear, all clear*—and the river ran on.
