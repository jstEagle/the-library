## 22. Current and Monsoon

The dry season came back the way it always did in the valley—sneaking up on you through your boots.

Felix Sterling noticed it first on a Tuesday morning, standing ankle-deep in the shallows below Junction Nine, when he realized the water that had spent four months trying to drown him was now politely declining to reach his knees. The river had dropped a full hand's width since the week before. Bao would say it meant the monsoon had finished its work and gone home to sleep. Amara would say it meant the sandbars were moving again and everyone should be very afraid. Jun would say both things at once, because Jun had learned early that agreeing with everybody kept the biscuits coming.

Felix just stood there with his trousers rolled and let the cold pull at his ankles, and thought: *I could leave now.*

That was the strange part. Not that the thought came—thoughts like that always came, they were his father's inheritance too, along with the debts and the compass—but that it arrived without any weight behind it. It knocked, looked around at the valley, and left.

Behind him, up the switchback path, Junction Nine was making its particular morning noise: hammering from the roof of the signal house where Odile was re-shingling despite having been told twice that she was a passenger, not staff; the clank-clank-hiss of Jun's improvised water pump, which worked beautifully except for the ten minutes after every meal; and somewhere underneath all of it, the low steady hum of the old generator that Bao's people had kept alive for thirty years the way you keep an elderly dog alive—out of love, out of habit, and out of a stubborn refusal to let the thing die before you did.

Six months since the flood. Six months since the gorge had swallowed Meridian's gunboats whole and spat their crews out downstream, dazed and mapless, telling stories nobody believed. Six months since Felix had fed his father's original survey drawings into the stove in the signal house and watched thirty years of secrets curl into ash.

He still dreamed about the fire sometimes. In the dreams the maps screamed. He'd talked about it exactly once, with Amara, on the boat, in the gray hour before dawn, and she'd said, "Maps don't scream, Felix. Men do. You're allowed to have been one of them." Then she'd gone back to sleep, which was her way of ending conversations that were going too well.

* * *

The supply run was his idea, though he'd never claimed credit for it, because claiming credit got you put on shingle duty with Odile.

It had started simply enough. The valley had people now—more than forty, by spring count, as word of Junction Nine filtered downriver in the careful, coded way such words travel among people who have all, at some point, needed a place that wasn't on any map. And people needed things. Salt. Lamp oil. Boot leather. A particular kind of valve gasket that only existed in the port town two hundred miles downstream and that Jun described, with genuine feeling, as "the difference between civilization and drinking out of your hands."

So Felix had gone to Bao with a proposal: run the boat downriver once a month. Buy supplies with money earned honestly—Amara piloting cargo for farmers who couldn't afford the cartel rates, Jun fixing engines in exchange for parts, Felix doing the paperwork because someone had to and because, as he'd discovered to his own astonishment, he didn't hate it. No guns. No secrets. Just a closed line running quietly, carrying what people needed and nothing that would hurt them.

Bao had listened to the whole proposal without expression, which was how Bao listened to everything, and then said, "You understand this makes you a ferryman."

"I understand it makes me useful."

"Ferryman is better," Bao said. "Useful men get used. Ferrymen get remembered." And that had been the entire negotiation, because Bao believed decisions should take no longer than the time required to make them correctly.

Now, six months in, the run had become ritual. Every third Thursday they took the boat—the same patched, scarred, resurrected hull that had nearly killed them all, which Jun insisted on calling *the Phoenix* and which everyone else called *the Boat*, over Jun's increasingly theatrical objections—and they went downriver and came back, and the valley breathed a little easier each time.

This morning was a third Thursday.

"You're standing in the river," Amara observed, arriving beside him with two cups of tea and handing him one, "which either means you've achieved enlightenment or you've forgotten we leave in an hour."

"Checking the depth."

"Mm. And?"

"And it's dropping. Sandbar off the second bend will be showing by next week."

Amara sipped her tea and looked at him sideways, in the way she had that made Felix feel simultaneously seen and mildly audited. "You know," she said, "a year ago I watched a man board my boat with his dead father's compass and a face like a man walking into his own funeral. Now he reads sandbars for fun."

"For necessity."

"For fun, Felix. Admit it. It's the closest thing you have to a hobby, and I refuse to let you pretend otherwise, because if you pretend otherwise you'll start believing it, and then you'll be insufferable instead of merely smug."

Felix smiled into his tea. "I'm not smug."

"You wrote a song about the sluice gate."

"It was a *work chant*. For morale."

"Jun harmonizes. There are *verses*."

"There are two verses."

"*Two.*" She shook her head with the deep weariness of a woman surrounded by idiots she loved. "Get your bag, ferryman. We sail in an hour, and if Jun has packed that engine room with spare gaskets again we won't have room for actual supplies."

* * *

Jun had, in fact, packed the engine room with spare gaskets.

"They're *strategic*," Jun said, emerging from the hold wearing goggles pushed up into their hair like a second pair of eyebrows, hands black to the elbow, voice cracking in the middle of the word strategic in a way that happened less often these days but still happened, and which everyone had silently agreed never to mention. "You don't understand, the last port had a *run* on them. A run! Some cartel buyer bought out the whole shelf. If we'd had spares we could've—"

"We could've sold them at a markup and become the thing we hate," Felix said. "I read the ledger, Jun. We have eleven gaskets. We need three."

"But what if—"

"What if the river rises and takes them? Then the river needed them more than we did." He held out his hand. Jun stared at it with the expression of a sixteen-year-old being asked to surrender a limb, then dug out eight gaskets and slapped them down with tremendous dignity.

"Eight," Jun said. "And I want it noted in the log that I was generous."

"Noted," Felix said, and wrote it in the log, because he did keep a log now, a proper one, in his father's old leather case, and because Jun checked it obsessively to see whether their generosity had been recorded and would absolutely notice if it hadn't.

That was another thing the valley had taught him: people watched what you wrote down. His father had understood that. It was why the old maps had been written in code and hidden, why the sabotage plans lived in drawings that looked like routine maintenance schedules. Felix's father had spent his life writing things down so that some people wouldn't know, and it had cost him everything—his name, his fortune, eventually, Felix suspected, his life.

Felix had decided to spend his life writing things down so that everybody knew.

It had taken him months to work up to it. The new map sat in the signal house now, pinned above Bao's ancient chart table, drawn in Felix's own careful hand: the river from the port to the headwaters, every bend, every sandbar, every depth sounding taken personally, in every season. The location of Junction Nine marked plainly. The sluice gate marked plainly. The cave where the advance team had survived, marked plainly. Everything. No codes, no blanks, no elegant white spaces where knowledge had been amputated for someone's protection.

When he'd first hung it up, Bao had studied it for a long time and then asked, quietly, "You're sure? Your father died keeping this valley secret."

"My father died keeping this valley safe," Felix had said. "Those turned out to be different jobs. The secrecy was Meridian's fault, not the valley's. Secrets were the weapon they forced us to use." He'd touched the edge of the map. "I'd rather win with honesty. If honesty loses, at least we'll know we lost fair."

Bao had considered this for a long moment, the lamplight moving in the deep lines of his face, and then said, "Your father argued the same thing, near the end. I told him the world wasn't ready." A pause. "Perhaps the world is slower than people. But it does arrive."

* * *

They cast off at mid-morning under a sky scrubbed clean, the dry-season blue that seemed almost indecent after the monsoon's permanent gray. Amara took the helm with the unhurried grace of a woman who had made peace with this river the way other people made peace with old enemies—warily, respectfully, and with a flask of tea always within reach.

Odile came aboard at the last minute, as she always did, carrying a crate of signal-house shingles she pretended was personal luggage.

"You know," Felix said, taking the other end of the crate, "you live there now. You could just... fix the roof while you're home."

"The roof is fine."

"There's a bucket in the kitchen."

"That bucket is *decorative*."

They loaded it aboard anyway, and Jun, passing on the gangplank with a coiled rope over one shoulder, said, "If you two are going to flirt via home repair, could you do it after we clear the rapids? Some of us are trying to work."

"We are not—" Odile began.

"—flirting," Felix finished, at exactly the wrong moment, so that it sounded like they were finishing each other's sentence, which was worse.

Jun grinned the whole way to the wheelhouse.

The river carried them down through country that Felix had first seen in terror and now knew the way you know a friend's laugh. The narrows where the radio had died. The cliff where the storm had cracked the hull and Jun had come up out of the engine hold soaked in bilge and courage. The long bend where they'd lost half their supplies overboard and Amara had threaded the rapids blind in the rain, swearing with a fluency that Jun had later described as "educational."

Every landmark had a story now. That was what a current was, Felix thought—not water, but story, moving.

At the second bend, the sandbar was already showing, a pale tongue of gravel breaking the surface exactly where he'd predicted. He pointed it out to Amara, who nodded and gave him the look she reserved for moments when she was proud of him and wanted him to know it without either of them having to say anything embarrassing aloud.

"Told you," he said.

"You told me nothing. I *agreed* with you, which is different, and I'll thank you to remember the distinction in the log."

* * *

The port town had changed too, or perhaps Felix had changed and the town had simply stopped bothering him. The rusting cranes still leaned over the docks like tired old men, and the harbor office still smelled of diesel and mildew, but there was life in it now—cargo moving, engines turning over, faces he knew. The farmer whose grain they hauled waved from a warehouse door. The parts merchant, a broad woman named Sefa who had spent two years pretending not to like Jun and had recently given up the pretense entirely, had their gaskets set aside before they even docked.

"Eleven?" she said, when Jun placed the order.

"Three."

Sefa looked at Jun over the counter for a long moment. Then she wrapped up five and charged them for three, and when Jun opened their mouth to argue, she said, "Generosity travels, little mechanic. Somebody upstream taught me that. Take it before I change my mind and charge you double for the sentiment."

On the walk back to the boat, Jun clutched the parcel and said, quietly, "She likes me."

"She likes you."

"Like, *likes* likes me?"

"I am not having this conversation."

"You're basically my dad, you have to have this conversation."

Felix stopped walking. The dock boards creaked under them. Somewhere behind them a crane groaned, and the river went on talking to itself the way it always did, patient and enormous and uninterested in human crises.

"I'm twenty-nine," he said finally.

"So? Dads can be young. Amara says you give dad advice. She does an impression."

"Of course she does an impression."

"It's very good. You do this thing with your eyebrows—"

"Jun."

"—and you say 'we'll log it' about *everything*, it's honestly a problem—"

"*Jun.*" He crouched slightly, so they were eye to eye, which was getting harder to arrange every month; the boy shot up like river grass. "Yes. I'll have the conversation. When there's a conversation to have. Which there isn't. Yet. And when there is, we'll have it properly, on the boat, where I can't escape." He straightened. "In the meantime: five gaskets, three paid for, and if you tell anyone I said yes to the dad thing, I'm promoting you to chief shingle officer under Odile."

Jun's face went through several expressions in rapid succession and settled on radiant. "Noted in the log," they said, and ran for the boat before he could retract it.

Felix stood a moment longer on the dock, watching the boy go, feeling something in his chest that had no business being there and that he had stopped fighting months ago. His father had left him debts, a compass, and a map full of holes. Nobody had told him the interest compounded into this.

* * *

They came back upriver two days later, holds full, running easy before a gentle headwind, and made the gorge by late afternoon. The light came slanting gold through the walls of stone, and the water ran green and quiet over the place where Meridian's gunboats still lay drowned in the deep channel—a fact the river had absorbed and moved past, the way it absorbed everything.

Junction Nine appeared around the last bend the way it always did: suddenly, completely, lamplit against the dusk. Forty windows glowing. The signal house rebuilt, taller than before, its lamp burning in the tower as it had burned, off and on, for thirty years—a light kept lit for travelers who might never come, until finally they did.

Bao stood on the landing stage, as he always stood, hands folded, watching the boat come in. Behind him the whole settlement had drifted down to the bank the way it did on supply days, because arrival was the valley's favorite entertainment and nobody pretended otherwise.

They unloaded by lantern light. Salt and oil and boot leather. Sefa's gaskets. A crate of oranges that made Jun gasp audibly. Odile's shingles, which she carried up the hill herself, daring anyone to comment. Nobody commented. That was also a kind of love.

Later, when the crates were stacked and the kettle was on and the generator hummed its old faithful hum, Felix climbed to the signal house alone. The new map hung above the chart table where it always hung, catching the lamplight. He stood in front of it for a while, checking nothing, changing nothing, just looking—at the bends he'd sounded, the depths he'd measured, the small neat letters spelling out *Junction Nine* in plain ink for anyone to read.

His father's compass sat on the table beside the lamp. He picked it up. The needle swung, settled, pointed north with its usual calm certainty, as if the last two years had been a rounding error.

*It points where you're needed,* Bao had said, giving it back. *Not where you're from.*

Felix turned it over in his hand. On the back, worn nearly smooth, his father had scratched a single mark years ago—a tiny sluice-gate symbol, the seal on a life of hidden work. Felix had thought once about scratching it out. He hadn't. Instead, below it, with the tip of a knife, working slowly by lamplight, he added a second mark: a small open circle. A junction. A place where lines meet and travelers choose their direction.

Then he opened the log to a fresh page and wrote, in his own careful hand:

*Third Thursday. Down and back. All hands accounted for. Cargo delivered. Gaskets: five acquired, three paid, generosity confirmed traveling. Sandbar forming at second bend—note for next run.*

He paused, pen hovering. Then, beneath the entries, he added the line he added every month, the one that had started as a joke and become a covenant:

*Line remains closed. Hearts remain open. River remains in charge.*

Downstairs, the door banged and Jun's voice came spiraling up the stairwell—something about oranges, and fairness, and the distribution thereof—and Amara's laugh answered it, low and real, and beneath both of them the river spoke its endless patient sentence against the banks.

Felix capped the pen and stood listening.

Home, he had learned, was not a place you found. Places you found could be taken away, flooded, erased from maps, sold out from under you by men with consortium badges. Home was a current. You didn't locate it—you joined it. You put your shoulder to the water moving in the direction you believed in, and you found, to your lasting surprise, that you were not swimming alone.

He blew out the lamp and went down toward the voices, and the light in the tower stayed burning behind him, and the river ran on through the dark valley, carrying its stories toward the sea.
