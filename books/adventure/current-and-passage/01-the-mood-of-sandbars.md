## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The fog came off the delta before dawn, the way it always did in the weeks before the monsoon, rising off the warm shallows like breath off a sleeping animal. Ansel Iversen stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* with her collar turned up and her eyes doing the work that no instrument aboard could do, reading a river that had no interest in being read.

She had given up on the chart an hour ago. It lay rolled on the chart table behind her, its ink lines drawn by a hydrographic office that had surveyed these waters forty years ago in a season when the river was feeling agreeable. Rivers were never agreeable for long. The delta shifted its mind every spring flood, shrugging sandbars from one channel into another the way a man in his sleep turns over, and the chart was a record of a river that no longer existed. The clerks at the harbormaster's offices still stamped it and sold it and believed in it, the way people believe in the honesty of a stranger's face.

Ansel believed in the water.

There was a mood to a sandbar, she had learned it from her father before she learned her letters, and she had never found a way to teach it because it wasn't knowledge, exactly. It was attention. A bar lying just under the surface didn't hide itself; it confessed, constantly, in small ways, to anyone patient enough to listen. Where the current crossed a shoal the water went a particular shade of olive, a half-tone lighter than the deep channel, like cloth faded at the fold. When the tide ran against the wind, a bar wore a ruffled cap of chop that broke in no particular direction, a confusion of small waves that argued with each other instead of with the shore. And in the last hour of the ebb, when the river was at its most honest, a bar gave off a smell — a green, mineral smell, the smell of mud holding its breath — that drifted downwind a full quarter mile before the bar itself came into view.

The *Kestrel* drew two meters and a hand. The channel ahead, according to the chart, held four. According to the river, this morning, it held two and a half, and only on the eastern side, where a drowned mangrove had spent thirty years quietly catching silt and building a bar that no surveyor had ever thought worth naming.

Ansel eased the throttle back until the engine's throb dropped to a murmur and the boat began to move at the speed of suspicion.

"You're slowing down." Toma's voice came up the engine-room ladder, along with the smell of diesel and the particular warmth of a man who had been awake since four making the old engine behave. He appeared at the top rung, wiping his hands on a rag that had been gray for as long as she'd known him. "We're making less headway than the fog is."

"The fog knows where it's going."

Toma grunted. He was a broad, slow-moving man with a face like an unplaned plank, and in nine years he had learned that Ansel's riddles were not riddles at all but instructions. He leaned on the rail beside her and squinted into the white.

"I don't see anything," he said.

"You're not supposed to. That's the point of it." She nodded at a stretch of water ahead and to port, indistinguishable from every other stretch of water. "There's a bar there. New since the spring flood. The chart will find out about it next decade."

Toma looked. The fog looked back. "How do you know?"

"The birds." And there they were, if a man knew where to look: three terns standing in the shallows over the bar, hip-deep in water that should have been over their heads, fishing a seam of trapped bait. Birds read water the way she did, without charts, without sentiment, and they had no reason to lie. "They're standing on the river's new opinion. We'll go around its old one."

She brought the wheel over in a slow arc, and the *Kestrel* leaned into the turn like a tired horse, and the bar she had named for no one slid past to port close enough that if she'd had a mind to she could have spat on it.

Toma watched the water slide by and said nothing for a while, which was his way of saying a great deal. Then: "We could make better time in the marked channel. The dredgers have been through it. There are beacons."

"Beacons get moved. Bars don't move — they just get born and die where they stand." She said it lightly, but her hands stayed on the wheel, and her eyes never came off the water. "The marked channel is where everyone goes. Everyone means inspectors, and toll clerks, and men with clipboards who want to know why a boat registered in a port that burned down nine years ago is carrying cargo with no bill of lading."

"You said the bill of lading was coming."

"The bill of lading is always coming, Toma. It's like the tide that way."

He went back down the ladder. She heard him muttering to the engine, the way he did, a low continuous complaint that was really a form of prayer. She envied him that — the ability to talk to a machine all day and call it a conversation. Her own conversations were all with water that never answered, and with a past that never stopped talking.

* * *

The fog burned off by mid-morning into a high, pale haze, and the delta opened around her — a country that was mostly water pretending, badly, to be land. Mangrove keys stood in rows like patient congregation. Fishing stakes leaned at angles of surrender. Somewhere behind the green wall of the banks, invisible, there were villages, markets, a whole human machinery of buying and selling and asking questions, and Ansel intended to touch as little of it as possible.

She had a system, refined over nine years, for the business of not being anywhere long. She took cargo that paid on delivery, never on credit, because credit meant a relationship and relationships meant records. She paid her harbor fees in cash and in full, because a debt was a hook and hooks stayed set. She never took a berth twice in the same season, never drank in the same tavern twice in the same month, and never — this rule she kept the way other people kept their faith — never gave her true family name to a clerk, a customs man, or anyone holding a pen over a form. On paper she was Ansel Voss, of Vossport, which had burned, which was convenient, which was a lie she had built the way a wren builds a nest: one stolen twig at a time, until it held her whole life.

The trouble with the system was that it required harbors, and harbors required clerks, and clerks were hired precisely because they were the sort of people who noticed things.

The harbor at Salter's Reach was a low wooden sprawl on the north bank where the delta narrowed into something that could still, generously, be called a river. Ansel brought the *Kestrel* in on the last of the flood tide, kissing the fenders against the pilings with a delicacy that drew an approving nod from a deckhand on the next berth over, and went up the ramp to the harbormaster's office to pay her fees and be on her way.

The clerk was new to her. That was the first bad sign. She kept a mental ledger of harbor clerks the way other captains kept a ledger of fuel prices, and Salter's Reach had been, for three years, the territory of a fat, half-blind old man named Corben who stamped papers without reading them and was the closest thing to a friend her system allowed. The man behind the desk this morning was young, lean, and had the unfortunate look of someone who had taken the civil service examination seriously.

"Vessel name?"

"*Kestrel*. River freighter, out of — "

"Vossport." He was already writing. "Yes. I have your prior entries here." He turned a ledger around and ran a finger down a column. "Eleven visits in four years. Interesting pattern."

"Is it?"

"You never stay more than two nights. You always pay cash." He looked up. His eyes were the color of dishwater and about as warm. "And your registration certificate is issued by a port office that the provincial records show was destroyed, with all its duplicate files, in the fire of nine years ago."

The fog had burned off, but Ansel felt it close over her again anyway, a white blindness behind the eyes. She kept her face in the expression she practiced in the mirror of the *Kestrel*'s head — pleasant, faintly bored, a woman thinking about cargo. "That's why the certificate is stamped *certified true copy*, officer. The harbormaster at Vossport reissued copies to all his registered masters before the fire. I understood the provincial office had been notified."

"By whom?"

"I assumed by the harbormaster."

"The harbormaster of Vossport," the clerk said, with the mild relish of a man laying cards face up one at a time, "drowned two years ago. There was no one to confirm the reissuance. There is no duplicate file. There is no — " he turned the ledger page, and his voice went softer, which was worse than louder, " — no record anywhere in this province of a vessel called *Kestrel* before four years ago. A river freighter is not built in a day, Captain. Where was she before?"

"She was working the outer coast," Ansel said. "Coastal registry. I brought her into river service."

"Coastal registry with which office?"

"You'd have to ask my previous agent. I change agents the way I change harbors. It's cheaper that way."

He smiled at that, a thin, paper-cut smile, and wrote something down. The scratch of his pen was the loudest sound in the room. Outside, through the fly-specked window, she could see the *Kestrel* riding at her berth, and Toma on deck coiling lines, and the ordinary, forgiving morning going on without her, and she understood with a cold clarity that the morning was not going to go on without her — not unless she handled this man correctly, right now, in the next thirty seconds.

The old Corben would have taken her fee, stamped her paper, and told her a story about his liver. The new one wanted a thread, and if he got one he would pull it, and the thread would not stop at Vossport. The thread went back through nine years of careful darkness to a burned office, a drowned man, and a name that, if spoken aloud in a provincial records room, would summon things she had spent a decade making sure had no address to find her at.

She opened the cash box in her mind and looked at the balance and winced. The cargo run she'd just finished had paid well, but well was a relative term on a riverboat with a hungry engine and an engineer who hadn't had new boots in two years. What she was about to do would cost her a third of it.

"Officer," she said, and let a different note into her voice, the note of a woman who is tired and reasonable and would very much like this to be simple. "I can see this is an irregularity. I don't like irregularities any more than you do. What I'd like is for my registration to be regular. I'm told the provincial office in Greyhaven can issue a provisional certificate on inspection — a survey, a fee, a week's waiting." She set her fee money on the desk, and on top of it, folded small, a second, quieter amount. "And I'm told the waiting can sometimes be shortened for a master in good standing who has a cargo commitment and can't afford a week at anchor."

The clerk looked at the money. He looked at it for a long time — long enough that she revised her estimate of him upward and her opinion of the morning downward — and then he took the fee, and left the folded amount where it was, and did not push it back across the desk, and did not put it in his pocket either, which was somehow the most frightening thing he could have done.

"Greyhaven," he said, writing it down. "Provincial office. I'll note that you've undertaken to regularize your papers." He stamped her harbor receipt and slid it across. "You'll want to do it soon, Captain Voss. There's a salvage survey fleet working this coast this season. They're thorough. They cross-check everything." He looked up, and the dishwater eyes were not unkind, which made it worse. "Everything."

* * *

She walked back down the ramp with her receipt in her fist and her heart going like the engine at full throttle, and made herself walk slowly, because a woman who runs from a harbormaster's office is a woman with something to run from, and the ramp had eyes.

Toma met her at the gangway with the manifest. "We're loaded by afternoon tide. The grain broker's man came by, wants to know if we'll take a second run — sealed crates, upriver, past the last beacon. Pays triple the going rate." He frowned. "I told him we'd ask you. It smells strange, Ansel. Triple rate for a short run."

"Everything on this river smells strange. The trick is knowing which strange pays." But her mind was not on the cargo. Her mind was on the phrase *salvage survey fleet*, turning it over, examining its underside. Salvage fleets did not come to a coast like this by accident. Someone had found something, or believed they had found something, or — and this was the thought she made herself look at directly, the way you look at the sun through smoked glass — someone was looking for something specific, and the coast she haunted was not large.

"Captain?" Toma said.

"Take the run," she said. "Triple rate buys a lot of forgiveness. We'll talk terms with the broker's man this afternoon." She looked out past the harbor mouth, to where the river widened and the haze stood over the water like a held breath, and somewhere out in that haze, she could not help feeling, something that had been patient for nine years was beginning to move.

"And Ansel," Toma said, quieter. "The clerk. He was asking Corben's old questions about you last night, down at the tavern. Where you berth, how long you've had the boat. Corben told him nothing, but Corben's not always here."

She looked at her engineer — her only crew, her only witness, the one man alive who knew her face without knowing her name — and gave him the truth, or the shape of it. "Then we'll be gone before he's back," she said. "Load fast, Toma. We sail on the afternoon tide."

The tide, as it happened, would be the lowest of its cycle — and the almanac pinned inside the *Kestrel*'s wheelhouse, in her father's handwriting, older than the fire, older than the lie, marked this week's tides with a single word she had never let herself understand.

*Hollow.*
