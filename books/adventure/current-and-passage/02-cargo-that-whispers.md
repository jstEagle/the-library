## 2. Cargo That Whispers

The fog came off the delta in layers, the way grief does — first the edges, then the middle, until you couldn't tell what you were standing in. Ansel Iversen stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* and read the water the way other people read letters from home: slowly, with dread, with a hunger she never admitted to.

No chart recorded what she was looking at. The charts showed the delta as a fan of blue lines and dotted shoals, tidy as a ledger, and the delta itself laughed at that. It rebuilt itself every monsoon, shuffled its sandbars like a card sharp, drowned last year's channel and cut a new one through mud that had been someone's field. The surveyors came every decade or so, took their soundings, printed their maps, and by the time the ink dried the river had lied to them again.

Ansel didn't use charts here. She used her eyes, her ears, and a knowledge she'd inherited the way other people inherit debts.

There — where the current flattened into that glassy, oily smoothness that novices took for deep water. Smooth water was a lie. Smooth water meant the current was sliding over a sandbar's shoulder, being held down, the way you'd hold down a dog that wanted to bark. She put the wheel over two spokes, gentle, and the *Kestrel* swung wide of nothing at all, of a hazard invisible to anyone but her, and slid back into the channel's throat.

Behind her, in the wheelhouse's dimness, the cargo whispered.

That was the only word for it. Six crates lashed down in the hold, each one sealed with tarred canvas and lead tape, each one silent — and yet not silent. Cargo had a sound. Grain breathed. Timber creaked as it took the river's humidity into its grain. Salt fish sang a thin dry song. Machine parts clinked when the hull rolled. But these crates made a sound like a held breath, like paper shifting against paper in a room where no one wanted to be heard. Documents, maybe. Or things wrapped in documents. Or something that made the men who packed it wrap everything else in silence too.

She'd asked, of course. She'd asked the broker at Sallow Point what was in them, and he'd smiled with the bottom half of his face and said, "Machine parts, Captain, sensitive to moisture," and paid her three times the going rate for the run upriver to Corren's Landing. Three times. Nobody paid three times for machine parts. Nobody paid three times for anything they wanted talked about.

Ansel had looked at the money, and at the fog, and at the crates sitting on her deck like six closed mouths, and she'd said yes.

That was the thing about never staying anywhere long: you learned to take the money that was offered, because the next berth might not offer any. And it was the thing about her name — the name on her papers, *Ansel Marrow*, a name she'd bought in a port town nine years ago from a clerk with a drinking problem and a stamp — that she couldn't afford questions. A woman who asked no questions got asked none. It was a fair trade, most days.

Most days.

The fog thinned as she came up on the Sallow Point roadstead, and the harbor resolved out of the grey the way a photograph develops: masts first, then the cranes, then the long low sheds, then the water itself, brown-green and patient. She brought the *Kestrel* alongside the customs berth with a touch so soft the fenders only sighed, and a line went down, and the riverboat was done moving for the afternoon.

The harbor clerk was waiting on the quay.

She noticed him before she tied off, because she noticed everyone who waited. Most clerks came to the boat when they were good and ready, coffee in hand, ledgers under arm, the whole machinery of official indifference deployed at its own pace. This one was already there. Young, maybe thirty, with a clerk's soft hands and a clerk's careful face, and a leather folder held against his chest the way a soldier holds a rifle in a country where soldiers get shot.

"Captain." He said it before she'd even dropped the gangplank. "Captain Marrow."

"Harbor's open," she said. "You can read that from your window. Why walk all the way down?"

"Registration check." He opened the folder. "Routine."

Routine. The word had a smell to it, like kerosene — useful, and dangerous near a flame. Ansel came down the plank slowly, giving her body time to arrange itself into the shape of a woman with nothing to hide: shoulders loose, hands easy, face set to a mild professional boredom she'd practiced in a hundred mirrors of a hundred wheelhouses.

"My registration's current," she said. "Renewed at Tarnmouth in the spring."

"Tarnmouth." He wrote it down, though she was sure he'd already read it off the paper in his folder. "That's a long way from your home port."

"I don't have one."

"Everyone has one."

"Then I haven't found it yet." She smiled, and let the smile do the work smiles do — a small door opened a crack, inviting him to look at nothing. "You have a name, clerk?"

"Ossen." He didn't offer a hand. "The *Kestrel* is registered as a river freighter, forty-one tons, single screw. But your hull lines—" he glanced past her at the boat, and something in his face sharpened, "—those are coastal lines. Deep-water sheer. A river hull doesn't flare at the bow like that. Where was she built?"

"Wherever her owner could afford it."

"Your owner."

"I'm the owner."

"Then where did *you* afford it?"

The fog was burning off, and the sun was coming up pale and thin over the sheds, and Ansel stood in it and felt the question go through her like a sounding line going through water — down, down, looking for the bottom, for the wreck of her. Clerk Ossen didn't know what he was fishing for. That was the horror of it. He was just a careful man doing careful work, and careful work, done in enough ports by enough careful men, would eventually find the seam in her papers the way a spring tide finds the seam in a seawall.

"Tarnmouth," she said again, pleasantly. "Built at Tarnmouth, from a coastal design, because I intended to work the coast and the river both, and the river pays better. Is there a fee for this routine check? I like to budget for routine."

Something flickered in his face — the flicker of a man who has been paid to be thorough and knows he hasn't been thorough enough. "No fee, Captain. I'll want to see the original papers. Tomorrow, perhaps, when you're not busy."

"Tomorrow I'm upriver."

"Then before you go upriver." He closed the folder, and the closing had a sound like a trap being set but not sprung. "Enjoy Sallow Point, Captain Marrow."

She watched him walk back up the quay, and she stood there in the strengthening sun and let herself feel, for exactly ten seconds, the fear she never let feel any longer than that. Ten seconds, timed against her own heartbeat, the way her father had taught her to time everything at sea. Then she locked it away in the hold where she kept it, down with the crates and the fog and everything else that whispered, and she went to find her client.

* * *

The broker's office smelled of ink and old rope, and the man who'd hired her was waiting in it, and he was not what she'd expected.

She'd expected a fat man with rings. What she got was lean and weathered, forty-something, with diver's shoulders and a face that had spent too many years in salt wind to ever quite relax — the skin around his eyes permanently narrowed, as if he were always looking into glare. He wore a plain oilskin coat, unadorned, and he stood when she came in, which brokers didn't do.

"Captain Marrow." He put out his hand. His grip was dry and hard, calloused in the pattern she knew: rope across the palm, steel along the fingers. A working hand. "Vey. I'm the one who packed your cargo."

"You're not a broker."

"I rent a broker's office. It's quieter than renting a warehouse." He gestured to a chair, and she took it, and he stayed standing, moving to the window where he could see the quay and the *Kestrel* beyond it. "You handled her in that fog this morning. I watched from the end of the pier."

"Watching my boat is a strange way to start a business relationship."

"Watching boats is my trade." He turned. "I'm a scout. Salvage. There's a fleet working this coast, and I find them things worth finding."

The word went into her like a fishhook going into a thumb — quick, and then the slow sick knowledge of the barb. *Salvage.* She kept her face still. Nine years of practice kept her face still.

"Then you've hired the wrong boat," she said. "I haul cargo. I don't dive."

"I know. The cargo's real. Machine parts, if you like, though they're mostly paper. It goes to Corren's Landing, to a survey office there, and it's paid for, and none of that is why I wanted to meet you." He came back from the window and sat on the edge of the desk, close enough that she could smell salt and tar on him, the honest smells, the ones she trusted more than any face. "I wanted to see how you handled a boat in fog. Because in my trade, the difference between a good skipper and a great one isn't nerve. It's reading. Anyone can be brave. Not everyone can look at smooth water and know it's lying."

"Flattery's extra."

"It's not flattery, it's a job offer. Not this run — that's done, you're paid. The next one." He leaned forward. "There's going to be a spring tide in eleven days. The lowest water this coast has seen in ten years. And when it goes out, it's going to bare things that haven't seen sun since before you had that boat. The fleet wants every ledge, every hulk, every drowned thing between here and the Headland charted before the monsoon comes and covers it all again. I need skippers who can move in shallow water, in bad weather, on water that isn't on any chart. I've watched you for two days, Captain. You're the best I've seen on this delta, and I've been on this delta six years."

"Eleven days," Ansel said, and heard her own voice come out flat and careful, "is a long way off."

"It is. And there's a ship coming to do the deep survey — a proper vessel, not barges like ours. She's called the *Halcyon Sound*." He said it easily, a name among names, a fact among facts, and then he reached into his coat for a chart to show her the survey grid, and Ansel stopped hearing him.

*Halcyon Sound.*

The wheelhouse of the *Meridian* had smelled of pipe smoke and pencil shavings, and her father had kept his charts rolled in tubes labeled in his own square handwriting, and one of those tubes — the one she'd burned, or thought she'd burned, or dreamed of burning, memory was a fog of its own — had had *HALCYON* written across it. The *Halcyon* had been a survey vessel in her father's time. She'd been the ship that mapped the coast, that found the wrecks, that — that —

That had been in the delta the summer her father died. Ansel was suddenly eight years old again, sitting on a bollard in a harbor she no longer admitted to, watching a white ship with a black funnel stand out to sea, and her mother's hand on her shoulder, gripping hard enough to leave a mark that Ansel could still, at forty-one tons' distance and twenty years' distance, almost feel.

"Captain?" Vey was watching her. Of course he was. A scout watched. It was his trade. "You know her?"

"The name," Ansel said. "From somewhere. Ships repeat names. It's a small coast."

"It's the same ship." Vey said it mildly, but his narrowed eyes didn't move off her face. "Refitted twice, but the same hull, the same registry. She's been surveying this coast for thirty years, on and off. Old institution, the *Halcyon Sound*." He paused, and the pause had weight, had cargo in it, had something wrapped in tarred canvas and lead tape. "You'd know that, though. If you were from here."

"I told you. I'm from wherever the work is."

"Mm." He stood, and offered nothing further, and that was its own kind of message. "Corren's Landing, three days, crates intact, seals unbroken. Then, if you're interested in the spring-tide work, come find me. I'll be at the Headland by then. Ask for Vey."

He walked her to the door, and just before she stepped out into the hard clean light of the quay, he said, quietly, not looking at her, looking at the *Kestrel* at her berth:

"Her lines are coastal. Her registration says river. And her captain reads water like she was born on it. You know what that adds up to, Captain Marrow? It adds up to a woman who used to be somebody else." He turned and smiled, and the smile was almost kind. "I don't care who you were. In my trade, the drowned don't care either. But somebody else might. Somebody's coming on the *Halcyon Sound*, and they're not hiring scouts for the wrecks alone."

* * *

She cast off at dusk, though the tide would serve as well at midnight, because she wanted to be moving. Moving was the oldest medicine she had. The *Kestrel* slid out of Sallow Point with her running lights doubled in the flat evening water, and Ansel stood at the wheel and did not look back at the town, because looking back was a habit she'd trained out of herself the way you train a horse out of shying — patiently, brutally, over years.

But the past didn't need her to look back. The past had learned to come alongside.

*Halcyon Sound.* She said the name to herself, silently, in the rhythm of the engine, and each repetition peeled something back. Her father's chart tubes. Her mother's grip on her shoulder. A white hull standing out of a harbor on a morning when the adults in her house had stopped speaking to each other in anything but weather reports. She'd been a child. Children collect the pieces of a shipwreck without knowing what the ship was. She had spent twenty years not assembling them.

And now a scout with a diver's hands had said the name aloud, twice, and watched her face while he did it, and told her that somebody was coming on that ship who wasn't hunting wrecks alone.

Six crates of paper whispered in her hold, bound for a survey office, paid for at three times the rate. The river breathed against the hull. Somewhere behind her, in the darkening south, a white ship with a black funnel was standing in toward a coast that remembered everything.

Ansel put the wheel over, reading the water by the last grey light, and the smooth patch off the point where the current lied — she saw it, and went wide, and the *Kestrel* passed over the drowned and the hidden and the waiting, and the past tapped on her hull, gently, like a knock at a door she had nailed shut twenty years ago.

It was not going to stop knocking.
