## 3. Six Tides

The forecast came in on the morning tide, as forecasts did in Saltmere — not by wireless, which the harbor office had never trusted, but by a boy with a chalkboard slung from his neck like a peddler's tray, walking the quay and calling out the numbers to anyone who'd listen.

"Spring tide Thursday! Lowest in ten years! Lowest since the winter of the great die-off!" He was maybe eleven, with a voice that cracked on *ten* and a chalk dust mustache he seemed proud of. "Harbor office says stand clear of your moorings! Fishermen say bring your grandfathers' stories!"

Ansel heard him from the deck of the Kestrel, where she was re-lashing cargo covers gone slack in the night wind, and her hands went still on the rope.

Lowest in ten years.

She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Ten years ago she'd been nineteen, newly forged, newly fled, learning to answer to a name that wasn't hers until it fit like a callus. Ten years ago the coast had last been stripped this bare, and she had been too far south, too busy becoming someone else, to hear what the sea had uncovered.

The boy moved on down the quay, chanting his numbers. Ansel finished the lashing, tied it off with a hitch her father had taught her before she could tie her own shoes, and stood looking at the water in the harbor basin as if it might confess something.

It didn't. Water never did. That was the whole trouble with water.

* * *

Saltmere was a town built on the assumption that the sea would occasionally give things back. The houses nearest the shore were raised on pilings scarred green with salt rot; their ground floors held nets, crab pots, and the particular furniture of waiting — chairs facing windows, windows facing water. The town's one street of shops sold rope, diesel, bait, and luck, in roughly equal quantities, and the luck shop did the best business during spring tides, when the receding water exposed the mudflats and everything the sea had borrowed: anchors, propellers, a church bell lost off the point in some grandfather's time, the occasional rib of a hull too old for anyone to claim.

Ansel had seen two such tides in her years on the coast, and both times she had made sure to be elsewhere. It wasn't difficult. A captain who never stayed anywhere long could arrange to be elsewhere for almost any occasion. She had missed weddings, funerals, the opening of a bridge she'd have liked to see, and every spring tide for a decade, all without anyone noticing the pattern, because no one watched her closely enough to find one.

But Vey was watching her now. She'd known it since the crates.

He found her that afternoon at the Saltwater Table, the harborside room where captains ate what the kitchen decided and paid what the blackboard said. He dropped into the bench across from her without asking, the way men did when they wanted you to know they considered permission a formality, and set a folded paper between them.

"You've seen the boy's board," he said. Not a question.

"I've seen a boy with a chalkboard."

"Same thing." Vey was a lean man with the sun-scoured look of someone who'd spent his twenties diving and his thirties apologizing for it — the pressure had left its signature in the burst capillaries of his ears and a certain careful way of moving, as if his body were a boat he no longer entirely trusted. He ordered two coffees without consulting her. "Ten-year tide. You know what that means out here."

"It means moorings part and fools wade out onto flats that fill faster than they empty."

"It means the Hollow." He said it lightly, the way a man says the name of a woman everyone at the table has loved and none will discuss. "You've heard the stories."

Ansel drank her coffee. It was terrible, overboiled, tasting of the pot more than the bean. "I've heard fishermen talk."

"Everyone's heard fishermen talk. I'm asking what you've heard."

She looked at him then, fully, letting him see the flat patience she'd spent nine years perfecting — the look of a woman with nothing behind her eyes but weather and freight rates. "Vey. I haul cargo. Crates, drums, timber, once a prize bull who cried the whole crossing. I don't dive, I don't salvage, and I don't chase stories."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You don't." He unfolded the paper. It was a chart — a proper survey chart, new ink, the kind the fleet produced — and he turned it so she could read the coastline she knew better than her own face. The reef line east of Greyhaven. The deep water beyond it. And there, where no chart she'd ever bought marked anything but open hazard, a small penciled circle with a notation in a surveyor's hand: *anomalous depth, unverified*.

"The Hollow," Vey said again. "A cave in the reef face. Dry at extreme low water — or so the story goes. The old-timers say it charges admission." He smiled. "In drowned men's debts."

"That's poetry, not navigation."

"Poetry is how coasts keep their secrets." He tapped the penciled circle. "The fleet thinks it's nothing. Rell Danforth thinks it's nothing. But the survey ship picked up an anomaly on the sonar, and anomalies are how I eat. So I'm asking around. And everywhere I ask, captain, I keep coming back to the same strange fact."

"Which is?"

"Which is that nobody knows anything about the Hollow, but everybody looks at me like I've said a word they weren't supposed to hear." He leaned back as the coffee arrived, wrapped both hands around the cup, and studied her over the rim with the frank, unhurried attention of a man reading water. "You didn't look surprised just now. You looked like a woman deciding how much to admit."

"I looked like a woman drinking bad coffee."

"You did that too."

Ansel set down her cup. The trap of curiosity, her father used to say — though he'd said it about engines, about the way a mechanic couldn't leave a knocking sound alone until he'd opened up the whole block and found the truth, even when the truth cost more than the engine. Curiosity was the only debt that collected itself.

"Thursday," she said, standing, gathering her gloves. "I'll be forty miles upriver by Thursday. There's cargo waiting."

"There's always cargo waiting," Vey said. "That's the beauty of cargo. It waits for anybody." He folded his chart away, unhurried, and added, almost gently: "Partnership stands if you change your mind. Fifty-fifty. My gear, your boat. I've seen how you handle the Kestrel in fog, captain. There aren't five pilots on this coast who read water like you do, and I'd bet my gear there isn't one who reads it like you."

She was already three steps away when he said the next part, quiet enough that it was clearly meant to reach her anyway:

"Your father's charts are still the best thing ever drawn of that reef line. Funny, isn't it — a man dies, and his daughter won't so much as look at the water he drowned in."

Ansel kept walking. Her boots sounded very loud on the boards of the quay, and the harbor smell — diesel, brine, fish blood, tar — rose around her like a language she'd sworn off speaking.

* * *

That night she took out the chart.

She told herself she wouldn't. She cooked dinner she didn't eat, checked the lashings twice, logged the day's run in a hand that wasn't hers — *M. Calloway, master, Kestrel* — and then, somewhere past midnight, with Toma asleep aft and the anchor light burning its small red eye above the water, she unlocked the drawer under the wheelhouse berth and spread the old chart on the table.

Her father's chart. The only thing of his she owned besides the knot in her fingers and the shape of his laugh, which she was losing year by year and could do nothing about.

It was a working chart, not a pretty one — soft as cloth at the folds, mended with tape gone amber, annotated in his small fierce pencil hand: soundings corrected, hazards noted, a sandbar here that no official survey had ever found, marked simply *K. only* — *Kestrel only*, though the Kestrel hadn't been his boat then, or anyone's. She'd pored over this chart as a child, tracing the coastline while he talked, and she knew every mark of his hand the way she knew her own reflection.

And near the reef line east of Greyhaven — she'd checked a hundred times over the years, and checked again now, telling herself it was habit — there was nothing. No cave. No Hollow. Just clean water-colored paper and his neat soundings.

Except.

Except that tonight, under the anchor light's red glow, she saw something she had somehow never seen, in all the hundreds of nights she'd studied this paper. A smudge. A place where the chart's surface had been rubbed — erased, carefully, thoroughly, but not quite completely. Pencil ghost. If you tilted the page against the light, the paper's tooth caught differently there, faintly rougher, the way skin does where a scar was scraped away.

Someone had erased something from her father's chart. And her father had let them — or hadn't noticed, which was impossible, because Elias Iversen noticed everything on paper, everything on water, everything.

Ansel sat back. The cabin felt smaller than it had a moment ago, the river sounds louder — the slap of water against the hull, the far hoot of a night bird, Toma turning over in his bunk aft.

*Charges admission,* Vey had said, *in drowned men's debts.*

Her father had drowned. That was the story, the whole architecture of her life built on it: the Meridian lost with all hands off the eastern reef, the storm, the grief, the silence her mother had kept afterward like a second widowhood, the way her mother had never once, in all the years before her own death, spoken the wreck's name aloud. Ansel had inherited that silence along with the forged papers and the fear of harbors. She had built a career on it. Never stay. Never register honestly. Never, ever go back to the water where the Meridian lay — because the water where the Meridian lay was full of questions, and questions were the one cargo she couldn't afford.

And now a survey ship was mapping the coast. Now a salvage scout with burst capillaries in his ears was buying her coffee and saying her father's name like a key tried in a lock. Now the lowest tide in ten years was four days out, and somewhere under the eastern reef, something her father had erased — or allowed to be erased — was waiting to be bared.

She should burn the thought out right now. That was the discipline. That was the whole trick of her survival: want nothing, ask nothing, follow nothing down. She had watched curiosity drown better captains than herself.

Ansel folded the chart along its amber-creased folds, put it back in the drawer, locked it, and sat in the dark listening to the water.

Four days. Forty miles of river between her and the reef line. Cargo waiting, as Vey said — cargo always waited.

She lay down and did not sleep, and somewhere in the gray hour before dawn she admitted the thing she'd been refusing since the boy with the chalkboard: she was going to be at the eastern reef on Thursday. She had known it since the word *ten*. All the refusing in between had just been the price of admission.

The Hollow charged in drowned men's debts. Fine. She had a debt of her own, ten years compounding.

The question — the question that followed her down into the thin sleep she finally got, and stood over her like a creditor — was who else would be paying Thursday's tide. Because Vey had a fleet behind him, and fleets did not send scouts to buy coffee for women they intended to forget.

And somewhere in the dark water of her mind, another question surfaced behind it, colder: *who had held the eraser?*

She dreamed, briefly, of her mother's handwriting — though in the dream she didn't yet know it was her mother's, only that it was a hand she almost recognized, writing something urgent on the edge of a map, and she woke with the words already dissolving, and the tide clock ticking, and three days left to decide what kind of fool she was going to be.
