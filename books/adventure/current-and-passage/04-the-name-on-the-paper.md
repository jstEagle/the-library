## 4. The Name on the Paper

The harbor office at Saltmere smelled of lamp oil and old paper, and Ansel hated it the way she hated all rooms with windows too small to see a tide through.

She had docked the *Kestrel* at the third berth because it was closest to the office door and farthest from the customs skiff, and she climbed the salt-stained steps with her papers rolled in an oilcloth sleeve under her arm, the way she always carried them — loosely, carelessly, as if they were nothing more than ballast for her hand. That was the trick of it. Papers were like knives. You showed them best when you seemed to have forgotten you were holding one.

Inside, a ceiling fan turned with the lazy reluctance of something that had given up on the coast years ago. A clerk dozed behind a counter scarred by decades of cargo hooks. And at the end desk, beneath a green-shaded lamp, sat the inspector.

She knew him before he looked up. Port inspectors had a posture — a way of sitting that said *I am paid to be unimpressed* — and this one wore it like a uniform older than his coat. Grey at the temples, hands flat on the desk, a ledger open before him and a magnifying loupe hanging from a cord around his neck like a saint's medal. His nameplate read **HARROW** in brass letters gone brown with tarnish.

"Vessel?" he said, without looking up.

"*Kestrel*. River freighter, forty-one feet. Inbound from the delta runs."

"Master's name?"

"Ansel Voss." She said it evenly, the way you say your own name when it isn't quite yours — no emphasis anywhere, no hitch. Nine years of practice lived inside those two words.

He looked up then. Not at her face — at her hands. Inspectors did that. Hands told them whether a woman actually worked her own deck or merely owned someone who did. Hers were rope-burned and tar-cracked, and whatever he saw there seemed to satisfy something, because he gestured her forward with two fingers.

"Registration, license, cargo manifest."

She laid them out on the scarred wood, one by one, unhurried. The registration first — that was the oldest forgery, bought in a river town whose name she'd let the rain wash out of her memory, and it was good work, good enough that three harbors had stamped it without a second glance. The pilot's license next. That was the dangerous one, because a license was a living document; it got endorsements, renewals, ink from clerks who changed every season. Forgeries aged badly the way fruit did. You had to keep moving before the rot showed.

Harrow picked up the license and held it to the lamp.

Ansel watched the fan turn. She counted its rotations the way other people counted breaths. Outside, gulls argued over something dead on the pilings, and the tide was making, and every instinct she owned said *walk out now, cast off now, be gone before this man finishes reading.* But running from a routine inspection was how you turned a seam into a confession. So she stood, and she waited, and she let the fan count for her.

Harrow tilted the license. Tilted it back. He took up the loupe and set it against his eye, and the lamplight caught the lens and threw a small bright coin onto the desk between them.

"You've been piloting the delta bar," he said. "Spring runs."

"Eleven seasons."

"The Sallow Cut at flood stage. The narrows below Corren's Teeth." He named the waterways slowly, tasting each one, watching her face over the rim of the license as though her answers might arrive somewhere other than her mouth. "That's deep-water certification territory. Your endorsement here is coastal only."

"I hold a delta rider. Stamped at Greyhaven, four years back."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise, exactly. Recognition of a landmark. "Greyhaven," he repeated. "You'll forgive me. We cross-check everything against the registry rolls now. New directive. Fleet business." He said *fleet* the way a man says *weather coming*, flatly, with respect and dread in equal measure. "There's a salvage consortium mapping the whole coast. They want clean records. They pay for thoroughness."

He opened his ledger and began to write.

Ansel felt the floor tilt — not literally, but the way a deck tilts when a swell comes under you from a direction the wind didn't promise. *Fleet business.* She'd heard the phrase twice already this month, once from a clerk downcoast and once from a drunk mate on a coal barge, and both times it had slid past her like driftwood. Now it had a weight. Somewhere out beyond the breakwater, ships were being paid to look closely at things, and looking closely was the one trade she could not survive.

Harrow turned the license over. And there — she saw it even from across the desk, saw it the way you see a hairline crack in a hull you're about to trust with your life — there was the seam.

The renewal stamp. Four years old, supposedly. But the ink had been applied by a stamp that had been re-inked unevenly, and the left edge of the harbor seal carried a double impression, a ghost ring, the kind of flaw that happened when a forger pressed twice because the first press came up light. A real clerk would never press twice. A real clerk didn't care enough.

Harrow saw it too. Of course he did. He'd probably seen it the moment she laid the paper down, and everything since — the questions about the delta, the mention of the fleet — had been prologue. He set the loupe down with a small deliberate click, like a man setting down a tool he would not need again.

"Interesting stamp," he said.

"It's four years old. Ink fades."

"Ink fades." He nodded, agreeable, terrible. "Seals don't ghost, though. Not real ones." He leaned back in his chair, and the chair complained, and outside a bell buoy clanged twice on the ebb. "Here's what happens next, Master Voss. I write a discrepancy report. It goes to the regional registrar. The registrar pulls the original roll from Greyhaven, and either your delta rider exists or it doesn't. If it doesn't, your license is void, your vessel is detained, and you're invited to explain yourself to people whose salaries depend on finding explanations. That process takes — oh, ten days? Two weeks? Long enough for the salvage fleet's surveyors to finish their mapping and move along." He smiled without warmth. "Unless."

The word hung in the lamplight like smoke.

Ansel had been poor before. She knew poverty the way sailors know weather — intimately, by its signs, by the feel of it in the rigging. She knew exactly what lay in the strongbox beneath the *Kestrel*'s floorboards: eleven coins of heavy gold and a scatter of silver, the residue of the well-paid run she'd taken off Vey's sealed crates, minus what she owed Toma and what the engine parts had eaten. Enough to winter on, if the winter was kind. Enough to run on, if the running was cheap.

It was not enough to buy an inspector. But it might be enough to buy a delay, and delay was the currency she'd traded in all her life.

"How much does thoroughness cost," she said quietly, "when the record turns out to be clean after all?"

Harrow didn't answer at once. He looked at the fan. He looked at the window, where the grey light of the overcast coast pressed against the glass like a face. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into a register meant for neither of them to remember later.

"There's a widow on Marsh Lane. Her husband drowned off the Teeth two winters back. The fleet's compensation board has been sitting on her claim for fourteen months. Paperwork, they say. A hundred and twenty crowns would clear it by Friday, and I would find — " he tapped the license, gently, right on the ghosted seal — "that my eye was tired today, and the stamp is consistent with the era, and no report is warranted."

*A hundred and twenty crowns.* More than half of everything she had. Ansel thought of the strongbox, of the arithmetic she'd done in the dark of the wheelhouse last night while Toma slept and the anchor light burned: *eleven weeks of fuel, six weeks of food, nothing left over for the next port's fees.* She thought of the survey vessels out there on the grey water, drawing the coast line by line, and of the name she'd recognized on Vey's manifest — a name from her father's era, from the world before the wreck, before the silence, before she'd become a woman made entirely of aliases.

And underneath all of it, rising like cold water into a bilge, came the memory she kept moored where she couldn't see it.

*Her father at the rail of the Meridian, the summer before the end, laughing, saying:* **A captain's name is the only cargo you can't insure, little fish. Guard it.** *And her mother standing apart on the dock, arms crossed against a wind that wasn't blowing, saying nothing — her mother who could fill any silence with talk except the ones that mattered, who went quiet for eleven years, quiet as ballast, quiet as the drowned.*

Ansel opened the oilcloth sleeve. She counted out a hundred and twenty crowns onto the scarred desk, and the sound of each coin was small and final, like rivets going into a coffin.

"For the widow," she said.

Harrow swept the coins into a drawer without counting them. He took up a pen, wrote one line in his ledger — she caught only the words *no discrepancy* — and slid her papers back across the desk with the smoothness of a man returning a borrowed knife.

"Saltmere thanks you for your visit, Master Voss. Mind the bar on your way out. Spring tides are coming early this year." He paused, and for one moment the inspector's mask slipped and she saw the man beneath it, tired and complicit and human. "For what it's worth — whoever cut that license spent money on the wrong details. The paper's perfect. The stamp's the tell. Fleet surveyors won't look at stamps, though. They look at names." He met her eyes. "They're compiling a register of every master working this coast. Cross-referenced against old crew rolls. Meridian-era rolls."

The room went very still, or she did.

"They asked me yesterday," Harrow continued, mild as milk, "if I'd processed anyone unusual lately. I told them Saltmere sees nothing but coal barges and fishermen." He closed the ledger. "I'd suggest your next port be one where nobody processes anything."

* * *

She walked down the harbor steps with her spine straight and her lungs full of iron.

The *Kestrel* rode at her berth, patched and patient, and Toma was in the engine well with his shoulders buried in the guts of the cooling pump, swearing at it in the gentle domestic way he swore at things he intended to fix rather than things he intended to abandon. He surfaced when her boots hit the deck, wiped his hands on a rag that had been black longer than it had ever been white, and read her face the way he read gauges — accurately, and without comment until the reading demanded one.

"That bad?"

"A hundred and twenty crowns bad."

Toma whistled low. "We had two hundred and forty."

"We have a hundred and twenty."

He looked at her for a long moment. Nine years he'd stood beside her, and in nine years he had never once asked why a woman with her hands and her instincts ran under a name that fit her like a borrowed coat. He'd watched her pay cash in ports where credit was offered. He'd watched her leave harbors at night with the tide still making. He'd watched her flinch — just barely, just once — whenever anyone mentioned the Meridian, the old wreck that the whole coast treated as legend and she treated as a wound.

"Ansel." He used the alias the way he always did, but today it sounded heavier in his mouth. "The fleet's buying up berths down the quay. Three survey ships and a tender, flying Danforth colors. They're asking questions at every office from here to the delta. Questions about wrecks." He hesitated. "Questions about crews. Old crews."

The wind came off the water and found the gap between her collar and her neck, cold as a hand.

"Then we aren't here when they finish asking," she said. "Get the pump buttoned up. We make the evening ebb."

Toma nodded slowly, and bent back into the engine well, and said nothing else. But she felt the question follow her into the wheelhouse like a cat that had learned the route: *what are we running from, Captain, and why does it wear your father's century?*

At the wheel, Ansel unrolled her father's chart across the binnacle — the one surviving sheet, soft as cloth from handling, annotated in his sure dark hand. And there, as always, her eye went to the second handwriting. The lighter script in the margins, slanting the other way, marking depths and headings in a hand she had told herself for nine years belonged to some clerk, some navigator, some stranger.

*Fleet business,* Harrow had said. *Meridian-era rolls,* he had said.

Outside, the evening ebb began to draw the water off the flats, and the *Kestrel* leaned at her lines like a dog feeling the slip of its chain. Ansel put her hand flat on the chart, over the strange second handwriting, and gave the order to cast off.
