## 5. Old Charts, Older Lies

The chart lived in a oilcloth sleeve inside the Kestrel's chart locker, beneath the false bottom Ansel had built herself the first winter she owned the boat outright — the winter she learned that papers could be searched and wood could lie for you. She had not unrolled it in three years. Not because she'd forgotten what was on it. Because she remembered too well.

Now, in the lamplit cabin while Toma slept off his watch and rain needled the deck above like a slow drumming of small bones, she drew it out and laid it flat on the table, weighting the corners with the salt dish, the brass ashtray, her own two fists.

Her father's handwriting came up to meet her first, the way it always did. Eben Iversen had written the way he steered: bold, economical, contemptuous of anything that wasted motion. *Shoal shifts after NE gales — sound it fresh.* *Channel marker lies; trust the church spire.* The letters were tall and slightly forward-leaning, as if even ink were in a hurry to get somewhere. She had grown up tracing those letters with a fingertip, learning the coast through them before she could read anything else. This was the chart he'd carried aboard the Meridian's sister runs, the one that had come home without him because it had never gone out with him — left behind in a drawer at Greyhaven, in the house where her mother had stopped speaking in full sentences and started speaking only in weather.

Ansel smoothed the paper flat. It was soft as cloth now, worn along its folds into pale rivers of its own. And there, in the margins, in the open water east of the reef line, in the blank spaces where honest charts said nothing because there was nothing to say —

Someone else had written.

She had known this for years, the way you know a scar is there without looking at it. A second hand, fine and slanted, in a brown-black ink that had faded differently than her father's iron-gall blue. The annotations clustered around one stretch of coast like birds around a gutted fish. Numbers. Tide references. Little arrows pointing at nothing visible, at water that every legitimate survey called empty.

*+3.1 at neap, −0.4 at spring.* An arrow. *Not before the fourth hour.*

And beneath it, smaller, almost hidden in the fold-crease: *the door opens from below.*

Ansel sat back. The lamp guttered in a draft from the companionway and steadied itself, and the shadows swung across the chart and settled again, and the second handwriting lay there looking up at her with the patience of something that had waited twenty years and could wait twenty more.

Whose hand was it?

She had asked herself the question a hundred times over the years and answered it a hundred ways. A rival skipper. A pilot her father had hired. Some salvage man trading secrets for shares. She had never once let herself consider the answer that kept surfacing anyway, the way drowned things surface, no matter how far down you sink them: that the hand was familiar. That the way it crossed its sevens, the little upward flick at the end of each numeral, the way it crowded its figures close together as if afraid of being overheard —

That she had seen that hand writing grocery lists. Writing her name on school forms. Writing, once, in the front of her childhood prayer book, *For Ansel, who must learn to hold on and let go in the same motion.*

No. Her mother had never been on the water. Her mother had hated the water, feared it, kept her back to the harbor windows in every room of that house. That was the story. That was the load-bearing wall of everything Ansel had built her life against: her father taken by the sea and by treachery, her mother broken by grief, and Ansel herself born with salt in the blood and a debt to collect from whoever had betrayed him.

She bent closer. Held the lamp low so the light raked sideways across the paper, the way Toma had taught her to check a hull plate for pitting — flaws show best when light comes in shallow and mean.

Under the raking light, more of the second hand emerged. Faint impressions where the pen had pressed hard enough to emboss the paper even where the ink had died. She followed them with a fingertip, reading braille more than script. A cluster of figures near the reef line that matched nothing on any tide table she'd ever bought — but matched, with an ugly little click she felt in her teeth, the numbers Vey had let drop over cards two nights ago. The lowest tide in a decade. The Hollow. The cave that charged admission in drowned men's debts.

Her mother's handwriting pointed at the Hollow.

Or someone's did. Someone whose sevens crossed themselves the same way.

Ansel straightened, and her knee hit the table, and the salt dish slid and she caught it without looking, the way her hands did things her mind wasn't consulted on. Nine years on this boat. Nine years of never sleeping two nights in the same anchorage if she could help it, of registering under a name stitched together from a dead aunt and a favorable clerk, of flinching every time a port official said *Iversen* in a tone that suggested the name was a question rather than a fact. She had told herself all of it was about the forgery. About the practical dangers of a forged master's license in waters thick with inspectors.

But the forgery was new. The running was old. The running predated the fake papers the way the keel predates the paint.

She had been running since Greyhaven, since the funeral that wasn't a funeral because they'd never recovered enough of him to bury, since her mother had stood at the window with her back to the sea and said, in the flattest voice Ansel had ever heard from a living mouth: *Whatever anyone tells you happened out there, remember that I loved him.* Not *I grieve him.* Not *I miss him.* *I loved him* — past tense, defensive, an answer to a question nobody had asked yet.

Ansel rolled the chart closed along its old creases, gently, the way you'd fold a letter you weren't finished reading. She did not put it back under the false bottom. She put it inside her sea chest, under her spare shirt, where a thief would have to want it specifically.

Above her, the rain changed its mind and became serious.

* * *

Vey stood in Rell Danforth's day cabin aboard the *Corvus* and tried to make himself comfortable, which was a thing the cabin permitted and the commander did not.

The *Corvus* rode at anchor in the roads off Greyhaven, and even at anchor she managed to look like she was going somewhere fast — black hull, raked masts, the salvage fleet's pennant snapping at her truck with the impatience of a dog on a chain. Inside, though, the day cabin was all warm lamplight and good wood and a decanter of something amber, because Rell Danforth believed comfort was a tool like any other and kept his sharpened.

"Sit," Danforth said, and poured two glasses without asking, which meant the meeting would be long and the information would be extracted slowly, like a splinter.

Vey sat. He took the glass. He was forty-one years old and had spent thirty of those years reading men the way other people read water, and he still couldn't read Danforth, which bothered him more than he showed. Most powerful men wanted things you could see — money, rank, reputation, revenge. You could steer around those. Danforth wanted something with no surface markers, and Vey had learned to navigate him by feel alone, by the pressure change before weather.

"The crates ran smooth," Vey said. "Your cargo's upriver. The captain took payment and didn't ask questions."

"I know she didn't ask questions." Danforth turned his glass, watching the lamplight move in it. "I paid enough that asking would've been rude. What I want to know is what she asked *before*. Before my cargo. Before your cards game."

"She doesn't ask much of anything. Keeps her own counsel. Good hands on the wheel — better than good. I watched her take the Kestrel through the Sedge cut at night, fog coming down, no pilot, no lights. She reads water like other people read print." Vey paused. "There's a rumor she's never slept twice in the same harbor. Nine years running this coast and no one can say where she winters."

"And her name?"

"Kestrel's registered to an Iversen. First name's a clerical smudge — different spellings in different ports, which usually means a woman who pays clerks to be careless." Vey turned the glass in his fingers. "But here's the part I keep chewing. When I mentioned the survey vessel mapping the coast — when I said the name *Corvus* — she went still. Not startled. Still. Like a heron when the shadow crosses. Startled birds fly. That one went still because stillness was how she survived whatever taught her about that ship."

Danforth set his glass down without drinking. "Say the name again."

"*Corvus.*"

"No. The other one."

Vey looked at him. Outside, the rain worked at the deck overhead, and somewhere aft a winch cable ticked against its mast with the swell. "Iversen," Vey said.

Danforth rose and went to the chart table in the corner — a real chart table, bolted down, the kind of furniture that told you the man used it rather than displayed it. He opened a drawer and took out a folder, thin, edges soft with handling, and laid it beside the lamp but did not open it.

"How long have you run cargo on this coast?" he asked.

"Long enough to owe money in six ports and be owed favors in four."

"Then you'll know the Meridian."

Everyone knew the Meridian. Twenty-two years down with all hands but one, lost off the reef line in weather that shouldn't have sunk her, in water her captain knew better than his own kitchen. The wreck had become legend, then a cautionary tale, then a riddle, and riddles on this coast accreted treasure stories the way hulls accreted barnacles.

"Eben Iversen commanded her," Danforth said. "He was also my partner. Half shares in a salvage venture that was going to make us both rich enough to retire kings. Then the Meridian went down, the venture died with her, and everyone agreed it was a tragedy." He opened the folder at last, and Vey saw that it held perhaps ten pages, and that Danforth touched them the way a man touches a wound to check whether it's healed. "Tragedies are convenient. They end questions. But I have spent twenty-two years paying for a tragedy I don't believe in, and lately the water's been giving things back."

"The spring tide," Vey said. "The Hollow."

"You've heard the legends."

"I've heard the legends. I've also seen the ledge myself, two days ago, at low water. There's a cave mouth under the reef shelf that no survey has ever charted, and I found timber on the outer rocks with a stamp I couldn't read but didn't need to." Vey drank, finally, because his throat had gone dry. "You already know. You've known longer than me."

"I've suspected. Suspicion is cheap. Proof costs." Danforth closed the folder. "Here's what proof costs right now. In six days the tide drops lower than it's dropped in a decade, and the monsoon comes behind it and seals that cave until the sea changes its mind, which will be never in our lifetimes. So in six days I intend to be inside the Hollow, and I intend to find out what happened to my partner and my money and my reputation, and I do not intend to share the finding with anyone who hasn't earned it." He looked up, and the lamplight made his eyes hard to read, which Vey suspected was the point of the lamplight. "Which brings us to the woman."

"The riverboat captain."

"There was a daughter. Ansel. Eight years old when the Meridian went down. Mother took her away from Greyhaven within the year — went inland, went quiet, went nowhere anyone could find. The daughter would be—" Danforth's jaw moved as he counted. "Thirty. Thirty-one. Running boats is a trade that gets into families like rot gets into timber." He slid a single sheet from the folder and turned it so Vey could see: a harbor registration abstract, years old, the ink furred with damp. One entry circled. *Iversen.*

"I want everything," Danforth said quietly. "Every port she's cleared, every cargo she's hauled, every clerk she's bribed, every sandbar she knows that isn't on the charts. I want to know what she eats and what she fears and who she writes to, if anyone. Because either that woman is Eben Iversen's daughter come home by accident, or she's come home on purpose, and either way she is sailing toward the one piece of water on this coast that matters, and I need to know what she carries." He tapped the folder. "He kept records. Eben kept records of everything — it was his virtue and it will be his epitaph. If any paper survived that sinking, it's in that cave, or it's in that woman's keeping. And Vey—"

"Sir."

"If you find her heading for the reef line, you send word before she clears the last buoy. Not after. Before." Danforth picked up his glass at last and drank, and for a moment something moved behind his face that was not calculation — something older and more tired, the look of a man standing at a grave he'd dug himself and finding it occupied by someone else. "I buried that man once already. I won't have his ghost telling the story without me there to argue."

Vey walked out into the rain feeling the pressure change he always felt around Danforth, the sense of weather assembling somewhere offshore. His launch boy had the engine warm. As they pulled away from the *Corvus*'s side, Vey looked back at the black ship riding her lights, then ahead toward the harbor town, toward the waterfront where a certain riverboat lay among the working craft with a patched seam and a captain who went still instead of flying.

*Iversen,* he thought. *Ansel.*

He said it silently, testing the weight of it, the way you test a doubtful mooring line — not pulling hard, just taking up the slack to see if it holds.

It held. God help him, it held, and he found he was already deciding, without having decided, which side of the rope he'd be standing on when it came taut.

Behind him the *Corvus* signaled her fleet: two lamps, three, two. *Prepare for survey operations.* The message crawled down the anchored line of hulls, light talking to light in the rain, and in the town above the waterfront a single window in a harborside inn stayed lit past midnight, where a woman sat with an old chart spread before her and her father's ghost on one side of the table and, though she did not yet know it, her mother's handwriting waiting in the folds like a letter twenty-two years late.

Six tides, now five and falling.
