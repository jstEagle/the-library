## 6. The Harbor She Avoided

The Kestrel's port shaft gave out eleven miles north of Greyhaven, with a sound like a bone breaking underwater — a soft, wet crack that Toma felt through the deck plates before Ansel heard it at all. He was already at the engine hatch when she came down, and he didn't look up, just held out one grease-blackened hand as if to say: don't ask yet.

She asked anyway. "How bad?"

"Bad enough that I can't fix it on the hook." He wiped his hands on a rag that had been white once, in some other decade. "I need a machine shop. Real bearings. And I need them before the spring tide window closes, or none of this matters."

Ansel stood in the engine room's amber light and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for nine years. The nearest machine shop worth trusting sat behind the breakwater of Greyhaven Harbor — the town she'd charted her whole life around, the way water charts around a reef. Every route she'd ever drawn bent away from it. Every contract she'd signed had a clause, explicit or not, that said *no deliveries to Greyhaven*.

"Alternatives?" she said, though she knew there weren't any.

"Cole's Landing has a shop, but Cole's man is a butcher. He'd have us down to one working shaft for a month, and we'd miss the tide entirely." Toma finally looked up. His face was carefully blank, which is how she knew he understood exactly what he was asking of her. Nine years together on the water teaches a man what not to say. "Or we go to Greyhaven."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere above them, gulls were arguing over something.

"How long for repairs?"

"Two days. Three if the shop's backed up."

Three days. Three tides. She thought about the forecast she'd read off the harbor board at the last port — the lowest spring tides in a decade, the kind of water that pulled back curtains the sea kept closed for generations. She thought about her father's chart, rolled and lashed under her bunk, with its two handwritings arguing across the vellum like two voices she couldn't silence.

"Greyhaven," she said.

Toma nodded once and turned back to the engine, and neither of them said anything else, because saying it aloud would have made it real, and they both preferred it as weather — something coming, unavoidable, that you simply dressed for.

* * *

They came in on the afternoon flood, riding a gray-green sea under a sky the color of pewter, and Greyhaven assembled itself out of the haze the way it must have assembled itself out of her father's eyes thirty years ago: the breakwater first, a long black spine of granite with its beacon blinking its old patient rhythm; then the masts, a thicket of them against the low hills; then the town itself, climbing its slope in terraces of slate roofs and salt-bleached clapboard, the cannery chimneys standing up like fingers pointing at nothing.

Ansel took the Kestrel in slow, one engine turning, and found that her hands knew the channel without consulting her. That was the worst part. Not the fear — she'd expected the fear, had braced for it like a woman bracing against a wave. What she hadn't prepared for was the familiarity. Her body remembered this harbor. Her hands remembered where the channel dog-legged around the oyster beds, where the current set hard toward the fish dock on an ebb, where the water went from brown to green in a line so sharp you could see it from the wheelhouse. She had learned all of this before she could write, in a boat her father let her stand at the wheel of while he smoked his pipe and pretended not to watch.

She moored at the commercial quay, far down from the harbor office, choosing the berth the way a fugitive chooses a seat in a tavern — back to the wall, sight lines clear, one quick path to open water. Toma went ashore to find the machinist. Ansel stayed aboard until dusk, telling herself it was prudence, knowing it was cowardice wearing prudence's coat.

When she finally stepped onto Greyhaven stone, the town received her without ceremony. That was the strange mercy of it: nobody looked twice. She was just another riverboat captain in a salt-stained coat, another stranger among the crates and fish carts and the men mending nets by lamplight. A ghost story doesn't recognize its own ghost walking past in daylight.

But the streets knew her feet.

She walked up from the quay along Harborside Row because it was the only way she knew, and the cobbles rose under her boots in the old worn pattern, hollowed by a century of feet, and somewhere in the middle of the row her stride lengthened without permission, taking the long step over the sunken drain grate outside what used to be Pell's Chandlery — a step she'd learned at seven years old, jumping that grate to make her father laugh. She stopped in the middle of the street like a woman who'd walked into glass.

A boy hauling a handcart muttered at her to move. She moved.

The chandlery was gone — replaced by a net loft, by the smell of tar and hemp instead of rope and paint. But the drain grate was still there, still sunken, still waiting for someone to jump it. She stood looking at it for too long. Then she walked on, and the town began, very quietly, to take her apart.

There was the seawall where the kids fished for sculpin, and she could have named, if she'd let herself, which of those flat stones she'd claimed as hers. There was the corner where the bakery had been, now a chandler's annex, but the smell of bread still lived in the brick somehow, baked into it decades ago and never quite escaped. There was the hill street that led up toward the captain's houses, the big ones with widow's walks, and she made herself not look up it, made herself keep her eyes on the cobbles, because at the top of that street was a house she had inherited at nine years old and had never once, in nineteen years, come back to see.

A woman selling mussels from a barrow glanced at her, glanced again. Ansel turned her collar up and kept walking.

That was the thing about small harbors. They didn't forget faces; they just filed them wrong. In twenty years her face would have changed enough to escape certainty but not enough to escape the itch of recognition. She'd be *someone*, forever, to everyone who looked at her twice. The Iversen girl grown. Or worse — the Iversen girl drowned, they'd say, surely drowned, no other reason a child vanishes off the coast like that — and here she was, walking proof that stories get things wrong.

She bought bread and coffee and a paper of dried figs from three different vendors, using three different accents, an old habit, and each time she spoke she heard her mother's vowels underneath her own, surfacing like something drowned coming home to roost. Her mother had been born forty miles up the coast, but she'd spent her girlhood summers here, in this harbor, and the town had gotten into her voice the way oil gets into a bilge — permanent, traceable, damning.

By full dark Ansel had walked every street she dared and was standing at the top of the lock stairs, looking down at the harbor basin where the Kestrel lay dark among the moored boats, and that was where the lockkeeper found her.

* * *

He came up the stairs slowly, an old man with a lantern in one hand and a cane he clearly resented, and he didn't speak until he was beside her, and then he didn't look at her at all — just set the lantern on the stone rail and looked out at the water the way she was doing, as if the harbor were a book they were both reading.

"Tide'll turn in an hour," he said. "You can hear it change, if you listen. Most folks can't anymore."

Ansel said nothing. She catalogued him the way she catalogued everything: mid-seventies, maybe older. Hands like braided rope. A canvas coat gone soft as cloth-of-gold from years of salt air. A face weathered down to its structure, all planes and patience. And something else — something in the way he stood, square to the water, feet planted, that reminded her so violently of her father that her chest hurt.

"You're the lockkeeper," she said. "Old Maren's post."

"Maren died eight years back. I've had it since." He tapped the rail. "My father had it before him. We keep the gates and we keep the books, and between the two we know more about this harbor than the harbor knows about itself." He turned his head then, slowly, and looked at her with eyes the pale gray of winter water. "You jumped the drain grate outside Pell's today. Nobody jumps that grate anymore. Nobody even knows why it's worth jumping."

Ansel's breath went thin.

"I wasn't sure," the old man said. "All afternoon I wasn't sure. But then you came up the lock stairs and you stood the way Hal Iversen used to stand when he was reading the water — weight on the balls of your feet, chin down, like you're listening to something the rest of us can't hear." He picked up his lantern. "Evening, Ansel. You've got your mother's walk and your father's eyes, and I'd know either one in the dark."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped anchor.

She should have denied it. That was the discipline, that was the whole architecture of her life — the forged papers, the changed accent, the name she'd worn so long it fit like her own skin. *Not Iversen. You're misremembering. Some other family.* She had rehearsed denials for twenty years. They were smooth as sea glass from handling.

What came out instead was silence, and in the silence the harbor filled up with sound — the slap of water on stone, the creak of moored boats, the far-off bell of the breakwater keeping its count — and under all of it, faintly, the sound of her own blood moving, loud as a tide.

"I don't use that name," she said finally. It wasn't a denial. It was the closest thing to surrender she'd offered anyone in nineteen years.

"No," the lockkeeper agreed. "I imagine you don't." He lifted the lantern and studied her face in its yellow light, unhurried, the way he might study a gauge. "Hal brought his boat through my locks more times than I can count. Always paid his tolls early. Always had a word for my father. And always—" he paused, choosing, "—always looked over his shoulder on the way out. Last year he came through, he looked back at this harbor a long time. Like a man memorizing something he expected to lose."

"He didn't lose it," Ansel said. "It lost him."

"Aye. So the story goes." Something moved behind the old man's eyes — a door opening onto a room he didn't mean to show her. "Stories are like charts, girl. Drawn by whoever survived to hold the pen."

The words went through her like cold water through a hull seam. She thought of the chart under her bunk, the two handwritings, the annotations that argued with each other across the vellum. She thought of her mother, who had never once spoken the Meridian's name in Ansel's hearing, who had packed one bag the year after the wreck and taken her daughter inland, away from water, away from harbors, away from every place a boat might be seen and a question might be asked.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.

"Because you came back by water." He said it simply, as if it explained everything, and perhaps to him it did. "And because I'm old, and old men get tired of carrying things." He raised the lantern and started down the stairs, then paused, not looking back. "Come by the lock house tomorrow, when your business is done. There's a packet I've kept twenty years, waiting on exactly this evening. Your father left instructions. He was particular about them — said the one who came back would come back by water, and I was to give it to no one else."

Ansel gripped the stone rail. Below her, the harbor breathed and shifted, and the Kestrel lay at her berth like a sleeping animal, and somewhere out beyond the breakwater the sea was already beginning to think about the great spring tides to come.

"What's in it?" she called down.

The lockkeeper's laugh drifted up, dry as old rope. "If I told you, you'd sleep worse than you already do. Tomorrow, Ansel. The gate opens at slack water." His lantern bobbed away down the stairs, and the dark folded in behind him.

She stood a long time at the top of the lock stairs, and the ache of being known sat in her chest like a coal — painful, and warm, and impossible to put down. Twenty years of being no one had kept her alive. One hour of being Ansel Iversen had made her feel more real than she had since she was nine years old.

She walked back down to the quay in the dark, jumped the drain grate without deciding to, and let herself aboard the Kestrel, where Toma was waiting with news that the machinist could start at dawn — two days, maybe three.

"And the part's ordered?" she asked, hanging up her coat.

"Ordered, paid, promised." Toma looked at her a moment longer than the answer required. "You were gone a while."

"I was walking."

"In Greyhaven."

"Yes."

He waited. She didn't fill the silence, and after a moment he nodded and went below, and Ansel stood alone in the wheelhouse with the harbor lights swimming in the black water, thinking about packets and instructions and a father who had known — somehow, twenty years ago, dead in a flooded cave — that his daughter would come home by water.

At slack water, right on schedule, she heard the lock gates groan open below the town, and the sound went through her like a summons.
