## 7. What the Lockkeeper Kept

The lockkeeper's cottage sat where the canal met the harbor, a squat stone thing furred with salt lichen, its windows fogged from the inside by decades of kettles and coal stoves. Ansel had walked past it twice already that morning, telling herself she was only checking the tide boards, only noting the water level in the outer basin, only doing the ordinary work of a captain whose boat was laid up with a cracked shaft bearing. The third time, she stopped pretending.

The door was open. In Greyhaven, doors were still open — that was one of the things she'd forgotten about the town, or perhaps one of the things she'd spent nine years forgetting on purpose. She could smell woodsmoke and tar and, beneath it, the particular green smell of the marsh at low water, the smell of her entire childhood compressed into a single breath.

"Come in before the wind does," said a voice from within. "It's been trying all week."

Old Maren Hask sat at his table with a mug of something steaming and a spread of papers he was weighting down with brass fittings, washers, bits of chain — the small metal debris of a life spent maintaining locks. He was older than she remembered, which was absurd, because she had no memory of him to be older than. She had left Greyhaven at nineteen, and Maren Hask had been old then too, the way children believe all adults past forty are simply old, fixed, permanent as pilings. Now he looked like what he was: a man who had outlasted most of the people who'd known her father.

"You knew," she said. It wasn't a question. "Last night. At the lock gate. You said my name."

"I said *Ansel Iversen*." He nodded slowly, as if confirming it to himself. "And you didn't flinch. That's how I knew for certain. A stranger would've corrected me. A woman running under a false name would've walked faster." He pushed a chair out from the table with his foot. "Sit. The tea's terrible but it's hot."

She sat. The chair creaked under her, and the sound of it — domestic, unguarded — made her realize how long it had been since she'd sat in a room where someone expected nothing from her but her presence.

"How did you know me?" she asked. "I was a girl when I left."

"You have your father's way of standing on a deck," Maren said. "Weight forward. Like the boat might leave without you if you don't lean into leaving first." He turned his mug a slow quarter-circle on the table. "Also, you've been asking after repairs at three different yards under the name Sandoval, and none of those yards can spell it the same way twice. And you walk past my window every morning looking at the tide board like it owes you money."

Ansel felt the familiar cold slide up her spine — the sensation of being *seen*, of the careful architecture of her false name developing hairline fractures. But Maren's face held no threat in it. Only a patience worn smooth as sea glass.

"My father," she said carefully. "You served with him?"

"Thirty years, off and on. I kept the locks; he ran the river. Half this coast's cargo went through gates I opened for him." His eyes went distant, toward some interior horizon. "I was the last man to talk to him before the *Meridian* sailed. Did you know that?"

Her throat tightened. "No."

"He came through the evening lock at dusk. Wanted to know the bar readings for the morning. I told him: two point one meters, falling, clean swell from the southeast. He wrote it in his little book — he always wrote everything in his little book — and he said—" Maren stopped. His jaw worked once. "He said, *If the spring tide comes early, Maren, mind the Hollow.* And I laughed at him. Told him the Hollow was a story we told green crews to keep them off the reef at night." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious at himself across fifteen years. "I laughed at a dead man's last words to me."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, a gull argued with the wind about something neither would win.

"Maren," Ansel said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, "why am I here? Why did you call me by my name?"

"Because your father left something with me." He rose, joints cracking like small-arms fire, and went to the corner of the room where a sea chest sat under a hanging oilskin. He knelt — slowly, wincing — and dragged the chest out into the light. It was iron-banded, canvas-covered, and heavy with the particular density of things that have not been moved in a very long time. "He gave it to me six months before he sailed. Said, *One day someone will come back by water, and they'll ask about tides, and you'll know.*"

Ansel's heart was suddenly very loud in her ears. "Asked about tides."

"You asked the yard clerk about the spring tide tables yesterday. Lowest water in a decade. Everyone in this town knows it; half of them are afraid of it." He set the chest on the table between them. The lid was locked, but the lock was a joke — a hasp meant to discourage casual hands, nothing more. He broke it open with his thumb and lifted out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with tarred twine, sealed with wax that had gone the color of dried blood.

On the wax, pressed deep and crisp despite the years, was a crest: a kestrel rising over three bars of water.

Her family crest. The same one stamped into the strongbox she'd pulled off the ledge two days ago, now hidden under Toma's floorboards in the *Kestrel*'s engine room.

"Your father's hand did that seal," Maren said quietly. "I watched him do it. He said—" and here the old man's voice dropped, reverent and broken at once, "*for the one who comes back by water.*"

Ansel reached for it and found her hands were shaking. Fifteen years of forged names and borrowed harbors, and here was her own name waiting for her in wax and oilcloth, patient as stone.

She cut the twine with her belt knife. The oilcloth fell away from a bundle: a ledger book swollen with damp but intact, a folded chart so old its paper had gone the color of weak tea, and a single sheet covered in columns of numbers.

"The ledger's his logbook copy," Maren said. "Or part of one. I never read it — never dared. The chart..." He hesitated. "The chart I did look at. Once. When I was young and stupid and thought dead men don't need their secrets."

Ansel unfolded it. It was the same survey chart she carried aboard the *Kestrel* — her father's master chart of the coast, the one she'd inherited through channels she'd never fully questioned. But this version was earlier, cleaner, less annotated. And there, in the lower right quadrant, in her father's unmistakable slanted hand, was an inscription:

*For A., who reads water better than I ever did. When you're ready, the numbers will show you the door.*

Below it, a string of figures that meant nothing and therefore everything:

**4 – 11 – 2 / 7 – 3 – 9 / 1 – 5 – 8**

"A.," Maren said softly. "Ansel."

She stared at the numbers until they blurred. Six digits in three pairs, arranged like coordinates but wrong somehow, incomplete, a lock missing its key. Her eyes dropped to the single sheet — the columns of numbers — and understanding arrived not as thought but as recognition, sudden and total, the way a channel opens up in fog.

Tide tables.

The sheet was a year's tide tables for Greyhaven roads — heights and times, hour by hour, day by day. And the numbers on the chart weren't coordinates at all. They were references. *4–11–2*: month four, day eleven, second high water. *7–3–9*: month seven, day three, ninth... no, there were never nine tides in a day. She frowned, traced the columns again, and then saw it: the third number wasn't a tide count. It was a height. Two meters. Three meters. Eight decimeters.

"It's a cipher," she breathed. "He encoded the cave's opening conditions into the tide tables. Month, day, water height." She looked up at Maren. "The Hollow doesn't just open at any spring tide. It opens at a *specific* tide. A specific height, a specific day. He hid the key inside the almanac."

"And the lowest tide in ten years is coming," Maren said, "in five days."

Ansel's blood went cold and hot at once. Five days. Six tides, Vey had said. And her father — dead fifteen years — had already known, had already planned for it, had already left the answer waiting in a lockkeeper's cottage for a daughter he must have suspected would come home by water someday, running under a name that wasn't hers.

"Why?" she whispered. "Why hide it like this? Why not just tell me?"

Maren's expression shifted — pity and something harder, something almost like fear. "Because whatever's in that cave, girl, he didn't want *anyone* finding it except you. Not the fleet. Not the port authority. Not—" he paused, choosing, "—not everyone who loved him."

The word *loved* landed strangely. She thought of her mother, silent for fifteen years behind a wall of grief and something else Ansel had never been able to name. She thought of the second handwriting on her chart — the annotations she couldn't place, delicate and precise, marking depths her father's bolder hand had never bothered with.

"There's something else," Maren said. He drew a folded paper from his shirt pocket. "Came through the harbor office this morning. Fleet notice. Salvage survey vessel *Corvus* is making for Greyhaven roads — arriving tomorrow, they say. Commanded by—"

He read the name aloud, and the name went through Ansel like cold water through a hull seam.

*Rell Danforth.*

---

She knew the name before she knew why she knew it. It lived in her body somewhere below memory — in the way her shoulders tightened, in the way her mother's voice had gone flat and careful whenever letters came from the salvage fleet, in the photograph she'd found as a child tucked inside her mother's sewing box: a young man in a fleet uniform, laughing on a dock, one arm around her father's shoulders, both of them squinting into sun. On the back, in her mother's hand: *R.D. & E.I., Meridian's last drydock, before everything.*

Before everything. The phrase her mother used for the wreck, for the grief, for the fifteen-year silence that had followed.

Ansel stood at the harbor wall that afternoon, hood up against the wind, and watched the *Corvus* come in.

The salvage vessel was beautiful in the way warships are beautiful — all function elevated to menace, gray hull, crane arms folded like sleeping insects, hydrographic arrays bristling along her flanks. She came through the outer marks at exactly the speed regulations allowed and not one knot slower, her wake spreading in long disciplined lines across the roads. Behind her, at a respectful distance, came two barges and a tender, the advance teeth of a fleet that could strip a wreck to its rivets in a week.

Rell Danforth stood on the wing of the bridge.

Even at four hundred meters, even through spray and the shimmer of heat off the water, she knew him. Older, yes — silver at the temples now, heavier through the shoulders, the young man's loose laughter replaced by something coiled and watchful. But the stance was the same as the photograph: weight forward, hands clasped behind him, chin slightly raised, a man surveying territory he considered his. Her father's stance. *Her* stance. She felt a sick kinship in it, seeing her own habit of standing echoed back at her from the deck of the ship hunting her.

He scanned the harbor as the *Corvus* made her approach — methodically, section by section, the way a man reads a chart. Moored fishing boats. The repair yards. The canal locks. And then, for a moment — she would swear it for the rest of her life — his gaze passed directly over the figure in the gray hood at the harbor wall, and stopped.

Not recognition. Something worse: *assessment*. The look of a man cataloguing every person present, filing each one under threat or irrelevance. She stood absolutely still, breathing shallow, and after three seconds that lasted a season, his gaze moved on.

The *Corvus* swung to her anchor with a rattle of chain that carried across the water like applause at a funeral.

Ansel stayed at the wall until dark, turning the cipher numbers over in her mind, matching them against the tide table she now carried in her head. Month four, day eleven, height two meters — that was nearly three weeks gone. Month seven, day three — weeks away yet, after the monsoon would have closed everything. But the third reference—

Month five. Day nine. Height eight decimeters.

Five days from now. The lowest tide in a decade, forecast at zero-point-eight meters below datum.

Her father hadn't just hidden the cave's location. He'd hidden the *appointment*. The Hollow would open its door exactly once more before the monsoon sealed it, and he had written the invitation fifteen years in advance, addressed to a daughter who hadn't yet been born into her grief.

Behind her, in the anchorage, the *Corvus* burned anchor lights like a row of cold eyes. Somewhere on that deck, Rell Danforth was reading the same tide forecasts she was. He had the fleet's hydrographers, their bottom-mappers, their side-scan sonar. What he didn't have was a dead man's cipher, or a lockkeeper's loyalty, or a mother's handwriting on a chart he'd never seen.

But he had time, and ships, and men. And tomorrow morning, Ansel knew with the certainty of water finding its level, the fleet's boats would begin sweeping the reef line.

She turned up her collar and walked back toward the *Kestrel*, her father's numbers burning in her memory like a brand. Five days. One door. And a man from her mother's photographs already anchored in the roadstead, waiting for the sea to give up its dead.

She just had to reach it first — and pray the truth her father had sealed in wax was worth what it would cost her to open it.
