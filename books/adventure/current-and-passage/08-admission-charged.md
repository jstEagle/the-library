## 8. Admission Charged

The reef line off the Hollow showed its teeth at dawn, and Vey went down before the light could reach it.

He had told himself it was routine work — a scout's diligence, walking the ground before the survey vessel arrived, the way a thief cases a house. But he knew, pulling on his fins in the grey water of the tender, that it was more than that. It was the way Ansel Iversen had refused him. Not the refusal itself — scouts heard *no* a hundred times a season — but the speed of it. The word had come out of her like a splinter leaving flesh, quick and clean and bleeding at the edges. People refused salvage partnerships because they were cautious. She had refused because she was afraid, and fear of that particular shape — sudden, flinching, aimed inward — meant she already knew what lay under the water.

Vey did not like knowing things other people wanted buried. It was an occupational hazard of his trade, and it had kept him alive through two wrecks, one mutiny, and a decade of Rell Danforth's patronage. So when the Kestrel had weighed anchor two nights ago and slipped north along the coast, Vey had done what any careful man would do.

He had come to look for himself.

* * *

The water was cold for the season, monsoon-cold, carrying the first rumor of the storm season in its green dark. He descended along the outer face of the reef where the coral fell away in terraces, each one older and deeper than the last, and the light came down with him in long slanted columns that bent and broke against the swell above. A school of silver fish turned around him like a single thinking thing. Below, the sand floor of the lagoon shelf spread out pale and featureless, rippled by currents into a pattern like handwriting no one living could read.

He swam the line of the reef for the better part of an hour, following the legend the way you'd follow a rumor through a port town — half believing, half humoring it. The locals said the Hollow charged admission in drowned men's debts. They said the cave opened only when the sea grew poor enough to want company. Fishermen crossed themselves going past this stretch of coast, and their grandmothers told stories about a ship that had gone in with all hands and never sent up so much as a plank.

But the sea was honest in a way people were not. It kept everything, eventually, and gave it back on its own schedule.

Vey found the ledge at the turn of the tide.

It was not dramatic. That was the first thing that struck him — how ordinary it looked from outside. A shelf of dark rock running out from the base of the reef like a lower lip, overhung by coral that had grown across it in the years since anything man-made had touched it. The kind of formation a survey would log as a navigational hazard and nothing more. But the current behaved strangely there. It came along the reef face and then, instead of continuing past the point, it folded inward, down and under the ledge, drawn into the dark like breath drawn into lungs. Water does not flow into solid rock. Water flows into openings.

He hung in the green half-light with his heart tapping against his ribs and looked at that fold of current for a long time.

Then he dropped below the lip of the ledge.

The space beneath was a throat — not the cave mouth itself, but the approach to it, a channel of smooth-worn stone wide enough for a diver, maybe barely wide enough for a boat's tender at extreme low tide, narrowing as it ran back into the hillside. The walls were grooved with old tool marks. That stopped him cold. Nature makes round holes; men make square ones. Someone had widened this passage once, deliberately, with chisels and patience, and then let the sea take it back. The grooves were furred with coral now, softened by decades, but they were there, regular as masonry, running back into a darkness that swallowed his torch beam after twenty feet.

He should have turned around then. Later, treading water beside the tender, hauling himself up over the gunwale with his hands shaking in a way he told himself was only cold, he would admit that he should have turned around. There is a difference between scouting a wreck and trespassing in a grave, and somewhere past those tool-marked walls he had crossed it without noticing.

But the timber was lying on the sand just inside the throat, half-buried, angled as if it had been carried partway out by some ancient surge and abandoned.

It was oak. Blackened oak, waterlogged to the density of iron, crusted with barnacle and tube worm, but sound along its grain — salvagers' oak, the old-growth stock they used for keels and frames because it held together even when everything else let go. Vey brushed the growth away from one face with his knife, working carefully, the way you'd clean a coin you suspected was gold.

The stamp was shallow, struck with a hand-held punch rather than a press, slightly crooked — the mark of a small yard, a proud yard, a yard that stamped every stick that left its shed because it stood behind its work.

**MERIDIAN — GREYHAVEN YARD — 18—**

The date was eaten away. Everything else was legible as scripture.

Vey stayed down there longer than he should have, kneeling on the sand beside that piece of wood while his air gauge crept toward the red, looking back into the dark channel, listening to the current breathe in and out of the hill. Somewhere beyond that darkness, if the legends were worth the salt they were soaked in, a ship sat upright in flooded silence with her dead still at their stations. And somewhere beyond *that*, in the world of ledgers and moorings and forged registration papers, a riverboat captain who refused salvage money too quickly was sailing north with a chart her father had died for.

He took the timber with him. It nearly beat him to death getting it up through the water — eighty pounds of drowned oak, awkward as a corpse — but he could not leave it. Proof was the only currency that mattered to Rell Danforth, and Vey had learned long ago that you brought the commander proof or you brought him nothing at all.

* * *

Rell Danforth received reports the way other men received communion: seated, silent, giving nothing back.

His cabin aboard the flagship *Corvus* was the cleanest room Vey had ever seen aboard a working vessel — charts squared to the table edges, instruments in felt-lined cases, a single lamp burning even in daylight because Rell distrusted windows. He was a big man gone spare with age, silver at the temples, hands like dock hardware, and he had a way of listening that made you feel the words being weighed as they left your mouth, each one set on a scale against the ones before it.

Vey laid the timber on the chart table between them.

Rell did not touch it at first. He looked at it the way a coroner looks at a body — professionally, without sentiment — and then he turned it over with two fingers, reading the stamp, and something moved behind his eyes that Vey had seen perhaps three times in ten years. Not surprise. Recognition. The look of a man finding a door he had bricked up still standing open.

"Where."

"Reef line off the Hollow. Outer ledge, just inside the throat of the approach." Vey kept his voice level. "There are tool marks on the passage walls. Old ones. Someone cut that entrance wider once and let the sea close over it. The timber was on the sand inside — carried out by a surge, I'd guess, decades back. There'll be more of her in there. Maybe all of her."

"The Meridian went down forty miles south," Rell said. "Official record has her on the Barrow shoals."

"Official record also has her cargo manifest signed twice in different inks." Vey had checked. Checking was his whole trade. "And official record has no explanation for why a ship with a full crew sent up no boats, no wreckage, no bodies. Ships that sink shed debris like wounded animals shed blood. This one didn't shed anything. You don't sink without a trace, Commander. You *disappear* without a trace. There's a difference, and the difference is intention."

The lamp hissed softly. Outside, gulls argued over something in the wake of the supply tender.

"You've been talking to the fishermen," Rell said.

"I've been talking to everyone. It's what you pay me for." Vey braced his hands on the edge of the table. "The locals say the cave opens at extreme low tide. The almanac has the lowest spring tide in a decade coming in six days. If the Meridian is in that cave, we have six tides to find her before the monsoon seals the approach until next season — and after this season, the storms may collapse the throat entirely. Whatever's in there won't wait for us, and it won't wait for anyone else either."

"Anyone else." Rell repeated it flatly, turning the phrase over.

"There's a riverboat called the Kestrel," Vey said. "Captain runs cargo up the coast. No registered homeport that checks out, papers that smell of fresh ink if you hold them to the light. She refused a salvage partnership with me two nights ago — fast, before I'd finished the offer. And she asked me one question about the survey fleet." He paused. "She asked which vessel. By name. When I told her, she went white around the mouth and paid for her drinks and walked out."

Rell had gone very still. It was the stillness of deep water, of weight settling.

"What name did you give her?"

"*Meridian*," Vey said. "The survey vessel mapping the coast is named the *Meridian*. Second ship to carry it. First one's been missing forty years."

For a long moment Rell Danforth looked at the blackened oak on his chart table, and Vey watched him do arithmetic with his whole body — the tiny shifts of jaw and shoulder, the fingers curling once and flattening again. Then he straightened, and the stillness broke, and he became the commander again, brisk and certain, the man who mobilized fleets before breakfast.

"Mobilize the fleet. All of it. Derricks, dive tenders, the demolition barge — everything that floats and pulls its weight. We make for the Hollow on the morning tide." He was already moving to the chart, marking positions with a grease pencil, his hand steady and fast. "Send to Greyhaven for blasting permits — tell the harbor office it's channel clearance work. Send to the company for the deep-water dive crew. I want the reef line buoyed within two days."

"And the Kestrel?" Vey asked.

Rell's pencil paused above the chart. Just for a heartbeat — a pause so small that a man who hadn't spent ten years reading this particular face would have missed it entirely.

"What about her?"

"She knows something. Or she's related to someone who did. Either way, she'll run for that cave, and she'll run early, and she knows waters out there that aren't on any chart we own. I've watched her pilot fog that would put a navy cutter on the rocks." Vey met the commander's eyes. "If we're racing anyone for that wreck, Commander, we're racing her."

"Then we won't race her." Rell resumed marking the chart, his voice dropping into the mild register he used when he was being most dangerous. "We'll simply know where she goes. Put a boat on her. Quietly — nothing that flies our colors, nothing her engineer would spot from the bridge. A fishing smack out of Greyhaven will do; half the smacks in that harbor owe the company money. I want her position reported every six hours, and I want to know the moment she turns toward the reef line."

"It's a big coast to shadow one riverboat."

"It's a small coast," Rell said, "for a woman with nowhere to hide." He set the pencil down and looked up, and for a moment the professional mask thinned, and Vey saw underneath it something old and hungry and grief-shaped, something that had been waiting forty years for this timber to wash onto a scout's knife blade. "You did well, Vey. Better than you know. Take the rest of the day — you've earned the dry clothes."

Vey gathered his fins and his wet bag and got as far as the cabin door before Rell spoke again, without turning from the chart.

"One more thing. The captain of the Kestrel. Her name — the name on her papers."

"Iversen," Vey said. "Ansel Iversen."

The lamp flame leaned in some draft Vey couldn't feel. When Rell answered, his voice was perfectly level, which was how Vey knew it had cost him something to keep it so.

"Watch her closely," the commander said. "Closely, and kindly. She may be worth more to us than the wreck."

* * *

That night, in the fo'c'sle of the survey tender, Vey dried his gear by the stove and found he could not stop seeing the tool marks on the cave walls.

Regular as masonry. Cut by patient hands, in an age when the Meridian was young and Greyhaven Yard stamped its oak with pride. Men had made that door on purpose, and men had let it close, and now the sea was deciding whether to open it one last time, and everyone with a stake in the answer was converging on the same stretch of empty water like iron filings toward a magnet.

He thought about Ansel Iversen going white around the mouth. He thought about the speed of her refusal, and the question she'd asked, and the way she'd paid for her drinks like a woman settling accounts before a long journey. He thought about Rell Danforth saying *watch her closely, and kindly*, and wondered which of those two words the commander believed, and which one was camouflage.

Six tides. The almanac didn't lie about such things.

Somewhere north along the darkening coast, a riverboat with a forged name was making choices too, and Vey had the uncomfortable, creeping certainty — the scout's instinct that had kept him alive through two wrecks and a mutiny — that whatever came out of that cave at the end of it would not be cargo, and would not be treasure, and would not be anything anyone aboard the *Corvus* was prepared to carry.

He banked the stove, turned in, and dreamed of black oak stamped with a crooked punch, rising slowly out of green water toward a surface he never reached.
