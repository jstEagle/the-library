## 10. The First Tide

The sea went out like a held breath.

Ansel felt it in the Kestrel's hull before she saw it in the water — a long, shuddering lightness, the boat lifting off the deep as the tide drained away beneath her. The barometer had been falling all night, and the dawn came up the color of pewter, flat and wide and utterly still, the kind of stillness that sailors on this coast called the sea's lie. Nothing about this morning was calm. The whole ocean was leaning toward the Hollow.

She stood at the wheel with the engine at half, threading the last of the channel markers, and let the old arithmetic run in her head. Six tides. The almanac said the lowest water in a decade would come with this one, and every tide after it a little higher, a little shorter, until the monsoon arrived and closed the book entirely. Somewhere ahead, under forty feet of green water, a cave mouth waited to be briefly, treacherously open.

Behind her, Toma was in the engine room doing something to the raw-water pump that involved a great deal of profanity delivered in three languages. He had not spoken to her properly since the night she lied to him about the sandbar. She had heard him not speaking to her all morning, the way you hear a door left ajar, the way you hear a clock in a room you're trying not to think about.

*Later*, she told herself. *After the tide.*

The lie was a familiar one. She had been telling it to herself in one form or another for nine years.

* * *

The reef announced itself the way it always did on this coast — a change in the water's voice. The deep swells that had rolled beneath them for hours began to stand up and comb over, and the color shifted from bottle-green to a pale, milky turquoise where the bottom came up to meet the light. Ansel killed the engine and let the Kestrel drift the last hundred yards, listening.

That was when she saw it.

The buoy sat on the reef's shoulder like a gull that had decided to stay. Orange, new-painted, with a radar reflector bolted on top that caught the flat light and threw it back in a hard white wink. A picket line driven into the coral beside it, a small pennant snapping at its head. Someone had been here before her. Someone with good gear, a fast boat, and the same almanac.

She stood at the rail for a long moment, one hand on the wheel, and felt something cold settle into her chest that had nothing to do with the water.

"They planted it," Toma said from the engine room hatch, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up its job years ago. He came up beside her and squinted at the buoy. "Fleet colors. I know that orange — Danforth's people mark everything they touch. Like a dog, but with paperwork."

"How long?"

"Paint's not weathered. A day, maybe two." He looked at her. His face, seamed and brown and usually so open, had closed into the expression he wore when he was deciding whether to ask a question he already knew the answer to. "Ansel. If they've found the ledge, they've found the cave mouth. If they've found the cave mouth—"

"Then they're waiting for the same tide I am." She said it evenly, and was proud of how evenly she said it. "Which means we have exactly as much time as they think we have, and no more."

"And what are we diving for?" He said it quietly. No anger in it. That was the worst of it — there was no anger in Toma, only the slow, patient erosion of a man watching his captain steer toward rocks she wouldn't name. "Nine years, Ansel. You've run contraband, you've run people, you've run cargo I didn't ask about and cargo I did. I never needed the why. But this—" he nodded at the buoy, at the reef, at the whole gray, leaning morning, "—this isn't cargo. This is you going down into water that eats people, for something you won't even say out loud."

The tide was still falling. She could see the reef's spine emerging now, black and glistening, and beyond it, in the deep channel between the outer rocks, a pale shape that might have been sand and might have been the ledge the legends spoke of. The Hollow, charging its admission in drowned men's debts.

"Toma," she said, "I need you to hold the boat. That's all I can give you right now. Hold the boat, watch the water, and if the fleet comes, you get us out of here whether I'm aboard or not. Do you understand me? Whether I'm aboard or not."

He looked at her for a long time. The wind had died entirely now; the pennant on the fleet's picket hung limp, and the silence was so complete she could hear the water draining off the reef in thin silver threads.

"Aye," he said at last. And then, because he was Toma, and because nine years is not nothing: "I'll hold the boat. But I'm keeping count, Captain. Of what you don't say."

* * *

She went down the anchor line into a world the color of drowned glass.

The visibility was better than she'd hoped — the flat calm had let the silt settle, and the water around the ledge burned with that peculiar luminous clarity that comes before a storm, as though the sea were showing off everything it was about to take away. Below her the reef fell away in terraces, and the ledge itself came into view the way a face comes into view in a photograph developing: gradually, then all at once.

It was a shelf of pale limestone, maybe thirty feet across, tilted slightly toward the deep, and it was covered in the wreckage of a hundred years of rumor. Anchor flukes furred with coral. A ship's bell so encrusted it had become a stone bell, ringing nothing forever. Bottles. A boot. Bones that might have been fish and might not. The debts of drowned men, exactly as the legend promised, paid out across the stone.

And at the ledge's seaward edge, a dark seam in the rock — the cave mouth, still sealed by the tide, water pressing into the black gap like a held tongue.

Ansel hung in the water above it and let herself feel the whole weight of what she was looking at. Her father had died within a mile of this stone. Every story she'd ever been told — the storm, the reef, the rival captain who supposedly cut the Meridian's fate loose — all of it had happened in this green light. She had spent nine years running from a ghost, and the ghost had been down here the whole time, waiting for a tide low enough to speak.

She was still taking it in when she saw the glint.

It was half-buried at the ledge's landward edge, tucked into a crevice where the coral had grown over it in a knuckle of purple and green — a corner of metal, green with a century of salt, but with a shape too regular to be reef. She kicked down. The water shallowed as the tide kept falling, and by the time she reached the crevice she was working in eight feet, close enough that the surface rippled silver above her like a ceiling of shaken mercury.

She dug with her dive knife, working the coral away in careful flakes. The object came free with a grinding reluctance, as if the reef itself had been asked to keep a secret and resented giving it up.

It was a strongbox. Iron, hand-forged, maybe two feet long, its hasp fused shut with corrosion, its body crusted in a shell of the sea's own making. She turned it in the water and her heart stopped — not from the effort, not from the depth, but from what the crust had not quite managed to bury.

Stamped into the iron, worn shallow but unmistakable, was a crest: a kestrel rising over a chevron of three waves.

Her family's mark. The mark on the stern of the boat she'd grown up aboard, the mark her father had burned into every crate, every document, every doorframe of her childhood. The mark she had spent nine years scrubbing out of her own name.

She stayed down there longer than she should have, the strongbox heavy against her chest, the tide clock running somewhere above her in a language of light and pressure. The cave mouth gaped beside her, still sealed, still dark, patient as everything on this coast. And Ansel Iversen — who was not Ansel Iversen, who was nobody, who was a forged name on a forged license running a stolen inheritance up and down a monsoon coast — hung in the green silence with her family crest pressed against her ribs, and understood, with a clarity that felt like drowning, that the legend was real.

It was real, and it was hers, and someone else had planted a flag on top of it.

* * *

She surfaced into a changed world.

The wind had come back while she was under — not the monsoon, not yet, but its outrider, a freshening southeast breeze that put a dark ripple on the water and made the fleet's pennant stand out straight and hard. The buoy bobbed. The reef's spine had risen further, black and dripping, and the tide pools between the rocks steamed faintly in the strengthening light.

Toma hauled the strongbox over the rail with a boat hook and then stood looking down at her as she came up the ladder, water streaming off her, the tank heavy on her back.

"There's a boat," he said. "Two miles east, hull-down. Been there since you went under. They're watching us, Ansel."

"Let them watch." She stripped the mask off and set the strongbox on the deck between them, and watched his face as he saw the crest. She watched him recognize it. She watched nine years of not-asking come apart on his features like a knot finally slipping.

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "I've seen that mark. On the old papers in the hold. The ones you keep locked in the chart drawer under the spare fathometer." His voice had gone very quiet. "That's your family."

"It's my family," she said.

The strongbox sat on the deck between them, green and fused and silent, and the wind came off the sea and pushed at the Kestrel's hull as though the boat itself were leaning toward the reef, toward the cave, toward whatever the next five tides would open.

"Get us out of the sightline," Ansel said. "Behind the outer islet. I want to open this thing where they can't see what we're doing." She looked east, at the hull-down shape on the horizon, and felt the cold thing in her chest turn over. "And Toma — get the cutting torch ready."

He was already moving, but he paused at the engine room hatch, one hand on the frame, and looked back at her with the tide still falling in his eyes.

"The torch," he said. "For the box."

"For whatever's inside the box," she said. "It's been down there a long time, Toma. Whatever it's been waiting to tell us — I want to hear it before the fleet does."

He held her gaze a moment longer, and then he nodded, once, and went below. A moment later she heard the compressor start, and the small, stubborn heartbeat of the Kestrel carried her away from the reef, away from the buoy and its watching pennant, out across the draining sea with her family's sealed past riding the deck behind her.

The tide would turn in four hours. She had five more after that.

And somewhere out there, hull-down on the horizon, Rell Danforth's fleet was counting them too.
