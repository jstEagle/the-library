## 12. Parley in the Roads

The white flag went up an hour after dawn, on a launch too small to be anything but a message.

Ansel saw it first from the wheelhouse, a smudge of pale cloth against the grey-green water of the roads, where the fleet had anchored in a loose, patient arc like gulls waiting on a fisherman. Three masted salvage barges, a steam tender, and the *Corvid* — Rell Danforth's flagship, black-hulled, her crane arm folded like a sleeping heron. They'd come in overnight. She'd heard their anchor chains through the Kestrel's hull while she lay awake counting tides, and she had pretended, in the way of people pretending at three in the morning, that the sound was only the mooring buoys knocking.

Toma came up the companionway with two mugs of coffee, saw the launch, and set both mugs down without drinking.

"They're flying parley," he said.

"I have eyes."

"They don't fly parley for cargo runners." He said it carefully, the way he said everything these days, each word set down like a crate he wasn't sure would hold. "They fly parley for people they want something from. Or people they're about to take something from, and want to feel civilized about it."

Ansel took the wheel and brought the Kestrel's head around slowly, so that the launch would have to come to her quarter rather than her bow. Small thing. Meaningless thing. But Rell Danforth would read it, and she wanted him to read it. *I am not coming to you. If there is a meeting, it happens on the water, between equals, or it doesn't happen.*

The launch was forty feet, white flag at the gaff, and it came on at an easy six knots with the confidence of a vessel that had never once been fired upon. Through the glass Ansel counted four men aboard, and then she stopped counting, because the man at the helm was wearing a fleet officer's coat with the collar open, and his hair was going grey at the temples in the same pattern she remembered from a photograph on her mother's dressing table — a photograph of three young people on a dock, laughing at something the camera hadn't caught, taken the summer before the Meridian went down.

Rell Danforth had been beautiful once. He was handsome still, in the way that harbors are handsome at dusk, when the rust and the rot go soft in the light. He stood at the launch's rail with one hand raised, palm out — *no weapons, no harm* — and Ansel felt the gesture land somewhere under her ribs with a precision she resented.

"Bring us to within hailing distance," she told Toma. "No closer. I'll take the launch's lines if he insists on boarding, but he boards alone."

"And if he doesn't insist?"

"Then we talk across the water like civilized people." She almost smiled. "His word, probably."

* * *

The launch came alongside with a fender-perfect approach, no scrape, no bump, and Rell Danforth stepped onto the Kestrel's deck as though he'd been born on it. Perhaps he had. Salvage men spent more of their lives on other people's boats than their own.

Up close he was taller than the photograph, broader through the shoulders, with the deep, wind-cured tan of a man who had never once worked below decks. He looked at the Kestrel — at her patched rail, her salt-stained canvas, her cargo boom with its fresh weld — with an appraiser's unhurried eye, and then he looked at Ansel, and everything appraising went out of his face and something older came in.

"Little Wren," he said.

The nickname went through her like a fishhook through soft flesh. No one had called her that in nineteen years. Her mother had coined it — *because you're small and you sing and you never stay in one cage* — and after the wreck, after the funeral that had no body, after the silence that filled the rest of her childhood, the name had been buried with everything else. Hearing it now, in this voice, on this deck, was like hearing a door open in a house she'd burned down.

"Captain Iversen," she said, giving him the forged name like a card laid face up on the table. *Let's both pretend. Let's see who pretends better.*

Something flickered in his eyes — acknowledgment, appreciation, maybe even a kind of tenderness — and he inclined his head, accepting the fiction. "Captain Iversen. May I?" He nodded at the wheelhouse, and without waiting for an answer stepped to it, resting one hand on the rail as though he'd owned a hundred boats exactly like her. "You've kept her well. The Kestrel. She was a good hull when the yard built her, and she's been loved since. That's rarer than you'd think."

"You didn't come forty miles in a launch to compliment my boat."

"No." He turned to face her fully, and up close she could see the cost the years had levied: fine lines around the eyes like stress fractures in glass, a scar along the jaw she hadn't been able to see in the photograph. "I came because I'd rather not see you hurt. That's the whole of it, Ansel. That's the truth I've rowed out here under a white flag to say."

"Ansel," she repeated. "Not Captain Iversen."

He smiled, and the smile was warm, and the warmth was the most frightening thing about him. "You have your father's way of not wasting motion. I can see it. Every time you shift your weight you're checking the trim of the boat. He did that. He'd stand on the Meridian's deck in a flat calm and still be reading her like weather."

"My father is dead."

"Your father," Rell said gently, "is the reason I'm here."

* * *

He asked for coffee, and she gave it to him, because refusing would have been a tell, and because Toma made coffee that could have raised the dead and she wanted, absurdly, for this man who had known her father to taste it. They stood in the wheelhouse with the fog lifting off the water in long grey banners and the fleet lying at anchor beyond, patient as arithmetic.

"Here's what I know," Rell said, wrapping both hands around the mug. "A riverboat captain with no history before six years ago has been asking questions about the Hollow. A salvage scout named Vey — I know Vey, he works for me when he remembers to — sold her a cargo run at three times market rate just to get a look at her charts. The same captain slipped Greyhaven at night through a sandbar channel that isn't on any chart made in the last forty years, which means it's on a chart that was. And the lowest tide in a decade is four days out, and the Hollow will be open, and there is a wreck inside that cave that I have spent half my life and most of my money trying to reach." He looked at her over the rim of the mug. "I don't know who you are, Captain Iversen. But I know whose daughter you are. And I think you know that I know."

The fog horn on the Corvid sounded, far off, a low animal note. Ansel kept her face still and her hands loose and thought about the strongbox below, with its crest and its lock and its papers she hadn't yet had the courage to read.

"Say what you came to say," she said.

"I'm offering you a way out of the cage, little Wren." He set the mug down. "A clean identity. New papers, real ones, fleet-issued — the kind that survive any inspection in any port on this coast. A commission. Salvage master, your own vessel, your own crew, fleet backing. You'd never forge another document or bribe another clerk as long as you lived. All I want in return is coordinates."

"Coordinates."

"The entrance to the Hollow. The inner passage, if you've found it. The tide windows." He spread his hands, reasonable, open. "That's all. You keep your boat, your freedom, your name — whichever name that turns out to be. The fleet does the heavy salvage. You walk away rich in the only currency that's ever mattered to you, which is not being found."

The wheelhouse was very quiet. Ansel could hear the water slapping the hull, the creak of the boom, Toma's boots on the deck below, moving — she noted it distantly — between the strongbox and the companionway, standing guard over things he didn't understand yet.

"You think I have coordinates," she said.

"I think you have a chart with two hands on it. I think one of those hands belongs to a man I signed a partnership with twenty-two years ago, and I would give a great deal to know what the other hand wrote in the margins." His voice didn't change, but something behind it did, a depth sounding, the lead line going down past the marked depth into unmarked water. "I think you've been running from something since before you were old enough to know what running was. And I think the thing you're running from is in that cave."

"And if I refuse?"

The warmth came back into his face like sun through a parting cloud, and it was worse than anything else he'd shown her.

"Then I'll be very sorry," he said. "And the fleet will do what fleets do. There's a magistrate in Corvane who owes me a favor, and a registration clerk in Greyhaven who's already been paid to look again at a certain license. A boat with forged papers is a boat without a country, Captain. She can be seized on any tide, in any water, by anyone with a writ. I'd hate for that to happen to a hull that's been so well loved." He picked up his mug again, unhurried, a man discussing cargo rates. "But the offer stands until the sixth tide. After that, the monsoon closes the door on all of us, and we all go home poorer and wiser."

* * *

She walked him to the rail because it was her deck and she would not let him leave it unescorted, and because she wanted to look at him a moment longer with the fog burning off behind him, silvering the water, making the fleet's masts stand up like a drowned forest.

"Why the white flag?" she asked. "You didn't need it. You could have just taken the boat."

"I could have." He stepped down into the launch, and the deck of the Kestrel rose and settled as his weight left her. He looked up at her from the waterline, and for one unguarded second the charm fell away entirely, and what was underneath was tired — tired in a way that went down to the keel. "Your father would have wanted me to try the courteous way first. He always said the sea takes enough without us helping." The launch's engine turned over. "Think about it, little Wren. Four days. The offer doesn't rot, but it does expire."

She watched the launch run back toward the fleet, the white flag snapping at the gaff, and she stood at the rail long after it was a speck, long after it was nothing, doing the arithmetic he had left her.

* * *

Toma found her there. He didn't say anything for a while. Below them the engine idled, and the tide turned, and the current in the roads shifted with a soft hiss along the hull, the water changing its mind.

"He called you little Wren," Toma said at last.

"Yes."

"Your mother's name for you. The lockkeeper said —" He stopped. He had been there in Greyhaven; he had heard the old man say *Ansel Iversen* across the lock like a bell being rung. "So Danforth knew your mother. Knew her well enough to use her words for you."

"He knew my father." She kept her eyes on the fleet. "That's what he'd say. But Toma — he didn't come here to recruit me. I understood that somewhere in the middle of his speech, and I've been checking the seams of it since, the way you check a hull for rot. Listen. A man who genuinely wants coordinates doesn't tip his hand that he knows who I am. He doesn't mention the magistrate and the clerk and the seizure writ. He doesn't give me four days. He takes the boat tonight, while I'm at anchor, while the fleet outguns me ten to one. He has the writ already — he all but showed it to me."

Toma's jaw worked. "Then why parley at all?"

"Because he needed me to refuse." She turned from the rail at last, and the fog had burned off completely now, and the light was hard and clear and showed everything, the way light does when it's getting ready to change. "A parley is a record. His officers saw the white flag; his log will show an offer made and refused; the magistrate's writ will show that the captain was given every chance. When the boarding party comes — and it's coming, Toma, probably at slack water, probably with the tide that's most convenient for them — it won't be piracy. It'll be procedure." She heard her own voice go flat and cold, the pilot's voice, the voice she used for channels with no margin. "He built the trap with the door left open, because a trap with an open door catches more than a trap that's shut. He wanted me to run."

"Then we don't run."

"No." She was already moving, already thinking past the fear into the water, into the tides, into the narrow arithmetic of hours. "Running is what he's priced in. Running is the move he's already bought." She stopped at the companionway and looked back at Toma, and for a moment the pilot's voice cracked and something younger showed through, something small that sang and never stayed in one cage. "He thinks he knows every channel I'll take, because he's been reading my father's charts for twenty years. He's never read my mother's."

Below, in the cabin, the strongbox sat open on the table, and the papers inside it — her father's handwriting, Rell Danforth's signature, a partnership that predated the wreck by three years — waited for her with the patience of the drowned.

The barometer on the bulkhead had begun, very slowly, to fall.
