## 13. Second Tide, Rising Cost

The squall came out of the southeast like something with a grudge.

Ansel felt it before she saw it — a pressure change in her back teeth, a flattening of the swell, the gulls going quiet all at once as if someone had drawn a curtain over the morning. She stood at the Kestrel's wheel with her hands loose on the spokes and read the sky the way other people read letters from home: closely, reluctantly, braced for bad news.

Behind the reef line, the lagoon lay green and glassy, deceptively calm. Beyond it, the horizon had gone the color of wet slate, and along its whole length ran a low dark seam — the monsoon's leading edge, arriving like an uninvited guest who'd come early to catch you unprepared.

"Toma," she called down the companionway. "Barometer."

"Twenty-nine ten and falling fast." His voice came up through the deck planks, gravel over iron. "Faster than it should. That's not a squall line, Ansel. That's the vanguard."

She knew. The charts had promised her six days of grace between the spring tides and the true onset of the monsoon, six days measured in the tidy arithmetic of men who drew isobars in warm offices. But the sea kept its own ledger, and it was calling in debts early.

* * *

Tide two would peak in four hours. She could feel it in the set of the current through the channel, the water sliding past the Kestrel's hull with that particular muscular urgency, like a horse that knows the road home.

Four hours. Enough time to shelter behind the reef and let the squall spend itself, then dive on tide three with dry gear and steady nerves. That was the sensible choice. Toma would make the sensible choice. Any captain worth her salt would make the sensible choice.

Ansel looked at the dark seam on the horizon and did the other arithmetic instead. If the monsoon's edge was here now, days ahead of schedule, then tide three might come wrapped in weather no diver could work through. Tide four might not come at all in any usable sense. The window she'd been counting wasn't a window anymore. It was a crack, and it was closing.

"We're diving this tide," she said.

Toma came up the ladder wiping his hands on a rag that had once been white. He was sixty-one years old and built like something the river had failed to erode, and he looked at her the way he'd been looking at her more and more these last two days — with a patience that had edges on it.

"In that?" He nodded at the sky. "Visibility'll be soup. Current in the passage will double when the squall hits the lagoon. You go down into that cave in surge conditions, you're not mapping, you're gambling."

"I'm gambling either way, Toma. This just has better odds."

He held her gaze a moment longer. Then he shrugged, which from Toma was a signature on a contract. "I'll keep the engine warm and the line tended. But Ansel—" He stopped. She watched him decide not to say whatever came next, and watched him file it away instead, in that ledger he kept behind his eyes that had been growing heavier every day since the strongbox.

Since the papers.

She turned away before he could see what crossed her face.

* * *

The squall hit while she was kitting up on the afterdeck, rain coming in slantwise sheets that turned the lagoon to hammered pewter. Thunder rolled along the reef like cargo being shifted in a hold. Ansel pulled her hood back and let the rain hammer her skull, because the cold helped, because pain was a kind of focus, because her father had taught her that a diver afraid of discomfort was a diver who made mistakes at depth where mistakes were paid for in air.

The strongbox's contents sat in a waterproof satchel below, and she had not opened them again since Toma had. She didn't need to. Her father's signature sat behind her eyelids now, next to Rell Danforth's, on a partnership agreement dated eleven months before the Meridian went down. Half shares. Joint salvage rights to the very reef she was about to dive. Her father and the man who had spent twenty years telling the coast a story about pirates and betrayal — partners, in ink, in blood-warm candlelight, in whatever smoky room men signed away each other's futures in.

And her mother's handwriting on the chart, older than the wreck, older than the lie.

She zipped the drysuit to her throat and told herself the shiver was the wind.

* * *

The cave mouth took her the way it always did: a slow swallowing, the light dying by degrees from green to gray to the brown-black of closed eyes. Her lamp beam cut a cone through water thick with stirred sediment, and the surge reached even here, forty feet inside, a slow breathing push-and-pull against her body like the cave itself inhaling.

She swam the inner passage with a slate in one hand and a compass in the other, and she worked the way her father had taught her to work a strange river — slowly, humbly, reading the rock. The passage bent left where the outer ledge had suggested right. A chimney of collapsed stone pinched it to barely twice her shoulder width at the twelve-minute mark, and she eased through sideways, exhaling carefully, feeling the rock press close like a crowd. Her bubbles went up into a ceiling fissure and did not come back. Air pocket, somewhere above. She marked it on the slate: *breathable, maybe. Not yet.*

The passage opened.

It happened the way cathedrals happen — suddenly, after a long dark corridor, a space too large for the body to understand. Her lamp reached maybe thirty feet and found nothing but water, and beyond the water's reach her imagination built walls. She hung there in the middle of the drowned hall, breathing hard through the regulator, listening to the surge moan somewhere far off through channels she couldn't see, and she understood that she was swimming inside the Hollow's heart, inside the place the legends charged admission for, and that the price list was still posted.

Then her lamp found the mast.

It stood out of the dark at a slight angle, still stepped, still rigged with shrouds furred white with coral, rising toward a ceiling she couldn't see — thirty feet of it visible, maybe forty, a spar as thick as her waist wearing two decades of the sea like a coat of office. She recognized the wood grain even through the growth. Teak. Meridian teak. She'd grown up sleeping in a bunk whose frame her father had milled from a sister ship's offcuts, and some knowledge lived in her hands deeper than memory.

She put her glove against the mast and the coral rasped under her palm, and something in her chest cracked along a seam she'd been keeping caulked for twenty years.

*You're here,* she thought. *Papa. I'm here.*

The wreck had to be below and beyond, down in the hall's floor where the lamplight gave out. Her pressure gauge said she had time for one reconnaissance run, no more — the swim back through the pinch point, the long haul to the entrance against the surge, the safety margin she'd already shaved thin. She marked the mast's position on the slate, shot a bearing to the passage mouth, noted the depth against the line on her reel, and made herself turn away.

It was the hardest thing she'd done since the harbor clerk in Chapter One of her life had asked her name and she'd lied.

* * *

The surface met her with rain and Toma's hands, both equally rough. She came over the gunwale in a graceless roll and lay on the deck plates gasping while the squall worked itself into a fury overhead, and Toma crouched beside her with a blanket and a face like a closing door.

"The mast," she managed. "Meridian's mast, standing. The hull's down in the deep section. I need three more dives, Toma. Three. Maybe four."

"You've got tides, not dives, and the tides are shrinking." He draped the blanket over her shoulders with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about him. "Storm's the leading edge. By tomorrow night we'll have thirty knots sustained and a lagoon full of confused sea. Whatever you're going to do down there, the sea's going to start charging interest."

She pushed herself upright and found him watching her — not the storm, not the reef, her. And she knew, with the sick certainty of a woman who has been avoiding a reef she can see dead ahead, that the conversation had arrived.

"The papers," Toma said.

"Toma—"

"No." Quiet. That was the frightening thing; he never raised his voice, so when he wanted to stop her, quiet did it. "Nine years, Ansel. Nine years I've kept your engines turning and your secrets turning with them. I don't ask where the cargo comes from. I don't ask why we never winter anywhere. I don't ask why a woman with your hands — pilot's hands, captain's hands — runs sealed crates for money she clearly doesn't need. I log the hours and I mind my own business, because you pay fair and you steer true and that's more family than I ever got from blood."

Rain drummed on the deckhouse roof. Somewhere beyond the reef, thunder rolled its barrels across the water.

"But I opened that box." His jaw worked. "Your father and Rell Danforth. Partners. Signed and sealed almost a year before the Meridian sank. And you've known — how long? Since Greyhaven? Longer? And you sent me down with the cutting torch and let me hand you the truth and then you just — swallowed it. Like it was ballast. Like it weighed nothing."

"It doesn't weigh nothing."

"Then say what it weighs!" For one instant his voice did rise, cracking against the storm, and then he mastered it, and that mastery cost him visibly. "Because here's what I know, Captain. Rell Danforth is anchored off this reef with a fleet and a demolition crew, and he wants this cave badly enough to blow solid rock for it. And your father's name is on paper tying him to that man. And you are diving a wreck that could hang them both or free them both, and I am standing on your boat holding a torch, and I don't know which side of that paper I'm standing on. So you tell me. You tell me what we're actually salvaging. The truth, or the lie?"

Ansel pulled the blanket tighter. The rain ran off the deckhouse in silver ropes. She thought about lying to him — she was good at it, she'd had twenty years of practice, and the words were right there, smooth and ready, the old currency she'd always paid loyal men in.

But Toma had cut open a strongbox for her. Toma had chosen, without being asked, and he deserved better than counterfeit coin.

"My mother," she said, "wrote annotations on my father's chart. Before the wreck. Annotations that map this cave." She watched him take that in. "Everyone always said a rival captain scuttled the Meridian. That's the story I was raised on. It's the story I built myself out of. And every piece of paper we've pulled out of the water says the story is wrong — and says the woman who taught me to read tide tables might be the one who sank him."

Toma was silent for a long moment. Rain, thunder, the groan of the anchor rode.

"And Danforth?"

"Was her father's partner. Which means either he helped sink his own ship, or he's spent twenty years hunting a wreck he half-owned, and I don't know which is worse." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "That's what we're salvaging, Toma. A grave. And everybody in it is guilty."

He looked at her for a long time, and she watched something shift in his face — not pity, she'd have thrown pity overboard, but recognition. The look of a man finding out the cargo he'd been hauling was heavier than the manifest said, and deciding, deliberately, to keep hauling anyway.

"All right," he said finally. "Now I know what we're running from." He stood, joints cracking, and gathered her dive gear to stow it. "Next tide, you rest. I mean it. Dead men find no logbooks."

"Toma."

He paused at the companionway.

"If the fleet comes in force — if Danforth decides to take the Kestrel rather than wait — you get the boat out. You don't wait for me. Swear it."

He looked at her the way he had that first day nine years ago, when she'd hired him with forged references and he'd pretended not to notice the ink still smudging.

"I'll swear it," he said, "the day you stop asking me to."

He went below. Ansel stayed on the deck in the rain until the squall began to spend itself, and then she went to the rail and looked east, where the dark seam of the monsoon had climbed another degree up the sky, closer now, patient as a creditor.

Below her, in the drowned cathedral behind the reef, her father's ship waited with its logbook and its lies. Ahead of her, somewhere in the weather, Rell Danforth waited with his barges and his dynamite.

And the tide table in her head turned its page to tide three, and she saw — with the clarity that comes only to people running out of both time and choices — that the next window might be the last one the sea intended to give anyone.

She went below to plot the dive, and outside, the first long rollers of the true monsoon began to arrive, marching in from the southeast like soldiers who had marched all night to be early.
