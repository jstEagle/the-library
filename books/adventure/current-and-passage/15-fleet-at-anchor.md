## 15. Fleet at Anchor

The fleet came in on the morning tide like a judgment.

Vey stood at the rail of the *Corvus*, Rell Danforth's flagship, and watched the anchorage fill. Four hulls in all — the *Corvus* with her crane arm folded like a sleeping heron, two salvage barges riding low with pumps and cutting gear, and the survey tender *Lanternfish*, whose sonar had found the Hollow's outer ledge three days before and had not stopped singing about it since. Behind them all, the monsoon's leading edge sat on the horizon like a bruise that hadn't decided whether to bloom.

The Hollow itself was invisible from this distance. That was its nature. A notch in a reef line that no chart had ever honored, a cave mouth drowned under six fathoms of green water, opening only when the tide dropped low enough and long enough to bare its threshold. The locals had names for it that Vey had collected over eleven years of scouting this coast — the Debt Collector, the Tollhouse, the Mouth That Counts. Children were told it charged admission in drowned men's debts. Adults told each other the same thing, quieter, as if the joke had stopped being a joke somewhere back along the line.

Rell Danforth did not believe in jokes about the sea. He believed in schedules, tonnage, and leverage, in roughly that order.

"Vey." The voice came from behind him, warm the way ballast is warm — heavy, and only because it has been sitting in one place a long time. "You're up early."

"I'm always up early, Commander."

"You're always up early *somewhere else*," Rell said, coming to stand beside him at the rail. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges the way driftwood goes soft — still hard where it counts, but the shape of him had been negotiated with time. His beard was iron-grey and trimmed with naval precision, a habit from a commission he'd lost twenty years ago and never stopped mourning. "Here you're just up early. I like that. It means you're invested."

Vey said nothing. Eleven years of scouting for the Meridian Line — that was what Rell called his fleet, though everyone knew the name was older than the fleet, knew it came from a ship that had gone down with all hands and half a fortune, knew that Rell Danforth had been chasing that particular ghost longer than Vey had been alive to scout anything. Rell talked about the *Meridian* the way some men talked about a first wife: with love, with grievance, with the certainty that she had wronged him and that he would be made whole.

"Look at it," Rell said now, gesturing at the water off the bow, at nothing, at everything under the nothing. "Eleven days of tide-tables and I can tell you exactly when that cave gives us the door. Fifth tide from now. Maybe the sixth if the storm drags its feet. And after that—" he closed one fist slowly, knuckles cracking like small-arms fire, "—the monsoon seals it until next spring, if it ever opens again. Which means we don't wait for tides."

Vey turned. "Sir?"

"We blow it."

The words went into Vey like a fishhook going into a thumb — quick, clean, and then suddenly impossible to ignore.

"The entrance is a limestone throat," Vey said. "Narrow. If we set charges at the ledge—"

"Not at the ledge. At the sill. Widen the mouth by four meters, drop the roof shelf, and my divers go in on scuba any tide we like. No more waiting for the sea to be generous." Rell smiled, and it was the smile he used on harbor boards and reluctant investors, the one that had teeth in it only if you looked too long. "The cave's been charging admission for thirty years, Vey. I'm done paying."

"And whatever's inside? Thirty years of silt, timber, cargo — a blast wave doesn't sort the valuable from the worthless. It just buries both."

"Then we dig. We have pumps. We have cranes. We have time before the storm — barely, but we have it." Rell clapped him on the shoulder, and his hand was heavier than it needed to be, the way it always was when he wanted you to feel the weight of his confidence. "You found the ledge. You found the timber with the stamp. This is your find, Vey. Your name goes in the report. Don't flinch now that it's paying out."

Vey looked out at the water again. Somewhere under that green ceiling was a hull timber stamped *MERIDIAN*, which he had touched with his own gloved hand three days ago, alone in the cold and the pressure, while something in the dark beyond his lamp light shifted with a current that had no business moving inside a sealed cave. He had reported it within the hour, because that was the job, because eleven years of loyalty was a habit worn smooth as river stone.

He had not reported the other thing. The thing he'd seen on the way back to the surface: a wake, far off, thin as a knife cut in silk. A riverboat running dark along the reef line where no riverboat had any reason to be.

He had told himself it was a fishing boat. He had almost believed it.

* * *

The fleet anchored in a crescent off the reef, and the day organized itself around Rell's will the way water organizes around a keel. Divers went down to plant marker buoys at the ledge. The *Lanternfish* ran sonar sweeps, painting the cave's interior in ghostly cross-sections that Vey studied longer than he needed to. The demolition crew — three men Rell had brought from upcoast, quiet professionals who spoke to each other in a shorthand of grunts and tape measures — began assembling their charges on the barge deck under tarps, though there was no one for miles to hide them from except the sea itself.

Vey spent the afternoon doing his actual work, which was knowing things. He walked the decks. He listened. He learned that the demolition crew's lead man was named Ostrick and had once dropped a charge on his own tender and swum away from the consequences; that the *Lanternfish*'s sonar operator thought the cave went back further than anyone guessed, a throat swallowing chambers swallowing more chambers, "like a cathedral that drowned mid-prayer"; and that Rell had sent a message ashore by launch that morning, addressed to a harbormaster in Greyhaven, requesting — the phrase stuck in Vey's ear like a splinter — *"everything current on one Kestrel, river registry, and the master thereof."*

The master thereof.

Vey stood at the stern rail as the light went copper and the monsoon's bruise on the horizon deepened toward purple, and he let himself think the name he had been not-thinking for three days.

Ansel Sandoval. Except that wasn't the name, and he'd known it wasn't the name since Greyhaven, since the lockkeeper with the white eyebrows had said *Iversen* across a dock at dusk like a man laying a card face-up, and Vey had seen the riverboat captain's shoulders change — a small collapse, a small surrender, the way a person looks when a door they've been holding shut finally swings open and there's no one behind it but themselves.

He'd known her nine years. Not well — she didn't let people know her well; knowing Ansel was like knowing the delta, you learned the channels and never the bottom. But he'd run cargo with her twice, drunk with her once, and shared a night watch with her a third time when the fog came down so thick they'd sat in the Kestrel's wheelhouse till dawn talking about nothing, or rather talking around everything, two people circling the same absence without naming it. She had a laugh she used maybe four times a year, and he had heard all four.

And he had sold her name to Rell Danforth anyway. Not directly — never directly; Vey was careful the way scavengers are careful — but he'd mentioned the riverboat, the skill of her, the way she read water no chart recorded, and Rell's appetite had done the rest. *Find me people who can do impossible things,* Rell liked to say. *Impossible things are where the money hides.*

Now the money hid in a drowned cathedral, and the woman who could reach it was being hunted by a fleet with explosives, and Vey stood at the rail of the flagship feeling something shift in him that had no name he wanted to use.

He told himself it was professional judgment. Blowing the entrance was bad salvage practice; the blast would collapse chambers, bury the wreck deeper, possibly destroy the very prize Rell was hunting. That was true. It was even, mostly, why his stomach hurt.

Mostly.

* * *

The old debt surfaced that night, as old debts do, at an inconvenient hour.

It was past midnight. The anchorage lay black and breathing, masts ticking against stars, and Vey sat in the *Corvus*'s chart room with a lamp turned low, pretending to review the dive schedule. Through the open port he could hear the demolition crew's barge creaking at its mooring, and beneath that, fainter, the sea working patiently at the reef, the sound it makes when it's counting.

The debt was this: nine years ago, in a port called Saltmarsh, Vey had owed money to men who collected it with boat hooks and broken fingers, and a riverboat captain he barely knew had paid it for him. All of it. Without being asked. He'd found the debt cleared and a note in his cabin in a slanted, unsentimental hand: *You're worth more free than broken. Don't make me wrong.* Signed with no name at all, because she'd known he'd know.

He had asked her once, years later, why. She'd shrugged and said, "Someone paid a debt for my father once. Didn't save him. But I liked owing it forward better than carrying it." And then she'd changed the subject with the practiced violence of a woman steering away from a reef.

That was the whole architecture of it. One act of unasked grace, nine years old, still standing.

Vey took out a sheet of paper and looked at it for a long time.

What he wrote was not treason, he told himself. It was information. It was a schedule. Fifth tide, dawn plus two hours, demolition charges placed at the cave sill. Sixth tide at latest, detonation, weather permitting — and the weather would permit, because the monsoon's edge would arrive the day after, sealing everything behind forty days of rain and fury. If she meant to go in, she had one window left, and Rell was about to brick it up with dynamite.

She deserved to know that. Anyone would deserve to know that. It was practically a maritime courtesy.

He wrote the times. He wrote the placement of the charges. He wrote the watch rotation, because a schedule without a watch rotation was just poetry. Then he sat with the paper under the lamp and admitted, in the privacy of his own skull, what he had been refusing to admit at the rail, in the chart room, on the dive, in every hour since the lockkeeper had spoken her true name into the dusk:

He was choosing a side. And he could not say why.

Not for the debt — debts get paid and die. Not for ambition; betraying Rell ended that forever. Not even, entirely, for her, because he hardly knew her, because she was a locked room he'd only ever stood outside of, listening. It was something less articulable than any of that. It was the look on her shoulders at the dock. It was the note in his cabin nine years ago. It was the sense — irrational, unfashionable, persistent as rust — that of all the people converging on the Hollow, she was the one the cave was waiting for, and everyone else, himself included, was just a thief at a funeral.

Rell wanted the *Meridian*'s fortune. Vey understood wanting; wanting was his profession. But Ansel — Ansel wanted something the fleet had no category for. She wanted to know. And Vey had spent eleven years learning that knowledge was the only cargo that never spoiled, and the only cargo men killed for without admitting it.

He folded the paper twice. Then, because he was careful the way scavengers are careful, he copied nothing, signed nothing, and burned the original in the lamp flame, watching the ink curl and go orange and vanish. What remained was memory, and memory could not be found in a cabin search.

There was a fisherman's shack on the spit below the lighthouse, three miles up the coast, that sold bait and ice and asked no questions. There was a boy who ran messages between the shack and the boats at anchor for coins, a sharp-eyed child of perhaps twelve who had once carried a note for Vey before and had the good sense to forget it immediately. At dawn, Vey told himself. At dawn he'd take the shore boat in with the water party, slip away for ten minutes, and the message would be on its way to whatever anchorage the Kestrel haunted.

Ten minutes. That was all a defector's first step required. After that, the rest of the body followed whether it agreed to or not.

He looked out the port at the black water. Somewhere out there, under the counting sea, a drowned ship held a dead man's secrets, and a living woman was racing the tide to steal them back, and a fleet with dynamite was anchored between her and the truth like a hand laid flat over a mouth.

"Hold on," Vey said quietly, to no one, to the water, to a debt nine years outstanding. "One more tide. I'll buy you one more tide."

The sea kept counting. It had heard promises before.

And far up the coast, past the lighthouse and the spit and the shack that sold bait and ice, a riverboat rode at anchor in a channel no chart recorded — and Vey, blowing out the lamp, allowed himself the first honest thought of the week: *I hope she reads water better than she reads people, because by tomorrow night, everyone she's running from will know exactly where she's running to.*

The dawn came grey and windless, the monsoon holding its breath.

So did he.
