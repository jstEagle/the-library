## 16. Fourth Tide, the Narrow Way

The tide turned at 3:17 in the morning, and Ansel was already awake to meet it.

She had not slept so much as drifted near sleep, the way a boat drifts near a shoal — close enough to feel it, never quite touching. Toma had the deck below her, coiling lines in the dark by touch because he refused to waste lamp oil on work he could do blind. The Kestrel lay off the reef line with her engine idling at bare steerageway, a heartbeat more than nothing, and the sea around them breathed in that long, gathering rhythm that meant the water was beginning to leave.

Ansel stood at the wheel with her mother's chart spread across the binnacle, weighted at the corners with two brass washers and a cold cup of coffee she'd forgotten existed. The annotations glowed faintly under the compass light — thin, slanted script in faded violet ink, the handwriting she had spent thirty years not recognizing and three days unable to stop seeing.

*Follow the green stain past the second breaker. Depth lies here. Trust the mud, not the sand.*

Her mother had written *trust the mud*. As if she'd stood at this wheel herself. As if she'd known, decades before Ansel was born, that some daughter of hers would one day need the sentence.

"Water's dropping," Toma called softly from the foredeck. "Half a fathom gone already. It's fast tonight."

"It's a spring tide," Ansel said. "It always runs fast when it means it."

Below them, the Hollow waited behind its wall of reef, and somewhere beyond the reef, anchored in the black roads, the salvage fleet waited too. She had counted their lights before full dark: nine vessels, riding lamps strung out like a string of patient beads. Somewhere among them rode Rell Danforth's command barge with its demolition crew and its crates of blasting gelatin, and Vey's survey skiff, and all the machinery of men who solved problems by removing what stood in front of them.

They would come at slack water after dawn. Vey's message — passed through a fisherman who owed an old debt and couldn't look at Ansel while delivering it — had been specific: *charges set for the morning tide. They mean to open the cave whether it opens itself or not.*

Which left her this: one night, one falling tide, and a channel that every chart in the archipelago marked as dead water.

"Ansel." Toma came up beside her, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being clean years ago. He didn't ask the question again. She'd heard it enough in the last two days — *what are we actually doing, what is this worth, what happens to us if you're wrong* — and she'd answered with silence both times, and he'd accepted the silence the way loyal men accept wounds they can't treat. But now he just looked at the chart, at the violet handwriting, and said quietly: "Whose writing is that?"

"My mother's."

He absorbed it the way he absorbed everything, slowly and completely, like ballast going into a hold. "You never talk about her."

"There wasn't anything to say." Ansel traced the channel line with one finger. "That's changing."

* * *

The entrance to the narrow way opened off the reef's eastern shoulder, where the coral fell away in a drowned staircase and the water went from bottle-green to ink within the length of the Kestrel herself. On the surface it looked like exactly what it was: a seam of slightly darker water between two walls of breaking foam, no wider than a cart road, curving away into darkness where the swell folded over itself and made no sound at all.

Every chart called it impassable. Every pilot in Greyhaven would have told her the same thing her father's own notes said, in his blunt pencil hand: *No draft over six feet. Not worth the risk.* The Kestrel drew seven and a half.

But her mother's ink ran beneath the pencil, older than it, and her mother had written: *The charts measure the wrong year. The bar shifts a boat-length north each decade. In flood it lies; in ebb it tells the truth.*

"Engine to half," Ansel said. "Toma, I need your eyes forward and your hands ready. If I call starboard, you throw the fender over the port side and pray."

"What do I pray to?"

"Whatever listens to riverboat pilots."

The first breaker passed off their beam, close enough that spray came aboard cold as thrown gravel. Ansel brought the bow around and felt the current take hold of the hull — not gently, not cruelly, but with the impersonal grip of something vastly larger deciding what it would allow. The ebb was running out through the seam now, pouring off the reef flat behind it, and where the water poured it carved. That was the secret her mother had understood and the charts had missed: the channel wasn't a fixed thing cut into stone. It was a living throat, scoured deeper twice a day by the tide's own breath, and it moved with the decades the way a river moves, and only someone who read water the way other people read letters could find where it had gone.

Ansel read it now.

The green stain her mother described was still there — a discoloration in the phosphorescence, a thread of paler water where the ebb dragged silt up from the channel floor. She steered for it. The depth sounder sang its little anxious note and she made herself stop listening to it, because her mother had promised the instrument would lie, and standing at a wheel in the dark choosing a dead woman's word over her own instruments was either faith or madness and there was no time to decide which.

"Foam ahead, both sides," Toma called. "Narrowing."

"I see it."

"Ansel—"

"I see it, Toma."

The walls of white closed in until the breakers were close enough to read the water inside them, and the Kestrel slid into the seam like a needle into cloth. The hull groaned once — a long wooden complaint, the sound of a boat being asked for everything she had — and then the current took them properly and the world became speed and noise and the violet sentence glowing in her mind: *trust the mud, not the sand.*

The bottom rose under them. She felt it before any instrument confirmed it — a change in the boat's vibration, a softness in the way the rudder bit, the particular shudder of a keel kissing ground that hasn't decided yet whether to keep her or let her go. Mud. Soft, deep, forgiving mud, laid down over the hard sand shelf that would have torn her open. Her mother hadn't just found the channel. She had learned what it was made of.

"Port ten," Ansel said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Then ease her. Let the current carry us — don't fight it, it knows the way better than I do."

"You're letting the water drive?"

"I'm letting my mother drive. I'm just holding the wheel."

The seam curved, tightened, opened. A breaker collapsed against the reef to starboard with a sound like a door slamming, and then another, and then — abruptly, completely — silence. The swell was behind them. Ahead, the water lay flat and black and sheltered, ringed by the reef's dark shoulder, and rising out of it, pale as bone in the starlight, the limestone face of the headland with the cave mouth somewhere in its roots, still drowned, still waiting for the tide to fall far enough.

The lagoon.

Ansel brought the Kestrel around in a slow circle, taking it in. Flat water. Deep enough, by the smell and the set of the current eddying off the rock, to float her at any tide. Hidden from the roads by the whole mass of the reef. A fortress, exactly as the legend promised — a fortress whose gate opened only for those who knew it was a gate.

Toma stood at the bow with the rag hanging forgotten from his fist. "I've run this coast nine years," he said finally. "Nine years, and I've never heard of anyone coming through there alive."

"No one has. Except whoever wrote that chart." Ansel killed the engine and the sudden quiet rang in her ears. "Get the anchors down, Toma. Both of them, well spread. We're not moving again until the story's finished."

* * *

Dawn came gray and slow, the monsoon light already thickening in the south like smoke behind glass. Ansel sat in the wheelhouse with the logbook's absence heavy in her chest — tomorrow, maybe. Tomorrow, on the fifth tide, she would go down into the cave and take it. Tonight had only been about arriving first.

They heard the fleet before they saw it: the deep stutter of a work barge's engine, distorted by the reef, carrying over the flat water like a voice through a wall. Then shapes at the reef's edge, beyond the line of breakers — the command barge holding station, a launch lowering away, figures in orange working jackets clustered at the reef shoulder where the narrow way hid itself in foam.

Through Toma's battered binoculars, Ansel watched Rell Danforth's demolition crew do what demolition crews do: measure, argue, point. She saw the survey skiff edge along the outside of the reef, sounding, finding nothing but the same dead water every chart promised. She saw a man in a clean coat — Rell himself, unmistakable even at distance, the way certain people remain themselves at any magnification — stand at the barge rail looking at the reef line with the expression of a man reading a document that keeps refusing to make sense.

Because it should have been simple. Blow the outer ledge at low water, enter on the next tide, take the wreck. That had been the plan since the fleet anchored. And the plan had assumed the cave belonged to whoever arrived with the most gelatin.

What the plan had not assumed was a boat already inside.

She watched the moment it happened. A lookout on the barge's masthead turned, shaded his eyes, and went very still. Then the glasses swung — she felt them pass over the lagoon like a searchlight — and stopped. Even at half a mile, through spray and gray light, she could see the stillness spread through the men on deck, one to another, the way wind moves through wheat.

The Kestrel sat in their fortress. Their impenetrable cave, their prize behind its wall of reef, and a forty-foot riverboat moored in the middle of it flying no flag at all, as if she had grown there, as if she had always been there, as if the impossible channel were simply a road that everyone else had been too deaf to hear.

Rell Danforth lowered his own glasses. Across the water, faint but clear in the morning hush, Ansel heard him give an order — she couldn't make out the words, only the tone, clipped and controlled and very slightly wrong, the tone of a man recalculating everything.

The launch turned back toward the barge. The demolition crew began, reluctantly, to repack their charges.

Toma appeared at the wheelhouse door with two cups of coffee and handed her one without a word. Together they watched the fleet rearrange itself around the fact of them.

"They'll try to come in," Toma said. "Same channel."

"They'll try," Ansel agreed. "And they'll spend a day learning what I learned in twenty minutes, except they'll be learning it with a fleet's draft instead of mine." She drank the coffee. It was terrible and hot and exactly right. "And when they fail, Rell will stop trying to get in himself, and start trying to get me out. He won't blow the reef with me inside it — not yet. First he'll offer me things."

"He already offered you things."

"This time he'll be angry about it." She set down the cup and picked up the chart, folding it along her mother's creases, careful as handling a letter. Outside, the southern sky had gone the color of wet slate, and the first true breath of the monsoon moved through the rigging, a low note that would build for days.

"Tide turns in four hours," she said. "Fifth tide. That's when I dive the cave."

"And me?"

Ansel looked at him — nine years of loyalty, one night of truth, and whatever was coming next — and found she was tired of lying to the only man who'd never asked her to.

"Keep the boat ready," she said. "And keep watch on that barge. Because after tomorrow, Toma, none of us are invisible anymore."
