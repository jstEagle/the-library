## 17. Siege at Slack Water

**Ansel**

Slack water came at dawn, and with it the fleet.

Ansel stood in the Kestrel's wheelhouse and watched them come through the morning haze the way she had once watched weather fronts build over open water — not as ships but as a pressure, a slow dark weight sliding across the lagoon's mouth. Three barges and a salvage tender, riding low with equipment. Cranes folded like sleeping herons. On the lead barge, men moved with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told no by the sea.

Behind the reef, the lagoon held its breath. It was a bowl of green glass ringed by coral walls, deep enough to float a boat of the Kestrel's draft and shallow enough everywhere else to break one. Her mother's annotations had brought her through the narrow way on the fourth tide — *the channel lies where the water lies*, written in that slanted hand Ansel now knew better than her own — and for two days she had been the only soul inside the fortress. Now the siege had arrived.

"They're anchoring off the entrance," Toma said from the deck below. He had the field glasses up, elbows braced on the rail. "Four points. They're not even trying to hide it."

"They don't need to." She came down the ladder and stood beside him. The air smelled of salt and wet stone and, faintly, of diesel drifting across the reef flat. "They know I'm in here. Rell put me here."

"You think it's a trap?"

"I think everything since Greyhaven has been a trap I walked into with my eyes open." She took the glasses from him. Through the lenses the tenders resolved into detail: divers' platforms, air hoses coiled like intestines, a barge stacked with crates she recognized by their stenciling. Demolition charges. Rell hadn't waited for tides after all; he meant to make his own door.

But he couldn't blow the entrance while she was inside. Not without burying the Meridian under half the reef — and not without killing her, which was a different kind of arithmetic entirely. A dead Iversen was a martyr. A living one, starved out or bargained down, was a signature on a document.

"He'll send someone," she said.

"Send someone where? He can't cross the reef except on the tide, and the tide won't turn for—"

The sound reached them then, thin across the water: an oar-lock, rhythmic, patient. A longboat rounding the southern horn of the lagoon through a gap in the coral that existed only at slack water and only for something small enough to thread it. Two men rowing. In the stern, wrapped against the spray in a grey coat, sat a figure who raised one hand in a gesture so old-fashioned, so absurdly courteous, that Ansel's stomach turned before her mind caught up.

A white flag. A rag tied to a boat hook, fluttering like a surrender nobody meant.

"That's parley," Toma said quietly.

"That's theater." But she was already unclipping the boarding ladder.

* * *

**Toma**

Toma had spent nine years learning the difference between Ansel's silences, and this morning's silence was the worst kind: the kind that had already decided something. She stood at the rail watching the longboat come, and her face did the thing it always did when she was about to spend money she didn't have — nothing at all.

"I'll go down," he said.

"No."

"Toma goes down," he said, using her own trick against her, naming himself in the third person the way she named the boat. "You dive on the fifth tide. That's four hours. You can't be shaking hands with Danforth four hours before you need your blood cold."

Her jaw worked. Out on the water the longboat rose and fell, patient as a bill collector.

"If he offers you anything," she said finally, "he means it as a knife. Whatever shape it comes in."

"I know what knives look like. I've been carrying yours for nine years."

Something crossed her face then — not quite a smile, not quite an apology — and she went aft to check her dive gear, which needed no checking, because she had checked it twice already. Toma went down the ladder to meet the parley alone.

The man in the grey coat was not what Toma expected. He was older than the photographs Ansel kept hidden in the chart drawer — Toma had seen them once, years ago, when she'd been drunk on arrack after a bad crossing and shown him a woman's face and said *that's my mother, don't ask me anything else*. Rell Danforth in the flesh was broader, grayer, sun-cured to the color of old rope, with a voice like a hull scraping a bar: smooth until it caught on something.

"You'd be the engineer," Danforth said, not climbing the ladder, just holding onto it, keeping the boats apart by a meter of green water. "Tomas Reyes. Nine years aboard. No family. No debts worth the name. A man who could walk away clean." He smiled, and it was a good smile, the kind that had signed away a hundred wrecks. "I admire that in a man."

"You've been reading my file."

"I read everyone's file. It's how I stay rich." He let the white flag dip, almost apologetic. "Here's the shape of it, engineer. The boat is yours. Papers drawn this afternoon, registered clean, no liens, no questions about how she was paid for. And a pardon — call it amnesty, call it whatever makes you comfortable — for anything you've carried across any line in nine years. I know what some of those cargoes were. I don't care. Walk away. Take the Kestrel to any harbor you like and live to be old."

Toma looked at him for a long moment. The lagoon lapped at the hulls. Somewhere overhead a frigatebird turned on the wind like a comma in an unfinished sentence.

"And Ansel?"

Danforth's smile didn't move, but something behind it did — a shutter closing, a ledger being balanced. "Ansel Iversen is standing between me and the last thing I'm owed in this world. She can swim out whenever she likes. I'm not offering her harm. I'm offering you a door, and doors close, engineer. This one closes with the tide."

"The fifth tide," Toma said. "That's when your door closes."

Danforth's eyes flickered — a fraction of surprise, quickly banked — and Toma understood that he had just told the man something, and hated himself for it, and filed the hatred away to examine later, if there was a later.

"Think about it," Danforth said. "You have until slack water ends. My boat will wait at the southern gap." He pushed off from the ladder, and the longboat swung away, oars dipping, the white rag still flying its little lie of peace.

Toma stood at the rail until the boat was gone, then climbed back aboard, and found Ansel waiting at the top of the ladder with her dive mask hanging around her neck like a question.

"Well?" she said.

"He offered me the boat."

She went very still. "And?"

"And I told him the fifth tide was when his door closed." Toma met her eyes. "Because I'm a fool, Ansel. Because nine years ago you pulled me out of a debtors' cell in Port Sarrow and never asked me one question I couldn't answer, and I decided that whatever you were running from, I'd run alongside. But I'm done pretending I don't see the cliff we're driving toward. So here's what I see. He wants you off this boat before the fifth tide. Which means the fifth tide is when you dive. Which means everything — the wreck, the logbook, whatever truth you've been choking on since Greyhaven — happens in the next four hours, while every man on that fleet is watching me decide whether to sell you."

The wind shifted. The first breath of the monsoon's leading edge came across the reef, warm and wrong, smelling of rain still hours out at sea.

"So don't decide," Ansel said. "Do something he can't price."

* * *

**Toma**

He thought about it the way he thought about engines: not what should happen, but what could, given the parts at hand.

The parts at hand were these. One boat, moored fore and aft to coral pinnacles with lines that took him twenty minutes to rig and would take anyone else twice that to understand. One lagoon with a single navigable exit that existed only near slack water. One fleet anchored outside, whose commander believed — had to believe — that the Kestrel would sit at anchor until her captain surfaced from her dive, because a boat at anchor was a hostage, and hostages made negotiations simple.

And one fact that Toma knew and Rell Danforth did not: the Kestrel could drift. She had been built to drift, rigged to drift, designed by a paranoid woman and maintained by a loyal man to float loose on any water deeper than her keel and go wherever the current pushed, silent, unlit, unstoppable — a ghost with an engine that could wake in ninety seconds.

If she stayed moored, she was a target. If she drifted, she was a decoy, a smoke screen, a moving question mark between the fleet's guns and the cave mouth where Ansel would surface with her father's logbook in a waterproof bag clenched in her teeth.

It would cost him the boat. Maybe the boat, maybe more. Drifting hulls got boarded. Drifting hulls got shot at by nervous men with demolition charges. And when it was over — if it was over — there would be no Kestrel to register in any harbor, no nine years of varnish and patched planking and 3 a.m. engine surgery to show for it.

He stood in the engine room with his hand on the main breaker and let himself feel the size of it. Then he felt it pass through him, the way a squall passes, leaving the same air behind but changed.

He went looking for her and found her at the cave mouth, checking the flood line, marking the water level on the stone with chalk the way she'd marked it every hour since they'd arrived. The fifth tide was building. He could hear it in the reef — a low hum through the rock, the sea drawing its breath.

"When you go down," he said, "I'm cutting the lines."

She turned. For once she didn't hide anything. What was on her face was naked enough to hurt: gratitude, and guilt, and underneath both the old fear that had made her forge a name and run a coast for fifteen years — the fear of being owed, and owing.

"Toma—"

"Don't argue. Arguing costs tide." He handed her the waterproof bag, already packed: flare pistol, signal mirror, the spare air bottle. "Drift plan's simple. I cut all four lines together, she swings free, current takes her north along the inner wall toward the narrows. From outside, they'll see a boat adrift with no one aboard and assume you've slipped out or drowned. Either way, every eye goes to the drifting hull and not to the cave mouth. You surface on the ebb, behind the northern horn, and I'll have the dinghy there. If I don't—" he shrugged, "—swim south along the wall, there's a shingle beach past the second pinnacle. We buried water and biscuit there yesterday. You remember."

"I remember everything," she said. "That's the problem with me."

"Then remember this too." He took her shoulders. She let him, which she almost never did. "Whatever's in that logbook — whoever it names, whatever it burns down — you come back up. I didn't spend nine years keeping this boat alive to fish a corpse out of a cave. You come back up, and we settle accounts like living people do."

For a moment her composure cracked along an old seam, and he saw the girl from the photographs, the one who'd lost a father and a name in the same season. Then it sealed again, and she was the captain.

"Fifth tide," she said. "Ninety seconds to start the engine if you need her."

"Ninety," he agreed. "Go."

* * *

**Ansel**

The water closed over her head and the world became the world she trusted most: green-gold light, the tick and groan of cooling rock, her own breath loud as surf in her ears. Below her the cave mouth opened downward into darkness, and she followed her mother's annotations the way a blind woman follows a railing — *past the third ledge, where the old scar in the stone runs white, turn left into the throat* — down through the flooded passage toward the Meridian.

Above her, muffled by fathoms of water and a hundred meters of coral, she heard — or imagined she heard — the small sharp sounds of a knife going through mooring lines, and then the strange seismic silence of a boat set loose.

* * *

**Toma**

The lines parted clean. All four, one stroke each, the way he'd rehearsed it in his head forty times since dawn.

The Kestrel sighed off the coral and began to move — slowly at first, then with the gathering certainty of a thing returning to its nature. The current took her north along the inner wall, exactly as promised, her hull gleaming wet in the rising light, decks empty, wheel spinning lazily in the swell like a hand waving goodbye.

Out beyond the reef, the fleet erupted. Voices carried across the flat calm water with terrible clarity. *Boat's adrift — she's moving — no crew visible — Danforth wants eyes on her — launch the tender.* A workboat churned away from the salvage tender, cutting north to intercept a ghost. Every glass on every barge swung to follow the drifting hull. Every man who mattered was suddenly watching Toma's nine years of work float away without him.

He stood at the northern horn with the dinghy's painter in his fist and the engine cover off beside him, tools laid out in a neat row he wouldn't need unless everything went wrong, and watched his life drift out of reach, and found — to his genuine surprise — that he was smiling.

"Loyalty," he said aloud, to the gulls, to the reef, to nobody. "Nine years, and it turns out it was a choice after all."

The fifth tide peaked. The water at the cave mouth stilled, held, and began to fall.

Down in the dark, somewhere beneath his feet, Ansel Iversen was swimming toward the truth that had killed her father — and Toma Reyes, boatless for the first time in thirty years, crouched at the waterline with a rope in his hands, ready to catch whatever she brought up.
