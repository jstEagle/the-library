## 18. Fifth Tide, the Meridian

The water in the Hollow did not move the way sea water moved. It breathed.

Ansel felt it the moment she slipped beneath the surface of the lagoon — a slow tidal pulse through the flooded cave's throat, drawing her inward and releasing her again, like something enormous asleep and dreaming with its mouth open. Her lamp threw a coin of light ahead of her that trembled on walls furred with pale growth, stone the color of drowned bone. Above her, the ceiling arched away into blackness where the air pocket lived, a hollow lung the cave kept for itself. Below, the passage dropped into a dark so complete it seemed to press against her skin.

She had planned this dive for two days and rehearsed it in her head for twenty years without knowing it.

*Fifth tide,* Toma had said at the mooring line, gripping her wrist harder than he needed to. *You said fifth tide is the long one. You come back up before slack ends or I'm coming in after you, papers or no papers.*

*I know,* she'd told him. *I know, old man.* And she had seen in his face that he knew she was lying about how simple it would be, and that he was letting her go anyway, which was its own kind of love.

Now there was only the breathing water and the line paying out behind her, knotted every ten meters so she could count her way home by touch. Fifty meters. The passage narrowed, walls closing until her shoulders nearly brushed them, and the lamp caught something that made her heart stop mid-beat: a groove cut into the rock, hand-chiseled, running along the wall at chest height. A guide channel. Someone had marked this passage once, deliberately, for boats or lines or men swimming in the dark with lamps guttering.

Her father's handwriting had been in the chart margins. But chisels were not his tool. He had been a captain, not a quarryman. Someone else had cut this channel — someone who had known the Hollow well enough to improve it.

She pushed the thought down with the air in her lungs and swam on.

Eighty meters. The knot-count said eighty, and the passage opened like a held breath released, and then the light found it.

The Meridian lay on her starboard side across the cave floor as if she had lain down to sleep and simply never been woken. Two hundred tons of coastal trader settled into silt, masts snapped but present, rigging hanging in ropes of soft gray weed, her hull furred white with mineral bloom so that in the lamplight she looked less like a wreck than like a reef that had dreamed itself into the shape of a ship. Ansel hung in the water above her and forgot, for three full seconds, to breathe.

She had been nine years old when they told her the Meridian went down with all hands off the Teeth, hull stove by the reef, no survivors, no wreckage worth finding. She had built her whole life on top of that sentence the way a barnacle builds on a piling. And here the ship was — not broken on any reef, not torn open by storm, but lying whole and deliberate inside a cave no honest captain had reason to enter.

Ships don't sail into caves, her father used to say, back when he still said things to her. Ships are sunk into them.

She kicked down along the hull, counting frames, reading the plating the way she read water. No breach on the seaward side. No reef damage at all. When she rounded toward the bilge strake, her lamp found the seacocks — two of them, both open, their wheels turned fully hand-tight and left that way. Deliberate. Patient. Someone had stood in this engine space with a lamp and time enough to spare, and opened the sea to the ship inch by inch while the rest of the crew slept or trusted or never knew.

Scuttled. Not lost. *Murdered*, if a ship could be murdered, and Ansel had spent thirty years believing she knew who held the knife: Rell Danforth, rival salvager, the man from her mother's photographs, the name her mother spat like a fishbone. Every story Ansel had ever told herself about her own life had Danforth's hands on the seacocks.

She found the companionway and went in through the broken skylight, into the belly of the thing.

* * *

Inside, the Meridian was a cathedral of stillness.

Silt had risen to bury the deck furniture; her lamp swept over the shapes of a chart table, a bench, a brass rail gone green as riverstone. Small fish scattered before her light like startled thoughts. The air pocket above the cabin was narrow and foul-smelling, and when she surfaced into it, her own breathing came back at her doubled and strange, echoing off stone close overhead.

The captain's cabin was aft. Of course it was aft. She had grown up aboard boats; she could have found it blind.

The door hung half off its hinges, and beyond it the cabin lay in a shroud of fine gray sediment that had fallen like snow over decades and never been disturbed. No diver had been here. In sixty years, no one had come into this room. Ansel hovered at the threshold and felt the strange vertigo of standing in a place that had been waiting for her specifically — because the lockkeeper's packet had said *for the one who comes back by water*, and here she was, back, by water, exactly as some dying man had gambled she would be.

The desk was against the far bulkhead, its drawers swollen shut with sixty years of swelling wood. She worked the top one loose with her knife, gently, the way you open anything that has been holding its breath since before you were born.

Papers first, fused into a brick of pulp she barely touched, afraid to destroy whatever words might survive in it. Then, beneath, wrapped in oilcloth tied with tarred twine — her father's habit, she knew it instantly, she had watched him wrap engine parts in just such cloth with just such knots — a book.

She brought it up into the air pocket and laid it on the edge of the desk where the silt was thinnest, and her hands were shaking so badly she had to press them flat against the wood and breathe before she could untie the twine.

The logbook of the *Meridian*. Master: E. Iversen. Her father's initials looking up at her out of the lamplight like a man raising his head.

The early pages were routine — cargo manifests, weather, soundings, the small dry poetry of a working captain's day. *Made fair passage. Crew grumbling about salt beef. Wind veering NNE.* She read fast, hungry, and the hunger made her careless with her tears, which fell and beaded on the waxed pages without soaking in, thank God, thank the tarred canvas and the careful dead man who had chosen it.

Then, near the end, the entries changed.

*Danforth came aboard at Greyhaven. Terms agreed. He takes the survey fees, I take the salvage rights, and the Meridian's books stay clean between us. Mother says I'm selling the ship twice. Mother is usually right and I have learned to hate it.*

Ansel stopped. Read it again. The lamp flame — she had switched to the sealed electric lamp for the dive, but the backup candle lantern she'd carried in its cage flickered anyway, or seemed to — the light trembled on the page.

*Salvage rights.* Her father and Rell Danforth, partners. Not rivals. Partners — running a fraud together, double-selling surveys, skimming wreck claims off a coast full of drowned men. The story her mother had fed her all her life — *Danforth betrayed your father, Danforth sank the Meridian, we fled because Danforth hunted us* — curdled in her chest and re-formed into something with an entirely different shape.

She turned the last leaf.

The final entry was dated four days before the official loss of the Meridian. The handwriting had deteriorated, the letters leaning like grass in wind, written fast by a man who knew what he was writing might be the last true thing anyone ever read.

*It is done and I cannot undo it. The seacocks are open. By morning she'll be on the floor of the Hollow where no survey will ever look, and the insurance will call her lost with all hands, and the fraud dies with the ship. That was the plan. That was always the plan.*

*But Maren opened the valves. Not me. I told her I would do it myself, one captain's debt to his own ship, and she looked at me the way she looks at a fouled chart and said, You'll lose your nerve, E., and she was right, and she went down the engine hatch with the lamp and did it in eleven minutes.*

*My wife scuttled my ship to save our skins, and she did it better than I could have, and I do not know anymore whether I married an angel or something the sea sent to collect on me. The crew goes ashore tonight at the fishing village. They think it's a liberty run. God forgive me. God forgive us both. If anyone reads this, know that the betrayal everyone will hunt for outside this ship was committed inside it, by the two people the boy—*

The entry stopped. The ink dragged off the edge of the word as though the pen had been lifted in haste — footsteps, perhaps, or a change of heart, or simply the end of what a man could make himself write.

Ansel floated in the foul air of the pocket and heard her own heartbeat in the stone.

*Maren.* Her mother. Not Danforth. Not a rival, not a hunter, not the villain of every bedtime story and every flinch Ansel had ever carried at the mention of the salvage fleet. Her mother had walked down an engine ladder with a lamp in her teeth and opened the sea into a ship with sleeping men aboard — no. No, the crew went ashore first. He wrote it. *They think it's a liberty run.* No one died. Only the ship died, and the truth, and the girl her daughter had been allowed to remain.

Everyone she inherited was guilty. Her father, the fraud. Her mother, the saboteur. Danforth, the partner. And herself — herself most of all, maybe, the loyal daughter who had spent thirty years running from a crime that had never been committed, carrying a hatred like a heirloom, polishing it, feeding it, building a whole fugitive life on its handle.

She laughed. It came out of her in the closed stone dark and echoed back wrong, too loud, a stranger's laugh. The candle flame bent.

And underneath the laugh, quieter, the other thing: *she knew.* Her mother had known where the Hollow was — had to have known, to choose it, to navigate a sinking ship into a cave mouth that only showed on the lowest tides of a decade. The second handwriting on the chart. The annotations predating the wreck. Her mother hadn't fled the coast in fear. Her mother had fled *toward* something, guarding the one map to the one place that could hang them all, and had raised her daughter on a lie to keep her from ever coming back to look.

All those years of silence. All those years Ansel had read her mother's stillness as grief. It had been surveillance. It had been a woman watching her child for signs of curiosity, and breathing easier every year the child showed none.

Until the spring tide came, and the cave bared its teeth, and the child — no longer a child, forged papers and stolen name and all — came back by water, exactly as promised.

* * *

She almost missed the tide.

She knew it was happening the way she knew everything about water, in her wrists and inner ear before her mind caught up: the breathing of the cave slowing, the pulse through the passage going slack, the whole drowned throat of the Hollow beginning to seal. Slack water. The turn. She had perhaps forty minutes before the current reversed and the entrance narrowed, and the swim out was eighty meters through a channel that would become a jetty of moving pressure.

She wrapped the logbook in the oilcloth, retied her father's knots over her own, and sealed it in the dry bag strapped to her harness. Her hand rested on it a moment longer than necessary. Sixty years in the dark, and now it would ride against her ribs through the cold, the closest thing to a heartbeat either of them had.

At the companionway she paused, lamp raised, and looked back down the length of the Meridian one last time — the furred hull, the open seacocks, the whole patient lie of her. *I'm sorry,* Ansel said, and the bubbles took the words somewhere she couldn't follow. *Not for you. For her. For all of us.*

Then she swam.

The passage out was harder than the passage in — the first breath of the turning tide already tugging at her fins, the guide-channel groove sliding under her gloved hand like a rope thrown to a drowning woman by someone sixty years dead. She counted knots. Forty. Twenty. Ten. The water brightened from black to green to the hammered silver of the lagoon, and she rose through it into rain.

The monsoon's leading edge had arrived while she was below — a gray curtain marching across the reef flat, wind flattening the lagoon into streaks, Toma hauling on the mooring line with his face turned up and shouting something the wind ate whole. She broke the surface and he had her arm over the gunwale before she'd finished spitting the sea out, and then she was on the deck of the Kestrel, on her knees, alive, with the dry bag clutched to her chest like a child.

"You're bleeding," Toma said.

"I'm *what*?"

"Your hand." He turned it over. A gash across the palm, deep, clean — she had no memory of it, must have taken it on the coral at the entrance or on the broken skylight, and hadn't felt a thing. The blood ran pink in the rain and dripped through the deck boards into the sea below, into the lagoon, into the Hollow, back to the ship.

Toma looked at the dry bag. Looked at her face. Whatever he saw there made him stop asking questions, which for Toma was a kind of surrender.

"The fleet," he said instead. "Rell's barges are moving. Vey's boat came through the pass twenty minutes ago, running dark, and headed straight for us. Ansel — the storm's here early and Danforth isn't turning back."

She got to her feet, logbook against her ribs, blood and rain mixing on the boards, and looked east through the gray curtain at the shapes assembling out of the squall — masts and cranes and the low black hulks of the demolition barges, coming for the cave, coming for the truth, coming for her.

"He can't blow the entrance," she said. "There's a man alive in this world who knows what's written in this book, and it isn't only me he should be afraid of."

Thunder answered her, out over the water, rolling in from the open sea like something finally waking up.
