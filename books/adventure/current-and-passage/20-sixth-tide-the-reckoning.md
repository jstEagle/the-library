## 20. Sixth Tide, the Reckoning

The monsoon did not end so much as exhaust itself.

All through the night the wind had torn at the lagoon like something trying to get in, and by grey morning it had spent its fury into long, ragged gusts that came off the water smelling of salt and drowned leaves. The rain fell now in a fine, persistent veil rather than sheets, and through it Ansel could see the fleet's remaining ships riding at anchor beyond the reef, their lights smeared into halos, their shapes more suggestion than fact. One barge was gone. She had watched it go down in the dark with eleven men aboard and had thrown them a line across sixty feet of chaos, and she had felt each of their hands close on it as if her own rope had grown fingers.

Toma stood beside her at the rail, a blanket around his shoulders, saying nothing. He had not said much since the fifth tide — since she had come up out of the cave with the logbook sealed in oilcloth against her chest, since he had seen her face and understood without asking that whatever was written there had cost her something no salvage could pay for.

"The sixth tide turns at noon," he said finally. "Slack's maybe twenty minutes either side. After that the entrance closes for the season."

"I know."

"You've known since midnight." He said it without accusation. Nine years will teach a man when a statement is an invitation and when it is only weather. "You're going to give it to him."

She looked at him. Rain ran down his seamed face and dripped from his beard, and he let it, the way he let most things, patient as ballast.

"Yes," she said.

"Everything? The wreck, the cargo, the rights?"

"Everything but this." She touched the oilcloth bundle under her jacket, over her heart, where she had carried it since surfacing. "The logbook is mine. The truth is mine. The rest of it can sink or float or rot; I don't care anymore."

Toma nodded slowly, and then he did the thing that undid her more than any argument could have: he reached over and squeezed her shoulder once, hard, the way he did when a line came taut and held. "Then we'd best make you presentable," he said. "You look like something the sea refused."

* * *

They dressed the Kestrel in what dignity remained to her. Toma rigged the signal halyard and ran up a white cloth — not a flag of surrender, Ansel told herself, though she knew how it would read from the fleet's decks, and knew too that she no longer minded how things read. For nine years she had lived by misreading: papers that lied, names that weren't hers, a history worn smooth by retelling until it fit comfortably in the hand. Today she wanted to be read plainly, once, by everyone.

The tide began its long exhale around ten. By eleven the reef's spine showed black and glistening through the thinning rain, and the cave mouth — the Hollow, the place that charged admission in drowned men's debts — stood open at its foot like a held breath. Water poured from the ledges in silver threads. Somewhere inside, forty feet back through the flooded passage she had mapped on the second tide and threaded again on the fourth and fifth, the Meridian lay on her beam ends in the green dark, her masts fanned across the stone like the ribs of a fallen animal, her captain's cabin still holding the logbook's ghost even now that the book itself was gone.

Ansel took the Kestrel out through the lagoon on the last of the ebb, Toma at the wheel, Vey standing awkwardly at the rail in borrowed oilskins.

Vey had come aboard before dawn, rowed out from the fleet in a skiff he had no orders to take, carrying nothing but himself and a face like a man who has jumped and not yet landed. He had not explained himself. He had said only, "I owe you from Colm's Reach, and I'm paying it," and Ansel, who kept her own ledger of debts both real and invented, had let him aboard. Now he watched the reef slide past with the expression of a man returning to a house he'd burgled.

"They'll have guns trained on us," he said. "Rell doesn't forgive the fourth tide. Or the fifth."

"He doesn't have to forgive me," Ansel said. "He just has to listen."

"And if he doesn't?"

She thought of the logbook's last entry, the ink browned with thirty years of brine, the handwriting steady until the final lines and then not steady at all. *Scuttled by my own hand's knowledge and another's order. M. opened the seacocks. God forgive us both, we were paid to lose her.* Her mother's initials. Her mother's hand, matching the annotations on the chart that had guided Ansel through channels no chart recorded. Everyone she had inherited was guilty — her father who defrauded, her mother who scuttled, Rell Danforth who had signed the partnership and then buried it beneath a legend of rival captains and treacherous seas. Thirty years of grief built on a fraud, and she had been its last custodian.

"Then he doesn't," she said aloud. "But I watched him lose a barge last night and send his divers after it anyway. A man that hungry isn't thinking about guns. He's thinking about what's in the cave."

* * *

The fleet's boats came out to meet them at the edge of the roads — two launches, gray with rain, each carrying armed men who tried to look bored and failed. Rell Danforth stood in the bow of the first, and seeing him whole and upright after the night he'd had gave Ansel a strange, unwelcome flicker of respect. His coat was soaked through. There was a bandage at his temple where a block had swung wild in the storm, and the blood had dried in a dark comma down his cheek that he had not bothered to wipe away. He looked older than the man from her mother's photographs, older than the charming commander who had hailed her under a white flag and called her by a childhood nickname like a key turning in a lock. He looked like a man who had bet everything on one more tide and was no longer sure the sea honored debts.

The launches closed. Grapnels kissed the Kestrel's hull. Hands caught lines, and then they were made fast, three vessels breathing together in the swell, and the armed men looked everywhere except at the woman in the oilskins who had beaten them all.

"Captain Iversen." Rell used the true name deliberately, and she heard in it both the threat it had been at their parley and something else now — something almost like grief. "You have a strange way of running a blockade. You sail into it."

"I'm not here to blockade anything." She lifted the oilcloth bundle. "I'm here to settle accounts."

His eyes went to the bundle and stayed there, and she saw hunger move through his face like current under ice. "That's fleet property," he said quietly. "Salvage law—"

"Salvage law belongs to whoever holds the paper." She unwrapped the oilcloth with deliberate slowness, letting the rain spot the old leather cover. "And here's the paper that matters. Your signature, Rell. My father's signature. A salvage partnership dated fourteen months before the Meridian sailed — a partnership to run her aground insured, to split the payout, to blame the weather and a dead man's rivals. You were partners. And when she went down for real, with my mother opening the seacocks on your orders, you got your payout and your legend, and my father got a grave with no body, and I got raised on a lie with a forged name to hide behind."

The launch went very quiet. The armed men had stopped pretending to look elsewhere.

Rell's face did something complicated — she watched it happen, watch-band tight, the charm and the command peeling away layer by layer like paint off a hull left too long in the sun. "You don't know what those years were," he said at last, and his voice had lost its music. "You don't know what we owed, what the insurance men—"

"I know what's written." She turned to the second launch, where a narrow, rain-bent figure stood clutching a brass-bound case to his chest: the fleet's own surveyor, a precise, unhappy man named Aldous Crane whom Vey had described as the only person in the fleet who cared more about accuracy than advancement. "Surveyor Crane. You'll want to verify the document. I want it verified. I want your report to say exactly what it says, in your hand, under your seal, so that no one — not the fleet, not the insurers, not some harbor clerk with too many questions — can ever call it forgery. I've had enough of forged papers to last me."

Crane came aboard without waiting for permission. He took the logbook with gloved hands, and Ansel watched him read — watched thirty years collapse behind his eyes in the space of four pages. When he looked up, he looked at Rell Danforth the way a man looks at a chart that has proven false.

"The signatures are consistent with fleet records," Crane said carefully. "The partnership terms match the Meridian's insurance filings. This is—" He stopped. Started again. "This is authentic."

* * *

What followed was not a battle, because Ansel had already won it, and winning by relinquishing meant there was nothing left to fight over.

She laid it out plainly, standing in the rain with the fleet's guns trained loosely on her and meaning less than the wet rope they gripped. The wreck of the Meridian, with whatever cargo and specie still lay in her — surrendered. All salvage rights, all claims, all title — surrendered, executed in writing before Crane's seal before the tide turned. The coordinates of the inner passage, which existed in no other living memory except perhaps her mother's, and her mother was eleven years in a churchyard at Greyhaven — given freely, drawn in ink on the back of her father's chart while they watched.

In exchange she asked for one thing, and it was so small that Rell flinched when she named it, because smallness in a negotiation means the other party wants it more than gold.

"The Kestrel goes free. My crew goes free. And my name goes in your report as Ansel Iversen, master, born Greyhaven — clean, witnessed, unforgeable. You made me a ghost, Rell. Unmake it."

"You could have sold this," Rell said. His voice was hoarse. He had not touched the logbook since Crane took it; he circled it the way a man circles a fire. "The cargo alone. The rights. You could have bought every harbor on this coast and never docked twice in the same one. That's what you wanted, isn't it? To never stay anywhere long enough to be found?"

"That's what I wanted," Ansel agreed. "It's also what you taught me to want. You and my mother and my father, between you, made staying the most dangerous thing a person could do." She looked at him steadily, and let him see that there was no hatred left in it, only the flat clear light of someone who has finished a long calculation. "I'm done being taught."

Rell Danforth stood in the rain for a long moment. The wind had died to almost nothing; the sea breathed in long slow swells; somewhere beyond the reef a curlew cried, absurd and ordinary. And then he did the thing she had not predicted, though later she would understand she should have: he laughed. It was a terrible sound, short and cracked, half sob.

"She knew," he said. "Your mother. All these years I told myself she was coerced, that Iversen forced her hand, that she was another victim of his greed — and the logbook says she volunteered. Says she asked which seacocks." He pressed the heel of his hand against the bandage at his temple. "I loved her, you know. Before any of it. Before the partnership, before the money. And she chose him, and then she chose the plan, and I have spent thirty years building a story where I was the one betrayed."

"I know," Ansel said quietly. "I built the same story. Different villain. Same architecture."

He looked at her then — really looked, past the oilskins and the salt-stiff hair and the nine years of running — and whatever he saw there, of her father or her mother or simply of herself, made him nod slowly, like a man conceding a point in an argument he'd been having alone for decades.

"Draw up the papers," he said to Crane. "Everything she asked. And put in the report—" his voice caught, steadied— "put in all of it. Every name. Mine first."

* * *

They signed on the Kestrel's deck, using her chart table, with rain drumming the coach roof overhead like impatient fingers. Crane wrote in his beautiful, joyless hand. Rell signed with a flourish that deteriorated halfway through his own name into something cramped and human. Ansel signed last — *Ansel Iversen* — and the letters felt strange under her pen, like shoes newly broken in, like a language she was remembering rather than learning.

When it was done, Crane closed his case and cleared his throat. "For the record, Captain. The survey will list the Meridian as fleet salvage, recovered under adverse conditions, with full historical disclosure. Your cooperation will be noted." A pause. "It should also be noted that without your channel notes, no vessel in this fleet could reach her. The annotations are—" he hesitated over the word— "remarkable."

"They're my mother's," Ansel said. "Give her credit in the report. She earned it, one way or the other."

The launches cast off as the tide began to turn. At the rail of the lead boat, Rell paused, one hand on the shrouds, and looked back at her across the widening water. He didn't call her by the childhood nickname this time. He didn't call her anything. He touched two fingers to his brow — a salute, or an apology, or a farewell, and perhaps on the monsoon coast those had always been the same gesture — and then the rain took them, and the fleet weighed anchor one ship at a time, lights dimming, shapes dissolving, until the roads held nothing but gulls and the long grey swell.

Ansel stood at the rail until they were gone. Then she went below, and took the logbook from where Crane had returned it, dried and wrapped in fresh oilcloth, and set it on the chart table beside her father's chart with its two hands of annotation — the saboteur's and the defrauder's, side by side, guiding their daughter home.

Toma appeared in the doorway with two cups of coffee that smelled burnt and tasted like mercy.

"Well," he said. "We're rich in nothing but the truth."

"It's the only cargo," Ansel said, "that's never once sunk on me."

He snorted and drank his coffee. Outside, the tide was turning, the Hollow closing its mouth for the season, the sea folding the Meridian back into its keeping — and this time, Ansel thought, honestly kept, honestly lost, honestly named.

She looked at the chart, and her finger found the coastline without her willing it, sliding south along the delta, past the sandbars no chart recorded, to a small harbor with a lock gate and a churchyard and a grave with her mother's name cut deep enough to outlast the weather.

Greyhaven was two days' sail. The forecast held fair.

"Toma," she said. "Set a course for home."

He didn't ask which home. He just went forward to wake the engine, and the Kestrel came about, and the open sea — which had been her whole world, her alibi, her hiding place — rolled away behind her like a page finally turned.
