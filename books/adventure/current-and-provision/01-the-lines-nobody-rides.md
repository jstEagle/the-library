## 1. The Lines Nobody Rides

The tide was out so far that morning it looked like the sea had resigned.

I stood on the embankment above Saltmere Strand with my theodolite over one shoulder and a thermos of tea gone bitter in my coat pocket, and I watched the water pull back from the mudflats like a man retreating from an argument he knows he's losing. Gulls walked around in the exposed muck with the aggrieved air of commuters whose train has been cancelled. Somewhere under all that grey silt lay the old branch line—mile and a quarter of track drowned in 1947 when they rerouted the estuary and never told anyone who mattered.

Nobody rides these lines. That's rather the point of them.

My name, for the purposes of this account and every other purpose I can arrange, is Xanthe Thackeray. I am a surveyor of closed railways. It is not a job, because jobs come with employers, and employers come with questions, and questions are the one commodity I have spent fifteen years refusing to stock. What it actually is: I walk the dead branches of this peninsula with instruments and notebooks, and I draw maps that no one has asked for, of places no one goes, and I file them nowhere. There are eleven of them now. Eleven closed lines, mapped to the last fishplate, in a waterproof case that lives in the bottom of my kit bag like a confession I haven't gotten around to making.

The peninsula doesn't mind me. That's the arrangement. The peninsula gets rain the way other countries get weather—continuously, vindictively, eight months of the year—and in exchange for enduring it, it lets a thin man with a walking stick and a surveyor's eye pass through its villages unremarked. The harbor master at Pellow's Quay thinks I'm a consultant for the drainage board. The woman who runs the tavern at Ferrymouth thinks I'm a government man counting culverts. Both fictions were supplied by me, both are boring enough to be believed, and neither requires me to have a past. A man who counts culverts is not asked where he went to school. A drainage consultant does not get invited to weddings.

This is what I built instead of a life, and I want to be clear that I consider it a good bargain.

* * *

That morning's work was the Saltmere branch proper—the stretch above the strand, where the rails still showed their shoulders through the gorse like the spines of something buried alive. I set up the theodolite at the old milepost 14, which some previous version of me had already scrubbed clean of lichen and marked with a dab of red paint, because a map is only as honest as its reference points and I had long ago decided that if nothing else in my life would be accurate, my maps would be.

The ritual goes like this. Arrive before the light is fully decided. Set the instrument. Take the bearings. Walk the line at survey pace—not walking pace, survey pace, which is slower and more attentive, the difference between passing through a place and taking its statement. Record everything: rail weight, sleeper condition, ballast depth, the angle of every culvert mouth, the species of every weed exploiting the right-of-way. Then, at the end, stand somewhere with a view of the whole alignment and drink the terrible tea, and say nothing to anyone, because there is no one to say anything to.

Fifteen years of that ritual. You could do worse. People do do worse, constantly; I read about them in the newspapers that drift into the villages wrapped around fish.

The Saltmere branch was in better condition than the ledger suggested—which is to say, my own ledger suggested, since the only ledgers involved in my life these days are mine, and they contain nothing but chainages and rail weights. The 1947 abandonment had been sloppy even by the standards of a railway administration that was, by then, mostly a filing cabinet with delusions. They'd pulled the rails from the first two miles and left the rest. Sleepers black as licorice, but sound. A signal post at the strand crossing still standing, its arm frozen at caution, which struck me then—as it strikes me now, and I noted it in the margin—as either an oversight or the most honest piece of infrastructure on the peninsula.

Caution, forever, pointing at nothing.

I worked the curve down toward the cliff section through the middle of the day, pausing once when a heron stood in my sightline with the confidence of a landowner. We regarded each other. The heron won, as herons do, by the simple method of not caring about me at all, which is a strategy I have studied with professional interest.

Here is what nobody tells you about self-erasure: it is meticulous work. It takes years. You don't just leave; you have to sand yourself down, plane off the edges people might recognize, re-grain the wood so it reads as something ordinary. I did mine properly. New name, chosen from a headstone in a parish I'd never lived in—apologies to the actual Xanthe Thackeray, wherever he's buried, but he wasn't using it. New trade, learned honestly, because a fake engineer is caught out in a week and a real one is merely pitied. New habits, drilled in until they ran on their own: never sleep twice in the same lodging within a month, never let anyone learn your handwriting well enough to match it, never, ever talk about your father's work.

And the maps. The maps came later, though I tell myself they're the whole point. First there was just the running, and then the running needed a shape, and the shape turned out to be a man with a theodolite measuring things that are already dead. There's a logic to it that I've stopped examining. A closed line can't hurt anyone. Its history is finished, its dangers are rusted over, and its silences ask nothing of you except that you record them accurately, which is the one promise I have ever been able to keep without flinching.

At half past three I reached the cliff tunnel.

* * *

The tunnel is called the Kestrel bore on my maps, after the birds that nest in the ventilation shaft, though the railway had called it something more administrative—Tunnel 9, or possibly 11; the paperwork died with the company. It runs four hundred yards through the headland above Saltmere Strand, unlined rock for most of its length, and it has been closed since before my time with a brick bulkhead at each portal, mortared by men who intended it to stay shut.

I don't go in tunnels. That's not superstition; it's policy. Tunnels are the one feature of a closed line that resists survey—you can't chain them, you can't sight through them, and entering one means trusting the intentions of whoever sealed it, decades ago, in weather like today's. My maps show tunnel portals as symbols and give interior lengths from the old company records, which may be lies, but they are somebody else's lies, and I have found that borrowing other people's lies is the cheapest way to stay honest.

So I did what I always do at Tunnel 9-or-11. I measured the portal. Height, width, condition of the brickwork, seepage patterns, the lean of the coping stones, all of it into the notebook in my flat, unhurried hand. And then I sat on the sea wall below it, where the headland drops to the strand, and drank the tea, and performed the final act of the ritual, which is the looking.

You stand where you can see the whole line at once—the curve down from milepost 14, the strand crossing with its cautious signal, the climb to the portal—and you look at it until it stops being a collection of measurements and becomes what it actually is, which is a sentence someone started writing eighty years ago and abandoned mid-clause. And you think, briefly, about the men who laid it, and the trains that ran it, and the fact that none of them knew it would end, and then you pack the instrument away and walk back along the top of the cliff, and that is the entire emotional budget for the day. Spent. Closed. Filed.

It works. I want to report that it works. Fifteen years of evidence says it works.

Except.

There is a moment—I will admit this to this notebook and no one else, and even the notebook only gets it in pencil—that comes near the end of every survey, usually at the looking stage, usually with the tea. It lasts perhaps four seconds. It is the moment when the line is most beautiful, when the light comes low across the water and the dead rails catch it, and some unguarded part of me thinks: *someone should see this.* Not me. Me seeing it changes nothing; I am the surveyor, and the surveyor is furniture. Someone else. Someone to hand it to. Someone to say *look* to, and have them look.

Four seconds. Then I cap the thermos, and the thought goes back in whatever drawer it lives in, and I am content again. Content the way a sealed tunnel is content. Structurally sound. Nothing getting in.

I mention it here only because that evening, at the strand crossing, the moment lasted longer than four seconds. I counted. It lasted through the whole cup of tea, which is unprecedented, and I blamed the light, which was doing something unseasonably generous with the clouds, and I packed up and walked the six miles back to Pellow's Quay in the dark and did not think about it again, much.

* * *

Pellow's Quay in October is eleven buildings, two of them churches, arguing with the wind. I keep a room above the chandler's that I've held, on and off, for three years—the longest I've stayed anywhere since the erasure, which either means the chandler asks fewer questions than most or that I'm getting careless, and I prefer the first explanation.

The room contains a cot, a stove, a table, and the waterproof case. The case contains the eleven maps. I unlocked it that night—it locks, yes, I lock my own maps, make of that what you will—and laid the Saltmere sheet out under the lamp to ink in the day's figures, because pencil notes are a draft and drafts are unfinished and I cannot abide leaving a thing unfinished, including, apparently, myself.

Inking is the meditative hour. Rail weights in blue. Chainages in black. Water features in the green I mix myself because cartographic green is too cheerful for this coast. And at the margins, the small annotations that no official map would carry and that are, if I'm honest, the closest thing I have to a diary:

*Mile 13¾ — badger sett under formation. Leave alone.*

*Mile 14 — milepost repainted. Third time. It'll need it again by spring.*

*Tunnel 9 (or 11) — portal sound. Arm at caution. Someone should decide.*

I wrote that last one and then sat looking at it for a while. *Someone should decide.* As though the decision weren't mine to make, as though I were waiting on instructions from a railway that hasn't existed in seventy years. The lamp hissed. Rain started against the window, the opening argument of the season, and I got out the ruler and drew the tunnel symbol—a rectangle with hatched ends, standard notation, meaning *sealed, contents unknown*—and as I finished it, my pen paused.

Because the tide that morning had been the lowest I'd seen in fifteen years of watching this water. Lowest, and still falling when I left the strand. The fishermen at the quay had been shaking their heads about it—*never seen her drop like that, not in forty year*—and I had recorded it in the notebook as a datum, because I record everything, and datums are safe.

But a datum is only safe until it points somewhere.

An extreme tide, I thought, inking slowly, exposes things. That is practically its definition. And directly beneath the Kestrel bore, according to the old company sections I'd copied years ago from a records office that didn't know what it had, the headland was hollow. A fault cavern, flooded at every tide since the tunnel was driven, sitting under four hundred yards of sealed brick like a secret kept so long everyone forgot there was a secret.

The villagers at Ferrymouth tell a story about a cave under the cliff, when tourists buy enough rounds. A wrecked payroll train, a vault, men who drowned reaching for it. I'd heard it twice and filed it under folklore, where I file everything that can't be chained and sighted.

But folklore, I reminded myself, setting down the pen, is just history that nobody's surveyed yet.

I looked at the tunnel symbol on my beautiful, accurate, harmless map of a line nobody rides, and for the first time in fifteen years I felt the map looking back at me.

The rain thickened. I capped the ink, locked the case, and lay in the dark listening to the season arrive, telling myself the tide would come back up tomorrow and cover everything, the way tides do, the way they always had.

They didn't.
