## 3. A Stranger at the Signal Box

The rain came in off the water the way it always did in the shoulder season—sideways, with intent—and I was three days into a survey of the Kestrel Point branch when I saw her coming up the ballast road.

I heard her first, actually. Nobody walks a closed line quietly. The sleepers are rotten, the drainage ditches have become small rivers, and every culvert crossing is a negotiation. You learn to read footsteps on old track the way other men read weather: the flat slap of someone who doesn't know the ground, the careful half-step of someone who does. Hers were neither. They were deliberate, evenly spaced, the walk of a person who had decided the ground would behave and was prepared to be annoyed if it didn't.

I was sitting in the old signal box at Milepost 11, which is not so much a building anymore as a suggestion of one—a timber frame gone silver-grey, a roof that kept out maybe sixty percent of the sky, and the great iron lever frame still bolted to the floor like the skeleton of some animal that died mid-stride. Forty levers. I'd counted them my first season, back when counting things was how I kept myself company. The signal wires were long since cut, the semaphores fallen or sold for scrap, but the levers still threw with a satisfying clunk, and on bad nights I would work them in sequence, calling out imaginary trains to nobody, running a railway that existed only in my hands.

It's a strange thing, admitting that. Let's move past it quickly.

I watched her come up the line through the rain-haze, and I did what I always do when something unexpected appears on my territory: I went still and let it commit itself. She wore oilskins that fit her too well to be borrowed and too new to be sentimental, carried a canvas rucksack with the straps cinched down tight, and held a folding umbrella she wasn't bothering with because the wind had turned it inside out two miles back and she hadn't yet admitted defeat. Her hair was dark, cropped short and practical, plastered to her forehead, and she walked with her chin up into the weather as though the weather were an employee who was about to receive a talking-to.

She stopped twenty feet from the signal box. Looked up at me where I sat in the shadow of the frame, and I'll grant her this: she didn't pretend surprise at finding anyone there. Most people do. Most people perform astonishment—a whole engineer, living out here, how remarkable—as though the discovery of another human being in an abandoned building required ceremony.

She just said, "You're the one who keeps the maps."

Not a question. Which told me two things: she'd asked after me somewhere, and whoever she'd asked had answered honestly, which narrowed the field considerably. There are perhaps four people on this peninsula who know I keep maps, and only one of them talks.

"Depends who's asking," I said.

"Wren Odile." She climbed the rotted steps without waiting for permission, which I noted, and tested the floorboards with her boot before committing her weight, which I noted harder. Careless people don't check floors. "I'm an archivist. Transport history, mostly colonial-era records. I'm working on the wreck."

"The wreck," I said, in the tone of a man being offered one of several wrecks.

"Don't do that," she said pleasantly. "There's one wreck on this coast that matters. The *Corvane Star*, 1911. Payroll train, cliff tunnel, forty-one dead, vault never recovered. Everyone on this peninsula can recite it. It's practically a nursery rhyme here."

"It's a story they charge tourists for," I said. "Ghost train walks. Tuppence a head. The tavern has a mural."

"I've seen the mural. The artist gave the locomotive a moustache." She set her rucksack down on the lever frame—the sacrilege of it made my teeth itch—and began unbuckling straps with quick, economical movements. "I'm cataloguing what actually survives. Records, plans, physical evidence. Which brings me to you, Mr.—"

"Thackeray."

"Mr. Thackeray. Because three weeks ago you filed a survey request with the district office for the Kestrel Point branch, including the cliff tunnel section, and the clerk who processed it says your notes ran to eleven pages on tidal scour patterns at the tunnel's seaward end." She looked up at me then, and her eyes were grey-green and entirely unfooled. "Eleven pages. On scour. For a line that hasn't run since before the war."

"Thoroughness is a habit."

"So is lying, apparently, but you're not very good at it."

I want to be clear about what happened in my chest at that moment, because it wasn't anger. It was something worse—it was recognition. This was a person built the same way I was built: all the softness packed away behind load-bearing walls, everything arranged so that nothing showed, everything checked twice. People like us don't fool each other. We recognize each other, the way two forgers will spot each other's work across a crowded room.

"You've got rain in your boots," I said, "and a tide table in your pocket—I can see the corner of it—and you came up a dead branch line in a rising sea fog to find a man whose name you learned from a clerk. Archivists don't do fieldwork in oilskins that new. So why don't we skip to whatever the second half of your story is, and I'll decide whether to keep listening."

For a moment—just a moment—something moved behind her face. Not guilt. Calculation. The rapid silent arithmetic of a person deciding how much truth a situation could bear. I know that arithmetic. I invented several of its operations.

"The second half," she said finally, "is that the wreck matters to people. Descendants. There's a historical society, there are families who never got answers, and there's a gap in the record exactly the size of a payroll vault. If the low tides this month have exposed anything at the cliff base—anything at all—an archivist would want to see it. Document it. Before the winter takes it back."

"And if something had been exposed?"

"Then it would be a find of real significance." She said it lightly, but her hands had stopped moving on the buckles. "And the person who found it would have choices to make about who he tells."

*Who he tells.* Not *what he does*. She'd already decided I'd found something. Which meant either she was guessing, or she had information I didn't know she had, and both possibilities were equally unpleasant.

Here is what I should tell you, so you understand the shape of the lie I chose: two days earlier, at the bottom of a tide lower than any I'd recorded in nine years of recording them, I had stood on wet sand beneath the cliff tunnel and looked into a cave mouth framed by rusted rail iron—actual rail, laid horizontally into the rock like teeth—and above it, carved deep into the stone where the sea couldn't easily reach, a marker. A date. A number. And a word in the old colonial lettering style that I had seen exactly once before, in a place I had spent fifteen years making sure I would never see again.

I had stood there until the water began to return, and then I had walked away, and I had told myself that walking away was a decision and not a postponement. I am very good at telling myself things. It's most of what I do out here.

"There's a cave," I said. "Everyone knows about the cave. The fishermen call it the Coffin. It floods at half tide, it's full of rockfall, and last week it was no more open than it's ever been. If your historical society wants photographs of a hole in a cliff, I can sell you directions for the price of a hot meal."

The lie came out smooth. That's the trouble with lies you've rehearsed against yourself—they're already broken in, comfortable, like old boots. But Wren Odile tilted her head perhaps two degrees, and I understood that I had just done the equivalent of a forger signing his own name.

"The Coffin," she repeated. "Right. And the eleven pages on tidal scour?"

"Habit."

"Mm." She refastened one of the buckles she'd undone, slowly, watching me do nothing with great attention. "You know what I find interesting about you, Mr. Thackeray? Everything about this setup is designed to make people look away. The derelict box, the dead line, the man who lives alone and answers letters late. It's very well done. But you surveyed scour patterns at the exact section of coast where a payroll vault has been missing for a hundred and fourteen years, three weeks before a once-in-a-generation spring tide. Either you're the luckiest surveyor alive, or you knew what the tide would give back before it gave it." She shouldered her rucksack. "Archivists believe in documents. Documents say you knew."

"And what do you believe?"

"I believe you're going to go back there." She smiled, and it was a good smile, warm even, and completely unconnected to anything she was actually thinking. "People like you always do. You'll tell yourself you're checking the measurements. You'll bring the tide table. You'll go at slack water, because you're careful." She paused at the top of the steps. "When you do, I'd like to come along. Officially. For the record."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially," she said, "I'd like to know what you're hiding, and I'd like you to know that I know you're hiding it. Consider it professional courtesy between colleagues."

"We're not colleagues."

"No," she agreed. "Not yet."

She went down the steps and out into the rain, and I watched her take the ballast road south toward the village with that same metronome stride, umbrella furled now like a weapon she'd decided against. At the bend by the old cattle grid she stopped, turned, and raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. *I see you. I'll see you again.*

Then the fog took her, and I was alone with forty levers and the sound of the sea working at the cliff.

* * *

I did not sleep that night, which was not unusual, but the quality of the not-sleeping was new. Usually my insomnia is a flat thing, a held breath, the mind idling in neutral. This had gears grinding in it.

She'd known about the survey filing. That meant she'd pulled district records—which meant money, or authority, or both. Archivists with grants don't track down lone surveyors on foot; archivists with grants write letters and wait. Wren Odile had come herself, up a drowned line, in weather that would turn a casual visitor back in the first mile. Whatever she was, the word *archivist* was doing heavy lifting, and underneath it was something with better funding and worse manners.

And yet.

And yet she'd checked the floorboards. She'd waited for me to speak first. When I lied to her she hadn't pressed it into cruelty—she'd named it, set it on the table between us like a card played face-up, and let me sit with it. There are gentler ways to tell a man he's transparent, and there are crueler ones, and she'd chosen the way that left the door open. That's not how hunters behave. That's how people behave who have spent enough time hiding themselves to develop manners about it.

I got up in the dark and worked the levers for a while. Number seven, number twelve, nineteen. The clunk of the iron, the ghost-weight of signals that would never again drop for any train. Somewhere below, the sea was turning over the sand at the cliff base, rearranging its holdings, keeping or taking according to rules written before there were railways or liars or women named after birds.

The legend, as the village tells it, goes like this: in 1911 the colonial railway's payroll train was caught on the cliff line by a storm, the tunnel mouth collapsed, and the carriage carrying the wages went into the sea with forty-one men. The company sealed the strongroom where the pay chests were stored rather than let looters reach it, and the sea kept the rest. Every few years somebody drowns trying for it. The tavern mural shows the locomotive with a moustache and the vault glowing gold through the rock, which tells you everything about how much truth the village thinks the story deserves.

What the village doesn't tell—what nobody tells—is that the collapse was never fully explained. The inquiry blamed the storm. Storms make convenient scapegoats; they can't be cross-examined. But rail iron doesn't shear the way the report described unless something helped it, and I grew up knowing more than I wanted to know about how things get helped.

That thought arrived, as it always did, and I put it away, as I always did, in the room at the back of my mind where I keep the things that have my father's handwriting on them.

Morning came grey and hard. I made tea on the spirit stove and ate the last of the bread, and I took out the tide table I'd bought in the village under a name that isn't mine, and I looked at the figures for the coming week, and the figures looked back at me like a debt collector.

Spring tides. Lower than the one that had shown me the cave. And behind them, riding up the shipping forecasts like a name on a warrant, the first bands of the monsoon.

If I went back, I would be going toward the thing I'd built my whole life to avoid. If I didn't go, I'd spend the rest of my life listening for her footsteps on the ballast road.

I was still holding the tide table when I heard them—deliberate, evenly spaced, the walk of a person who has decided the ground will behave—coming up the line from the south. Two days early. Carrying two enamel mugs.

Wren Odile came up the rotted steps, checked the floorboards out of pure habit this time, and held one mug out to me like a warrant.

"Tea," she said. "And a proposition. But drink the tea first. You look like a man who's been arguing with himself all night, and I find those men say yes more often on a full stomach."

I took the mug. It was hot, and strong, and I noticed—because noticing is the only skill I've never managed to bury—that she'd brought sugar without being told.

Which meant she'd already learned things about me I hadn't given her.

The proposition, it turned out, was worse than the tea was good.
