## 4. The Legend Charges Admission

The tavern was called the Drowned Bell, which told you everything about the local relationship with optimism before you'd finished crossing the threshold.

I'd been in a hundred taverns like it—low beams, salt-swollen wood, a bar that had been polished by more elbows than any craftsman would admit to. But this one had something the others didn't: a bell. An actual bell, green with sea age, hanging above the hearth on a chain gone black with rust, and beneath it a brass plaque so worn you had to tilt your head and let the firelight rake across it sideways to read the words.

*Salvaged from the *Meridian Star*, lost with all hands, 14th of March, 1911.*

All hands except the ones that mattered to my employers, anyway.

I took a stool at the end of the bar, ordered whatever the woman pouring considered local, and did what I do best, which is be forgettable. Archivists are supposed to be forgettable. We're the furniture of scholarship. I had the canvas satchel, the sensible boots, the notebook with its soft cover going furry at the corners. I even had the pencil behind my ear, because nothing says *harmless academic* quite like a pencil behind the ear. The one thing I didn't have was an honest reason to be here, but I've found that's rarely a disqualifying quality in this line of work.

The room filled as the light died. Fishermen first, then the quarry men off the inland road, then the older ones who came early and stayed late and occupied their corner by the window like a garrison that had never been relieved. I nursed my drink and listened.

It took forty minutes for the legend to come out, and when it did, it wasn't the landlord who told it. It was an old man with hands like knotted rope—quarry hands, or maybe rope hands themselves—who'd clearly told this story so many times he no longer needed to think about it, which meant every detail was load-bearing and none of them were true, or all of them were, depending on how you weighted the evidence.

"Fourteenth of March," he said, tapping the table with a finger that had maybe two joints left in it. "Nineteen eleven. The coastal express, down from the capital with the quarterly payroll for the whole line—every stationmaster's wages, every navvy's pay packet, three months of it, because the directors wouldn't risk sending it monthly through the storm season. One train. One strongroom carriage. And the cliff tunnel at Graywater Point."

He paused there, the way a man pauses when he knows everyone in the room already knows the next part but wants them to lean in anyway. They leaned in. So did I, though I kept my eyes on my glass.

"The tunnel flooded," someone said, prompting him, because the ritual required it.

"The tunnel flooded." He nodded gravely. "Or so the railway said. Rockfall, they claimed. Blocked the bore, the sea came in through the old workings, and the engine went into the cut with the whole payroll aboard. Eleven men drowned in the dark, and the company paid out eleven widows' pensions and not one copper more."

"And the vault?" This from a young fisherman, playing his part.

"Ah." The old man held up his ruined finger. "The vault. Because here's what the company didn't say. The strongroom carriage didn't go into the cut. It went into the *cave*. There's a sea cave under Graywater Point—the old miners knew it, before the railway came, they called it the Money Hole, which tells you what kind of people we're descended from—and when the tunnel collapsed, the carriage went through the broken floor of the bore and into the cave below, and the sea closed over it. And the company sent divers. Sent them for six years. And every man who went down either came up empty or didn't come up at all. Four of them. Four divers, drowned in a hole full of money, and after the fourth the company wrote the whole thing off, sold the line, and let the sea keep its secret."

He sat back, satisfied, and the tavern made the noise taverns make—not applause exactly, but acknowledgment, the sound of a story being paid its due.

"That's rubbish," said a woman near the door, without heat. She'd said it before, clearly. Many times. "My grandmother said the divers were a story the company put about so nobody else would look."

"Your grandmother," the old man replied with dignity, "was a Thackeray, and Thackerays have opinions."

Something cold walked down my spine on small feet.

I kept my face still. I'd gotten good at that—you don't last long in salvage work if your face does your thinking for you—but inside, everything had gone very quiet and very fast, the way it does when a piece of information slides into a slot you didn't know was empty.

Thackeray. The surveyor on the closed branch. The man who'd lied to me about the tide pools with the flat calm of a professional liar, which is to say, of a professional. Xanthe Thackeray, who kept meticulous maps of lines nobody rode, whose notes I'd read over his shoulder at the signal box while pretending to admire his penmanship.

Because I *had* read them. That was the thing. That was the thing sitting in my chest now like a swallowed stone.

I opened my notebook under the bar, casually, the way scholars do, and turned to the pages I'd copied from his survey ledger during our conversation—copied openly, actually, because he'd watched me do it and done nothing, which should have told me something right there. A man who guards nothing has usually already buried it deeper than you can dig.

His coordinates for the derelict branch. His tide tables. His notation for the cliff section at Graywater Point—a section he'd surveyed with a granularity that made no sense for a dead line. Soundings. Substrate readings. A note about "anomalous void signatures below the bore," written in the dry shorthand of a man recording something extraordinary in language designed to make it boring.

And now the old man was saying the cave lay under the cliff tunnel at Graywater Point, and the numbers in my head were doing the arithmetic on their own, unbidden, and the answer came out matching. Not roughly. Exactly. To the decimal. Xanthe Thackeray hadn't stumbled onto the wreck site. He'd *surveyed* it. He knew precisely where the Money Hole was, had known, probably, for years, and had written it down in code so dry that only another mapmaker—or another liar—would ever see it for what it was.

I closed the notebook.

Here's what nobody tells you about guilt: it doesn't announce itself. It doesn't arrive wearing sackcloth. It arrives as arithmetic. You're sitting in a warm tavern listening to an old man talk about drowned men, and somewhere in the back of your skull a voice that sounds suspiciously like your own says: *you know where the bodies are, Wren. You're being paid to find out if the money's still there too.*

Corvane Salvage Syndicate. My commission, folded flat in my luggage along with the wreck report, the diver manifests, the insurance filings—all of it archival paper, all of it genuine, because Corvane liked its lies built on true foundations. Find the vault. Confirm the coordinates. Assess recovery feasibility. Standard language. Clean verbs. Nothing in the contract about eleven drowned railwaymen or four drowned divers or a village that charged admission to its grief in the form of beer and a good story.

I looked at the brass plaque above the hearth again. *Lost with all hands.* Except the hands hadn't been lost, had they? They'd been *written off*. There was a difference, and the difference had a market value, and I was the instrument currently being used to calculate it.

"You're the archivist," the old man said suddenly, and I realized he'd crossed the room and was standing at my elbow, looking at my notebook with the frank appraisal of a man who'd spent seventy years reading weather and water and could read a stranger just as easy. "Come about the wreck, have you?"

"About the records," I said. "Shipping registers, insurance claims. The paper trail."

"Mm." He pulled out the stool beside mine and sat, uninvited, with the authority of age. "Paper. The company burned most of its paper, you know. After the fourth diver. There was an inquiry—quiet one, not the public sort—and afterward the directors ordered the files sealed. My father hauled the crates to the incinerator himself. Said some of them were heavy. Ledgers, he thought." He looked at me. "You won't find the truth in paper, girl. The company made sure of that. The truth's under the water, where they left it, and the water keeps better faith than any clerk."

"I'm sure it does," I said.

"But you're not here for the truth." He said it mildly, almost kindly, the way you'd note that the sky looked like rain. "You're here for the same thing the others come for. The money. Only the others are honest about it—they buy a round, hear the story, go home. You—" he tapped the notebook once, gently, "—you take notes like a woman counting."

The tavern noise seemed to recede slightly, the way it does when your blood decides your ears need it more than your social graces do.

"And what would you say," I asked, "if I told you I was counting?"

He considered me for a long moment. Firelight moved in the wrinkles around his eyes.

"I'd say the tide gives back what it takes, sooner or later, and it doesn't care whose hands it puts things into." He stood, joints cracking like small-arms fire, and picked up his glass. "I'd also say that the last man who mapped that cave shore was named Thackeray, and he was the inspector's son, and he stopped coming to this village the year after the inquiry. Whatever you're counting, count slow. Some doors open easier than they close."

He shuffled back to his corner garrison, leaving me with my drink going warm and my heart going cold, and both of those facts felt like information.

*The inspector's son.*

I knew that. Of course I knew that—I'd read the personnel files, the inquiry transcripts, the whole sad architecture of the 1911 disaster. Inspector Edmund Thackeray, the officer who signed the tunnel maintenance certificates, the man whose negligence—or whose signature, at least—was written all over the collapse. Dead thirty years, disgraced quietly, survived by a son who'd vanished from every record I could find sometime in his twenties, like a pencil line rubbed out.

And now here was a man calling himself Xanthe Thackeray, surveying dead lines with a precision that bordered on devotion, keeping a private atlas of every route the railway had abandoned—including, at its center, the one route the railway had every reason to want forgotten.

Either he was the son, come back under his own name because no one remembered it anymore. Or he was something else entirely, and the name was a mask worn so long it had grown into the face.

Either way, Corvane had sent me to him. Either way, the contract in my luggage had his name on it—not as a target, exactly. As a *resource*. Locate the surveyor. Leverage his knowledge. The verbs in that document were very clean.

I finished my drink. Outside, the harbor lamps were coming on one by one, and beyond them the sea lay flat and pewter-colored under a sky going the color of a bruise, and somewhere out past the point, under forty feet of cold green water, a strongroom door was waiting with my employers' name on the other side of it.

The barwoman came to collect my glass. "Staying long?" she asked, in the tone of a woman asking whether she should remember my face.

"A few days," I said. "There's a storm coming, they tell me. I'll want shelter."

"There's always a storm coming, this time of year." She glanced toward the window, toward the darkening sea, and her voice dropped half a register. "But the harbor master's been on the barometer since noon, and he doesn't like what it's saying. If you're staying, stay *in*. When the monsoon makes up its mind here, it doesn't knock."

She moved off down the bar, and I sat alone with my notebook and the fire and the green bell overhead, and I did the thing I'd been avoiding since the old man walked away, which was open the contract and look at the clean verbs again.

*Locate. Leverage. Assess.*

Eleven men in a flooded tunnel. Four divers in a money hole. A village that told the story every night like tending a grave. And a surveyor with buried eyes who'd looked at me across a signal-box table and lied beautifully, and whom I was going to have to lie to better, because one of us had to hold the line, and I was being paid to be the one who didn't.

I told myself it was just work. I told myself the tightness under my ribs was the storm coming, pressure dropping, nothing personal.

The barometer behind the bar fell a tenth of an inch while I watched, and outside, far out past the point, the first cloud bank stood up off the horizon like something rising to take a breath.

* * *

I found Xanthe the next morning at the signal box, because of course I did; a man who maps everything is easy to find, you just follow the maps.

He was on the gallery platform with his theodolite, shooting angles across the harbor mouth, and he didn't turn when I came up the stairs—he'd heard me, obviously, catalogued my weight and my gait and filed them somewhere in that meticulous head. Only when I reached the rail beside him did he glance over, and his eyes did the thing they always did, the quick flicker of assessment, the door closing politely behind the welcome.

"Storm coming," I said, nodding at the sky, which had gone the yellow-gray of old newspaper.

"So the barometer says."

"The harbor master's hauling boats."

"He'd be a fool not to." He bent back to his instrument, and I watched his hands—steady, precise, hands that had never once in their life touched anything carelessly. "You'll want to get inland before it lands. The coast road floods at Kiln Bridge. After that, you're on the peninsula until the water drops."

"I'm not leaving yet."

He straightened then, slowly, and looked at me fully, and I gave him the look I give people when I want them to think I'm showing them everything. He gave me back the look of a man who recognizes the trick because he invented it.

"No?" he said.

"There's a window before the storm. Two days, maybe three, of calm before the leading edge. The low tide cycle peaks day after tomorrow—the lowest of the season, the harbor master confirmed it." I kept my voice level, scholarly, reasonable. "If there were anything worth seeing on the exposed foreshore, that would be the time to see it."

"Would it."

"Your survey notes," I said. "The void signatures below the bore at Graywater Point. I've been thinking about them. About what kind of anomaly produces a reading like that."

For a moment—half a second, maybe less—something moved behind his eyes. Not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a lock begin to turn from the inside.

"Anomalies," he said, "are usually just errors."

"Usually."

"And even when they're not, chasing them gets people wet." He turned back to his theodolite, adjusting a screw that didn't need adjusting. "The storm will make the whole question academic. Whatever the tide exposes tomorrow will be underwater again by the weekend, and probably the cliff face with it. Some things are better left where the sea put them."

"Some things," I agreed. "But you haven't answered my question."

"I didn't know I was being asked one."

"You're always being asked one, Mr. Thackeray. Everyone is. Most people just aren't listening for it." I took out my notebook, and I watched him watch it, and I saw him decide—deliberately, visibly—to let me believe he believed my cover. "The wreck records list the *Meridian Star*'s cargo manifest. Payroll bullion and specie, insured, never recovered. The claim was paid by the insurers, which means legally the money belongs to whoever holds the underwriters' interest now. Which means if it's still down there, it isn't treasure. It's *assets*. Recoverable assets."

"Is that what you're here for?" he asked quietly. "Assets?"

"I'm here for the records," I said, and heard the lie come out smooth and clean, and hated it a little less than I should have, and hated myself a little more for hating it less.

He studied me for a long moment. The wind was picking up off the harbor, carrying the smell of the storm still hours away, and his coat snapped around him like something alive.

"The tide peaks day after tomorrow," he said at last, noncommittally, turning back to his instrument. "If I were a man given to chasing anomalies—which I'm not—I'd want to know the exact hour of slack water. And I'd want a boat that didn't leak. And I'd want to know why an archivist cares."

"Maybe I'm writing a book."

"Archivists don't write books. They write finding aids." He sighted through the theodolite, and his voice went mild as milk. "Enjoy the storm, Miss Odile. Ferrymouth's chapel has a fine archive. Very safe. Very dry. Full of things nobody's looked at in fifty years."

It was a dismissal, delivered with perfect courtesy, and underneath it, unmistakable as bedrock under topsoil, was a message: *I know what you're doing. Show me yours first.*

I went down the stairs with my notebook in my pocket and the contract's clean verbs in my head, and behind me I could feel him watching, measuring, deciding—and ahead of me the sea lay waiting, flat and patient, holding its door shut for two more days.

Two days. Then the tide would pull back farther than it had in a generation, and the Money Hole would stand open in the cliff, and one of us was going to walk into it.

The only question—the one tightening around my ribs like a band, the one I refused to look at directly, the one I filed under *storm pressure, nothing personal*—was whether I'd be walking in beside him as a colleague, or whether I'd already sold him before we ever got our boots wet.

Out past the point, the cloud bank had begun, very slowly, to move.
