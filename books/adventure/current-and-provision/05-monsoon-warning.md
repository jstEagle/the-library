## 5. Monsoon Warning

The barometer in the signal box had been my grandfather's, or possibly a dead stationmaster's—provenance was one of the things I'd stopped keeping track of when I stopped keeping track of myself. It hung on the wall by the door, brass-cased and yellow-dialled, and for eleven years it had done nothing more dramatic than drift between *Fair* and *Change*, like an old man commenting on the weather from a chair he never left.

On the morning after I found the cave, it read 981 millibars and falling.

I tapped the glass. It didn't change its mind. Barometers are honest that way; they have no reputation to protect.

"Damn," I said, to the room, because there was no one else to say it to, and because some sentences need to be said out loud before they become real. The monsoon wasn't due for another nine days by the almanac. The almanac had been wrong before. The sea outside the window was already the color of wet slate, and the gulls had gone inland, which is the oldest forecast on the coast and the only one that's never lied to me.

I did what I always do when the world starts moving too fast: I made tea, and then I made lists.

* * *

The harbor master's name is Dree Hallow—no relation to anyone worth mentioning—and she runs the harbor at Saltcote with the authority of a woman who has personally hauled every boat in it out of the water at least twice. By eight o'clock she was standing on the slipway with a megaphone she didn't need, because her voice carries on its own keel.

"Out! All of you! If your boat's still floating by noon, I'm charging you rent on the water!"

I watched from the seawall while the fishermen worked their winches, dragging hulls up the ramp two at a time, the whole village performing a choreography it had rehearsed for two hundred years. There's something almost beautiful about it, if you can look past the panic: the way a place prepares to be cut off from the world. Crates of rice and lamp oil going up to the high stores. The chapel roof being lashed down. Children sent inland to grandmothers with the solemnity of evacuees in a war film.

"You," Dree said, materializing beside me. She does that. "Your boat's still on the mooring."

"I know."

"The tide last night. Lowest anyone's recorded. Old Pell swears he saw the seabed off the point wearing rocks nobody's seen since his grandfather drowned." She squinted at me. "You were out there."

"I was surveying."

"At midnight. At a minus tide." She let that sit between us like a fish she hadn't decided whether to gut. "Whatever you found down there, engineer, the sea's about to take it back. That's how it works here. The water gives, and then the water collects."

"I'm aware of the hydrology, Dree."

"Aware." She snorted. "You're the most aware man on this coast and it's done you no good at all." Then she went back to shouting at boats, having delivered whatever it was she'd come to deliver.

The thing about living alone among people who've known you for years without knowing anything about you is that they develop a habit of saying true things sideways. Dree had just told me, in her way, that she knew I'd found something, and that she expected me to be stupid about it, and that she'd already forgiven me in advance. It was, I reflected, the closest thing to affection I'd received in months.

* * *

Wren Odile was waiting at the signal box when I got back.

This should not have surprised me. I'd left her at the tavern the previous evening with a promise of nothing, and I'd spent the night telling myself she'd lose interest, that archivists were creatures of libraries and would balk at actual weather. But she was sitting on my steps under an umbrella she'd clearly bought that morning—too new, too clean, village-shop canvas with a price sticker still on the ferrule—and she had the expression of someone who had slept well on bad intentions.

"Barometer's at 978," she said, before I'd even unlocked the door. "The chandler showed me. He seemed excited about it."

"He gets excited about weather the way other men get excited about football."

"And the flood cuts the peninsula road at the causeway, yes? I asked around. Six days, maybe seven, until the low route goes under, and then this whole coast is unreachable except by boat, and the boats all come out of the water." She tilted her head. "Which means if anyone wants to visit a certain cave, they have perhaps five days to do it. Before the spring tides bury it again until next year. Or forever, depending on what the storm does to the cliff."

I put the key in the lock with great deliberation. "I don't know what you're talking about. I never said anything about a cave."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You described, in remarkable detail, the tidal coefficients of a cove you claimed you'd never surveyed, and then you misquoted a legend that every child in this village can recite correctly. You lie like a man reading from notes." She stood, folding the absurd umbrella. "It's actually rather impressive. Most people improvise."

Inside, I filled the kettle because my hands wanted employment. Wren took off her coat uninvited and looked at my maps.

That was the moment I understood how badly I'd miscalculated. People see the maps and think they're looking at a hobby—the obsessive cartography of a man with too much time. What they miss is that the maps are a confession. Every closed line I'd walked and measured and drawn was a year of my life laid flat on paper, and Wren Odile stood over them reading me the way I read track gauges: quickly, professionally, missing nothing.

"You've surveyed the whole branch," she said. "Culvert by culvert. Mileage to the tenth of a chain. Nobody surveys a dead line like this unless—" She stopped. I watched her decide not to finish the sentence, which was its own kind of courtesy, or its own kind of trap.

"Unless they have nothing better to do," I offered.

"Unless the line matters to them personally." She turned. "Thackeray. I'll be direct, since subtlety appears wasted on you. I want to go into that cave. I have money—not village-tavern money, real money—and equipment, and five days, and I am offering you a partnership. You know the water, the rock, the tides, and apparently the entire drowned railway system of this peninsula. I know what's supposed to be inside. Together we could reach it before the monsoon closes the window."

"And if I say no?"

She smiled. It was a good smile, deployed with the precision of a tool. "Then I find another boat. This is a fishing village, Thackeray. Boats are not rare. Competence is."

* * *

Here is what I should have said: no. Flat, immediate, final. Here is what I actually felt, which is different from what I said:

I sat with my tea going cold and looked at the barometer—976 now, the needle moving with the slow certainty of something that has never once been argued out of its opinion—and I thought about the cave. About the rail iron framing its mouth like a wound that had healed around the metal. About the carved marker, and the legend, and the fact that my map—the map I'd made to keep the past at arm's length, chain by chain, culvert by culvert—had walked me straight into the middle of the one story on this coast I had spent fifteen years refusing to hear.

My father drove trains on this line. That much the village knew, or the old ones did, though they'd stopped saying it around me years ago, politely, the way you stop mentioning a limp. The wreck of the *Provisioner* was in every tourist pamphlet in the county: the payroll train, the storm, the carriage that went into the sea with the month's wages for three hundred men. A story with a hole in it, the size of a man's name.

And I had found the door to that hole at the bottom of the sea, alone, at midnight, and my first instinct had been to lie about it to the first stranger who asked.

You can tell yourself for a long time that avoidance is peace. Eleven years is a long time. But standing in my own signal box, listening to a woman I didn't trust describe my own secret back to me with better grammar than I'd have used, I had to admit that the arm's-length life had a flaw in its design: it kept everything out, including the parts of me that wanted, badly, stupidly, to know.

"I'm not interested in treasure," I said.

"Neither am I."

"That's a lie. Everyone's interested in treasure."

"I'm interested in records," she said. "Paper. The vault was a railway strongroom, Thackeray. Payroll vaults don't hold just coin. They hold manifests, ledgers, correspondence. Who signed for what. Who ordered what carried. Why a payroll train was running in a named storm when every other train on the division was tied down." Her voice had changed—quieter, faster, the professional calm showing seams. "There are questions about that wreck that have never been answered, and the answers are sitting in an iron room under a cliff, and in six days the sea is going to shut the book for another year. Maybe forever."

She believed what she was saying. That was the unsettling part. Liars, I know from long practice, perform conviction; Wren Odile simply had it, leaking out of her like heat from a boiler. Whatever she was, whoever was paying for her clean boots and her new umbrella, she wanted that room open for reasons that mattered to her personally.

Which meant she was dangerous in exactly the way I was dangerous. Which meant I recognized her.

"The answer's no," I said.

Her face didn't move. "All right."

"It's a bad season, a bad boat, and a bad idea, and I don't work with strangers."

"All right," she said again, and picked up her coat, and I understood that I had just watched a woman accept a first offer. She paused at the door. "For what it's worth—I checked the tide tables. Slack water at the cave, tomorrow morning, 0640. After that, the springs build and the entrance won't be diveable even for fools. So whatever you decide not to do, you should decide it soon."

Then she was gone, umbrella and all, and the rain arrived as if it had been waiting for her cue.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I told myself it was the barometer—low pressure does something to the skull, makes the whole world feel pressurized, urgent—but that was a lie too, and I was collecting them at a remarkable rate that day.

At two in the morning I got out the charts and did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do. Storm surge on top of spring tides: the cave mouth would be under four meters of water, minimum, probably more with the swell wrapping the point. The cliff itself was soft marl below the limestone cap; I'd noted it years ago in my survey, *erosion active, do not trust the overhang*, in my own tidy hand, written by a man who trusted nothing except measurements. One good monsoon and the whole face could let go. The cave, the marker, whatever the strongroom held—all of it could be rubble and silt by Sunday.

The water gives, Dree had said, and then the water collects.

I thought about the men who'd drowned trying to reach the vault. That was part of the legend too, the part the tourists liked least: the salvage attempts, the divers, the brothers Voss who went in with lamps in 1911 and came out as names on a chapel plaque. The sea had been guarding that door for eighty years and had killed everyone who knocked. And here I was, doing the same arithmetic they must have done, telling myself the same comforting lies about slack water and favorable conditions, because the alternative was admitting that curiosity isn't a feeling at all. It's a load. And once it's on the truck, it doesn't come off. It only gets delivered.

At four I went out and stood in the rain and looked at the boat.

She's called the *Marguerite*, which I didn't name her, and she leaks, which I mostly manage. Twenty feet of clinker-built stubbornness with a salvaged engine I'd rebuilt from three dead engines, and rail fittings where proper marine hardware ought to be, because that's what I had and what I am. In fair weather she handles like a wheelbarrow. In a rising sea she handles like a grudge.

"She's not up to it," I told her, and she dripped in agreement.

But the thing about a boat, the thing I love about boats and locomotives and every machine ever built by people who respected physics: they don't care about your history. The *Marguerite* didn't know whose son I was. The cave didn't either. Only I knew, and I was so tired of being the only one who knew.

I went back inside and wrote two lists, because that's the ritual, that's the liturgy. On the first list: reasons to refuse. Bad weather, bad boat, bad stranger, worse past, the dead of 1911, my own name, which I had buried so deep even I sometimes couldn't find it. The list filled the page. It was, honestly, a masterpiece of self-preservation.

On the second list: tide times. Slack water, 0640. Fuel, fresh water, lamp oil, rope, the good lanterns, the pump. The location of the old tunnel plans—chapel archive, Ferrymouth, if they still existed. And at the bottom, in smaller letters, as if writing it small could make it less true:

*One question. Make her answer one question, honestly, before the end.*

Because if I was going to stop being invisible—if the sea was going to drag me into the light whether I liked it or not—then I was not going to be the only one exposed. Wren Odile wanted a partnership. Fine. Partners know each other's names. Real ones.

The barometer read 969. Outside, the first real bands of wind were testing the roof, running their hands over the tiles like burglars checking the locks. Somewhere down the coast the causeway would be measuring its last dry hours. The clock wasn't ticking anymore; it was tolling.

I put both lists in my coat pocket, banked the stove, and lay down without sleeping.

At dawn, Wren Odile was on my steps again. No umbrella this time—rain hammering straight down, her hood up, her eyes bright with the particular madness of people who have gotten what they wanted and are pretending to be patient.

"I suppose," I said through the door, "you'll want to provision today."

She smiled like sunrise through storm cloud. "I brought a list."
