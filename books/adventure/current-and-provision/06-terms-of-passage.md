## 6. Terms of Passage

I told her no twice more that night, which by my own accounting meant I was already losing.

The third refusal came out of me around midnight, delivered to the ceiling of the signal box while Wren Odile sat on the opposite bench with her boots off and her notebook open, writing in a hand so small it looked like she was rationing ink against some future scarcity. She didn't look up when I said it. She just made a small mark in the margin and said, "That's three. I'm keeping score."

"Score of what?"

"Of how many times you say no before you say yes. Most men I've worked with crack at two." She turned a page. "You're built better than you look."

"I look fine."

"You look like a man who irons his shirts in a building with no electricity," she said, "which is its own kind of confession."

I should have put her out then. The signal box had one door and I had the key to it, and the monsoon was still two days out on the water, and there was nothing in the world obligating me to share my roof, my charts, or my silence with a stranger who asked questions like she was taking inventory. But she'd brought tea, actual tea, hoarded from somewhere, and she'd made it without asking where anything was, which meant she'd been watching me closely enough to know where I kept things, and I found — sitting there in the dark with the barometer falling and the lamp hissing — that being watched wasn't entirely unpleasant.

That was the loneliness talking. I knew that even then. Fifteen years of keeping my own company had taught me to recognize its voice the way a harbor master recognizes fog: not by sight but by what it makes you agree to.

* * *

In the morning I walked down to the boatyard with the tide coming in over the mudflats and found Wren already there, standing at the edge of the slipway in oilskins two sizes too big for her, negotiating with old Maren Voss about the rental of my own boat.

"It's not your boat," Maren was saying. "It's his boat. He keeps it in my yard because he doesn't trust himself to keep it anywhere else, and because I charge him nothing and he can't argue with free."

"He could argue with free," Wren said. "He argues with everything."

"Only with things that matter," Maren said, and caught my eye over Wren's shoulder with an expression I chose to interpret as neutral.

The boat in question was the *Kestrel* — thirty feet of clinker-built stubbornness that had started life as a pilot cutter and ended life as whatever I needed her to be. She leaked. Not dangerously; she leaked the way old houses breathe, a slow seep through a seam near the sternpost that I bailed every second week and thought of as rent. Her engine was a marine conversion of something originally designed to drive a generator in a signal relay shed, which meant it ran on neglect and prayer and had never once failed me, possibly because it shared my philosophy of wanting very little.

"She's seaworthy for the harbor run," I told Wren. "She is not seaworthy for what you're proposing."

"What am I proposing?"

"You want to go to the cave. Before the storm closes the window. You've wanted to since the moment I mentioned it, and you've spent two days pretending you hadn't, which is the same trick I use, and I resent seeing it done well."

Wren smiled. It changed her whole face, the way a struck match changes a room — suddenly there was light where you'd assumed only furniture. "So take me."

"No."

"That's four."

"Wren." I looked at the boat, at the flat grey water beyond the mole, at the sky doing its slow bruising work in the southeast. "Do you know what happens to this coast when the monsoon makes landfall? The swell comes in from open water with nothing between here and the deep sea to break it. The cave mouth sits below the cliff tunnel at maybe four meters above low tide. In a storm surge, that mouth is underwater and the water inside it is moving like a piston. People have died in that cave in *good weather*. There's a legend about it. You heard the legend. The legend is mostly true."

"The legend says the vault's still there."

"The legend says a lot of things. Legends are written by survivors, and survivors are liars with good memories."

Maren snorted. "He talks like this all day," she told Wren. "Fifteen years. You get used to it or you leave."

Wren didn't leave. She came around the slipway rail until she was standing close enough that I could see the salt drying white along the seams of her borrowed oilskins, and she said, quietly, without performance: "Xanthe. I know you don't owe me anything. I know you found that cave and it's yours until you decide otherwise. But I also know that after the storm, the tide won't do what it did again for years — maybe decades. And I know that whatever Corvane wants in there, they'll send someone worse than me if I come back empty."

There it was. First time she'd said the name aloud to me. I'd heard it from her once before, half-swallowed, in the tavern doorway on the night we met — a syllable or two of corporate Latin dropped among the wreck-records talk — and I'd filed it away the way I file everything: carefully, and against future need.

"Corvane Salvage," I said. "Say the rest of it."

Her jaw tightened. Whatever speech she'd prepared, I'd knocked it sideways. "They're a recovery firm. They locate assets lost in maritime incidents. They were engaged by parties interested in the historical record of the Meridian Coast Railway."

"Engaged by parties," I repeated. "You talk like a contract."

"I *am* a contract," she said, and for a moment neither of us smiled, because we both understood exactly what kind of honesty that was — the kind that tells you everything except the thing itself. "But I'm the version of this that asks permission. That's worth something. It's worth more than you think."

I looked at the boat again. The *Kestrel* sat on her trailer with her paint gone chalky and her name faded to a ghost, and I swear she looked back at me with the particular patience of a vessel that has been idle too long. Fifteen years I'd kept her for runs I never took, provisioned for voyages I never made, and told myself it was prudence. It was not prudence. It was readiness, and readiness is just hope wearing work clothes.

"One question first," I said.

"Ask."

"Not now. At the end. When we're back inside the mole, whatever happens out there — you answer one question of mine, honestly, completely, no contract language. That's the price of passage. Along with the other terms."

"Name them."

"You touch nothing in that cave without my say-so. Nothing. You photograph what you like, you look as long as you like, but your hands stay in your pockets unless I tell you otherwise. Second: my navigation stands. You don't question a course decision underway, you don't grab the tiller, you don't 'help.' Third—" I hesitated, because the third term was the one that cost me, and she watched me hesitate with those archivist's eyes that missed nothing and recorded everything. "Third. If I say turn back, we turn back. No argument, no renegotiation. The sea gets a vote and I speak for it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you can hire a fisherman who doesn't know the waters, and I'll read about the result in the casualty lists."

Wren held out her hand. Her grip was dry and strong and calloused in places an archivist's hands shouldn't be calloused, and I noted that too, added it to the file I was keeping on her, which was getting thick.

"Agreed," she said. "All terms. And I get one too."

"I'm setting the terms—"

"You set terms for the voyage. This one's for the partnership." She let go of my hand. "When you ask your question at the end, you have to actually want the answer. Not the answer you've already decided on. If you ask knowing you'll disbelieve whatever I say, the deal's void and you can throw me overboard."

"That's not a term, that's a threat."

"It's both. I find the best terms usually are."

* * *

We provisioned through the afternoon, and provisioning with Wren Odile turned out to be an education in how other people survive.

My method was arithmetic: calories per person per day, water at four liters, fuel at consumption plus fifty percent, everything weighed and logged in a ledger I'd kept since the boat was new. Wren's method was to walk into Voss's chandlery, survey the shelves for eleven seconds, and buy things I would never have selected — tinned peaches ("morale has a caloric value"), a coil of tarred marline twice what any sane repair required ("rope you don't need weighs nothing; rope you need and lack weighs everything"), a block of paraffin wax, a bundle of candles, and a canvas satchel of tools that she laid out on the floor of the boat later that evening with the reverence of a surgeon presenting instruments.

"Where did you learn to splice?" I asked, watching her work a soft eye into the end of a mooring line with fingers that moved like they'd done it ten thousand times.

"Archives have practical sections," she said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's a true statement that isn't an answer. You use them yourself. I've watched."

Fair. I bent back over the engine.

The *Kestrel*'s leak was the real work. That seam aft had been weeping since before I owned her, managed rather than mended, and if we were going outside the harbor into monsoon weather, managed wouldn't do. The proper fix was re-caulking, which meant hauling her out for two days we didn't have. The improper fix was mine, and it was the kind of solution that only occurs to someone who has spent fifteen years living among dead railways: I went to the rusting heap behind Maren's yard where she stored scrap rail fittings, and I came back with a length of worn checkrail, a handful of spring washers the size of saucers, and a strip of rubber torn from an old point-machine gasket.

"You're going to fix a boat with train parts," Wren said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm going to *patch* a boat with train parts. Fixing is for people with time." I showed her the logic while we worked — the checkrail ground and drilled to bridge the working seam, the rubber compressed under it, the washers spreading the load across planking that had no business being trusted with anything. "Rail iron is overbuilt for its job. Everything on a railway is engineered to carry forty tons and then carries five for eighty years. A boat seam is nothing. It's a rounding error."

"You miss it," she said. "The railways. The live ones."

The wrench in my hand stopped moving for exactly one second. I made sure it was only one.

"I keep busy," I said.

"Mm," said Wren, and drove a screw home.

We worked until dark, and then past dark by lamplight slung from the boom, and somewhere in the middle of it — I couldn't tell you precisely when, which bothered me afterward like a word on the tip of the tongue — the war between us stopped being a war. It happened in stages. It happened when she held the checkrail flush without being asked, reading my hands instead of my instructions. It happened when I caught myself explaining the tide tables to her unbidden, walking her through why slack water at the cave would fall at 0440 and last perhaps twenty minutes, and realized I was doing it not because she'd asked but because I wanted her to understand, which was a different animal altogether. It happened most decisively, though, when the rubber gasket tore.

I'd cut it wrong — misjudged the stretch, and the strip parted across the middle with a sound like a small insult. I sat back on my heels in the bilge, lamplight swinging, and said a word I hadn't said aloud in years, a word from a vocabulary I'd deliberately retired along with my name.

Wren laughed.

Not politely. Not the careful social laugh she'd deployed in the tavern. This was sudden and unguarded and slightly appalled at itself, the laugh of someone ambushed by their own amusement, and it bounced around the hull of the little boat like loose cargo. "Your face," she managed. "You looked at that rubber like it had betrayed the family."

"It *has* betrayed the family. That gasket was the last piece of a point machine installed in 1911. It survived two wars and forty years of salt fog and it couldn't survive one evening of your expedition."

"My expedition? An hour ago it was your boat and my money."

"It's everyone's failure now. That's what partnership means."

And then — God help me — I laughed too. It came up out of me rough and rusty, from a room in my chest I'd boarded over, and for a few seconds the two of us sat in the bilge of a leaking boat laughing at a strip of dead rubber, while the barometer fell and the first far-off thunder muttered along the edge of the horizon like a landlord clearing his throat.

I want to be precise about what that moment was, because precision is the only honesty I've ever been reliably good at. It was not trust. Trust takes longer than an evening and costs more than laughter. It was a ceasefire. Two people fluent in concealment, recognizing each other across the workbench, agreeing — without saying it — to suspend hostilities until the voyage was done. I knew she was lying to me about something large. I was confident she knew I was lying to her about something larger. We had each packed our lies carefully in the provisions, alongside the peaches and the spare rope, and we would carry them out to the cave together, and at journey's end, per the terms, one of us would be asked to set theirs down.

I intended to make it hers. I had the question ready already; I'd had it since the slipway. *Who gave you my name?* Because that was the splinter under all of it, the thing I'd been turning over in the dark hours since she arrived: not that a syndicate wanted the vault, but that they had sent someone to a forgotten branch-line engineer whose existence was, by every measure I controlled, invisible. Somebody, somewhere, had known where to find me. And there was only one category of person who could have known that, and he had been dead for fifteen years, and dead men do not write letters.

Unless, of course, they do. Unless they wrote them long ago, and left them sealed in stone, waiting for a tide low enough to give them back.

* * *

At midnight we finished. The patch sat over the seam like a medal awarded for dubious service, ugly and solid, and when I pumped the bilge dry and watched it over an hour, she took barely a cupful. The *Kestrel* was ready, or as ready as she'd ever be, which is the most any vessel and any man can honestly claim.

Wren stood on the quay looking southeast, where the lightning was now visible — silent, patient flashes inside the cloud, like someone signaling from a long way off in a language neither of us spoke.

"How long?" she asked.

"Landfall in thirty-six hours, give or take. The eye passes over or near us. Which means a window." I checked my watch against the harbor clock, an old habit, though the harbor clock had been wrong for a decade and I knew by exactly how much. "Storm's leading bands arrive tomorrow night. Behind the eye, there'll be a lull — six hours, maybe eight, if the track holds. Slack water in the cave during the lull falls at 0440. We go out with the calm, we're inside the rock when the back side of the storm comes ashore, and we come home through the worst weather of the year."

"That's insane."

"Yes."

"We're going anyway."

"You agreed to my terms. Term three applies to both of us." I threw her the mooring line and she caught it against her chest, hugging it like something alive. "Sleep aboard tonight. We cast off at 0300. And Wren—"

She paused, halfway up the gunwale, silhouetted against the lightning.

"When we're out there," I said, "don't lie to the sea. It's the only party to this arrangement that always knows."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "Is that a term?"

"It's advice. Advice is free. It's the only thing on this boat that is."

She laughed again — smaller this time, but real — and swung down into the cockpit, and I stood on the quay a while longer watching the storm build itself on the horizon, brick by black brick, and tried to remember the last time I had felt afraid and eager in the same breath.

I couldn't. That was the trouble. Fifteen years, and I couldn't remember it at all.
