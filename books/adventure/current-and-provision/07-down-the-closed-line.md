## 7. Down the Closed Line

We left the harbor at four in the morning, which is the hour the sea keeps its opinions to itself.

I had the boat loaded the night before: lamp oil, two storm lanterns, a coil of rope, chalk, my survey satchel, bread and cheese wrapped in oilcloth, and a hand pump that had been old when I was young. The boat itself was a sixteen-foot skiff with a two-stroke engine of dubious loyalty, patched along her port side with a plate of boiler iron I'd cut from a scrapyard behind the old goods shed. I'd bolted it through with fishplates—the steel bars railroads use to join rail ends—and sealed the seams with tar and prayer. She was, by any honest assessment, a floating argument. But she floated, and on this peninsula that put her ahead of most things.

Wren Odile arrived before dawn, as instructed, wearing oilskins too new to have ever seen weather and carrying a canvas bag she set down with the care of someone transporting either instruments or explosives. I decided not to ask which.

"Rules," I said, holding up one finger. "You touch nothing without me. You go where I say. And when I say we leave, we leave—no discussion, no negotiation, no *one more minute*."

"You mentioned the terms already."

"I'm mentioning them again. Repetition is how sailors stay alive."

"And my end of the bargain?"

"One question, answered honestly, at journey's end. Any question I like."

She considered that. In the gray light her face was unreadable, which I noted professionally, the way you note a signal showing green when you know the block ahead is occupied. "Agreed," she said.

I handed her a bailer. "Then earn your passage."

* * *

The drowned line runs west out of the harbor for eleven miles, or it did once, before they closed the branch and let the sea come back for what it was owed. The track bed sits below high water mark for most of that distance—a causeway of stone and slag built by men who believed, with the magnificent arrogance of their century, that they could dictate terms to an ocean. The rails themselves were pulled up in places, left to rust in others, and in one memorable stretch near Milepost Four the sea had simply folded the whole formation over like a page being turned.

Most boats took the open water route around the point. That added three hours and exposed you to the swell rolling in off the deep channel. The track bed route was faster—but it meant threading submerged culverts, culverts I knew only because I'd spent six years mapping them at low water, wading through channels no living person had reason to enter, sounding depths with a bamboo pole and writing down numbers nobody would ever read.

Until now. It turns out that a map made for no purpose becomes suddenly purposeful the moment a stranger appears asking questions. I try not to draw conclusions from this about the universe's sense of humor.

"Follow the causeway," I told Wren as we cleared the breakwater. "Stay ten feet off the wall on your side. Where the rails show, the water's shallow enough to see bottom—watch for the dark gaps."

"What are the dark gaps?"

"Culverts. Drainage arches under the embankment. Some are passable, some will take the bottom out of this boat, and one of them—" I checked my chart, laminated against spray, "—is exactly wide enough to be tempting and exactly shallow enough to kill us. Milepost Six. We go around it over the bank."

She looked at the water sliding past, black-green and opaque, then at me. "You mapped all this yourself?"

"Somebody had to."

"Why?"

It was a fair question and I gave it the answer I gave everyone, which was none. I throttled up instead, and the engine coughed twice and caught, and the conversation drowned in noise, which is where I prefer conversations to drown.

The first hour went clean. The causeway ran straight as a rule beneath us, the old rails surfacing now and then like the spines of sleeping animals, furred with weed. Gulls worked the shallows. Behind us the sun came up the color of weak tea, and ahead the cliffs rose in long wet tiers toward the tunnel mouth, still invisible around the shoulder of the headland.

At Milepost Three I killed the engine and we drifted while I sounded with the pole. "Culvert," I said. "Arched, brick, nine feet clear. Water's four feet below the crown at today's tide. We'll row through—engine off, heads down."

Wren shipped the bailer, took an oar without being told which end to hold, and watched the water. "There's a current pulling left."

"There is. Compensate right."

She compensated right. We slid into the arch, and the world became the sound of oar-tholes and dripping brick and our own breathing amplified by stone. Barnacles studded the walls close enough to scrape. Then daylight again, and the boat rocked in the wake of our own passage, and Wren exhaled through her nose—not quite a laugh.

"My last employer," she said, "would have paid a month's wages for a run like that."

"What did you do before archiving?"

"Retrieval." She said it lightly, the way you'd say *clerical work*, but her eyes stayed on the water. "Documents get lost in inconvenient places. I go and un-lose them."

"Inconvenient like flooded culverts."

"Inconvenient like that, sometimes."

I let it lie. There's a skill in letting things lie, same as there's a skill in picking them up, and I've had more practice at the first.

* * *

Milepost Six nearly ended the partnership, though not through any fault of the map.

The crossing over the bank required beaching the skiff on a shelf of slag, hauling her over a spine of ballast stone on rollers improvised from driftwood, and relaunching on the far side—all inside a tide window of perhaps ninety minutes. I'd done it alone twice. With two people it should have been easier.

It was not easier, because Wren Odile turned out to be stronger than she looked and considerably less patient than she sounded. When I called for a rest halfway up the bank, she kept hauling. When I told her the hull plate was catching, she didn't wait for instruction—she got her shoulder under the keel, found the angle herself, and freed it with a grunt that I suspect she'd have denied making under oath.

"You've moved boats before," I said, when we finally slid her down the far-side shingle.

"Ferries. Rivers. A canal barge in another life." She wrung out her sleeves. "You keep being surprised that I'm competent. I find it slightly insulting."

"I keep being surprised because competence usually announces itself. Yours has been hiding in your luggage."

"Everything worth having hides in luggage." She glanced at me sideways. "Yours included. Those maps aren't hobby work. Nobody surveys drainage culverts for pleasure."

"I do."

"No," she said, not unkindly, "you don't. You survey them so the lines exist somewhere, even if only on paper. Closed lines want witnesses. You're their witness."

I had no answer for that, so I busied myself with the engine, which appreciated the attention more than I did.

* * *

By early afternoon the headland stood between us and the sky, and the cliff tunnel came into view—or rather, the place where the cliff tunnel had been. The portal was half-swallowed by rockfall, decades of it, a fan of shattered stone spilling down to the tideline. Below the fall, at the base of the cliff, the sea lay flat and pale as hammered tin.

Slack water. The window I'd waited six years for without knowing it.

I brought the boat in slow, shipping the engine and rowing the last hundred yards, reading the water the way you read a gauge: pressure, color, movement. Along the base of the cliff, where the charts showed nothing but rock, there was a seam—a darkness in the stone, low, half-drowned, framed in something that wasn't rock.

Iron. Rail iron, rusted to lace, arched over a cave mouth barely above the waterline. The old builders had braced the sea cave the way they braced everything else, and the sea had spent eighty years collecting interest.

"There," Wren said quietly. Not a question. She'd seen it too.

"Aye."

"That's the marker from the legend. The carved stone." Her voice had changed—flatter, more careful, a woman handling something fragile. "The villagers say the vault's behind a door marked with the railway crest."

"The villagers also say a ghost walks the tunnel on fog nights and steals fish from the smokehouses. Legends charge admission; facts just sit there getting wet." I shipped the oars. "We go in on foot, wading. Lamp first, me first, you second. Nothing gets touched until I've seen what's holding up what."

"Your rules."

"My rules."

We anchored the skiff to a knob of rock with the spare anchor, loaded the lamps and the rope, and waded in. The water came to my waist, then my chest, then fell away as the floor of the cave rose—and then we were standing in air that tasted of salt and old iron, in a dark so complete the lamplight seemed to make a sound going into it.

The beam found walls ribbed with mineral stain, red and white, like the ribs of something enormous. Found the rail iron overhead, arched and corroded, holding back a mountain. Found, forty feet in, the forward carriage.

I stopped walking.

I have spent my adult life among dead railways. I know what abandoned rolling stock looks like—soft-edged, collapsed, returned politely to the earth. This was different. The carriage was fused to the rock, grown into it, its frame welded by corrosion into the cave floor as if the cliff had swallowed it whole and kept it. The windows were gone. The door hung open on a hinge of rust. Inside the lamplight, I could see the shadow of bench seats, and on one of them—

Nothing. Just seats. Eighty years of empty seats waiting for passengers who never came.

"Xanthe." Wren's voice, low. "Look at the far wall."

Beyond the carriage, the cave narrowed, and the narrowing ended in masonry. Dressed stone, laid in courses, deliberate as a signature. Set into it, a door.

Even crusted with salt, even furred with mineral bloom, I knew what it was. A strongroom door, the kind they built into terminal vaults and pay offices. And stamped across its face in raised brass, green with age but legible as yesterday:

**PENINSULA & COASTAL RAILWAY — STRONGROOM No. 4**

And beneath the crest, smaller, punched into the metal by a man whose job it was to certify such installations:

**Inspector No. 114.**

My father's number.

I don't remember sitting down. I was on a rock at the cave's edge with the lamp shaking slightly in my hand, and Wren was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read in that light, and the sea outside had begun, very faintly, to speak—the first whisper of the turn, water moving where water had been still.

"Inspector One-fourteen," Wren said softly. Not a question either. She said it the way you'd lay a card on a table.

"How do you know that number?" I asked.

The water whispered. Somewhere above us, in the flooded tunnel, something shifted—a groan of settling stone, or the sea finding a new crack, or nothing at all.

"Journey's end isn't yet," Wren said. "But Xanthe—I think we should discuss the tide."

Behind the strongroom door, in the dark, the past waited with its collar in its fist.
