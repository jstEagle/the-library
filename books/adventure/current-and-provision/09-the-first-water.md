## 9. The First Water

I have been in exactly two caves in my life. The first was a tourist grotto on the Corvane coast with electric lights and a gift shop shaped like a nautilus shell. The second was this one, and I want it noted for the record that the second one did not come with a gift shop, or lights, or any exit strategy that did not involve Xanthe Thackeray's opinion of tide tables.

"Slack water holds forty minutes," he said, wading the boat up the shingle inside the cave mouth until the keel kissed stone. "Thirty-five if you're being careful. Twenty if you're being me."

"And how long are you being?"

He checked his watch against a battered almanac wrapped in oilcloth. "Careful."

The lamplight made the cave into a cathedral that had been robbed and then flooded and then forgotten about by God. The roof arched away overhead into blackness where water dripped in slow percussion, each drop landing somewhere we couldn't see, keeping time like a metronome set to *dread*. And there, half-swallowed by the rock wall as though the cliff had decided mid-meal that it wasn't hungry after all: the forward carriage of the *Meridian Star*, the payroll train that had gone off the old cliff tunnel ninety-one years ago with eleven men and four thousand pounds of colonial silver aboard.

Rust had eaten it down to suggestion. You could see what it had been — the riveted ribs, the curve of a wheel still proud of the silt, a door frame hanging open like a jaw. Barnacles had colonized the lower panels in white crusts. Somebody had carved initials into the outer skin at shoulder height, and the salt air had preserved them better than anything else on the wreck: *E.M. + J.T., 1888.* Eleven years before the wreck. Somebody's sweetheart. Somebody who never came back.

"Don't touch," Xanthe said, without looking at me.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking about it. Your whole posture is thinking about it."

I put my hands in my pockets like a scolded schoolgirl and followed him along the carriage's flank to where the rock narrowed into a passage barely wider than my shoulders. He went first with the lamp held high, and I watched his silhouette move through the stone with the unhurried economy of a man walking a corridor he'd memorized from drawings rather than memory. Which, I realized, was exactly what he'd done. His survey notes. His maps of closed lines. He hadn't just found this cave on a low tide — he'd been *looking* for it, in his quiet, sideways, nobody-rides-these-tracks way, for longer than he'd ever admit.

The passage opened into a chamber the size of a chapel, and there it was.

The strongroom door.

It stood flush in the living rock, iron-faced, five feet of riveted certainty, stamped at eye level with the crest of the Colonial Peninsula Railway — a winged wheel over crossed keys — and beneath it, in raised brass numerals, an inspector's number.

*INSP. 4471.*

Xanthe stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into his back. The lamp trembled once — once only — and then went steady, because he was the kind of man who could order his own hands to behave. But I'd seen it. In the lamplight, in that single stutter of shadow across the rivets, I saw a man get grabbed by the collar by something that had been waiting under the sea since before either of us was born.

"That number," I said carefully. "It mean something?"

"It means the railway assigned its inspectors sequentially." His voice was flat as a railhead. "4460s were stationed upcountry. 4480s came later. It's cataloguing information."

"It's more than that and we both know it."

"We don't both know anything, Ms. Odile. That's rather the arrangement."

Fair. I let it go, because I was carrying secrets of my own with better posture and worse contents, and because the door itself was doing the talking. Four thousand pounds of silver payroll, sealed behind iron by men who expected to come back for it and didn't. The legend said they'd drowned trying. The village tavern told it with beer stains and hand gestures; the archivists' files told it in dry columns; the wreck report — which I had read eleven times on the crossing, in a cabin paid for by people whose interest in those columns was not academic — told it in the passive voice, which is how institutions say *we did it* without saying it.

"Photographs first," Xanthe said, and became, before my eyes, a different man. Not the hermit of the signal box, not the sardonic ferryman. A surveyor. He braced the lamp on a ledge, chalked a scale marker onto the rock beside the door, and worked the camera with hands that had clearly done this ten thousand times in worse light. "Plate numbers, stamping, hinge configuration, lock housing. Then the surrounding strata. Then you can pry your brass off."

"My brass?"

"The plate. You've been staring at it since we rounded the carriage. You have the expression of a woman deciding whether to steal a spoon."

I looked at the small brass rectangle bolted below the crest — a maker's plate, *Harrow & Vane, Strongroom Works, Bristol, 1897* — and felt caught, and hated feeling caught, and reached for my pry bar anyway.

We worked. That's the honest heart of it, whatever came after: for twenty minutes we were just two competent people doing competent things in a hole in the world, and it was — I'll admit it here, where no one from Corvane will ever read it — good. He called out measurements; I wrote them on slate. I held the lamp; he read the stampings with his nose six inches from the metal, lips moving, transcribing. When my pry bar slipped and cracked loudly against the rock, he said "language," even though I hadn't said anything, and I laughed — actually laughed, unguarded, the sound bouncing around the chamber like something escaped from a cage — and he almost smiled, which for Xanthe Thackeray is roughly equivalent to a standing ovation.

Then the water spoke.

It wasn't dramatic. That's what I need people to understand about the sea, having now been formally introduced: it doesn't announce itself. There was simply a sound beneath our feet, a soft shifting of gravel, and when I glanced back down the passage the shingle where we'd beached the boat was underwater. An inch. Maybe two.

"Xanthe."

"I see it." He was already moving, lamp in one hand, camera case slung, and the careful man was gone and the other one — the fast one, the one built out of fifteen years of surviving alone on forgotten lines — had taken his place. "Tide turned early. Barometric suction off the storm front pulls the low water deeper and snaps the turn back harder. I accounted for it. I didn't account enough."

"How much not-enough?"

"Run."

We ran. I use the word generously; the passage allowed nothing faster than a crouched, lamp-jostling scramble, water already sheeting cold over our boots, and every yard of it I spent composing the sentence I would say to the syndicate's lawyers from the bottom of the sea: *the asset was lost due to unforeseen tidal variance.* Behind us the chamber filled with a sound like slow applause — water finding the low places, claiming them one by one. At the mouth of the passage I risked one look back. The strongroom door stood in the lamplight's last reach, water climbing its iron face inch by patient inch, the inspector's number gleaming above the floodline like a signature on a confession.

*4471.*

I pried the maker's plate off the doorframe on my way past. Don't ask me why. It took three seconds and cost us maybe five, and Xanthe swore at me with real feeling, and I said "evidence," and he said "you're evidence of something, all right," and then the water took the passage behind us and the argument was over.

* * *

The boat cleared the cave mouth into a grey-green evening that had gone wrong while we were underground.

I want to describe it precisely, because precision is the only thing I brought to that coast worth anything. The sky to the south had organized itself into shelves — not clouds so much as architecture, tiered and bruise-colored, stacked toward the horizon like slate roofing laid by a giant with a grudge. The wind had a new note in it, a lower register, steady instead of gusty. The harbor master's boats were hauled up above the tideline in rows, hulls tipped like sleeping animals, and the village beyond them had gone quiet in the specific way of places that have done this before and are done talking about it.

Xanthe got the engine going on the fourth pull, which under the circumstances I considered a personal triumph for both of us, and we ran north along the drowned track bed with the swells coming in long and lazy, the calm before the arithmetic.

Because that's what it was. Arithmetic. I'd grown up on a salvage tug; I knew weather the way other children knew bedtime stories. Those cloud shelves weren't a squall. They were the leading edge of a system two hundred miles wide, and the barometer in Xanthe's jacket — he checked it twice in ten minutes, and the second time he held it up to the failing light and said nothing at all, which was worse than swearing — was falling like it had somewhere to be.

"First bands hit within the hour," he said finally. "Real ones by dark. It'll run two days, maybe three."

"Three days of what, exactly?"

"Rain you can't see through. Wind that takes masts. Seas that close the harbor and everything resembling a harbor." He steered around a submerged culvert marking with the ease of a man steering around furniture in his own house. "The peninsula floods. Roads go. Rail goes. For the duration, this coast is an island with one population and no visitors."

"And the cave?"

"Gone. Sealed till spring, likely. The storm surge will push the beach back thirty yards and pile the entrance under rock and sand." He glanced at me, and there was something in it I couldn't file — apology, or warning, or the particular pity of a man watching someone realize their timetable has been cancelled. "Whatever Corvane wanted out of there, they'll want it more now, and they'll want it through us."

"They don't know where we are."

"Wren." He said my name the way you'd set a broken thing down gently. "A syndicate that sends an archivist with a pry bar and a camera doesn't lose track of its archivist. Whatever comes next comes next. Right now the water is the only thing with a claim on us, and the water is not negotiating."

The first band hit us nine minutes later, and it hit like a door slammed by the whole sky.

Rain came sideways, instant and total, a white roaring curtain that erased the coast, the boat, my own hands on the gunwale. The temperature dropped ten degrees between one wave and the next. Xanthe shouted something — I caught only "*—sheet!*" — and I hauled the canvas tarp over the supplies while the boat yawed under us and the swells, which had been long and lazy, began arriving shorter and steeper, stacked up by wind that hadn't existed twenty minutes ago.

We ran blind for an hour. I will not romanticize it. There was nothing in it but noise and cold and the animal work of keeping a small boat pointed somewhere survivable, bailing with a tin scoop while Xanthe fought the engine and the compass and the growing conviction, visible in the set of his shoulders, that the coast we were aiming for was no longer reachable tonight. Twice a swell lifted us and dropped us hard enough to ring the hull like a bell. Once lightning walked down the water ahead of us, close enough to taste, and lit the whole drowned world white for a half-second — the track bed running under the waves, mile after mile of it, rails glinting like the spine of something enormous swimming alongside.

And in that half-second of lightning, I thought about the plate in my coat. *Harrow & Vane, Bristol, 1897.* Brass off a door stamped with a dead man's number, bought and paid for by a syndicate whose founders' names I'd seen in the margins of the wreck file, annotated in pencil by someone who wanted them remembered. I thought about the tavern keeper's granddaughter asking me if I'd really come all this way for *old papers*, and how I'd smiled, and what the smile had cost.

Then the dark came back and there was only the water again, and the water does not care what you're carrying.

"Quarry!" Xanthe bellowed, and pointed.

I saw it as a black notch in the blacker land — the old quarry cutting he'd marked on his charts, a flooded pit inland of the shore with a rail cutting running into it, sheltered on the seaward side by fifty feet of blasted granite. We came in on the back of a swell, scraped something that groaned, and slid into water so suddenly calm it felt like stepping indoors. Rain still hammered down, but the wind broke apart overhead and the walls held the worst of it out. Somewhere above us, unseen, the storm went on organizing its next argument.

We tied off to a rusted mooring ring — a rail stanchion driven into the quarry lip decades ago, another piece of the closed line holding on to its job — and sat in the bilge under the tarp, breathing.

My hands were shaking. I watched them shake with a kind of detached professional interest, the way you watch equipment behave badly. Across from me, Xanthe wrung out his cap, checked the barometer a third time, and read the number by matchlight.

"Well?" I said.

"It's going to be a long stay." He struck another match and looked at me across the small yellow room of its light, and his face did something complicated — the wariness still there, but underneath it something rawer, something that had been exposed in that cave when the lamp shook, and hadn't finished going back into hiding. "You pried a plate off a sealed door belonging to Inspector 4471, and you didn't hesitate, and you didn't ask. Which tells me you knew what that number meant before we ever launched this boat."

The rain filled the silence like static.

"So here's my question, Ms. Odile," he said, and the match burned down between us, and neither of us moved to shield it. "And remember — at journey's end, you owe me one answer, honestly given. I'm calling it early. Who sent you?"

I opened my mouth to lie. I'd been opening my mouth to lie for three weeks; it was practically muscle memory. But the storm was overhead, and the truth was sitting in my coat pocket in brass, and the man across from me had run back into rising water to make sure I got out first.

The match went out.

"In the morning," I said. "When we can see each other's faces. You'll want to sit down for this one, Xanthe."

He laughed — short, humorless, a sound with no trust left in it anywhere. "Wren. I've been sitting down for this one for fifteen years. I just didn't know there'd be company."

Above us, the monsoon settled in over the peninsula like a tenant with a long lease, and the first night of it began.
