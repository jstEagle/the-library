## 10. Leaking Boat, Rising Sea

The first band of the monsoon hit us like a door slammed by an angry god, and I learned in that instant that the sea had been merely warming up all day.

The rain didn't fall so much as arrive sideways, in sheets thick enough to drown in if you tilted your head wrong. The boat—my boat, my beloved, treacherous, salvaged-from-three-different-graveyards boat—took the first gust broadside and slewed toward the cliffs before I could bring her nose around. Wren, who had been sitting with the smug stillness of a person who believed the worst was behind them, went across the thwart like a dropped tool.

"Bail!" I shouted, which was redundant, because the water was already bailing itself in. "And when you're done bailing, bail some more!"

She found the tin scoop I kept lashed under the gunwale and went to work without argument, which was the first genuinely encouraging thing I'd seen in six hours. There are two kinds of passengers in a storm: the kind who panic and the kind who work. Panic is contagious. So is work. I'd take a competent stranger over a weeping friend in open water, and I was beginning to suspect that Wren Odile was neither stranger nor friend but something I hadn't got a name for yet.

The engine coughed, caught, coughed again. Salt water in the carburetor, or the fuel line, or both, or everything—when a marine engine starts making that particular sound, it's not asking a question, it's delivering a verdict.

"No," I told it.

It coughed again, more emphatically.

"No," I repeated, and put my hand flat on the cowling like I was taking an oath. Fifteen years of coaxing dead machines back to life along dead lines, and I still talked to engines like they were mules. They respect it, though. Mules and engines both.

The coast was a rumor to the west, somewhere behind the rain wall. I'd been steering by a compass, a wristwatch, and the growing certainty that I had misjudged the distance by a margin that was about to become fatal. In surveying we call that "accumulated error." In open water in a monsoon we call it "drowning with extra steps."

The engine died for good a quarter mile off a black headland I didn't recognize, which frightened me more than the storm did. I know this coast the way other men know their own kitchens. A headland I couldn't name meant the sea had moved my landmarks, or the rain had eaten them, or I had been steering in circles while congratulating myself on my seamanship. Any of the three was possible. All of them had happened to better men.

"Oars," I said.

"There are no oars," Wren said. "You said—" she bailed, spat, bailed again "—you said the tide would carry us."

"The tide is a liar. I told you that at the cave."

"You told me the tide *gives back*."

"It gives back what it likes, to whom it likes. Tonight it does not like us." I got the sweep—the long steering oar I kept for exactly this occasion and had privately hoped never to use—into the rowlock and put my back into it. The boat weighed a thousand pounds with water in her. The wind weighed considerably more.

We made the headland the way a drunk makes a doorway—by accident, at an angle, with a great deal of noise. Behind it the land folded into a narrow bight, and in the bight, half-hidden by rain, I saw it: a cutting. A man-made cutting, straight-sided, running inland like a corridor. An old quarry tramway, probably, or a branch I'd never mapped because the maps I'd inherited had it drawn in pencil and then erased.

"You're smiling," Wren said. She had stopped bailing, which told me how far gone the situation was; nobody stops bailing voluntarily.

"I know what that is."

"It's a hole in the ground."

"It's a *railway* hole in the ground. There's a difference. A hole in the ground is where you die. A railway hole in the ground has grade, drainage, and a level floor, which means it's where you *don't* die, or at least where you die more comfortably." I shipped the sweep. "Ride the swell in. When I say down, get flat."

The entrance was maybe forty feet wide, a dark slot in the rock with the sea already pushing into it in long white tongues. I timed it the way you time anything on this coast—by feel, by prayer, by the small animal arithmetic that lives below thought. Swell. Swell. Bigger swell. *Now.*

We came in on the back of the largest wave of the night, and the boat surfed twenty feet up the flooded cutting floor and grounded with a crunch I felt in my teeth. The wave withdrew, sucking at the keel, and I was over the side in water to my waist with the painter in my fist before the next one arrived. Wren, to her credit, had the toolbox out and was passing it to me before I asked.

We hauled her up another ten feet, wedged her between two rusted iron plates that had probably been a tip wagon in a former life, and got the anchor—such as it was—wrapped around a bollard that had been waiting in the dark for someone to need it for eighty years.

Then the storm made landfall, and the night stopped being a problem and became a condition.

* * *

The cutting ran back into the hill perhaps two hundred yards, rising gently, and the sea filled only the first third of it. The rest was wet gravel, standing water in the drainage channel, and—this was the miracle I kept returning to, the small gift I didn't deserve—dry-ish rock under an overhang where the old loading platform had been, its timber roof mostly intact, its iron brackets furred with rust but sound.

I got a fire going. Not because I'm clever, but because I hoard. There was a tin of solid fuel and a bundle of tarred rag in the boat's locker, the kind of thing a man carries for fifteen years without needing and then needs once, completely. The flame caught and threw our shadows up the wet rock face, and I sat back on my heels and took inventory of myself the way you inventory a damaged machine: hands, cut, working. Ribs, bruised, working. Everything else, exhausted, working.

Wren sat opposite me, wringing out her hair. In the firelight she looked younger and older at once, the way people do when the performance is washed off them. All day she had been the archivist, the passenger, the woman with the notebook and the questions. Now she was just a soaked person on a rock, and I found I liked her better this way, which was a thought I filed away for later examination and did not like the look of even as I filed it.

"Your boat," she said, "is held together with railway fittings."

"Railway fittings are the best fittings. They were built to be neglected." I nodded at the hull, where a fishplate I'd bolted across a split plank two years ago was doing the work of a shipwright. "Everything on that boat has already survived being abandoned. It's the only qualification I look for."

"Is that a philosophy or an autobiography?"

"Yes," I said, and she laughed—short, surprised at itself, gone in a second, but real. It was the first time I'd heard it. I pretended not to notice, in the manner of a man not noticing a lit fuse.

The boat was the next problem. The crunch on the gravel had opened the split the fishplate was bridging, and the sea, being thorough, would finish the job by morning if the surge reached her. I stripped my oilskin, found the spare canvas and the pitch pot, and went to work in the shallows by lantern light, which is to say I stood in cold water up to my thighs for an hour, cursing with real feeling and some technical range.

Wren bailed her out every time the surge came, and held the lantern, and at some point without discussion began handing me things before I asked for them. Pitch pot. Canvas. The awl. The awl again, because I'd dropped it. It was the closest thing to conversation we'd had that wasn't a negotiation.

"Tell me something true," she said, near midnight.

I nearly cut myself. "Why?"

"Because we're going to die out here, statistically, and I'd hate for my last data point on you to be a man who names boats after grudges."

"This boat doesn't have a name."

"Exactly."

I drove the awl through canvas and plank and thought about it. The rain hammered the cutting like a thousand small inspectors demanding paperwork. "I used to be afraid of tunnels," I said finally. "As a boy. My father took me through them on inspection runs, and I'd count the seconds to daylight. Every one. I know every tunnel on every closed line on this coast by how many seconds of dark it has." I tied off the patch. "That's the truest thing I have that doesn't hurt to say."

She was quiet a moment. "How many seconds does the cliff tunnel have?"

"Forty-one."

"You've counted."

"I count everything. It's not a virtue. It's a symptom."

She looked at the fire, and I watched her decide something and un-decide it, the way you watch a signal arm rise and fall. "My father," she said, and stopped. Started again. "My father worked for a company that—"

The sea interrupted. A surge bigger than the others came up the cutting, reached the boat, and found the patch, and the patch held, but the mooring bollard shifted with a sound like a tooth coming loose, and we both went into the water again. After that there was no conversation, only work: re-securing, re-wedging, hauling her up another five feet on the flood of adrenaline that had nothing to do with the tide.

By the time we got her settled, the fire had gone to embers and neither of us suggested rebuilding it. We sat under the loading platform's overhang instead, shoulder to shoulder against the cold, listening to the storm work the headland like a lockpick.

* * *

Sometime in the black hours, shivering, teeth chattering hard enough to make speech an act of will, Wren started talking.

She didn't look at me. She spoke to the fire's ghost, in fragments, the way you'd read a water-damaged letter—here a legible phrase, there a smear, the sense arriving late and sideways.

"I wasn't sent to find a cave," she said. "I was sent to find a *man*. The cave was the bait. They knew you'd go. They said—he said—'He'll survey his way to it eventually. He can't help himself. He's his father's son.'"

The words went through me like a spike through a rail. I didn't move. I kept my breathing even and my eyes on the dark water, because if I turned to look at her she would stop, and I wanted—God help me, I *wanted*—to hear the rest.

"They gave me your survey notes. Not copies. Your actual field books, pages of them. Coordinates I was meant to 'discover' matched against wreck records." A breath. "I thought it was scholarship. That's what I told myself. Archival work. Corvane funds archives, they fund *everything*, they have a foundation, they have a—there's a whole building, glass, in the capital, with the wreck's bell in the lobby." Her voice cracked on the bell. "I stood in that lobby and thought how nice it was that they remembered."

The rain filled the silence. I let it.

"And then your notes." She pulled her knees up. "Nobody keeps notes like that. Nobody maps closed lines for fifteen years for no one, in that hand, that careful, that—*lonely*. I've read a thousand field books. They're records. Yours are—" She stopped. "They're letters to nobody."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't cost me, and I was already bleeding from a dozen cuts I hadn't agreed to.

"I'm going to tell you everything," she said. "I am. But not—" her teeth chattered through it "—not all at once. Not in the dark. Not while we might not live to be angry about it properly."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in fifteen years," I said. "The *not all at once* part. Everyone else leads with everything and means none of it."

She turned her head then, finally, and looked at me across the ember-light. Her face was wrecked—salt, exhaustion, the beginning of something like shame. "You're not surprised."

"I've been not-surprised since the signal box. You held your compass like a salvage assessor, not an archivist. Archivists cradle them. Assessors *aim* them." I fed the last of the tarred rag to the coals. "I let you aboard anyway. Make of that what you like."

"What I like," she said, "is that you're either the most forgiving man on this coast or the biggest fool, and I've watched you work all day, and it isn't forgiveness."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

We sat with that. The storm reached some private agreement with the headland and settled into a steady, grinding roar, the sound of a thing that intended to continue for days. The water in the cutting crept up the gravel, stopped, held.

"Xanthe," she said. "Is that even your name?"

I watched the coals. Forty-one seconds of dark, that tunnel. I'd counted them my whole life, and here in a quarry cutting with a woman who'd been sent to find me, the dark felt considerably longer.

"Ask me at journey's end," I said. "Those were the terms. You get one honest answer, at the end."

"I know. I wrote the terms. I mean—" she almost laughed, a broken little sound "—I mean, when the storm's over. When we're ashore. When you can walk away from the answer. That's when I'll ask."

"And I'll answer."

"You will?"

"I said one honest answer. I've been thinking about which one to give you." I lay back against the cold rock and closed my eyes. "I've had fifteen years to choose it. It turns out I've known all along."

She didn't ask what I meant. That, more than anything she'd said all night, was how I knew the fragments were true—because a liar pushes, and Wren Odile, whatever she was, had stopped pushing and started waiting, and waiting is a thing only the honest and the very patient know how to do.

Sleep took me in pieces. The last thing I registered was the sea retreating down the cutting, inch by inch, and the boat holding, and the small treacherous warmth of another person's shoulder against mine in the cold.

I woke to grey light and a silence with a wrongness in it, the way a paused engine has a wrongness. The storm had eased to a hard drizzle. The cutting was calm. And far off, back toward the coast we'd failed to reach, there was a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat and had hoped never to hear again in my life.

Timber, under great strain. A trestle, somewhere inland, groaning under a flood it was never built to see—and from the direction of it, the only high ground between us and the open valley was the quarry rim, and the only way to the rim was the flooded tramway cutting itself.

Wren was already awake, watching me watch the treeline. "Tell me that's not what I think it is," she said.

"It's a trestle," I said, standing, reaching for my maps. "And it's about to give us a very bad morning."
