## 11. What Wren Knows

The quarry had been a railway cutting once—you could still see the drill marks in the walls, neat rows of them like a ledger's ruled lines—and now it was a bowl of brown water with a shelf of gravel high enough to stay dry. We dragged the boat up onto that shelf at what I estimated was two in the morning and what Xanthe estimated to the minute, because of course he did. The man carried time like a tool in his pocket, alongside the pliers and the tide tables.

The rain came down in ropes. Not bands, not sheets—ropes, thick as a man's arm, hammering the flooded cutting into white noise. Somewhere above us the storm was making landfall with all the subtlety of a debt collector.

We got a fire going under an overhang of rock, using driftwood and half a drum of diesel-soaked waste from some long-dead worksite, and Xanthe rigged a tarp over it with the competence of a man who had slept rough on purpose for years. I watched him do it and thought: *he has done this so many times he doesn't think about it anymore.* And then I thought: *and you are about to make him think about everything.*

I'd been carrying the confession since the signal box. Longer, if I'm honest—since the day Corvane put his name in front of me and I signed the contract anyway. It sat in my chest like ballast, and ballast is useful right up until the moment you need to throw it overboard or sink.

He fed the fire. He checked the boat's seams by lamplight, marked the worst one with chalk, said nothing about it, which was its own kind of eloquence. Then he sat down across from me, wrapped in a wool blanket that smelled of forty years of signal-box dust, and looked at me with those grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

"Well," he said. "You promised fragments in the boat. I've counted four so far. I believe the arithmetic suggests there's more."

"There's more."

"Then I'll stoke the fire." He did. "Take your time. The storm appears to be taking its own."

* * *

Here is what I should have told him first, and didn't, because cowardice arranges itself in convenient orders:

My name is Wren Odile because I chose it, the way he chose his. The name on my birth certificate belongs to a girl who grew up in a salvage town on the other coast, a place where they strip wrecks the way vultures strip carcasses—efficiently, without sentiment, and leaving nothing for anyone else. My father worked the yards. So did I, from the time I was twelve. I can tell you the scrap value of a locomotive by the sound it makes when you drop a hammer on it. I can tell you which shipping companies insured their hulls and then quietly hoped for weather.

Corvane Salvage Syndicate found me when I was twenty-three, because I was good at finding things. Records, mostly. I have a memory for documents the way Xanthe has a memory for culverts. Corvane doesn't employ divers and dynamite men the way people imagine. They employ archivists. They buy defunct railway companies' paper trails by the ton—deeds, insurance claims, crew manifests, wreck inquiries—and they read them the way prospectors pan silt, looking for the glint.

The glint, this time, was a payroll vault.

"The Meridian Coastal Railway," I said, watching his face. "Nineteen-oh-eight. The cliff tunnel. You know the story—the villagers tell it for tourist coin, but the bones of it are real. A pay train went into the tunnel during a storm and never came out. Eleven men drowned. The company paid out on eleven lives and wrote off one strongroom door, and then spent the next decade insisting the payroll had been recovered before the wreck, which was a lie, because the insurance settlement says otherwise. Corvane bought the Meridian's successor company's archives in bulk eight years ago. I was assigned to the file three years ago."

Xanthe didn't move. The firelight moved on him instead, shadows sliding across a face that had gone very still, very careful—the face of a man listening to a rail joint for the crack that means the whole span is rotten.

"Go on," he said.

"I reconstructed the wreck's true position from tide records and a diver's report that was suppressed in 1911. The official coordinates were wrong—deliberately wrong, I think, entered by someone who wanted the site forgotten. But the tide tables don't lie, and neither does the cliff geology. When I plotted the corrected position—" I made myself keep looking at him. "It fell inside the survey grid of a closed coastal branch. One branch. With one active surveyor listed in the regional registry. Filed under a name that didn't exist fifteen years ago, attached to a man whose work habits matched a description I'd been given."

"What description?"

"Left-handed. Carries his maps rolled, never folded. Surveys alone. Answers correspondence addressed to the regional office but never opens mail addressed to himself." I paused. "And a scar through the left eyebrow, from a childhood accident involving a signal lever, per a school medical record from a town he has never once mentioned."

The fire popped. Rain hammered the tarp. Xanthe reached up and touched the scar, an unconscious gesture, the way a man touches a wound to confirm it's real.

"You were given my name," he said. Not a question.

"I was given your name, your old name, your father's service number, and a photograph of you at about nineteen years old standing in front of a locomotive." I heard my own voice go flat and professional, the voice I used in Corvane's reading rooms, and hated it, and couldn't stop it. "Xanthe Thackeray. Born Aldous Vane. Son of Inspector Edmund Vane, Meridian Coastal Railway, traffic and safety division. The inspector who certified the cliff tunnel as safe six weeks before the wreck."

* * *

I want to be fair to myself in this account, and fairness requires me to admit that I watched him take that blow the way you watch a bridge take a flood—looking for the moment of failure.

It didn't come. That was the terrible thing. He absorbed it. His hands stayed flat on his knees. His breathing didn't change. Only his eyes went somewhere else—to a place fifteen years away—and came back, and when they came back they were colder than the rain outside.

"My father," he said, "has been dead nine years."

"Yes."

"And Corvane kept his file open anyway."

"Corvane keeps everything open. That's rather their whole nature." I pulled my knees up, wrapped my arms around them. "They're not sentimental about it. It's inventory. Edmund Vane's certification of the tunnel is the linchpin of their claim—if the tunnel was certified sound, then the wreck was an act of God, the insurance payout stands as settled history, and any salvage rights to the cave vest cleanly in the successor company's title, which Corvane holds. If the certification was false—if the inspector knew the cliff was unstable, or worse, if the wreck wasn't an act of God at all—then the whole chain of title is poisoned, the settlement reopens, and Corvane's claim becomes worth exactly nothing except lawsuits."

"So they need the vault," Xanthe said slowly, working it through the way he'd work through a gradient profile, "to prove the certification was sound."

"They need the vault to prove whatever the vault proves. They sent me to find it and assess. Quietly. No demolition, no publicity, no inquiry. An assessment, a report, and then their lawyers decide what the world gets to know." I swallowed. "That was the assignment. Locate the site, confirm the strongroom, document, withdraw. The name they gave me—the surveyor who'd already found the approach—was a courtesy. A shortcut. 'He'll lead you there,' my handler said. 'He's been mapping that line for years. He just doesn't know why.'"

The fire hissed as a gust drove rain under the tarp's edge. Xanthe leaned forward and adjusted the driftwood with a stick, precise, unhurried, and I understood he was doing it to have something to do with his hands, and I let him have the moment.

"Why does a salvage syndicate," he said finally, "know what happened to a dead inspector's son? Why does it know he changed his name? Why does it care where he went?" He looked up. "Archivists find documents, Wren. Documents don't follow a boy across fifteen years and three counties."

Because I asked that same question, in a carpeted office that smelled of cedar and money, and got an answer I have been trying to unhear ever since.

"Because Corvane's founders," I said, "were the Meridian's directors."

The stick stopped moving.

"Three of the names on the syndicate's founding charter are names from the Meridian board of 1908. The same board that collected the insurance settlement. The same board that declared the payroll lost. The same board that buried the diver's report and falsified the coordinates." I kept my voice level. It cost me. "Corvane isn't hunting the vault despite its history, Xanthe. It's hunting the vault to finish that history. Whatever is sealed behind that strongroom door, the men who built this syndicate were the men who sealed it—or the men who ordered it sealed. They've waited a hundred years for the tide to give it back. They gave me your name because your father's file is the thickest one in the box, and because a man who's spent fifteen years hiding knows every way in and out of a coastline, and because—"

"Because I'm leverage," he said. "Or a liability. Depending on how the assessment goes."

"Because you're both," I said, "and I was told to determine which."

* * *

The silence after that lasted long enough for me to count the drips coming through the tarp. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Somewhere in the dark above us, water was moving through the flooded cutting with intent, patient as interest accruing.

When Xanthe finally spoke, his voice was quiet and terribly steady, the way water is steady right before it finds the crack.

"How long have you known? Exactly. Not the assignment—I mean me. This man sitting in front of you. How long?"

"Since before I met you." I made myself say it plainly; he deserved plainness if he deserved anything. "There was a photograph. There was a service record. There was a psychological note in your father's file—a note written by a company doctor in 1909, after the inquiry, saying the inspector's son, aged nine, had been found in the records room at night, and that the boy had seen something, and that the family should be watched. Corvane inherited that note with the archives. They've known about you since you were nine years old."

His hand went to the scar again. Stopped halfway. Came down.

"They've been watching me," he said, "my whole life."

"No. They've been *filing* you. There's a difference, and it matters, and I need you to hear it because it's the only defense I have left." I uncurled and faced him fully, firelight between us. "Nobody followed you. Nobody cared enough to follow you. You were a page in a box—a loose end, a curiosity, a possible asset filed under *pending*. For fifteen years you were safe precisely because you were invisible, and the tragedy of that is so large I can't fit it into a sentence, but it's true. You erased yourself and it *worked*. Right up until the tide went out further than it's gone in a century, and the site surfaced, and suddenly the pending file became urgent, and they handed it to me because I'm the best finder they have."

"And you took it."

"I took it."

"Knowing who I was."

"Knowing who you were." I held his gaze. "Yes."

"Why?"

And here is the part I had rehearsed on the boat, in the dark, while he bailed, and every rehearsal collapsed, because there is no clean answer. There is only the true one.

"Because I told myself it was just records," I said. "Because I'm very good at telling myself things. Because Corvane pays for independence the way other employers pay wages, and I told myself I'd locate the site, write the report, and walk away before it ever touched a living person. Because the file was a puzzle and puzzles are how I keep the world at arm's length, and I recognized—" my voice cracked, and I let it, because hiding it would have been one lie too many, "—I recognized the technique. The meticulousness. The ritual. The beautiful, armored solitude. I read your survey notes, Xanthe. Before I ever met you. I read three years of your field books, borrowed under a records request, and I knew within twenty pages that I was reading the work of a man doing exactly what I do—building a map so complete that no one could ever ask him to be in it."

The rain filled the pause. He let it.

"So no," I said. "I didn't come here innocent, and I won't insult you by pretending the last two weeks were anything but the assignment wearing a friendly face. I lied about being an archivist—well. I *am* an archivist. I lied about what I archive for. I let you believe I stumbled onto the legend in a tavern when I'd had the coordinates memorized for a year. I watched you lie to me about the cave and I let you, because a liar always prefers company. And then the trestle went out, and the storm came, and we nearly drowned together, and you gave me your dry blanket and taught me to read the current off the surface of the water, and somewhere between the cave and this quarry I stopped being able to find the line between the job and—" I gestured at the fire, the rain, him, the absurd little island of light we'd built in a drowning world. "This. Whatever this is."

"That's not a confession," Xanthe said. "That's a weather report. It describes conditions. It doesn't say what you intend to do about them."

Fair. Brutally fair. He was, I was learning, a brutally fair man.

"So here's the intention," I said. "My contract requires me to report the site confirmed, the strongroom located, and the local surveyor identified as cooperative or obstructive. I have a transmitter in my kit—short-range, useless in this storm, but the storm ends. When it ends, Corvane expects a signal. Halloway's advance team is already on the peninsula; I saw their launch in the harbor two days ago and didn't tell you, which is another item for the list of things I owe you." I held up my hand before he could speak. "If I send that signal, Corvane comes for the vault with lawyers and divers and whatever force they've decided the situation requires, and they come knowing everything about you, and they will use it. If I don't send it, I'm in breach, and Corvane doesn't sue people like me, Xanthe. It *files* them. I have seen what happens to finders who go off-book. There's a wreck yard on the other coast with a section nobody asks about."

"So you're choosing," he said.

"I'm choosing. Here. Now. In a flooded quarry, in the worst storm in thirty years, with a leaking boat and a man I've betrayed, because I don't get a better moment and neither do you." I reached into my coat—the movement made him tense, and I noted it, and noted that I'd earned it—and drew out the contract. Water-spotted, dog-eared, my signature at the bottom in ink that had run slightly in the cave. I held it over the fire.

"Wren." His voice had an edge I hadn't heard before. "Wait. That's evidence. That's proof of—"

"Of everything I've just told you, yes." I looked at him across the flames. "Burn it or keep it. Your call. You're the one it was used against. But understand what it means either way: I am not sending the signal. I am not writing the report. As far as Corvane will ever hear from me, the site was never confirmed and the surveyor was never found. Which means that when the storm clears and I miss my check-in, they will stop assuming and start looking. And the first place they'll look is the last place I was seen—which is beside you."

I turned the contract toward the flames. He caught my wrist—not hard, but absolutely, the grip of a man who handles machinery and respects what machinery can do.

"Keep it," he said.

I blinked. "Why?"

"Because burned paper proves nothing and a live witness proves everything." He took the contract from my hand, folded it along its existing creases with that maddening precision, and tucked it inside his oilskin, against the maps. "Because if this goes where I think it goes, I'll want it. And because—" he paused, and something moved behind the grey eyes that wasn't cold at all, that was exhausted and human and almost, almost warm, "—because you told me the truth in the order I'd have told it. Worst first. That's the honest order. I know. I've been saving mine up the same way."

"Yours," I said. "Your father's file. What's actually in it."

Something crossed his face like a shadow crossing a cutting.

"The certification," he said quietly. "Whether Edmund Vane knew the cliff was unstable. Whether the wreck was an act of God or an act of paperwork." He looked into the fire. "I can answer that question, Wren. I've been able to answer it since I was nine years old. I found his ledger. I've carried it in my head for thirty years, and I changed my name and buried my life to keep it underground, and tomorrow—when the storm gives us a window, if it gives us a window—I'll tell you what's in it. And then you'll understand why Corvane didn't send lawyers to find me."

He fed the last of the driftwood to the fire and lay back against the pack, eyes open, watching the rain.

"They sent an archivist," he said, "because they already know what I know. They just need to know whether *you* do. Sleep if you can. I'll take first watch."

I didn't sleep. I lay on the gravel shelf listening to the water rise in the dark, doing the arithmetic of a man who has just made himself homeless in the only country he had—Corvane's paper empire—and discovering, to my considerable surprise, that the arithmetic balanced. That the loss felt less like drowning and more like setting something heavy down on solid ground.

Above us, unheard beneath the storm, the old quarry trestle creaked on its footings, testing the flood, deciding how much longer it intended to stand.
