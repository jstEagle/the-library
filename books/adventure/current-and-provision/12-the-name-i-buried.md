## 12. The Name I Buried

The fire in the quarry cutting burned low and orange, throwing our shadows up the wet stone walls like two figures being slowly erased. Wren had finished her confession—Corvane, the contract, my name on a file she'd been handed like a shopping list—and now the silence sat between us with the particular weight of a debt neither of us wanted to acknowledge.

She'd told me everything. It was only fair, I supposed, that I return the courtesy.

The trouble with fairness is that it always arrives at the worst possible moment.

"You knew," she said quietly. "When you saw that inspector number stamped on the strongroom door. You went white as ballast lime. I've watched you face down a flooding culvert without changing expression, Xanthe. But that number—" She shook her head. "That number did something to you."

I fed a piece of broken pallet to the fire and watched it catch. Somewhere above us, the monsoon worked at the world like a patient carpenter, prying things loose.

"My name isn't Xanthe Thackeray," I said.

I hadn't planned to say it. It simply came out, the way water comes out of a cracked pipe—not all at once, but steadily, and then all at once.

* * *

"Fifteen years ago I was called Elias Verrin. My father was Nathaniel Verrin, senior track inspector for the Corridor Coast Railway—the same railway whose payroll train went into the sea in '09. The same railway whose crest is stamped on that strongroom door." I kept my eyes on the fire. It was easier than watching her face. "And the same railway, as you've no doubt already deduced from whatever Corvane put in your briefing folder, that my father helped destroy."

Wren didn't move. In my experience, people who work in concealment know when to hold still.

"I was eleven years old," I said. "Eleven-year-olds are not supposed to find anything important. That's the whole design of childhood—you're given a small world with small mysteries, and the large ones are kept behind locked doors in your father's study. Except my father was a meticulous man. Meticulous men keep ledgers. And meticulous men who are also guilty men keep *very* careful ledgers, because guilt is a form of bookkeeping too."

"What kind of ledger?" Her voice was careful. Flat, even. Archivist's voice.

"The kind with dates and tide tables and payments recorded against them. The kind where you can watch a man talk himself into treason one entry at a time. He wrote it all down—every consultation with the men who paid him, every survey he falsified, every warning he suppressed about the cliff cut above Marrow Point. He documented his own crime so thoroughly that if you read it front to back, you could almost admire him. Almost." I turned the stick in the fire. "He was proud of the work. That's what I couldn't get past, even at eleven. Not that he'd done it. That he'd done it *well*, and he knew it, and somewhere in those pages he was pleased with himself."

"And you found it how?"

"Looking for stamps." I laughed, and it came out wrong, hollow as an empty culvert. "I collected stamps. Terrible hobby for an inspector's son—all that paper, all that order. His study was forbidden territory, which of course meant it was the most interesting room in the house. I went in for the stamp album. It wasn't where he kept it. The ledger was underneath."

The fire popped. Out in the flooded quarry, rain needled the standing water, ten thousand small interruptions.

"I put it back," I said. "That's the part I've never told anyone. I read it three times over four days—I checked his tide tables against the almanac myself, because some part of me was certain an eleven-year-old had made an arithmetic error—and then I put it back exactly where I'd found it, and I went downstairs and ate dinner, and I passed him the salt, and I said nothing. For six weeks I said nothing. I was waiting for him to confess. I think children always wait for that. You do something monstrous in your head and then you wait for the adult world to make it right, because surely they can see it on your face, surely they know."

"He never confessed."

"He never stopped." I looked at her then. "Six weeks after I found the ledger, the payroll train went into the sea. Forty-one men. I heard the whistle of the relief engine from my bedroom window. I was eleven years old and I knew, Wren. I knew before the harbor master knew. Before the inquiry knew. I sat on my bed in the dark doing sums—forty-one men, minus however many the cave took, times the pension my mother would never ask about—and I said nothing. Again."

"That's not a child's sin."

"No," I agreed. "It's not. I've had fifteen years to arrive at that conclusion and I still don't fully believe it. That's the architecture of a thing like this. You build your whole self around the load-bearing wall of your own guilt, and even when you learn the wall isn't structural, you've built so much on top of it that taking it down feels like collapse."

* * *

Wren shifted closer to the fire. Her shoulder was bound under her coat—she'd taken the knock escaping the cave, though she'd lied about its severity, which by then I'd come to expect from both of us.

"After the wreck," I went on, "there was an inquiry. My father testified. He was very good at it—calm, precise, sorrowful in the correct amounts. They cleared him entirely. Commended him, even, for his service record. And I stood in the public gallery with my mother's hand on my shoulder, holding the knowledge of what he actually was, and I understood something that has governed my life since: the truth doesn't surface just because someone knows it. Truth needs a vessel. It needs someone willing to be destroyed by carrying it."

"Why destroyed?"

"Because look at what happened to everyone who tried." I counted them off on my fingers, an old habit, a surveyor's tally. "The signalman who swore he'd been given the wrong orders—he drowned the following spring. Accident. The clerk who pulled the freight manifests—disappeared between stations, literally; there's a gap in the record you could drive a locomotive through. The widow who petitioned for a second inquiry—ruined, quietly, by debts she couldn't have incurred honestly. Every person who reached for that truth lost their grip on their own life. And I was eleven, and I had watched my father erase better people than me, and I drew the only conclusion available: if I ever spoke, I would vanish too. So I chose to vanish first. On my own terms."

"How old were you when you left?"

"Seventeen. I'd spent six years learning everything he could teach me about railways—because here's the joke, Wren, the bitter little joke at the center of my life: I loved the work. I loved it the way he did. Track geometry, drainage, the physics of a curve taken at speed. He gave me a whole craft, and the craft was stained through, and I couldn't put it down any more than I could put down my own hands." I fed the fire another board. "So I took the craft and I changed my name—Xanthe Thackeray, names lifted off a gravestone in a parish two counties east, chosen precisely because nobody connected to either family would ever come looking—and I went to work on lines nobody rode. Branches. Spur tracks. Lines closed or closing, where the paperwork was thin and the supervisors were distant and no one ever asked a man where he came from, because on a dead line, nobody comes from anywhere. You're just the person who keeps the culverts clear."

"The map," she said. "Your survey book. All those closed lines."

"Every one of them walked and measured and drawn. Fifteen years of it." I shrugged, and the shrug cost more than I meant it to. "People assume it's eccentricity. A man who maps dead railways must be a romantic, or a crank. It's neither. It's penance with a chain and tape. Every mile of abandoned track I surveyed was a mile where nothing terrible had happened yet—a mile that was merely forgotten instead of murdered. I told myself I was preserving records. What I was actually doing was staying in motion. A moving target can't be asked questions."

"And the vault? When the tide gave back the cave—did you know then?"

"I suspected. The marker stone matched the legend, and the legend matched coordinates I'd had in my book for years—coordinates I'd surveyed from the cliff top and never once let myself interpret." I shook my head. "You want to hear the truly absurd part? I'd mapped that stretch of coast hoping to prove the legend false. Hoping the cave didn't exist, or that it was a natural formation with no strongroom in it. Because if the vault was real, then the ledger my father kept might still exist too—and I have spent half my life making sure that ledger stayed lost, and the other half unable to stop looking for it. Both halves at once. That's the trick of it. You can carry a secret and hunt it in the same body. Nobody warns you."

* * *

The rain intensified, drumming on the quarry lip, and I raised my voice slightly against it.

"When I saw his inspector number on that door—N-4471—I felt him grab me. Through forty feet of rock and sixty years of salt water, I felt his hand close on my collar the way it used to when I'd tracked mud across his clean floor. And my first thought wasn't grief. It wasn't even fear. It was *he's still inspecting*. Still checking that everything is sealed properly. Even dead, even drowned, the man audits."

Wren was quiet a long moment. Then: "Why tell me this? Now?"

"Because you emptied your pockets first." I gestured at her—at the ruined archivist's cover story, the syndicate money, the name on the contract. "You handed me the worst of yourself across a campfire and waited to see if I'd run. That took more nerve than the cave did. And because—" I stopped. Restarted. This was the part I'd been circling all night, the reef beneath the waterline. "Because for fifteen years, the only person who knew who I was, was me. And a secret held alone stops being a possession. It becomes a cell. You were hired to find me, Wren. You found me. Which means somebody out there already knows the shape of my grave. And I find that I'd rather the person sitting next to me in the flood knows the whole map than half of it."

"So we're even," she said. "Two liars, empty."

"Completely empty. It's remarkable. I feel lighter and more exposed simultaneously, like a bridge after they strip the cladding for inspection." I managed something that was mostly a smile. "Is it always like this? Telling the truth?"

"I wouldn't know," she said. "It's my first time."

And then—we both laughed. Not the wry, armored exchange of the boat, not the nervous bark after a near-drowning, but an actual laugh, ragged and surprised, two exhausted people discovering that the bottom of honesty is a floor and not a drop. It echoed off the quarry walls and came back to us sounding like other people, younger people, and for perhaps a minute the monsoon was only weather.

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Elias," she said, testing it. "It suits you less than Xanthe."

"It suits me exactly," I said. "That's the problem. Xanthe is a man who owns nothing and owes nothing. Elias is the inspector's son who passed the salt to a saboteur for six weeks and said nothing. I buried him in a parish two counties east and I've been visiting the grave ever since, every time I open that survey book."

"Then maybe," Wren said, and her voice had gone quiet and level in the way I was beginning to recognize as her most dangerous register, "it's time to dig him up. Because here's what I didn't finish telling you, Xanthe—what I couldn't finish, before. Corvane didn't just give me your name. They gave me a reason. They know who your father was. They've known for years. And Halloway's brief said—" she hesitated, and I watched her decide, deliberately, to stop protecting me, "—Halloway's brief said the son might cooperate willingly, *given his history*. Given that the last thing Elias Verrin would ever want is for that ledger to surface under his real name."

The fire crackled. The rain hammered. Somewhere inland, faint but unmistakable, came a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat: a deep, structural groan of timber and stone under hydraulic load, like the earth clearing its throat.

The trestle at the quarry's mouth had begun to complain.

"They're not just coming for the vault," Wren said, rising, one hand pressed to her bad shoulder. "Xanthe—they're coming whether we lead them to it or not. And I don't think they intend to leave the cliff standing when they go."
