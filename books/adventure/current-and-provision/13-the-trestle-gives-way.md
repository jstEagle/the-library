## 13. The Trestle Gives Way

The trestle announced its intentions the way old men announce theirs: with a groan at four in the morning that neither of us pretended to sleep through.

I had been awake anyway. The quarry cutting had turned into a brown lake overnight, and the water had opinions about everything—about the boat we'd dragged up the slope and left half-sunk in the reeds, about the fire Wren was trying to coax out of sodden coal dust, about me. Mostly it seemed to have opinions about me.

"Listen," Wren said.

"I'm listening."

"No, actually listen." She cocked her head, and I did too, and beneath the rain's static there it was: a deep, structural complaint coming from the direction of the rail trestle that spanned the quarry mouth—the one we'd sheltered under like a bridge of civilization, as if a bridge meant anything anymore. It was a lattice of riveted iron, older than either of us, built to carry ore wagons across the gap when this quarry still had an economy attached to it. Now it carried nothing but our fire, our boat, and two liars drying their socks over coals.

"That's not settling," she said. "That's moving."

"It's iron. Iron doesn't move."

"Everything moves. You taught me that. You spent three days teaching me that."

She had me there. Down the drowned track bed, navigating culverts by my own hand-drawn maps while the monsoon's first bands came in sideways, I'd delivered a lecture on the subject of permanent way—that nothing on a railway is permanent, that every rail creeps, every ballast shifts, every structure is just a slow argument between steel and gravity that gravity eventually wins. She'd listened with the particular attention of someone memorizing things for later use. At the time I'd taken it for professional courtesy. I was beginning to take less of her at face value, which was progress of a kind.

The groan came again, longer this time, and under it a sound like gravel being swallowed.

I got up and went to the edge of the overhang where we could see the trestle against the grey churn of the morning. The floodwater below it had risen past the third cross-brace overnight. Where the water met the stone abutment on the far side, it wasn't flowing past—it was going *into*, pouring through some cavity in the quarry wall with a sound like a drain the size of a cathedral. And the abutment, I realized, was not stone. Not all of it. It was stone facing packed over fill, and fill, when you soak it for eighteen hours, becomes soup.

"The footing's washing out," I said. "The far pier is standing in porridge."

"Can it fall?"

I looked at the span, at the angle of the water, at the scarp slope above us where the cutting walls were already shedding ribbons of clay. "It can fall toward us or away from us. Fifty-fifty."

"You're an engineer. Can't you be more precise?"

"I'm an engineer of *closed lines*, Wren. Precision is what happens to other people."

But I was already moving, because fifty-fifty is not odds you sit under. We kicked dirt over the fire, hauled the boat higher on its rope, and started gathering what mattered, which took no time at all: her satchel, my map roll wrapped in oilcloth, the brass plate from the strongroom door that she refused to leave behind and I refused to ask about again, a tin of biscuits, the lamp, the axe. Everything else we owned fit in a biscuit tin too, spiritually speaking.

The third groan was different. It had a crack in the middle of it, a sound like a tooth giving up.

"Run," I said, and we ran—not away from the trestle, which would have been sensible if the cutting hadn't been a dead end, but along it, upstream along the quarry rim toward the old incline path I'd marked on my survey two years ago and never expected to need. Behind us the sound arrived that I will hear in my sleep for the rest of my life: not an explosion, not a crash, but a long tearing sigh, iron unzipping itself from iron, and then the impact, and then the water rising up through the gap like something let out of a room.

I didn't look back. Looking back is how people drown. I grabbed Wren's good arm and pulled her up the incline path, boots skating on clay gone to grease, until the ground leveled into heath and the rain could hit us from all sides equally, which felt almost democratic after the quarry's bad manners.

We stopped at the top, gasping. Below, the trestle lay folded into the flood like a dropped accordion, and the water was already spreading wider, finding new channels, doing what water does when you give it a railway's worth of room.

"The boat," Wren said.

"Gone. Or buried. Either way, not ours anymore."

"So we walk."

"We walk."

* * *

Walking, in a monsoon, on a peninsula whose roads are mostly suggestions, is a discipline. The first hour I navigated by memory and gradient—the land here tilts inland, so water runs inland, so you keep to the spine of the ridge where the ground is oldest and meanest. The second hour Wren stopped talking, which worried me more than any sound the trestle had made. By the third hour she was listing to starboard, and when I put my hand on her shoulder to steady her she made a noise she tried very hard not to make, and failed.

"How long," I said.

"Since the cave."

"Since the *cave*? That was—" I did the arithmetic and didn't like the total. Two days of hauling, bailing, climbing, running, on a shoulder that had taken the door frame's full opinion when the first water came through. "Why didn't you say?"

"Because you'd have slowed down. Because I've worked with worse. Because—" She stopped, jaw tight, rain running off her chin. "Because I don't know how to be the one who needs carrying. It hasn't come up."

"Well, congratulations. It's come up."

I got her arm across my shoulders and took her weight, which was less than I expected and more than she admitted. Her satchel I slung across my own chest, and inside it, against my ribs, I could feel the brass plate's cold rectangle, and I chose not to think about what Corvane's people would pay for it, or what they'd do to get it, because thinking about that required believing we'd live to see them.

The ridge path gave onto an old cart track, and the cart track gave onto something better: rails. A branch line, single track, weed-choked but intact, curving inland through gorse and wind-bent pines. I knew it before I saw the milepost. I'd walked it in a dry October three years ago and noted in my book: *Ferrymouth branch, 4 miles 22 chains, track fair, traffic nil, last train unknown.*

"Ferrymouth," I said aloud, and Wren lifted her head.

"There's people there?"

"Some. Fewer every year, according to the census, and the census flatters them. There's a chapel, a kitchen that feeds whoever shows up, and a station building somebody refuses to let fall down. I surveyed through once. They fed me mackerel and asked me nothing, which was the finest hospitality I've ever received."

"Asked you nothing?"

"Not one question. In this part of the world, questions are a bill you present later. They don't present bills."

We followed the line. Rails are honest company in a flood—they go where the ground is sound, because that's where the builders put them—and by late afternoon the rain slackened from malicious to merely biblical, and the trees opened, and there it was: Ferrymouth, a scatter of slate roofs around a tidal inlet, half its windows dark, the other half glowing with the stubborn amber of paraffin lamps. The station building stood at the head of a lane, paint gone to bare wood in stripes, and beside it a signal post with its arm frozen at caution, which struck me as the most truthful piece of public signage I'd ever encountered.

A woman was nailing boards over a window. She looked up at us—two drowned rats, one of them limping, both of them carrying nothing visible but trouble—and she put down her hammer.

"You'll be off the water," she said. Not a question. See?

"The water's off us, more like," I said.

"Mm." She looked at Wren's shoulder, at the way she held it, and then at me, and whatever she concluded she kept. "Netty!" she called toward the station, without raising her voice much, and a girl of perhaps nine appeared in the doorway as if she'd been waiting there all day for exactly this errand. "Boil water. And tell Tam we'll want the big kettle." Then, to us: "In. Before you're washed down the hill and I have to fish you out twice."

* * *

The station waiting room had become, by the slow alchemy of abandonment and necessity, a kind of common room: benches pushed together, a stove that had clearly never been allowed to die, walls papered with tide tables going back decades and children's drawings of boats, most of the boats smiling. Netty—small, ferocious, hair cut with what I suspected was garden shears—took one look at Wren and appointed herself medical staff.

"Sit," Netty commanded, pointing at a bench with the authority of a harbourmaster. Wren, who had stared down salvage syndicates and flooded caves, sat.

The woman with the hammer—I learned her name was Maren, and that she was, in the absence of anyone else claiming the job, the closest thing Ferrymouth had to a mayor—brought a basin, a length of clean linen, and a bottle of something that smelled like it could strip paint or disinfect a wound, possibly both. She examined Wren's shoulder with hands that had clearly set bones before, probably fish bones, possibly human ones, and pronounced it "not broke, but angry."

"It's been angry for two days," Wren said through her teeth as Maren bound it.

"Then it's had time to form a grievance. Hold still."

Tam arrived with the big kettle and turned out to be seventy if he was a day, with forearms like mooring rope and the unhurried manner of a man who has survived several storms by refusing to be impressed by them. He fed the stove, looked at me, and said, "Railway man."

It wasn't a question either. This village had a strict policy on questions.

"Surveyor," I said.

"Mm. Walked the branch a few years back. Ate my mackerel."

"You remember that?"

"I remember everyone who eats my mackerel. It's how I count my friends." He poured tea into enamel mugs and handed them round. "Line's dead now. Flood took the trestle at the quarry, I expect. Took the ferry road too, and the low fields won't drain for a week. You'll stay."

Not *can* you stay. Not *would* you like to. *You'll stay.* I found, to my considerable surprise, that arguing did not even occur to me. Fifteen years of arranging my life so that no one could make claims on me or issue me instructions, and one sentence from a man with a kettle dismantled the whole apparatus.

"We can't pay," Wren said. It came out stiff, the words of a woman reading from a contract she hated.

Maren snorted. "Pay. What would you pay with? Everyone here is rich in the same thing, which is nothing, and poor in the same thing, which is also nothing. You'll work instead. There's boards to nail, water to haul, and Tam's roof lost slates in the night. That's the whole economy. Welcome to it."

Netty, who had finished supervising Wren's bandage to her own satisfaction, climbed onto the bench beside me and studied my map roll, which I'd set down to free my hands.

"What's that?"

"Maps."

"Of what?"

"Lines. Railway lines. Ones nobody uses."

Her eyes went wide with the particular hunger of a child shown a secret. "Are there *treasure* ones?"

"Netty," Maren said, in the tone of a woman who has had this conversation before.

"They're just surveys," I said. "Depths, gradients, culverts. Nothing treasure."

But Netty had already spotted the brass plate sticking out of Wren's satchel where the flap had come loose, and she reached for it with both hands, reverent, the way children reach for things they've only heard about in stories told for tourist coin.

"That's from the cave," she whispered. "That's from the payroll vault. Da says the men who went in for it are still down there, holding it shut."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when a child says the true thing everyone else has agreed not to say. Wren and I looked at each other across the stove, and I watched her decide, visibly, to say nothing, and I decided the same, and the silence between us was becoming a country we shared.

"Da says a lot," Maren said briskly, taking the plate gently from her daughter and handing it back to Wren. "He also says the moon's made of kelp. Eat your tea."

* * *

They put us in the old stationmaster's office upstairs, which had a cot, a chair, a window with a view of the flooded inlet, and a smell of coal tar and old paper that I found more restful than any perfume ever devised. Rain ticked on the slate above. Somewhere below, the inlet gnawed at its new shoreline.

Wren sat on the cot with her arm strapped across her chest, looking out the window at the water, and for a long time neither of us spoke. Then she said, "They didn't ask."

"No."

"I had answers ready. Three versions. I've been carrying three versions since the cave, and they just—" She shook her head. "They handed me tea."

"That's how it works here. You'll get used to it or it'll ruin you."

"Which happened to you?"

I considered lying, out of habit, and found I didn't have the energy for it. "Ask me at journey's end," I said. "Those were the terms. One question, answered honestly. You haven't spent yours yet."

"I'm saving it."

"Sensible. Interest accrues."

She almost smiled, then winced, then settled for something in between. Outside, the last light went off the water, and down the lane a lantern moved from house to house as the villagers checked on one another, door to door, light to light, a chain of small attentions holding the dark off.

I should have slept. Instead I lay awake listening to the rain and doing the thing I always did, the thing that had kept me alive and alone for fifteen years: counting exits, weighing risks, mapping the ways tomorrow could go wrong. The trestle was gone, which meant the quarry road was gone, which meant Corvane's people—if they were coming—would come by sea or by the coast road, and the coast road, I remembered from my survey, crossed the estuary on a causeway that would be under six feet of water for days. We were sealed in here with these people, these mackerel-counting, question-refusing, board-nailing people, sealed in with our secrets like a vault ourselves.

And the strange thing, the thing I turned over and over as the rain kept on, was that it didn't feel like a trap. It felt like the opposite of a trap. It felt like the first room in fifteen years I hadn't built myself.

Near midnight, footsteps on the stairs, soft. Maren's voice through the door, low, not expecting an answer:

"Lamp's lit in the kitchen if you wake hungry. And whatever it is you two are carrying—it can wait till morning. Most things can. The ones that can't, we'll deal with together."

Then her steps went back down, and the lantern light passed under the door and faded, and Wren said quietly, from the cot, "Xanthe."

"Yes?"

"When the storm breaks—when we can move—there's something I have to tell you. All of it. Before Corvane finds us, because they *will* find us, and you deserve to hear it from me first."

I stared at the ceiling, at the water-stain shaped like a coastline, and thought about the brass plate in her satchel, and the inspector number stamped on a strongroom door, and the name I hadn't spoken in fifteen years.

"Morning's fine," I said. "We've got a village between us and the world tonight. Let's let it hold the line a few more hours."

Below us, the flood rose against Ferrymouth's seawall, patient as arithmetic. And somewhere out in the dark, I couldn't help feeling, something else was rising too—something with a polite face and a contract in its pocket, already counting the hours until the water went down.
