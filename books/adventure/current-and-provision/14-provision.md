## 14. Provision

The flood did not arrive. It *settled*, the way a debt settles on a household—gradually, then all at once, then for weeks.

By the third morning in Ferrymouth, the village had become an archipelago. The lower lane was a brown canal running fast between the stone walls of houses; the harbor had climbed its own stairs and taken up residence in the fish cellars; and the chapel, being built on the only high ground that wasn't already spoken for by graves, had become everything at once—dormitory, infirmary, meeting hall, and, most importantly to me, kitchen.

I woke on a pallet among nine strangers to the smell of woodsmoke and onions, and lay there a moment doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the trestle came down: no road out, no rail out, no boat that could live in that water, and somewhere behind us, upstream of us, *inside* us now, Corvane's people asking their polite questions.

Beside me, Xanthe slept like a man who'd been hit with something soft but heavy. His maps were under the pallet, wrapped in oilcloth, because he'd learned somewhere in his vanished life never to let them be more than an arm's reach away. His injured hands were curled loosely against his chest. Even asleep he looked like a man keeping books.

A child's face appeared over the edge of my pallet. Small girl, maybe seven, hair cut short in the practical way of children whose mothers have no patience for lice, eyes the flat gray of the sea that morning.

"You're the one what fell off the bridge," she said.

"Technically the bridge fell off *under* me," I said.

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Tam says you screamed."

"Tam is a liar and also I did not."

"He screamed too. Louder." She stuck out a hand, palm first, the way children do when they've decided the interview is over and friendship has begun. "I'm Bree. That's my gran's stove you're sleeping next to. Don't touch it, it bites."

I shook the small hand. Somewhere in the last ten days I had been shot at by circumstance, drowned twice, betrayed once—by myself, mostly—and here was a seven-year-old assigning me seating etiquette. It was, absurdly, the most normal conversation I'd had since I'd walked into a signal box and lied to a railroad engineer about being an archivist.

"Bree," said a woman from the kitchen doorway, flour to her elbows, "leave them be."

"I'm *helping*."

"You're collecting gossip."

"Same thing," said Bree, with total conviction, and withdrew to report elsewhere.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Ferrymouth before the flood: nothing. Here is what I knew after three days: how many people it held (sixty-one, forty-one of them sheltering in the chapel and the two stone houses above the waterline), where the well was (behind the smithy, and you pumped it eight times before it gave clean water), and that Mrs. Callow—the flour-elbowed woman, Bree's grandmother—ran the communal kitchen with the gentle tyranny of a woman who has fed a fishing fleet through four bad seasons.

And here is what I learned about Xanthe Thackeray, which was more than I'd learned in all our weeks of wary chess:

He couldn't stop working.

It was in him the way the tide was in the peninsula. On the first morning he was hauling sandbags before anyone asked. By noon he'd found the village's one hand-pump cart and re-shimmed its axle with a piece of scrap he picked up off the midden. By evening he was up on the chapel roof re-lashing loose slates in wind that wanted him dead, while Mrs. Callow stood below shouting up instructions and insults in equal measure, both delivered with affection.

"You'll fall and crack your head and I'll have to feed you through a straw," she called.

"Noted," he called back.

"And mind the north gutter, it's been loose since the Coronation!"

He minded the gutter. Of course he minded the gutter. He was a man who kept maps of lines nobody rode; of course he would fix a gutter nobody thanked him for.

I watched him from the doorway with my arm strapped across my chest—the shoulder Wren Odile had very nearly lost to the quarry collapse, along with, I was beginning to suspect, several other things—and I felt the thing I had been paid not to feel, which was guilt. Not the sharp kind. The slow kind. The kind that soaks in like damp into plaster and doesn't show until the wall comes down.

Because here was the truth I hadn't said yet, the one I'd fed him in fragments by firelight in the quarry: I worked for Corvane. I had been sent to find him. And every hour I spent watching this man fix gutters for people who didn't know his real name, I understood a little better exactly what my employers had sent me to take.

Not the payroll. The payroll was long gone; we both suspected it by now. What Corvane wanted was the vault's *contents*—the paper, the ledgers, whatever the wreck had sealed away that made a salvage syndicate spend this much money on a rumor. And what they'd sent me to take, specifically, was the man who could open it, and the man's silence, and the man's past, packaged neatly for disposal.

They hadn't told me that part in those words. They'd told it in the language corporations use, which is designed so you can sign it without reading it. But I'd read it now. I'd read it in Halloway's name appearing in Xanthe's story, and in the fact that someone had known enough to give them *mine*.

* * *

On the fourth day the rain eased to a gray drizzle and the village exhaled just enough to do its work. Boats were bailed and turned. Nets were mended in the chapel aisle between the pallets, old men arguing knots with the serenity of bishops. And the kitchen became the center of the world, as kitchens are.

I peeled potatoes. It was the first useful thing my shoulder allowed, and Mrs. Callow supervised my technique with the air of a woman grading an exam I was failing.

"You hold it like it owes you money," she said.

"It does owe me money."

"Everything owes everyone money, love. Peel it like you forgive it."

Xanthe sat at the end of the bench, hands wrapped around a mug of tea he wasn't drinking, watching the room with that surveyor's habit of his—cataloguing exits, load-bearing walls, the depth of the water in the lane beyond the window. Except that today, for the first time since I'd known him, he was also cataloguing something else. People. He'd stopped pretending he wasn't part of the room.

Bree solved the problem of him by simply refusing to acknowledge that he was a stranger. She appeared at his elbow with a broken clockwork train—a tin thing, wheels gone, key snapped—and set it in front of him like a summons.

"It doesn't go," she said.

"No," he agreed.

"Gran says you fix things."

"Gran says a great deal."

"She says you fixed the pump cart and the roof and Tam's dad's winch brake, and Tam's dad says nobody fixes a winch brake like that unless they drove engines." She leaned in, lowering her voice to a stage whisper audible in the next parish. "*Did you drive engines?*"

I watched him weigh the lie. I watched him decide, visibly, not to tell it—not the whole one, anyway.

"I've driven a few things," he said.

"Fix it, then." She tapped the train. "You can't say no, you're the engineer. Gran says you're the engineer. Everybody says."

And that was how Xanthe Thackeray, the man who had spent fifteen years erasing himself from every record and every memory, became the personal property of a seven-year-old. He took the tin train apart on the bench with a penknife and a jeweler's patience while Bree narrated its history (it had belonged to her father, who was on the boats, and it had fallen in the water, which explained the rust, which she described in forensic detail), and I watched his shoulders come down half an inch for the first time since I'd met him.

He fixed it. Of course he fixed it. When the little mechanism turned over and the wheels spun, Bree shrieked loud enough to startle the nets, threw her arms around his neck, and announced to the entire kitchen: "He's MY engineer. You can borrow him but you have to give him back."

Mrs. Callow caught my eye across the room. She had the look of women who miss nothing, which in villages is a survival trait.

"You two running from something?" she asked mildly, peeling beside me.

"The weather," I said.

"Mm," she said, in the tone of a woman filing a document she'll produce later.

* * *

It was Bree, again, who brought the warning—though she didn't know it was one.

Fifth day. Rain back in earnest, the lane a river again, the whole village pressed indoors and simmering gently. Bree came pelting into the chapel, dripping, full of news the way children are full of stones they've pocketed.

"There's men at the Harrow," she reported to the room at large. "Three of 'em. Come before the flood closed the coast road, they're stuck same as us. One's got a motorcar up on blocks at the inn. Tam says it's got brass lamps. *Brass.*"

Something went cold in me, precise and specific, like a key turning.

"Did they say what they wanted?" I asked. Carefully. Casually. A person asking about strangers.

Bree shrugged. "Fisherman talk. They ask after the cave, Tam says. And—" she screwed up her face, retrieving it— "they showed a picture round. Of a man. Tam's dad saw it. Said it looked a bit like—" she pointed, cheerful, oblivious, at the man repairing her father's clockwork train— "*him*. Only older in the picture. Or younger. Tam says younger."

The kitchen noise went on—pots, argument, rain—but for me it receded, the way sound goes when you're underwater.

I looked at Xanthe. He had gone very still over the tin train, the penknife paused mid-motion, and I knew he'd heard every word, because Xanthe always heard every word; it was one of the things I'd liked about him, back when liking things was still permitted by my contract.

"They ask after the cave," Bree repeated, bored with us now, already turning away. "And there's a lady with them. Tam says she's got a city coat. Tam says—"

"That's enough, love," Mrs. Callow said quietly, and Bree went, mercifully, to torment someone else.

For a long moment neither of us spoke. The rain filled the silence for us.

"Advance team," I said finally. "Halloway wouldn't come himself. Not through this. He'd send people ahead to map the ground, buy the goodwill, find you." I swallowed. "Find us. The photograph—that's mine. My file photo. Corvane keeps files on its contractors. They'd have used both of ours."

Xanthe set down the penknife. He looked at the tin train, its little wheels spinning slowly to a stop, and when he spoke his voice was level in the way I'd learned meant danger, though not to me. Not yet.

"How long," he said, "before they stop asking politely?"

I thought about Halloway's polished patience, the syndicate's money, the cliff line above the cave, and the demolition charges I knew Corvane's marine division kept catalogued and ready for exactly this kind of inconvenience.

"Soon," I said. "And Xanthe—they won't come through the front of the cave. They'll come through the cliff. It's faster, and they don't care what falls."

He nodded slowly, and I watched him do the thing he did—turn from a man in a warm kitchen back into a surveyor, running lines and loads and failure points behind his eyes. But this time, when he looked up, he wasn't looking at exits.

He was looking at the sixty-one people of Ferrymouth, and the chapel roof he'd lashed, and the child's train in his hands, and the water rising in the lane.

"They're sitting on top of it," he said quietly. "The whole village. If that cliff goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong," I said. "That's the problem. It'll go exactly the way Corvane calculates it. They just don't include people in the calculation."

Mrs. Callow set down another basket of potatoes beside us, having heard precisely none of that or precisely all of it, impossible to say. "More peeling," she said. "Whatever you two are planning, plan it fed."

And outside, under the rain, up the hill at the Harrow Inn, three strangers with a brass-lamped motorcar were showing a photograph of the man who had just become a village's personal engineer—and waiting, patient as tide, for the water to drop.
