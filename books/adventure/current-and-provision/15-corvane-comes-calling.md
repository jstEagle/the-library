## 15. Corvane Comes Calling

The rain had settled into its long argument with the peninsula by the fourth day, and I had settled into the role of a man who fixed things for people who fed him. There were worse trades. I'd held several.

I was on the roof of the chapel annex that morning, re-laying slates that the wind had peeled back like orange rind, when I saw the boat come up the flooded street. Not a village boat — the village boats were dories and punt hulls, patched and honest about their age. This was a rigid inflatable with an outboard worth more than the whole harbor fleet, and it carried two men in matching oilskins that had never seen a working day.

They moored at the chapel steps because the chapel steps were now a quay, which told you everything about how the week was going. The taller of the two looked up at me on the roof with the expression of a man checking an item off a list, and I felt the particular cold that comes down the spine when you realize your anonymity has been a lease, not a deed, and someone has come to buy out the property.

"Mr. Thackeray," he called up, pleasantly. "Or should I say—"

"Don't," I said.

He smiled as if I'd passed a test. "Halloway. Corvane Salvage Syndicate. Might I trouble you to come down? I promise this is a conversation, not an abduction. Abduction is expensive and the paperwork is appalling."

There was no version of refusing that kept him from finding me again, so I came down. Wren was in the kitchen below, elbow-deep in a flour sack, helping Marta Osei stretch the village's remaining yeast into another week of bread. She saw my face before she saw his, and her hands went still in the dough. She knew. Of course she knew. She'd been waiting four days for this knock, and I could see her deciding whether to come up swinging or stay very small by the oven.

She stayed by the oven. Her eyes never left Halloway.

* * *

We talked in the vestry, because it was the only room in Ferrymouth with a door that latched, and Halloway appreciated doors. He took off his hat, set it precisely on the table, and sat down like a man visiting a solicitor about a will. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with the kind of face that had been arranged to be forgettable and had succeeded too well — I would struggle to describe him later, which I understood even then was the point.

"You've been busy," he said. "The photographs. The brass plate. Very thorough work. My compliments to Ms. Odile — she was always good at getting close to difficult men."

"She's not yours anymore."

"We'll see." He produced a folder from inside his coat and laid it flat between us without opening it, the way you'd lay down a card you wanted me to stare at. "Here is what I know, Mr. Thackeray, so we can skip the part where you pretend otherwise. You found the cave. You entered it. You photographed a strongroom door bearing the crest of the old Coastal & Peninsula Railway and the inspector number 4-1-1-7."

My hands were in my pockets, which is where they go when they want to do something stupid. "That's a lot of numbers for a man who promised conversation."

"The number interests us greatly. Because the man who held it — Inspector Edmund Thackeray — signed off on the track certification the night the payroll train went through the cliff tunnel and into the sea. Forty-one dead. A vault of company scrip and bullion certificates sealed behind him. And a son who changed his name at nineteen and spent fifteen years walking closed lines like a ghost haunting his own family tree." He said it all gently, almost kindly, the way a doctor delivers a diagnosis he's delivered a hundred times. "You've done a remarkable job of disappearing, Mr. Thackeray. Truly. But Corvane has been looking for Edmund Thackeray's next of kin for eleven years, and you are the only one left."

"And there it is," I said. "The offer?"

"The offer." He opened the folder at last. Inside were documents — real ones, I could tell by the weight of the paper and the boredom of their formatting. "Item one: Ms. Odile's contract, terminated, effective immediately. No further obligations to the syndicate, no consequences for her rather creative interpretation of her assignment. She walks away clean. Item two: your history remains exactly where you buried it. Nobody publishes the name Thackeray. Nobody connects the inspector's son to the engineer on the coastal branch. Your maps, your signal box, your quiet life — all of it stays quiet. Item three—" he tapped the folder, "—the contents of the strongroom. Whatever the vault holds, Corvane takes title. In exchange, you hand over the photographs, the plate, and any notes pertaining to the site, and you never go near that cliff line again."

I looked at the papers for a long moment. Rain drummed on the vestry roof like impatient fingers.

"You're offering me my own life back," I said. "Which I notice I already had, before your archivist washed up in it."

"I'm offering you certainty. What you have now is a life that lasts exactly as long as nobody looks too hard." He leaned forward, and for the first time something moved behind the pleasant arrangement of his face — not menace exactly, but the machinery under the upholstery. "Consider Ms. Odile's position. She breached contract. Syndicates are not sentimental about that. Right now her file says *asset, reassigned*. It could very easily say something else. And consider your own file, Mr. Thackeray, which currently doesn't exist — and how much effort it would take to create one."

"There it is," I said again. "Under the politeness. Took you nine minutes. That's actually slow for a fixer; I'm told the good ones manage it in five."

Something flickered across his face — annoyance, maybe, or respect. "You've met my kind before."

"I grew up around railway directors. Same animal, better tailoring."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened inside me in that vestry, because honesty is the whole point of this account, and because what happened wasn't noble. For about ten seconds — maybe fifteen — I did the arithmetic. Wren's contract erased. My name staying dead. The past staying past. All I had to do was give up a hole in a rock full of paper fifty years old, paper that named a man I had spent my whole adult life pretending wasn't mine.

Fifteen seconds. Then I thought of Pip Osei standing on a chair in the communal kitchen the night before, solemnly appointing me Chief Engineer of Ferrymouth, an office whose entire jurisdiction was a broken hand-crank lantern and a girl's absolute confidence that I could fix anything. I thought of Marta sliding bread toward Wren without being asked. I thought of forty-one men going into the water while directors signed things in warm rooms, and my father's handwriting in a ledger I'd burned the memory of but never the fact of.

And I thought: if I take this deal, I will spend the rest of my life on closed lines again, and this time I'll know I sold the open ones.

"No," I said.

Halloway sighed, and put on his hat, and became somehow both more polite and less human. "May I ask why? Genuinely. I find it useful to understand refusals."

"Because the vault isn't money. If it were money, I might have taken your deal — I'm not romantic about other people's gold. But a strongroom sealed with an inspector's number, on a wreck the company never properly investigated, holds paper. Testimony. And testimony is the one thing Corvane can't buy twice."

He studied me the way I study a bridge with hairline cracks in the pier — not with malice, with assessment. Then he nodded slowly, and said the thing that turned the whole conversation from a negotiation into a catastrophe.

"Well. It hardly matters now." He stood, buttoned his coat. "You should know that we were never entirely dependent on your cooperation. Or on the cave mouth. The Coastal & Peninsula kept engineering surveys of that cliff — blast assessments, back in the day, when they considered quarrying the headland for the mainline cut. We bought the originals eleven years ago. There's a fault plane running behind the tunnel gallery, Mr. Thackeray, and it runs all the way up into the cliff your new friends built their village against."

The rain seemed suddenly louder, though it hadn't changed.

"You're going to blast the cave," I said.

"We're going to *access* the cave," Halloway corrected mildly. "Controlled demolition at the seaward face, once this weather gives us a window. The charge package is already aboard our ship. It's regrettable about the cliff line — geology is rarely tidy — but the syndicate's engineers assure me the risk profile is acceptable."

"Acceptable for whom?"

"For the shareholders." He picked up the folder, considered it, and left it on the table — a courtesy or a taunt, impossible to say. "The offer stands until the weather clears. After that, the terms change, and not in anyone's favor. Do think about Ms. Odile's file. Sentiment is expensive, Mr. Thackeray. I'd hate for you to keep paying for it."

He let himself out. Through the vestry window I watched him pick his way down to the inflatable, step in without a drop of water touching him, and motor away south toward the gray wall of the storm's edge, where somewhere beyond it a ship sat holding enough explosive to bring a cliff — and every house on top of it — down onto the heads of the people who had given me bread and a title and a chair by their fire.

Wren was in the doorway. She must have heard most of it; her face had the gray, stripped look of someone who has just heard her own price quoted and found it reasonable.

"He'll do it," she said. "Halloway doesn't bluff. Bluffing wastes leverage."

"I know."

"Xanthe." She crossed the room. "If they set charges on that fault plane with the ground this saturated — it won't just open the cave. The whole headland above the tunnel gallery is sitting on that fracture. Ferrymouth's lower street, the chapel, the Oseis' house—"

"I heard him." I was already pulling my survey satchel from under the vestry bench, already unrolling the cliff-section drawings I'd made in another life, when mapping derelict lines was a way of hiding and not — as it was about to become — a way of fighting. "And here's what he doesn't know, because he bought surveys from men who never walked that cliff in the rain. That fault plane doesn't run where his engineers think it runs. I've been over that headland on foot. Twice."

Wren looked at the drawings, then at me. "How much does that matter?"

"It matters," I said, "if we get to that cliff before his demolition crew does. Which means we don't have until the weather clears. We have until the eye of this storm passes — and Wren, I need you to tell me everything you remember about Corvane's ship, their boats, their schedule, and every man on that crew. Because the village is about to decide whether we fight for that rock. And I intend to give them something worth deciding with."

Outside, the rain went on hammering the roof, patient as tide, and far to the south, under all that gray, the eye of the monsoon was turning toward us like a key looking for its lock.
