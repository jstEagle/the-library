## 16. The Village Decides

I have sat through enough hostile meetings to know the anatomy of one. The folded arms. The chairs angled toward the door. The particular silence that isn't listening but *sentencing*. I'd rehearsed this room in my head a dozen times since dawn—Xanthe standing at the front of Ferrymouth's chapel hall, rain hammering the slates like an impatient creditor, and forty-odd villagers learning that the quiet surveyor who'd been sleeping in their loft was born under another name, son of the man whose sabotage killed eleven railwaymen and drowned a payroll train into legend.

What I hadn't rehearsed was Mrs. Tam bringing out the good teapot.

That was the detail that undid me, sitting on the back bench with my shoulder strapped and useless: she'd brought out the *good* teapot, the glazed one with the chip hidden under the handle, the one reserved for funerals and weddings and other occasions when people needed to be reminded they were among family. She set it down on the trestle table as if Xanthe were about to announce a betrothal instead of confess to being the son of a saboteur.

"Say it plain," old Brannick called from the front row. He'd been a fisherman for sixty years and had the face of driftwood left out in all weathers. "Whatever it is. Plain."

Xanthe stood with his hands at his sides, which is where he always put them when he wanted them not to shake. He'd laid his maps out on the table first—the whole rolled bundle of them, fifteen years of closed lines—and I understood that was deliberate. He was showing them the shape of his life before he showed them its contents.

"My name isn't Thackeray," he said.

The rain filled the pause. Nobody gasped. Ferrymouth had heard stranger openings; half the village was built by people running from something.

"It's Vane. Xanthe Vane. My father was Elias Vane, inspector of works on the coastal division." His voice stayed level the way a bridge stays level—by engineering, not by nature. "In 1911 he signed off on the cliff tunnel cuttings knowing the drainage had been falsified. The directors ordered it done cheap. He kept the ledger that proved it, and when the tunnel flooded during the wreck, eleven men drowned in water that should never have been there. The vault in the cave below—that's the payroll strongroom. And the ledger is still inside it."

He told it plainly, as asked. The wreck. The cave. Corvane Salvage and the syndicate's founders sitting on the original board that had ordered the corners cut. Halloway's offer. The demolition charge already staged somewhere above the cliff line, the one that would bring the whole headland down—*their* headland, the one Ferrymouth's eastern houses leaned against like drunks against a wall.

"I'm telling you," he finished, "because if Corvane goes ahead, the rockfall won't stop at the cave mouth. It'll take the cliff terrace with it. Six houses. The chapel roof, maybe. I helped draw the load calculations once, years ago, before I—" He stopped. Started again. "Before I became nobody. I know what that charge will do, and I know what it will do to you."

Then he did the thing I will remember until I die, or until something better replaces it, whichever comes first. He stepped back from the table. Away from the maps. Away from us.

"You owe me nothing," he said. "I came here under a false name and I've eaten your bread under it. If you want me gone before the storm season ends, I'll walk tonight, and I'll take Corvane's interest with me so they follow me and not you."

The silence afterward had texture. I could feel the village thinking, and it thought nothing like any boardroom I'd ever sat in.

It was the child who broke it—not Pip, though Pip was there, perched on her grandmother's knee with the gravity of a small duchess—but Brannick's granddaughter, sixteen and bored by catastrophe, who said, "Is the payroll still down there?"

"No," Xanthe said. "It went out with the wreck. What's down there is paper. Proof."

"Huh," she said, in the tone of someone who'd hoped for treasure and settled for justice, which is more maturity than most adults manage.

Brannick stood. It took him a while—he made no effort to hide how long—and when he finally got upright he looked at Xanthe the way you look at a knot you're deciding whether to cut or work loose.

"Elias Vane," he said slowly. "My father hauled stone for that cutting. Came home coughing red for a year after. Always said the drainage was wrong, always got told to mind his gauge." He turned the name over like a coin of doubtful metal. "So your father knew. And he wrote it down. And he hid it where the directors couldn't burn it."

"He hid it where *nobody* could reach it," Xanthe said. "Including me. For fifteen years."

"Aye, well." Brannick scratched his jaw. "Men hide things badly. It's why we have each other."

Mrs. Tam poured tea then, decisively, as if the matter were closed, and perhaps in Ferrymouth it was. But it wasn't quite, because a woman near the window—I didn't know her name yet, one of the oyster-bed families—asked the question I'd been dreading:

"And her?" A chin jerked in my direction. "She's the one Corvane sent. We've heard what she is."

Every face turned to me. Xanthe started to speak—to defend me, God help him, after everything—and I stood up, because if there was one thing I would not allow, it was being defended from my own arithmetic.

"She's right," I said. "Corvane hired me. They gave me his name"—I nodded at Xanthe—"and a contract to find the vault and keep it quiet. I lied to him on the first day we met and most days since. The only reason I'm telling you now is that they stopped paying me to be honest and started paying me to disappear people, and I found I had opinions about that." I looked around the room, at the faces lit by lamplight and low weather. "You don't owe me anything either. I'm telling you what I am so you can decide with your eyes open."

The oyster woman considered me for a long moment. Then she said, "Well. At least you said it in the chapel," and several people laughed, and something in my chest that had been clenched since the quarry loosened by a single degree.

* * *

Decisions in Ferrymouth are not taken by vote, I learned that night. They're taken by inventory.

Brannick led it, walking the hall with a slate, calling out what the village had as if reading a menu aloud. "Three seaworthy boats, four if we patch the Kestrel. Twelve men who can handle a line in that surf. The chapel archive has the old tunnel plans—originals, 1908, before the falsifications. Father Anselm keeps them under the vestment chest and pretends they're hymnals." A ripple of fond contempt ran through the room; everyone, apparently, had known about the hymnals. "And we have," he added, tapping the slate, "the only man alive who can read those plans and mean it."

He meant Xanthe. Xanthe looked like a man who'd been handed a live wire and told it was a handshake.

"You understand what you're offering," he said. "If Corvane learns you helped me, they'll come for the village too. Contracts, lawsuits, worse. Halloway doesn't lose politely."

"Son," Brannick said, "we're oyster farmers on a floodplain peninsula. Corvane's lawyers have never once been out in weather. Let them come."

It was Mrs. Tam, packing away the good teapot with ceremonial slowness, who delivered the sentence I think actually landed. "You spent fifteen years keeping a secret so heavy it bent you crooked," she told Xanthe, not unkindly. "A thing like that isn't carried. It's set down. So set it down, and pick up something useful."

Xanthe sat down heavily on the bench beside me. Up close I could see what the evening had cost him—the fine tremor in his hands now that they weren't performing steadiness, the way he breathed like a man surfacing.

"They chose you back," I said quietly. "Do you understand that? You gave them every reason to put you out, and they inventoried their boats instead."

"I heard," he said. His voice was rough. "I keep waiting for the part where it makes sense."

"It doesn't make sense," I said. "That's what makes it family. Debt makes sense. This doesn't."

He almost smiled. Then Father Anselm appeared at our elbows—a small, quick man who smelled of lamp oil and candlewax—with a key on a ribbon around his neck, and said, "The archive opens at dawn. Bring strong arms and someone who can tell 1908 ironwork from 1911 lies."

"We can do both," Xanthe said.

"I know," the priest said mildly. "I've been reading your survey notes since Tuesday. You annotate in the margins like a man arguing with the map. Only engineers and theologians do that." He patted Xanthe's arm and drifted off, leaving Xanthe staring after him with the expression of a man discovering he'd been observed by the one witness he'd never suspected: a clergyman with a taste for marginalia.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, the monsoon holding its breath between bands, and the whole village turned out to open a chest.

That sounds like nothing. It wasn't nothing. Forty people crowded into a chapel vestry the size of a galley kitchen while Father Anselm unlocked the cabinet beneath the vestments and lifted out oilcloth-wrapped bundles that had waited in the dark for thirty years. When the wrapping came off the first drawing, the smell rose up—old linen, iron-gall ink, damp stone—and Xanthe made a sound I'd never heard from him, low and involuntary, the sound of a man meeting a ghost he'd been hoping to meet.

"There," he said, finger hovering over the section drawing without touching it. "Original drainage spec. Twin culverts at the third gallery, twelve-inch bore. And here—" he pulled the later revision across, the one bearing his father's countersignature, and laid the two side by side. The lie was visible in a single glance: same tunnel, half the drainage, one signature where two belonged. "He countersigned it alone. That's not how it worked. Two inspectors, always. The second signature was forged—or he was made to sign alone so the blame would pool on him."

The vestry went very quiet.

"That means—" I started.

"That means the ledger might say so," Xanthe said. His voice had changed, gone thin and fast. "If there's a second inspector's copy, if there's correspondence, if there's *anything*—then my father might not be the man I've hated since I was nine years old. Or he might be exactly that man, and the paper will say which. Either way it's down there, behind a door keyed to his number, and Corvane is going to blow the cliff apart rather than let it be read."

Brannick cleared his throat. "Then we'd best get to it before they do. Tide turns at slack water day after tomorrow. Storm eye passes over tomorrow night—half a day of calm, no more."

"Half a day," Xanthe repeated. He was already spreading drawings across the vestry floor, kneeling among them, and villagers knelt with him—oyster farmers, net-menders, the bored granddaughter, Pip solemnly placing herself next to him as though apprenticeship had been arranged at birth. Watching them arrange themselves around his maps like iron filings around a lodestone, I felt the last of my contract die somewhere quietly inside me, unmourned.

Father Anselm touched my elbow. "And you," he said. "What will you carry?"

I looked at the room—at Xanthe surrounded by people who'd known him three weeks and chosen him anyway, at the plans of a dead man's honesty spread on a chapel floor.

"The truth," I said. "All of it, this time. In order."

"Good girl," he said, which from anyone else would have been insufferable, and from him sounded like absolution.

Outside, the harbor master was already hauling boats above the tideline, and far up the coast, faint even through the wind, came the sound I'd been dreading all morning: engines. More than one. Working their way along the drowned road toward Ferrymouth.

Corvane hadn't waited for the eye of the storm.

Neither, it seemed, would we.
