## 17. Race the Slack Water

The eye of a monsoon is a liar. It looks like mercy—wind dropping to nothing, rain thinning to a fine salt mist, the sea going flat and gray as sheet iron—and it asks you to believe the worst is over. I have read enough barometers to know better. An eye is just the storm drawing breath. The question is never whether the back half comes. The question is what you can carry through the gap.

I stood on Ferrymouth's seawall at four in the morning with a tide table in one hand and my father's ghost in the other, doing arithmetic.

"Say it again," Wren said beside me. She had her shoulder strapped tight under her coat and a lantern hanging from her good hand, and she had not slept, and neither had I, and we were both pretending that was fine.

"Slack water at the cave mouth is nine-forty," I said. "Lowest water of the cycle, and the swell's still down from the eye. We get maybe ninety minutes of workable conditions before the back bands arrive and the surge comes up the cliff face like a fist."

"And Corvane?"

"Halloway said they had a second way in. That means charges, which means they'll want calm water too. They're reading the same sky I am." I folded the tide table. "Which means we're racing them to the same door, and they have a boat with an engine that isn't held together with rail spikes and prayer."

Wren looked out at the harbor, where three boats rode at their moorings in the unnatural stillness. "Then we'd better pray louder."

* * *

The village decided faster than any committee I've ever sat in, mostly because it wasn't a committee. It was Maren Oduya, the harbormaster's widow, slapping a chart down on the chapel table and saying, "The *Kestrel's* engine is sound and I'm the only one here who can run her, so the argument's over before it starts." Beside her stood Tam Broadbelt, who was seventy if he was a day and had spent forty years cutting stone in the cliff quarries, and who brought with him a canvas roll of drills and wedges that clinked like a promise. And beside him, twelve years old and furious about being left behind, was Pip Salloway, who had adopted me as her personal engineer sometime around day two of the flood and took my departure as a professional betrayal.

"You need someone small," she argued, planting herself in the doorway. "Small people fit places."

"Pip." I crouched to her level. "If the cliff starts coming down, I need to know you're somewhere the rock can't argue with. That's not smallness. That's the most important job on this whole coast." I handed her my spare notebook. "Log everything. Times, wind, water height. When I come back I want a full survey of the flood. Can you do that?"

She glared at me with the particular hatred children reserve for adults who are right, then snatched the notebook. "Nine-forty slack," she said. "I know how to read your tables. I've been watching you write them for two days."

Of course she had. I made a mental note to be more careful about what I let children watch me do, and then another note that it was probably already too late.

Maren's crew was three, then: Maren at the wheel, Tam with his drills, Wren with her shoulder and her secrets, and me with a satchel of maps fifteen years out of date and somehow still the best survey of that coastline in existence. Every skill I had buried—culvert navigation, tidal timing, the black art of reading water over drowned track—I was about to use all at once, in front of witnesses, for a purpose. There is a strange feeling in taking tools out of a case you welded shut yourself. They are heavier than you remember. They also fit your hands exactly.

We cast off at seven-ten, into a sea so flat it felt borrowed.

* * *

The run down the drowned branch line was familiar now—the track bed running out under us, culverts rising and falling in the swell, the old mileposts standing in the shallows like men waiting for a train that stopped coming decades ago. But the eye had changed the light. Everything lay under a bruised, sourceless glow, the clouds torn open above us to show a strip of high pale sky, and the whole drowned railway looked less like a ruin than like something holding its breath.

"Talk me through it again," Wren said. She'd wedged herself against the gunwale where the motion hurt her least, and she was watching me work the chart with the expression of someone memorizing.

"The cave sits under the old cliff tunnel, thirty feet below the spring high-water line. The tunnel approach runs along a shelf of limestone that's been shedding into the sea since before the railway was built. Corvane's second way in will be from above—they'll drop charges on the shelf to bring the tunnel mouth down or open it up, depending on how careless they're feeling. Either way, the shock goes through the whole cliff face." I tapped the chart. "Tam says there's a natural fault line here—" his thick finger came down next to mine "—and if they set charges anywhere near it, they don't just close the cave. They take the headland with it. And the headland has Ferrymouth's north fields on top of it."

"So we're not racing for treasure," Tam rumbled. He was coiling rope with hands like weathered mooring posts. "We're racing for the fuse."

"That's the spirit," I said, because wryness is free and courage costs.

Wren didn't laugh. She was looking at the fault line on the chart, and then at me, and I saw her doing the same cold sum I'd been avoiding since Halloway's visit: that the ledger inside that strongroom was worth more than money now. It was worth the headland, if Corvane thought destroying it would bury their founders' names along with the payroll train. Evidence that could hang a syndicate, sealed behind a door only one number could open, sitting on a cliff they were willing to demolish.

"They won't leave witnesses either," she said quietly. "Not if the ledger burns."

"No," I agreed. "They won't."

Nobody said anything for a while after that. The engine knocked along. The drowned miles went by.

* * *

We reached the cave anchorage at eight-fifty, fifty minutes ahead of slack, and found the sea behaving exactly as the barometer promised—flat, glassy, holding its breath between storms. The cave mouth yawned at the base of the cliff, framed in rusted rail iron, dark as a throat.

And anchored off the point, half a mile south, sat a boat with a new engine and no name painted on her hull.

"There's our friends," Maren said.

I put glasses on her. Two figures on deck, working at crates. Even at this distance I knew what was in the crates. You don't spend years around railways without learning the silhouette of a demolition charge.

"They'll wait for slack too," I said. "Charges in moving water are wasted charges. So we have the same window, and they have to row farther." I turned to Tam. "How fast can you shore a fault line?"

"Fast?" He considered. "No. But I can slow it. Give me the crack and an hour and I'll pack it with wedges and timber so anything short of a direct hit spends its force sideways. Won't save the cliff. Might save the village on top of it."

"An hour's what you have. Wren—with me. We go for the vault. Maren holds the boat; if we're not out by ten-thirty, you leave."

"I don't leave passengers," Maren said.

"Maren. If the back bands hit with you sitting under that cliff—"

"I know my water, engineer. Ten-thirty. Not a minute sooner, and not because you told me—because I say so." She spat over the side in the manner of a woman concluding negotiations.

We went in by lamplight, Wren and I, with Tam's gear ferried to the shelf inside the mouth where he could work. The cave swallowed our little boat the way it always did, the echo of our oarlocks going ahead of us into the dark like a warning we chose not to hear.

* * *

Inside, we split. Tam knelt at the fault line with his wedges, tapping, listening to the stone the way a doctor listens to a chest, and began his slow argument with the cliff. Wren and I took the lamps and waded the last stretch to the wreck's forward carriage, fused into the rock these sixty years, barnacled and black, its windows long gone, its seats furred with white coral growth. Past it, the strongroom door stood in its frame of living stone, stamped with the railway crest and, beneath it, the inspector's number.

My father's number. 4471. I had known it would be there. Knowing did nothing at all.

"You okay?" Wren asked.

"He built this door," I said. "Or signed off on whoever did. Either way, he stood where I'm standing." I got my hands on the mechanism. "Let's find out what he was standing guard over."

The lock was a combination safe of the old pattern—five wheels, each set by a numbered dial, the kind of mechanism that outlives its makers out of sheer stubbornness. I'd studied its cousins in signal boxes all my life. And I had known, since the night I pried the brass plate off and read the stamp, what the combination would be. Not because I'd guessed it. Because I'd grown up watching the man choose numbers, and a man who hides a thing in plain sight once will hide it in plain sight forever.

4-4-7-1, and then the year. The year of the wreck. The year he stopped sleeping.

The wheels turned with a sound like teeth. On the fifth, my hands were shaking badly enough that Wren put hers over mine—not guiding, just steadying—and together we brought the last dial home.

The door swung inward six inches and stopped on something soft.

* * *

It was not money. I think some part of me had been braced for coin, for bullion, for the glittering payoff of every legend the tavern ever sold. What blocked the door was paper. Ledgers, bundles of correspondence, oilcloth-wrapped and swollen with damp but intact, packed floor to ceiling in a room barely bigger than a coffin. The payroll itself was gone—there was a single empty cash box with its lid open, and a receipt clipped to it, dated three days *before* the wreck. The money had been moved out in advance. The train had been sent down the coast empty except for its crew, and its crew had not been told.

Wren lifted the receipt out with both hands, careful as a relic. "'Authorized disbursement, per directive of the Board,'" she read. Her voice had gone very flat. "Xanthe. They emptied it first. The wreck was—"

"A performance," I said. "With a body count." My father's confession was in the second bundle, in his upright clerk's hand, and I didn't need lamplight to know what it said, because I had read a stolen copy of it when I was eleven years old. But seeing the original—the signed original, with the directors' countersignatures, the names, Corvane's founders among them, ordering the line closed by catastrophe and the loss written off as weather—that was different. A copy is a secret. This was a witness.

"We take all of it," Wren said. "Every page."

"It's heavy."

"Then we make two trips."

There was no time for two trips. Even as she said it, I felt it—a pressure change in the cave, a faint suck of air toward the entrance, the sea beginning to think about turning. And underneath it, distant but unmistakable, a sound I had hoped not to hear until it was far too late to matter.

A detonation. Muffled, miles of rock away, but the cliff carried it straight down through the stone into my boots.

Tam's voice came echoing along the tunnel, cracked with strain: "That's early! That's their first shot and it's *early*—"

Dust sifted down through the lamplight. Somewhere overhead, deep in the headland, the fault line that Tam had spent an hour arguing with began, very quietly, to disagree.

I stuffed the confession inside my coat, against my chest, where a man keeps the things that are killing him. Wren filled her satchel with ledgers. Behind us, at the far end of the cave, the water at the entrance had begun to move—not the gentle breathing of a normal turn, but a shove, a shoulder thrown against the rock, the back bands of the monsoon arriving early and angry, pushing the surge ahead of them into the cave mouth.

And from the direction of the point, across the water, came the sound of an engine starting up—Corvane's unnamed boat, coming to see what their charge had bought them.

"Wren," I said. "Run."
