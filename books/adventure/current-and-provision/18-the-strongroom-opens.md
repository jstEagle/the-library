## 18. The Strongroom Opens

The eye of the storm lasted, by the harbor master's old almanac, four hours. By my watch it lasted three and a half. By the look on Wren's face when we came out of Ferrymouth with three boats, five people, and every lamp in the chapel archive, it lasted about ninety seconds.

The sea had gone strange in the calm—flat as poured tin, a green so deep it looked like something you could fall into forever. The cliff that held the cave stood up out of it black and dripping, and above us, faint through the last ragged cloud, gulls were screaming like they'd been paid to.

"Slack water's at eleven," I said. "We go in at slack water or we don't go in at all."

"And Corvane's crew?" Wren asked.

"Corvane's crew will be waiting for their own window. Which means we have maybe an hour where nobody is trying to kill us." I paused. "Enjoy it."

Old Tammas took the tiller of the lead boat because Tammas had been taking tillers since before either of us was born and saw no reason to stop for weather. Behind us came his grandson Pell, seventeen and built like a bollard, and Marta Osei, who kept the chapel archive and had brought the original tunnel plans wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a sleeping child. The plans were older than the wreck. Older than the railway, some of them. Marta handled them the way you handle a thing that has outlived everyone who made it.

We ran the drowned track bed again, the same route Wren and I had taken weeks ago, except now the culverts I'd mapped in another life were half-silted with storm debris and the water ran brown with the land's insides. I navigated by memory and by the sound of the water over the rails—a trick I'd learned surveying closed lines, where your eyes lie to you but the rails always tell the truth. Under the hull, each joint sang a small note. *Clack. Clack.* Like the ghost of a train keeping pace with us.

Wren sat forward with her shoulder strapped and her good hand on the gunwale, watching me read the water.

"You're different out here," she said.

"Different how?"

"Less careful."

I fed the engine a little more fuel. "Out here there's nothing left to be careful of. That's why I liked these lines."

"That's the saddest thing anyone's ever said to me."

"It wasn't meant to be sad."

"I know," she said. "That's what makes it sad."

* * *

The cave mouth opened at eleven exactly, the way it had before—the tide drawing back its tongue, the rusted rail iron framing the dark like a jaw. But the storm had changed it. A section of the cliff face above had sloughed away, and fresh rock showed pale as bone behind the old black stone, and the smell coming out of the cave was different too: wet iron and wet chalk and something older underneath, something like a cellar that had been shut for sixty years.

Marta crossed herself. Tammas spat over the side, which I believe is the maritime equivalent.

"Everyone stays within arm's reach of me," I said. "The floor inside isn't a floor. It's the roof of a lower gallery, and parts of it are paper. You step where I step or you don't step."

"Charming host," said Pell.

"He's worse at home," Wren said, and something in my chest did a thing I decided not to examine.

We went in by lamplight, five flames in a throat of stone. The forward carriage was still there, still fused to the rock where the sea had welded it sixty years ago—iron gone to lace, the windows long since eaten through, and inside it, when I raised my lamp, the shapes that the sea had not been permitted to take back. The villagers knew the story. They'd told it for tourist coin until the tourists stopped coming. But knowing a story and standing inside it are different animals entirely.

Nobody spoke. Even Pell.

The strongroom door was where I'd left it, set back in the rock past the carriage, iron gone the color of dried blood, stamped with the crest of the colonial railway and beneath it, small and neat as a signature, the inspector's number.

**4471.**

My father's number. The number he'd recited like a prayer every morning of my childhood, tapping his badge on the table at breakfast. *Four-four-seven-one, lad. A man is only as good as what he signs.*

I stood in front of it and the past reached through the stone and took me by the collar, the way it had the first time, and this time I let it.

"Xanthe." Wren's voice, low. Not touching me. Giving me the room.

"The lock is a combination mechanism," I heard myself say, in my surveyor's voice, the one that reports facts. "Colonial pattern. Five wheels. The factory default would have been changed on installation. The only person who'd know the setting is whoever sealed it."

"Your father," Marta said quietly.

"My father sealed it. He was the inspector of record. Whatever happened on this line—" I put my hand flat against the cold iron. "Whatever he did, he signed it. He'd have used the only key he trusted."

"Which is?" Pell asked.

"Himself."

I turned the first wheel. 4. The second. 4. Third, 7. Fourth, 1. My hands were steady, which surprised me, because nothing else was. On the fifth wheel I stopped.

Because here is the thing I hadn't told them, hadn't told Wren, had barely told myself in fifteen years of erasure: I didn't know if I wanted the door open. Every version of my life I'd built—the maps, the closed lines, the name like a coat turned inside out—all of it rested on that door staying shut. Open it, and the boy who found the ledger in the attic would finally finish what he'd started. Leave it shut, and I could go on being no one, safely, forever, in the company of people who were rapidly becoming impossible to be no one around.

Wren stepped up beside me. Close enough that her sleeve brushed mine.

"Truth as the only key," she said. "You told me that once. In the quarry, when you were pretending you weren't crying, which you absolutely were."

"I don't cry."

"You wept like a drainage ditch."

"That was condensation."

"Turn the wheel, Xanthe."

I turned it. 4-4-7-1-4. The last digit was the first. A loop. A man signing his own name at both ends.

Somewhere inside the door, sixty years of salt and silence let go all at once. The mechanism clunked through its wheels like a slow heartbeat finding its rhythm, and then the bolt drew back with a sound I felt in my teeth, and the door swung inward on hinges that someone, once, had thought to grease.

Cold air came out. Cellar air. Air from a room that had been holding its breath since before I was born.

* * *

The payroll was gone.

I want to be clear about that, because everything else follows from it. The strongroom was supposed to hold the quarterly wages for six hundred men—gold and notes and the scrip the company paid instead of money when money was inconvenient. Sixty years of legend had grown fat on it. Men had drowned in the flood tide outside this very door chasing it. And the shelves inside were empty except for dust, a dead rat curled like a comma, and a row of ledger boxes that had clearly never contained a single coin.

"They cleared it," Wren said. Her lamp swept the shelves. "Before the wreck. Or—"

"Or it was never here." I lifted down the first box. It was heavy, but not gold-heavy. Paper-heavy. "Look at the shelving. Reinforced for weight, braced, load-rated. This room was built to hold treasure. And then somebody filled it with paper instead."

Marta leaned in, her lamp close to the box in my hands. "What is it?"

It was the sabotage ledger. My father's hand—I'd know it anywhere, that upright engineer's script, each letter standing at attention—and it was not a confession hidden in the margins. It was the whole account, page after page, dated, initialed, witnessed. The trestle cuts. The signal failures. The ballast pulled from the cliff-side curve two nights before the train was due. And beside each entry, in a second hand, a third, a fourth—signatures. Approvals. Countersignatures.

I read the names and the cave went quiet around me in a new way.

Because the signatures belonged to directors. Board members of the colonial railway itself. And among them, in ink still sharp enough to cut, were two names that Wren read aloud before I could stop her, her voice going flat and strange:

"Corvane. *Corvane Holdings*, predecessor interest. Here, and here, and here."

The syndicate that had hired her. The syndicate whose fixer had offered to erase her contract and bury my past. The founders of Corvane Salvage had signed off on the wreck of the payroll train—had ordered it, funded it, insured against it, and collected. The vault was never robbed. The vault was a stage set. The payroll was moved out ahead of time, the train was sent into the sea, the dead were written off as losses, and the company collected on its own murder.

And my father—the saboteur, the monster of the legend, the reason I'd buried my name under a rock on a closed branch line—my father had done the cutting, yes. Had laid the charges and pulled the ballast with his own hands. But he had done it under orders, and then he had done the one thing none of the directors expected a hired hand to do.

He'd written it all down. Every entry countersigned by the men who gave the orders. And he'd locked it in the strongroom with their own crest on the door, behind a combination that was his own signature, and he'd walked into the flood tide after it was sealed, and the legend said he died reaching for gold.

He died making sure the truth had a witness. He'd just assumed the witness would be the sea.

"Oh," Marta said softly, reading over my arm. "Oh, you poor man. All these years."

"I thought he was a monster," I said. My voice came out sanded flat. "I found the ledger when I was twelve. Half of it. The pages with the cuts and the charges. No signatures. I built my whole life on half a document."

Wren took the box from my hands, gently, the way you take a weapon from someone before they hurt themselves with it. "And the other half was here the entire time. Waiting."

"Behind a door only he could open."

"Behind a door only *you* could open." She looked at me, and her eyes were bright and furious and kind all at once. "Fifteen years, Xanthe. Fifteen years hiding on lines nobody rides, and the answer was always your own name. Four-four-seven-one. You were the key. You were always the key."

I sat down on an empty shelf bracket, because my legs had resigned without notice, and I laughed—one short, ugly, relieved bark of a laugh that echoed off the rock and came back sounding like someone else.

"The vault was never about money," I said.

"No," said Marta, laying her oilcloth-wrapped plans aside and putting both hands on the ledger box like a woman taking possession of something in church. "It was about *this*. It was always about this. And the sea kept it safe for you."

Tammas, who had said nothing through all of it, who had stood at the cave mouth with his lamp held high like a man keeping watch over a grave, spoke at last.

"Then we take it out," he said. "All of it. Every page. And we read it out loud in the village square, and we nail it to the chapel door if we have to, and no Corvane man alive stops us."

"We'll need copies," Marta said practically, already unwrapping her plans. "Inquiry-grade copies. Photographs, Wren—you still have the camera?"

"Waterproof case. Never left my side."

"Then we work fast. Slack water won't hold."

It didn't. We worked with lamps propped on brackets and hands moving quick and careful, Wren photographing page after page while Marta logged them against the archive index and Pell ferried boxes to the boats, and I stood in the middle of my father's strongroom reading his confession—the full confession, the one that ended not with excuses but with a single line, underlined twice:

*I sign this so that my son, if he ever reads it, will know the difference between what a man does and what a man is made to do. Four-four-seven-one. Both ends of the rope.*

Both ends of the rope. The loop in the combination. He'd known. Somehow, in that last year, he'd known there'd be a boy someday turning these wheels, and he'd built the lock into a message: *the rope runs both ways, lad. Pull it.*

I folded the page along its old crease and put it inside my coat, against my chest, where Marta's plans had ridden.

And that was when Pell, out at the cave mouth on loading detail, shouted down the throat of the stone in a voice cracked clean in half:

"Tide's turning! Tide's turning early—and there's a boat coming round the point! Two boats! Corvane colors!"

Wren's head came up like a gun dog's. The water below us had begun its first treacherous creep across the track bed, and far out past the cliff, riding the last of the calm, two black hulls were closing fast—with the sound, faint and wrong and unmistakable, of demolition charges being passed hand to hand.

"They're not waiting for slack water," Wren said. "They're going to bring the cliff down on top of us."

I looked once more at the strongroom—empty shelves, dead rat, the whole rotten architecture of a respectable crime—and I picked up the last box.

"Then we've got everything we came for," I said. "Run."
