## 19. Collapse

The eye of the storm lasted forty minutes, and we spent thirty-eight of them running.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only penance I have left. Forty minutes of impossible calm—gulls confused into flying, the sea gone flat and gray as slate, the cliff line at Blackwater Head standing sharp against a bruise-colored sky. Xanthe had counted the window twice on his watch and said, "If we're not out by slack water plus twenty, the tide does Corvane's work for them." And then we ran, because there was nothing else to do with the time but spend it in motion.

Our team was five. Xanthe, carrying his father's number folded into oilcloth against his chest like a relic or an accusation. Me, with the rope bag and the medical kit I'd packed without admitting to myself why. Old Sef, the harbor master's brother, who had fished these waters for fifty years and treated the storm as a personal discourtesy. His nephew Tam, nineteen and built like a capstan, who had volunteered before anyone asked him to. And Marta Ellery, the chapel archivist's widow, seventy-one years old, who had looked at the tunnel plans she'd carried down from Ferrymouth and said, "I drew copies as a girl. I'll know if the shoring's wrong."

Xanthe had tried to leave her behind. She had informed him, in the tone of a woman who had buried a husband and raised four children through two floods, that the plans were in her head and her head was coming along. He hadn't argued. I think he understood something about stubbornness that day that he'd spent fifteen years refusing to understand about himself.

We came up on the cave mouth from the water side, the boat grinding over the drowned track bed one last time, and what we found there stopped even Sef mid-curse.

Corvane's demolition crew had been busy. Charges were already set along the cliff approach—the old haulage road above the tunnel, the rock face itself, the places where a century of stone held back a century of sea. Orange wire snaked down the cliff like the tentacles of some industrial anemone. A barge sat anchored off the headland, riding light, its crane folded like a sleeping insect.

"They're not waiting for the eye to pass," Xanthe said. His voice had gone flat, which I'd learned meant the opposite of calm. It meant every part of him not needed for thought had been shut down and banked. "They're using it. Blasting during the window so the rubble settles before anyone can inspect."

"Can you disarm them?" Tam asked.

"No." He said it simply, without shame. "Demolition charges aren't my trade, and half-wired detonators are how engineers die. We go in fast, we get the ledger, we shore the inner fault line like the plans say, and we are off this rock before they fire."

Marta, squinting up at the cliff: "And if they fire early?"

Xanthe looked at her, and then at me, and something passed between us that I will spend the rest of my life trying to deserve. "Then we find out how fast we really are," he said.

* * *

Inside, the cave was a cathedral that hated us.

The first water had wrecked it—silt banks reshaped, the forward carriage shifted a full meter off its resting place, the strongroom door standing open now like a pulled tooth. Xanthe had left it open when we fled, ten days and a lifetime ago. Beyond it, the vault chamber waited exactly as he'd described: dry-stacked ledgers in waxed canvas, the payroll cage empty and scoured, and on the desk bolted to the floor, under a century of dust, the confession of Inspector Edmund Thackeray.

Xanthe knelt over the canvas bundles and did not touch them for a long moment. Then he began packing them into the waterproof satchels we'd brought, working with the economy of a man who had rehearsed this in his sleep, because he had. I knew. I'd heard him talking through it in the quarry, half-dreaming, naming each step.

Tam hauled satchels. Sef kept the lamp trained on the water line, which was already creeping up the shingle with the tide's slow return. Marta stood at the inner fault—the crack in the cave wall that the old plans marked in red ink—and directed me and Xanthe in setting the timber braces we'd cut in Ferrymouth, wedging them against the rock with iron plates salvaged from the track bed.

"The syndicate wants the cliff down," she said, watching us work. "This crack is how the sea would do it. You block the sea's road, maybe the charges only make noise."

"Maybe," Xanthe said.

"You don't believe maybe."

"I believe in doing the work anyway." He drove a wedge home with the flat of his hand, then his maul. "Belief's just labor you haven't finished yet."

I loved him then. I want that on the record too, since I'm confessing everything else. Not the way the tavern songs would have it—I loved him the way you love a lighthouse, which is to say I had steered by him for weeks without ever quite admitting the light was there, and in that timber-braced dark, watching him work, I finally let myself look straight at it.

That was when the world ended, the first time.

It wasn't sound so much as pressure—a fist through the whole cave, dust falling in ropes, the lamp flame lying flat. Then a second concussion, closer, and the particular deep groan of stone deciding something about itself.

"They've fired the outer face," Sef called. "Early. God help us, they've fired early."

"Out!" Xanthe was already moving, satchel over his shoulder, counting heads the way he counted everything—fast, exact, no sentiment wasted. "Sef, Tam, Marta, Wren—out now, the water's our exit, go!"

We went. The cave mouth, when we reached it, had changed. The blast above had brought down the cliff approach, and the fall had half-choked the entrance—not sealed, but narrowed, and the sea, feeling the vibration the way a dog feels thunder, had begun to come in faster than any tide table promised. Water to our knees, then our thighs, cold enough to stop breath.

Sef went through first, sideways, cursing with the fluency of fifty years' practice. Marta followed, and I pushed her through ahead of me because she'd earned the order and would never have accepted it otherwise. Tam went third, hauling two satchels, and turned in the gap to hold the lamp high so Xanthe and I could see the opening.

Then the second collapse came.

Not the charges this time. The charges had loosened things; the sea finished the argument. A section of the cave roof behind us let go with a sound like the peninsula clearing its throat, and the rockfall came down between us and the mouth, and when the spray settled, Tam was on the wrong side of it.

I heard him before I saw him—one shout, thin and young, from somewhere behind the fresh wall of stone. The lamp he'd held was gone. The gap where he'd stood was gone. There was only rubble, black water rising through it, and a voice.

"Tam!" Sef was screaming from outside, clawing at the rockfall with bare hands. "TAM!"

Xanthe stood hip-deep in the flood, and I watched him do the arithmetic. I watched him arrive at the answer. The cave was flooding; the roof was still moving; the air behind the rockfall was a shrinking pocket; and the boy beyond it had, at best, minutes. Every sane calculation said *impossible*. Every sane calculation said *you will die too, and the ledger dies with you, and it will have been for nothing.*

Xanthe Thackeray had spent fifteen years making sane calculations. That was the whole architecture of his life—every closed line surveyed, every name changed, every risk declined, all of it one long exercise in never again being the man who went back.

He handed me the satchel.

"Hold my coat," he said, which was absurd, because I was holding a satchel of irreplaceable evidence and he was about to climb into a collapsing tomb, and coats were suddenly the currency of farewell. But I took it. I took it because refusing would have cost him three seconds he didn't have.

"Wren." He gripped my shoulder, hard, through the wet wool. "If I'm not out in ten minutes, take the ledger to the inquiry. Don't wait. Don't come in after me. Promise me."

"I don't make promises I intend to break."

"That's not—" He almost laughed, there in the drowning dark. "Fine. Then hurry me up. Light the far side if you can find a gap."

And he went up the rockfall like a man climbing his own history, hand over hand, into the black.

* * *

Here is what I did instead of panicking, and I tell it plainly because it is the only good thing I did that day that didn't belong to someone else.

I tore the lamp from its bracket, found a fissure in the new rockfall—hairline, breathing a thread of dust—and put the light to it. And then I talked. Loudly. Through the crack, into the dark, telling Xanthe where the gap breathed, telling Tam someone was there, telling the whole indifferent wilderness that it did not get to have them, that I had sold fourteen months of my life to a syndicate of vultures and I would be damned before the invoice came due in dead children and drowned engineers.

Because that was the other arithmetic, the one I'd been avoiding since a signal box in the rain. My contract named the vault. My contract said *locate and secure, discretion assured.* Nothing in it said anything about letting a village's son drown behind a wall of rock while I safeguarded paper. Corvane had hired my eyes and gotten my hands, and somewhere between the quarry firelight and this flooding dark, the remainder of me had quietly resigned.

I heard Xanthe moving inside—rock shifting, water churning, once a sound I refused to identify as a cry. Sef was hauling at the rubble from outside, screaming himself hoarse. Marta had both hands pressed to the fissure as if she could hold the cave together by prayer and friction.

Four minutes. Five. The water climbed my ribs.

Six minutes, and the rockfall moved.

Not dramatically—that's the lie the stories tell. It moved the way a patient moves when fever breaks: a grudging settling, a grind, a hand appearing through a gap no wider than a coffin lid, gripping stone, hauling. Then Tam's head, bloodied, gasping, dragged up through the closing seam by hands I couldn't see, and then Xanthe's voice, wrecked and ordinary, saying, "He's breathing. Rope. Somebody give me a rope before I change my mind about physics."

We got the rope through. We got Tam through—Sef pulling, me pulling, the sea pushing, all of it graceless and screaming and alive. And then the gap began to close, the rockfall settling into its final sentence, and Xanthe was still on the wrong side of it with the water at his chin.

"XANTHE!" I got my arm through the fissure to the shoulder. Foolish. Suicidal. The kind of thing he'd have calculated against and done anyway. "Take my arm or I swear I'll come in after you and we'll both drown and it'll be your fault for teaching me to be stubborn!"

His hand found mine in the dark. Cold, cut, certain.

"Ten minutes," he said, right against the crack, and I could hear the grin in it, the first unforced grin since the leaking boat, since terms of passage, since a stranger at a signal box who lied beautifully and meant well underneath. "You're two minutes early. I'm keeping the coat."

Then he came through—shoulders scraping, lungs burning, the sea taking its last swipe at his boots—and the rockfall sealed behind him with a boom like a door closing on a room none of us would ever enter again.

* * *

We made the boat with the eye's last minutes dying around us. The far horizon had already gone the color of a healing bruise; the wind was returning in long, low sighs, the storm turning over in its sleep before the second act. Sef ran the engine while Tam, wrapped in every scrap of canvas we owned, shook and swore and lived. Marta held the boy's head in her lap and told him, in the tone she probably used on all four children, that he would absolutely not die today, she had plans.

Xanthe sat beside me in the stern, bleeding from a dozen small wounds, holding his father's ledger against his chest with both arms, and shook. Not from cold—or not only from cold. From the sheer animal aftershock of having been, for six minutes, entirely honest with death.

"You came back for him," I said. Stupidly. Obviously.

"He came back for him," Xanthe said. "I just carried the rope." He looked at me, and the gray eyes that had spent fifteen years practicing invisibility were, for once, fully lit. "You talked me through the wall. I heard you the whole time. You sounded furious."

"I was furious."

"At the cave?"

"At the contract." I said it before I could dress it up, and once it was out, there was nothing to do but finish it. "It's ash, Xanthe. Whatever Corvane thinks I owe them—it burned in that cave. They fired early with people inside. That's not a client anymore. That's just the storm wearing a suit."

He studied me for a long moment, the way he studied track beds and tide tables, looking for the flaw in the survey.

"And when we get back," he said carefully, "and Halloway's people are asking what happened to their investment—what do I tell the village about you?"

"You tell them the truth," I said. "It seems to be catching."

The first band of the returning storm hit us a mile off Ferrymouth, rain like gravel, the sea standing up in ridges. Sef laughed into it, wild and old and unafraid, and opened the throttle, and we ran for home with the ledger dry against Xanthe's heart and a boy's pulse tapping under Marta's palm like a telegraph key sending the only message that mattered.

Behind us, Blackwater Head was missing a piece of itself, and the cave that had held a hundred years of secrets was a grave of seawater and stone.

Ahead of us, lights were coming out along the village front—not a few lights, but all of them, every window in Ferrymouth lit against the dark, the whole settlement burning bright as a signal box, as if they'd known. As if they'd been watching the headland this whole time, counting us out and counting us back.

Counting five out.

Counting five back.

Xanthe saw it too. He didn't say anything. He just put his shoulder against mine, the way timber takes a load, and we came home through the rain to people who had left every lamp burning.
