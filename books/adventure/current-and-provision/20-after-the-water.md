## 20. After the Water

I woke to the smell of bread and the sound of someone arguing about me in the third person, which is a peculiar way to learn you're still alive.

"—fever's broken, I'm telling you. Look at his color."

"His color looks like something the tide left on a piling."

"That *is* an improvement over yesterday."

I opened my eyes. The ceiling above me was whitewashed stone, cracked in a pattern like a river delta, and hung with drying bundles of herbs that swayed whenever the door opened. Ferrymouth. The chapel vestry, or what passed for one. Someone had put a quilt on me that smelled of woodsmoke and lavender and about forty years of other people sleeping under it.

Maren Kettle stood at the foot of the cot with her arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman who had personally argued with death and won on points. Beside her was old Tam Broadhurst, who had fished these waters for longer than the railway had existed and looked it.

"He's awake," Maren announced, as if reporting weather.

"Barely," said Tam.

"Awake is awake." She leaned over me. "Thackeray. Blink twice if you can hear me."

"I can hear you fine," I said. My voice came out like gravel dragged over gravel. "You're arguing about my complexion."

"We argue about everything here. It keeps the blood warm." But her hands, when she pressed them flat against my chest to check—God knows what, my ribs, my pulse, whether I was real—were gentle as anything. "Don't try to sit up. You've got water in places water has no business being, and Tam pulled you out of the channel by your collar like a drowned cat."

"You were heavier than you look," Tam said.

"Everyone says that."

* * *

Here is what they told me, in pieces, over the next day, while I lay there learning how to breathe all the way down again.

The rescue had worked. Barely. That word did a lot of lifting.

When Corvane's crew fired their early charge, the cliff approach came down in a single groaning exhalation, and the sea took the cave back the way it always takes things—completely and without apology. Wren had gotten Odell Pike out through the flooded gallery by what she later described, with characteristic understatement, as "swimming and shouting." Xanthe Thackeray—that is, I—had gone back into the collapse for Odell after Wren brought him out, because apparently I had decided somewhere in the last week that sense was optional, and had been caught by the second surge against the far wall of the tunnel mouth.

Tam had seen me go under from the boat. He told this part plainly, without drama, the way fishermen tell things: he'd run the skiff into water no sane man would enter, hooked my collar with a gaff—my collar, not my flesh, he was specific about that—and hauled. Between him and Wren and two of the Pike cousins, they'd gotten me onto the rocks at Ferrymouth, where Maren Kettle, who delivered babies and set bones and buried people with equal efficiency, spent a night doing things to my chest that I was grateful not to remember.

"And Halloway?" I asked, the first time I could ask anything.

The room went quiet in a particular way. Maren and Tam exchanged a look.

"The flood took him," Tam said finally. "Him and the man with the detonator both. We found the launch wrecked on the bar at low tide. One body. The other's still out there somewhere, or isn't. The sea settles its own accounts."

I thought about Halloway's polite voice, his clean coat, his offer made in the chapel doorway like a man discussing the price of fence posts. *The vault's contents for the girl's contract and your good name.* He had ordered a cliff full of homes undermined to save himself a season's work. And then the monsoon—the same arithmetic he'd tried to use on us—had simply done to him what it does to everything that stands in the channel when it decides to move.

I couldn't say I felt justice. What I felt was colder and more ordinary: the understanding that the wilderness forgives nothing, including the men who think they've hired it.

"And the ledger?"

That question Maren answered by reaching under the cot and producing an oilskin packet, tied with fishing twine, dry as bone. "Wren's orders. Nobody opens it but you two. It's been under more guards than the church silver, and half those guards are children, so take that as you like."

I held the packet against my chest and didn't open it. There would be time. For once in my life, there would be time.

* * *

On the third day they let me up, and I discovered what it means to be an invalid in a village of two hundred people who have collectively decided you belong to them.

It began with breakfast. Mrs. Odell Pike—no relation to Odell that anyone could explain to me, which in Ferrymouth meant there were three relations nobody mentioned—arrived at the vestry with a pot of fish stew and instructions that I was to eat all of it, and stood watching until I had. Then came the cobbler's boy with my boots, dried and resoled, "since you'll be wanting to walk out of here eventually and not barefoot like a heathen." Then a girl of perhaps nine named Sef, who had appointed herself my engineer during the fever—I learned this from Maren, who reported it with visible delight—and arrived with a notebook in which she had drawn, in ferocious pencil, a diagram of my own heart labeled with boiler terminology.

"This is the firebox," she explained, tapping the drawing. "It got flooded. This is the smokebox. It's full of snot."

"Accurate," I said.

"When you're better, can I see the maps? The real ones? Tam says you've got every closed line on the coast in your head."

"Sef," said Maren warningly.

"It's all right," I said, and found, to my surprise, that it was. "Come back tomorrow. Bring the notebook. If we're going to do this properly, you'll need to learn notation."

She left at a dead run, and I sat there on the edge of the cot with my ribs aching and something loosening in my chest that had nothing to do with the water I'd taken in.

Fifteen years I had kept myself a stranger. Fifteen years of signal boxes and empty platforms, of knowing every stationmaster's dog by name and no stationmaster by more than a nod, of maps drawn in a hand I'd practiced until it stopped looking like mine. I had called it contentment. Some of it had even been true. But lying in that vestry, drowning in other people's soup and solicitude, I understood that the hairline crack I'd carried all along hadn't been loneliness exactly. It had been the effort. The daily labor of being no one, which turns out to be much harder work than being someone, if the someones are willing to carry a bit of the weight.

These people had refused to let me drown alone. Not because of the vault, or the ledger, or what my name might be worth to them. Because I was in the channel, and they had a boat.

* * *

Wren came on the fourth day.

I heard her before I saw her—her uneven stride in the corridor, favoring the shoulder Maren had strapped, and then she filled the doorway with her usual air of having audited the room and found it acceptable.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You should see the other fellow. Actually, no one should. The other fellow is at the bottom of the channel."

Something crossed her face—not quite a flinch, but adjacent to one. She'd been out to the bar at low tide herself, I'd heard. Looking. Not for Halloway, I suspected. For the man with the detonator, whose name she knew and I never learned, and whose death she carried differently than I carried mine. Guilt is a cargo that distributes itself unevenly between partners.

She dragged a stool to the bedside and sat, and for a while neither of us said anything, which between Wren Odile and myself had long since stopped being awkward and become a kind of conversation.

"The ledger's safe," she said finally.

"I know. Maren showed me."

"Have you opened it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

I looked at the oilskin packet where it sat on the table beside the stew pot and Sef's abandoned pencil. "Because the last time I read my father's handwriting, I was eleven years old, and I spent fifteen years running from it. And because—" I turned the packet over in my hands. "Because once it's open, it stops being mine. It becomes everyone's. And I find I want one more day of it being just a thing under a table in a room where people bring me soup."

Wren nodded slowly. "That's the sanest thing I've ever heard you say."

"Don't spread it around. I have a reputation."

"A reputation for what? You've been unconscious for four days."

"Precisely. Very restful. I recommend it."

And then she laughed—a short, unguarded sound, quickly bitten off by the pull of her shoulder, but a laugh—and I realized it was the first time either of us had laughed without a storm or a corpse in the immediate vicinity. It changed the shape of the room.

"Wren." I said it carefully. "Your contract. With Corvane."

"Torn up. Physically. I burned it in the communal kitchen the second night, and Mrs. Odell Pike made me toast the marshmallows afterward, so now it's also dessert." She shrugged, winced, continued. "There will be others. Corvane doesn't forgive a breach any more than the sea does. They'll send someone quieter next time, or they'll go legal, or they'll simply wait ten years and buy the cliff through a shell company. I know how they operate. I operated for them."

"So what do we do?"

"*We,*" she repeated, and raised an eyebrow. "Listen to you."

"Don't make it strange."

"Too late. It's strange. It's been strange since the signal box." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the lamplight caught the salt-scars on her hands. "What we do is what you already said. We open it. All of it. The ledger, the confession, the directors' names—including yours, including mine, including every dirty hand that signed off on sinking that train. We give it to the inquiry, to the newspapers, to anyone who'll print it. We make it so loud that Corvane can't bury it under lawyers, because burying it would confirm it. And then—" she paused, and for the first time since I'd known her, Wren Odile looked uncertain. "Then I don't know. I've never done anything that didn't end with me leaving."

"Neither have I," I said. "Ended with staying, I mean. Everything I've ever done ended with leaving. It's rather my specialty."

"Between us we might manage one whole life."

"It's worth attempting."

* * *

That evening, they moved me to the communal kitchen.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only honesty I've ever been reliably good at: they did not ask me. Maren simply appeared with a blanket over her arm and said, "You're not spending another night alone in the vestry, the damp's getting into your lungs," and Tam and two of the Pikes carried me—carried me, a grown man, a railroad engineer, an alleged professional—through the rain to the long low building at the center of the village where everyone ate when the weather made separate suppers impossible.

They put me in a chair by the fire with the authority of men stacking cargo, and then the village proceeded to have an evening around me as though I were furniture. Children did lessons at the end of the long table. Someone tuned a fiddle badly and was told so. Mrs. Odell Pike dished out stew. Sef arrived with her notebook and installed herself at my elbow, demanding notation. Rain hammered the roof, the sea spoke its endless monologue beyond the seawall, and the fire cracked, and I sat in the middle of it with a bowl in my hands and a child annotating my circulatory system, and I did not leave.

I want to be clear: I could have. The door was right there. Nobody was guarding me. In fifteen years I had walked away from warmer rooms than this, and the skill hadn't rusted.

But the map of my life had always been drawn in closed lines—routes surveyed, catalogued, and abandoned, beautiful and useless, kept at arm's length so they could never fail me again. And here was a line that ran straight through the middle of everything, through the vault and the ledger and my father's name and the channel where Tam's gaff had caught my collar, and it terminated not in a derelict platform but in a kitchen full of people eating stew in a storm, and every instinct I owned said *close it, close it before it closes on you.*

Instead I said, "Sef, give me the pencil. A firebox draws like this."

She watched my hand with the total absorption of the young, and around us the evening went on, and Wren caught my eye across the table—she'd come in quietly, shoulder bound, hair still wet from the walk between buildings—and she didn't smile, exactly. She gave me the small nod of one surveyor to another, confirming a bearing.

The oilskin packet sat upstairs in the vestry, waiting. Tomorrow, or the day after, I would untie the twine and read my father's confession and my family's shame in daylight, and give it away, and become a man with a public name again, with all the exposure and peril that entailed. Corvane would not vanish because one fixer drowned. Somewhere beyond the rain, accountants were already adjusting.

But tonight there was bread, and a fiddle finding its tuning, and a girl asking earnest questions about culverts, and a woman across the table who had chosen my side over her contract and never once made me thank her for it.

"Thackeray," Tam said, passing behind my chair with the bottle. He paused, and his hand rested briefly on my shoulder—the grip of a man checking that cargo was secure. "Good to have you back among the living."

"Not back," I said. "I don't think I was ever among them before."

He grunted, which from Tam was a benediction, and moved on.

Outside, the storm was turning. Maren had said it at supper—the pressure rising, the bands spacing out, the sea dropping its voice by degrees. By morning the eye would pass, and after it, whatever came next: the inquiry, the newspapers, the slow machinery of a truth too large for any syndicate to sit on.

Sef tugged my sleeve. "Draw the tunnel," she said. "The one under the cliff. The real one, before it fell in. Did trains really go through it?"

"They did," I said. "Fast ones. Full ones. Right over the water."

"Will they again?"

I looked at the fire, and at Wren, and at the rain beginning, very slowly, to slacken against the dark windows.

"Yes," I said. "I believe they will."
