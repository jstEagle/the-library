## 21. Testimony

The inquiry was held in the assize hall at Marrowgate, which had been built to try smugglers and now found itself trying a railway. The building had the same problem Corvane's founders had: too much marble and not enough honesty. I sat in the public gallery between a fishwife who smelled of brine and a retired schoolmaster who kept correcting the barristers under his breath, and I watched Xanthe Thackeray walk to the witness box with his shoulders set like a man walking out onto a trestle he already knew was rotten.

He'd shaved for it. That was the detail that undid me, if I'm honest. Not the ledger, not the testimony, not the moment when the whole room went quiet as a held breath—he'd shaved, and combed his hair, and put on a collar that didn't fit him, because some part of him believed that the truth deserved to be presented properly. Fifteen years of erasure, and he still couldn't quite kill the inspector's son in him. The one who did things by the book.

The book, as it turned out, was the problem.

* * *

I should go back. Wren Odile keeps her own ledgers, even the ones written in guilt instead of ink, and this chapter deserves its entries in order.

We came down from Ferrymouth on the third day after the flood, on a mail boat whose captain charged us double and then gave half of it back when he heard what we were carrying. The ledger rode in an oilskin wrap inside Xanthe's survey satchel, and Xanthe rode beside it with his hand resting on the flap like a man guarding a sleeping animal that might bite anyone but him. His father's handwriting was in there. His father's confession was in there. Forty-one names of men who drowned in the wreck of the *Provision*—that was the train's name, God help us all, the directors actually named it *Provision*—and the orders that put them on that stretch of coast at that hour of that night, signed three levels up, initialed two levels higher than that, all the way to a boardroom where five men had decided that a delayed shipment cost more than forty-one lives and had arranged, quietly, through their inspector on the ground, for the line to fail.

My father's employers' grandfathers, more or less. Corvane Salvage Syndicate hadn't been founded on salvage. It had been founded on making sure nothing worth salvaging ever surfaced.

"You're going to have to testify too," Xanthe said to me on that boat. He wasn't looking at me. He was looking at the water, which was doing what water does after a monsoon—all its secrets churned up and floating past, planks and roof tiles and one very confused cow.

"I know."

"They'll ask what you were hired to do."

"I know."

"And you'll tell them."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway, because he'd earned an answer and because saying it out loud made it real in a way I needed. "Every word. Including the parts where I lied to you." I paused. "Especially those."

He nodded once, the way he nodded at gauge readings, and kept watching the water, and I understood that this was as much absolution as I was going to get before the lawyers got hold of me, and that it would have to be enough.

It was enough. That surprised me most of all.

* * *

The inquiry ran eleven days. I attended every one of them, sitting in the gallery with my notebook, taking notes nobody had asked me for, because old habits are load-bearing structures and mine were built to document everything.

The first three days belonged to the engineers. They established the tide tables, the track geometry, the metallurgy of the failed rail—the physical facts of how a train goes into the sea. Dry work, competent work, and underneath it all the slow dawning horror of the gallery as people realized that none of it had been an accident. A rail doesn't loosen itself. A fishplate doesn't walk away from its bolts. Somebody had done arithmetic with human beings as the rounding error.

Day four, they called the ledger into evidence, and the hall changed temperature.

Xanthe identified it. His voice was steady until they asked him to confirm the hand that had written the maintenance entries, and then there was a silence of perhaps four seconds—four seconds is a long time in a courtroom; I counted them against my pulse—and he said, "That is my father's writing. Elias Thackeray. Inspector number 2-1-7." And then, because he was who he was, he added: "The entries are accurate. Whatever else he was, he kept honest records of dishonest work."

The barristers didn't know what to do with that. Neither, I think, did Xanthe. It was grief wearing a technician's coat, and it fit him badly, and he wore it anyway, because somebody had to stand up in daylight and say *my father did this* so that the dead could stop being a legend and start being a crime.

Days six through nine belonged to the documents. The confession. The correspondence. The routing order that sent the payroll train along the cliff branch at night, off schedule, unlit, with the section foreman told to expect "no traffic" — a sentence that reads differently once you've seen it next to a photograph of a carriage fused to cave rock. The directors' initials climbed the margins like ivy. Halloway's predecessors had signed their names to murder in fountain pen, because in those days the powerful still believed paper was private.

Paper is never private. Paper waits. Paper is patient the way water is patient, and eventually both of them find the crack.

* * *

On day eight, they called me.

I'd expected hostility. What I got was something worse: courtesy. The Corvane counsel—a silver-haired man named Vess who smelled of good wool and worse intentions—treated me like a colleague who'd wandered onto the wrong side of a site fence. He wanted to establish that my commission had been narrow. Locate the vault. Assess contents. Report. Nothing about the ledger, nothing about the sabotage, certainly nothing about the cliff demolition that Halloway had ordered while I was still technically on Corvane's payroll.

"So you were never instructed," Vess said, "to conceal evidence of criminal acts."

"No," I agreed pleasantly. "I was instructed to locate a vault. Concealing evidence wasn't in the scope. It was just in the practice."

Someone in the gallery laughed. The fishwife, I think. I was starting to like her.

Vess tried another angle. "You betrayed your employer's confidence."

"I did," I said. "Let me save us both an afternoon. Yes, I took their money. Yes, I broke my contract. Yes, I handed their founding documents to a provincial inquiry. Here's why." I turned, deliberately, to face the gallery—not the bench, the gallery, the fishwife and the schoolmaster and the two grey-haired sisters from Ferrymouth who'd come all this way to watch—and I said, "Because they sent me to take something from people who had already lost everything once, and they knew exactly what they were sending me into, and they didn't care. Because Halloway ordered a cliff blown up over a village full of children to protect a signature. And because somewhere between a signal box and a flooded quarry, I discovered that loyalty to a contract is just obedience with better stationery, and I'd rather be loyal to something that can be loyal back."

The bench reminded me that I was there to answer questions, not deliver orations. But the schoolmaster wrote my last line down in his notebook, and I saw him do it, and I regret nothing.

* * *

Day ten was the day they read the names.

Forty-one men. The youngest was nineteen; his name was Tam Osgood and he'd taken the run because it paid double and he was saving for a boat. The oldest was the fireman, sixty-one, three months from pension. A clerk read them out in alphabetical order, one by one, into a hall gone absolutely silent, and the families stood up as each name landed—some of them descendants, some of them villagers who'd come because forty-one men had been a legend for seventy years and legends don't get to be mourned properly, only retold.

Tam Osgood's great-niece stood for him. She was maybe eighty herself.

And then the clerk reached the T's, and read: "Elias Thackeray, inspector, cited in evidence as the railway's agent in the sabotage of the coastal branch, deceased 1949," and the whole hall turned, as one animal, toward the witness box.

Xanthe stood up.

He didn't have to. Nobody asked him to. But he stood, and he said, in a voice that carried to the back wall without effort because fifteen years of talking to gulls teaches you projection, "That was my father. I'm his son. My name is Xanthe Thackeray, and before any of you ask—I knew. I found his ledger when I was twelve years old, and I ran, and I hid, and I let forty-one people stay a ghost story because facing it meant burying him twice." His hands were flat on the rail of the box. Steady. "I'm done hiding. Ask me anything."

They asked him everything. He answered for four hours. And when it was over, when the president of the inquiry adjourned the session and the hall emptied, the Osgood woman stopped at the box on her way out and looked up at him and said, "Your father's name being said out loud—that's the first time this has felt finished. Thank you for standing up for it."

I watched him receive that the way a dry field receives rain. Not gracefully. Desperately.

* * *

The verdicts came on day eleven, and they were everything the papers promised and more. The wreck declared unlawful killing by corporate act. The five directors named posthumously, their estates opened to claims, their foundations dissolved. Corvane Salvage Syndicate's charter suspended pending criminal referral—which is lawyer for *dead*, though the lawyers insisted on the longer word. Halloway himself was beyond the court's reach; the sea had handled his sentencing personally, and I won't pretend I lost sleep over the jurisdictional question.

Outside the hall, the press wanted Xanthe. He gave them ninety seconds, standing on the steps with the light doing something kind to his face, and said only: "Read the ledger. Then go look at the sea wall at Ferrymouth, because the village that caught these men rebuilt it themselves, and if you want to know what the opposite of Corvane looks like, it looks like that." Then he walked away from the microphones, which may have been the most newsworthy thing he did all week.

Which left me, on the steps, holding an envelope.

My contract. The original, couriered to my lodging that morning by a Corvane liquidator with the cheerful efficiency of a man shredding an empire—one copy for my records, madam, per the dissolution terms. My name on it. My commission. The clause binding me to report the vault's location and contents to the syndicate and no other party, under penalty.

Seventy years of Corvane, and it ended with clerks mailing out termination paperwork.

I read it once, standing on the steps in the cold, and then I tore it. Lengthwise first, then across, then across again, because I am thorough, and dropped the pieces into the bin by the door. It felt smaller than it should have. Contracts always do, afterward. All that weight they carry while you're carrying them, and then they're confetti.

Xanthe was waiting at the bottom of the steps, hands in his pockets, watching me with the expression he wore when a reading came in exactly as predicted.

"Well?" he said.

"Well what?"

"You tore it up. I want to hear you say why. You're an archivist. You don't destroy primary sources without a statement of provenance."

I laughed—actually laughed, out loud, on the courthouse steps, and the fishwife grinned at me from the crowd like she was proud of me, which is a sentence I never expected to compose about myself. "Provenance," I said. "Fine. Document Wren Odile, formerly of the Corvane Salvage Syndicate, currently unemployed, currently—" I looked at him. At this man I'd lied to, followed, flooded with, been flooded beside, been forgiven by without ever quite asking for it. "Currently intending to stay where she's known and liked anyway. Which is a first."

"Ferrymouth's short on archivists," he said, studying the middle distance, which was how he managed to say enormous things without looking at them. "Chapel archive needs cataloguing. The tunnel plans alone would keep someone busy till spring. And the schoolmaster wants the wreck history written up properly, for the memorial. Someone will have to interview people. Cross-reference. Do it right."

"That sounds like a commission."

"It's not a commission. Commissions end." He finally looked at me. "This is just work. The kind that's still there tomorrow."

I put my hand through his arm, because I am occasionally brave in ways that have nothing to do with caves or storms, and we walked down the steps together into a street full of ordinary noise—carts, gulls, a child shrieking about something trivial and wonderful—and behind us the assize hall stood in its marble, holding its new truths, lighter by seventy years than it had been that morning.

The past, I thought, doesn't get set down all at once. You set it down the way you set down anything heavy: carefully, in pieces, with someone spotting you. But it does get set down. I had proof of it now, in triplicate, filed where it belonged.

"Spring," Xanthe said, as we turned toward the harbor. "If they reopen the line by spring—"

"If they do," I said, "someone will have to drive the first train through."

He smiled at the harbor, at the boats, at the whole improbable visible world. "I might know a man."

*I might know a man.* As if the man weren't standing right there, signing his own name again, learning the weight of it and finding it bearable. As if any of us had ever really been strangers at all—we'd only been waiting, all of us, for the tide to go far enough out to show what was true.

The gulls wheeled over the water, and the light came up gold on the headland, and somewhere north of us a closed line waited to be open.
