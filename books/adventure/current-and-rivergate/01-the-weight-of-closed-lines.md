## 1. The Weight of Closed Lines

The ink on the grade report was still wet when Freya Joubert refused to sign it.

She sat at the long drafting table in the survey office at Verrin's Crossing, the lamp turned low because the company counted every ounce of oil, and read the figures a third time to be certain. She was certain. The spur line out to the Kessel pits was drawn at a rise of one foot in forty, and the ground itself said otherwise. She had walked that ground for eleven days with her level and her chain, through mud that sucked the boots off a man's feet and rain that came sideways off the Current valley like something thrown. One foot in thirty-five, maybe worse where the cut crossed the old slide. The difference did not sound like much to anyone who had never watched a loaded train try to climb a lie.

One foot in forty would sell. One foot in thirty-five would cost Rivergate Consolidated another season of grading and perhaps a bridge they didn't want to build. So the report on her desk said forty, in a fair clerk's hand, and above it waited a line for her signature — *surveyed and certified by F. Joubert, assistant engineer* — and beneath that, the small unspoken arithmetic of what happened to surveyors who did not certify what the company required.

Bram Hollis, the chief of the corps, stood at the window with his back to her. He was a heavy man gone soft around the middle, but his hands still remembered instruments; he had taught her to hold a level true when she was nineteen and hungry enough to be grateful for any work at all. He did not turn around.

"You walked it," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I walked it."

"And you won't sign."

Freya set down her pen. "The grades are wrong, Mr. Hollis. Not optimistic. Wrong. There's a slide scar under the fill at Chainage 88 and the whole embankment will creep every spring thaw until it takes a locomotive with it. I told you this in my field notes. My field notes say thirty-five."

"Your field notes," Hollis said, "are not the report." He turned then, and she saw he had aged since spring, or perhaps she had simply never looked at him properly before. His face had the gray look of a man who had already lost an argument with himself. "Freya. Sit down."

"I am sitting."

"Then listen." He came back to the table and put both hands flat on it, on either side of the false report, as if to hold it down against a wind only he could feel. "The concession runs to the Kessel pits whether the grade is forty or thirty-five. The board has approved the estimate on forty. If you amend it, the estimate goes back to committee, the permit window closes, and the spur dies — and with it, the corps' contract, and with it your position, and mine, and the positions of eleven other people who did nothing wrong except employ you."

"The alternative," Freya said, "is that some driver takes a hundred tons of ore up that grade in February and finds out the truth at speed."

"No one will die. Trains crawl on spurs."

"Trains stall on spurs. And stalled trains roll back down slides." She kept her voice level, because she had learned long ago that a woman who raised her voice in a survey office was dismissed twice — once from the room and once from the record. "I can't sign it. You know I can't. You taught me the numbers don't care whose name is on them."

Hollis closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, all the argument had gone out of him, and that frightened her more than anger would have. "Then we're finished here," he said quietly. "I'm sorry, Freya. I'll write it up as a reduction in force. It's kinder than the truth, and the truth wouldn't help you anyway."

* * *

She packed her kit in the last hour of daylight, while the office emptied around her and no one quite met her eye. That was the ritual of dismissal; everyone knew its steps. The junior men found reasons to be at the far cabinets. Mrs. Tallow, who kept the books, suddenly needed tea. Only the boy Pell, sixteen and all elbows, dared come near, and he did it by pretending to return a scale rule she had lent him weeks ago.

"They said you wouldn't sign," he whispered.

"They said right."

"It was just a number."

Freya looked at him across the drafting table, at the pencil behind his ear and the ink stains on his fingers, and thought about how many years it took to learn that a number was never just a number. "Every number on a drawing is a promise," she said. "Somebody builds their life on it. Remember that when it's your name going on things."

He nodded too fast, not understanding yet, and fled.

Her own belongings fit into one canvas satchel: two pencils, a scale rule of her own, a folding rule worn smooth at the joints, a tin of instrument oil, and the notebook of field observations she had kept against regulations because regulations belonged to the company and the observations belonged to her. From the bottom drawer, wrapped in oilcloth, she took the two things that were not the company's and never had been.

The first was a brass compass, old-fashioned, cased in leather gone dark as chestnut. Her father's. It had come to her by post seven years ago, after the river took him — or after whatever had taken him upriver took him — sent by no sender she ever identified, wrapped in paper postmarked from a town she'd never heard of. The needle swung free and true, or seemed to; she had checked it against solar bearings a hundred times and it never lied to her, which was more than she could say for most people. On the inside of the case lid, scratched deep with a fine tool, someone had cut a single letter: **J**.

The second thing was the map.

It was hers by theft, if she was honest — a fair copy she had made herself, years ago, of the company's master chart of closed lines: every rail line surveyed, chartered, graded, and then abandoned across three hundred miles of the Current watershed. Lines killed by politics, by floods, by estimates that came in ugly. The company called them closed lines because closing a line was cheaper than finishing one. Freya called them something else. She had spent four years copying them, in her own hand, onto wax cloth, annotating each closure with the reason pulled from the files — *grade impractical*, *bridge washed*, *concession lapsed* — and somewhere in the doing of it she had stopped making a copy and started making an argument. Three hundred miles of promises the company had made and unmade. Someday, she had always told herself, someone would want to know why.

She rolled it now and bound it with cord, and felt the weight of it in the satchel beside the compass, and understood that she was packing not tools but a history — hers and her father's, braided together by brass and wax cloth, though she couldn't yet have said how.

* * *

The notice was waiting at her lodging house, slid under the door of her room at the Ferryman's Rest as if the messenger had been ashamed to knock.

*To F. JOUBERT: Be advised that the outstanding balance of your indenture account with Rivergate Consolidated Survey & Transport Company, being the sum of £84 12s., advanced against training, instruments, and passage, is hereby CALLED DUE within THIRTY (30) DAYS of this date. Failure to discharge said balance will result in referral to the magistrate's court at Rivergate city, with costs.*

Eighty-four pounds, twelve shillings. The price of her apprenticeship, her first transit, her instruments — money the company had advanced to a girl of seventeen with no father and no name worth anything, and had recouped ever since out of wages calculated to keep her forever a few pounds short of free. Eighty-four pounds was nearly two years of a surveyor's pay, if a surveyor could find pay. In thirty days it would be a warrant.

Freya sat on the edge of the narrow bed with the notice in her hands and let the fear come, because she had learned that fear was like water: you could dam it, but it always found the crack, and it was better to give it a channel. She let it run through her, cold and complete — the magistrate's court, the debtors' cell, the work assignment they gave women with debts, which was not surveying — and then she folded the notice along its printed creases and put it away in the satchel with the map, filed between the past and the future like a datum point.

Outside, Verrin's Crossing went about its evening. The town existed for one reason only — the junction where the main line met the Current valley branch — and it had the temporary look of all such places, timber and tar paper and mud streets laid out by someone in a hurry. Down at the yards a switching engine worked the night's consists, and the sound of couplings slamming carried up the hill like slow applause. Somewhere a harmonica played in a bunkhouse, badly. The Current itself she could not see from her window, but she could feel it, the way you feel a large animal standing near you in the dark: the valley's whole shape bent around its presence, the fog that came off it at dusk, the smell of cold stone and drowned leaves.

Her father had gone up that river. Everyone said so. Elias Joubert, senior surveyor, transferred to the upper Current expedition in the spring of the year she turned nine, and the letters came regular for fourteen months and then stopped, and the company's eventual answer to her mother's inquiries was a single line: *Mr. Joubert's engagement concluded upstream.* No body. No effects beyond the compass that arrived years later by mystery post. No grave. Her mother had died inside five years of waiting, and Freya had grown up in the space between those two silences, learning to measure everything because nothing else stayed.

She took out the compass and opened it on her knee. The needle settled, trembling, pointing north — or pointing wherever north was, up there beyond the closed lines and the dead towns and the source of the Current that no chart showed because the company forbade charts of it. She had never asked herself why a railroad concern would forbid the mapping of a river's headwaters. She had accepted it the way everyone accepted it, as one more fact of the company's power, like the tolls and the private police and the founder's portrait in every station house.

Tonight, holding the brass case warm in her palm, she asked it for the first time. And the asking changed the shape of the silence, the way a question changes a room.

Eighty-four pounds, twelve shillings. Thirty days. A satchel with a stolen map and a dead man's compass, and a road out of town that ran, like every road in the valley, along the river.

Freya closed the compass, snuffed the lamp, and began, in the dark, to count her money.

* * *

She left at dawn, before the yard engines woke, before Mrs. Tallow could press bread and pity on her. The road east out of Verrin's Crossing followed the high bank of the Current through birch scrub and abandoned cordwood camps, and she walked it with her satchel strap crossed over her chest and her collar up against the river fog, and she did not look back at the town because looking back was a habit of people who expected to return.

At the mile post where the road bent away from the water, she stopped — not from doubt, but from the surveyor's discipline of fixing her position before moving on. Behind her: the crossing, the yards, the life signed away over a single figure in a report. Ahead: eighty-four pounds of debt, thirty days of clock, and a river running the wrong way for anyone in a hurry.

She fixed the bearing in her mind, adjusted the satchel, and walked on.

Two miles further, in the ditch where the road dropped toward the ferry landing, she saw the man lying among the alders — and thought at first he was dead.
