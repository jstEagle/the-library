## 2. A Dying Man's Bargain

The road out of Verrin's Crossing ran west along the old river terrace, and Freya Joubert walked it with her collar up and her satchel heavy against her hip, as though the weight of it might anchor her to something. Behind her the town sat low in the morning haze, all smoke and timber and the smell of creosote from the tie-yard, and she did not look back at it. Looking back was a habit she could not afford. Looking back was how a woman talked herself into returning to a place that had already decided what she was worth.

The map of closed lines rode against her ribs, rolled in oilcloth. Her father's compass rode in her breast pocket, cold as a coin. Thirty days. The company clerk had said it almost gently, the way a man tells you a train is leaving, as if the schedule were nobody's fault. Thirty days to settle the debt her father's name had left on the books, or the company would take the compass, the map, the deed to a cabin she hadn't seen in eleven years, and anything else they could find with her name scratched on it.

She had walked out of the survey office rather than sign. She had walked out with her chin level and her hands steady, and now, four miles down the terrace road with the sun climbing and no destination but *away*, her hands were not steady anymore. Fear, she had learned, did not arrive all at once. It arrived in installments, like a bill.

The Current River showed itself through the trees on her left, broad and brown-green and moving with that patient, muscular indifference rivers have. Barges crawled upstream under the company's flag, coal smoke trailing. Somewhere up that river, past the Splits, past the gorges where the charts went blank and the company's permits said *closed to survey*, her father had walked into the blankness and never walked out. She had spent her whole life measuring distances from the edge of that blankness. Today, apparently, she was going to walk off it.

She heard the mule before she saw the man.

It was a small, ragged sound at first — a hoof scuffing stone, then a groan that might have been the mule and might not. The road bent around a bluff of grey limestone furred with moss, and where it bent, in the shallow ditch where the terrace dropped toward an old landing, a mule stood hipshot and trembling under a load of panniers, and beside it, half in the ditch, lay a man.

Freya stopped. Every instinct she had learned in rail camps said *keep walking* — a man in a ditch off the company road was either a trap or a funeral, and both were somebody else's problem. But the man moved. He turned his head, and even at forty yards she could see the effort it cost him, the way a man turns his head when he has decided he is going to die and wants to spend what's left of him on one last piece of business.

"Miss." His voice was a dry whisper, carried up the slope on the river wind. "Miss. For God's sake."

She came down the bank with her hand nowhere near her knife, the way you approach a strange dog or a strange man. Up close he was sixty, maybe older, grey-bearded and lean as a fence rail, dressed in the layered wool of a man who had lived in the bush a long time. There was blood on the wool. A great deal of it, dark and dried and fresh all at once, soaked high on his left side where a coat should have been and wasn't. His right hand, when he lifted it, shook like a compass needle in a storm.

"They took my coat," he said, as if that were the important thing. "Took my coat and my rifle and left me. Left me for the crows."

"Who did?" Freya knelt in the ditch, careful of the blood, and looked at the wound. She had seen gunshot wounds before — rail camps were not gentle places — and she knew the look of one that had gone deep and was not going to be argued with. This one had gone deep. The blood had pooled in the gravel and gone black at the edges. He had been here since yesterday, she judged. Maybe longer. The mule had not wandered because the mule was loyal or stupid, and she did not know which was sadder.

"Badges," the man said. "Rivergate badges. Blue and silver." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough brought up something dark that he spat into the gravel. "Company men. Shot me for a map, and then they didn't even take the map. Didn't find it. Took the coat instead." He looked at her, and his eyes were fever-bright and terribly clear, the way dying men's eyes get when the fever has burned away everything except the one thing they refuse to die without doing. "You're not company."

"No," Freya said. "I'm the opposite of company."

That seemed to please him. "Opposite of company," he repeated, tasting it. "Good. Good. Help me sit up. I can't talk lying down. Water runs out of me when I'm flat."

She got her shoulder under him, braced against the slope, and eased him upright against a root-buttressed alder. The effort left her breathing hard and him grey-lipped, but he settled, and the mule leaned its nose against his shoulder with a gentleness that undid her a little.

"Name's Wren," he said. "Aldous Wren. Prospector. Thirty-one years on the Current and its feeders, and I'll thank you to remember that, because nobody else will after today." He fumbled at his shirt with the shaking hand. "You got a name, opposite-of-company?"

"Joubert," she said. "Freya Joubert."

His hand stopped fumbling.

"Joubert," he said again, and something moved through his face — recognition, then a strange, grim satisfaction, like a man who has laid a bet years ago and just seen the horse come in. "Joubert. Well. Well, then. Maybe it wasn't the crows after all. Maybe it was the river." He began to laugh again, quietly, wincing around it. "Help me with the pannier. Left side. The one with the oilskin under the grain sack."

Freya hesitated. Everything in her said this was a door, and doors on empty roads with bleeding men behind them were how a woman ended up in a river. But the man had said her father's name the way you say a name you've carried a long time, and she was, she admitted to herself, a woman who had never once in her life walked past a locked thing without wanting to know what was behind it.

She opened the pannier. Under a grain sack stiff with mold there was a bundle in waxed canvas, tied with cord, and inside the waxed canvas — she felt it before she saw it, the particular stiff slickness of it — was a map drawn on wax-cloth, the kind prospectors used because it could be rolled, soaked, buried, and still read. She unrolled a corner. Ink lines, fine and confident, ran across the cloth in her hands, and a river ran down the middle of them, and the river was the Current — she knew it the way you know your own handwriting, by the way the surveyor drew the bends, the little hooked marks for rapids, the dotted lines where the chart gave up.

But this chart did not give up. It went past the Splits. It went past the gorge where every company map in Rivergate ended in a printed border and the words *CONCESSION LIMIT — CLOSED TO SURVEY*. It went on, up a second channel that no map she had ever seen admitted existed, to a small, dense knot of markings at the head of the valley. And beside the knot, in a cramped, hard hand: *SOURCE. SPRING-FED. LIMESTONE. — A.W.*

And lower, in different ink, older, in a hand that made her breath stop in her throat — because she knew that hand, had grown up watching it move across ledger pages and margin notes and the backs of envelopes — a single word: *Joubert.*

"What is this?" she asked, and her voice came out wrong.

"That," said Aldous Wren, "is the only reason I'm still alive, and I'm not even sure I am." He held out the shaking hand. "Give it here. I'll show you."

She gave it to him, and he unrolled it across his knees with the tenderness of a man handling a child, tracing the river with one cracked fingernail.

"Thirty-one years I've walked this river," he said. "Silver in the feeders, everybody knows it, that's why the company holds the concession — exclusive rights, all the ground above the Splits, granted to old Mericault himself, God rot his name. And everybody knows there's a mother lode up there somewhere, because the ore dust comes down the water and it don't come from anywhere the company's ever been allowed to dig." His finger stopped at the knot of markings at the valley's head. "Because the source is behind the limestone shelf, and the limestone shelf is off every chart, and every man who's gone looking for it has either come back empty or not come back." His finger moved to the older word. "Except one."

Freya could not speak. The river went on behind them, patient and brown-green, carrying its barges and its secrets down toward the sea.

"Joubert," Wren said. "Surveyor. Came up with the first concession expedition, twenty-two years back, before the permits closed the valley. Found the shelf. Found the spring. Found the silver — enough silver to buy the territory, girl, enough to buy the *company*." He looked up at her, and his fever-bright eyes held hers. "And then he came back down, and he filed his report, and six months later the concession was granted to Mericault with the silver rights written in, and Joubert's report was never entered in the record. Closed. Sealed. And then Joubert went back up the river, alone, in the spring of the year, and the river kept him."

"You're lying," Freya said. It came out flat and automatic, the way you say *no* to a thing that is true.

"I'm dying," Wren said. "It's a bad time to start lying. I was there, girl. I was the packer on that first expedition. I carried his theodolite up the gorge. I saw him set his instruments on the shelf and read the spring. I saw Mericault's man — a hard one, a clerk with a gun, name of Crane — take the original field notes off him at the landing and tell him the company would honor his claim when the paperwork was done." Wren coughed, long and wet, and when he could speak again his voice had dropped to a thread. "The paperwork was never done. The paperwork was a knife. And twenty-two years later I find the spring again on my own, and I file a claim on the feeder ground, and I register it honest in Rivergate under my own name — and two weeks later I'm shot on the terrace road by men wearing blue and silver."

The mule sighed. Somewhere upstream a barge whistle blew, long and low, and the sound of it seemed to come from another country.

"Why me?" Freya said. "Why tell me this?"

Wren's shaking hand went into his shirt and came out with a packet — oilskin, tied, small, the shape of folded papers. He held it in his palm and looked at it for a long moment, as if it were heavier than it could possibly be.

"Because I'm not going to make it to Rivergate," he said. "And my girl is there. Marta. Marta Wren. She keeps a room over the chandlery on Rope Walk. She thinks I'm rich, God help her, because I always wrote home like I was about to be." His mouth twisted. "I'm not rich. I'm a man with a claim and a map and six weeks."

"Six weeks?"

"The concession permits expire at the end of the season," Wren said. "Six weeks from tomorrow, the Current concession lapses unless the company renews, and they'll renew, they always renew — but between the lapse and the renewal, the ground is open. Any registered claim can be read into the record. Any honest paper can beat a forged one, for one day, if it's spoken on time." He pressed the packet toward her. "My claim papers. Registered, sealed, legal. They shot me for the map, but the map's no good to them without the claim, and the claim's no good to anybody sitting in a ditch."

Freya looked at the packet and did not take it. Her heart was going hard, and her mind — her good surveyor's mind, the mind that had refused to sign a falsified grade that very week — was already running the line of this like a level run, and every reading came back wrong. A dying stranger. A treasure map. A debt she owed and a debt she was being asked to collect. Men had been killed on this road for what he was holding, and he wanted to hand it to a woman he'd known for ten minutes because of a name.

"I can't," she said. "I'm not — Wren, I owe the company money. Thirty days. They'll have men watching the roads. If I'm caught carrying a claim against their concession—"

"You'll be caught carrying it anyway," Wren said, "if they search you, because they'll search everyone going upriver this season, because they know I filed, and they know I know where the spring is, and they are not going to let an old man's paper spoil twenty-two years of theft." He gripped her wrist, and for a dying man his grip was astonishing, hard as a dog's jaw. "Girl. Freya Joubert. Your father's bones are up that river. His survey is in that company's vault, stolen, or burned, or worse. And I am the only man alive who can put your hand on the proof, and I am going to be dead inside a day."

The wind came down the terrace and moved the alder branches, and the light came through in shifting coins, and Freya knelt in another man's blood on a company road and understood that her life had just forked, the way rivers fork, and that she was going to take the channel with no chart.

"Say it," Wren said. "I need to hear it sworn. Not promised. Sworn. Take the papers to my daughter before the permits expire. Tell her the claim is hers, and the map, and tell her—" his voice caught, and for a moment the prospector was gone and there was only an old man, frightened, "—tell her I did my best by it. Tell her I did my best."

"I'm a stranger," Freya said. "You'd swear a stranger to this?"

"I'd swear the devil himself," Wren said, "if the devil wasn't wearing a badge." He pushed the packet against her chest until she took it. The oilskin was warm from his body. "Swear it."

And here was the thing she would remember all her life, the thing she would turn over at night in boats and tents and strange beds: she did not want to swear it. She wanted to walk away. She wanted the road west and the debt and the small, survivable ruin she had already grieved for that morning. Taking this packet meant taking the river, the gorge, the blank on the map, the company's enforcers, the whole terrible weight of a dead man's unfinished business — and she was tired, and afraid, and the fear had been arriving in installments all morning.

But he had said *Joubert* like a man setting down a load. And somewhere up that river was a word in her father's handwriting on a chart that should not exist.

"I swear it," Freya said, and her voice was steady now, steady the way it had been in the survey office, and she understood that the steadiness was not courage but decision — the difference between them being almost nothing, and everything. "Before the permits expire, your papers reach Marta Wren in Rivergate, and the claim is read into the record. On my name. On my father's name."

Wren closed his eyes. The breath went out of him, long and rattling, and when he opened his eyes again they were clearer than ever, and calmer, as if the oath had done for him what a surgeon's steel could not.

"Good," he whispered. "Good girl." His shaking hand found the wax-cloth map where it lay across his knees and folded it, clumsily, into quarters. "Then take this too. She'll need it to prove the ground. Datum's marked — the spring, the shelf, the bearing off the north peak. Your father's figures are still true, girl. I checked them for twenty years. They're the truest thing on the river."

He pressed the folded map into her hand beside the packet, and then he lay back against the alder, and the fever took the clarity from him in slow degrees, the way dusk takes a hillside. Freya did what she could — water from her flask, her coat rolled under his head, the mule unloaded and hobbled where the grass grew — and she sat with him through the middle of the day while his breathing shortened and the barges went by on the river, and toward late afternoon, with the light going long and gold on the water, Aldous Wren stopped breathing altogether, between one ripple of the Current and the next, as quietly as a man closing a ledger.

She buried him above the landing, under an alder, with a flat stone at his head and his name cut into it with her knife: *ALDOUS WREN — THIRTY-ONE YEARS ON THE CURRENT.* It took her until dark. The mule stood watch the whole time, ears forward, and she talked to it without meaning to, the way you talk when you are alone and have just sworn an oath that is going to cost you everything.

* * *

She camped that night in the lee of the bluff, a small fire, the mule picketed, the river talking to itself below. The packet sat in her lap. She did not open it. She had not opened it since the ditch; opening it felt like a thing that could not be undone, and she wanted one more hour of being a woman who could still walk west.

But she was an engineer, and engineers read the ground they're standing on.

She unrolled Wren's map by firelight and laid her father's map of closed lines beside it, and set her compass on the wax-cloth at the datum mark, and watched the needle swing and settle. It settled true. It settled along a bearing she did not recognize, off every line on either chart, pointing up-valley toward the limestone and the blankness and the spring.

And that was when she saw it — the thing that turned wonder cold in her chest. The datum point on Wren's map and the datum point on her father's closed-line survey were not merely similar.

They were the same stone.

Her father had been here first. Her father had drawn this river before Aldous Wren ever packed a theodolite up a gorge. Which meant the old prospector had not found the source. He had found *her father's* source — and someone, twenty-two years ago, had wanted that fact buried so badly they had sealed the survey, closed the valley, and let the river keep the man who drew it.

Freya sat back from the fire, the compass cold in her palm, and looked upriver into the dark, where the Current ran on toward the Splits and the gorges and the shelf of limestone that was on no chart.

Six weeks. And somewhere downstream, in Rivergate, the company already knew an old man had filed a claim — which meant the company already knew a woman might be carrying it.

She banked the fire, and lay down with the packet against her heart, and did not sleep.
