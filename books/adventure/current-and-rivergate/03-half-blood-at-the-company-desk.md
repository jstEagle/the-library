## 3. Half-Blood at the Company Desk

The ledgers of the Rivergate Concession Company smelled of tallow and old rain. Tomas Joubert had noticed this on his first day as a junior clerk, seven years ago, when he had still believed that a smell could tell him something true about a thing. Now he knew better. The ledgers smelled of whatever the clerks were given to preserve them — tallow, camphor, sometimes vinegar in the wet months — and what they contained had no smell at all. It was numbers. Numbers did not rot, and they did not confess.

He sat at his desk in the long permit room on the third floor of the company house, where the windows faced the river and the light came through glass so old it had begun to sag, so that every vessel on the Current appeared to be sinking. Forty-one desks. Forty-one clerks. The scratch of forty-one nibs like insects in a wall. Tomas's desk was the fourth from the stairwell door, chosen years ago for reasons he could no longer reconstruct — perhaps because it was near the exit, though he had never been the sort of man who thought about exits. He was the sort who thought about doors already closed.

"Joubert." Mr. Halloway, the section master, dropped a folder on his blotter without breaking stride. "Current River concessions. Renewals. They go to the board Thursday."

Tomas looked at the folder the way a man looks at weather coming over a ridge. "How many?"

"Eleven active claims, two lapsed, one under review." Halloway was already moving. "Under review is yours too. Don't lose it."

*Don't lose it.* As if things were lost by accident. Tomas untied the string and opened the folder, and there it was, printed in the company's own cramped type: **CONCESSION NO. 9 — THE CURRENT RIVER AND ALL TRIBUTARIES ABOVE THE SPLITS — EXPIRY OF TERM: THE TWENTY-SIXTH DAY OF THE HARVEST MONTH.**

Six weeks. He read it twice, because the date meant nothing to him, and then it meant everything, and he read it a third time to make sure his hands weren't shaking enough to blur the ink.

The twenty-sixth day of the harvest month. A dying man on the road out of Verrin's Crossing — no. Not a dying man. A description in a letter, forwarded from a station agent, that he had read three nights ago and filed without thinking: *a prospector named Wren, shot by parties unknown, last seen trading papers with a woman traveling south.* He had filed it because filing was what he did. He had not let himself wonder what papers.

Now the date sat in front of him in the company's own type, and the wondering would not stay filed.

* * *

Tomas Joubert had been raised legitimate, which is a way of saying he had been raised in a house with his name on the lintel and a father who came home to it, while a girl with the same father and a different mother grew up four hundred miles upriver learning surveying from that same father's absences and resentments. He had been eleven when he understood this fully — eleven, sitting on the porch steps of the house on Chandler Street, watching his father pack a transit case for another season upstream, and asking, in the blunt way of children, whether there were other children.

His father had looked at him for a long moment with an expression Tomas would spend thirty years failing to name. Not guilt. Something more practical than guilt. The look of a man checking a ledger.

"There's a girl," his father said finally. "She has her mother's people. She's fine."

*She's fine.* Three words, and his father shouldered the transit case and went down to the landing, and Tomas sat on the steps and understood that somewhere upriver there was a half of himself being raised on less, and that his father considered this an entry already balanced.

He had met her exactly once before the betrayal. That was the part nobody believed, afterward — that all of it, the whole ruin, had come out of a single afternoon. She was sixteen and he was nineteen, home from the academy, and she had come down to Rivergate with their father's compass in her pocket and a chip on her shoulder the size of the company house, and she had asked him — she had actually asked him — to help her get a copy of their father's concession filings. Just copies. She said their father had promised her mother a share of something and she wanted to see the numbers herself.

And Tomas, nineteen years old and terrified of his father, terrified of the house, terrified most of all of the small hot thing in his chest that wanted to say yes — Tomas had gone to his father instead and told him everything.

He told himself, for years, that he had done it because it was right. Because the filings were company property. Because a girl with no standing had no claim. He told himself this the way men tell themselves things in the dark, which is to say fluently and without conviction. What he had actually done was choose the warm house over the cold sister, and he had known it even as he did it, and his father had known it too, and his father had thanked him — thanked him, with a hand on the shoulder, the only time in his life — and that thanks had been the most sickening thing Tomas had ever felt, because it confirmed that the choice had been a choice.

Freya had found out. Of course she had. She had stood in the clerk's room doorway — he remembered her exactly, mud to the knee, hair tied back with string, the compass making a small hard shape in her jacket pocket — and she had not shouted. That was the worst of it. She had looked at him the way you look at a structure you are deciding whether to condemn, and she had said, "I thought you might be different," and turned around, and left, and he had watched her go through the sagging window glass looking like a ship going down.

That was nine years ago. He had risen steadily since. Junior clerk to clerk to senior clerk, legitimate as a church bell, and every promotion had felt like a payment received on a debt he hadn't agreed to owe.

* * *

At noon he took his lunch to the records annex, which was where the dead files lived — the closed lines, the lapsed claims, the surveys withdrawn from circulation. Nobody went there. The annex smelled of camphor and mouse, and the light came through one high window in a single dusty blade, and Tomas liked it precisely because it was where the company kept the things it preferred not to look at.

He pulled the drawer marked **CURRENT — SURVEYS, CLOSED** and took out the file he had taken out maybe six times in seven years, always alone, always with the same mixture of shame and hunger.

**JOUBERT, E. — LINE SURVEYS, CURRENT RIVER HEADWATERS — WITHDRAWN AT FOUNDER'S DIRECTION.**

His father's surveys. Withdrawn — not rejected, not superseded, *withdrawn* — eleven years before his father vanished upriver for the last time. Tomas had never been able to make the dates sit right. The withdrawal order bore the founder's signature, old Amiel Verrec himself, and it predated the founder's death by a decade, and it gave no reason, and inside the folder the survey sheets were intact and beautiful — his father's hand, precise as engraving, grades and gradients and datum points laid down with a care that bordered on devotion.

And one sheet, the last one, the headwaters sheet, was incomplete. It ended mid-line, at a bearing penciled but never inked, as if the man had stopped drawing and started hiding.

Tomas stood in the dusty blade of light and held his father's unfinished line and thought about a woman traveling south with papers.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not avoid, now that the expiry date had surfaced it: the Current concession expired on the twenty-sixth. His father had surveyed the headwaters and the survey had been withdrawn. His father had gone upriver and not come back. And now a prospector had been shot by men wearing company badges, and a woman had walked away from that shooting with maps, and Silas Crane — Crane, whose name appeared nowhere in any file Tomas could find and yet who signed requisitions with the authority of the founder's ghost — Crane had put a description into circulation.

*A thieving woman with a map.*

He had heard it yesterday, secondhand, from the desk nearest the stairwell. Crane's runner had come through with instructions for the landing watch, and the phrase had passed over the forty-one desks like weather. *Thieving woman with a map. Intercept and hold. Compensation per schedule.* And Tomas had kept his nib moving and his eyes down, and had thought: *that describes half the women on the riverfront,* and had almost laughed, and then the runner had added the particulars — traveling alone, engineer's kit, southern road out of Verrin's Crossing — and the laugh had died in his throat like a bird hitting glass.

Engineer's kit. Southern road. He had not needed the description beyond that. There was only one person in the world who fit it, and he had put her there himself, nine years ago, with a sentence spoken in this same building: *she asked me for the filings, Father.*

* * *

Crane sent for him at four o'clock.

Everyone was afraid of Silas Crane, but they were afraid of him the way they were afraid of weather — generally, superstitiously, without specifics. Tomas was afraid of him specifically. Crane occupied a corner office on the second floor that had belonged, everyone assumed, to some directorate or other, though no one could say which, and he occupied it the way a spider occupies the center of a web: motionless, patient, at the exact point where everything must eventually pass.

"Tomas Joubert." Crane pronounced the surname with a faint pleasure, as if tasting it. "You're the Joubert. The surveyor's son."

"My father surveyed for the company."

"He did." Crane sat behind a desk bare except for a lamp and a single ledger, closed, its corners squared to the desk edge with a precision that made Tomas's skin crawl. Everything about Crane was squared away. His coat, his cuffs, his voice. "Sit."

Tomas sat. The chair was lower than Crane's, which he supposed was deliberate, and he resented noticing that he'd noticed.

"You process renewals," Crane said. "Current River. Among others."

"I do."

"Then you know the term expires." It wasn't a question, and Crane didn't wait for it to become one. "What you may not know is that the renewal is not a formality. The source country above the Splits has never been properly worked. Never properly *surveyed*, some would say." A pause, thin as wire. "Your father claimed to have surveyed it. His filings were found to be irregular. You'd know that."

"They were withdrawn," Tomas said carefully. "I've seen the withdrawal order."

Something moved behind Crane's eyes — not surprise; recalibration. "Have you," he said softly. "Well. Then you'll understand the company's position. An unsurveyed source, an expiring concession, and rumors of unregistered silver moving down from the headwaters. Silver that belongs, by right of concession, to Rivergate. And now—" he opened the closed ledger, turned it so Tomas could see a page of neat entries, and closed it again before anything on it could be read— "now a thief is carrying stolen papers toward this office, or toward whoever will pay for them. A woman. Engineer's training. Traveling with maps she has no right to."

"And you want me to intercept her."

"I want the landing watch to intercept her. I want *you*" — Crane leaned forward, and the lamplight made his face briefly older and much colder — "to review whatever she carries, once she's held, and tell me what's genuine. You're the surveyor's son. You can read a line the way your father drew them. No one else in this building can." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man offering a gift he had already charged you for. "There's reinstatement in it. Your father's old commission, if you want it. The house on Chandler Street stands empty. It could stand less empty."

The house on Chandler Street. Their father's house, sold after the disappearance, bought back by the company, kept vacant nine years like a mouth waiting to be filled. Crane had done his research. Crane had done his research the way a flood does.

"I'm a clerk," Tomas said. "I file permits."

"You file permits," Crane agreed, "for a company whose concession dies in six weeks unless someone finds out what that woman is carrying. Think of it as a renewal task." He squared the ledger to the desk edge, one final small violence of precision. "The watch will bring her in within the week. Be ready."

* * *

He walked home along the levee because he could not bear the tram, and the river ran beside him all the way, brown and patient and full of silt from country he had never seen. Barges went down against the current-light. Gulls argued over the fish offal behind the packing houses. Somewhere upstream — above the Splits, above the gorge, above the last place any chart bothered to name — a woman was walking south with maps, and the whole machinery of the company was turning to meet her, and he had just agreed to stand at the point where the machinery and the woman would intersect.

Had he agreed? He turned it over as he walked. He had not said yes. He had not said no. He had said *I'm a clerk,* which Crane had heard, correctly, as a man negotiating the price of his own surrender rather than refusing to sell.

Nine years ago he had made one phone call, one sentence, one betrayal, and it had cost a girl with mud to her knees her faith in the only family she had. And he had spent nine years telling himself it was a single failure, an aberration, a door closed once and never again. But doors, he understood now, walking the levee with the river talking beside him — doors don't close. They wait. The same door, the same dark on the other side of it, and Crane had just handed him the key and called it a promotion.

The house on Chandler Street. God help him, some part of him had *wanted* it. Some part of him had heard *your father's house* and felt the old warmth, the legitimate warmth, the warmth that had been purchased once before at exactly this price.

He stopped at the rail where the levee overlooked the landing. Down below, a company runner was nailing a notice to the watch board, and even at this distance Tomas could guess what it said: descriptions, rewards, the schedule of compensation for intercepted persons. The gulls wheeled. The barges sank eternally in the sagging light of the upper windows, though these were open sky, and nothing was sinking, and everything was.

*She asked me for the filings, Father.* One sentence, nine years ago. He had a week to decide what sentence he would speak this time, and to whom, and whether a man got to be different the second time a door waited for him — or whether he was simply what his choices had made him, a clerk, a filer, a son who chose the warm house, standing at a desk on the fourth floor with a key in his hand.

The notice below fluttered in the river wind. The runner finished nailing it and walked away, and Tomas stood a long time watching the paper move, as if it might, by flapping, tell him which way to run.

Then he turned from the rail, and went home to a house that was not his father's, and did not sleep, and in the morning he requested three days' leave, and began — without yet admitting to himself what he was doing — to plan a lie.
