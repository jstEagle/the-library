## 4. The Pilot Who Drowned

The river town of Lasseau stank of tar and old rain, and Freya Joubert walked into it with Wren's map pressed flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

Three days she had been on the road since the place where the prospector had bled out under a stand of alders, three days of walking with his promise stitched into her like a seam that would not lie flat. *Deliver the papers to my daughter in Rivergate before the permits expire.* Six weeks, he had said. She had counted the days against her knuckles each morning, and each morning the number came back smaller, and each morning the map under her coat came back heavier.

She had come to Lasseau for one reason, and it was not the reason the town would have guessed. Lasseau was a barge town, a fueling and transfer station on the lower Current where the company's flatboats took on coal and discharged their contempt in equal measure. Every pilot, every deckhand, every man who knew the water between here and the headwaters drank at the Tin Lantern or slept at the company hostel and said nothing to strangers. Which meant that if a woman wanted to go upstream — truly upstream, past the last surveyed landing, into the water the company's charts marked with a single dismissive word, *impassable* — Lasseau was the only door, and every door in Lasseau was watched.

Freya had learned to be watched without being seen. It was a skill her father's profession had taught her without meaning to: a surveyor's daughter spends her childhood learning that the land is never what the men with instruments say it is, that every line on a map is an argument, and that the people who draw the lines are usually paid to win the argument rather than to tell the truth. She had signed her name to honest grades for eleven years. She had refused to sign a lie for exactly one afternoon, and that afternoon had cost her the corps, her lodging, and very nearly her name.

She was not going to lose the rest of it to a river town's gossip. So she did not ask for a pilot. Not at first.

* * *

She spent the first day listening.

She sat in the Tin Lantern from mid-afternoon until the lamps were lit, nursing a cup of coffee gone bitter as iron, and she let the room talk over her the way water talks over a stone. She learned which barge captains were drunk before noon. She learned that the coal price had gone up and the deckhands' pay had not. She learned, in the loose, unguarded way that men speak when they believe no one is counting, about a woman who lived at the end of the pilings past the boatyard — a woman the deckhands called *la noyée*, the drowned one.

"Marchand," said a barge cook with flour to his elbows, and then he spat, delicately, into the sawdust. "Don't say her name on the water. Company says she's dead. Company says a lot of things."

"What happened?" Freya asked, in the flat, incurious tone of a woman making conversation.

The cook looked at her the way men look at women who ask questions in river towns. "Spring flood, six years back. Water over the banks at Corben's Bend, whole valley a brown sea. The company had eleven barges loaded with rails and machinery, and the dispatcher — some clerk two hundred miles away with a dry collar and a dry conscience — orders the flotilla through anyway. Says the cargo can't sit. Says the water's *manageable*." He said the word as though it had personally insulted his mother. "Every pilot on the river refused. Every one but the ones who were too scared, and they ran aground in the first hour. Marchand was the senior pilot on the lower Current. She told the dispatcher to his face, by wire, that the run would kill men. He told her she'd be replaced. So she took her own boat out — not to run the barges, mind, but to pull the crews off the ones that broke up."

The cook's voice had gone quiet in the way of a man telling a story he had told before and never gotten used to.

"She saved four. Eleven drowned anyway. And when it was done, the company needed the disaster to be somebody's fault, and it wasn't going to be the dispatcher's. So they put it on her. Said she'd run the barges against her own judgment out of vanity. Struck her license. Struck her name off the rolls. And because a pilot with no license is legally dead on the company's water, that's what they wrote in the book. *Drowned.*" He wiped his hands on his apron. "She's alive. You can see her light out past the boatyard some nights. But on paper, Odile Marchand has been at the bottom of the Current for six years."

"And nobody hires her?"

"Nobody *admits* to hiring her." The cook gathered his flour and his opinion and left.

Freya sat a long while after that, turning the story over the way she turned ore samples in her hand, looking for the seam. A woman erased. A woman the company had killed on paper because it could not kill her in fact, and because the paperwork was cheaper. Freya thought of her own dismissal letter, with its careful, bloodless phrasing — *services no longer required* — and of the way her father's name had been struck from the survey rolls twenty years ago with the same bloodless pen, and she felt something in her chest that was not quite anger and not quite recognition but was made of the same material as both.

That night she walked out along the pilings.

* * *

The boatyard ended in a spine of rotten timber that reached into the Current like the finger of a drowned giant pointing upstream. Past its last joint, moored in the shadow of a leaning crane, sat a boat that made Freya's surveyor's heart turn over with something close to love.

She was maybe thirty feet on the waterline, shallow as a skiff, with a hull of blackened cedar that flared at the bow like a bird's breast and a twin-screw arrangement Freya had only ever seen in diagrams — two small wheels set wide in tunnels, the kind of arrangement that could turn in its own length and climb water that would break a conventional hull's back. She had no name painted on her. She had, instead, a name burned into the starboard rail in small, deliberate letters, half sanded and half stubborn: *MIREILLE*.

A lantern burned at the stern. Beneath it, a woman sat mending a net with hands that did not mend so much as command — quick, economical, the fingers of someone who had spent thirty years making the river do what she wanted and had run out of patience for anything that resisted.

"You've been standing on that piling for ten minutes," the woman said, without looking up. "Either you're deciding whether to jump, or you're deciding whether to talk to me. The water's cold either way. Talk."

Freya came down the pilings and stood on the bank above the boat. Up close, Odile Marchand was not what the Tin Lantern's stories had built her. The stories had made her either a hag or a martyr, and she was neither — a broad-shouldered woman of fifty-odd, gray shot through black hair cut short as a man's, a face weathered into deep, patient lines around a mouth that looked as though it had been designed primarily for saying no.

"I need to go upstream," Freya said.

"Everyone needs to go upstream. The river's that way." A jerk of the mending needle. "Go."

"Past the Splits. Into the upper Current."

The needle stopped. Odile looked up then, and her eyes were the exact color of the river in flood — brown with something bright moving underneath — and they went over Freya the way a pilot reads water: quickly, completely, noting every rock. The surveyor's case. The road dust. The way Freya's left arm kept its position against her ribs, guarding the flat rectangle of the map.

"No," Odile said, and went back to her net.

"I haven't told you what I'll pay."

"You haven't told me what'll get you killed, which is the only thing I need to know, and you'd lie about it anyway. Everybody does. They come down here with their money and their reasons and their maps—" the needle flicked toward Freya's arm, and Freya's blood went cold, "—and they think the river is a road and I'm a horse. No."

"I know what the company did to you."

The net came down. Odile Marchand stood, and the lantern light climbed her like water finding its level, and Freya understood that she had just made the first mistake of the negotiation, possibly the last.

"You know what the *cook* told you," Odile said. "You know a story. Stories are what people bring me instead of respect. Sit down, surveyor—" and Freya started, because she had not said what she was, "—yes, I can see it, the case, the way you look at my hull like you're checking her lines, the way you stand with your feet planted like the ground might move. Sit. You'll hear it right or you'll hear nothing."

Freya sat on the piling's edge, boots hanging over black water. Odile did not sit. She stood at the rail with the net across her knees like a drawn bow.

"Here is what happened, since you'll only make it worse in your head otherwise. The flood came. The wire came. I said no. Eleven men died anyway, because the company ran the barges with pilots who were afraid of losing their licenses more than of losing their lives, and the Current ate them. I went out and pulled four men off the wreckage, and one of them was my brother Émile, and I could not pull him onto the boat alive. I got him aboard breathing. He died at dawn with his hand in mine, and the company's lawyer arrived at noon." Her voice did not change at all, and that was the worst of it; it was a voice worn smooth, like a stone in a current, all the roughness long since ground away. "They offered me a settlement to sign a statement saying the dispatcher's orders had been reasonable. I declined. They offered again. I declined louder. So they took my license, and then they took my name off the rolls, and then — because a dead pilot can be blamed for anything — they wrote me into the accident report as the cause. *Pilot error.* I have read my own death certificate. It is very well drafted. The company hires good clerks."

The last sentence had an edge on it, and the edge was aimed somewhere past Freya, upstream, toward a city and a desk.

"I'm not the company," Freya said.

"No. The company sends men. You're something else." Odile's flood-colored eyes came back to her. "You're a woman with a map she's carrying like it's a child, asking to go into water the company closed, in the same season the Current concession comes up for renewal, which every rat on this river can feel coming the way animals feel weather. So before I say no again, you're going to tell me one true thing. Not your name — I'll get that or I won't. One true thing about why."

Freya looked at the lantern, at the black water, at the burned name on the rail — *MIREILLE*, and who had Mireille been, and had the river taken her too — and she decided, with the particular exhaustion of a woman who has been lying by omission for three hundred miles, to stop.

"My father surveyed the upper Current," she said. "Twenty-two years ago. The company says his lines were never filed. The company says a lot of things. I have his map of the closed lines, and I have a dead man's map of the source, and the dead man was shot by men wearing Rivergate badges, and I promised him something I intend to keep. I don't know what's up there. I know it's worth killing for, because they're already killing for it. And I know the permit expires in six weeks, and after that the company owns every inch of that water and everything the water is hiding, forever, and no one will ever be able to prove what they did to get it."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere out on the Current, a night bird called, one note, upstream.

Odile Marchand was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, it was not to Freya; she was looking at the water, at the dark seam of the current, at whatever pilots see when they look at a river that other people only see the surface of.

"Émile was a deckhand," she said. "He used to say the river keeps every secret it's ever been given, and charges interest." She rolled the net, stowed it, and turned. "Here are my terms, and they are not negotiable, and if you haggle I will put you ashore at the boatyard and you can explain to the company's watchers why you were talking to a dead woman. I take you upstream as far as the Splits. Not past. At the Splits I put you off with your maps and your promises and your dead man's errand, and I go my way, and you go yours, and if you drown above the Splits it will be with a clear conscience — mine, I mean. I take no passengers into the gorge. I've fed that water enough."

"Why the Splits? What's there?"

"None of your business, and all of mine." Odile's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "You'll pay half now, half at the Splits. You'll stand no watch at the wheel. You'll do what I say on the water the instant I say it, without discussion, because discussion is for banks and rivers don't have any. And you'll tell me your name, because I don't run water for strangers."

"Freya Joubert."

The name landed between them like a dropped tool. Odile went very still, and Freya watched six years and twenty years move behind the flood-colored eyes, and she understood, with a lurch, that the name had meant something — that *Joubert* meant something on this river, to this woman, and that she had handed over more than a name.

"Joubert," Odile repeated, softly, the way one reads a word on a chart that shouldn't be there. "Well. Well, then." She looked up, and whatever she had seen in the name, she folded it away, deep, where pilots keep the things the water has taught them. "Cast off in the morning, an hour before light. Bring your maps. Bring whatever that dead man gave you. And Freya Joubert —" the lantern caught the bright moving thing under the brown of her eyes, "— when we're on the water, you'll tell me the rest. Because I knew a surveyor named Joubert once, and the company drowned him on paper too, and I have been waiting twenty-two years to hear how that story ends."

Freya stood on the pilings with the Current sliding black and patient beneath her, carrying her father's name upstream in its cold hands, and understood that she had not hired a pilot.

She had been claimed.
