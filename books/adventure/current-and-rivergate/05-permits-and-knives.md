## 5. Permits and Knives

The Permit Bureau of the Rivergate Consolidated Navigation & Rail Company smelled of ink, cold tea, and the particular despair of men who spent their lives converting other men's ambitions into triplicate. Tomas Joubert had worked at the same slanted desk for eleven years, in the same corner, beneath the same water stain shaped like a hand reaching for the window, and he had long ago stopped noticing any of it. This morning he noticed everything. The scratch of his nib sounded like a confession.

The manifest book lay open before him: *Travel Permits, Upriver Traffic, Week Ending the 14th*. Column one, vessel. Column two, cargo. Column three, destination. Column four, name and signature of filing party. It was the most ordinary document in the building, and he was about to make it the most dangerous.

Three days. That was what he could buy her. The Current concession — he had found the file yesterday, pulled it himself, his own hands on the folder, his own eyes reading the expiry date — the concession lapsed in six weeks, and everything upstream of the Splits would revert to open claim. Whatever Aldous Wren had sold his sister, whatever map or promise or dying fever-dream, the clock on it had already started. Crane wanted her stopped before she reached the water. Tomas could not stop her from reaching the water. But he could decide which water she was believed to have reached.

He dipped his pen.

The lie, when it came, was beautifully small. That was the thing about fraud that no one warned you about — not the seminary tutors who'd washed out his father's donations, not the company manuals with their stiff warnings against *irregularity*. Fraud was not a great black deed. It was a comma moved. A digit altered. A destination written where another destination should have been.

*Vessel:* *Marrow*, shallow-draft freight skiff, Odile Marchand, master. He had checked the registry twice. She was listed as dead — drowned, officially, eleven years ago, in the flood she'd refused to run. The company kept its dead pilots on the books for insurance purposes, a bureaucratic purgatory that meant her name was still valid paper, and no clerk downstream would question a ghost running freight. There was an irony in that Tomas chose not to examine.

*Cargo:* general goods, salt pork, lamp oil, survey instruments. *Destination:* Lower Marsh landing, the tobacco flats. *Filing party:* T. Joubert, clerk, Permit Bureau, on behalf of the Consignment Office.

He signed it. His hand did not shake. He almost wished it would have, because a shaking hand would at least have been honest about what he was.

The manifest went into the day-box with forty others, and the day-box went to the records room, and by noon the lie would be buried under four hundred other documents, unremarkable, unremarked. Three days. Downstream landings would be watching for a woman traveling under her own permit, filed through the Bureau, flagged by Crane's order. They would not be watching for salt pork bound for the tobacco flats under a dead woman's name. By the time anyone thought to check the *Marrow*'s actual heading, Freya would be three days upriver and the river would have swallowed her wake.

Tomas sat back and looked at his hands. They were his father's hands, narrow and clerkish, good with figures and bad with rope. His father had used those hands to sign things too. Tomas had spent his whole life telling himself the difference between them was that his father had signed away what wasn't his, while Tomas only ever signed what the company told him to. It had seemed, until this morning, like a meaningful distinction.

Now he had signed something the company had not told him to. And the strange thing — the thing that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone — was not guilt. It was how *light* the pen had felt. How easily the choice had come, after eleven years of not choosing.

He could still choose. That was the discovery, and it was terrible, because a man who could not choose had an excuse, and Tomas had been living on that excuse for thirty years. *I was raised legitimate. I took the name. I took the schooling. What was I supposed to do, refuse?* A man who could not choose was a man to whom nothing could be attributed. And now he had chosen, and the choice fit in the space of a comma, and it would hang him if Crane ever saw it, and he had made it anyway, and he hated — he hated with a clean and precise hatred — how small the whole thing was. Not a rebellion. A comma.

"Joubert."

He looked up. The floor supervisor, a gray man named Pell, stood at the end of the row of desks with a slip of paper. "Mr. Crane wants the Current concession file on his desk within the hour. You pulled it yesterday. Where is it?"

"Filed back," Tomas said. "Records room, third bay, under Navigation."

"Fetch it yourself. He wants the clerk who read it to summarize it. Verbally." Pell's face did something that on another man might have been sympathy. "He's in the old assay office. Don't keep him waiting."

* * *

The old assay office sat at the end of the company wharf, where the building could watch the water, and Silas Crane had chosen it for exactly that reason. It had been stripped to its bones — one table, one lamp, one chair, and a wall of maps hung like trophies or like wanted posters. Crane stood before them with his back to the door, and he did not turn when Tomas came in, and Tomas understood that the not-turning was itself a message: *you will wait until I am ready, and you will be comfortable waiting, because comfort is not something I intend to give you.*

Tomas waited. He counted the maps on the wall to keep his breathing even. Fourteen of them. River charts, mostly, the Current and its feeders, and pinned among them — he felt his stomach drop with a slow, cold patience — survey drawings in a hand he recognized without wanting to. Closed lines. Dashed contours. The notation style of a man who had taught himself engineering from borrowed books and gotten the letterforms slightly wrong in a way that no one else in the world would ever notice.

His father's hand. On Silas Crane's wall.

"You found the concession file," Crane said, still facing the maps. It was not a question.

"Yes, sir. Navigation, third bay. I brought it." Tomas set the folder on the table.

"Summarize it."

"The Current concession, granted to Rivergate Consolidated under the original charter, forty-one years ago. Exclusive navigation and extraction rights from the mouth to the headwaters, contingent on continuous operation. Expiry of the renewal window—" he did not let himself pause, did not let the number mean anything out loud— "in six weeks. If no renewal is filed and no continuous operation is demonstrated, the upper reaches revert to open claim."

"And who can file an open claim?"

"Any party. With proof of discovery and a registered assay."

"Any party." Crane turned then. He was not a large man, which Tomas had always found to be the most frightening thing about him; large men spent their size, but Crane spent nothing. He had a face like a ledger's spine — narrow, closed, ruled. "A dying prospector could file. A prospector's daughter could file." He came to the table and opened the concession file without looking at it, the way a man opens a book he wrote himself. "A surveyor's daughter could file."

Tomas kept his face at clerk's neutrality, which was the one skill eleven years at the slanted desk had actually given him. "I don't follow, sir."

"No," Crane agreed. "You don't. You follow instructions, Joubert. That's what you're for." He laid a paper on the table between them — a wharf watchman's report, and Tomas read it upside down with the automatic fluency of a man who had spent his life reading other people's documents: *Woman, thirties, travel-worn, carrying a map case, asking after upstream passage. Refused at the toll office. Inquired at the pilot registry. Left on foot along the north levee.*

"We have a thieving woman with a map," Crane said. "She stole from a man named Wren — papers, a claim, possibly more. She'll need a boat, and there are perhaps six pilots on this river who'll run above the Splits at all, and perhaps one of those who'll run it for a woman with no permit." He looked up, and his eyes were patient the way a trap is patient. "You process the pilot registry, Joubert. You know who's on it. Even the ones who are dead."

There it was. Not a question. A weight placed on the table next to the file, next to the watchman's report, and Crane's hand resting flat beside both, waiting to see which one Tomas would reach for.

"The registry's current as of spring," Tomas said. "Marchand's still listed. Deceased, but listed."

"And?"

"And she won't run for the company, sir. She refused a direct order in the flood year. They struck her name and let her live, which was the company being generous, and she knows it. She hates this company more than she loves money, and that's a rare thing on this river." All of it true. All of it a wall built of true stones. "If your thieving woman went to her, Marchand would run her upriver out of pure spite. But she'd run her on the *Marrow*, and the *Marrow* filed for the tobacco flats this morning, and I countersigned the manifest myself."

He heard his own voice say it — the lie delivered as truth, the truth deployed as cover — and something in him detached and stood in the corner of the assay office watching, appalled not by the lying but by how *good* he was at it. Eleven years of converting other men's ambitions into triplicate had apparently been training for exactly this.

Crane studied him for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Out the stripped window, the river moved brown and patient toward the sea, carrying everything to the sea eventually, the honest cargo and the drowned men alike.

"Tobacco flats," Crane repeated.

"Yes, sir. Lower Marsh landing."

"Mm." Crane closed the concession file and slid it back across the table. "Thank you, Joubert. You've been helpful." And then, as Tomas turned to go, in the same flat tone, the same ruled voice: "Your sister. Freya. She was dismissed from the survey corps in Verrin's Crossing last month. Refused to sign the spur-line grades." A pause measured to the half-second. "You didn't mention you'd reviewed her file."

Tomas stopped with his hand on the door. He had not known he was being tested until the test was already over, and he had already — he could feel it, the way you feel a cut before the blood comes — he had already failed it or passed it, and he did not know which.

"I processed her dismissal paperwork," he said. "It was routine. She was one of four."

"She was one of four," Crane agreed. "But she's the one with a map case, walking north on the levee, and the *Marrow* is bound south for tobacco. One of those things is a coincidence, Joubert, and I have never once in my life needed a coincidence." He picked up his pen. "You may go."

* * *

Tomas did not go back to the Permit Bureau. He walked the wharf instead, all of it, end to end, past the barge tiers and the coaling yard, to where the company steamer lay at the deep-water berth with her name on the paddle housing in gilt that the river had been eating for twenty years: *PERPETUAL*.

She was the largest thing on the Current and she was a joke and everyone knew it — a deep-draft riverboat on a shallow, violent, rock-toothed river, good only for the lower forty miles and for impressing shareholders and for carrying men with rifles to places where men with rifles had no business being efficient. She could not run the rapids. She could not pass the Splits. But she could patrol every landing from the mouth to the fork, and her launch could go where she could not, and a man standing on her hurricane deck could watch the whole lower river the way Crane watched everything: patiently, without blinking.

Crane was already aboard. Of course he was. Tomas watched from the coal shed's shadow as the enforcers came down the gangway in their gray company coats — eight of them, then four more, then the two with the crate that everyone on the wharf pretended not to know held Winchesters. A requisition order was nailed to the berth post, and Tomas read it from forty yards because he could not help reading anything, ever: *Requisitioned for company service, upper river patrol, duration of concession window. Signed S. Crane, Chief of Security.*

*Upper river.* The *Perpetual* could not reach the upper river. Which meant the order was not about the steamer. It was about the launch, and the men, and the message, and the time. Crane had not believed him. Crane had listened to the whole beautiful wall of true stones and thanked him politely and gone straight to the berth, because Crane did not need Tomas to be loyal. He needed only to make sure that if Tomas was not loyal, the cost of it would arrive before the profit did.

Tomas stood in the coal dust and did the arithmetic he could not stop himself from doing. The *Marrow* had left on the morning tide, if Odile Marchand kept her word, and Odile Marchand was a woman who had kept one word — *never again* — for eleven years, so perhaps she kept others. Three days of lead. Against that: the *Perpetual* could be at the Lower Marsh landing by tomorrow noon, confirm there was no tobacco cargo and no woman, and be back at the Splits inside four. His three days had just become one, and the one was already bleeding.

And there was a worse arithmetic under that one, the kind he was trained never to write down. Crane had asked about Freya by name. Crane had mentioned the survey corps, the spur line, the file Tomas had processed — details that were in no wharf report, no watchman's slip. Which meant Crane had pulled Tomas's own personnel history, or Freya's, or their father's, and had done it quietly, and had chosen to say the name *sister* out loud in a stripped room with one lamp, the way a man lays a card face up not because he needs the trick but because he wants to watch your hands.

He knew. Or he didn't know, and was building the trap that would tell him. Either way, Tomas had signed a false manifest with his own name, countersigned, filed, buried in the day-box, and the only man in the world who could connect it to him was standing on a steamship giving orders to men with rifles.

He could still choose. The thought came again, unwelcome, insistent, like water finding a seam. He had chosen this morning, small and cowardly and effective, and it had bought his sister a day, maybe two, and it had cost him — what? Everything, if it was found out. Nothing, if it wasn't. A comma's worth of courage. He hated it. He hated that his whole moral life had been reduced to punctuation, that the grand question of what kind of man he was had come down to whether he could move a digit in column three and hold his face still while Silas Crane said the word *sister*.

His father had faced the same question once, Tomas thought. His father had answered it by taking the house and the name and letting the river keep the rest, and the river had kept it for thirty years, and now it was sending it back upstream in the hands of a woman with a map case.

The *Perpetual*'s whistle blew — one long note, departure — and the gray coats began casting off the lines, and the great wheel began to turn, slapping the brown water in a rhythm like a slow heartbeat. Tomas stood in the coal shed's shadow and watched her pull out, and understood, with the clarity that comes only after a thing is too late to change, that he had three days to decide what he was, and that Crane had already scheduled the exam.

He turned back toward the Bureau, toward his slanted desk and his triplicate and his ink-stained hands, and he was already — he could feel it, the pen wanting to be in them again — he was already thinking about what else the records room held, and what else a clerk who could choose might pull from the third bay, and how many days a man could buy with nothing but paper and a steady hand.

Behind him the *Perpetual* beat her slow way upriver, toward the tobacco flats that held nothing, toward the Splits, toward the water that kept what it was given.
