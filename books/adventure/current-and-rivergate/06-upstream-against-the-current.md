## 6. Upstream Against the Current

The boat was called the *Magpie*, and she was ugly in the way that honest things are ugly: flat-bottomed, shallow as a dinner plate, patched with tin where the hull had been opened and closed again by rocks Odile Marchand plainly considered old acquaintances.

"Sit," Odile said, when Freya reached for a line on the first morning out of Sainte-Anne's landing. "You'll want to touch everything. Don't. The river will tell you what needs touching."

Freya sat. She was not good at sitting. She was an engineer; her hands wanted work the way other people's hands wanted warmth. But she sat, and she watched, and within an hour she understood that she had hired not a pilot but a kind of instrument — one calibrated over thirty years against this particular water.

The Current ran backward here, or so it seemed: they were going up it, and it was going down with all the temper of a thing that knew exactly where it meant to end. The *Magpie* fought with a small stern paddle wheel and a long push pole for the shallows, and Odile used both the way a surgeon uses two instruments, without looking at either.

"Read it to me," Odile said, near midday of the first day. She nodded at the water ahead, where the current bent around a bluff of red shale and came apart into what looked like chaos — white seams, dark seams, a standing wave that seemed to be climbing uphill.

Freya looked. She thought about grades. A grade was just water slowed down and made visible, laid across the land instead of running through it; a surveyor read a cut bank the way a pilot read a riffle, and the mathematics underneath were cousins if not sisters.

"The fast seam runs left," she said slowly, "but the left is a lie. See how the foam curls back on itself? That's an eddy behind the point. The deep channel is right of center — the dark water — but there's a rock under it. The boil above it marks its head." She pointed. "So you take the left seam until the eddy, then cross through the slack behind the wave before the rock's wash pushes you down onto it."

Odile said nothing for a long moment. The bluff slid past. Then, without turning: "You've run water before."

"I've read ground before. It's the same book in a different binding."

That earned her something that was almost a smile, gone as quickly as it came, like a fish showing its side. "Same book," Odile agreed. "But the river tears pages out and puts others in overnight. Your ground holds still while you measure it. This doesn't. That's the whole difference, and men have drowned forgetting it."

They ran the rapid. Freya's hands ached from gripping the gunwale, and she found she was grinning, and she made herself stop.

* * *

The days took on a rhythm that felt dangerously like happiness. Mornings, Odile called the water aloud — *seam, boil, tongue, hole* — and made Freya answer before they committed the boat to any of it. Afternoons, when the light went flat and the reading got hard, they tied up in the willows and Odile slept while Freya worked the maps.

She worked them the way she'd once worked survey sheets: on a plank laid across two crates, weighted at the corners with stones, squared to the boat's centerline. Her father's map of closed lines she kept rolled tight in oilcloth, and unrolling it always cost her something. It was drawn in his hand — she knew his hand better than her own face, having inherited it along with the compass — every contour feathered fine as hair, every elevation noted to the tenth of a foot, and across the upper reaches, in his own ink, the words that had ended his career and begun her exile: *CLOSED BY ORDER OF THE RIVERGATE CONCESSION COMPANY. SURVEY SEALED.*

Closed lines. In engineering, a closed line was a loop that returned to its beginning, proof that the measurements held together, that the arithmetic had come home. Her father had chosen the phrase deliberately, she'd always believed. The company had closed his survey. He had answered by making the word mean both things at once.

Wren's map was another animal entirely. Wax-cloth, soft with handling, drawn in charcoal and grease pencil by someone who had never been taught drafting but had spent forty years being taught by the river instead. No contours. No elevations. Instead: depths sounded in fathoms written beside the channel like beads on a string, notes on the color of the water, sketches of bluffs shaped like animals — *the Bear's Head, keep to the Bear's left ear* — and, at the top of the sheet, past a gorge rendered in furious cross-hatching, a small circle with a star inside and three words: *THE SOURCE. SILVER.*

Two maps. Two languages. One river.

On the fourth night, camped on a gravel bar below the tobacco flats, with the fire low because Odile insisted fires be small enough to smother with one boot, Freya did the thing she had been circling toward since the first day: she laid Wren's map over her father's, up against the underside of the plank, and held a lantern beneath them both.

Light through paper. Both maps ghosted into visibility at once, superimposed, the charcoal channels floating over the inked ones.

They matched. Of course they matched — same river, same bends. But matching wasn't what stopped her breath.

It was the datum.

Every survey begins somewhere: a fixed point, brass or stone or bedrock, from which all other measurements are chained. Her father's datum for the Current survey was marked on his map in his precise little capitals — *D.P. No. 1, CUT BANK BELOW THE SPLITS, ELEV. 412.6, CHISELED CROSS ON LEDGE* — and Freya had known that mark since she was nine years old, had traced it with her finger a thousand times in the lamplight of rooms they'd been thrown out of.

Wren's map had no datum. Prospectors didn't think in datums. But Wren had needed one fixed reference anyway, the way any careful man does, and he'd chosen it the way a prospector chooses: something permanent, something findable, something the river couldn't eat. On his map, at the head of the long reach below the Splits, in his blunt charcoal hand: *OLD SURVEYOR'S STONE IN THE BANK. ALL DISTANCES FROM HERE.*

Below the Splits. Cut bank. Stone in the ledge.

Freya set the lantern down. Her hands were not quite steady.

Wren hadn't picked his starting point at random. He had picked *her father's* starting point — the chiseled cross on the ledge at elevation 412.6, set there by a survey crew twenty years ago, before the company sealed the maps and fired the man who made them. Which meant Wren had known the stone was there. Which meant Wren had either found it himself, by luck, in all that wilderness —

— or he had known about the survey. Had seen the stone and understood what it was, because the man who shot him — no. Because the men who shot him wore Rivergate badges, and Rivergate had sealed the survey, and the only reason to seal a survey of a silver-bearing river was that the survey proved something the company could not afford to have proven.

Her father had surveyed the source first.

Not Wren. Not the founder whose name was stamped on every permit and every share certificate in Rivergate city. Étienne Joubert, dismissed engineer, illegitimate man, dead these fifteen years in some unmarked place upstream — he had run his chains to the head of the Current, had measured the silver country foot by foot, and had put a datum stone in the bank below the Splits like a man driving a stake through a claim.

And then the company had closed the lines. Sealed the survey. Erased him from the record so thoroughly that his own daughter had grown up believing he'd simply failed — believing the compass he left her was a poor man's relic, a tool for a trade that had rejected him.

Wonder came first. It came like heat, like the lantern-light through the paper: *he was there, he was there before any of them, he found it.* Her father, whom she remembered mostly as a smell of graphite and river mud and a voice reading elevations aloud to her like bedtime stories — her father had stood at the source of the Current.

Then the wonder curdled, the way milk curdles, all at once and irreversibly.

Because men who find silver don't stay poor. Men who complete a survey don't get erased unless someone very powerful needs them erased. And the last time anyone had spoken of Étienne Joubert in her hearing, it was a company clerk saying *deserted his post, went upriver, no doubt drowned* — the official version, filed and forgotten.

He hadn't deserted anything. He had been silenced. And the sealing of his survey — the closing of his lines — had been done by the same company whose badges Wren's shooters had worn, whose permits were expiring in five weeks, whose enforcer was even now beating upriver behind them in a steamer named *Perpetual*.

"Odile," Freya said.

The pilot opened one eye. She slept lightly, half-sitting against a crate, hat over her face. "Mm."

"When you piloted for the company — before the flood — did you ever carry a survey crew? Twenty years ago. Up past the Splits."

The hat came off. Odile looked at her across the small fire, and whatever she saw in Freya's face made her sit up properly.

"There was a surveyor," she said slowly. "Before my time on the water, but the old pilots talked of him. A quiet man. Half-blood, they said — said it wrong, the way they say such things. Ran his lines all the way to the head of the river when nobody else would go up there. Then the company pulled everyone off the upper Current, sealed the charts, and told us the water above the Splits didn't exist anymore." She paused. "Joubert. That was his name. Why?"

Freya looked down at the two maps glowing against each other, the charcoal and the ink, the dead prospector's distances and her father's elevations, both chained to the same stone in the same bank.

"Because I'm going to stand on his datum," she said, "and I need to know who else knows it's there."

Odile was quiet a long moment. The river talked to itself in the dark beyond the willows, patient, enormous, keeping its accounts.

"Company knows," she said at last. "Company always knows. That's what a company *is* — a machine for knowing things and burying them." She lay back down and pulled the hat over her face again. "Sleep, surveyor's daughter. Tomorrow we run the Splits, and after the Splits there's no more charts for anybody, theirs or yours. Just the water and what it remembers."

Freya did not sleep, not really. She sat by the banked fire with the compass open in her palm, its needle trembling toward a north that suddenly felt like a direction someone had lied to her about, and she thought about her father's hands setting a chisel against a ledge twenty years ago, cutting a cross into stone that he must have known — must have *hoped* — someone would come looking for someday.

She had assumed she was following Wren's map upstream.

Lying awake on the gravel bar with the Current murmuring its one long sentence, Freya understood that she might be following something much older than that. The maps shared a datum. The datum shared a grave, somewhere ahead, or a living man, or a secret with a company's fingerprints all over it.

Above the Splits, the charts ended.

Whatever came next, no one had closed those lines yet.
