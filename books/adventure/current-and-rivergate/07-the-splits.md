## 7. The Splits

The river told Odile Marchand things before it told anyone else. It had always been that way — a tremor in the water ahead of a shoal, a change in the birds' behavior before a squall, a smell of wet limestone where the current ran over stone no chart had ever bothered to name. She had learned to read it as a girl, standing barefoot on her father's barge deck with her brother Étienne laughing at her from the tiller, both of them certain the Current would carry them anywhere they wanted to go for the rest of their lives.

The river had been right about everything except that.

Now, three days upstream from Verrin's Crossing with the Joubert woman and her borrowed pilot-boat, Odile felt the river trying to tell her something again, and she did not want to hear it. The water had gone strange in the last hour — slower, wider, the color of old pewter where it should have run green over gravel. The banks had begun to lean inward. Somewhere ahead, out of sight around a bluff thick with cedar, the Current was preparing to make its argument about which way a body ought to go.

She knew what the argument was. She had been dreading it since Verrin's Crossing, since the moment she'd said *as far as the Splits* and watched the engineer woman nod as though that were a place like any other, a bend, a landing, a town.

The Splits was not a place like any other. The Splits was where the river asked you what you were willing to drown for.

"You've gone quiet," Freya Joubert said from the bow.

Odile kept her hands on the wheel spokes. The boat was her own — or had been, before the company struck her name from the pilots' rolls and the rivermen stopped speaking it aloud; a thirty-foot shallow-draft sternwheeler she'd bought with the money she swore she would never spend and had spent anyway, because a woman could not haunt the levees forever without something to haunt in. *The Magpie*, her father had called her. A bird that stole bright things. A bird that talked.

"I'm always quiet," Odile said.

"You're always quiet *while working*," Freya said. "This is a different quiet. I've spent six years reading men who go quiet when they're deciding whether to tell me something."

Odile almost smiled. Almost. The woman had a surveyor's eye and a surveyor's bluntness, and both of those had been wearing at Odile's silence for three days like water on a lock gate. She had hired on for coin and for the small, mean pleasure of taking Rivergate money to do something Rivergate would hate, and she had told herself the trip would end at the Splits, at the legal channel, at the moment she pointed downstream toward the eastern fork and said *that way lies the concession, and I'm done*, and the woman would go on alone into whatever the company had waiting for her, and Odile would go back to being dead.

That had been the plan. Plans, on the Current, were what you made so the river could have something to laugh at.

"There's a landing ahead," she said instead. "Old logging stage, rotted through, but it'll hold us till morning. I don't run the fork in the dark, and I don't run it hungry."

Freya looked at her a moment longer — that level gray look, the compass of a woman who had been navigating other people's lies since she was old enough to hold a rod and chain — and then turned forward without another word and began making ready to tie off. Odile was grateful for that. Gratitude was not something she had expected to feel toward a Joubert.

***

The old logging stage groaned under the Magpie's lines all night, a sound like a man talking in his sleep. Odile sat in the wheelhouse with a tin lamp turned low and a bottle of white rum she had promised herself she wouldn't open and opened anyway, and she watched the fork take shape in the last gray light.

The Current came down out of the north in two arms. The eastern channel was the legal one — wide, charted, buoyed by the company in the season when the company bothered, the channel every permit and every manifest and every honest or dishonest pilot's license recognized as the way up to the concession lands. It was a good channel. She had run it a hundred times. It went where it said it went, past the assay camps and the company landings, and at the end of it, if the map the dying man had traded Freya Joubert was true, lay the forbidden source and whatever it was that had gotten Aldous Wren shot and left in the ditch outside Verrin's Crossing.

The western channel had no name on any chart. The rivermen who still spoke of it called it the gorge, or the dead pilot's run, or — and this only ever in a whisper, and only ever with a glance at Odile, and only ever before she stopped drinking in company — *Marchand's Folly*. It went up through limestone country where the river pinched itself down to a third of its width and dropped through rapids that no company underwriter would insure and no company pilot was licensed to attempt. It was shorter to the headwaters by four days. It was also, in the arithmetic of the Current, where eleven men had gone into the water and not come out.

Odile had been at the wheel.

That was the sentence she carried. Not the company's sentence — the company had given her a different one, a worse one, which was a check and a signature and the instruction to say the flood had been an act of God and the barges had been lost with all hands despite every precaution. Eleven men. The *Belle Rivièr*e's tow, caught in the spring rise, ordered through the narrows at a stage the company's own gauge said was suicide, because the silver contract had a penalty clause and the penalty clause had teeth and Silas Crane's signature was at the bottom of the order. Odile had refused. Crane had taken the wheel from her — no, that wasn't true, and she made herself keep it true even now, even alone, even with the rum — Crane had not taken the wheel. Crane had simply reminded her, very quietly, that her brother was one of the eleven, that Étienne was a deckhand on the second barge and a man did not get to choose which orders he obeyed and which he inherited, and Odile had looked at the water and the water had looked like a wall, and she had taken the tow through anyway, because she was the best pilot on the river and the best pilot on the river should have been able to do the impossible, and the river had shown her the difference between best and able.

Nine men from the first barge. Étienne and one other from the second. The company had paid for the funerals and then paid for the silence, and Odile had taken both, and the second payment was the one she could still feel in her hands, the way a woman who has hauled a drowning man feels the rope afterward, for years, in her sleep.

She had signed. She had taken the money. She had let the company write *act of God* into the record with her name underneath as witness, and she had told herself it was for her mother, for the house, for the grave marker Étienne would not have gotten otherwise, and all of that was even true, and none of it had made the rope let go.

The lamp hissed. Out in the dark, the fork waited.

She heard Freya's boots on the deck below — the woman slept like a cat, in short watches, always surfacing — and then the hatch, and then Freya climbed to the wheelhouse with two cups of coffee that smelled like it had been boiled three times, which on the Magpie it likely had.

"You didn't sleep," Freya said.

"I don't, much." Odile took the cup. Their fingers didn't touch; Freya had the particular care of a woman who had learned not to reach for people.

"Tomorrow we take the east channel," Freya said. It wasn't quite a question. "You said as far as the Splits. The Splits is tomorrow. I'll want to know what you'll want for the run back down, so I can plan the money."

And there it was, the whole ugly transaction laid out neat as a ledger line, and Odile found she could not bear the neatness of it. She set the coffee down on the chart table, on top of Wren's wax-cloth map, and looked at the fork through the wheelhouse glass, and heard herself say:

"Your father's map. The closed lines. May I see it again?"

Freya unrolled it without hesitation — that was the thing about her, Odile thought, she trusted like a woman who had already survived being wrong about it — and weighted the corners with the coffee cups and the rum bottle and a brass fitting from the floor. The old survey spread out under the lamp: the Current in its company-approved course, and then the closed lines, the ones the Rivergate company had struck from the record, running up the western fork, up through the gorge, up to a datum point marked in a dead surveyor's small, exact hand.

Odile put her finger on the western channel.

"I ran this water," she said. "Before. Before it was closed. My father ran it before me, and his father before him, and we were the only ones who ever ran it regular, because it takes a family's memory to run water that isn't written down. Every eddy, every ledge, every rock that moves in the spring ice and settles somewhere new by June. The gorge is runnable. It was always runnable. What it isn't is *forgivable*."

Freya said nothing. She had the patience of her profession; Odile had watched her stand over a theodolite for an hour without shifting her weight. It was infuriating, and it was, tonight, exactly what Odile needed.

"Six years ago," Odile said, "the company ordered a tow through the narrows at flood stage. Eleven men. I was the pilot. I said no, and then I was made to say yes, and the river took all eleven." Her voice did not shake. She had rehearsed it not shaking for six years, in the dark, alone, and the rehearsal held. "One of the eleven was my brother."

Freya's breath went out of her, slow and controlled, the way it did when she was absorbing a load rather than resisting it.

"The company paid me," Odile went on, because now that the gate was open the whole current came through at once, six years of it. "Not compensation — I never signed for compensation. They paid me to witness the finding. *Act of God. Unprecedented rise. All precautions observed.* My name is on that finding. My signature. The company keeps it in a drawer in Rivergate, and as long as it's in the drawer, I am officially dead — they struck me off the rolls the same week, drowned in effigy, so no one would ever ask the witness anything awkward. And I took the money, Joubert. I want you to understand that part, because you're about to ask me for something, and you should know what it is you're asking. I took the money and I let them bury my brother under a lie, and I have spent six years being paid for it in a coin that doesn't spend."

The lamp hissed. The stage groaned. Freya looked at the map, at the closed lines, at the western fork, and Odile watched her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic Odile had been doing all night, and arriving, Odile could see, at the same terrible sum.

"Crane signed the tow order," Freya said quietly.

"Crane signed everything. Crane signs everything. That's what he's *for*." Odile took up her coffee and drank it, bitter and triple-boiled, and it steadied her. "You want to know why I'm telling you this now, at the fork, instead of at Verrin's Crossing when you hired me. I'll tell you. Because at Verrin's Crossing I still believed I'd take you up the east channel, point you at the concession, and wash my hands. Because I told myself the dead don't need anything from me but the marker I bought them. And because three days on this boat with you and that map have made a liar out of me, Joubert, and I can't run the legal channel tomorrow knowing what I know."

"Which is what?"

"Which is that the east channel is a company channel." Odile laid her finger on it, on the neat dotted line of the charted course. "There's a toll station at the upper landing. There's a company pilot stationed there — a man named Gaspard, who is a coward and a drunk, which makes him reliable in one way only: he reports everything. The moment your boat touches that landing, Crane knows where you are, what you're carrying, and how many hours he needs to be ahead of you. And there's this." She moved her finger to the closed lines, to the gorge, to the datum point. "Your father surveyed the western fork. Whatever he found at the end of it, the company closed this channel the same season he disappeared. You don't close a river because it's dangerous, Joubert. The Current is dangerous everywhere. You close a river because of what's at the end of it."

Freya was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "You'd run the gorge."

"I'd run the gorge," Odile said, and the words came out of her like water through a breached sluice, six years of held-back current, and she found that her hands were steady on the chart, steadier than they had been since the day she signed. "Not because I'm brave. I'm not. I've had six years to learn exactly how brave I'm not. I'd run it because my brother went into the water on a company order and the company wrote *act of God* over his name, and I took their money, and there is only one place on this river where I can put any of that down, and it's not in a drawer in Rivergate. It's up that channel. Whatever's at the end of it — your father, Wren's silver, Crane himself — I'd rather meet it than meet another morning with that signature in my hands."

She had not meant to say all of it. She had meant to say half of it, the practical half, the half about Gaspard and the toll station. But the river had gone on telling her things all night, and the thing it had been telling her was that the debt had come due, the way debts on the Current always came due — not when you could pay, but when the water rose.

Freya reached across the chart and put her hand flat on the closed lines, over the datum point, and Odile saw that the engineer woman's hands were not quite steady either, and understood that she was not the only one on this boat carrying a rope-mark.

"The east channel is four days slower and a company ear," Freya said. "The gorge is three days of water that's killed everyone who ran it carelessly."

"Everyone who ran it *carelessly*," Odile said. "I never ran it carelessly in my life. I ran it once frightened, on a bad order, at the wrong stage, and I've had six years to learn every lesson the river taught me that night. It won't be the same water. It's never the same water. But I know its temper better than any living pilot, and I'm done being dead." She looked up. "There's one condition."

"Name it."

"When we come back down — if we come back down — we come down through the gorge again, and when we reach Rivergate, you put my name in whatever record you're building. Not Marchand the dead pilot. Odile Marchand, witness. I signed one lie for that company. I'll sign the truth against it."

Freya extended her hand across the chart, over the closed lines, and Odile took it, and the grip was a surveyor's grip — measured, deliberate, a woman driving a stake.

***

Dawn came up the color of pewter and old silver, and the Current ran high and fast with the melt from the limestone country, and the fork lay ahead of them like a held breath.

Odile stood at the wheel with the Magpie's engine turning over slow beneath her feet, and she felt the rope-mark in her hands one last time — and then she let it go, the way you let go of a line only when the new line is made fast. Freya stood at her shoulder with Wren's map and her father's compass, and below them the river split around the cedar bluff, east toward the company and its ledgers, west toward the gorge and whatever the dead had left there.

Then Freya's hand closed on Odile's arm, hard, and pointed downstream.

Smoke. A smudge of it, low against the southern haze, moving — and under it, catching the early light, the black stack and white superstructure of a steamer coming up the legal channel with the current behind her, big, fast, flying no flag she needed to see to know. Odile had watched that boat built. She knew her lines the way a woman knows the lines of the man who ruined her.

The *Perpetual*. Crane's boat. Crane's crew. A day behind them, maybe less, and closing on the east channel — the channel Odile had been about to tell her passenger they'd be fools to take, and which now, with the company's own steamer climbing it toward them, was no channel at all.

"There's your answer," Odile said, and put the wheel over west.

The Magpie came about, and the Current took her bow, and the dead pilot's run opened ahead of them — narrow, loud, and waiting.
