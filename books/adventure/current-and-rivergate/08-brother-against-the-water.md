## 8. Brother Against the Water

They came for him at half past six in the morning, when the permit office smelled of cold ink and yesterday's tobacco, and Silas Crane did not knock so much as let the door admit him.

"Joubert." Crane stood in the doorway with his hat in one hand — he was one of those men who removed his hat indoors not out of courtesy but to make you look at his face, and his face was a ledger itself, every entry balanced, nothing forgiven. "You'll ride with me."

Tomas set down his pen with exaggerated care, as though the interruption were a small thing, an inconvenience of no consequence. He had learned long ago that fear was best disguised as mild irritation; it was the one performance his face could sustain without rehearsal. "Ride where?"

"The Perpetual. She steams on the morning tide." Crane laid the hat on the corner of Tomas's desk, precisely centered, and that small act frightened Tomas more than anything else the man had done, because it said: *I am not leaving until this is settled.* "There's a woman upstream with a map she has no right to. I'm told you know her."

"I know a great many women," Tomas said. "I file their permits. I renew their concessions. It's dull work, but someone must do it."

Crane smiled. It was not a comforting sight. It was the smile of a man who had already read the last page of your account and was simply waiting for you to catch up. "The manifest you filed Tuesday. The *Larkspur*, outbound, three passengers, freight listed as survey instruments." He turned the hat a quarter-turn on the desk. "The Larkspur doesn't carry survey instruments. The Larkspur carries salt pork and rope. And the clerk who signed her manifest — a Mr. T. Joubert, whose hand I would know anywhere, being a student of hands — dated her departure two days after she'd actually cleared the dock. Three days of fog, Joubert. You sold her three days of fog, and you did it badly enough that I found it inside a day."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the harbor bells were ringing the change of tide, and Tomas heard each stroke as though it were struck against his own ribs.

"If there's been an error in my filing—"

"There has been an error in your loyalty," Crane said, without heat, which was worse. "That's all right. Errors can be corrected. That's what I do. I correct errors." He picked up the hat again. "You'll come aboard. Not as crew. As insurance. A company insures its cargo, and you, Joubert, are cargo now — mine. If the woman is who I think she is, your presence will simplify matters considerably. Families should travel together."

He said it the way a butcher says *families should eat together*, and then he waited by the door while Tomas collected his coat, because Crane was a patient man, and patience, Tomas was beginning to understand, was the most terrible instrument in his case.

* * *

The Perpetual was the finest vessel Rivergate Consolidated owned, and she wore her money openly: brass rails polished to mirrors, paddle housings painted the deep green of banknotes, a whistle that spoke in the imperious voice of a cathedral organ. She drew too much water for the upper Current and everyone aboard knew it, which was why she carried a full complement of enforcers instead of deckhands — fourteen men in company gray who moved with the unhurried confidence of people paid to be feared rather than liked.

Tomas spent the first day learning them by not looking at them. He learned that they called Crane nothing at all, that orders passed between them in a shorthand of glances, that none of them drank, and that they treated Tomas with a courtesy so complete it functioned as a cage. They brought him coffee. They asked after his health. One of them, a broad man with a burn-scarred jaw named Hackett, showed him how the railings unscrewed from the deck plates, in case, Hackett said, Mr. Joubert felt unsteady in the swells.

*In case I need to be restrained,* Tomas translated, and thanked him warmly.

By the second night he had mapped the boat in his head the way he mapped everything — permits, manifests, the geography of his own cowardice. The engine room lay aft, below the waterline, hot as a confession booth. The boiler gauges were read every hour by an engineer named Prewitt, an old man with cotton in his ears and a saint's indifference to conversation. The gauge glass on the starboard boiler — the one Crane's own cabin sat directly above, Tomas noted, noting it the way a man notes the location of his own heart — was hairline-cracked near the fitting. He saw it on the second evening, during the aimless walk he took each night past the engine room hatch, and he stood over it for perhaps four seconds, four seconds in which his entire life rearranged itself around a single crack in a tube of glass.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not stop performing: the Perpetual was fast. Faster than anything Odile Marchand could put on the river. Every hour she ran true closed the gap to Freya by miles, and at the end of those miles was Crane, and at the end of Crane was whatever happened to women who carried maps the company wanted burned. His sister. Half-blood, illegitimate, raised on the wrong side of their father's respectability — and still, God help him, his sister, the girl who had once walked eleven miles in the rain to bring him a book he'd mentioned wanting, back when they were children and the family's divisions hadn't yet hardened into law.

He had betrayed her before. He knew the shape of it from the inside, the way a man knows a scar. Five years ago, when the company had asked him to confirm that Freya's field figures were unreliable — a signature, only a signature, and his probation made permanent, his name entered into the legitimate world at last — he had signed. He had told himself it was survival. He had told himself she'd understand. She had never spoken of it since, which was not the same as forgiveness, and he had built his whole careful life on top of that silence like a house built on a filled-in well.

And now he stood above a cracked gauge glass, doing sums.

If the boiler failed — if the starboard gauge burst and the pressure ran wild and the Perpetual limped into some riverside yard for repairs — the pursuit would lose a day. Perhaps two. Two days was the margin Freya needed; he had bought her three with the manifest, and Crane had taken two of them back before the paint was dry.

It was not a plan. He understood that even as he made it. It was a fever dressed as a plan. But fevers have their own logic, and by the third night, anchored at the Splits while Crane sent scouts up both channels, Tomas had stopped arguing with his.

* * *

He did it badly, and later he would understand that this was the mercy in the whole affair — that his incompetence saved more lives than his courage ever could.

Prewitt slept between readings, his chair tipped against the bulkhead, his deafness a wall around him. The engine room at midnight was a cathedral of red light and rhythmic iron, and Tomas came down the ladder with a wrench he had no use for and a story about a dripping fitting if anyone asked. No one asked. The fireman on watch was dozing against the coal bunker with the beautiful shamelessness of the exhausted.

The gauge glass was mounted in a brass fitting, and the fitting was held by two nuts, and Tomas — a clerk, a man of ledgers, whose hands knew pens and nothing else — went at the lower nut with fingers gone stupid from heat and fear. The nut turned. The crack widened with a sound like a held breath being released, and a thread of scalding water jumped out and caught him across the knuckles, and he jerked back, and the wrench rang off the deck plate like a church bell announcing something.

The whole engine room woke at once. Prewitt came out of his chair already shouting. The fireman came up swinging a shovel. And through the hatch, unhurried as weather, came Silas Crane.

What happened next took ninety seconds and taught Tomas more about the man than five years of watching him across permit desks. Crane did not run. He crossed the engine room, looked at the weeping crack, looked at the wrench, looked at Tomas's scalded hand — and then he reached past all of it and closed the isolation valve himself, two turns, smooth as closing a ledger, and the jet of water died to a drip and the pressure needle began its slow, obedient fall.

"Stand her down to half steam," Crane said to Prewitt. "She'll hold till morning at half." Then he turned to Tomas, and the engine room was very quiet except for the iron, and Crane said, in the tone of a man discussing freight rates:

"You'll want that hand seen to. Hackett keeps a salve."

"That's it?" Tomas heard himself say. "I've just tried to cripple your boat, and that's it?"

Crane studied him for a moment, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was the most frightening thing yet. "Do you know what I did before I worked for Rivergate, Joubert? Twenty-two years. Do you know how many men tried to slow me down, in twenty-two years?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Every one of them. And do you know what I learned?" He stepped closer, and the boiler's heat made the air between them shimmer. "A man who sabotages something isn't dangerous. A man who sabotages something is *bound*. Because now he's done it. Now there's no going back to being the clerk who filed a bad manifest. Now he's the man who tried to kill a boatload of his fellow employees, and when this reaches Rivergate — and it will reach Rivergate, because I write my reports carefully — there is exactly one person in the world who can keep him out of prison." Crane smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, and Tomas wished devoutly that it hadn't. "Me."

"You knew," Tomas said. The words came out flat, dead, like figures that wouldn't balance. "You knew about the gauge. You let me—"

"I suspected. I watched. I let." Crane picked up the fallen wrench, weighed it, set it on Prewitt's bench with a neatness that was somehow obscene. "A man's first choice tells you what he is. His second tells you what he'll always be. Your father chose Rivergate over his own blood, Joubert, and everyone said what a fine, sensible choice it was. I merely gave you the same examination." He moved toward the hatch, then paused, one hand on the rail, and delivered the rest without turning around, which was his way of making even cruelty feel like paperwork. "Your sister is running the gorge channel. My pilot read their wake at dusk — shallow draft, cutting for the dead water past the Splits. Nobody runs the gorge. So either she's desperate, or she's got somebody desperate piloting her, and either way she'll be in the narrows by tomorrow noon, and the Perpetual will be waiting at the far end with half steam and a full hold." He glanced back, finally, and his voice dropped into something almost kind. "Get your hand bandaged, Mr. Joubert. You'll want to be presentable when you see her. Family reunions are rare things on this river. I'd hate for you to miss yours."

Then he was gone up the ladder, and the engine room closed around Tomas like a fist.

* * *

He sat afterward on the forward deck with his bandaged hand in his lap and the river sliding black beneath the running lights, and he did the only arithmetic left to him.

He had thought, going down that ladder with the wrench, that he was choosing his sister. That was the story he had told himself in the hot dark, the romance of it, the redemption: the clerk who finally, at cost, chose right. And instead he had chosen nothing. Worse than nothing. Crane had used his rebellion as rivets — had let him swing the wrench, had let the crack spread, had stood aside and timed it all so that Tomas's one act of courage became the chain that bound him tighter than any signature ever had. He was not a man who had defected. He was a weapon that had been fired and then reloaded, and the firing had been witnessed, and the ammunition was his own guilt.

His father had stood in some room like this once — Tomas was sure of it now, sure in his bones — and weighed the same scales, and chosen the house and the name and the legitimate son, and let the daughter and the compass and the river go. Everyone had called it sensible. Crane had just shown Tomas the machinery underneath the word *sensible*: you did the small betrayal, and then the small betrayal did you, and after that you were company property, maintained and insured and aimed.

Below his feet, the boilers ticked as they cooled toward half steam, gathering themselves for morning. Ahead, past the dark shoulder of the Splits, the gorge channel muttered to itself in the language of white water — a sound like a crowd, like a verdict being read aloud. Somewhere in that noise was a shallow-draft boat, a drowned-woman pilot, and his sister, running water that killed eleven men the last time anyone tried it.

Tomas looked at his bandaged hand, at the blisters already rising pink beneath the linen, and understood that Crane had miscalculated after all — not about the trap, which had closed perfectly, but about the product. A weapon, fired, does not necessarily stay aimed where its owner points it. Somewhere between the wrench and the scalding water, something had broken in Tomas Joubert that was not the gauge glass, and it was not going to be repaired by salve or by silence or by any report Silas Crane cared to write.

At dawn the Perpetual raised half steam and turned her bow toward the gorge, and Tomas stood at the rail in the gray light, watching the walls of the narrows rise on either side like the pages of a book closing, and he began — quietly, methodically, with a clerk's attention to detail — to count the seconds between the boat's engine beats, and to think about what a man could do with ninety seconds, if he was willing to pay for them with everything he had left.
