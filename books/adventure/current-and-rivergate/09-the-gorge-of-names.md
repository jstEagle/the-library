## 9. The Gorge of Names

The river changed its voice at the mouth of the gorge.

Freya had learned its moods in the six days since the Splits — the low mutter of shallow water over gravel, the deep organ-note where it ran against bedrock, the sudden silence of a pool too deep to speak. But here, between walls that rose sheer and gray on either side, the Current made a sound she had no word for. It was less a voice than a crowd of them, a thousand murmurs layered over a single sustained roar, as if the water were arguing with itself about which way to go.

Odile stood at the tiller with her weight forward, reading the water the way Freya read a grade sheet — not looking at it so much as through it. "Hands off the gunwales," she said without turning. "The rock here is limestone. It flakes. You put your fingers in a crack and the river takes the rest of you."

The boat was a flat-bottomed skiff they'd taken from a fishing camp two days back, patched with pine pitch and prayer. Tomas sat amidships with his arms wrapped around his knees, still gray from his night in the water, saying little. He had said little for three days. Freya found she preferred it to the alternative; when Tomas spoke, he spoke like a clerk, qualifying everything, and she had no patience left for qualifications.

The walls closed in. The sky narrowed to a ribbon of pale blue so high above it seemed painted on. And then, as Odile eased the skiff toward a shelf of gravel on the eastern bank — a shelf that had no business being there, a pocket of calm in all that violence — Freya saw the first slate.

It stood upright in the stones, knee-high, wedged against a boulder: a flat piece of dark shale, its face scratched with letters that time and weather had half-erased but not defeated. She could read them even before the bow ground onto the shingle.

*HOLLIS. 1871.*

She stepped out of the boat before it fully stopped, and her boots were wet, and she did not care.

There was another behind it. *PERRAULT.* Another. *M. OKONKWO, 1868.* A fourth whose name had flaked away entirely, leaving only the year, and then a fifth, and a sixth, and Freya walked up the gravel bar among them with her breath going strange in her chest, counting, because counting was what she did when she could not think — grades, gradients, distances, dead men.

"Odile," she said. Her voice came out wrong, thin. "Odile, how many are there?"

The pilot had not left the boat. She stood with one hand on the tiller and looked up the bar with an expression Freya had never seen on her — not the wry competence, not the hard humor, but something older and more tired, the look of a woman returning to a debt she had hoped was paid.

"Forty-one," Odile said. "When I counted last. That was eleven years ago."

"Who were they?"

"Prospectors." Odile climbed out at last, moving carefully, as if the graves might object. "Every one of them. Men who came up the Current looking for the source. Some came alone. Some came in pairs. None of them came back down."

Freya turned in a slow circle. The slates stood scattered along perhaps sixty yards of bank, some clustered, some solitary, each one angled the same way — facing the water, facing downstream, as if the dead had been laid out to watch the current carry their news home to whoever would listen. The stone of the gorge rose behind them, three hundred feet of gray limestone streaked white with mineral stain, and somewhere above, unseen, the river's source waited behind whatever wall the company had built around it on paper.

"Nobody came back," Freya repeated. "And nobody asked why?"

"Somebody asked." Odile crouched beside the nearest slate and touched its edge with two fingers, almost gently. "I asked. I asked loud enough that the company gave me a job running barges instead of questions, and when I kept asking after the flood, they gave me a grave instead. On paper." She smiled without any warmth in it. "You've seen my headstone, I'm told. Very tasteful. Rivergate Cemetery, plot forty-four. There's a stranger in it. Probably he doesn't mind."

Tomas had come ashore too. He stood at the edge of the bar with his hands in his pockets, and Freya watched him read the names the way he read everything — lips moving slightly, cataloguing. Then he stopped moving. He had reached a slate near the upstream end, one set apart from the others beneath a leaning birch whose roots had grown around it like a hand closing.

He didn't say anything. He just went still, the way a man goes still when he has stepped on something that might be a snake.

Freya crossed the gravel to him. The birch's shadow lay cold across the stone, and the lichen had crept in from the edges, but the letters had been cut deep — cut by someone who wanted them to last, who had taken the time to go over every stroke twice.

*E. JOUBERT.*

*1866.*

The gorge roared. The ribbon of sky went very far away.

"No," Freya said. She heard herself say it and heard how useless it was, the word falling into the sound of the water like a coin into a well. "No, that's — there are other Jouberts. It's a common name. It's—"

"It isn't," Tomas said quietly. "You know it isn't."

She knelt. Her knees hit the gravel and she barely felt it. The E was unmistakable — Étienne, cut with the accent mark rendered as a small notch, the way her father had always written it on his survey headers, quick and impatient, the notch more suggestion than letter. She knew that hand. She had spent fifteen years telling herself she remembered nothing of him — he'd gone upriver when she was nine, and what does a nine-year-old keep? — but she kept this. She kept the way he crossed his T's like a level line, perfectly horizontal, a man who could not draw a crooked stroke even in grief or haste.

Someone had carved this. Someone with steady hands and time enough to do it twice over.

"He died in Verrin's Crossing," she said. "That's the story. He drowned in the spring flood, 1866, and they never found the body, and Mama took the compensation money and drank most of it, and—" Her voice cracked along a fault line she hadn't known was there. "That's the story, Tomas. That's the story we were both raised on."

"The story the company told," Tomas said.

"Yes. The story the company told." She pressed her palm flat against the slate. The stone was cold even through the afternoon sun, cold as the water, cold as the arithmetic assembling itself in her head whether she wanted it or not. 1866. Her father had come upriver in 1865 with a survey contract — she knew that much, it was the one thing her mother had said clearly near the end, *your father went to measure the river and the river measured him instead*, drunk words that Freya had filed away as drunkenness rather than testimony. If he had died here, in this gorge, in 1866—

Then he hadn't drowned in the spring flood at Verrin's Crossing.

Then someone had carried the story four hundred miles downstream and set it in place like a keystone, and built the rest of her childhood on top of it.

"Grief comes before proof," Odile said behind her. Freya hadn't heard her approach. "Let it. Proof will keep. Grief won't wait, and if you make it wait it'll come back later at a bad time, in the dark, when you need your hands steady." The pilot crouched beside her, not touching, just present, the way she was at the tiller — reading, waiting. "My brother's name is on a plaque in Saint-Aubin. Eleven names, cast in bronze, right by the dock. I used to go and stand there until I couldn't feel my feet. It didn't bring him back and it didn't make the company sorry. But it was mine. Nobody could take the standing there away from me."

Freya realized she was crying only when a drop fell on the slate and darkened the lichen. She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, roughly, furious at the weakness and knowing, in Odile's terms, that it wasn't weakness at all — it was the toll. The river charged it. Everyone who came up here paid.

"He reached the source," she said. It wasn't a question anymore. She could feel it settling into place like a datum point, fixed and final. "He came up here in '65 under contract, he found something — the silver, Wren's silver, whatever's up there — and he came back down this gorge, and somebody killed him for it, and then they wrote him a drowning in a town he never saw again." She looked up at Odile. "Forty-one graves. How many of those men found something?"

"All of them," Odile said. "That's why they're here."

* * *

They made camp on the gravel bar because Odile refused to run the next stretch in failing light, and because, she admitted, the dead made better neighbors than the rapids ahead. They ate hard bread and river fish Tomas caught with a hook and line from the supply pack, and the fire was small because smoke drew eyes, though whose eyes Odile wouldn't say.

Freya walked the bar again after eating, slate by slate, with her notebook open. It was work and work was the only rope she had. Name, date, position relative to the waterline, depth of lichen, angle of stone. Forty-one entries. The dates ran backward as she moved upstream — 1871, 1869, 1868, 1867 — and stopped at 1866, at Étienne Joubert, at the birch. No one had come after him. Or no one had been permitted to stay long enough to be buried.

"They're in order," Tomas said. He had followed her without asking, and she was too raw to resent it. He crouched by the 1867 stone. "Oldest downstream, newest upstream. Someone buried them walking up. Someone who was here the whole time."

"Or someone who came back," Freya said. "Year after year. To add to it."

"A caretaker."

"A witness." She closed the notebook. "Companies don't send caretakers, Tomas. Companies send lawyers and enforcers. Whoever cut these names wanted them found eventually. Wanted someone to walk this bar and count."

"Crane's men didn't count," Tomas said. "Crane's men shot Aldous Wren and left him on the road. Whatever this is, it isn't company practice."

"No," Freya agreed. "This is conscience. Which is rarer, and harder to kill."

The firelight reached them only faintly here at the upstream end of the bar. Above the last slate, the gorge wall leaned out, and Freya saw now what she had missed in the shock of finding the name — a seam in the limestone, a vertical crack wider than a door, black as a held breath, with water seeping from its base in a thread of silver that joined the Current below. Not the main channel. Something else. Something the river fed from, or fed into.

"Odile," she called. "Come look at this."

The pilot came, looked, and was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, she spoke to the seam, not to Freya.

"My father piloted this river before the company owned it. He had charts — real ones, ink on linen, drawn by men who'd walked every bank. When Rivergate took the concession, they recalled every chart in the territory. Paid good money for them, too. Burned them in the yard behind the permit office, my father said, in a fire that burned green because of the copper in the ink." She traced the air along the seam. "But a chart is only paper. The river keeps its own copy."

"What's in the seam?" Tomas asked.

"A second channel." Odile turned to face them, and the firelight made her face half bronze, half shadow. "The gorge forks. Not like the Splits — not a fork you can see from the water. The main channel runs straight through to the falls, and the falls are a wall, and every map ever printed says the source is beyond the falls, which is true, and also a lie, because the falls can't be run and the company knows it and lets everyone break their boats and their backs trying." She pointed at the black seam. "The second channel goes through. Inside the rock. My father called it the Under-River. He ran it twice, before I was born. He said it was the finest water he ever steered and he'd never run it again, and he never said why, and I was young enough to think that was caution."

"And now?" Freya asked.

"Now I think he knew what this bar was. I think he helped cut some of these names." Odile's jaw tightened. "And I think the reason he never ran it again is the same reason the company burns its own charts. What's inside the rock doesn't want to be on paper."

Freya looked at the seam. Cold air breathed out of it, smelling of stone and deep water and something mineral, something bright. Her father's compass hung at her neck on its cord, and she became aware of it suddenly, a small weight against her sternum, as if the instrument had opinions of its own about where they were standing.

"If we take the main channel," Tomas said slowly, working it through, clerk-fashion, "we reach the falls. We stop. Crane reaches the falls behind us, and he has the Perpetual, and we have a patched skiff, and that's the end of the arithmetic."

"If we take the seam," Freya said, "we go where the maps don't."

"Where the maps were *made not to go*," Odile corrected. "Understand the difference. This isn't blank paper. This is erased paper. Somebody drew this channel once and then drew a line through it, and people have died for the difference." She looked at the graves, at the birch, at the slate with the level-crossed T's. "I swore off this water. I told you at the Splits I'd take you as far as the gorge and no farther, and here we are, and here's where I get to be a coward with a clear conscience, because technically I kept my word."

"But," Freya said.

Odile was quiet. Downstream, the fire popped. Somewhere above them, invisible in the dark, the source poured its silver thread into the world, unmeasured, unmapped, unclaimed except by forty-one slates and one woman's oath to a dying man.

"But my brother ran it twice," Odile said at last, "and I owe him a run I never gave him. And I owe eleven men in Saint-Aubin a truth I sold cheap. And I am tired," and here her voice did something Freya had never heard it do — it frayed, just slightly, at one edge — "of being dead on paper while other men spend my river."

She turned and walked back toward the fire, and her footsteps on the gravel sounded, to Freya, like a decision already made.

Freya stayed. She knelt once more at the birch, at the slate, and took the compass from around her neck and held it flat in her palm above the carved name. The needle swung, settled, steadied — and it did not point north. It pointed up the bar, past the last grave, directly at the black seam in the rock, and it trembled there, quivering like a struck string, as if it had been waiting fifteen years to be brought to this exact spot and allowed, at last, to say what it knew.

"Tomas," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "Bring the light. Bring the light, and bring me Wren's map. I want to know what Papa was pointing at."
