## 10. Wren's Daughter

Saint-Aubin announced itself the way dying things do — by smell before sight. Woodsmoke and river mud first, then the sweeter rot of the mission's orchard, apples left to fall where the brothers no longer had hands enough to gather them. The town sat on a shelf of clay above the Current's western bank, a scatter of whitewashed buildings gone gray at the waterline, dominated by the mission itself: a chapel with a leaning bell tower, a dormitory roofed in tin that flashed like a signal mirror when the sun found it, and behind both, a walled garden where something green still grew against all sense.

Odile brought the boat in against the current with the economy of long habit, feathering the sweep so they slid into the lee of the mission dock without a wasted stroke.

"Twenty minutes," she said. "The brothers will sell us flour and salt pork if we don't frighten them. They'll sell it faster if we do."

Freya was already standing, the oilcloth packet heavy inside her coat, strapped flat against her ribs where it had ridden for nine days. She had slept with it there. She had bathed around it. It had become less a thing she carried than an organ she could not spare.

"Tomas," she said, and stopped. Her half-brother was at the bow, coiling line, and he looked up with that expression he wore now — the one of a man perpetually listening for footsteps behind him. "Stay with Odile. Keep the boat ready to leave."

"You're going to send them." Not a question. He'd watched her check the packet every morning since the gorge, since the slate markers, since *Joubert* scratched into stone by a hand too weak to cut deep.

"I gave my word."

"To a dead man."

"To a man who was dying when I found him and dead by the time I buried him, which is not the same as never having lived." She pulled her collar up. "The papers go to his daughter. That's the whole of my part in this, Tomas. Everything else — the source, the silver, my father — that's mine. But the papers were never mine to keep."

He said nothing to that, which from Tomas was a kind of agreement. He had been saying very little since the boiler gauge aboard the *Perpetual*, since Crane's hand on his shoulder and the understanding that had come with it. He flinched now at authority the way she flinched at falsified figures.

* * *

The mission's store room smelled of candle wax and dried fish. Brother Anselme — a wide, slow man whose apron bore the stains of a hundred kitchens — counted out flour and hardtack while Freya asked her questions with the careful casualness of someone who had learned, over nine days of being hunted, to make ordinary requests sound ordinary.

"The mail rider," she said. "Does he still come through?"

"Thursdays." Anselme weighed salt pork on a brass scale, his thumb pressing the pan down as though honesty required assistance. "Gaspard. He goes downriver to Ferriol, catches the company packet from there. You have letters?"

"One letter. One package." She kept her voice level. "It needs to reach Rivergate. A woman named Marta Wren, care of the assayer's office on Cordwainer Street. Can you write that for me? My hand is..." She showed him her palm, blistered and split from the sweeps and the lines. It was even true.

Anselme wrote the direction in a round, patient script, reading it back twice. Freya paid him in coin and then, because the brothers of Saint-Aubin had a reputation for discretion that outlasted their poverty, paid him again for silence. He took the second payment without offense, which told her how often it happened.

She sealed the packet herself, at the corner of the store room, with her back to the wall. Inside: Wren's claim papers, folded soft as cloth from years in his shirt, and a letter in her own hand stating plainly what she knew — that Aldous Wren held a registered claim to the silver lodes at the head of the Current, that his permit renewal was due within the month, that the papers should be lodged with the assayer before the concession board met. She did not mention the source. She did not mention her father. A promise kept in full did not require her whole life attached to it.

Through the store room's small window she could see the river, brown-green and muscular, and beyond it the eastern hills where the Current went up into country no chart admitted to. Somewhere past those hills was a limestone shelf and a cleft in the rock and, if the compass spoke true, everything her father had died beside. But that was four days upstream and a different errand. This one was older. This one she had sworn on a dying man's breath.

"Gaspard leaves at noon," Anselme said. "You want to give it to him yourself? He likes to know what he carries. Superstitious."

"No." She had imagined it already — handing the packet to a stranger, watching him ride off with the only piece of Wren she could still honor — and the imagining had felt like setting something afloat on a flood. Better it simply be gone. "Tell him it's church business."

Anselme's eyebrows rose a fraction. "It isn't."

"It's a kind of sacrament."

* * *

They ate in the mission refectory because Odile insisted hot food was fuel and cold food was punishment, and because Saint-Aubin was the last place between here and the source where anything hot existed. The brothers served beans and river fish without ceremony. Freya ate mechanically, her eyes going to the window, to the road that climbed away from the town toward the south — the mail road, rutted and pale, empty under a high thin sky.

"He'll make Ferriol by tomorrow night," Odile said quietly. "Packet to Rivergate in three days after. If the assayer lodges the claim same day, Wren's daughter has her rights before your permits expire." She tore bread with her teeth. "It can work."

"You don't believe it can work."

"I believe Gaspard is honest and slow and rides a horse older than the mission." Odile's eyes, dark and unblinking, held hers across the table. "I believe Silas Crane does not pay men to fail. You put a paper on a slow horse, madame. Now we pray the horse outruns the money."

Tomas set down his spoon. "There's another way. I know the manifest schedules. I could—"

"You could get yourself hanged for tampering," Freya said, "and it wouldn't be faster. It's done. It's sent." But her hand had gone, without her willing it, to her ribs, to the place where the packet's weight had lived for nine days, and found only the absence there, strange as a missing tooth.

That absence was the trouble. All the way upriver she had thought of the papers as cargo, as burden, as the price of an oath. Now that they were gone she understood they had also been armor — proof, carried against her heart, that she was a woman who kept her word even when keeping it cost her. Without them she was just a dismissed surveyor with a stolen map and a compass that lied about north, running from a company that owned every mile of the river below the Splits.

She finished her beans. Outside, somewhere, a horse was being saddled.

* * *

She heard the shot just after two o'clock.

One crack, flat and small with distance, rolling down from the southern road. Then nothing. Birds lifted from the orchard in a ragged sheet and resettled. In the refectory, Brother Anselme paused in his washing-up, dishcloth dripping, and looked at the window with the expression of a man who had heard such sounds before and learned not to name them.

Freya was moving before the birds settled.

The southern road climbed through the orchard, between rows of abandoned apple trees, and she ran it with her boots throwing mud, her breath burning, telling herself the whole way that a mail rider's pistol misfiring was a common thing, that horses threw shoes, that the world contained a thousand explanations that were not Silas Crane. The compass swung at her neck on its cord; she gripped it without thinking, the brass warm from her skin.

She found the horse first.

It stood in the middle of the road thirty paces past the last apple tree, grazing with terrible calm, its saddle twisted sideways, one stirrup swinging. Beyond it, in the ditch where the road banked above a drainage cut, lay Gaspard.

He was alive. That was the first thing — the terrible, complicating first thing. He was on his side with his hands pressed to his thigh, and blood came through his fingers in pulses, and his face was the color of the clay banks, and when he saw her his eyes went wide and he tried to say something that came out as a wet rattle.

"Don't talk." She knelt in the ditch, hauling off her coat, wadding it against his leg. His satchel lay open three feet away, contents scattered: letters, a parish bulletin, a tin of tobacco. No packet. No oilcloth square with her seal on it. "Where is it? The package — the one I gave you —"

"Two of them." His voice was a thread. "Came out of the trees. Badges. Company badges. One held me, one went through the bag." He coughed. "Took the big one. Left the rest. Said—" his breath caught as she pressed harder on the wound, "—said to tell you thanks for making it easy."

The world narrowed to the size of a ditch. Freya knelt in another man's blood and saw it whole: the road, the rider, the timing. Crane hadn't intercepted the mail. Crane had *waited* for it. Which meant he had known she would stop at Saint-Aubin, known why, known that a promise was a route on a map as legible as any survey line. She had drawn her own position on his chart with her own oath.

And they had let Gaspard live.

That was the part that turned her cold. Men who wanted silence killed riders in ditches. Men who wanted messages delivered wounded them just enough to be believed, and sent them limping home with words in their mouths. *Thanks for making it easy.* Words meant to travel. Words meant to reach her ears and tell her: we have the papers, we know the daughter's name, we know the assayer's office, and every step you take toward Rivergate now walks into our hand.

"Madame." Gaspard had caught her sleeve with a bloody hand. "The other one. The one who held me. He wasn't wearing his badge right — pinned crooked, like a man dressed in a hurry. And he had a scar." He touched his own jaw, tracing a line. "Here. Down to the chin."

She knew it. Everyone on the Current knew it. Dessaix, Crane's ferret, the man who had once thrown a fisherman off the Ferriol dock for mooring ten minutes past permit. Crooked badge, scarred jaw, and a smile like a hook.

"Can you ride?" she asked.

"On this leg?" He almost laughed, and it cost him. "Brothers will come. Ring the bell, they come running. Go, madame. Whatever's in that paper, it's bought me enough blood for one day."

She rang the bell herself, hauling the rope in the leaning tower until the sound rolled out over the river and the orchards, and then she ran back down through the apple trees with her mind moving faster than her feet.

* * *

Odile listened without interrupting, which was worse than argument. She stood at the rail of the boat, arms crossed, looking at the southern road as if she could read it.

"So," she said at last. "They have the papers. They know the daughter. They know the office. And they've made sure you know they know." She turned. "Crane doesn't destroy evidence, madame. Not anymore, not since the gorge — I've been thinking on it. A man who burns a thing ends the story. Crane wants the story to walk where he wants it. Those papers are worth more to him in his pocket than in a fire, because now they're bait."

"Bait for what? He thinks I'm running for the source. Let him chase me upstream."

"Does he?" Odile's voice was gentle, which from her was alarming. "Or does he think you'll do exactly what you're doing right now — stand on a dock looking like a woman whose promise has been stolen? Because there are two ways to take bait, madame. Chase it, or become it."

Freya stared at the river. The Current moved past the dock, patient, immense, indifferent, carrying everything downstream toward Rivergate — toward Marta Wren, who would wait for papers that were never coming; toward the assayer's office on Cordwainer Street, where a claim unfiled by the expiry date was a claim dead in law; toward Crane, who was counting on the fact that she had sworn an oath and oaths, unlike permits, did not expire.

"They'll deliver the papers themselves," she said slowly. The thought assembled itself as she spoke, ugly and precise. "That's the trap. They lodge the claim — or pretend to — in Wren's daughter's name, and they tell her the woman who promised to bring them wants meeting. Or worse: they use the papers to prove *they* hold the claim's chain of custody, file a salvage action, poison the whole record. Either way, my promise becomes their instrument. Every honest thing I do next, they've already priced."

"Then don't do the honest thing next."

"I swore."

"You swore to see his claim honored." Odile leaned close, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, pilot's voice, the voice for shouting over rapids compressed to a whisper. "Not to see it honored stupidly. A pilot who runs the marked channel because the chart says so, when the chart is six years old and the channel has moved — that pilot drowns eleven men. I know. I was her." She straightened. "The promise hasn't changed. The water has. So read the water."

Freya's hand closed around the compass at her throat. Brass, worn smooth, her father's thumb-grease still in the knurling. It pointed, here at Saint-Aubin, at nothing useful — north, or near enough, or wherever its secret bearing bent it. But her father had built truths into instruments. Hidden bearings inside closed-line maps. A man who hid truth in plain instruments would understand hiding a promise in plain sight.

The papers were gone. But she had written the letter. She had written it in her own hand, and she had read it aloud to herself twice on the ninth night, checking every word by candlelight, and she remembered every word now, and more than the words — she remembered what she had *not* put in it. No mention of the source. No mention of the datum. No mention of the compass, or the shelf, or the second channel. The papers proved Wren's claim. Only she and Tomas and Odile — and the dead — knew where the proof behind the proof lay.

"Crane holds the claim papers," Freya said. "Fine. Claims are arguments about land. I'm holding the land." She looked up at the eastern hills, where the Current went up into the unmapped country, four days' climb from the water's end. "Let him carry his bait to Rivergate. We go up. We find what my father found. And then, Marta Wren won't need papers delivered to her — she'll have something better. Something Crane can't intercept, because it will already be true."

Odile studied her for a long moment. "Four days up, four days down, and the permits expire in—" she calculated, lips moving, "—nineteen. It's close. It's pilot's-margin close."

"It's engineer's-margin close," Freya said. "Which is tighter."

Behind them, from the direction of the mission, came the sound of the bell changing rhythm — the brothers' signal, Anselme had explained, for *come quickly, bring help*. Somewhere in the orchard, men were carrying a mail rider on a door. Somewhere downriver, a steamer with a repaired boiler was burning coal to catch them. And somewhere ahead, up the current and off every chart, a limestone shelf waited over a bright and forbidden water.

Freya stepped aboard.

"Cast off," she said. "We're done letting Crane set our course."

But as the boat swung out into the Current's grip, as Saint-Aubin fell away behind them and the eastern hills rose to meet them, she could not shake the image of Gaspard in the ditch, pressing his coat-wadded wound, saying *thanks for making it easy* — and beneath it, colder, the question she would carry all four days upstream:

If the papers were bait, what, exactly, had Crane expected the bait to catch?

Because it had not caught her. She was upstream and free.

Which meant the trap had never been for her at all.
