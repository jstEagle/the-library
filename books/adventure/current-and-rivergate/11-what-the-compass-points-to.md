## 11. What the Compass Points To

The rain came at dusk and did not stop, drumming on the canvas lean-to Odile had rigged between two leaning cedars above the datum point. Freya sat beneath it with her father's compass open in her palm, and for the third time that evening she asked it a question it refused to answer.

North was wrong.

Not slightly wrong — not the lazy drift of a needle near iron ore, which she had learned to correct in her surveying years by stepping away from any instrument case with a brass hinge. This was something else. The needle swung, settled, trembled, and pointed — steadily, insistently — east-northeast, off the line of the river entirely, into the black wall of the gorge country where no chart she had ever seen marked so much as a tributary.

She had checked it against the stars when the clouds tore open for ten minutes after midnight. Polaris sat where Polaris should sit, and the compass ignored it completely.

"You keep looking at that thing like it owes you money," Odile said from the fire, where she was coaxing a stubborn flame out of river-wet birch. Her voice carried the flat calm of a woman who had spent thirty years reading water and had stopped being surprised by anything that floated.

"It's lying," Freya said.

"Or telling you something you don't want to hear. Those are usually the same thing." Odile fed another stick to the flame. "Your father's?"

Freya turned the instrument over. The brass case was worn smooth at the edges where a thumb had worried it across decades of fieldwork, and the hinge pin had been replaced once with a rivet from something else — a boat, maybe, or a rifle stock. Inside the lid, engraved in a hand she knew better than her own: *E.J. — Current Survey, Year One.* Édouard Joubert. Her father. The man who had walked up this river with a chain and a theodolite and never walked back down.

"He carried it on every closed-line run," she said. "It's in half his field notes. 'Compass true,' he'd write, over and over, like a prayer. Compass true, compass true."

"And now it isn't."

"Now it isn't." Freya spread Wren's wax-cloth map flat on her knees, weighting the corners with stones. The dying prospector's hand had been shaky but sure — a man drawing what he had seen rather than what he had measured. The Current ran up the center in blue-black ink, the forbidden upper reaches sketched in with a confidence that bordered on blasphemy, and there, at the head of it all, a small circle with a cross inside it. The source. The datum point they had reached that afternoon, a spring-fed pool beneath a shelf of grey limestone where the water came up cold and clean and utterly ordinary.

Ordinary except for one thing. She had knelt at its edge and tasted it, an old habit from grade-checking, and found a faint mineral sweetness, a ghost of metal. Silver-bright, Wren's map had promised. She hadn't believed it until she'd cupped the water in her hands and watched the last light catch a shimmer in it, fine as flour, fine as dust ground from ore somewhere upstream. Somewhere the river began in silver.

But the compass didn't care about the spring. The compass pointed past it, through it, as if the source were only a waystation.

* * *

She worked by lamplight, because some problems surrendered only to method, and method was the one inheritance she trusted more than blood.

First she ruled out interference. She stripped off her belt buckle, moved the lamp, walked the compass fifty yards in four directions through the wet grass. The needle held its bearing like a mule holds a grudge. East-northeast, give or take two degrees, unwavering.

Then she got out her father's field notebooks.

She had carried them since Verrin's Crossing — three oilcloth-wrapped volumes recovered from the company archive when she'd cleared her desk, pulled from a drawer marked DISPOSED that some clerk had never gotten around to disposing. They were the closed-line surveys, the runs the company had struck from the public record: grades too steep, tunnels too costly, spurs that led nowhere anyone official would admit to wanting to go. She had read them a hundred times as a girl, before she understood what they were. She read them now as an engineer, and as a daughter, and the two readings kept colliding.

The closed lines were strange. That had always been true, even when she was twelve and tracing the inked routes with a finger. A closed line, properly drawn, is a loop — a survey that returns to its origin, closing the circuit, proving the arithmetic honest. But her father's loops were never quite closed. Every one of them ended a few chains short, a few degrees off, and then simply stopped, the final bearing left hanging like a sentence without its verb. She had assumed, as a child, that he'd died mid-survey. As an engineer she'd assumed sloppy closure, though sloppy was a word she could never make fit the rest of his work, which was immaculate to the second decimal.

Tonight, with a lying compass in her hand, she saw it.

She took a fresh page and plotted the terminal bearings of all three closed lines — the Verrin run, the Kettle Falls run, the abandoned Splits traverse. Three surveys, three years apart, three different starting points along the river. And every one of them terminated on the same bearing: seventy-eight degrees east of north. East-northeast. The exact heading her father's compass was pointing right now, forty feet from her knee, in the rain.

Her breath went shallow. She made herself check it twice, plotting each terminal bearing with the protractor, measuring each angle against the others. Seventy-eight. Seventy-seven and a half. Seventy-eight. It wasn't error. Error scatters. This converged.

He hadn't failed to close his loops. He had *opened* them — deliberately, three times, in three different places, all of them pointing like fingers at the same patch of unmapped country. Any surveyor reviewing the work would see incomplete closures and shake his head. But a surveyor who stood at the end of any one of those dead-end lines and raised his compass would find himself facing the same invisible destination. It was a message written in a language only three kinds of people could read: surveyors, liars, and sons.

And daughters, she thought. Daughters who inherited the compass.

"What have you got?" Odile had come to stand under the lean-to, dripping, her grey hair plastered to her skull. She looked at the maps spread out and her eyes narrowed with a pilot's instinct for anything that changed the shape of the water.

"My father hid a line inside his closed lines." Freya's voice came out steadier than she felt. "Three of them. All ending on the same bearing. And the compass—" she held it up, the needle trembling in its bowl of glass, faithful to something no star could confirm— "the compass is standing on that line right now. We're not at the source, Odile. We're at the beginning of the trail to it."

Odile crouched, studied the converging bearings, and let out a long breath through her nose. "Wren's map puts the source at the spring."

"Wren's map puts *a* source at the spring. A spring with silver dust in it." Freya tapped the little circled cross. "But look at his own notes here — see? He wrote *source waters* twice, in two different places. Once at the spring, once further up, in the margin, almost scratched out. A man who's dying doesn't make drafting errors. He makes distinctions."

"Two sources."

"One true and one shown. Or one that feeds the river everyone knows, and one that feeds everything else." Freya pulled the wax-cloth map closer and looked at the country beyond the spring as Wren had rendered it: hatched shading, the universal cartographer's shrug for *rock wall, don't bother*. Limestone country. She'd seen the shelf herself that afternoon, a pale rampart running northeast from the pool, weathered into pinnacles, seamed with dark fissures. Everyone — Wren included, maybe — had looked at that wall and seen the end of the world.

But limestone dissolves. Limestone opens. Any child who'd grown up near karst knew that the rock kept doors, and kept them shut, and kept its secrets behind them.

"The bearing runs straight into that shelf," she said quietly. "Seventy-eight degrees, if I extend it from the datum — nine, maybe ten miles. Right through the middle of the hatching. There's no valley there. No canyon. Nothing on any chart."

"So either your father aimed his life's work at a blank wall," Odile said, "or the wall has a hole in it."

Freya looked down at the compass. The needle lay quiet now, as if satisfied, as if it had been waiting forty years for someone to stand in the right place and ask the right question. *Compass true,* her father had written, over and over, in notebook after notebook. She had read it as piety. It wasn't. It was a claim. The compass was true — truer than north, truer than the company's charts, truer than the men who had struck his lines from the record and his name from the rolls. He had built his secret out of the one material no one ever audits: the honesty of an instrument nobody thought to question.

She laughed, once, and it came out cracked. "He knew they'd purge him. He knew they'd burn the real maps. So he left the truth where only a surveyor would find it — folded inside the very surveys they threw away. They kept his garbage, Odile. They filed his riddle in a drawer marked DISPOSED and handed it to me when they fired me."

"That's either providence or irony, and I've never learned to tell them apart on this river."

"It's engineering," Freya said. "Redundancy. You never trust a single line. He gave the same bearing three times so that losing one wouldn't lose the truth." She smoothed the wax-cloth map one more time, and her finger traced the route: up from the spring, along the base of the limestone, into the hatched nothing where the compass insisted the world continued. "Behind that shelf. Behind the wall every chart stops at. That's where the water comes from. That's where the silver comes from. And I think—" her voice caught, and she let it— "I think that's where he stopped walking."

Odile put a hand briefly on her shoulder, heavy and warm, and said nothing, which was the pilot's way of saying everything.

* * *

They broke camp before first light, in that grey hour when the river smoked and the limestone glowed faintly bone-white above the trees. Freya stood at the edge of the spring and took her final bearing, kneeling in the wet gravel with the compass level, sighting along the needle's insistence toward the pale mass of the shelf.

Ten miles of country with no trail, no chart, and no reason for anyone to go there — except a dead man's hidden arithmetic and a living woman's refusal to leave a line unclosed.

She thought of Tomas, somewhere downstream on Crane's steamer or ashore and running; thought of Wren's papers, riding toward Rivergate in a mail saddlebag or already in worse hands; thought of the permit clock ticking down weeks that felt now like days. Every part of her that had survived the company said *go back, file the claim, fight on paper where engineers win.*

But her father had not fought on paper. He had walked upstream until the paper ran out, and he had hidden the rest of the road where only she would ever think to look.

"Compass true," she said aloud, to the smoke and the water and the man whose bones waited somewhere past the wall.

The needle did not waver.

Behind them, far downriver, so faint it might have been thunder deciding whether to become weather, came the sound of a steam whistle.
