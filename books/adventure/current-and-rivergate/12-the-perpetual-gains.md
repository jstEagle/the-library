## 12. The Perpetual Gains

The boiler gauge was the first thing Tomas looked at each morning now, and the last thing he touched each night. He had learned its moods in four days of running upstream: the way the needle trembled at pressure like a nervous animal, the hairline seam where his file work had thinned the metal just enough to promise failure without guaranteeing it. He had made that promise himself, in the dark of the Perpetual's engine room, with a file no longer than his thumb and his heart going like a paddle wheel.

The repair crew had found it on the second day. A cracked fitting, they called it. A lucky catch. They had sweated a new joint into place, hammered the seams true, and by noon of the third day the Perpetual was making better time than she had all voyage — eight knots against the current when the channel allowed it, the gorge walls sliding past close enough that the wake slapped stone and came back as an echo.

Tomas stood at the rail and watched the gap between them and Freya's boat close like a wound healing, which was what it felt like: something knitting shut that ought to have stayed open.

"You're up early."

Crane's voice always arrived before Crane did. Tomas turned. The enforcer stood at the rail's end with a tin cup of coffee he never seemed to drink, steam rising past a face that gave away nothing except intention. The morning light came down the gorge in a single narrow band, gold on black water, and it lit half of Crane's face and left the other half to the shadow, which felt less like accident than policy.

"I couldn't sleep," Tomas said.

"Guilty men sleep fine." Crane sipped, or pretended to. "It's honest men who lie awake doing arithmetic. Which are you?"

"I'm a clerk," Tomas said. "Arithmetic is my trade."

Something moved at the corner of Crane's mouth — not quite a smile, but the memory of one, filed away for later use. "Then you'll have done this sum already. We're two days behind them. Maybe three if the water turns. Their pilot is Marchand, and Marchand knows this river the way you know your ledgers, which means she knows every lie in it too. But we're heavier, faster, and we don't stop to sleep ashore." He set the cup on the rail, untouched. "We gain. Every hour, we gain. That's not opinion. That's subtraction."

* * *

The Perpetual was a company steamer built for freight, and she carried her purpose in her bones: iron-bowed, shallow-keeled, with a cargo deck that had been cleared of bales and stacked instead with crates that clinked when the boat rolled. Enforcers slept among the crates in hammocks slung from the deck beams, and their rifles lay racked along the bulkhead like organ pipes. There were fourteen of them. Tomas had counted twice, hoping the number would shrink out of embarrassment. It hadn't.

He had his own cabin — small courtesy, or small cage — with a desk bolted to the wall because Crane understood that a clerk without paper grew restless, and restlessness bred invention. So Tomas sat at the bolted desk each evening and copied manifests in a fair hand while the gorge narrowed around them and the Current climbed beneath the hull, loud as a crowd.

It was on the fifth night that Crane came to him without knocking.

The lamp was low. Tomas had been pretending to work, which had become his principal occupation, and the pages in front of him were three days old. Crane filled the doorway the way weather fills a window, then stepped inside and closed the door behind him, which he had never done before. The click of the latch sounded very loud.

"Do you know why I brought you aboard?" Crane asked.

"To keep an eye on me."

"To keep you *safe*," Crane said, and let the word sit there until its absurdity became its own kind of insult. "There are men on this boat who would put you over the side for what you've cost us in days and coal, if I told them to. I haven't told them to. Consider what that's worth."

"I consider it constantly," Tomas said. "It keeps me awake."

Crane pulled the desk chair from under him — uninvited, unhurried — and sat, knees nearly to the little desk, so that they were eye to eye and the room belonged entirely to him. Up close there were lines around Crane's eyes that the lamplight deepened into something almost like age, almost like weariness, though Tomas had long since stopped believing in either.

"Your sister has something in her possession," Crane said. "Papers. A map. Possibly a journal. What matters is not what it is but what it proves, and what it proves is worth more than every crate on this deck." He folded his hands. "The company wants it back quietly. Quietly costs money. Loudly costs blood. I prefer money. Most sensible men do."

"And if she won't sell?"

"Everyone sells." Crane said it without cruelty, which was worse. "Some want coin. Some want their name back. Some only want to be told they were right all along. Your sister wants to be right. That's the most expensive kind, but it's still a price." He reached into his coat and laid a document on the desk between them, squared to the edge, the way Tomas himself would have squared it. "So here is mine."

Tomas looked down. It was a letter of reinstatement, drawn up on Rivergate company stock, with the seal already pressed into the wax — not pending, not conditional. His name was written across it in a clerk's hand better than his own. Full restoration of position. Back pay accrued from the date of his suspension. And beneath that, in the same fair hand, a line that stopped his breath:

*Together with conveyance of the Joubert residence on Calloway Street, per the original grant.*

Their father's house. The house with the crooked chimney and the walnut tree in the yard, the house Tomas had grown up in while Freya grew up wherever her mother could keep them both fed, the house that had been sold out from under the family when their father vanished upriver and the company called in his debts. The company had bought it at auction. The company had held it for eleven years like a man holding a dog's favorite bone just out of reach.

"He asked for it back once," Crane said, watching him. "Your father. Did you know that? Wrote to the board, polite as a church bulletin, asking that the house be restored to his children. The board never answered. I read the letter myself." He tapped the wax seal with one finger. "I can answer it."

Tomas heard his own pulse. "In exchange for what."

"For her. Alive." Crane leaned back, and the chair creaked. "Not harmed. Not frightened into silence. Delivered — that's the word I want — delivered to me with whatever she carries, and you walk off this boat at Rivergate with your name, your wages, and your father's roof over your head. You'd be doing her a kindness, if it helps. She's running toward something that will kill her, and you'd be pulling her out of it. Brothers do that."

*Brothers do that.* Tomas stared at the letter and thought: *he rehearsed this.* Every word of it, rehearsed, tested against some earlier man in some earlier cabin on some earlier river, because Silas Crane had been running errands up this water since before Tomas was born, and errands like this one did not come to a man for the first time at fifty.

And underneath the rehearsal, underneath the wax and the fair hand, Tomas saw the shape of the thing plainly, the way you see a forgery plainly once someone shows you the watermark. Because he knew this offer. He had watched it accepted before. He had lived in the house it bought.

His father had been offered exactly this — not the same words, but the same architecture. Legitimacy for silence. A roof for a signature. Elias Joubert had been a surveyor with an illegitimate daughter and a legitimate son and a hunger to make the world square for at least one of them, and the company had come to him with a pen and said: *sign, and your boy will have a name, a school, a street address, a future.* And Elias had signed. And the silver rights had flowed through that signature like water through a broken flume, and the house had been granted, and the school fees paid, and the name polished — and then Elias Joubert had gone upriver one last time, whether from guilt or greed or some arithmetic of his own, and had not come back, and the company had taken even the house back, because that was the other half of the bargain nobody wrote down: what the company gave, the company kept, and what the company kept, it kept forever.

Tomas had eaten his whole life out of that signature. His schooling, his clerkship, his clean boots and his legitimate name — all of it purchased with his father's pen, and serviced ever since by Tomas's own, line by line, ledger by ledger, the interest compounding quietly in a drawer in Rivergate.

Now Crane slid the pen across the desk. Of course he had brought a pen.

"You hesitate," Crane observed.

"I'm reading."

"You've read it. You read everything twice; it's in your file." Crane's voice stayed level, almost gentle, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing in the room. "Take the time you need. The river gives us both plenty."

But Tomas was no longer reading the letter. He was looking through it, down the long shaft of years, at a walnut tree and a crooked chimney and a father who had chosen the house over the truth and died with the truth anyway, somewhere ahead of them in the dark of this gorge, waiting in the water like a debt.

*The same bargain,* he thought. *Word for word, the same bargain. And they're offering it to me because it worked the first time.*

* * *

He signed nothing. He told Crane he needed the night, and Crane inclined his head as though the answer were itself informative, and left the letter on the desk — deliberately, Tomas was sure, so that it could go on working on him in the dark like damp working into plaster.

It worked. That was the humiliation of it. Tomas lay in his bunk and listened to the engines and the river fighting them, and the letter lay on the desk with its seal gleaming faintly whenever the lamp flame moved, and he wanted it. God help him, he wanted it with a simplicity that shamed every clever thing he had ever thought about himself. He wanted the house. He wanted the name without the asterisk. He wanted to walk down Calloway Street and have Mrs. Aldous from next door say *Master Joubert, home at last* and mean nothing by it but welcome.

He got up twice and stood at the desk with the pen in his hand. Both times he set it down again, and both times the reason was different and insufficient: first because Freya would call it betrayal, and then — worse, truer — because his father had already spent this exact coin, and look what it had bought. A grave with no marker. A daughter with a grudge. A son who filed permits for the men who collected the debt.

Somewhere past midnight he stopped lying to himself about the third option, the one he had been circling for five days. There was no version of this where he delivered Freya and lived comfortably after. There was no version where he stayed aboard and remained innocent. The letter on the desk was not an exit. It was a test with one question, and the question was not *what will you choose* but *whose son are you.*

He dressed in the dark. He took the letter — he couldn't say afterward why, except that evidence was a habit — folded it into his coat, and took also the small oilskin packet of copied manifests he had been building all voyage, page by page, in his fair clerk's hand: dates, headings, coal purchases, Crane's requisitions, the false travel manifest Tomas himself had filed in Rivergate and the corrections he had later entered to cover his tracks. A clerk's confession, complete and cross-referenced. If he was going to drown tonight, the paperwork should at least survive him.

The deck was black. The Perpetual ran with a single stern lantern, and the gorge rose on either side higher than the mast, walls of wet stone breathing cold. Below the rail the Current went by with a sound like a crowd whispering a name over and over, and Tomas leaned out and could see nothing beneath the blackness at all — no water, no depth, only the sound and the cold coming up off it.

They were in the narrows below the Splits. Odile Marchand's country. He remembered her from Rivergate, years ago — the pilot the company had erased, the woman whose brother was in the flood tally. If anyone plied these waters at night, it would be her boat, somewhere ahead in the dark, running the channels only the dead knew.

Aft, the watchman made his turn. Tomas counted his footsteps — he had counted them every night, eleven paces to the wheelhouse door, forty seconds at the rail, eleven back — and when the man's boots faded into the wheelhouse, Tomas went over the side.

The water hit him like a verdict. Cold went through his chest and closed his hands for him, and the river took him under and spun him, and for a long blind moment he was nothing but a body in a black current, the letter crushed in his fist, the oilskin packet dragging at his neck, his father's name and his own rising together in his throat as he fought for the surface.

He broke air gasping. The Perpetual's lantern was already shrinking astern, her paddles beating on without him, carrying Crane and his fourteen rifles onward toward whatever waited at the source. Ahead there was only the gorge and the dark and the sound of water over stone.

Tomas turned downstream, and swam.

Behind him, faint through the rock, he thought he heard a whistle from the wheelhouse — the watchman, finding the rail empty. Ahead, around the bend where the gorge split its throat, a single answering note floated back: low, patient, human. A boat bell. Or a signal lamp being unshuttered.

Someone was waiting in the dark, and they had seen him jump.
