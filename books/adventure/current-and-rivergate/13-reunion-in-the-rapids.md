## 13. Reunion in the Rapids

She saw him before she understood what she was seeing — a pale shape turning in the white water below the gorge wall, arms windmilling with the graceless desperation of a man who had never learned to swim and was learning now, at speed, under duress.

"Tomas." The name left her mouth flat and strange, stripped of everything it should have carried.

Odile heard it anyway. She was already moving, throwing her weight against the sweep oar, hauling the bow around so the current would carry them to him rather than past. "Grab the line," she snapped. "The coiled line, girl, *now*."

Freya's hands did what her mind could not. She snatched the coil from its peg, paid out rope, and looped it twice around her forearm the way Odile had shown her three days ago when the meaning of every knot on this boat had still been new to her. The boat bucked through the standing waves. Spray came over the gunwale in cold sheets. Below, the shape went under, came up again, went under longer.

"He jumped," she said, and did not know until she said it that she had been certain of that from the first moment. Nobody fell off the *Perpetual*. Not at night. Not in the gorge. "Odile, he *jumped*."

"Then he'd best learn to float before the river decides what he's worth." Odile's voice was iron over something softer. Freya had learned to hear that softness — it lived there whenever the pilot spoke of men who chose water over company. Her brother had been one of eleven once. She never said so. She only steered harder.

The line went out. It took three casts. The first fell short and was swallowed; the second slapped the water a hand's width from his reaching fingers; the third caught him across the chest, and Freya felt the weight come up the rope like an anchor being raised from a depth no anchor should have found.

Hauling him aboard took both of them and cost the boat a bad moment broadside to the current that Odile swore about in three languages. Then Tomas was on the deck planks, on his side, vomiting river water and bile in long convulsive heaves, and Freya knelt beside him with her hands hovering over his shoulders, not touching, because touching would make him real.

He was thinner than she remembered. That was the first concrete thing. The last time she had seen him — nine years ago, in the company office in Verrin's Crossing, when she had come to contest the debt and he had sat behind the clerk's desk with their father's surname written neatly after his own — he had been soft with legitimate living, well-fed, well-schooled, wearing a collar that had never known coal dust. Now the collar was gone, the coat was Crane's livery grey and soaked through, and his cheekbones stood out like survey stakes driven into poor ground.

He looked up at her. Water ran from his hair into his eyes and he didn't blink it away.

"You're real," he rasped. "I kept telling myself you wouldn't actually be here. That it was Crane's imagination, or mine."

"I'm real." She pulled off her coat and dropped it over him, because his shaking had passed the point where pride could pretend otherwise. "You're an idiot."

"Yes." He said it simply, without argument, and then he was sick again.

* * *

They made camp an hour later in a gravel eddy beneath an overhang of black rock, where the gorge widened just enough to permit a fire if you fed it driftwood sparingly. Odile built it small and screened it with the boat's canvas, saying nothing about light discipline, doing everything about it. She had seen steamboat lanterns upstream at dusk. Everyone had pretended they hadn't.

Tomas lay wrapped in blankets near the fire, shivering in diminishing waves, and Freya sat across the flames from him with her knees drawn up and her father's compass in her pocket, its brass case pressing against her hip like a hand demanding attention.

Odile, with the tact of a woman who had spent years reading rooms she wasn't welcome in, announced that she was going to check the boat's lines, and walked away down the gravel bar into the dark, taking the last of the dry tobacco with her.

The silence between the siblings had weight and grain, like sand in a bearing.

"You sabotaged the gauge," Freya said at last. "On the *Perpetual*. I know you did. Odile heard the crew talking at Saint-Aubin — the boiler nearly went, half the engine room scalded, and nobody could explain how a pressure gauge read true while the drum ran hot."

Tomas stared into the fire. "I read the manuals wrong on purpose for two days before that. Filed the maintenance requests wrong. Slowed them however I could without — " He stopped. Started again. "I wanted to buy you time. I didn't want anyone hurt."

"But people were hurt."

"People were always going to be hurt." His voice cracked on it, and he pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "That's the thing you don't understand, Freya, because you got out. You got dismissed, which felt like dying, but it was *out*. Inside — inside you can't move your arm without moving someone else's. Every choice is someone else's injury wearing your handwriting. So yes. I broke the gauge. And Crane let me."

Freya went still. "Let you?"

"He knew. From the beginning, I think — or from Saint-Aubin, certainly. He watched me file the false manifest and he watched me lie about your heading and he said nothing, and I told myself I was clever, I was winning, I was buying you three days, four days — " A sound came out of him that was almost a laugh and entirely not one. "He was letting me hang myself with my own ledger entries. Because a man who betrays once can be made to betray again, and a man who betrays for love — " he gestured at the dark gorge walls, at the whole upstream world where she had gone — "a man who betrays for love is the most useful instrument a company ever owned. Until tonight. Tonight he offered me reinstatement. Full salary. Our father's house on Cordwainer Street, back in the family name. All I had to do was deliver you alive at the source."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, faint as memory, a steamer whistle sounded and died.

"And you jumped instead," Freya said.

"I jumped instead." He lowered his hand. In the firelight his face was naked in a way she had never permitted herself to see it, all the clerk's composure burned away. "And I want you to understand something, because I've had six hours in the water to get it straight. I didn't jump for you. Not only. I jumped because I finally read the offer correctly. That house, that name, that desk — it's the same bargain Father took. Do you know what he traded? Do you even know?"

"I know what he lost."

"You know what *you* lost." Tomas pushed himself up on one elbow, and the shivering had stopped now, replaced by a feverish clarity. "I got the house, Freya. I grew up in it. I found his papers in the attic when I was fourteen — the ones he left behind, the ones Mother burned half of. I read enough to know he took money from Rivergate to close certain lines and keep certain mouths shut, and I read enough to know he hated himself for it, and I read enough to know that whatever happened to him upriver, the company's fingerprints were on it. And I said nothing. For nine years I filed permits and renewed concessions and I said *nothing*, because the house was warm and the name was clean and you were — "

"Say it."

"You were the proof of what he'd done wrong." The words came out ragged. "Not the compass. Not the debts. *You.* Every time I saw your name on a claim form, every time the old men in the office talked about Joubert's by-blow who thought she could hold a transit level, I heard the house settling around me and I kept my head down. When you came to contest the debt, I denied the appeal before I'd finished reading it. I did it fast so I wouldn't have time to be ashamed."

Freya had imagined this conversation for nine years. She had rehearsed it on night shifts and long surveys, in the voiceless theater behind her eyes, and in every version she had said something devastating and he had crumbled and somehow that had fixed things. None of the versions had prepared her for how small he looked saying it, or how little satisfaction there was in it, or how the satisfaction curdled instantly into something that tasted like grief.

"The compass," she said quietly. "When he left, he gave me the compass and the debts, and everyone said it proved I was the afterthought. The illegitimate daughter gets the trinket; the son gets the estate. But I've been thinking, these last weeks, reading his closed lines, following his datum points. A compass is a working tool. Debts are a working burden. He gave me the work, Tomas. He gave you the reward. And I used to think that meant he loved you more."

"And now?"

"Now I think he knew exactly what each of us could survive."

Tomas turned his face away. His shoulders moved once, twice — not sobbing, or fighting not to, she couldn't tell and didn't try to.

* * *

Odile returned at some point during the silence that followed, moving quietly, and sat down at the edge of the firelight without pretending she hadn't heard any of it. She poured river water into the dented kettle, set it on the stones, and produced from somewhere in her coat three strips of dried fish and a heel of bread gone hard as slate. She divided it into thirds without comment and handed the largest portion to Tomas, because drowned men needed feeding and grudges could wait.

They ate. The river talked to itself in the dark, that endless low syllable it never stopped making, and Freya realized she had begun to find it companionable, which frightened her more than Crane did.

"The papers," Tomas said eventually. "Wren's claim papers. Did they reach Saint-Aubin? Did the mail rider — "

"They were intercepted." Freya kept her voice level. "Crane's agents took them off the rider outside the mission. Which means Wren's daughter has heard nothing, and the papers are bait now. He'll use them to draw me into Rivergate on his terms, or he'll destroy them and call the claim void for want of documentation."

"Not necessarily both," Tomas said slowly. He had shifted upright, and something in his posture had changed — the clerk coming back online, the man who had spent his life inside other people's paperwork sitting forward toward the fire. "If he intercepts mail, he has to log it. Everything Crane does goes into a ledger — I've seen the requisition codes. Intercepted correspondence gets a destruction order or an evidence hold, and either way it's dated, initialed, and filed within forty-eight hours. If we know the date of interception, we know the filing window."

"Why does that matter?" Odile asked.

"Because a destruction order requires a countersignature from the concession office, and the concession expires in — " he closed his eyes, calculating against a calendar he clearly kept in his skull — "eleven days. Four of those are travel downstream if Odile runs the water hard. If the papers are destroyed, the destruction record becomes evidence in itself: proof Crane had them, proof he suppressed a lawful claim. Either way, the paper trail leads to Rivergate. He can't burn the claim without burning himself."

Freya stared at him. "You memorized the filing schedules."

"I'm a clerk," he said, with the ghost of the family stubbornness in it. "It's what I'm *for*."

Something loosened in her chest that she refused to name. She took out the compass instead, because hands needed work, and opened it on her knee, and the needle swung and settled — not north, not quite, holding that persistent quarter-turn east of true that had troubled her since the Splits.

Tomas leaned forward despite himself. "That's Father's."

"It is."

"It's pointing wrong."

"It's pointing *true*," Freya said. "Just not north. Watch." She turned the compass housing slowly, and the needle held its bearing relative to the case no matter how she rotated it — locked, faithful, aimed along a line only a surveyor would think to read. "There's a bearing hidden in the instrument. Father built it in. It's been trying to tell me something since Verrin's Crossing, and I think — I *think* — it's the line to the source. Off every chart. Behind the limestone shelf Odile says isn't on any map."

Odile's jaw tightened at that, but she said nothing, which from Odile was a paragraph.

"A hidden bearing in a compass," Tomas murmured. "That's — he was good. He was better than good. Why hide it?"

"Because a visible map can be confiscated," Freya said. "A secret inside a dead woman's keepsake can't. He hid the truth where only someone who thought like him would ever look." She paused, and the next words came out before she could weigh them. "Where only his children would ever look."

The fire settled. Tomas reached out — hesitated — and touched the compass case with one finger, lightly, the way a man touches a thing he has no right to and knows it.

"I can't ask you to forgive me," he said. "I don't have the standing. Nine years of filings, Freya. I buried your appeals in the same drawer where the company buries everything. I won't insult you by asking for absolution over one night of wet courage."

"Good," Freya said. "Because I'm not offering it." She met his eyes across the fire and held them. "But I'll tell you what I am offering. There's a bearing on this compass and a datum point on two maps that shouldn't share one, and in four days — maybe five — a concession dies and everything it protected dies with it. I need someone who thinks in ledgers and filing windows and countersignatures. Someone who knows Crane's habits from the inside. Someone whose betrayal he's already catalogued, which makes you the one man in this territory he cannot predict anymore."

Tomas let out a breath that shook slightly at the edges. "You're hiring me."

"I'm *agreeing* with you," she corrected. "For the duration. No more, no less. We agree on the bearing, we agree on the deadline, and we settle the rest of it — the house, the name, the nine years — after, if there is an after. Loyalty first. Forgiveness never promised."

From the edge of the firelight, Odile snorted softly. "Marriage vows are simpler in Rivergate, I hear."

"Rivergate marriages don't run the gorge," Freya said.

"No," Odile agreed, and there was something almost warm under the dryness. "They don't."

Tomas looked at the compass, at his sister, at the black water sliding past the gravel bar with all its patience and all its power. "Then show me the maps," he said. "Both of them. And the journal pages you copied. If we're going to do this, we do it properly — datum, bearing, deadline, and every piece of paper Crane doesn't know we have."

Freya unrolled the wax-cloth map on the flat stone between them, weighted its corners, and laid her father's closed-lines sheet beside it. The two rivers of ink converged on the same invisible point, the way, she thought, two children of the same flawed man had finally converged on the same stretch of dangerous water.

And it was Tomas — leaning over the maps, water still dripping from his hair, the legitimate name suddenly worth exactly nothing out here — who went rigid and pointed one trembling finger at the overlap.

"Freya," he whispered. "Look at the annotation. On Father's sheet, at the datum. There's writing in the margin. Faint. I almost — *look at it*."

She bent close. The firelight caught it: a few letters in her father's cramped surveyor's hand, so faded they might have been nothing, might have been a smudge, might have been waiting nine years in the dark for someone who knew how to read them.

*"Beneath the shelf, ask the river who it buried."*

Upstream, faint and getting less faint, the *Perpetual*'s whistle sounded again — closer now, patient as arithmetic — and Odile was already on her feet, kicking dirt over the fire.
