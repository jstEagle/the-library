## 14. Crane's Ledger

The mission town of Saint-Aubin smelled of woodsmoke and river mud, and Silas Crane had always found the combination honest in a way that cities were not. Cities lied with their paint and their paving stones. A town like this told you plainly what it was: a place where things came apart, where men arrived with less than they'd left with, where the river took its tithe of everything that floated on it.

He stood in the shadow of the mission's bell tower and watched the mail rider come down the hill road, dust rising behind his mule like a slow exhalation. The rider was young, sunburned, careless — a boy who had never been shot at, never buried a friend in unmarked ground, never carried a ledger whose pages weighed more than any satchel he would ever sling over his shoulder. Crane envied him nothing. Envy was for men who believed the world owed them something. Crane had learned, forty years ago, standing on a riverbank with water in his boots and a dead man's map in his coat, that the world owed no one anything. It only kept accounts.

The rider passed within ten feet of him without a glance. In the boy's saddlebag, wrapped in oilcloth and wedged between a preacher's correspondence and a widow's letter to her sister, were Aldous Wren's claim papers. Crane knew this the way he knew most things — not by luck, but by having paid for the knowledge in advance. He had bought the mission's postmaster three months ago, before there was anything worth buying him for, because Silas Crane did not chase information. He cultivated it, the way an orchardist cultivates fruit, patiently, in rows, against a season he could name.

He let the rider pass. There was no urgency. The papers would sit in the mail room until morning, when the packet went downstream to Rivergate, and by then they would be ash.

* * *

Crane took a room at the mission's lodging house because it was expected of him — a company man traveling under permit, paying his way, signing the register in a careful clerk's hand. He ate the evening meal with two bargemen and a tanner, listened to them complain about freight rates and the low water, and said nothing worth remembering. He had spent a lifetime being a man no one remembered clearly afterward. It was a discipline, like surveying. You did not become invisible. You made yourself unremarkable, measurement by measurement, until witnesses could not swear to your height or the color of your coat.

In his room, he locked the door, set a candle on the table, and opened his ledger.

It was not a company ledger. The company kept its own books, and Crane kept those too, immaculately, every entry balanced to the cent, because sloppy accounting was how men got caught. This one was different. It was bound in gray leather gone soft as cloth at the corners, and it had traveled with him for thirty-one years, through four postings and two changes of the company's name, from Rivergate Consolidated to Rivergate Navigation & Concession to whatever they called themselves this decade. Companies changed their names the way rivers changed their courses. The ledger stayed.

He turned past the early pages — the ones written in a younger hand, ink browned now, entries dated back to the year the Current concession was first filed. He did not need to read them. He knew them the way other men knew prayers.

*Escorted J. Joubert, surveyor, upriver. Six days to the Splits. Man talked too much about datum points.*

That was the first entry concerning Joubert. There were others. He had been a younger man then, already good at his work, which was why they had sent him. His work, understood plainly, was this: the company made promises, and Crane made sure the promises stayed quiet. When a prospector found something the company preferred unfound, Crane negotiated. When negotiation failed, Crane arranged. And when arranging failed — when a man proved stubborn, principled, the kind of fool who would rather lose everything than sign his name to a lie — then Crane did what needed doing, and entered it in the ledger in his small even hand, because a debt unpaid was a debt that gathered interest, and Silas Crane did not allow interest to accumulate.

*A. Wren, claim filed at Saint-Aubin registry, 14th of the month. Silver-bearing strata, source region. Unregistered. Advise resolution.*

Resolution. That was the word he had used, decades ago, in the margin beside Joubert's name. Resolution. He had been younger and more delicate with language then. Lately his entries had grown plainer. *Shot Wren at the bend below the Splits. Left him. Did not confirm.* He had written that six weeks ago, sitting in a company steamer's cabin while the Perpetual's engines knocked beneath him like a bad heart. He had not confirmed the kill because confirming meant going ashore, and going ashore meant time, and time was the one commodity the calendar was spending faster than he could earn it back.

The permits expired in nineteen days. After that, the source country reverted to open claim, and every prospector, speculator, and thief between here and the coast would come up the Current like flies to a wound. Everything the company had kept buried for forty years — the forged concession, the stolen rights, the bones — would surface, and no ledger of his could balance it.

Unless the papers burned. Unless the maps burned. Unless the woman with her father's compass drowned in the gorge, or turned back, or was taken quietly somewhere the record would show she had simply vanished, the way her father had vanished, the way Wren had very nearly vanished.

Crane closed the ledger and sat with his hands flat upon it, feeling its weight, and admitted to himself — because he lied to everyone except himself; lying to oneself was the first corruption, and he had never permitted it — that he felt no triumph. Only tidiness. The accounts were old. Joubert senior had been a debt forty years outstanding. Wren had been a newer entry, but of the same kind: a man who had seen too much and would not be paid into silence. Crane had offered Wren money once, years back, through an intermediary. Wren had thrown the intermediary's offer into the river. Men like that thought they were better than the rest of the world. They were not better. They were merely inconvenient, and inconvenience, left unresolved, became catastrophe.

He was closing accounts. That was all. Anyone who mistook it for crime had never watched a company die of unpaid debts.

* * *

He went out after midnight.

The moon was down and the river ran loud enough to cover footsteps, a gray sound filling the valley like held breath. The mission's mail room stood apart from the chapel, a low plank building with a shingled roof and a stove pipe, built by men who had feared fire enough to site it away from the other structures and not feared it enough to do anything else. Inside: sorting shelves, a canvas sack of outgoing letters, the postmaster's desk with its drawer of undelivered items, and, on the second shelf from the window, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with tarred string, the packet marked for delivery to Madeline Wren, Rivergate city.

Crane had watched the building for two evenings. He knew the mission's one watchman made his rounds at eleven and again at three, walking the chapel side and never the river side, because the river side was mud and mosquitoes and no one had ever come from there. No one had ever come from there. Until now.

He carried no lantern. He had done this kind of work by feel since before the watchman was born, and his hands knew the latch, the hinge-squeak, the geometry of the shelves. The oilcloth packet came off the shelf with a whisper. He weighed it in his palm — heavier than letters alone, and inside, when he slit the string with his thumbnail, the stiff crackle of paper that had been folded many times. Claim papers. And beneath them, folded smaller, a square of waxed cloth with the particular slickness of a field map drawn to survive weather.

Wren's map. Or a copy of it. Either way, it ended tonight.

He stood in the dark mail room holding another man's life's work, and for a moment — a moment he would deny under any oath — he almost admired it. Wren had spent thirty years crawling through the source country, half-starved, alone, keeping his discovery off every registry, hiding his silver the way a miser hides coins in a mattress. All of it to hand down to a daughter. All of it traded, in the end, to a stranger on a road, for a promise. Crane had interrogated the dying man himself, or near enough — had stood over him after the shooting, in the rain, and asked where the papers were, and Wren had smiled through the blood and said nothing at all. Stubborn to the last breath. Principled fools. The world was full of them, and the world belonged, in the end, to the men willing to write down what needed writing down.

He put the packet in his coat. Then he opened the stove, crumpled the canvas mail sack into it, and laid the claim papers and the wax-cloth map on top, and struck a match.

The fire caught slowly at first — paper needs coaxing — and then all at once, the way fires do, the way debts do. Heat climbed his face. Light leapt up the walls and made the sorting shelves stand out in orange relief, each pigeonhole bristling with other people's correspondence, other people's small important business, none of which concerned him. He fed the fire the widow's letter and the preacher's sermons and the whole undelivered drawer, because a partial burn invited questions and a total burn invited only grief. The stove pipe began to draw, a low moan, and smoke curled up and out into the night sky where it would read, by morning, as a chimney fire, an accident, a tragedy for a small town, quickly forgotten.

He watched until the wax-cloth map curled and blackened and came apart. He watched until the claim papers' seals bubbled and ran. He watched until there was nothing in the stove but a red breathing mass, and then he raked it fine with the poker, closed the door, and let himself out the way he had come.

On the riverbank, downwind of the smoke, he stopped and took out the ledger and wrote by feel, in the dark, trusting thirty years of habit to keep his lines straight:

*Wren papers destroyed at Saint-Aubin. Mail room burned. No witnesses. Account closed.*

Then, beneath it, because the account was not quite closed and he did not lie to himself:

*Joubert girl still upstream. Tomas Joubert aboard Perpetual — uncertain. Watch him.*

* * *

Uncertain. He disliked the word. He disliked it the way an engineer dislikes a reading that will not repeat.

Tomas Joubert was a problem Crane had created for himself, and he knew it, and knowing it did not make it simpler. He had taken the man aboard the Perpetual as insurance — a son in the hand to buy a daughter's surrender — but insurance only worked if the insured party behaved predictably, and Tomas Joubert had begun, in the last week, to behave like his father. Small errors. A travel manifest filed with dates that didn't quite cohere. A report of the woman's direction that placed her two hundred miles from where Crane's own informants placed her. Nothing provable. Everything suggestive. The same pattern, Crane realized with a cold professional displeasure, that Joubert senior had shown forty years ago: a company man whose figures kept drifting toward the truth.

He had let the sabotage happen. The boiler gauge, the tampered fitting — he had known within an hour, had ordered the engine crew to run the pressure low and say nothing, and had watched his trap close around the young clerk like a snare around a rabbit's leg. Now Tomas was bound to him: complicit, compromised, unable to go to the company's lawyers without hanging himself. That had been the design. But designs assumed the animal struggled the way animals struggle, and Tomas Joubert had not struggled. He had gone quiet. Quiet men were either broken or deciding, and Crane had not yet determined which.

He climbed the hill road above Saint-Aubin and looked back. The mission slept below him, dark except for one window in the lodging house where the postmaster — his postmaster — sat up late, sweating, telling himself the smell of smoke was imagination. Downstream, miles off, the Current ran black between its banks, carrying toward the sea every drop of rain these mountains would give it, indifferent as arithmetic. Somewhere up that river, in the gorge or beyond it, a woman with a dead man's compass was reading water and reading maps, getting closer to a place Crane had visited once, forty years ago, and had spent forty years making sure no honest chart would ever reach.

He remembered the source. That was the thing no one understood, the thing that would have surprised anyone who thought of him as merely the company's blunt instrument. He remembered it. The cleft in the limestone. The water running silver-bright in the lantern light, so thick with ore dust it seemed lit from beneath. Joubert senior standing at the edge of it with his instruments out, weeping — actually weeping, a grown man, a surveyor, tears cutting tracks through the cave dust on his face — saying, *This belongs in the record. This belongs to everyone.* And Crane standing behind him with his orders in his pocket, understanding even then that the man was already dead, that he had been dead since the day he refused to sign, and that the only question left was where the river would keep him.

They had built the sluice together afterward — Crane and the pilot's brother and two others — to raise the lower passage, to drown the approach, to make the cavern a place a man could enter only by accident or obsession. Odile Marchand's brother. Another name in the ledger, another account. Everyone who touched the source ended up in Crane's book eventually, one way or another. It was almost a law of nature, like the river finding the sea.

He opened the ledger one more time, there in the dark on the hill, and turned to the oldest page, and read the first line he had ever written in it, in a hand steadier than his own had ever been since:

*What is hidden must be guarded. What is guarded must be paid for. I am the payment.*

He had been twenty-three. He had believed it was a vow of service. Thirty-one years later he understood it had been a purchase — the company had bought him with that sentence, and he had signed, and there was no clause anywhere in the gray leather book permitting resale.

Below him, faint on the wind, he heard the mission's watchman shout — the fire discovered at last, bells beginning, voices gathering, buckets and grief and confusion. By dawn the town would know its mail was lost. By noon the story would reach Rivergate: accident, chimney fire, papers destroyed, such a shame. And Madeline Wren would wait for a packet that would never come, and conclude — as she was meant to conclude — that her father's promise had died with him.

Except.

Crane stood very still on the hill road, listening to the bells, and let himself consider the exception he had been refusing to consider for six weeks. The woman had the map. Not the copy — the original, the one Wren had carried, the one Crane had failed to take from a bleeding man because he had chosen speed over certainty. One error. One entry in forty years of clean accounting. And errors, in his experience, did not stay errors. They compounded. They gathered interest. They came due at the worst possible hour, with the permits expiring and the assayer's office opening and a clerk's son learning, perhaps, what his father's house had really been built on.

He closed the ledger. The bells rang on below him, urgent and useless, ringing over ashes.

"Run your errand," he said to the dark upstream, to the woman he could not see, in the flat voice he used for entering figures. "Bring me everything. Bring me the journal, the bones, the whole buried account. I have been waiting forty years to balance this book."

He turned downhill, toward the river, toward the Perpetual waiting at the landing with her boilers cooled and her crew sleeping, and behind him Saint-Aubin burned its small honest fire, and ahead of him the Current ran on through the gorge of names, carrying his past toward him faster than he could walk to meet it.
