## 15. The Limestone Shelf

The second channel began where the river forgot its own name.

Freya saw it first as a wrongness in the water — a seam of darker current sliding beneath the main flow, the way a lie slides beneath an honest sentence. Odile had poled them into the gorge's shadow before dawn, and now, with the sun still hours from clearing the eastern rim, they drifted in a corridor of stone so narrow that Freya could have touched both walls at once if she'd stretched her arms. The water here ran green-black and silent, deeper than their poles could tell, and the cliffs rose so sheer above them that the sky was only a bright thread stitched between two edges of the world.

"There," Odile said.

She said it the way she said everything — flat, economical, as though words cost her something she couldn't afford to spend. But her chin lifted toward the western wall, and Freya followed the line of it and understood.

The seam. A cleft in the limestone no wider than a wagon track, half-drowned, exhaling a faint cold breath of air that smelled of wet mineral and old dark. The main river slid past it without a ripple, indifferent, but at the mouth of the cleft the water *shivered* — a fine trembling Freya would have missed entirely a year ago, before she'd learned to read gradients the way other people read faces.

"It doesn't show on any chart," Tomas said behind her. His voice was thin. He'd been thin-voiced since the gorge of names, since he'd knelt beside a slate marker scratched with their father's name and said nothing at all for a long time. "I checked every survey in the company archive. Every one. There's nothing here."

"That's rather the point," Odile said.

Freya didn't answer. She had the compass out, holding it level on her palm the way her father must have held it thirty years ago, when these waters were younger and he was alive and the company that would kill him was still called something else. The needle swung — not north, not anywhere near north — and settled along a bearing that pointed directly into the drowned cleft.

Her father had hidden a line inside his closed-line maps. A bearing only a surveyor would recognize, folded into the geometry like a key pressed into wax. And the key fit.

"The map was true," she said quietly. She hadn't meant to say it aloud.

"Aye," Odile said. "And now you understand why that's the terrifying part."

* * *

They went in on foot, towing the boat.

The cleft took them at once into a world without weather. The walls closed overhead within twenty paces, limestone banded grey and cream like the strata of some enormous sleeping animal, and the water dropped to their knees, then their ankles, then gave out entirely onto a floor of smooth pale gravel that crunched like frozen snow underfoot. Odile went first with a lantern; Tomas came second, carrying the oilcloth-wrapped journal against his chest the way he'd carried nothing else in his life; Freya came last, because someone had to keep the compass, and because she wanted to be the one who turned around.

Behind them, the entrance shrank to a coin of grey light, then a thread, then nothing.

The dark that replaced it was total in a way Freya had never known. Not the dark of night, which always holds a memory of light, but the dark of places that have never been touched by it. Their lantern made a small trembling island, and beyond its edge the silence was so complete that Freya could hear her own blood moving.

"Steady," Odile murmured, and Freya realized her breathing had gone ragged.

"I'm all right."

"You're not. Nobody is, first time. You just keep walking."

So she kept walking. The passage descended by gentle steps, and the engineer in her began, despite herself, to take measurements — the fall of the floor, perhaps one foot in forty; the width, consistent, cut by water moving with patience rather than violence; the temperature, falling steadily, which meant airflow, which meant this cavern breathed, which meant somewhere ahead there was an opening or a chimney or a throat. She caught herself estimating volumes and had to stop, because the numbers were making it real, and some part of her wanted desperately for it not to be real yet. As long as they were only walking through stone, her father might still be a man who had simply gone away.

The passage opened without warning.

One moment the lantern lit walls on either side; the next, those walls fell away and the light went out into a space so vast it refused the eye. Odile raised the lantern high and the flame bent sideways — there was wind down here, real wind, moving through the dark — and by its guttering Freya saw the cavern unfold: a cathedral of stone ribbed with flowstone, columns rising from floor to ceiling in pale clusters, stalactites hanging like the pipes of some drowned organ. And everywhere, everywhere, the sound of water.

Not rushing. *Rising.* A deep, rhythmic breathing, as though the mountain itself inhaled and exhaled.

"Spring-fed," Tomas whispered. He had his clerk's instincts even here; he'd understood before she did. "The whole system. It's not a cave with a river in it. It's a well with a roof over it."

"Where does the water go?" Freya asked.

Odile turned the lantern toward the far side of the chamber, and Freya felt the answer arrive in her chest before her eyes confirmed it. Along the base of the far wall, a channel of water flowed — clear, swift, purposeful — gathering itself from seeps and springs along the rock and running off into a low archway, out, away, down through miles of stone to become, eventually, the Current River. This was the source. Not a lake on a map, not a marked headwater. A secret the size of a church, hidden behind a shelf of limestone that appeared on no chart because no one who found it had ever come back to draw it.

Except one.

"Freya." Odile's voice had changed. Lower. Careful. "Look at the water."

Freya looked.

The channel glowed.

There was no other word for it. In the lantern light the flowing water carried a brightness that wasn't reflection — it came from within, a faint silver luminescence, as though the stream were molten moonlight. Where the current broke over a stone, the spray hung glittering in the air like shaken foil. Along the margins, where the water slowed, a fine pale sediment lay on the gravel in drifts, and the drifts shone.

"Ore dust," Tomas said. His voice cracked on the second word. "Native silver, ground fine. It's been washing out of the veins upstream for — God knows how long. Centuries. That's what the prospectors' stories were about. That's why the founder—" He stopped himself, but too late; the name he wouldn't say filled the cavern anyway.

Freya crouched at the channel's edge and put her fingers in the water.

It was cold enough to ache. When she lifted her hand, the droplets clung to her skin and caught the light, and for a moment her own fingers looked dipped in starlight. She thought: *this is what men kill for.* She thought: *this is what my father died beside.* The two thoughts arrived together and would not separate, and she understood, kneeling there with silver-bright water running over her hand, exactly what Odile had meant. The map was true. Every line of it. And a true map of a thing like this was worth more than any fortune, and cost more than any fortune, and the proof of both lay in the graves upriver, each one wearing a name scratched into slate.

"We should keep moving," she said, and heard how steady she made her voice, and hated herself a little for the steadiness.

* * *

They followed the silver channel upstream.

The cavern narrowed again beyond the great chamber, the passage climbing gently along the watercourse, and the ore dust grew thicker — the gravel underfoot glittered now with every step, and in places the walls themselves were veined with threads of native silver, wire-thin filaments branching through the limestone like frost ferns on glass. Tomas walked with his lantern close to the walls and said nothing for a long time, and when he finally spoke his voice was strange.

"Do you know what this is worth? Not roughly. Do you actually know? I processed the assayer's schedules for the northern claims. The richest vein in the concession runs maybe four ounces to the ton. This—" he laid his palm flat against a silver-threaded wall, gently, the way you'd touch something sleeping— "this isn't a vein. This is a body. The whole mountain is one deposit."

"Then it was never meant to be dug," Freya said.

"That's not how the world works."

"No," she agreed. "That's how my father worked. And look where the world put him."

Tomas fell silent. Ahead, Odile's lantern swung steadily, and the passage turned, and turned again, following the water's patient climb toward its origin, and Freya let herself think about the datum point — the single shared coordinate where Wren's map and her father's closed lines crossed — and about what it meant that two men, decades apart, had both drawn their secrets back to the same invisible point in the wilderness. Wren had come for silver. Her father had come for truth. They had arrived at the same coordinates anyway, because the truth and the treasure, here, were the same thing, and that was the cruelest joke the river had ever played.

The passage ended at a curtain of flowstone, and Odile stopped so suddenly that Freya nearly walked into her.

"Light," the pilot said.

"What?"

"Daylight. Faint. Feel the air." She was right — the breeze was stronger here, cooler, carrying something green and living. "There's an opening above. Chimney, maybe. The spring comes up from below and the mountain lets the sky in somewhere."

They found the way up on hands and knees, a scramble through a broken chute of fallen slabs, and then Freya pulled herself over a lip of wet stone into light, and stopped breathing.

A shaft of open sky, perhaps thirty feet across, fell straight down into the heart of the mountain. Ferns clung to the shaft's rim far overhead, impossibly green against the blue, and the daylight came down in a column and struck the pool below — the source itself, a welling basin of water rising from the deep rock, fed from underneath by pressure older than any map — and where the light touched it, the pool burned silver. Not reflected. *Filled.* The whole basin shimmered like a coin at the bottom of the world, and the overflow spilled from its rim in a bright ribbon that became, downstream, in the dark, the Current River.

Freya stood at the edge of it and felt the awe move through her like a fever.

Then she saw the camp.

It was tucked into an alcove at the shaft's base, sheltered from the drip line: a rusted stove, a collapsed tent frame, a crate gone soft with rot. And beside the alcove, set upright with deliberate care in a niche of stone, a slate marker, and on the slate, scratched deep by a hand that had been steady even at the end:

**J. JOUBERT — SURVEYOR — HE FOUND IT FIRST**

Beside the marker, weighted under a flat stone, lay a leather case, swollen with damp but intact.

Nobody moved. The silver water breathed and shone. Somewhere far above, water dripped from a fern with the regularity of a clock.

"That's not possible," Tomas said. "He's buried in the gorge. We saw the marker."

"No," Freya said. Her voice came out very quiet and very level, and she was distantly proud of it. "We saw *a* marker. Someone put a name on a grave in the gorge so that anyone following would stop looking here." She stepped toward the alcove. "He reached the source. He surveyed it. And then someone carried him downriver and buried him where the story would end."

She knelt and drew the leather case out from under the stone. The buckle was green with age but it opened, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth that her father — practical to the last, engineer to the last — had sealed with wax, lay a surveyor's journal and a folded instrument: a plumb line, a scale rule, the tools of a man who intended to work.

Freya held the case against her chest and did not open it. Not yet. Some readings deserved daylight and steady hands, and some truths, once spoken, could never be taken back into the dark.

Above her, far up the shaft, a bird crossed the circle of sky and was gone.

And below them, faint but unmistakable, rising through the stone itself, came a sound that did not belong to the mountain: the deep-throated pulse of a steam whistle, echoing up the waterways of the Current, and Odile's face, when Freya turned to her, had gone the color of the limestone.

"The Perpetual," Odile said. "She's in the gorge."
