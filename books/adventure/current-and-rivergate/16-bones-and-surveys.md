## 16. Bones and Surveys

The light changed before the air did.

Freya noticed it first as a quality of sound — the drip of water off the limestone taking on a hollowness, a resonance, as though the cavern behind the cleft were a bell with a crack in it. Then Odile's lantern swung through the narrow passage and the glow came back to them doubled, tripled, thrown from walls furred with pale mineral bloom, and Freya understood that they had passed out of the river's country and into something older. The channel narrowed to little more than a shoulder's width, then opened all at once into a chamber so tall their lamplight could not find its ceiling. The Current ran through it shallow and impossibly clear, sliding over white stone, and where the light touched the water it did not reflect so much as glitter — a fine suspended dust of silver, ground fine as flour by centuries of current, turning the whole stream into a vein of living ore.

"Mon Dieu," Odile said softly, and then, in her flat pilot's way, as if reciting soundings: "The spring is ahead. I can smell the cold coming off it."

Freya did not answer. She had stopped walking.

Twenty paces up the gravel bar at the chamber's edge, half-sheltered beneath an overhang of flowstone, stood a camp. Not a ruin — a camp. A canvas lean-to gone stiff and gray with age, its guy-lines still taut, tied off to iron pitons driven with the neat, economical spacing of a man who had done such work ten thousand times. A surveyor's tripod stood folded against the rock. A tin plate sat on a flat stone beside a cold fire ring, and on the plate, absurdly, unbearably, was a spoon.

She knew the spoon. She had eaten with that spoon. It had a dent in the bowl shaped like a crescent moon because her father had dropped it down a well when she was six and fished it out rather than buy another, saying *a tool that has survived a mistake has earned its keep.*

Her legs carried her forward without her permission. The compass hung at her neck on its cord, and she felt it swing against her sternum like a small insistent heart.

* * *

The camp told its story in the order she read it, and she read it the way she read grades and gradients — methodically, because method was the only thing holding her upright.

He had come upriver in the dry season; she could tell from the height of the stain-line on the canvas. He had surveyed extensively: the tripod, the chain box, a leather case of field books stacked with their spines outward, numbered in his hand — *J.F.J.* for Jules Frédéric Joubert, and beneath each number, the datum notation she had spent three nights finding on Wren's map. He had planned to stay. There was a second blanket. There was a small oilcloth packet of tea. There was a woman's ribbon, faded past color, tied around the handle of the tin cup — her mother's ribbon, which her mother had reported lost years before she died, and which her father must have taken without telling anyone, the way men take relics of the dead.

And there was the journal.

It lay on the flat stone where the plate was, weighted under a piece of galena the size of a fist — silver-lead ore, heavy as sin, bright as a promise. Freya lifted the weight away with both hands. Her fingers were steady. She noted this distantly, the way she noted instrument readings, and understood that some part of her had already known what the camp would hold, had known since the gorge of names, since the slate scratched with *JOUBERT* among thirty other slates marking thirty other men the company had fed to this river.

The journal's cover was swollen with damp but the pages inside were dry, protected by waxed cloth the way Wren's map had been protected. Her father had trusted waxed cloth. So had Aldous Wren. Two men, one secret, decades apart, both of them betting everything on oilskin and the honesty of whoever came next.

She opened it by lamplight while Odile stood watch at the cleft, her silhouette motionless against the silver-bright water.

* * *

The early entries were survey notes, beautiful ones. Freya had grown up on her father's hand and had never seen it better than here: elevations carried to the hundredth of a foot across country with no benchmarks at all, levels run twice from opposite ends and reconciled, sketches of the cavern system rendered in section views that any engineer would weep over. He had mapped the spring. He had measured the flow. He had assayed the ore dust in the water and written, in a hand gone suddenly large:

*The deposit is not a pocket. It is the throat of the mountain. Every claim staked downstream is a straw stuck in a lake.*

Then the entries turned. She read them twice, kneeling on the cold stone, the lantern held close, and the second reading was worse than the first because the first time she had been able to pretend it was only history.

*Met with Aubert de Rivergate's agents at Verrin's Crossing. Presented my preliminary claim under the Territorial Act. They were courteous. Courteous the way a flood is courteous.*

*They have offered me a position. Chief of surveys, generous salary, my name on the company letterhead. In exchange I withdraw the filing and assign my discovery "for development." I asked what development means. Nobody would say the word silver aloud. That is how I know what it means.*

*I have refused. M— says I am proud. Perhaps. But a survey is a signature. If I sign away what I found, every line I ever run afterward is drawn with someone else's hand guiding mine.*

*They filed first. I do not understand how — my filing predates theirs by eleven days. I have the receipt. Unless the receipt itself can be made to disappear, or the date altered, or the clerk bought. The concession they hold bears a date nine days BEFORE my own. Someone has forged it, or someone has been paid to backdate it, and either way the paper now says the mountain belongs to them.*

*I went to the land office. The registrar was polite. The registrar would not meet my eye. I know that look. I wore it myself once, when I let a grade be recorded wrong because a man with a badge stood behind my chair.*

*Last entry before I go up to take final measurements. If anything happens to me, the truth is in these numbers. Numbers do not lie. Men lie about numbers. Let whoever finds this run the figures themselves — the dates cannot survive arithmetic.*

And beneath that, dated four days later, in a hand that had begun to shake:

*A man came to the camp tonight. Company badge. Young. He said his name was Crane. He said Mr. de Rivergate wanted no trouble and neither did I. He looked at my field books the way a man looks at a debt. I have moved the originals. He will find copies if he searches, and copies can be argued with. Originals go where only a surveyor would think to look.*

Freya lowered the journal. The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere deep in the rock, water moved with the patience of geology.

"He was the witness," she said. Her voice sounded foreign in the vastness. "Crane. He witnessed the concession. My father wrote it — the forged paper needed a sworn witness of service, and Crane signed it. He was young. He was *young*, Odile. He has spent forty years being the company's memory of this crime."

Odile came back from the cleft, boots silent on the wet stone. She looked at the journal, at the camp, at the ribbon on the cup, and something in her face that was usually locked tight behind pilot's discipline came loose for a moment.

"And he shot Wren for carrying the same truth," she said. "Forty years, and he is still closing accounts."

Freya nodded, but she was already moving toward the back of the chamber, toward the spring, because her father had written *originals go where only a surveyor would think to look,* and she was a surveyor, and she thought she knew.

* * *

The spring rose at the chamber's far end from a fissure in the limestone, cold and constant, feeding a pool that spilled over into the silver-bright stream. Beside it, set into the rock with the same economical precision as the tent pitons, was a stone.

Not a grave marker. A datum stone. A surveyor's monument, cut square, its top dressed flat, and on its face, chiseled deep enough to outlast the company, the letters:

**D.B. No. 1 — CURRENT RIVER SOURCE — J.F.J. 18—**

Beneath the monument, laid with care, wrapped in a surveyor's ground cloth, rested a skeleton.

Freya knelt. She did not weep, though she felt the tears standing in her eyes like water behind a gauge board, held, measured, ready. She had imagined this moment a hundred ways on the long upstream and none of them had prepared her for the dignity of it — that he had chosen this place, beside his own datum, above his own discovery, and laid himself down with his instruments. His brass transit lay across his chest the way a sword lies across a knight's. His boots were worn through. One hand bone rested near the ground cloth's fold, and tucked beneath the finger bones, deliberate, was a folded paper.

She drew it out with hands that finally trembled.

The original concession filing. Her father's own hand, the territorial seal still affixed, the registrar's stamp legible, the date clear as a bell: eleven days before the company's paper. And pinned to it, in her father's writing, the arithmetic that destroyed the forgery — the subtraction performed, the difference noted, the conclusion stated in one line:

*Eleven days. Count them. Truth keeps better books than thieves.*

Behind the filing, deeper in the fold, were the original field books. All of them. He had hidden them with his own bones, the one place no thief would dig.

Freya sat back on her heels in the cold breath of the spring and let the grief come, finally, all at once, silently, the way a river takes a bank — not dramatically, but completely. Her father had not vanished. He had been erased, and he had answered erasure the only way left to him: he had become his own archive. He had made his body the vault.

"I found you," she whispered. "Papa. I found you, and I read your numbers."

The compass at her neck pointed, for the first time in her life, at nothing she needed corrected.

* * *

She heard Tomas before she saw him — the scramble of boots on wet limestone, the ragged breathing, and then he was stumbling out of the cleft passage with Odile's spare lantern swinging wild, soaked to the waist, a gash above his eyebrow sheeting blood into his collar, and behind him, far below and muffled by miles of stone, the Perpetual's whistle sounded once off the canyon walls like something hunting by echo.

"Tomas—" Freya was up, catching his arm before he fell.

"They repaired the boiler." He gulped air. "Crane — Crane knew about the gauge, Freya, he *let* me do it, it was a test, and I jumped at the gorge in the dark, I don't even know how I'm alive, the river put me ashore two bends down and I followed your marks on the stone, your chalk arrows, I saw them—" He stopped. He saw the camp. He saw the monument. He saw, with the terrible quick comprehension of a clerk, exactly what the skeleton and the papers meant.

"The permit," Freya said. "How long?"

His face did the calculation before his voice caught up, and she watched the number arrive in his eyes like a verdict.

"Four days," he said. "The concession expires in four days. After that it lapses unless renewed, and renewal requires proof of continuous working claim — which Crane cannot produce honestly, which means he'll file a salvage claim on the cavern instead, and salvage claims are heard at the assayer's office in Rivergate, and they move *fast*, Freya, faster than the permit board, and if he files first with his version of events—"

"Then we have four days to get the truth downstream faster than a steamer can carry a lie."

"Yes." He was staring at the datum stone. At the name chiseled in it. Their name. "Is that—"

"That's Papa."

Tomas went very still. Freya watched her half-brother — raised legitimate, raised in the house, raised on the schooling she'd been denied — stand in front of the father who had given her the compass and given him the name, and she watched him kneel in the wet gravel as though his legs had been called to order. He did not touch the bones. He took off his ruined hat, which was somehow the most formal thing Freya had ever seen anyone do in a cave.

"He hid the originals with himself," Tomas said at last. His clerk's voice, unsteady. "Because documents can be stolen from anywhere except a dead man nobody's looking for."

"He bet on us finding him."

"No." Tomas shook his head slowly. "He bet on *arithmetic*. We're just the ones doing it." He looked up at her, blood-streaked, half-drowned, and for once there was no ledger between them, no legitimate and illegitimate, no house and no compass. Only the math. "Four days. Can Odile run us down?"

Freya looked toward the cleft, where Odile stood with the lantern high, and beyond her, faint as a pulse through miles of stone, the Current went on sliding over its silver, patient, ancient, keeping its own books.

"She swore she'd never run the lower river again," Freya said. "But she came this far for her brother's name. And we're going to need every drop of speed she has — because somewhere behind us, Silas Crane is already counting down those same four days, and he has forty years of practice at making the paperwork come out his way."

She closed her father's journal, sealed the originals in oilcloth against her chest, and stood.

"We leave within the hour."
