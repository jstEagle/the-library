## 17. The Stand at the Source

The water spoke first.

Crane heard it before his men did — a low, resonant thrumming through the limestone, like a finger drawn along the rim of an enormous glass. He had been hearing that sound for thirty years, in dreams he never admitted to having, and now it was real again, and it was louder than memory. He raised one gloved hand and the column of enforcers behind him stopped on the wet shelf of rock, lanterns hooded to slits of amber.

The cavern mouth opened out of the gorge wall like a wound. Water poured from it — not a trickle but a broad, silvered sheet, catching the last gray light of evening and throwing it back broken. The ore dust gave the current a strange luminance, as though the river were lit from beneath by its own secret. Crane stood at the edge of the spray and let it mist his face, and for a moment — only a moment — he was twenty-six years old again, standing in this same place beside a thin man with a surveyor's chain and a compass he consulted like scripture.

He put the moment away. That was what he did with moments. He folded them small and put them away, and they stayed put.

"Torches," he said quietly. "Wide spacing. Nobody fires unless I say the word."

The men obeyed. They always obeyed; that was the architecture of Silas Crane's life, and he had built it himself, stone by stone, out of other people's fear. But as the torchlight spread and the cavern swallowed them — the ceiling rising into blackness where the light could not follow, the walls glittering faintly with mineral frost — Crane felt something he had not felt since the flood year. Not fear. Weight. The sense of walking into a room where all his accounts were kept, and every page turned toward him.

The passage narrowed, then opened. And there, forty yards in, silhouetted against a glow that came from somewhere deeper — the silver-bright water itself, he realized, glowing like drowned moonlight — stood Freya Joubert with a lantern in her hand and her brother at her shoulder.

Behind them, half-hidden in a cleft of rock, a third figure: Odile Marchand, whom Crane had personally entered into the company rolls as dead eleven years ago.

"Well," Crane said, and his voice carried down the stone corridor with terrible mildness. "The dead do keep busy."

* * *

Freya had heard them coming for ten minutes — boots on wet rock, the clink of gear, the particular discipline of men who moved when told and stopped when told. She had time to choose her ground, and she chose it the way she would have chosen it for a bridge abutment: back to solid stone, lantern high, the journal sealed in oilcloth pressed against her ribs beneath her coat. Tomas stood close enough that she could feel his breathing, quick and shallow. Odile had melted into the cleft behind them with a coil of rope and a look Freya had learned to read on the river: *I have one thing left to do, and I will do it whether you approve or not.*

Now Crane came forward alone, past his torch line, and stopped at the edge of the glow. Up close he was smaller than she remembered from Verrin's Crossing — or perhaps she had grown. His coat was dry where every other man's was soaked. He looked at her the way an assayer looks at ore: weighing, testing, finding the grade.

"Miss Joubert," he said. "You've cost me a steamer, a mail rider, two weeks, and four good men's tempers. I want you to know I don't hold any of it against you. You couldn't help yourself. It's in the blood."

"You'd know," Freya said. "You watched my father bleed it."

Something crossed Crane's face — not guilt; she understood, even then, that guilt was not a tool he carried — but recognition. The pleasure of meeting, at last, someone who knew the shape of the thing.

"Your father," Crane said, "was the finest surveyor this territory ever produced, and the worst businessman God ever failed to correct. He found all this." He gestured, and the gesture took in the glowing water, the glittering walls, the dark throat of the cavern going back and back. "Every foot of it. And then he sat down with a ledger instead of a gun, and he thought the ledger would protect him." Crane shook his head slowly. "Paper doesn't protect anyone, Miss Joubert. Paper is just a slower kind of burning."

"Then you won't mind," Freya said, "if I keep mine dry."

She saw Tomas shift beside her. She saw Crane see it too, and file it away, because Crane filed everything.

"I'm going to make you an offer," Crane said. "One offer. I've read your record — the grades you wouldn't sign, the spur line, the whole stubborn history. You're worth ten of the men behind me, and the company wastes people like you the way the Current wastes water. So here it is." He reached into his coat and drew out a document, folded once, and held it up so the lantern light caught the seal. "Full concession rights to the source, registered in your name — your name, Miss Joubert, clean and legitimate, no questions about blood or birthright. A fortune in unregistered silver. Your father's claim honored at last, in the only way claims are ever honored: by being paid."

Freya said nothing. The water said it for her, thrumming against the stone.

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange you burn the journal." Crane's voice did not change at all. "There's a brazier at the camp. We'll walk out together, you'll feed it the pages yourself, and tomorrow morning the Current belongs to the Joubert family. All of it. Everything Aldous Wren promised you and more, because Wren's claim dies with his papers, and his daughter gets nothing, and the world goes on exactly as it has gone on for thirty years." He tilted his head. "Or you keep your paper, and I take it off your body, and the body stays here where the river keeps everything else it's owed. I've left men in this gorge before. It's very good at keeping secrets. It's the one honest employee the company ever had."

The silence stretched. Freya became aware of her own heartbeat, slow and enormous, and of the compass in her pocket, and of her father's bones somewhere behind her in the dark, waiting to hear what she would say.

"You're right about one thing," she said finally. "Paper doesn't protect anyone. But paper isn't for protection, Mr. Crane. It's for testimony. My father understood that. He wrote it all down knowing it might kill him, because a survey isn't finished until someone reads it." She lifted her chin. "So no. I won't burn it. And you can't take it, because if you raise a hand to me, every man behind you becomes a witness to murder inside a cavern full of my father's evidence, and even Rivergate's lawyers can't burn testimony out of twelve sworn depositions."

Crane smiled. It was the worst thing she had seen yet, that smile — patient, almost fond.

"Depositions," he repeated softly. "Yes. About that."

* * *

Tomas had been counting.

It was what he was for; he had known it since he was nine years old and first understood that his father kept two families the way a bank keeps two ledgers, one for the light and one for the dark, and that Tomas had been assigned to the light. He counted things. He counted Crane's men — eight visible, two holding the passage mouth, which meant ten, which meant Crane expected resistance and had brought enough force to end it. He counted the seconds between the thrumming of the water and the drip from the ceiling, and found them irregular, which bothered him in a way he couldn't name yet. He counted the words in Crane's offer and noted what was missing from it: any mention of Odile Marchand, still hidden in the cleft, still unaccounted for.

And he counted, over the past three days, the entries in a certain leather ledger he had seen with his own eyes — because Silas Crane, who trusted no one, nevertheless made his clerk copy figures, and a clerk's eyes are the one instrument no master ever thinks to blind.

"Mr. Crane," Tomas said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "Before my sister answers anything else — there's a question of accounts."

Crane's gaze slid to him, and Tomas felt it like a hand on the back of his neck. But he stepped forward anyway, out of Freya's shadow, into the silvered light.

"The Perpetual's requisition file," Tomas said. "I processed it. Every voucher passes through my desk — that's how you used me, and we both know it. And vouchers leave copies." He let that sit. "Page forty-one. Disbursement dated the spring of the Wren shooting. Two hundred dollars, entered as 'claim settlement, A.W., Current headwaters.' Authorized signature: S. Crane. Paid the same week a prospector named Aldous Wren was found shot on the Verrin road by men wearing company badges."

The torches crackled. One of the enforcers shifted his weight, and the sound of it was very loud.

"A settlement," Crane said mildly, "is not a confession."

"No," Tomas agreed. "But a settlement paid *before* the incident was reported is. The report of the shooting reached the Rivergate office nine days after your disbursement date. I checked. Checking is also what I'm for." He took another step, and now his voice dropped, and it was worse than shouting, because it was quiet and precise and true. "And there's more in that ledger than Wren. There's an entry from thirty-one years ago — escort fees, provisions for a survey party of two, upriver, off the books. Party of two, Mr. Crane. One of them was you. The other was a surveyor named Joubert." He heard Freya's breath catch beside him and did not turn to look at her; he could not have survived looking at her just then. "You didn't just witness what happened to my father. You walked him up here. You were the last man to see him alive besides whoever did the killing, and the money says the company paid for the trip in advance."

"Tomas." Crane said his name the way a man sets down something fragile, deciding whether to keep it or break it. "You're describing a book you copied under orders. Those copies are company property. If they exist outside company custody, you're a thief, and thieves I can deal with." He paused. "If they exist."

"They exist," Tomas said, "in a place you won't find them, addressed to a person you can't reach, to be opened if anything happens to me. I learned contingency work at your desk, sir. You taught me everything I know about protecting paper." He allowed himself the smallest breath of a laugh, and hated how much it shook. "You can't burn it, and you can't buy it, and you can't shoot it without confirming every word. For the first time in your career, Mr. Crane, the ledger is reading *you*."

For a long moment Crane said nothing at all. Then he looked at Tomas with something that was almost — almost — respect, the way a craftsman respects a well-made trap, even the one closed on his own leg.

"Your father," Crane said, "would have been proud. He also thought paper would save him."

And he snapped his fingers, and the torches began to move.

* * *

Odile had been in the cleft for an hour, and her hands had known what they were doing for fifty-five minutes of it.

She had found the sluice gate on the way in — she had known it would be there, because her brother Étienne had helped build it, and Étienne built things to last, which was the joke of his whole short life. The old company sluice: a timber channel cut into the lower passage to divert spring meltwater around the workings during the flood season, gated with an oak paddle and an iron spindle. Étienne had carved his initials into the spindle housing the day they set it, laughing, saying *now the river will remember me.* Eleven months later the river remembered him differently, and the company paid Odile to pretend she hadn't heard the news, and she had taken the money, and she had been drowning slowly ever since in air instead of water.

The gate was rusted but sound. The spindle would turn. She had tested it with two fingers, gently, an hour ago, and felt the whole mechanism shudder like a sleeping animal, and above her, far up the channel, she had heard the water waiting — the spring melt coming down off the high country, backed up behind the gate, patient as everything else in this gorge.

Now she heard the torches moving, and Crane's voice saying something she couldn't make out, and she knew the arithmetic was finished. Ten men against three. Lanterns against darkness. And between her and the lower passage, thirty feet of open rock she would have to cross while every eye in the cavern was fixed on the Jouberts.

She thought of Étienne's initials. She thought of the eleven names she had never spoken aloud. She thought of the check, cashed, that had bought her silence, and how interest compounds.

Then she stepped out of the cleft, and crossed the open rock in eight strides, and no one saw her, because everyone in the world was watching Freya Joubert refuse to burn a book.

The spindle fought her. Rust flaked beneath her palms; the iron groaned; somewhere above, the backed-up water heard the gate move and began, with a sound like a long-held breath released, to come down. Odile set her feet and threw her whole weight against the spindle, and thought — absurdly, perfectly — of her brother's laugh, and gave the wheel the last turn it had waited eleven years for.

The oak paddle swung free.

The river came through the lower passage like judgment, like a debt called due, like eleven voices speaking at last in one voice. The roar filled the cavern, tore the torch smoke sideways, and Odile stood in the spray with her arms spread against the spindle housing, over her brother's initials, and shouted into the flood with everything she had:

"*Étienne! C'est payé!*"

It is paid.

* * *

The water hit the torch line first.

Freya saw the flames die in a hissing wall of spray, saw Crane's enforcers scatter backward up the shelf rock, saw the silvered glow swallowed by churning white — and then Crane's hand closed on her arm, iron through wet wool, and he hauled her toward the upper passage with a strength that had nothing to do with age.

"The journal," he shouted over the roar. "Give it to me and I'll carry you out!"

"It's already given!" she shouted back, and it was true, and the truth of it nearly broke her open: she had pressed the oilcloth packet into Tomas's hands an hour ago, before the torches came, because she had counted Crane's men too, and because trust, once re-forged in rapids, either holds or it doesn't, and she had chosen to find out. "You're holding nothing, Mr. Crane! You've been holding nothing since Verrin's Crossing!"

Something went out of Crane's face then — not defeat; she doubted the man owned any — but recalculation, fast and cold, a surveyor abandoning a bearing. He released her arm, turned into the chaos, and was gone upstream along the shelf with his men, choosing higher ground, choosing escape, choosing — she understood with a lurch — Rivergate. Four days. The permits. The salvage claim he could still file if he reached the assayer's office first.

"Tomas!" Her voice cracked against the flood-noise. "Tomas, where—"

A hand seized hers out of the white water. Her brother, soaked to the skin, the oilcloth packet strapped across his chest with Odile's rope, his eyes wide and terrified and absolutely steady.

"The upper gallery," he yelled. "Odile says it climbs — she says the flood fills the lower passage but the gallery drains east — she says *run*, Freya, she says the water doesn't stop at the gate!"

Behind them, the river rose. Behind them, the sluice roared its long-paid account. Somewhere in the dark below, the water was climbing toward the inner caverns — toward the camp, the survey, the bones — and Freya felt the grief of it like a physical blow, her father's resting place drowning a second time, and no time to mourn, no time for anything but the climb.

She ran. Tomas ran beside her, and ahead of them, a lantern swinging, Odile Marchand — officially dead these eleven years, and moving like a woman finally, finally alive — led them up into the black throat of the gallery as the source of the Current River closed its bright silver eye beneath the flood.
