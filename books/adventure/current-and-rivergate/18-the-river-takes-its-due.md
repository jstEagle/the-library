## 18. The River Takes Its Due

The water came the way Odile had promised it would — not as a wave but as a decision.

Freya heard it first as a change in the cavern's breathing. The spring-fed pool at the heart of the source had been murmuring since they entered, a low conversation between stone and current, and then that conversation stopped, the way a room goes quiet before bad news. In its place rose a sound she knew from her years on the survey corps: the groan of hydraulic pressure finding a new path, deep in the limestone, like a giant clearing its throat.

"Odile's breached it," Tomas said. He stood at the cleft mouth, torch raised, watching the lower passage where Crane and his enforcers had been moments before. The light trembled with his hand. "She's actually done it."

"She said she would." Freya was already moving, because there was no time to stand in awe of anything, not even betrayal turned to courage. "And when she does, this whole system becomes a chimney. Every drop the river pushes into the lower galleries has to come up through here."

The proof arrived while she was still speaking. Water burst from the fissure-mouths along the eastern wall — thin silver ropes at first, then thickening, hissing where they struck the torch-lit stone. The floor of the inner cavern, dry as old bone when they'd found her father's camp, began to shine. Then to run. Then to pour.

The camp itself was already lost. Freya had taken what mattered — the journal wrapped in oilcloth, the final survey sheets rolled tight inside a tin cylinder, the compass riding against her breastbone on its cord — but her father's rusted stove, his rotted bedroll, the eleven years of his exile marked in candle soot on the ceiling: all of it was going under, and going under fast.

"Tomas." She grabbed his sleeve. "We can't go back the way we came."

He turned from the cleft. Beyond it, the passage they'd entered by was no longer a passage; it was a brown throat swallowing itself, the water standing in it waist-deep and rising with a speed that made her stomach clench the way a collapsing grade used to. Somewhere down that black corridor, Odile was running for the boat with Crane's crew stampeding ahead of her, and Freya sent up a prayer she didn't believe in that the pilot knew the second channel better than the river knew its own name.

"So we drown here," Tomas said. Not a question. A clerk's statement of account, delivered flat because flatness was all he had left.

"No." Freya pulled the compass from her neck and held it out over the rising water, watching the needle. It swung, steadied, swung again — and settled, as it had settled at the datum point, on a bearing that was not north. Northeast by east, give or take the distortion of the ore in the walls. Her father's hidden line. "We go up."

* * *

The fissures were in the far wall, three of them, breathing cold air — which meant they went somewhere, because dead ends didn't breathe. Freya had catalogued them an hour ago with a surveyor's idle habit, measuring widths with her span, noting the flowstone slicking their mouths. Now that habit was the only currency she had.

"The middle one," she said. "It's widest at the base and it angles upward. I felt the draft strongest there."

"You *felt* it." Tomas's voice cracked on the word. He was soaked to the thighs now, the water climbing him like something patient. "Freya, you're gambling our lives on a draft."

"I'm gambling them on arithmetic." She waded toward the wall, the current shoving at her hips now, trying to turn her, to fold her into the brown flood filling the chamber. "Water rises at a rate we can measure. Air moves through rock at a rate we can measure. If that fissure connects to the upper galleries — and it must, or my father never could have provisioned this camp from above — then every foot we climb is a foot the river can't follow. That's not faith, Tomas. That's gradient."

Something moved behind his eyes — the old resentment, maybe, the half-brother who'd been given the schooling watching the sister who hadn't been given anything do the mathematics anyway. But he followed her. That was the thing about Tomas she could never make peace with and could never entirely despise: whatever else he'd sold, he always, in the end, followed.

The middle fissure took them the way a fist takes a ring — tight, unyielding, then suddenly generous. Freya went first, torch held high in one hand, pressing herself sideways through the flowstone throat. The rock scraped her pack, caught her shoulder, released her. Behind her she could hear Tomas's breath coming fast and shallow, and beneath it, muffled by stone, the river arriving in the chamber they'd abandoned — a sound like applause, enormous and indifferent.

Then the fissure opened into a gallery, and Freya stopped, and laughed once, a short ugly sound of relief.

It was a surveyor's gallery. Cut, not carved — chisel marks still visible in the walls, timber cribs rotting at intervals, and a rusted iron rail bolted into the floor, disappearing into the dark on both sides. An old working. Silver mining, decades old, abandoned — or hidden. Her father hadn't found the source by accident. He'd found the *mine*, and the mine had led him to the fraud.

"He worked here," Tomas breathed. His torch found a crate stenciled with lettering gone soft with rust: RIVERGATE CONSOLIDATED — CURRENT DIVISION. He touched the stencil like a man touching a scar. "This is the original concession. Before the forgery. This is—"

"Later." The water was audible below them now, a rising hum in the floor, and Freya did not trust limestone floors that hummed. "Up. Always up. Find me the adit."

They climbed the rail line. The gallery rose in switchbacks, following the ore body — and the ore body was everywhere, veins of it glittering in their torchlight like frost, like the river's own brightness translated into stone. Freya understood, with a lurch, why the company had sealed this division off its maps. You didn't need Wren's cache of unregistered silver when you had *this*. Unless the cache and the vein were the same thing, and the concession papers that had drowned her father's name had been written specifically to bury this gallery under someone else's signature.

The air stayed cold and moving. The draft strengthened. And twice, as they climbed, the floor shuddered beneath them, and water sounded in the walls around them like something pacing.

* * *

The third switchback ended at a collapse — old timber, fallen rock, a gap a child could crawl through if the child were desperate enough. They were desperate enough. Freya went through with her pack straps cutting her shoulders and the oilcloth journal pressed between her chest and the stone, and she thought, absurdly, of her father doing this same crawl thirty years ago, older tools, same fear, same bearing. Inheritance wasn't the compass. Inheritance was this: the willingness to put your body where the truth was trapped and dig it out.

Beyond the collapse the gallery narrowed to a natural fissure again, and here the water found them.

It came without warning — a spout from a side crack, pressurized, hitting Tomas square in the chest and knocking him off the ledge. Freya lunged, caught his wrist, and for three full seconds she hung over the dark with her brother's weight tearing at her shoulder and the river roaring somewhere below, and she thought with perfect clarity: *this is the moment. This is where I learn whether he'd have caught me.*

She hauled. He came up gasping, soaked, his torch dead and hers guttering, and he didn't say thank you. He said, "The journal — is it wet?"

And Freya, hanging onto a ledge in a flooding mine with her arm screaming, started to laugh and couldn't stop.

"It's fine," she managed. "Oilcloth. Clerk-proof. Come *on*."

* * *

They broke surface-level — she felt it rather than measured it, the pressure in her ears easing, the air turning drier — and twenty minutes after that, the fissure gave them daylight.

Not much. A thread of it, gray-green, filtering down through a crack overhead where the limestone thinned toward the gorge wall. But it was daylight, and it meant the outside world, and it meant the river had not won. Freya sat down hard on the rail tie and let her hands shake now that shaking was affordable.

Tomas sat beside her. For a long time neither of them spoke. Below them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, the flood was still working, still filling the lower galleries, erasing the camp, the cleft, the evidence of their entrance — and, Freya realized, sealing Crane's crew out as thoroughly as it had nearly sealed them in. Odile's sluice had been a weapon aimed at everyone in the cavern. It had simply missed the two people who kept climbing.

"She knew," Tomas said finally. "Marchand. She timed it so we'd have the fissures."

Freya shook her head. "She timed it so *she'd* have the boat and Crane wouldn't. We were a rounding error she hoped for." But even as she said it, she remembered the pilot's face at the Splits, the guilt worn smooth as river stone, and amended silently: *maybe.*

Tomas pulled his satchel around and opened it with fingers that wouldn't quite obey him. Inside, wrapped in its own oilcloth, lay a bundle of pages — his own handwriting, close and clerk-neat, covering sheet after sheet copied by torchlight in the hours before the standoff. Her father's journal. The forged concession. The ledger entries in Rivergate founder Amiel Vasseur's own hand. Everything.

"You copied all of it," Freya said.

"You read it aloud. I wrote it down." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him a wince — his shoulder was wrenched where the water had hit him. "That's the job, isn't it? Somebody reads the truth and somebody records it. I've spent ten years recording lies for them. Seemed time I balanced the books."

Freya looked at him — really looked, for the first time since the Splits, since the boiler gauge, since the jump into the gorge. He was pale, cut, half-drowned, sitting in an abandoned silver mine with stolen documents against his chest, and he had never looked more like their father than he did right now, and she suspected he would hate hearing it.

"The permit expires in four days," she said. "Crane will file a salvage claim the moment he can reach an office. If he files first, everything down there—" she nodded at the flooded dark, at the veins glittering unseen in the walls, at the bones of Aldous Wren's partners and possibly, someday, their own father's grave site upstream at the spring— "becomes legally his. Legally theirs."

"Then we don't go back through the cavern." Tomas was already thinking like a clerk again, and she hated how much comfort she took from it. "We come out of the gorge wall, we find Odile, and we run the Current downstream faster than it's ever been run. Four days. Saint-Aubin's a day and a half if the water cooperates. Rivergate's another two."

"And if the water doesn't cooperate?"

He almost smiled. "Then we lie to it. It's what we're best at, this family."

Freya got up. Her legs held. She took out the compass one more time — habit now, or sacrament — and watched the needle settle on its impossible bearing, pointing northeast by east along her father's hidden line, straight through the mountain, straight toward the river and the sea and the city where the truth would either be spoken on time or buried forever under four days' worth of ink and signatures.

Above them, through the crack in the stone, the sky was the color of pewter, and the wind had shifted, and it smelled like rain.

A lot of rain.

"Odile said the Current floods fast in the high country," Freya said quietly. "If this storm reaches the headwaters before we reach Saint-Aubin..."

"Then the river takes its due from whoever's slowest." Tomas stood, shouldering his satchel, and for once there was nothing small in his face at all. "So we're not going to be slowest."

They climbed toward the thread of light together.
