## 19. Four Days to Rivergate

The river had a temper, and Odile Marchand had spent eleven years pretending she didn't know it personally.

She stood at the tiller of the *Mireille* — the leaky, borrowed, half-drowned excuse for a boat that had carried them out of the gorge with the water still roaring behind them — and let the Current take her hands the way it always had, the way it had before the flood, before the eleven names, before the company paid her to be dead. The boat was riding high. Too high. They'd jettisoned everything that wasn't food, paper, or Freya Joubert's stubbornness, and now the hull slapped the water like a flat stone, quick and skittish, wanting to run.

Good. Let it want to run. She wanted to run too.

Behind her, Tomas Joubert lay wrapped in every dry blanket they owned, shivering out the last of the gorge. He'd gone into the water a company clerk and come out something else — she'd seen the change in his face when Freya hauled him over the gunwale, the look of a man who had finally understood what his name cost. He hadn't spoken much since. He didn't need to. He'd started copying the journal by firelight, page after page in that small clerk's hand, and when Odile asked him why he was duplicating what was already sealed in oilcloth against his sister's chest, he'd said only: "Because Crane burns things."

Crane burned things. Odile knew that better than most. She'd watched him burn a pilot's license once — hers — in an iron brazier on the Rivergate docks while two company men held her arms and a third read out the findings of an inquiry she'd never been allowed to attend. *Negligence. Eleven souls lost through the fault of the pilot.* As if the river had asked her permission. As if she'd been given a choice between running the barges through that flood-water and letting them sit at the landing until the company found another fool.

She had refused. That was the whole of her crime. She had looked at the brown, swollen Current and said no, and eleven men had gone anyway, under another pilot, in boats she'd inspected and condemned, and the river had taken them all in the space of one afternoon below the Splits.

Her brother Étienne had been the youngest. Nineteen. He'd waved to her from the deck of the second barge as it cast off, because he still believed the company would listen to reason if you just kept asking.

Odile put her hands harder on the tiller and did not think about Étienne. There would be time for thinking later, if they lived, and if they didn't there would be no point.

"Water's dropping," Freya said, coming forward along the thwarts with the chart case under her arm. She moved like a surveyor even now — measuring everything, trusting nothing she hadn't measured twice. "Two fingers since dawn. Maybe three."

"That's not dropping," Odile said. "That's the gorge drinking. It'll give it back by evening, and meaner." She nodded upstream, where the cleft behind the limestone shelf had already swallowed itself back into the rock, the spring's silver brightness hidden again behind falling water. "We have four days. The permits die at noon on the fourth, and Crane will have a salvage claim drafted already — he's had it drafted for twenty years, I'd wager, waiting on someone else to find the source for him. Paper travels faster than boats, Freya. We're not racing the river anymore. We're racing ink."

Freya looked at her a long moment. "Then we don't sleep."

"We sleep in shifts," Odile said. "I'm not killing us to beat a clerk's pen. Dead women file no claims."

She said it lightly, and Freya almost smiled, and neither of them mentioned the eleven.

* * *

They ran the lower gorge in the gold light of late afternoon, and this was the part Odile had sworn never to do again: the Narrows, where the Current pinched itself between walls close enough to spit across, where the water came down in standing waves taller than a man and the air itself seemed to shout. Eleven years ago she had stood on the bank above this stretch with a company contract in her fist and said no. Now she stood at the tiller of a boat barely bigger than a coffin lid and said yes, and the difference between those two words was the whole shape of her life.

But the river was low. Low water changed everything. The standing waves flattened into long, glassy flumes; the holes that could hold a boat and turn it opened their dark mouths and then shut them; the channel that had killed Étienne's barge showed its bones — a reef of black limestone right down the center, plain as a written line, avoidable by any pilot who could read.

"You see it?" she called back.

"I see it," Freya said. She was at the bow with a setting pole, and she read water the way she read grades — Odile had watched her learn it in six days, what took most pilots six years, because the woman couldn't help herself, couldn't look at moving water without wanting to know its gradient, its fall per mile, its mind. "Left of the reef. There's a seam — darker line, two boat-widths wide."

"There's your door. Hold us in it."

The *Mireille* went through the Narrows like a needle through cloth. The walls closed in, the roar climbed up inside Odile's chest and shook her ribs, spray hit them in cold sheets, and the boat ran the seam true — left of the reef, right of the hole, exactly where the dead water of eleven years ago had shown her it wanted to be run. For half a minute she was nineteen years younger and unruined, doing the only thing she had ever loved, and then the gorge spat them out into the widening valley below and the moment was gone, and she was crying without any sound at all, the way she'd learned to cry, steering with wet cheeks in the wind.

Freya came aft and said nothing. That was the thing about the surveyor: she knew when a measurement was private.

"He ran this water," Odile said at last. "Étienne. Not here — below the Splits, in flood. But he'd have run it fine. He had my eyes for it." She wiped her face with her sleeve, rough, angry at the tears. "I told the inquiry the barges weren't sound. I put it in writing. They lost the writing."

"They won't lose this one," Freya said. She touched the oilcloth packet at her chest, then the journal copy lashed under the thwart. "Paper's all we are now, Odile. All any of us ever were to that company — entries in a ledger. So we make our entry first, and we make it loud."

"The river's helping," Odile said, and meant it strangely. All her life the Current had been the obstacle, the enemy, the thing between her and every wage and every grave. But running downstream with the current under her keel and the truth in the boat, it felt like what it must have felt like to her father's father, to the first pilots: not a wall but a road. The river wanted to carry them. It had always wanted to carry them. It was the company that had taught everyone to fear it, because a feared river is a tollable river.

Four days. Two hundred miles. The Current, for once, flowing their way.

* * *

They passed the Splits at dusk and did not stop.

Odile had promised herself she'd never look at the place again, and then she looked. The fork of the river spread out below the last ridge, silver-gray in the dying light, calm as a held breath, and somewhere under that calm were eleven men and one barge and a boy of nineteen who had waved goodbye. The company had fished out what it could and paid what it had to and erased the rest. There was no marker. There had never been a marker. You could stand on the bank and see nothing but pretty water.

Tomas sat up in his blankets and followed her gaze. He'd heard the story from Freya, Odile supposed, sometime in the nights she'd slept. He didn't say anything either. But when they'd cleared the fork and the Splits slid astern, he took off his hat — the good company hat, ruined now, brim torn from the gorge — and held it against his chest until the bend took the place away.

Well, Odile thought. Maybe the legitimate name was worth something after all, if the man wearing it had learned to grieve other people's dead.

They made nine miles that night by moonlight, Odile steering on memory, Freya sounding with the pole when the moon went behind cloud. In the small hours Odile slept in the bilge and dreamed of the inquiry room, and woke with her hands clenched around a tiller that wasn't there, and lay listening to the water slap the hull and reminding herself: *this time they believe you. This time the paper is real. This time someone will read it aloud.*

* * *

The second day they passed the first sign of the company's reach: a wood raft tied up at the old Kessel landing, raftsmen who stared at their strange little boat and called out questions Odile didn't answer. By noon they saw smoke on the east bank — a signal station, company-built, one of the relay fires used to pass word up and down the Current faster than any boat. Someone there had seen them. Someone there was counting.

"They'll wire ahead from Verrin's Crossing," Tomas said. He was stronger now, eating, moving, the clerk surfacing in him again — and for once Odile was glad of it. "Crane can't outrun us on water, so he'll outrun us on paper. A telegram to the assayer's office: seal the records pending investigation. A telegram to the permit board. A telegram to whoever holds Wren's daughter's address." He paused. "And one to the mission at Saint-Aubin, if he thinks the papers survived the mail rider."

"Do they?" Freya asked quietly. "Survive?"

"I sent them by Marta Lune," Tomas said. "The mail rider. She's my godmother's sister and she hates Silas Crane more than God hates sin. If anyone got those papers through, it's Marta." He said it like a man reciting a prayer whose answer he didn't trust. "But we plan as if she failed."

So they planned as if she failed. The assayer's office opened at eight. The permit board sat at noon on the fourth day. Wren's daughter — Odile tried to hold the name in her head, *Marguerite Wren*, a schoolteacher, a woman who had spent years writing letters to a mining office about a claim nobody honored — could be found at the girls' school on Cordwainer Lane. Three doors to knock on, three filings to make, and every one of them worthless if Crane's ink arrived first.

"Then we arrive first," Freya said, and began re-lashing the cargo, and Odile turned the boat into the main channel and gave her the river's full speed.

* * *

On the third day the Current gave them its gift.

It came as weather — a hard rain far up in the western hills, days old, arriving now as a rise: six inches, then a foot, the river swelling with borrowed water, and every riffle that would have dragged the shallow *Mireille* aground drowned deep and runnable. Odile had spent her life cursing rises. This one she blessed aloud, standing at the tiller with rain streaming off her chin, laughing like a madwoman as the boat surged down reaches she'd have portaged a week ago.

Freya stood beside her and read the new water hungrily, calling depths, calling seams, and Odile realized the woman was happy — really happy, in the way of people who have stopped being afraid of the right things. The river that had been the obstacle their whole journey had turned underneath them and become the road. Even the rain was theirs. Even the flood.

Only once did the day go dark. Near evening they rounded a bend and found wreckage on a gravel bar: planking, a stove door, a scrap of canvas stamped with the Rivergate house-mark. The *Perpetual*'s shore party, or Crane's advance men, or both — abandoned, hurried, days old. And nailed to a riverside post, water-swollen but legible, a printed notice:

*BY ORDER OF THE RIVERGATE CONCESSION — ALL TRAFFIC BELOW SAINT-AUBIN SUBJECT TO INSPECTION. SALVAGE RIGHTS PENDING ON CERTAIN MINERAL DEPOSITS. REWARD FOR INFORMATION.*

Salvage rights pending. There it was, in company type: Crane's claim, drafted and waiting, needing only his signature and a compliant board. Odile read it twice and felt the old cold move through her, the cold of the inquiry room, of paper deciding lives in rooms the paper never visits.

"He wrote it before we ever left," she said. "Before Wren died, maybe. It was always waiting."

"Then it dies tomorrow," Freya said, "at noon, with the permits. Paper against paper, Odile. His is older. Ours is truer."

They ran on into the dark, and Odile steered by the feel of the current against the rudder, and did not swear at the river once.

* * *

The fourth day came gray and fast.

They smelled Rivergate before they saw it — coal smoke and tanneries and the river-town stink of a hundred landings — and then the city rose out of the morning haze, docks and warehouses and the company tower standing over all of it like a headstone. Odile brought the *Mireille* in past the customs wharf, past the ferry slip, and set her nose for the little stairs below the assayer's office, the poor man's landing, where no company clerk bothered to watch.

Eight bells from the cathedral as they tied off. Eight o'clock. The office would be open. Somewhere across town, on Cordwainer Lane, a school bell would be ringing for Marguerite Wren's first class. Somewhere in the company tower, a man with burned hands and a private ledger would be dictating telegrams.

Tomas was on the stairs before the boat finished bumping, hatless, coatless, his journal-copy bundle strapped tight, taking the steps two at a time like a man going to confession. Freya followed with the oilcloth packet, and Odile came last, pausing with one hand on the mooring line, looking back downriver — the way they'd come, two hundred miles of water, the gorge, the Splits, the source, the bones.

"Keep her ready," she told the empty boat. "Whatever happens up there, keep her ready."

Then she climbed the stairs into Rivergate, into the noise and the coal smoke, toward the assayer's door — and stopped, because the door was already open, and a girl of perhaps ten stood in it, out of breath, school satchel still on her shoulder, staring at Odile as if she'd been sent.

"You're the boat from upriver," the girl said. "Madame Wren said — she said if anyone came off the water this morning, before the tall gentleman in gray, I was to run and fetch her." The child swallowed. "The tall gentleman's clerk has been sitting in the assayer's office since seven o'clock. Madame Wren says you've got until he signs."

Down the street, toward Cordwainer Lane, the school bell began to ring.
