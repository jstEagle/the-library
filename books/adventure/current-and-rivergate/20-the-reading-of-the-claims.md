## 20. The Reading of the Claims

The permit board convened in the assayer's hall on Corben Street, a room built to make ordinary people feel like ore waiting to be weighed. Freya felt it at once — the long benches of dark cedar, the raised dais where three commissioners sat beneath the Rivergate Company's charter seal, the clerks' desks arranged in tiers like sluice boxes designed to catch whatever value trickled down from the public and let the rest run off into company channels.

She had dressed as an engineer, not a petitioner. Her survey coat was brushed clean of four days' river grime but not of its history; the oilcloth packet rode inside it against her chest, damp still at one corner where the flood had found it. Her hands were steady. She noticed that with a kind of detached surprise, the way she'd once noticed her own calm while reading grades through a rockslide. Fear had been left somewhere upstream, in the water.

Tomas stood beside her in his clerk's gray, his collar crisp, his face pale as fresh paper. He had not slept. Neither had she, but he wore his sleeplessness like a confession and she wore hers like armor, and between them they made a strange matched pair — the legitimate child and the illegitimate one, both of them carrying their father's name into a room that had spent thirty years pretending he never existed.

Odile waited outside. She had said she would, and Freya believed her, which was itself a small revolution. *I'll be at the dock,* Odile had said, *because if this goes wrong I want to be on water when I hear about it.* She had run the Current downstream in forty hours on a channel she'd sworn never to run again, and her hands still shook when she thought no one was looking. But she had brought them in alive. That debt would take longer than a morning to settle.

The hall filled slowly. Clerks, claim-holders, a scattering of the curious. And near the back, in a plain dress with her hair pinned severely and her father's claim papers folded in a leather wallet gone soft with age, sat Marta Wren.

Freya had found her the night before, at the boarding house on Tanner's Row where Aldous Wren's letters had been returned unopened for two years. Marta had opened the door, looked at the papers in Freya's hand, and gone very still — the stillness of a woman who has stopped expecting anything and must now relearn how to hope without being destroyed by it.

"He talked about you," Freya had said. "At the end. He said — deliver these before the permits expire. He made me swear."

Marta had taken the wallet the way one takes a dying man's hand. Then she had looked up, and her eyes were her father's eyes, and she had said: "Then we go together. I want to watch them read it."

* * *

The chairman of the board was a commissioner named Halvard, a dry man with dry hands who spoke as though reading freight tariffs aloud for his own amusement. The docket ran long. Spur-line easements. A gravel concession on the lower Verrin. Freya sat through all of it, feeling the oilcloth against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and watched the clock above the dais crawl toward eleven.

It was Tomas who moved first. When Halvard called the Current River concession — *renewal, standard terms, consent agenda* — Tomas rose from the clerks' tier, which he had no right to do, and said, in a voice pitched to carry:

"Commissioner. There is a matter of record concerning that item."

Every head turned. Freya saw Silas Crane then, for the first time since the cavern — standing by the west doors with two of his enforcers, dressed in civilian black, his face composed into the mild patience of a man attending a funeral for someone he disliked. His eyes found Freya and did not move from her. He had come to watch the salvage claim be filed, she realized. He had come to see the paperwork bury them.

Halvard frowned over his spectacles. "Mr. Joubert. You are not counsel of record."

"No, sir. I am a clerk of this company." A pause, deliberate, letting the words land. "Which is why I know what is in the file."

"State your matter briefly or sit down."

"I'd rather not state it briefly," Tomas said. "I'd rather read it into the record. All of it. With the board's permission — and if the board denies permission, then with the board's refusal noted, in my hand, dated today."

Something shifted in the room. Freya felt it the way she felt a change in current through the soles of her boots — a pressure rearranging itself. Halvard glanced at his fellow commissioners, at the company's counsel seated to one side, and Freya understood then what Odile had told her on the last night of the run: *the company is not a man, chérie. It is a ledger. It does not love Crane. It loves only what balances.*

"The board will hear the clerk," Halvard said.

* * *

What followed took ninety minutes, and Freya remembered most of it afterward in fragments, the way one remembers a flood — not as a continuous event but as a series of things that should not have been possible happening one after another.

Tomas read first. He read the concession grant of thirty-one years ago, and then he read the survey of Joubert senior — her father's final survey, copied in Tomas's precise clerk's hand from the journal pages, every bearing and datum transcribed exactly — and he read the forged transfer instrument by which the silver rights of the upper Current had passed from Joubert to the company's founder, witnessed by one S. Crane, then a junior agent, whose signature appeared on both documents in two different years under two different commissions that could not both have been true.

He read Crane's own ledger. The real one, recovered from the Perpetual's strongbox after the flood, its entries in Crane's careful hand: payments to silence, payments to burn, and the entry from decades ago that Freya had made herself read twice because once was not enough to believe — *Escorted J. upriver per instructions. Returned alone.*

When Tomas's voice reached that line, the room went absolutely silent, and Freya heard her own breath, and thought: *there it is. My father, spoken aloud in a room full of strangers, at last.*

Then it was her turn.

She rose. She untied the oilcloth packet and unwrapped it on the petitioners' table, and the journal lay there — swollen with river water, its pages warped, its ink faded but legible, because her father had written in iron gall ink the way he did everything else: built to outlast him.

"My name," she said, "is Freya Joubert. My father was Étienne Joubert, surveyor, licensed in this territory twenty-nine years ago, struck from the rolls the year he died — though no one troubled to record that he had died, or how, or where. I hold his compass. I hold his map of closed lines. And I hold this."

She held the journal up so the commissioners could see it. "I am not here to claim the silver. Before this board hears anything else, I want that understood. I made a promise to a dying man named Aldous Wren, and I am keeping it."

She nodded toward the back of the hall, and Marta Wren rose and walked forward the whole length of the room, past every bench, and laid her father's claim papers on the table beside the journal. Her hands were shaking. Her voice was not.

"Aldous Wren located and registered intent on the source workings of the Current River in the spring of the year the elder Joubert disappeared. His claim predates the company's renewal filings by eleven months. The filing fee was paid. The receipt exists. The company's agents shot him on the Verrin road six weeks ago to prevent this document from reaching me." She looked at the commissioners, one by one. "I am his daughter. I am here before the permits expire. Read the date, gentlemen. We are inside the window. Barely. Because a pilot named Odile Marchand — whom this company lists as dead — ran us downriver through the gorge in forty hours to make it so."

Halvard read the date. Freya watched him read it, watched his dry finger move along the line, watched him look up at the company's counsel and receive in return a small, terrible nod — the nod of a lawyer who has counted the days himself and knows the arithmetic is lost.

* * *

Crane moved at the end, because of course he did. He had always been a man who closed accounts.

"Commissioners." His voice was quiet, reasonable, almost gentle. "These are forgeries and the testimony of a dismissed engineer, a drowned pilot, and a dead prospector's daughter. The company's position—"

"Mr. Crane." Halvard's voice had changed entirely; the tariff-reading dryness was gone, replaced by something colder. "You will be silent. Counsel for the company will address the board."

And the company's counsel — a narrow, gray man named Pellier whom Freya had never seen before and would remember all her life — stood, straightened his papers, and said:

"The company dissociates itself from Mr. Crane effective this hour. We have reviewed the documents entered this morning. We have also, since yesterday evening, been conducting our own inquiry." A pause, elegant as a blade laid flat on felt. "Mr. Crane's salvage filing, submitted at dawn, was filed under authority the company had already revoked. The board will find our letter of revocation in its correspondence, timestamped last night at nine-forty. Whatever Mr. Crane did on the Current River, he did it as a private man pursuing a private fortune. The company thanks the board for its attention and consents to the collapse of the concession."

Freya stared at him. Beside her, Tomas exhaled slowly through his nose — the sound of a clerk watching the largest entry of his career post itself, and understanding exactly what it meant. The company did not love Crane. The company loved what balanced. And this morning, with a journal and a ledger and a claim receipt sitting on a public table, Crane had stopped balancing and started costing.

Two men Freya did not know — big, quiet, wearing the company's colors under their coats — crossed the hall and stood on either side of Silas Crane. He did not resist. That was the worst of it, Freya thought later. He looked at her across the length of the room, and there was no hatred in his face at all, only a kind of weary bookkeeping, as if he were totaling a column he had known for thirty years would not sum. Then he said, just loud enough for her to hear:

"Your father begged too. At the end. He asked me to send word to his children." His eyes went from her to Tomas and back. "I didn't. Consider the account settled."

They walked him out through the west doors into the light, and the doors swung shut behind him, and the room began, all at once, to talk.

* * *

The formal order came down within the hour: the Current River concession declared void ab initio, the founder's grant rescinded, the upper workings reverted to the territorial register pending lawful claim. Halvard read it in his flat voice, and Freya listened to every word, and when he finished she found that her legs wanted very much to sit down and she refused to let them.

Outside, the afternoon had turned golden and cold. Odile was leaning against a mooring post on the Corben Street dock as promised, arms crossed, watching the water as if it might yet betray her. When she saw the three of them coming — Freya, Tomas, and Marta Wren walking abreast — she pushed off the post and searched their faces, and Freya watched the answer arrive in her before anyone spoke.

"Well?" Odile said anyway.

"It's done," Freya said. "The concession is dead. Crane is in custody — the company's own lawyers took him. Marta's claim stands. Wren's daughter holds the source."

Odile looked at Marta for a long moment. Then she said, "Your father ran supplies for me once, years ago. Never asked for pay. Said he was saving his money for something better than money." She shrugged, rough-eyed. "I suppose this was it."

Marta Wren, who had not cried at her father's grave-marker words or at the reading of his claim, cried then — briefly, furiously, wiping her face with the heel of her hand as if annoyed at it — and Odile put an arm around her shoulders and let her, staring out at the river the whole time as though giving her privacy by refusing to witness it.

Tomas stood a little apart. He had his clerk's satchel over his shoulder and his hands in his pockets, and he was looking at the water too, and Freya came to stand beside him because someone had to, and because she was beginning to understand that someone always had to, with him.

"They offered me reinstatement," he said quietly. "Pellier. After the session. Full restoration, back pay, a desk with a window." He laughed, short and hollow. "I told him I'd think about it. Which is more than I deserve, because we both know what I'm thinking about."

"What are you thinking about?"

"That I burned a mail room's worth of lies to buy you three days, and sabotaged a boiler, and jumped into a gorge, and none of it buys back the thing I did when we were young." He turned to look at her then, and his eyes were red-rimmed and honest, and she hated him a little and loved him a little and knew both would last. "You don't have to say anything, Freya. I just needed it said out loud, on land, in daylight, where it goes in the record."

Freya looked at the Current, running wide and brown-gold below the docks, carrying everything downstream the way it always had — silt, secrets, the occasional truth. She thought of her father's bones beside the bright water, of the compass in her pocket that had finally pointed true, of a promise kept to a man she'd known for one hour and would keep for the rest of her life.

"There's work," she said. "On the upper Current. Honest survey work, under Marta's claim. It'll take a season. Maybe two." She paused. "The pay is terrible and the water is worse."

Tomas looked at her. Something moved through his face — hope arriving cautiously, like a man testing ice.

"You're asking?"

"I'm informing you of an opening," Freya said. "Applications close tomorrow morning. At the dock. Bring your own pen."

Behind them, Marta Wren laughed — the first sound Freya had ever heard her make that wasn't grief or steel — and Odile grinned like a woman who had just run a rapid and won, and far upstream, at the source of the Current, the water came up silver-bright out of the limestone, running free for the first time in thirty years toward a river that finally knew its own name.
