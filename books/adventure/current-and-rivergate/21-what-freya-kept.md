## 21. What Freya Kept

The offer came on company paper, which was the first thing wrong with it.

Freya read it three times in the parlor of the Rivergate survey office, where a junior clerk had been sent to fetch her and where the windows had been washed for her arrival — she noticed that too, the way she noticed everything now, the habit of a woman who had spent six weeks learning that small details were where men hid their knives. The letter bore the seal of the Rivergate Consolidated Navigation & Concession Company, and beneath the seal, in a hand far too careful to be sincere, an invitation: *The Board, having reviewed the circumstances of your dismissal from the survey corps, wishes to extend its formal apology and to offer you the position of Chief Engineer of the Current Survey, with full authority over all lines, grades, and reports pertaining thereto.*

Full authority. She turned the phrase over like a stone found in a stream bed, checking its underside.

They were offering her the river. That was the astonishing part. Six weeks ago they had taken her name off a payroll and called her insubordinate; now they wanted to hang it on a door. And she understood why, understood it with the cold clarity of someone who had read a dead man's journal by lamplight in a cavern full of silver water: the concession had collapsed, the permits had lapsed, the founder's forged rights had been read aloud into the record before the permit board while Silas Crane sat flanked by his own company's lawyers with his ledger open on the table between them. The Current was unclaimed water now. Whoever surveyed it first would write its truth, and the company had learned — at ruinous cost — what happened when other people wrote the truth instead.

So they wanted her hand on the pen. Her name on the datum.

She set the letter down on the clerk's desk and looked out the window at the river itself, broad and brown-green and patient below the city's warehouses, carrying its freight as if nothing had ever been hidden in it. Somewhere up there, past the Splits, past the gorge with its slates of scratched names, the spring ran bright with ore dust behind a limestone shelf, and beside it a cairn of stones her own hands had rebuilt, and under the stones a man who had taught her to read grades before she could read letters.

*Chief Engineer of the Current Survey.* It was, she admitted to herself, standing there in her one good coat with the compass heavy in her pocket, the most dangerous thing anyone had ever offered her. Not because it was false — that was the terrible elegance of it; the position was real, the authority was real, the apology might even have been sincere in the way institutions are sincere, which is to say sincerely frightened. It was dangerous because she wanted it. Some tired, starved part of her, the part that had stood in Verrin's Crossing with thirty days of debt called due and no work and no name, heard *chief engineer* and felt the old wound close over with warm flesh.

But she had learned something upstream that no salary could buy back or buy off. An inheritance is not what a man leaves you. It is what he asks of you.

Her father had not left her treasure. He had left her a map of closed lines — every route the company had shut down, bought out, buried, falsified — and a compass that did not point north, and a grave beside a spring, and a survey that proved the whole glittering edifice of Rivergate rested on a forged page. He had left her *work*. And work, unlike treasure, could not be surrendered, sold, or accepted back into the fold without becoming something else entirely.

If she took their commission, the honest survey would be honest the way a cage is clean. Every line she ran would pass through offices that had once demanded her signature on a lie. Every report would be filed in buildings built with stolen silver. She would spend ten years measuring a river whose first measurement had killed her father, and at the end of it her name would sit in their ledgers, tidy and domesticated, another asset of the Rivergate Consolidated — and the closed-lines map, her father's true testament, would molder in some board archive stamped *superseded*.

No. She folded the letter along its creases until it was a small hard rectangle, the way she'd fold anything she meant to keep but never meant to obey.

"I'll need my answer in writing," the junior clerk said nervously, because clerks always said things like that when the moment required silence.

"You'll have it," Freya said. "In writing. Today."

* * *

Wren's daughter kept rooms above a chandler's shop on Rope Walk, where the smell of tallow and tar came through the floorboards and the window looked out on nothing but rooftops and gulls. Her name was Marta Wren, and she received Freya standing, as if she had been standing for six weeks, which perhaps she had.

She was not what Freya had expected, though Freya could not have said what she had expected — someone older, someone harder, someone shaped by the same long absence that had shaped her own life. Instead Marta Wren was perhaps thirty, plain-faced, quick-eyed, with ink stains on her fingers and a claim agent's ledgers stacked on her table like fortifications. She had spent those six weeks doing exactly what Aldous Wren must have known she would do: fighting. Filing. Arguing the claim papers through every office in Rivergate with the patience of a woman who had inherited her father's stubbornness if not his wandering feet.

"He told me once," she said, pouring tea Freya did not want but drank anyway, "that if a stranger ever brought me his papers, I should look at her hands first. He said you can tell what a person's carried by their hands." She glanced across the table. "Yours are a surveyor's hands."

"They're an engineer's hands."

"Same calluses." Marta set down the pot. "The assayer's office confirmed the seam last week. Provisional registration under my father's name, pending proper survey." She said it flatly, without triumph, and Freya understood why: there was no triumph available in a fortune purchased with a father's blood, only the strange grief of receiving a gift you'd have traded anything to return unwrapped. "You know what he asked of you."

"He asked me to see his claim honored. Nothing more."

"And the silver?"

"Is yours." Freya said it simply, because it was simple, because she had decided it in the dark of the inner cavern with water rising around her boots and her brother's hand gripping hers, and she had not un-decided it since. "Every registered ounce. I never wanted it. I want—" She stopped, and started again, more carefully. "I want the survey done fairly. That's all I've ever wanted from any line I've run. So here is what I propose, and you may refuse it."

She took from her satchel the closed-lines map — her father's map, wax-cloth and worn soft at the folds, every abandoned grade and shuttered spur marked in his small exact hand — and laid it on the table between them. Beside it she placed a single sheet of her own drafting, blank except for a title block.

"A contract. You engage me — privately, under your claim's authority, not the company's — to survey the silver seam honestly. Full measurements, full disclosure, filed with the territorial assayer under both our names. I take no percentage of the ore. My fee is my fee, fair and fixed, and when the work is done the maps belong to you and to the record, not to me and not to Rivergate." She tapped the closed-lines map. "And this stays mine. It's not part of any transaction. It's the only thing my father ever finished, and I mean to finish it properly myself."

Marta Wren looked at the map for a long time. Then she looked at Freya, and something passed between them that needed no contract language — the recognition, rare and unsentimental, of two women who had each been handed a dead man's unfinished business and had chosen to carry it rather than cash it.

"My father said you'd swear the oath properly," Marta said. "He said most people swear promises the way they sign receipts. He said you wouldn't."

"He was right about me. It's the only reason I'm alive to be right about anything else."

They wrote the contract together at the chandler's table, Marta drafting clauses in her quick ink-stained hand, Freya correcting the technical language until the survey terms would have satisfied a territorial examiner. When it was done they signed it, and Marta pressed her thumb below her signature the way prospectors sealed claims, and Freya — after a moment's thought — did the same. Two thumbprints in ink. No witnesses but the gulls on the roofline and the ghost of a prospector who had traded his life's last discovery for a promise, and gotten, against every odd the river offered, exactly what he'd asked for.

* * *

Tomas found her that evening on the levee, which surprised her less than it should have. Since the permit board, since Crane had been led away between lawyers with his ledger read aloud against him, her half-brother had been a man walking around inside a demolished house, cataloguing what remained. The company had offered him reinstatement too — she'd heard it secondhand, the way she now heard everything about Tomas, through Odile, through clerks, through the small gossiping currents of Rivergate that ran faster than the river itself. He had declined it. Nobody could say why. Freya thought she could.

He came along the levee boards with his coat collar up against the river wind, and for a while they simply stood side by side watching the water, two people who shared a father and twenty years of silence and four days in a flooding cavern, in roughly ascending order of intimacy.

"The board's offering you the Current," he said at last. "Chief Engineer."

"You've been reading my mail."

"Everyone has. It's the biggest appointment they've made in a decade. They're calling it the Joubert Survey already." He said the words carefully, testing each one. "Your name on it. Their money under it."

"Yes."

"Are you taking it?"

She watched the water a while longer. A barge went downstream, riding low with mission freight, its pilot a boy of maybe nineteen leaning easy on the wheel the way Odile had once leaned, before the flood, before the eleven names, before the company paid her to bury her brother twice.

"No," Freya said.

Tomas exhaled — not surprise, she thought, but confirmation. "I told them you wouldn't."

"You told them?"

"I told Crane, months ago, on the Perpetual. I told him you'd never come back inside their walls, not for anything, and he laughed and said everyone comes back inside eventually, it's just a question of price." Tomas's mouth twisted. "He priced himself wrong. It's the only entry in his ledger he got wrong, and it ruined him."

"It didn't ruin him. His own handwriting ruined him. We just held the book open."

They stood in the wind. Downriver the lamps of the warehouse district were coming on one by one, and somewhere a steam whistle announced a departure, and the Current went on doing what it had always done, which was carry everything toward the sea and keep nothing.

"I signed the contract with Marta Wren today," Freya said. "Private survey of the silver claim. Honest measurements, filed openly, no company involvement. It's a small job, Tomas. One seam, one watershed, a season's work. It won't make either of our names the way the chief engineer's chair would have."

"I know."

"It will make them *ours*, though. That's the difference. Nobody gave it to us and nobody can take it back."

Tomas nodded slowly. Then he turned to face her, and in the lamplight she saw how much the gorge had changed him — the clerk's smoothness cracked away, something rawer and more decided underneath, a man who had jumped into black water rather than accept a house, and had surfaced knowing exactly why.

"I want to come," he said. "On the survey. I'm not asking for partnership or a title. I'll keep the books, file the papers, run the numbers — I'm good at it, God knows it's all I'm good at, but I'm genuinely good at it. And you'll need someone at the assayer's office who isn't afraid of company lawyers, because they'll contest the provisional registration the moment your figures threaten their founder's grave, and they will, Freya, they absolutely will, the concession collapsed but the family didn't." He stopped, breath fogging in the cold. "I betrayed you once. In this city, at that desk, I did worse than betray you — I chose the name over the blood, and it nearly killed us both. I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm asking for work. Give me work instead of forgiveness. I'll earn the one and we can leave the other where it lies."

Freya looked at her brother for a long moment. She thought of the boiler gauge he had sabotaged, and the horror on his face when he understood that Crane had let him do it; of his hand locked around hers in the rising water while she calculated the fissure bearings; of the oilcloth packet against her chest with the journal copied in his precise clerk's hand, every figure rendered true, because he had refused to copy it any other way even with death in the room.

Their father had left her the compass and left Tomas the legitimate name, and both inheritances had turned out to be debts. But debts, she had learned, could be worked off. That was the whole difference between a debt and a curse.

"The pay is poor," she said. "The hours are worse. Odile will pilot for us as far as the Splits and she'll argue with you the entire way, and you'll deserve most of it. And if you ever put a false figure in one of my books — not one digit, Tomas — I'll leave you at the Splits with your share of the gear and no boat."

Something moved through his face that was almost a smile. "That's the fairest offer I've had all year."

"Then it's settled." She pulled the closed-lines map from her satchel and held it up between them, the wax-cloth catching the lamplight, her father's small exact hand marching across every closed line like a man still walking. "We start with the claim, and then — when the season turns — we finish this. Every closed line on this map, reopened and measured and written down true. All of them. It'll take years."

"Good," said Tomas. "I've had enough of fast work."

Below the levee the Current slid past in the dark, and Freya stood with her brother and her map and her father's compass, and felt — not peace, not yet, peace was a thing that had to be surveyed and staked and earned like anything else — but something better than peace: a bearing, and a crew, and work ahead of her long enough to fill a life.

Two days later, Odile Marchand came to Rope Walk with tide tables under her arm and a rumor in her mouth: men asking questions in Saint-Aubin about the limestone shelf, strangers with no company badges and no company manners, and the first honest sounding of the upper channel showing fresh drill marks on the shelf stone.

Someone else, it seemed, had read the dead man's map.

Freya rolled the chart closed and reached for her coat.
