## 22. The Source, Measured

The morning they set the datum stone, the Current ran so clear that Freya could count the pebbles on its bed from the gunwale of Odile's boat.

A year ago she would not have believed such water existed anywhere above Verrin's Crossing. A year ago she had believed very little that she could not grade with a level and a chain — and even those beliefs had been taken from her, one falsified signature at a time, until she'd walked out of the survey corps office at Verrin's Crossing with her father's compass in her pocket and nothing else to her name but a map of closed lines and thirty days of debt. She remembered the weight of that morning. She carried it still, the way an old injury carries weather. But this morning the river was bright, and the debt was paid, and the stone sat on the bank wrapped in sacking like something sleeping.

"Last sounding," Odile said, and there was no ceremony in it, because Odile Marchand did not believe in ceremony. She believed in water. "Then we're done, and I'll say what I've been wanting to say since Rope Walk: nobody pays me enough for this river twice."

"You're being paid exactly enough," Tomas said from the stern, where he sat with the field book open across his knees, his clerk's hand already cramped from eleven months of figures. "You insisted on negotiating the contract yourself."

"And I'll insist again." Odile leaned on the sweep and brought the shallow-draft boat around in a long, easy arc that no company pilot could have matched, because no company pilot alive knew this channel the way she did — not from charts, not from orders, but from the years when the river had erased her name and she had gone on learning it anyway, alone, the way a widow learns a house. "Bring your line in, engineer. We're at the mark."

Freya hauled in the sounding line hand over hand, letting the wet cord run through her palm. She had measured every mile of the Current from mouth to source now — the broad brown reach below Rivergate where the barges went down heavy with ore; the middle river with its grain towns and its ferry landings; the Splits, where the legal channel forked away from the dead pilot's shortcut through the gorge; the gorge itself, with its slate graves and its scratched names; the second channel behind the limestone shelf, which appeared on no chart but hers and was about to appear on one more. Eleven months. Two hundred and six miles. Four thousand soundings, each one honest, each one entered in the field book in ink that could not be erased without leaving a scar on the page.

She had learned, over that year, that honesty was not a virtue so much as a discipline — a thing done daily, tediously, in cold water and bad light, by people who had decided once and for all what kind of line they would draw and then refused, mile after mile, to draw any other.

* * *

They landed at the spring in the last hour of the morning, when the sun stood high enough to drop straight into the cavern mouth and light the water from within.

Freya had seen it a hundred times now and it had never once become ordinary. The source of the Current was not a trickle under a rock, as the old charts implied, nor the grand torrent the company's prospectors had promised their investors. It was a spring-fed system rising through limestone, and where the water came up through the ore-bearing seams it carried silver dust in suspension, so that the pool at the cavern's mouth glowed a pale, impossible brightness — the color of moonlight poured over coins, the color Wren must have seen when he was young and whole and thought he'd found his fortune. The map had been true. That had always been the terrifying part. Every line Aldous Wren had drawn in wax-cloth and desperation had led here, to water worth more than the town downstream, and to bones.

They climbed the path to the grave first. That was the order of things now, and had been since the first survey party came upriver in the spring: the grave before the work, always.

Elias Joubert lay beneath a cairn of river stones on the shelf above the spring, where Freya and Tomas had moved him the previous autumn, out of the dark of the inner cavern and into the light he had surveyed toward for the last miles of his life. The slate marker bore his name in Freya's chisel-work, and beneath it, smaller, the words *He measured truly* — four words she had chosen after discarding forty others, because in the end they were the only epitaph that mattered to any of them. A surveyor's whole life in four words. Her father had spent his measuring truly, and it had cost him the concession, the credit, the family, and finally the river itself; and yet the lines he drew had held. They had held for twenty years in the closed files of a company that thought truth was a thing you could shelve. And when his daughter finally read them, they had held well enough to bring down Silas Crane, collapse the forged concession, and put the silver rights back where the law — the real law, the one written before men started writing convenient ones — said they belonged.

"We're at the mark," Freya said quietly, though her father could not hear her, or perhaps precisely because he could not, and someone ought to say these things aloud anyway. "Two hundred and six miles. Mouth to source. Datum to be set today at the spring, elevation taken off the barometer and checked against the gorge falls. Your bearing held, Papa. All the way up."

Tomas stood beside her with his hat off. He did that every time they came here, and he never said anything, and Freya had stopped expecting him to. That was the shape their reconciliation had settled into over the year — not forgiveness, which neither of them pretended to, but attendance. He showed up. He kept the books true. When the permit board had tried, last winter, to slip a salvage rider into the new concession language, it was Tomas who caught it, line by line, in his small exact clerk's hand, and killed it in committee. He had chosen exile from the company house and the legitimate name and everything their father's bargain had bought, and he had never once asked to be thanked for it, which Freya had come to understand was its own kind of apology, and the only kind he was capable of making.

"The stone," he said at last, and lifted the sacking.

* * *

The datum stone had come upriver with them from Rivergate — a block of granite cut by the same mason who had dressed the cornerstone of the assayer's office, because Freya wanted the two stones to be kin. On its face, once it was bedded, would be cut the mark that every future survey of the Current would be measured from: elevation, latitude, longitude, and the date. Not the company's date. Not the founder's forged concession date. Today's date, and beneath it the words *First honest survey*, which the mason had objected to as unprofessional and which Freya had insisted on with a flatness that ended the discussion.

They set it together, the three of them, because some things should not be delegated.

Odile and Tomas levered it upright while Freya guided the base onto the leveled footing she and Tomas had prepared on their last trip, packed gravel and cut timber, engineered to stand a hundred years of flood. The stone went down into its bed with a sound like a door closing — a deep, final knock that Freya felt through her boots and up through her chest, and that the river seemed to answer, the spring-water brightening for a moment as the sun found the seam of ore.

"Tape," she said, and Tomas stretched the steel tape from the stone's face to the water's edge while she read the level. "Elevation confirmed. Two thousand one hundred and forty feet above Rivergate gauge. Enter it."

He entered it. She watched him do it — watched the nib touch the page, watch the figure go down clean, no rounding, no adjustment, no little falsification smoothed over for anyone's convenience. Eleven months ago that hand had filed false manifests to buy her three days of lead time, and lied to Silas Crane's face, and sabotaged a boiler gauge on the *Perpetual* nearly killing everyone aboard, and learned in the smoke and scalding steam that Crane had expected all of it and let it happen, the way a man lets a dog prove it will bite. Eleven months ago that hand belonged to a man becoming a weapon. Now it wrote elevations. Freya thought there might be no better use of a redeemed life than that.

"Done," Tomas said, and blotted the page.

Odile had drifted back to the boat and stood at the gunwale looking upstream, past the spring, to where the second channel wound away into the limestone. "You know what I keep thinking," she said. "Eleven men. My brother among them. All this time the company told Rivergate the flood took them — bad water, bad luck, pilot error, my error, that's what the inquiry said, my error." She turned. Her face, seamed and brown and hard as the river stones, did something Freya had seen only twice before: it softened. "And the whole time, the reason they were running barges through that flood at all was a forged paper. A lie at the bottom of everything, all the way down to the source. You pull that one lie out, and look what stands up." She nodded at the stone. "I used to think the river owed me. It doesn't. It was owed."

"It's being paid," Freya said.

"Not all of it. Never all of it." But Odile touched the gunwale the way another woman might cross herself, and said no more.

* * *

They ate a cold lunch on the bank — bread, hard cheese, the bitter wild greens Odile gathered without thinking, food of the river country — and afterward Freya sent them both ahead to begin the return soundings, and stayed behind alone at the spring, as she had known since Rope Walk that she would.

There was one thing left to do, and it was not on any survey schedule.

She took out her father's compass.

It was a plain instrument, brass-cased, the needle jeweled and true, the kind issued to corps surveyors in the year Elias Joubert had joined the service — except that it had never been quite true, not in the ordinary way. At the datum, at certain bearings, at the limestone shelf, it had pointed not north but along a hidden line, a bearing buried in the closed-line maps like a message sewn into a coat lining. *A line only a surveyor would recognize.* She had spent a season of her life learning to read it, and it had led her upriver through pursuit and flood and grief to this bank, to this stone, to the truth of how the Current's silver had been stolen and from whom.

But that was finished now. The bearing had done its work. The journal was read into the record; the ledger was in the lawyers' hands; the concession was ash; the mason had cut the stone. There was no further secret in the instrument. Which meant — and she had understood this for months, and had waited anyway, because some understandings need a season to ripen — that the compass could finally do the only thing a compass was ever meant to do.

She opened the case and held it level in both hands.

The needle swung, wavered, steadied.

North. True north, unhidden, unbent, pointing at nothing but the pole and the honest geometry of the earth. No secret bearing. No dead man's message. Just north, the way it would point for anyone, anywhere, forever — the way it would have pointed all along if the world had not been full of men who needed it to point somewhere else.

Freya laughed. It came out of her unexpectedly, a short rough sound that startled a heron off the far bank, and she wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist and laughed again, because after everything — the dismissal, the debt, the dying man on the road, the brother at the desk, the gorge of names, the flood in the dark — after all of it, the great revelation, the inheritance understood at last, was simply this: that when a thing was made right, it pointed the way it was always supposed to point.

She knelt at the datum stone and set the compass on its flat crown, open, the needle floating free, the case turned so that any surveyor who came after — any honest one, any child with a chain and a level and a stubborn heart — would find it waiting, and know what it meant, and know that it had been left there deliberately, by someone who no longer needed it.

"Keep it true," she said to whoever would come. Then, to her father, silently, in the private language of people who measure: *Your line holds.*

* * *

She found the boat waiting a half-mile downstream, Odile holding her against the current in the shade of a leaning sycamore, Tomas sketching the profile of the gorge falls into the margin of the field book the way he did when his hands needed drawing instead of writing.

"Well?" Odile said.

"Well." Freya stepped aboard and took her place amidships, where the balance was best, where she had ridden for eleven months. "Datum's set. Compass is on the stone. The Current is measured, mouth to source, and every figure in the book will bear reading by anyone, anytime, forever."

Tomas looked up from his sketch. For a moment neither of them said anything, and the river said it for them, sliding past the hull with that low continuous voice it had, the voice that had carried their father's secrets and Wren's bargain and Crane's crimes and Odile's drowned brother, and that carried none of it anymore, having delivered all of it at last to the sea.

"Then we're done," Tomas said.

"No," Freya said, and smiled, and nodded upstream, where the water came down bright out of the limestone, silver-flecked and cold and running, as it had run before any of them and would run after, toward the mouth, toward the maps, toward whatever came next. "Now we go home, and write it all down fairly — and then, brother mine, we start the next survey."

Odile swung the boat downstream, and the Current, measured at last, carried them.
