## 1. The Thief Who Wasn't

The thief had a walk like a man who'd never once been followed, which told me two things before the sun cleared the ridge: he was young, and he was going to die stupid unless somebody taught him better.

I wasn't planning to be that somebody. Teaching costs time, and time in the flats is paid for in water.

He'd taken a coil of copper wire and a tin of lamp oil from the trading post at Cinder Wells, and old Marta had put three coppers on his head—not because she cared about the wire, but because nobody steals from Marta twice and lives comfortable with themselves. The wire was worth maybe eight coppers. The arithmetic of bounty hunting has never favored anybody but the bounty poster, but I've never been in it for the math.

I'd been following him since before dawn, when his tracks were still crisp enough to read like handwriting. Long stride, rolling heel—someone who'd grown up on hard ground, not sand. He stopped twice to rest, which was a mistake, because resting men leave impressions. He drank from his skin at the second stop, and I could tell by the angle how much he had left. Not enough. It never is, out here. The flats don't care what you carry; they care what you know.

My name, if you're wondering—and you shouldn't wonder, wondering is how people get found—is not the one on the warrant. The warrant says *Ivo Amberdale, ranger, third district, wanted for theft of official records and the murder of a clerk.* The name I answer to changes with the season. This month I was answering to "Ash," a drover's widow out of nowhere in particular, which was convenient because widows get asked fewer questions than single women and none at all if they keep their eyes down and their gear patched.

That's the whole discipline of it, really. You never stop moving long enough for anyone to look at your face twice. Stopping means campfires, campfires mean company, company means questions, questions mean names, and names mean the noose they tied for me eleven years ago finally gets its chance to do some work. So I move. I've crossed these flats so many times I know where the ground forgives a boot and where it tattles. I can read a horizon the way literate folk read a page, and I have read this particular page—the gray shimmer east of Cinder Wells, the broken mesa standing up like a rotten tooth—for so long that it's less a landscape than a habit.

People call that discipline. Rangers used to say it about me back when rangers said nice things: *Amberdale never stops, Amberdale never sleeps.* They meant it as praise. It isn't discipline. Discipline implies you want something better and are holding yourself to it. What I have is simpler. I have a rule, and the rule is *don't be known*, and rules are easy when you've got nothing left that wants breaking them.

Mostly easy.

* * *

By midmorning the thief had made his second mistake, which was cutting toward the salt pans instead of swinging north along the hardpan. Maybe he thought the pans would hide his tracks. They do, if you cross them fast and light. He crossed them slow and heavy, and the crust printed him like wax seals every thirty feet. I could have walked beside him humming a song and he'd have been none the wiser.

I let him get out onto the white expanse proper before I closed the distance, because there's an art to an arrest and the first rule of it is: never take a man where he's got somewhere to run *to*. Out here, surrounded by nothing, a man's only options are surrender or sunstroke, and sunstroke does the paperwork for you.

"Morning," I said.

He came apart beautifully. Spun, dropped the oil tin, snatched at a knife he didn't have—patted his belt for it, actually patted, like the knife might have wandered off on its own—then straightened up and tried to look like a man who hadn't just done any of that. Young. Nineteen, twenty. Sand-colored hair going stiff with sweat, lips already cracked, eyes with the particular shine of someone who hasn't eaten properly in days. His shirt had been good once. Now it was a shirt-shaped memory.

"You startled me," he said.

"That's generally the idea." I nodded at the oil tin rocking on the crust. "You'll want to pick that up. Oil's dearer than you think."

"I didn't—" He looked at the wire coiled over his shoulder, the tin at his feet, and visibly abandoned whatever lie he'd been building. That was another mark against him as a criminal: honesty arriving too late to be useful. "It's for my mother's lamp. She can't see after dark anymore, and the wick frame broke, and the smith wanted four coppers for the wire alone."

Four coppers. I looked at him a long moment. Four coppers and a tin of oil, and a man runs into the flats in dry season with half a waterskin and no knife. There were easier crimes. There were smarter ones. Which meant he hadn't chosen this crime at all—he'd simply arrived at it, the way water arrives at a low place, because everything else had failed first.

"What's your name?" I asked, and then immediately regretted asking, because it was a question, and questions are debts, and I try not to owe anything out here.

"Doss," he said. Then, hearing how that sounded, added, "Just Doss."

"Just Doss." I crouched, slow, and picked up the oil tin myself, weighed it, handed it back. His hands shook taking it. Not fear-shaking. Hunger-shaking. I know the difference the way I know the difference between rain and dust on the wind. "You know what happens to thieves at Cinder Wells?"

"They hang them," he said. "Or the magistrate sells the sentence to the mines, and the mines hang you slower."

There it was. The magistrate. I felt that word go through me like a splinter working toward the bone, and I kept my face doing nothing, because my face has had eleven years of practice doing nothing. Magistrate Veylan Hargrave, who signed my warrant with the same hand that signed away the water grants, whose signature I have spent a decade wanting to hold up to a light. But that's a story for a braver chapter.

"Well," I said. "Good news. I'm not from Cinder Wells."

His eyes did the quick arithmetic—my gear, my boots, the ranger's knot on my bedroll that he was clearly too green to recognize—and landed somewhere between hope and suspicion. "You're a bounty hunter."

"I'm a woman walking in the same direction as you."

"You tracked me across the flats."

"The flats track everybody. I just walked politely behind."

Something flickered in his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if he'd eaten recently enough to afford one. And I caught myself almost liking him, which was exactly the kind of thing I couldn't afford, and I hardened up inside the way you pull a tarp tight before weather.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about catching people: the catching is easy. It's the moment after, the moment where you decide what they are, that's hard. A bounty is a story somebody else wrote about a person, and my job—my old job—was to make the person fit the story. Rope doesn't care if the story's true. Rope just closes.

I had rope. I always carry rope. It's the honest tool of a dishonest trade.

"Marta will pay three coppers for bringing you back," I said. "Less the cost of the wire, she'll probably net one."

"One copper," Doss repeated. He looked out at the flats, all that white light, all that nothing, and I watched him do the sum of his own life and come up short. "One copper to hang for a lamp."

"Yeah," I said. "The arithmetic's bad all around."

* * *

We stood there while the sun climbed and the salt glare ate our shadows, and I want to tell you I was thinking strategically—about routes, about water, about the smart play. Some of me was. The part of me that's kept breathing for eleven years sat at the front of my skull doing sums: take him back, collect the copper, be gone by dark, nobody asks the widow Ash her business. Clean. Efficient. The discipline holding, the way it always holds.

But there was another voice, quieter, that I mostly drown under mileage. It said: *you know what it is to be a story somebody else wrote.* It said: *you know what it is to run for something you didn't do, and to have everyone with a badge and a ledger agree on what you are without ever once asking.* It said: *he stole a lamp for his mother. You ran for your life. Where exactly is the line you're drawing, and who drew it?*

I hate that voice. It's the last piece of the person I used to be, the ranger who believed the law was a river and we were all just keeping its banks clear. She drowned eleven years ago, or I tell myself she did. But she surfaces. Usually at the worst moments. Usually when a hungry boy stands in front of me holding a tin of lamp oil like it's a lantern for the whole dark world.

"Where's your mother?" I asked.

"East settlement. Saltrow." He hesitated. "She doesn't know I came out here. She thinks I'm working the caravan yards at Cinder Wells."

"And instead you're going to die of thirst forty miles from anywhere with a coil of wire over your shoulder."

"It sounds worse when you say it."

"It is worse. I'm just saying it accurately." I pulled my own waterskin off my hip and held it out. He stared at it like it might be a trick. "Drink. Small sips. If you gulp it you'll lose half of it to sweat and I'll be annoyed, and you don't want me annoyed, I'm told I get sarcastic."

He drank the way I told him, small sips, careful, and watching him I realized I'd already decided. I don't know when. Somewhere between *one copper to hang for a lamp* and the way his hands shook, the decision had made itself while the disciplined part of me was busy pretending to deliberate. That's how it always goes, I think. We decide with the oldest parts of ourselves and then send the clever parts out afterward to write up a report.

"I'm not taking you to Marta," I said.

Doss lowered the skin. "You're letting me go?"

"I'm not *taking* you to Marta. Whether you go is your business." I shouldered my pack. "But if you head east with that wire and that oil, you'll be dead by tomorrow noon, and then Marta gets her copper anyway and your mother gets a lamp she can't light. So here's the shape of it: there's a well, dry on top but wet at forty feet, six miles south. I know where the bucket rope is stashed. You help me draw water, you fill your skin, and then you walk the hardpan north to the caravan road and flag a westbound freight. Drivers will take a strong back as far as Saltrow for work, no questions. That's the best offer on the table, and the table's very small."

"Why?" he said. Just that. Why. And it was such a ranger's question, such a clean honest question, that it hurt more than an accusation would have.

Because I was you once, I didn't say. Because a man with a seal and a warrant wrote a story about me and everyone believed it, and I have been running from the ending ever since. Because every time I drag someone back in ropes I hear the ropes on my own wrists. Because out here, in all this white nothing, you're the first person in months who's looked at me like a person instead of a purse.

"Because one copper's a stupid price for a man," I said. "And I don't work for stupid prices anymore."

He studied me with those too-bright eyes, and I could see him deciding whether I was a saint or a swindler, and I wished him luck, because I've been trying to work out which I am for a decade and the evidence keeps coming back inconclusive.

* * *

We walked south together, because the well was south, and because—as I reminded myself several times, firmly—it was on my way. Everything is on my way. That's the trick of a moving life: you never stay anywhere long enough to admit you chose it.

Doss talked. Of course he talked. Twenty years old and starved and he talked the whole six miles, about his mother's failing eyes and the price of lamp oil and the caravan yards and a dog he'd had as a boy, and I gave him nothing back, one-word answers, grunts, the full armored silence. But I listened. That's the weakness in my armor, if you're cataloguing: I listen. Eleven years of talking only to myself, out loud to the horses, muttering to the stars, and now here was a human voice aimed at me, asking nothing, just spilling over, and I drank it the way he'd drunk my water. Small sips. Careful. Losing half of it anyway.

At the well I showed him the cache under the flat stone, and his face when the bucket came up dripping was worth more than any bounty I ever collected, which is a sentence I will deny having thought if you quote me.

He filled his skin. I filled mine. We stood in the well's shadow while the afternoon leaned long, and I pointed him north to the hardpan and the road, and he thanked me in a stumbling way, and then he did the thing that undid me a little, the thing I'm still chewing on tonight as I write this by a fire I shouldn't have lit:

He said, "You never told me your name."

"No," I agreed. "I didn't."

And he nodded, and took it for mystery, for romance, for the ways of the flats—but it wasn't any of those. It was the oldest rule I have. *Don't be known.* Don't stop. Don't answer questions. Because questions mean names, and my name is a warrant, and my name is a grave with a clerk in it that I didn't kill, and my name is a river grant signed away by a magistrate while my family watched their orchards turn to sticks.

Ivo Amberdale. Say it three times and the desert grows ears.

I watched Doss shrink into the heat-shimmer going north, a smudge, then a suggestion, then nothing, and I turned south because south was my direction, south was always my direction, and that's when I saw it.

Smoke. Thin, wrong-colored, rising off the flats maybe ten miles out—too far from any route a sane traveler would take, too steady to be an accident. Somebody was camped where nobody camps, burning something that burned green-gray, and in eleven years of reading horizons I have learned that smoke in the wrong place is never smoke.

It's a message. Or a trap. Or, knowing my luck lately, both.

I stood there a long moment with my hand shading my eyes, and the disciplined voice said *walk away, Ash, walk away, you owe the dead nothing,* and the other voice—the drowned ranger, the one who won't stay sunk—said nothing at all.

She just started walking.
