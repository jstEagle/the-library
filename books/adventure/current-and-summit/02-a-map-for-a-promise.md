## 2. A Map for a Promise

The thief led me north for two days before the trail turned strange.

I want to be clear about that, because everything that came after gets blamed on the map, and the map deserves only part of it. The strangeness started with Doss's tracks. A man running scared leaves a particular signature—long strides, no pauses, ground eaten in straight panicked lines. Doss had run like that out of Perdition Flats, all elbows and fear. But somewhere past the third night his stride shortened. He stopped wandering. He started *following* something.

By the morning of the fourth day I understood why. There were older tracks under his, half-eaten by wind but still legible to anyone who'd spent a decade reading sand the way other people read letters. Boot prints with a cracked left heel. A walking stick dragged on the right side, its tip gouging little commas into the hardpan. And every few hundred yards, a cairn—three stones stacked with the fussy precision of a man who expected someone specific to come looking.

Someone was leading my thief. And my thief was following.

Which meant that whatever waited at the end of this trail wasn't Doss anymore. He was just the thread. Somebody else had been pulling it across the flats for days, maybe weeks, and they'd pulled it straight into country where even the buzzards got lost.

I should have turned back. Let me say that plainly, since I'll spend the rest of this account pretending I didn't know. The smart move—the move the woman I was pretending to be would have made—was to let Doss go, circle south, and lose myself in the basin towns until spring. Stopping means questions. Questions mean my name. Ten years of discipline said turn around.

But there were cairns. And cairns are a kind of question too, and I have never once in my life managed to leave one unanswered.

* * *

I found him at noon on the fifth day, at a well that had died.

It was one of the old survey wells, sunk back when the territories still believed in their own future—stone-lipped, windlass rotted, the rope long since crumbled to red fuzz. Someone had built a lean-to of canvas and rib bones beside it, the ribs of some desert ox, lashed together with wire. Under the lean-to, in the strip of shade no wider than a coffin, lay a man.

He was past the point where a stranger's arrival startles him. That's how far gone he was. His eyes found me and tracked me the way eyes track weather—interested, resigned, already calculating what it would cost him. His lips had split into a black crust. His hands, resting on his chest, were swollen like twice-baked bread, and clutched between them was a leather roll tied with waxed cord.

"Water," he said. It came out as mostly air. "Please. Even dirty."

I had water. Of course I had water; I'm not a monster, whatever the warrants say. But I stood at the edge of that scrap of shade for a moment longer than kindness strictly required, because I've learned the hard way that dying men ask for more than water, and I wanted to see what shape this one's asking would take.

Then I knelt and gave him my flask, propping his head up so he could drink in small sips. He drank like a man who knew exactly how fast a body can betray you—slow, measured, hating every pause. When I took the flask back he exhaled, long and shuddering, and laughed. It was a terrible sound. Dry wood snapping.

"Well," he said. "Either my luck's turned or the desert finally sent me something interesting."

"Halvard Brine," I said.

His eyebrows climbed as far as the dehydration allowed. "You know me?"

"I know your name from a supply manifest in Perdition Flats. Prospector's license, expedition permit, six names on the roster." I tipped water onto a cloth and pressed it to his forehead. "Nobody's seen any of the seven of you in eleven weeks."

"Six," he said. "Six names on the roster now."

The wind moved through the dead well and made a low sound, like the hole itself breathing. I decided not to ask which name had come off. Some questions you can feel the weight of before you lift them.

"You're the one who's been leaving cairns," I said.

"I'm the one who's been leaving cairns." He tried to sit up and failed, then settled for turning his head to look at me properly. Up close his face was all geography—canyons and ridges, sun-scars and squint-lines, a nose broken sometime in a decade I couldn't guess at. "And you're the one following them. Took you long enough. I figured anyone reading my trail would've caught up two days ago."

"I stopped to arrest somebody."

"Doss." He said the name without surprise. "Skinny fellow, quick hands, cries easy?"

"You've met him."

"He caught up to me four nights back. Half-dead himself, half-mad with thirst. I gave him water and told him if he wanted to live he should follow my cairns and find whoever came looking for me." Halvard Brine smiled with cracked lips. "Figured the desert owed me one rescue, and I intended to collect it through intermediaries."

So the thief hadn't been fleeing anything after all. He'd been herded. Somewhere behind us, Doss was presumably still stumbling along the cairn line toward this exact spot, and I felt an odd, unwelcome flicker of respect for the dying man's arithmetic.

"Why leave a trail at all?" I asked. "Why not stay near the trade roads? Someone would have found you."

"Someone did." His eyes went to my hip, to the ranger's iron I still carried out of habit and stubbornness both. "Found a ranger, no less. Though you walk like a woman trying not to be found, which is a contradiction I'd enjoy discussing if I had the moisture to spare."

I kept my face still. Ten years of practice. "You don't know who I am."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But I know what you are. And right now that's the only thing I need." He lifted the leather roll off his chest with both hands, and I saw how much the small motion cost him—the tremor in the swollen fingers, the breath held against pain. "This is for you. If you'll take it."

"I don't take things from dying men. Bad for the conscience."

"Good," he said. "Keep the conscience. You'll need it." He worked at the cord with his teeth, which cost him another minute and a grunt of frustration, and unrolled the leather flat on the sand between us.

It was a map. Not a survey office map—nothing with clean lines and confident borders. This was drawn in ink over graphite, corrected and re-corrected, margins crowded with notes in a cramped, furious hand. Elevations scratched out and argued with. A canyon system rendered in cross-section, layer under layer, like the rings of a cut tree. And at the center, circled twice and underlined three times, two words:

**SUNKEN SUMMIT.**

"I know the Summit," I said carefully. "Everyone knows it. Dead rock. No water within forty miles. That's why nobody claims it."

"That's why nobody *looks* at it." His finger, trembling, came down on the canyon cross-section. "There's a river under it. A real one. Running dark through the limestone, sixty feet down, moving maybe two thousand gallons a minute. Enough to water a hundred settlements if you can reach it. Enough to end the drought in the eastern basins outright." He looked up at me, and there was fever in his eyes but something else underneath it, bedrock-steady. "I found it. Eleven weeks ago I stood on its bank and heard it running."

The wind breathed through the well again. I looked at the map, and I will admit—I wanted it. Not for the water, not at first. I wanted it because it was beautiful, because it was the work of a patient obsessive mind, because it was proof that the desert still kept secrets worth keeping. It had been a long time since anything had offered me a reason to keep moving that wasn't just fear.

"Why show me?" I asked. "Why not take it to the land office? File a claim? Get rich?"

Something crossed his face then—something old and bitter, older than the thirst. "Because I filed once already. Eleven years ago. And the land office wrote my discovery off as 'hydrologically nonviable' and granted the water rights to parties unnamed." His laugh again, that dry-wood snap. "Parties unnamed. Have you ever noticed, ranger, how much trouble hides inside that phrase?"

I had noticed. I had noticed nothing else for ten years.

"So I came back," he went on, "with my own money and five fools who believed in me, to prove it on paper they couldn't suppress. Survey documents. Sealed samples. Testimony." His voice frayed. "We were coming out with proof when the heat took us apart. One by one. Like it always does." He closed his eyes. "I sent them ahead. Told them to shelter at the second camp and wait. They waited. I kept moving because someone had to carry the map, and here I am, and here you are, and the mathematics are very simple."

"The mathematics," I said, "are that you're dying, and I'm a stranger, and strangers don't swear oaths for maps."

"They do," Halvard Brine said, "when the oath is the map."

* * *

Here is what he asked, and I set it down exactly as he said it, because I've gone over the words enough times since that they've worn grooves in me:

"Bring them home. My people. The ones waiting at the camps along the route. Don't file reports about them, don't mark their graves and ride on—*bring them home*, to their kin, whole, named, witnessed. Carry my map and my proofs out of the Summit and put them in front of people who can't be bought. And when you've done that—" his breath caught, and he rode through it, "—then the water is yours. The claim, the credit, all of it. I sign it over. I just need someone with iron on their hip and stubbornness in their spine to finish what I started."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I die here with my map, and my daughter never buries her father, and the river stays buried with us, and the men who wrote it off get to go on being unnamed." He smiled faintly. "No pressure."

I looked at the map. I looked at the well, breathing its dead breath. I thought about Doss stumbling along the cairns behind us, and about six names on a roster, and about a phrase I hadn't let myself think in years: *parties unnamed.*

Because here's the thing I haven't said yet. I knew that handwriting on the margin notes—not the hand itself, but the territory it described. The Sunken Summit sat inside the old Meridian Grant boundaries. The same grant lines that had featured, prominently, in a certain trial ten years ago. The same grant lines whose revocation had dried up a dozen settlements and ruined a certain family whose name I had spent a decade making sure no one spoke.

The past doesn't chase you in this country. It just digs wells and waits.

"I'm not a good person to swear oaths," I told him. "Ask around. Ask anyone."

"I did ask around," he said. "In Perdition Flats. The stableman says you paid your tab in full before vanishing, which no fugitive does. The ferrywoman says you pulled two children out of the flood channel last monsoon and left before anyone could thank you, which no coward does." His eyes held mine, mild and merciless. "I asked around quite thoroughly, ranger. I chose you."

For a moment I couldn't speak. Being seen, after ten years of careful invisibility, is a violence all its own. I looked away first, out at the heat-shimmer flattening the horizon, and understood with terrible clarity that I was going to say yes—that I'd probably said yes the moment I knelt and gave him my flask—and that some part of me had known it since the first cairn.

"If I take this," I said slowly, "I do it my way. No land offices. No magistrates. Nobody learns what's in that roll until I decide they learn it. And I decide who comes home and in what order."

"Agreed."

"And if I fail—if the desert takes me too—you get no promises about the map. It goes where it goes."

"Agreed," he said again, and then, softer: "It will go where it goes regardless. Maps are like rivers that way. I'm just asking you to be the channel."

I put my hand on the leather roll. His hand came over mine, swollen fingers and all, and gripped with a strength that surprised us both.

"Swear it," he whispered.

"I swear," I said, and the words came out steady, which frightened me more than any warrant ever had. "Your people come home. Your proofs see daylight. On my life."

Halvard Brine exhaled—a long, releasing breath, the kind a man gives when he sets down something he's carried too far. He signed the transfer on the map's margin with a pencil stub, his letters dissolving halfway through his own name, and then he lay back against the ox ribs and looked up at the strip of white sky above the lean-to.

"My daughter," he said. "Sera. She'll be in Corder's Landing, if she's anywhere. She won't want to see you. She hasn't wanted to see me in nine years, and she had better reasons than you do." His voice was going quiet now, evening-out, the tide going down. "Tell her it wasn't the prospecting. Tell her I'd have come home even if I'd found nothing. Tell her—" He stopped. Started again. "Tell her the cairns were for her. Every one. In case she ever came looking."

"I'll tell her."

"Good." His eyes drifted shut. "Good. Then we understand each—"

The sentence never landed. The desert took the rest of it the way it takes everything—without malice, without ceremony, mid-word, as though the word had never mattered.

I sat with him while the light went long and copper. I dug the grave myself, because I'd sworn an oath about bringing people home and I figured I ought to start practicing. I marked it with three stones, fussy and precise, the way he would have wanted.

Then I sat back on my heels and looked at the map in my hands—at the canyon cross-sections, the furious marginal notes, the two circled words—and felt the full weight of what I'd done settle onto my shoulders like a yoke.

An inheritance. That's what it was. The unwanted kind, the kind that arrives with debts attached and no instructions except *finish it.* Ten years I'd kept my ledger empty on purpose: no ties, no promises, no one whose grief could reach me. And in one afternoon a dying stranger had loaded me down with six bodies, a buried river, a estranged daughter, and a truth with teeth.

Somewhere to the south, a thin figure crested a dune—Doss, upright by sheer spite, weaving along the cairn line toward the well. Toward me. Toward whatever came next.

I rolled up the map, tied the cord, and tucked it inside my coat, against my heart, where inconvenient things belong.

"Cairns were for her," I said to the grave. "Every one. I'll make sure she hears it."

The wind moved through the dead well one last time, and I could have sworn it sounded less like breathing now, and more like release.

Then I stood, checked my water, and turned north—to the Summit, to the dead, and to the first honest work I'd done in ten years.

Behind me, Doss called out something hoarse and desperate. I didn't slow down.

He'd catch up. Cairns had brought him this far.
