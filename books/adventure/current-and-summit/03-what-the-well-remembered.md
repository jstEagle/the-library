## 3. What the Well Remembered

I spent the night in the shadow of Halvard Brine's dry well with his map spread across my knees and a candle stub burning down faster than my patience.

Maps are honest things, mostly. They lie about distances and flatter about water, but they don't lie about where they're pointing. This one was drawn on oiled canvas, the kind that survives being folded into a coat for years, and it had been folded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. Somebody had loved this map. Somebody had carried it against his chest while he died.

I'd taken it off him at dawn—or rather, he'd pressed it into my hands at dawn, along with an oath I still hadn't decided whether to regret. Bring them home, he'd said. My people. What's left of them at the Sunken Summit. Swear it, ranger, and the map is yours.

I'd sworn. Against every instinct I'd polished over seven years of running, I'd sworn. There's a particular foolishness in making promises to dying men; they collect them like debts and you can't dispute the terms later. But his hand had been so light on mine, and his eyes had been doing that thing dying eyes do—going somewhere past you, checking on something you couldn't see—and what was I going to say? No, old man, I've got a thief to drag back to a magistrate who'll hang him, and after that I've got nowhere to be, which is different from nothing to do?

So now I had a map, a promise, and a thief snoring under a saltbush twenty feet away with his wrists tied in front of him because I wasn't cruel enough to tie them behind and not stupid enough to leave them free.

The map showed the eastern reaches. That much I'd known from the moment Brine unrolled it—the hatching style, the surveyor's marks, the little cursive annotations in a hand I recognized before I understood why I recognized it. Then I found the boundary line.

It ran along the northern edge of the map, a heavy ink stroke with a name beside it: the Amberdale Grant.

My candle guttered. Or maybe I just stopped breathing for a while and blamed the candle.

* * *

Here's what most people never learn about deserts: they aren't empty. They're full of paper.

Every well, every grazing strip, every miserable acre of scrub that could keep three goats alive through a bad summer—that acre has a document behind it, a grant or a lease or a revocation, filed in some magistrate's office by men who never sweated a day on the land itself. The desert doesn't belong to the people who know it. It belongs to whoever holds the paper. And seven years ago, I learned exactly how far men will go to hold paper.

My father held the Amberdale Grant once. Four thousand acres of the best shallow aquifer country in the eastern flats, enough water under it to green a settlement the size of any of the river towns. He didn't find it—he inherited the finding—but he kept it, and keeping it meant surveys, and surveys meant records, and records meant that when the magistrate Aldous Veyne decided he wanted that aquifer's water rights redirected to the new speculative holdings downstream, there was a tidy legal path to taking them.

There was also my father's signature on documents he never signed. There were witnesses who swore they'd seen him sell water he'd never touched. There was a trial that lasted four days, and there was me, nineteen years old, watching from the gallery while men in good coats explained to a packed courtroom that the Amberdales had defrauded the territorial water authority, that the grant was forfeit, that the family would pay restitution they could never afford.

They took the land. They took the name—Ivo Amberdale became a cautionary tale told in settlement schools, the daughter of the water-thief. And when I stood up in that courtroom and said the signatures were forged, said I'd watched my father sign his name a thousand times and these weren't his strokes, one of Veyne's clerks produced a witness who placed me at the forgery myself. Accessory. Fugitive-in-waiting, though nobody used those words until I actually ran.

Seven years. Seven years of a borrowed surname and a ranger's badge from a district too understaffed to check references, moving settlement to settlement, never staying longer than a season, never letting anyone ask twice about where I came from. Stopping means questions. Questions mean my name. My name means Veyne's people, and Veyne's people mean chains, and chains mean I die in a cell while the men who ruined my family drink clean water bought with my father's ruin.

And now here was his map—Halvard Brine's map—with the Sunken Summit sitting square in the middle of territory that bordered the old Amberdale Grant like a scar borders a wound.

Not inside it. I checked three times, tilting the canvas toward the candle, tracing the boundary with a fingertip gone white at the nail. The Summit lay beyond the northern line, in the high broken country the grants had never reached. But the surveyor's annotations—those cursive notes in the familiar hand—referenced the Amberdale wells as comparative readings. *Depth to water table, cf. Amberdale east shaft.* Whoever drew this map had used my family's data. Had walked our land, measured our water, and carried those numbers north into country where he believed a buried river ran.

Which meant the man who surveyed the Summit had been part of the original assessment of the Amberdale Grant. Which meant he'd been in Veyne's orbit. Which meant—

I sat back and let the cold come, the specific cold that isn't temperature. The past doesn't chase you, out here. People think running is about outrunning things, but the desert teaches you better. The past is patient. It sits down in front of you like a snake at dusk, motionless, already across your path, and waits for you to walk into it.

Every road I'd ever taken away from that courtroom had, apparently, been a circle.

* * *

Doss woke an hour before dawn, the way guilty men do, all at once and quietly, checking first for the rope and then for me.

"You slept," he observed.

"Some."

"That's a lie."

"It is," I agreed, and he almost smiled, then remembered he was supposed to be terrified of me and rearranged his face accordingly. He wasn't good at it. Terror sits badly on hungry men; their faces keep reverting to arithmetic—calculating odds, exits, the likelihood that the tall ranger would share her water if asked nicely.

He'd asked nicely twice already. I'd shared both times, which probably undermined my authority, but I've never been able to watch a man go dry in front of me. It's a character flaw. It's also, I was beginning to suspect, why I was in this mess.

"You've been looking at that map all night," he said, shuffling closer on his haunches. His wrists were raw where the rope had worked during the night. "You found what you were looking for?"

"I found something."

"Bad something or good something?"

"Doss."

"Ivo." He said my name deliberately, tasting it, and I felt the small familiar lurch of hearing it spoken aloud—it had been months since anyone but me had used it. "That's your real one, then. You gave it to the dying man, so it must be."

"The dead get the truth. It's tradition."

"And me?" He lifted his bound hands. "What am I, mid-tradition?"

I rolled the map and tied it with its cord. Dawn was coming up gray behind the eastern ridges, the color of old dishwater, and somewhere out on the flats a bird was making the sound birds make when they've realized the water hole is a lie. "You're a thief I caught stealing medicine from the Redford clinic," I said. "Who I was legally obligated to return to the magistrate at Copper Wells. Who will hang, because Copper Wells hasn't hanged anything but thieves in six years and Magistrate Veyne believes in setting examples."

His face did the arithmetic again, fast this time, and came up with a number he didn't like.

"Veyne," he repeated. "That's the one from the trials. The Amberdale woman's trial."

The cold again, sliding up my spine. "You've heard of it."

"Everyone's heard of it. My mother used to say—" He stopped. Recalculated. Men like Doss are quick to sense when a topic has teeth. "Point is," he said carefully, "you're not taking me to Copper Wells."

"I'm legally obligated—"

"You're a fugitive."

The word dropped between us like a stone into a well, and we both listened for the splash.

"I don't know what you—"

"I've been riding with you for two days," Doss said. "You check the horizon every quarter hour like the horizon owes you money. You sleep sitting up. You gave your real name to a stranger because he was dying and wouldn't remember it. And last night, when you thought I was asleep, you read that map and then you sat very still for a long time, the way people sit when they've seen a ghost wearing a familiar coat." He swallowed. "I steal things, Ranger. I'm good at noticing what people hide. It's the whole trade."

I should have denied it. A smart woman would have denied it, laughed it off, tightened the rope, and started walking him east toward the gallows and her own destruction. Instead I looked at him—this wiry, unlucky boy with salt-cracked lips and a clinic's worth of fever medicine bundled in his shirt, stolen for a sister I hadn't asked about because I hadn't wanted to know—and I made the second mistake in as many days.

"Say what you want," I said.

"Take me with you." The words came out of him fast, like water through a crack. "Wherever the map goes. The Summit, the buried river, whatever's out there—I heard the old man talk, before you found him. Water enough for a hundred settlements. Nobody hangs the man who helped find that. Nobody even remembers him. Take me north, and when we come back, I walk into Copper Wells a hero instead of an example."

"And if we don't come back?"

"Then I'm no worse off than hanging."

I turned away and looked north, where the ridges were starting to burn pink at their edges. Somewhere out there was a canyon full of dead men waiting on a promise, a buried river that could rewrite the fate of a hundred settlements, and whatever remained of the truth about my father's signature. Somewhere out there, too, was the strong possibility that Veyne's people had never stopped watching the eastern country, because men who steal aquifers don't stop guarding them.

Going alone was the sane choice. Going alone meant speed, silence, no witnesses to my face or my name. Going alone was seven more years of the same discipline that had kept me breathing since I was nineteen.

But Halvard Brine had died holding my hand and calling me *ranger* like it was a title worth something, and his map had led me straight back to the one place in the world I'd sworn never to look at again, and I was tired—I was so tired—of mistaking motion for freedom.

"He'll send trackers," I said. "Veyne. When you don't arrive. He'll send Corvane."

Doss frowned. "Who's Corvane?"

"The man who arrested me." I untied his wrists. He rubbed them and stared at me like I'd handed him the sun. "Don't make me regret this. If you slow us down, I'll leave you for the vultures with a note pinned to you explaining the delay."

"What'll the note say?"

"*Ate the wrong lizard.*"

He grinned—actually grinned, gap-toothed and absurd—and for one unguarded moment he looked about fourteen years old. Then he shouldered his bundle of stolen medicine and fell into step beside me, and we started north together, a fugitive and a thief, toward the past I'd spent seven years outrunning.

It hadn't been chasing me after all, I realized. It had simply dug a well in the middle of my road and waited for me to get thirsty.

Behind us, Halvard Brine's dry well caught the sunrise and threw it back, silver and brief, like something remembering water.
