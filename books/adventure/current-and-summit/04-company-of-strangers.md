## 4. Company of Strangers

I'd like to tell you that crossing the flats with Ivo Amberdale was a bonding experience, that we shared stories around a small fire and came to understand one another as travelers do. What actually happened was three days of her walking and me trying to keep up, punctuated by conversations that went like this:

"Water."

I'd drink.

"Walk."

I'd walk.

Once, I made the mistake of asking her where we were going, and she looked at me for so long I checked my own shadow to see if I'd grown a second head. "Last settlement before the deep dunes," she said finally. "Redwater. We need supplies. And we need someone who knows the Summit country."

"And that's in Redwater?"

"That's in Redwater."

That was the whole conversation. I've had longer exchanges with cacti, and the cacti were friendlier.

But here's what I learned about Ivo Amberdale in those three days of silence, because a man walking behind someone for three days learns things whether he wants to or not. She never once looked at her feet. Not when the ground got treacherous, not when we picked through a field of broken shale that would have turned an ankle on a saint. She watched the horizon the whole time, sweeping it left to right like a woman reading a page she expected to change. And she never walked in a straight line when a crooked one would do. We came over ridges on the blind side. We crossed a wash at a bend instead of a ford. I don't think she was avoiding anything in particular. I think she was avoiding the *habit* of being seen, the way some men avoid debt.

You don't spend three days with a woman like that without starting to wonder what she's running from. And you don't spend three days owing her your neck without starting to worry that the answer's worse than hanging.

* * *

Redwater wasn't much to look at, which is the nicest thing anyone's ever said about it. A cluster of mudbrick buildings huddled around a spring that had seen better centuries, a trading post, a dry-goods store with a porch that sagged like a tired mule, and a saloon that called itself the Last Drop, which I figured was either optimism or a warning. The whole town had the look of a place that had turned its back on the desert and was pretending not to hear it breathing down its neck.

Ivo went straight to the trading post and started buying supplies with coins she counted out like they were teeth she'd pulled herself. Rope. Salt pork. A second waterskin. A canvas tarp. I stood by the door doing my best impression of a man who belonged to somebody, and mostly failing, because the storekeeper kept looking at me the way you look at a dog that's wandered in from the desert—curious about where it came from, mildly concerned about what it might be carrying.

"You her man?" the storekeeper asked, weighing out salt pork.

"Her prisoner," I said, because it seemed easier than explaining.

He nodded like that was a perfectly normal arrangement out here, and maybe it was.

It was on the porch of the Last Drop that we found her. Sera Brine.

I knew it was her before anyone said a name, and I'll tell you how: she had her father's hands. Halvard Brine, dying at that dry well, had wrapped those big cracked hands around a map like it was the last warm thing in the world, and the woman on the porch had the same hands—broad-knuckled, scarred across the back, a surveyor's hands or a smith's, hands that had worked at something hard for a long time. She was sitting with her boots up on the rail, mending a strap, and she had the particular stillness of a person who has stopped waiting for something and hasn't yet admitted it.

Ivo walked up to her without preamble, which was, I was learning, her only setting. "Sera Brine?"

The strap-mending didn't stop. "Who's asking?"

"My name's Ivo Amberdale. I'm a—"

"I know what you are." Now the hands stopped. Sera Brine looked up, and I got my first real look at her face, and it was like looking at a cliff face that had decided to be disappointed in you. "The coat gives it away. Ranger gray. You people come out here when somebody's dead or somebody's owed, and either way it costs somebody who can't pay."

"I'm not here on corps business."

"Rangers are always on corps business. They just don't always know whose."

I winced. Ivo didn't. I was beginning to understand that very little got through that coat, and what did got filed away rather than felt.

"Halvard Brine is dead," Ivo said.

The porch went quiet. Somewhere down the street a door banged, and the sound seemed very far away, like it belonged to a different town. Sera Brine put the strap down. Folded the leather over once, twice, precise as a surgeon. When she looked up again her face had done something complicated—gone smooth and hard, like water freezing, and I understood I was watching grief put on its armor, buckle by buckle, because the alternative was to come apart in front of strangers on a sagging porch in a town that had never given her anything.

"Dead how?" she said.

"Thirst. At a dry well, forty miles southwest. He'd been walking a long time."

"Alone?"

"He was alone when I found him."

Something flickered behind the frozen surface. "Then he died alone too. That's the part that matters. That's the part you came to tell me, dressed up in whatever else you're selling." She stood, and she was taller than I'd guessed, and she looked at Ivo the way you look at weather you can't do anything about. "Did the corps send you?"

"No one sent me."

"Rangers don't move without orders. I grew up watching them. My father used to say the corps was a compass that only pointed at money." Her jaw tightened. "So either you're lying, or you're off the map, and I don't much like either option."

I should probably say here that I liked her immediately. There's a kind of person who's been hurt so thoroughly and so specifically that they've turned the wound into a worldview, and you always know where you stand with them, which is usually in front of a closed door. I've been that door myself. It was almost restful, meeting someone blunter than my own conscience.

Ivo set a folded square of oilcloth on the rail between them. "He was carrying a map. To a place called the Sunken Summit. He asked me for something before he died, and I gave my word on it."

Sera didn't touch the oilcloth. "My father chased that summit for eleven years. Eleven years of leaving. My mother waited through nine of them and buried her hope in the tenth, and I stopped counting after that." Her voice stayed level in a way that took visible work. "He'd come home with sand in everything we owned and that look on his face, the one men get when they've seen water in a mirage and can't admit it was a mirage. And every time, the corps laughed at his surveys. Every time, some clerk in some office stamped *insufficient evidence* on a river my father could have *drunk from*, if anyone had bothered to follow him." She picked the strap back up, then put it down again, like her hands needed something to betray. "So no. I don't want the map. I don't want the summit. I want what you're not offering."

"Which is?"

"His body." The word came out flat and hard, a nail driven flush. "You found him. You know where. I want my father brought home, and I want it done proper, and I don't want a ranger's promise, because I've seen what those are worth." She looked from Ivo to me and back again, and I did my best to look like honest labor, which has never once worked. "Who's this?"

"Doss," I said. "Thief."

"He your prisoner?"

"Something like that."

"He steal from you?"

"From a granary."

"Then you're either very honest or very stupid, and I'll find out which." She turned back to Ivo, and here's the thing—her voice never rose, never cracked, but there was something underneath it, something with a pulse. "I'll take you to the Summit country. I know those canyons better than any map your corps ever stamped. I grew up in them while my father was off finding them. But understand me, Ranger. I'm not going for your water. I'm not going for your promise. I'm going for my father, and when I've got him, I'm done. Whatever you're really after out there, you can drown in it."

Ivo was quiet a moment. I was learning to read her silences the way sailors read weather, and this one had a storm in it. "There are things out there you'll want to see," she said at last. "Things he found."

"I doubt it."

"He marked them. In his own hand."

That landed. I watched it land—watched Sera Brine's armor take the hit and hold, mostly, except for her hands, which closed on the strap hard enough to whiten the knuckles. Eleven years of a father choosing a mirage over a daughter, and here was a stranger saying: *he wrote to you the whole time, in the only language he had.*

"When do we leave?" she said.

* * *

We spent that night in Redwater, because even Ivo admitted that going into the deep dunes without sleep was just a slower way of dying. She took the room above the trading post. I took the stable loft, on the theory that a thief sleeping under a roof he'd paid for was a thief asking for trouble, and Sera took a corner of the saloon and a bottle she barely touched, which told me more about her than the whole conversation on the porch.

I found her there near midnight, sitting with the bottle and the strap she was mending, except now I could see the strap was a bridle rein, cut short, the kind of thing you'd keep for a horse that wasn't coming back. I sat down across from her because I've never once had the sense to leave grief alone.

"Your pa," I said. "Was he a good man?"

She looked at me for a long time. "You ask that of everyone you meet, thief?"

"Only the ones who look like they already know the answer."

Something almost like a smile moved across her face and died of exposure. "He was a good man who was bad at being one. He loved a hole in the ground more than he loved us, and he'd have told you the hole was us, that everything he did was for us, and he'd have half believed it." She turned the bottle but didn't drink. "The corps laughed him out of every office from here to the coast. You know what that does to a man? It doesn't make him quit. It makes him *certain*. Certainty was all he had left to bring home, and you can't eat certainty, and you can't sleep next to it." She finally looked at me straight. "Your ranger. She's the same breed."

"She's not my ranger."

"Everyone's somebody's. Question is whose." She set the bottle down. "You watch her, thief. Watch how she walks the edges of things. Watch how she never gives you her back. I've known men like that. They're either running from something or hunting something, and either way, when it catches up, everyone standing close gets spattered."

I could have told her then what I'd started to suspect—that Ivo Amberdale wasn't running toward the Summit so much as *from* something behind us, something with a badge on it. But I'd given my word about a different promise, and anyway, I was learning that out here, a man's history was the one thing he carried that nobody wanted to buy.

"I'll watch her," I said.

"Do that." She picked up the rein, the cut one, the one for the horse that wasn't coming back, and turned it over in her father's hands. "And thief? When we find him—when we find my father—you make sure she keeps her word. Rangers give promises like they give receipts. Easy to write. Hard to collect."

* * *

We left at first light, three of us and two mules, and I'll tell you what struck me about it: how little any of us said. Ivo led. Sera walked beside her, not behind, and the two of them rode the edge of each other's silence like horses testing a fence. And I brought up the rear with the mules, which suited me fine, because the rear is where a man can watch both of them and the horizon all at once, and I'd decided the horizon was worth watching.

Because about a mile out of Redwater, Ivo had done it again. The horizon sweep, left to right, slow, like a woman reading a page. And this time—and maybe it was the light, and maybe it was Sera's warning still rattling in my skull—I could have sworn she lingered on the ridge to the northeast. The one we'd crossed three days ago. The one that stood between us and everywhere Ivo Amberdale had supposedly come from.

She looked at it the way you look at a door you've locked and can still hear rattling.

"Trouble?" I asked, coming up beside her.

She didn't look at me. "No."

"Because it looked like—"

"It's not trouble, Doss." And then, quieter, almost to herself, in a voice I wasn't supposed to hear: "It's just a direction."

We walked on. Behind us, the ridge stood empty against the morning.

For now.
