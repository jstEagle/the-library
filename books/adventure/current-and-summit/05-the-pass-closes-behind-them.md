## 5. The Pass Closes Behind Them

My father used to say that the desert doesn't kill you quickly, it just makes you wait for it. He said this the way other men talk about weather—pleasantly, over breakfast, with crumbs in his beard. I thought it was one of his jokes. Halvard Brine had a hundred of them, and most of them were true, which is the worst kind.

I was thinking about that joke three days out from Last Well, when the mountain decided to have the last word.

* * *

The pass was called Kestrel's Throat, and it was exactly as charming as it sounds—a slot of shadow between two walls of rust-colored stone, narrow enough that a loaded cart would scrape sparks off both sides. It was the way home. Not the easy way home, not anymore, but the only way home before the dry season turned every other route into a slow, polite form of suicide.

We reached its mouth at midmorning, and I remember thinking the light looked wrong. Too gold. Too clean. The kind of light that makes everything look like a memory of itself.

Ivo Amberdale stopped so abruptly that Doss walked into her back, which he did roughly four times an hour anyway. She stood there with her head tilted, listening to something I couldn't hear.

"What," I said. Not a question. By then I'd learned that questions were currency in this company, and Ivo hoarded them like a dragon.

"Quiet," she said.

"It's a canyon. Canyons are quiet."

"Not this quiet."

I opened my mouth to tell her exactly what I thought of rangers who spoke in fortune-cookie fragments—and then I heard it too. A low grinding, somewhere above us, like the mountain clearing its throat. Or chewing.

"Back," Ivo said. "Now. Don't run, walk fast. Running shakes the scree."

We walked fast. Doss walked faster than fast, which is called fleeing, and I don't blame him, because behind us the sound grew teeth. A crack like green wood splitting. Then a long, rolling roar, deep enough that I felt it in my back teeth, in my ribs, in whatever part of a person holds their childhood fears.

Dust came first—a brown wall of it, boiling out of the Throat like the mountain exhaling. Then the rocks came, or rather they'd already come, and what we saw afterward was the result: where the pass had been, there was now a slope of broken stone forty feet high, tumbled blocks the size of houses, a wound of raw rock still bleeding dust into the air.

Kestrel's Throat was closed.

I stood there breathing grit and doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. The dry season was eleven days out. Maybe twelve if we were lucky, and nobody in this party had been lucky lately. The southern routes around the mountains added nine days minimum. The eastern flats added more, plus water we didn't have, plus heat that could cook a man's judgment right out of his skull.

"Well," Doss said, in the small voice of a man watching his life expectancy recalculate itself. "That's. Hm."

"That's that," Ivo said.

She wasn't looking at the rockslide. She was looking at the sky, at the angle of the sun, at some private ledger she kept behind her eyes. And I realized—with a cold feeling that had nothing to do with morning—that she wasn't surprised.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a ridge, close enough to the rockslide that I could see it against the stars: a black shoulder where there should have been a throat. I built the fire because nobody else would, and because building a fire is something you do with your hands when your hands need to be doing anything but shaking.

My father's map—the one he'd pressed into a stranger's hands while dying at a dry well, the inheritance I hadn't asked for—was folded inside my coat. I'd read it a hundred times since Last Well. I knew its lines better than I knew my father's face, which is a sentence I'll regret writing even if no one ever reads it.

The Sunken Summit sat on the far side of these mountains. So did the water my father had died chasing. So did, apparently, whatever business Ivo Amberdale had with this country—business she mentioned the way you mention weather. Vaguely. Ominously. Never twice the same way.

"So," I said, when the fire had settled and Doss had gone to check the mule for the third time, which was his way of avoiding conversation. "You knew."

Ivo sat across the flames, cleaning a knife she never seemed to use. "Knew what?"

"The pass. You heard it coming. You weren't surprised."

"I heard something. Rock groans. Mountains settle." She tested the blade's edge against her thumb. "Doesn't mean I knew."

"You're lying."

"I'm being precise. There's a difference, though I understand it's a subtle one."

I threw a stick into the fire, harder than necessary. Sparks went up like angry thoughts. "Eleven days, ranger. That's what's left before the dry season seals the southern routes. Nine extra days around the mountains. Do the sums. Then tell me again how precise you're being."

She did the sums. I watched her do them—that flicker behind her eyes, quick and cold, a clerk tallying lives. When she looked up, her face was as smooth as a river stone.

"We'll make it," she said.

"How?"

"There's a route."

"What route?"

"One that works."

"That's not an answer, that's a horoscope." I leaned forward. "Three people and a mule are going to die out here on your horoscope, ranger. So either you tell me what the plan is, or I take my father's map and find my own way, and you can explain to his ghost why you lost his daughter in the same country that killed him."

For a moment—one long, firelit moment—I thought she might actually answer. Something moved behind her face, some door opening onto a lit room. Then it closed.

"The route goes through the Summit," she said. "That's all you need tonight."

"All I *need*—" I laughed, and it came out ugly. "My father went through the Summit. Look how that ended. His whole expedition, gone. Bones in a canyon. And you want to walk us down the same throat because—what? Because you've got a feeling? Because you've got some plan you can't share with the two people whose lives depend on it?"

"Yes," Ivo said.

Just that. Yes. No apology, no explanation, not even the decency to dress it up. She sheathed her knife and looked at me across the fire with those flat gray eyes, and I understood suddenly why men had chased this woman across half the desert for years and never caught her. It wasn't speed. It was that there was nothing to grab hold of.

"You know what my father said about you?" I said. "Before he died. He gave you his map. His life's work. To a stranger with a warrant out on her. And I keep asking myself what he saw in you, and I keep coming up empty."

Something crossed her face then. Quick, gone before I could name it—not guilt exactly, more like the shadow guilt casts when it passes overhead.

"He saw someone who keeps promises," she said quietly. "Even the ones they shouldn't make."

"And do you? Keep them?"

"I'm still here, aren't I."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." And she rolled over and went to sleep, or performed going to sleep, which with her may be the same discipline.

I sat by the fire a long time after that, feeding it sticks I didn't need to feed it, hating her with the particular, exhausted hatred you can only feel for someone you've tied your life to. My father had done the same thing, I realized. Tied himself to her. Chose her, out of everyone the desert offered him, to carry his last wish.

And she wouldn't even tell us the route.

* * *

Doss found me at dawn, sitting on the ridge with my knees pulled up, watching the sun come up over the closed pass. He handed me a cup of coffee that tasted like boiled bark and regret. In our company, that passed for kindness.

"She won't tell us," I said. "Whatever the plan is. She'll feed it to us one crumb at a time, whenever it suits her, and we're supposed to just follow along and trust that the crumbs lead somewhere."

Doss lowered himself onto the rock beside me with the care of a man whose body had learned not to trust him. He was quiet for a while. Out here he was quiet a lot; I think silence was the one thing he'd stolen honestly.

"My sister," he said, finally. "The reason I took the purse in Corvane's Crossing. She's got the lung-rot. Medicine costs more than I'd make in ten honest years. So I made one dishonest one." He shrugged. "Point is, I know what it's like to bet everything on somebody and not get a vote."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. It's supposed to make you feel less alone in it." He sipped his coffee. "Also, for what it's worth—I've been watching her. The ranger. She checks the horizon every hour, same direction, every time. Like clockwork. Like she's expecting something."

"Expecting what?"

"If I had to guess? Riders." He said it lightly, but his hands weren't light around the cup. "Corvane's trackers were sniffing the flats behind us back at Last Well. If they picked up our trail..." He let it hang.

Wonderful. So the plan, such as it was, might include a posse. I looked down at the camp, at Ivo already moving through her morning ritual—checking the mule's feet, counting the water skins, doing all the small competent things that made it so much harder to hate her than it should have been.

"She knows something about the Summit," I said. "About my father's expedition. More than she's saying. You see it? Every time I mention him, she flinches. Not outside—in. Like something landing on a bruise."

"Maybe she knew him."

"Maybe she *is* him," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because grief makes you say stupid things, and Doss was looking at me with too much sympathy to bear.

Below, Ivo finished loading the mule and looked up at us on the ridge. Even at that distance, even in the flat morning light, there was something unnerving about how directly she looked—as if she'd been waiting for us to finish talking about her.

"Southern route," she called up. "Two hours to the fork. Eat while you walk."

And that was it. That was the whole briefing. Two hours to the fork, eat while you walk, and somewhere ahead of us lay a buried canyon full of dead men and secrets, and behind us lay a wall of rock and possibly a posse, and between the two, walking point with her back straight as a pikestaff, was a woman who had decided—without asking any of us—that we were committed.

I drained the bark-coffee and stood.

"You know the worst part?" I said to Doss as we climbed down. "She's probably right. Whatever the plan is. She's probably right, and she knows she's right, and that's why she doesn't bother explaining. Because explaining is for people who might be wrong."

Doss considered this. "My grandmother was like that," he said. "Died stubborn and vindicated. Whole town hated her at the funeral."

"Lovely."

"Oh, it was. Best turnout in years."

We broke camp. We walked south, toward the fork, toward the Summit, toward whatever my father had found and whatever Ivo Amberdale refused to say aloud. Behind us, Kestrel's Throat sat sealed under its shroud of broken stone, and the dry season ticked down like something patient counting our steps.

It was sometime around noon, crossing a rise of hardpan, that I noticed the dust.

Not our dust. It hung off to the west, a thin gray smudge against the heat shimmer, moving the way dust moves when something organized is under it. Moving the way dust moves under riders.

Ivo saw it the same moment I did. She didn't stop, didn't speak, didn't change pace by so much as a breath. But her hand drifted to the map case at her belt, and her jaw set in a way I was beginning to recognize, and I understood that whatever she'd been checking the horizon for—

—it had found us.
