## 6. Salt Flats, Salt Wounds

There are two kinds of people in the desert: those who talk to fill the silence and those who let the silence do the talking. I am firmly the first kind. It's a character flaw, my mother said, right before she sold me to a caravan cook for three goats and a promise. Talking keeps you alive out here, though. A man who goes quiet starts believing his own thoughts, and my thoughts have never once had my best interests at heart.

So on the second day of crossing the salt flats, with the crust crackling under our boots like old bread and the sun pressing down on us like a debt collector, I talked.

"You know what salt does to leather?" I asked Sera Brine, who was walking ahead of me with her father's map case slung across her back like it weighed forty pounds instead of four. "Eats it. My uncle had a pair of boots he left near the flats one season. Came back for them and they'd gone soft as cheese. Walked home barefoot. Lost two toes."

"Nobody asked," Sera said.

"That's the thing about conversation," I said. "It works best when nobody asks."

Ivo Amberdale walked ten paces ahead of both of us and said nothing at all, which was her way. She'd been saying nothing since we'd left the last settlement, saying nothing through the first night's cold camp, saying nothing now as the flats opened up before us like a page nobody had bothered to write on. She checked our heading against the sun every hour without breaking stride. She drank on a schedule, not when thirsty. She slept in shifts she assigned us without asking whether we agreed.

I'd known hard people before. I'd been caught by hard people, chased by them, robbed by them once — which still stung, professionally speaking. But Ivo was something else. Hardness usually has a shape. You can see where it comes from: a scar, a grudge, a dead brother. Ivo's hardness had no visible shape. It was just there, all the way through her, like salt in this ground.

Which is why, around midday, when the heat made everything shimmer and honesty got cheap as water wasn't, I decided to tell her.

* * *

Here's the thing you should understand about me: I'm not a good thief. I'm a *convenient* one. There's a difference. A good thief plans, cases the place, has exits. A convenient thief is broke, hungry, standing next to an unattended strongbox, and tells himself that fate is offering him a hand. Fate offered me a hand in Copperwash, and I took it, and that's how a ranger spent three days tracking me across the flats with the patience of a woman watching paint dry.

What I stole was a ledger page. One page, torn from a shipping manifest in the factor's office at Copperwash. It listed grain shipments — quantities, destinations, dates. Boring as sand. Except the quantities didn't match what actually arrived in the settlement stores, and I knew it because my sister worked the storehouse tallies, and she'd been crying about the numbers for a month before I did anything stupid about it.

The settlements were being shorted. Systematically. Grain marked as delivered, then rerouted somewhere else — somewhere with deeper pockets. And when my sister raised it, she was told her arithmetic was wrong, and then she was told it again louder, and then she was told that a woman who couldn't count shouldn't hold a tally position, and she lost the work. So I took the page that proved the numbers, thinking I'd find someone who cared.

Instead I found a ranger on my heels and a flats crossing ahead of me and, eventually, a dying prospector at a dry well and a map I had no business carrying.

"So that's it," I finished, squinting at Ivo's back. "That's my whole crime. One page of somebody else's arithmetic. You tracked a man across half the wastes for a piece of paper."

She kept walking. The salt crunched. Somewhere behind us, Sera snorted — either at my story or at my delivery, and honestly both were fair.

"I'm telling you," I pressed, "because if we're going to die out here together, you should know you're dying with a reformer. Practically honest. I've been reconsidering my entire—"

"Save your breath," Ivo said.

Not cruelly. That was the strange part. Not cruel, not warm. Just flat, like a road sign. *Save your breath.* As if breath were a resource she'd already budgeted for me, and confession hadn't made the allocation.

"It's a long crossing," I tried.

"Then walk it quietly."

Sera fell back a step to walk beside me. Up close I could see the resemblance to Halvard Brine that she probably hated — the same stubborn line of jaw, the same deep-set eyes that looked at horizons instead of faces. She lowered her voice, which meant she wanted me to hear it.

"She's not going to thank you for that."

"I wasn't fishing for thanks."

"Everyone fishes for something." She adjusted the map case strap. "You fished for absolution just now. Cheap water, out here."

"That's harsh."

"That's the flats." She nodded at the white expanse running to every edge of the world. "Nothing out here pretends to be anything else. You should try it."

Then she lengthened her stride and went back to her place behind Ivo, and I was left alone with the crunching and the heat and the distinct feeling of having been weighed and found lighter than expected.

* * *

We camped that night in a shallow depression where the salt had buckled into ridges high enough to break the wind. Ivo built no fire. She explained why in six words — "Light carries. Smoke carries farther." — and then sat with her back against a ridge, eating strips of dried meat with the mechanical patience of a woman feeding a machine.

I ate beside her because I have no dignity. Sera sat across the hollow, wrapped in her blanket, watching us both over the top of it like a woman watching weather she didn't trust.

"Ask her," Sera said suddenly.

"Ask her what?"

"Anything. She'll answer in four words or fewer. It's a game I've been playing with myself. Keeps me sane." She ticked fingers. "*Where's the next water?* Four words. *How far?* Two. *Why won't you tell us the plan?* Three words, and the third one is 'later.'"

Ivo chewed. Swallowed. Said nothing, which technically broke the game by refusing to play.

"Why did you take the oath?" I asked her directly. "Halvard's oath. You didn't know him. You didn't know us. A dying stranger hands you a map and a promise and you just — took it. Nobody swears oaths to strangers. Only fools and saints, and you don't strike me as either."

The silence stretched long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer at all. Then, quietly, in a voice with no performance in it:

"He was dying alone. I've been alone a long time. It seemed rude."

Sera laughed — one short, startled bark, quickly smothered, like she'd surprised herself. Ivo didn't smile. But something moved behind her eyes, some small tectonic shift, and I understood I'd been given more than four words, and that the gift had cost her something.

Then she turned her head east, toward the black horizon, and whatever had shifted shifted back.

She did that all evening. Every few minutes, her gaze would slide off to the east and rest there, patient and total, the way a hare listens. Not glancing. *Watching.* Like the dark out there owed her money. Like something might come up out of it at any moment, and she intended to see it first.

I mentioned it to Sera later, when Ivo had taken first watch and the two of us lay side by side under a sky so crowded with stars it looked like spilled salt.

"She keeps looking east," I whispered.

"I've seen it."

"You think she's expecting someone?"

"I think," Sera said slowly, "that a woman doesn't learn to watch a horizon like that unless she's been followed before. And I think a woman who's been followed before doesn't take two strangers across open ground without a reason she hasn't told us." She pulled the blanket higher. "And I think she saved your life at the well, Doss, and mine at the settlement, and she's never once asked either of us for anything except walking pace. So whatever's chasing her, I'd rather be next to her than behind her when it arrives."

"That's grimly practical."

"My father used to say the desert rewards the practical and buries everyone else." Her voice went rough on *father*, and she stopped talking, and I had the grace — rare for me — to let her stop.

* * *

The third day, the flats tried to kill us.

Nothing dramatic. No storm, no beasts. Just the sun climbing into its full authority and the salt throwing its glare up into our eyes until the world became a white scream. By noon my lips had cracked and split, and Sera had gone quiet in the way that worried me more than complaint would have, and even Ivo's schedule-drinking had shortened the intervals.

We walked. That was the whole of existence: we walked. I recited grain prices in my head to stay awake. I composed my own eulogy. I decided that if I survived, I would never again complain about any weather anywhere, a vow I fully intended to break within the week.

It was late afternoon, light going long and gold across the crust, when I saw Ivo stop.

She didn't shout. She raised one fist — a ranger signal, apparently universal — and stood absolutely still, head tilted, reading the ground ahead of us the way other men read letters. We caught up to her and I saw what she'd seen: a patch of salt maybe twenty feet across where the crust had gone thin and gray, sagging slightly in the middle like bad plaster.

"Brine pocket," she said. "Crust over wet salt. Step wide."

"How wide?"

"Wide enough that you ask questions from back there."

We skirted it. Sera went first, testing each step, and I followed in her prints like a child following a parent across ice. Behind us the gray patch sat in the golden light, innocent as a puddle, and I thought about how much of this country was exactly like that — solid right up until it swallowed you.

It was after we'd cleared it, when the tension loosened and my mouth started running again the way it always does, that I said the thing I'd been circling for two days.

"You could have taken the reward."

Ivo glanced back. First time in three days she'd granted me eye contact longer than a heartbeat.

"In Copperwash," I said. "On me. There's a bounty — modest, but real. You tracked me three days. You could've dragged me back, collected, and been drinking on it by sundown. Instead you—" I gestured vaguely at her, at the map case on Sera's back, at the whole absurd expedition. "Instead you recruited me. Why?"

She considered me for a long moment. Out here, with the flats burning white-gold around her and the wind pulling at her scarf, she looked less like a person than like something the desert had grown on purpose.

"A man who steals proof," she said finally, "instead of coin, is worth more free than hanged."

Then she turned and walked on, and that was it. That was the whole speech. Fourteen words, and I stood there in the middle of the salt flats feeling like I'd been handed a medal and slapped in the same motion.

Sera came back for me, because I'd stopped walking.

"Move," she said.

"She counted my words."

"She counts everything. Come on. Light's going."

But as I fell into step, I caught it again — Ivo's face turning east, toward the dark coming on, toward the low blue line of hills we'd reach tomorrow if the country allowed it. Watching. Waiting. That hare-stillness in her shoulders.

And this time Sera saw it too, and this time she didn't look away, and when she spoke her voice had dropped into something flat and certain.

"There's something out there," she said. "Isn't there. Something she's been counting miles ahead of us the whole way."

Ahead of us, Ivo Amberdale said nothing at all, and kept walking, and watched the east like a woman listening for hoofbeats only she could hear.

* * *

We made the edge of the flats at dusk, where the salt gave way to gravel and the gravel to the first stubborn scrub, and Ivo called an early camp with a tightness in her voice I hadn't heard before. She set watches — herself first, again — and positioned us in the lee of a rock shelf with our backs to stone and our faces to the open ground.

"To the east," she said. Not a question. An instruction.

"What's east?" Sera asked.

Ivo fed a strip of meat into the small spirit flame she permitted herself, heating it soft, and for a moment the firelight found her eyes and I saw what lived in them. Not fear. Something older and more tired than fear. Recognition.

"Riders," she said. "If my arithmetic holds, they crossed onto the flats today. Behind us." She chewed, swallowed, and looked out at the gathering dark with the expression of a woman greeting an old acquaintance she'd hoped was dead. "By tomorrow night, they'll have our trail."

I opened my mouth to ask the obvious question — *whose riders?* — but Sera's hand closed on my arm, and her eyes never left Ivo's face, and I understood that we were about to find out anyway.

Because far out on the darkening plain, small as sparks, two fires had just lit themselves.

And they weren't ours.
