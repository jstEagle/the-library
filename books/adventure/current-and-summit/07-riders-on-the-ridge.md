## 7. Riders on the Ridge

There is a particular quality to silence in the desert that most people mistake for emptiness. It isn't empty. It's listening. And on the third morning out of Sera Brine's father's last known camp, the silence changed its mind about us.

I heard it before I saw anything: the faint, rhythmic complaint of leather and tack carried on a wind that had no business carrying anything but heat. Hooves. More than two. Fewer than ten. Moving at a walk, which was worse than a gallop, because galloping men are chasing something they can see, and walking men are hunting something they can't.

I put my hand flat on the rock and felt for vibration, an old ranger trick, and the stone told me what the wind already suspected. Riders on the ridge above the wash where we'd made camp. Four, maybe five.

"Doss." I didn't whisper. Whispering carries farther than you'd think across bare stone; it has a way of finding ears. "Wake up. Slowly."

He woke up the way he did everything—badly, loudly, and with commentary. "Is it breakfast? Tell me it's breakfast. If it's another lesson about condensation traps I'm going to lie back down and die here as a matter of principle."

"Riders."

The word worked better than any alarm bell ever invented. He sat up so fast his bedroll tried to come with him. "Rangers?"

"Maybe. Maybe salt traders. Maybe ghosts." I was already moving along the lip of the wash, keeping low, reading the ridge line the way you'd read a letter from someone who owed you money. "Either way, we're not going to stand around and be introduced."

Sera was awake. She was always awake before me, though she pretended otherwise, emerging from her blankets each morning with the practiced yawn of someone who wanted credit for effort. Now there was no pretense at all. She crouched beside her pack with her hand on her knife and her eyes on mine, waiting—not for comfort, because she'd made it plain early on that she considered comfort a form of lying, but for information.

"How many?" she asked.

"Four or five. Ridge north of us. Walking pace."

"That's a patrol pattern," she said. "Traders don't walk. Traders are either running rich or crawling broke. Nobody walks."

I looked at her a little differently after that. Halvard Brine had taught his daughter more than he'd ever taught her forgiveness, apparently. "Agreed. Which means we have maybe twenty minutes before they crest high enough to look down into this wash, and when they do, they'll see three sets of tracks leading in and none leading out, and they'll start asking questions I've spent seven years making sure nobody gets to ask."

Doss had gone pale in the way he did when the situation stopped being theoretical. "Questions like what? Like 'who are you'? Because I've got answers for that. I've got *several* answers for that. I've been a tanner's apprentice, a caravan guard, and briefly, tragically, a mime."

"You were never a mime."

"I was between jobs. It was a dark season." He swallowed. "What kind of questions?"

"The kind with my name at the end of them," I said, and watched it land on both of them. Sera knew some of it by now—the shape of it, if not the details. Doss knew nothing except that I paid him in food and never slept facing the same direction twice.

I'll give them this much: neither of them wasted time asking. The desert teaches priorities quickly if you let it.

* * *

Here is what I will tell you about being hunted, since I've had considerable practice: the first instinct is always wrong. The first instinct is *run*. Run is honest, run is clean, run feels like virtue. But run leaves tracks, and tracks leave stories, and stories leave witnesses. Seven years ago, running had been exactly right—I'd been young, alone, and fast, and I'd traded everything I owned for distance. But I wasn't alone anymore. Doss couldn't run a mile without complaining himself into a hernia, and Sera wouldn't run at all while her father's map rode in my coat, and besides—

Besides, I was tired. That was the truth under all the strategy. I was tired of being a rumor of a woman, tired of erasing myself one horizon at a time. So when the choice came, I found I didn't want to run. I wanted to *lie*. There's a difference. Running says *you can't catch me.* Lying says *you caught something, but it isn't me.*

I climbed the eastern wall of the wash while the others struck camp, and I looked at our position the way Corvane would look at it. That was the unpleasant part. I hadn't let myself think his name in years, and now I was building a house inside his head and furnishing it with his own habits.

Corvane tracked the way water cut stone: patiently, along the lines of least resistance. He'd look for the obvious exit first—the western gap, the easy slope toward the flats—and when he found it empty he'd assume flight and push hard, and hard-pushed men make mistakes. Unless. Unless he found something that told him there was nothing left to chase.

Which meant I needed to give him a body.

Not a real one. A story of one. Fire does that beautifully. Burned supplies, burned bedding, a camp consumed past forensics—every ranger learns to read fire scenes, and every ranger knows that ash keeps its secrets. Add the right props and even a good tracker will grieve instead of investigate, because grief is easier and trackers are human before they are anything else.

I came down off the wall and started giving orders, and I'll admit I enjoyed it more than was decent.

"Doss. Everything flammable that we can spare goes in the center of camp. Bedrolls, the spare canvas, the oilcloth, half the lamp oil. Not the food—we need the food—but make it look like the food burned too. Scatter some grain in the ash."

"What about my spare shirt?"

"Especially your spare shirt. It's hideous. Let it die with honor."

"It's a *practical* shirt—"

"Doss."

He went. Sera didn't move. She stood with her arms crossed, watching me with those flat gray eyes that were her father's eyes wearing a younger face, and I could feel the question assembling itself behind them.

"Burning our supplies," she said. "That's a decision people make when they're desperate. Or stupid. Or—" and then she got there, because she was quick, quicker than she liked to admit, "—or when they want someone to think they died."

"Yes."

"And the someone is rangers."

"Yes."

"And one of them is the man who arrested you." She'd pried that much out of me during the long nights on the flats, in the trade of small confidences that happens between people sharing a bedroll's worth of trust. She'd never used it against me until now. "You're going to stand in the ashes of our own camp and watch him mourn us."

"If I do it right, yes."

"And how does it feel? Planning your own funeral?"

I took the lamp oil from her hands and uncorked it. "Like a rehearsal," I said. "I've been dead seven years already. This just makes it official."

* * *

We built the pyre in the deepest hollow of the wash, where smoke would rise slow and cling to the walls, drifting out over the ridge in thin gray threads—a signal flag written in the only language the Corps respects. Disaster. We arranged it with a craftsman's care, and I mean that sincerely; there is real artistry in faking a catastrophe. Doss's shirt went on top, soaked in oil, positioned so its buttons would survive. Buttons survive everything. They're the cockroaches of personal effects.

Then the details, and the details are where lies live or die. A boot, half-burned, wedged under a rock as if kicked there in a struggle. A scatter of spilled grain leading toward the fire, as if someone had been hauling stores when it caught. Sera contributed her father's old tin cup, dented, worthless, and I hesitated over it—one beat, no more—and she saw me hesitate.

"He'd have understood," she said quietly. "He spent his whole life leaving things behind in the desert. One cup won't break the habit."

I set it by the ashes where the light would find it.

Last, I took the strip of cloth I'd prepared the night before—cut from my own spare bandana, scorched at one edge, knotted in the ranger-corps field style that any tracker would recognize—and weighted it with a stone near the fire's edge. A remnant. A thread of evidence. Enough to suggest that whatever had happened here had happened to someone who once wore the uniform.

Because that was the cruelest part of the plan, and I chose it deliberately, and I'm not proud, but I'm not sorry either. Corvane would find that scrap. Corvane would know the knot. And somewhere in the arithmetic of his duty, the sum would come out: *Amberdale is dead. The file can close.* Grief closes files faster than justice ever did. I'd learned that the hard way.

We climbed out through the eastern scree—an ugly route, trackless, chosen precisely because it was ugly—and gained the shelf of rock above the wash as the sun cleared the ridge. From there we could see everything, including, eventually, them.

They came over the ridge at midmorning, four riders in the dun-and-dust colors of the Corps, moving in the loose diamond formation that meant patrol rather than pursuit. I lay flat on the stone with the spyglass I'd taken off a dead man years ago and hadn't yet forgiven myself for, and I put the glass on the lead rider, and there he was.

Corvane.

Seven years had silvered him at the temples and taught his face to hold still, but it was the same face that had read me my charges in a courtroom that smelled of ink and lilies, the same steady gray eyes that had believed, completely, in the verdict he'd helped deliver. That was the thing I'd never managed to hate properly. He hadn't been cruel. He'd been *certain*, and certainty had been enough to ruin me, and I had spent seven years learning that certainty is the most dangerous thing a good man can carry.

Beside me, Sera went very still. Doss made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling.

"That's him," Sera breathed. "That's the one."

"That's him."

"What are you going to do? Shoot him?" Doss whispered. "Please say no. Please include me in the no."

"I'm going to watch him bury us," I said, "and then we're going to leave."

Below, the riders had reached the wash. Even at this distance I could read the change in their movement—the sudden stillness, the dismounting, the way they converged on the smoke stain with the careful urgency of men who already feared the answer. Corvane swung down first. Of course he did. He walked into the ashes alone, the way he did everything, and I watched him kneel.

I watched him pick up the tin cup.

I watched him turn it over in his hands, and stand, and stand there, motionless, for longer than procedure allows. The other riders gave him room. One of them said something I couldn't hear, and Corvane shook his head, just once, and set the cup down with a gentleness that went through me like a splinter working toward the bone.

He thought we were dead. He thought *I* was dead, and he was mourning it, this man whose testimony had ended my life, and the worst part—the part I'd carry up the next canyon and the one after—was that the mourning looked genuine. He held that scrap of my bandana like it was a letter from someone he'd failed.

"Good," Sera said softly, and there was no pleasure in it, only the flat satisfaction of a trap closing on someone else for once. "Let him feel it."

But I kept the glass on him a moment longer, because I'd seen something else, something the others missed. When Corvane rose from the ashes, he didn't order the patrol to mark the site and ride on, which was procedure. Instead he turned slowly, full circle, scanning the ridgelines—the eastern scree, our scree—with an expression I recognized because I wore it every day of my life.

It was the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

And then he raised one hand, and pointed—not at the wash, not at the fire, but east. Toward the Summit country. Toward the map in my coat. And his voice carried up the wind in fragments, just enough:

"—not enough remains. Check the gear counts. Nobody burns a full camp and walks away with *nothing*—"

Doss exhaled beside me. "He doesn't believe it."

"No," I said, and lowered the spyglass, and felt the strange double weight of relief and dread settling into my chest like sand finding its level. "He doesn't. Which means we bought ourselves time, not safety. He'll follow the same trail we're following—east, into the Summit country—and he'll do it quietly, because he suspects something his superiors don't want found."

Sera looked at me. "How do you know?"

"Because I know what it looks like when a man starts doubting the story he was handed," I said, shouldering my pack, turning my back on the smoke of my own funeral. "I've been that man for seven years. Come on. We have a river to find before he finds us—and this time, I intend to be holding something worth more than a name when he does."

Behind us, far below, a thin column of smoke kept rising from the place where Ivo Amberdale had officially ceased to exist. I didn't look back. Whoever climbed down into that wash next would have to decide what the ashes meant.

I already had.
