## 8. The First Marker

My father taught me to read a horizon the way other fathers teach their daughters to read scripture: reverently, and with the understanding that most of what it says is lies.

"See that dark line, little gull?" he'd say, pointing at a shimmer that was either distant water or distant nothing. "That's the desert promising you something. Desert's a great promiser. Worst payer you'll ever meet."

I was seven. I believed him about everything except the part where he kept leaving. He promised he'd come back from the Summit. The desert paid out on that one the way deserts do.

So when Ivo Amberdale reined up at the base of a ridge on our ninth day out and said, in that flat ranger voice she uses like other people use doors—"There. That's a marker stone"—I felt something in my chest turn over, the way a wagon turns over: slowly at first, then all at once, and then there's no un-turning it.

The stone stood maybe knee-high, half-swallowed by sand, but someone had squared its top with a chisel. Someone had cared about it. And carved into its face, worn shallow but legible, were three words:

*BRINE PARTY — WATER.*

Not *water?* Not *water.* Just—water. A statement. My father had climbed down off his horse in whatever year that was, chiseled those letters into rock, and ridden on, certain enough of what lay ahead to leave it written in stone for anyone who came after.

"He marked his camps," Ivo said. She'd dismounted and was brushing sand from the base of the stone with her fingers, gentle as if she were clearing sleep from a child's face. "Standard survey practice. You set a marker at your first good camp so the resupply teams can find the route." She paused. "He knew I'd come looking. Or he hoped."

"He didn't know me," I said. "He wouldn't have known I'd care."

Ivo looked up at me then, and her eyes did the thing they do—the thing I've spent nine days learning to hate, which is see straight through me without comment. "People always hope," she said. "It's the cheapest thing they carry and the heaviest to put down."

Then she turned back to the ridge, because that's what Ivo Amberdale does with feelings: she files them somewhere behind her sternum and keeps walking.

Doss, trailing behind with the pack mules, muttered, "Cheerful lot, this expedition," and I didn't have the heart or the energy to tell him to shut up. Some days the thief is the only one of us who says what everyone's thinking. It's probably why he's such a good thief. Honesty is just burglary with the timing changed.

We crested the ridge an hour before noon, and below us, tucked into the lee of a broken cliff, lay my father's first camp.

* * *

I want to describe it honestly, because honesty is the only inheritance Halvard Brine left me and I intend to spend every coin of it.

From above, it looked like nothing. A scatter of pale shapes against the canyon floor, the color of bone, the color of salt. From above, dead camps all look the same—just the desert digesting another meal. But we rode down into it, and the desert stopped digesting, and I started understanding.

The tents had collapsed but not rotted. Dry country does that; it mummifies everything, grief included. The canvas lay over the frames like sheets over bodies, and I suppose in a way that's exactly what they were. Cooking gear sat where hands had set it down. A boot stood upright beside a cold fire ring, laced, waiting for a foot. There was a tin cup on a flat rock with a stone on top of it—a weight, a deliberate weight—and under the cup, when I lifted it with hands I pretended weren't shaking, was a folded page.

"You read it," Ivo said.

"It's his handwriting," I said, as if she couldn't see me holding it, as if the fact needed announcing to the canyon, to the sky, to the whole indifferent architecture of the world. "It's his handwriting. He used to write supply lists like this. Same slant. He pressed too hard on the downstrokes and tore the paper sometimes, and Mother used to yell at him about wasting good stock, and—"

"Sera."

"I'm reading it."

I read it. Out loud, because Doss had gone quiet in a way I'd never heard him quiet, and because Ivo doesn't ask twice, and mostly because if I didn't give the words air I thought they might stay inside me and grow into something I couldn't carry.

*"Day eleven. Found seep water in the east fork—not enough, but honest. Followed the limestone shelf two miles further and heard it before I saw it: running water, underground, coming up through a fracture in the canyon floor. Enough flow to fill a cistern in a night. Enough to fill a hundred cisterns. I sat by it until dark and let myself believe. God forgive me, I let myself believe."*

There was more. His hand got faster toward the bottom, the letters leaning like runners:

*"If this page is found and I am not, know that the water is real. Whatever they told you at the land office, whatever the surveys say, the surveys are wrong, or bought, or both. The Summit is not dry. The Summit was made dry. There is a river under this stone and men with clean hands decided it would stay there. Find it. Count it. Write it down where no one can burn it."*

Nobody spoke. The wind moved through the camp, lifting grit off the collapsed tents, letting it fall again. Somewhere a crow—or the desert's version of a crow, the kind that sounds like a hinge that needs oil—made its one remark and left.

"Made dry," Doss repeated. He'd taken off his hat, which for Doss is roughly equivalent to a funeral procession. "What's that mean, made dry?"

"It means somebody revoked the water grants," Ivo said. Her voice was level, but I've learned her levels. This one had a current under it. "Land grants carry water rights. Kill the grant, kill the right, and the settlements above the Summit lose their claim to what flows beneath them. Then the speculators buy the worthless land cheap, and wait." She picked up the boot by the fire ring, looked at it, set it back exactly as it had been. "Someone waited."

"And my father found out." I heard my own voice come out strange. Small and hard at once, like a fist around a pebble. "He found out and he wrote it down and they let him die out here for it."

"We don't know that yet," Ivo said.

"Don't we?" I gestured at the camp—at the tents, the boot, the cup with its careful stone. "Look at it, ranger. Look at it! Eleven days out and half his party's gear abandoned, and the man writes *God forgive me* about being happy near a spring. What happened here? What are we walking into?"

"I don't know," Ivo said. "But I know how to find out, and I know how to keep us alive doing it, and those are the only two things I can promise you today."

"That's not nothing," Doss offered quietly.

"It's less than a daughter wants," I said.

But I folded the page along my father's creases, and I put it inside my shirt, against my skin, because paper burns and chests don't, or anyway mine hadn't yet, and I meant to keep it that way.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of the marker ridge, close enough to walk back to my father's camp but not in it. Ivo's rule. "You don't sleep in the dead's house," she said. "You'll dream wrong." Doss asked how she knew. She said, "Because I have," and closed the conversation like a ledger.

I couldn't sleep, which surprised nobody, least of all me. So I walked back alone in the moonlight—I told myself it was to check the gear for anything salvageable, and I almost believed it—and I went through my father's belongings the way you go through a house after a funeral: touching everything, keeping nothing, keeping everything.

His sextant was there. Scratched, dented, the brass gone green at the joints, but there. He'd carried it on every trip since I was small; he used to let me hold it and pretend to shoot the sun, and he'd call out absurd coordinates—"Little gull, you've discovered a new ocean, it's entirely in the flour barrel"—and I'd laugh, and he'd look at me with that expression I could never name and now, standing over his dead camp, I named it at last.

It was the look of a man memorizing something he expected to lose.

I sat down in the dirt among his tents and I talked to him. Out loud. I'm not ashamed of it, or I'm ashamed of it, both, equally.

"You absolute bastard," I told him. "You found a river. You found a *river*, and you wrote it on a scrap of paper under a cup, and you didn't send word, you didn't come home, you didn't—you just went deeper, didn't you? Deeper and deeper, chasing the source, because that's what you do, that's what you always did, you never met a mystery you wouldn't walk into like a doorway—"

My voice cracked on *doorway,* which was undignified, and I let it crack, because there was no one there to see except the moon and a dead man's canvas and, apparently, Ivo Amberdale, who had followed me at a distance and now stood at the edge of the camp with her arms crossed, saying nothing, offering nothing, staying anyway.

"How long have you been there?" I asked.

"Long enough."

"Spying on me?"

"Making sure the desert didn't take you." She came closer, slow, the way you approach a spooked animal, and sat down on a supply crate across from me without asking permission, which is either rude or familiar, and with Ivo it's impossible to tell which. "Your father and I had three conversations," she said. "All of them short. One of them was about you."

The moon went cold in my chest. "What did he say?"

"He said his daughter hated him, and he deserved it, and he was going to make it right or die trying, and either way the water would be real." Ivo looked at the collapsed tents, at the boot, at the cup. "I told him daughters remember longer than rivers run. He laughed. Said, 'Good. Somebody should.'"

I sat with that. The wind moved. Somewhere out in the dark the desert made its small promises, and for once I didn't check the horizon to see if any of them were true, because I was looking at something else: the shape of my father's last years, which I had always read as abandonment, and which now, by lantern-light of a dead camp, resolved into something else entirely.

A man building a case. A man who knew the land office was rotten and knew no one would believe a prospector's word against a magistrate's seal, and so went out to get proof worth more than his life, because that was the only currency the courts accepted.

He hadn't left us. He'd left himself. He'd spent himself, deliberately, the way you spend your last coin on the one thing that matters, and he'd done it hoping—hoping, that cheapest and heaviest thing—that someday someone who loved him would walk this route and understand.

"I'm starting to understand," I said aloud, to Ivo, to the camp, to him.

"I know," Ivo said. "I watched it happen. It's why I stopped at the marker instead of riding past."

And then, because the moment had gotten too large and I am my father's daughter and we Brines deflect like we breathe, I said, "If you tell anyone I cried in a dead man's camp, I'll deny it and then ruin you."

"Wouldn't dream of it," Ivo said, and for the first time since I'd met her, she smiled. It was a small smile, crooked, quickly filed away behind the sternum where she keeps everything, but it was real, and it changed her face the way water changes a canyon: not much, and completely.

We walked back to our own fire together. Doss was awake, pretending not to be, hat over his eyes. As I lay down, I felt the journal page against my ribs, warm from my body, and I thought: *Day eleven. He let himself believe.*

Day twelve, tomorrow. We ride east, following the fracture line, following the limestone shelf, following a dead man's handwriting toward the thing he died to prove.

I slept, and dreamed wrong anyway, and woke before dawn to Ivo shaking my shoulder, her face lit orange by a low-burning fire, her voice stripped of everything except urgency.

"Sera," she said. "Get up. There's riders on the south rim."
