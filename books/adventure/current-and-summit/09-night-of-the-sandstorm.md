## 9. Night of the Sandstorm

The sky turned the color of an old bruise around midday, and Ivo Amberdale started walking faster.

I want it noted that I noticed this before either of the others did. Sera Brine was busy pretending she wasn't limping, and I was busy pretending I hadn't eaten a scorpion on purpose two nights back because my stomach had stopped accepting the concept of food as anything other than a rumor. But Ivo's stride lengthened by about four inches and her head came up like a deerhound's, and I thought: *there it is.* The desert had said something to her that it hadn't bothered saying to us.

"Storm?" Sera asked.

"Sandstorm." Ivo didn't slow down. "Big one. We've got maybe three hours."

"Three hours until what?"

"Until the air stops being air." She squinted at the horizon, where a brown smear had begun assembling itself out of nothing, patient as debt. "We need rock. Overhang, cave, anything with a back wall."

"And if we don't find one?"

"We find one."

That was Ivo in miniature, really. No reassurance, no doom, just the next necessary thing stated flatly, as though the universe were a contract she'd already read all the fine print of. I'd been traveling with her for — what, nine days? Ten? Long enough to know that when she went quiet in that particular way, arguing was a waste of breath you might need later.

So we walked. And behind us, the desert began to stand up.

* * *

You want to know what a sandstorm looks like from the wrong side? It looks like a mountain deciding to come to you. A wall of ochre and rust, miles wide, rolling forward with a sound like the world's largest millstone grinding the world's smallest grain. The first thing it takes is the sun. The second is the horizon. The third is your sense that you are a person at all — you become a mouth full of grit and a pair of eyes that have forgotten how eyes work.

We found our cave twenty minutes before the wall hit. A limestone pocket in the flank of a mesa, half-choked with old flood debris, barely tall enough for me to crouch in without performing some kind of apology to my spine. Ivo got us into it with the brisk efficiency of someone who'd done this drill before, and then we sealed the entrance with a groundcloth and our bodies and waited for the world to end.

It took about six hours.

I'm not going to romanticize those hours. They were loud. The wind screamed against the rock like it held a personal grudge, and fine sand found every gap in our shelter and sifted down onto us in a thin, relentless rain of the desert's opinion about visitors. You couldn't talk without tasting it. You couldn't sleep without dreaming in it. At some point Sera's mare — no, wait, we'd lost the mules at the pass, so scratch that; at some point *I* considered whether a person could drown in dry sand, and decided the answer was probably yes but only in this desert, which would absolutely do it out of spite.

When the roar finally faded to a grumble and then to silence, Ivo peeled back the groundcloth and let in a light so changed it took me a moment to recognize it as daylight. Everything outside was new. The dunes had been redrawn overnight, our trail erased so completely it was as if we'd never walked it, as if the desert had reached back with a great flat hand and smoothed us out of its memory.

"Well," Sera said, looking at the blankness where our route had been. "That's that, then."

"That's not that." Ivo was already studying the rock face above us. "There's water seepage in this stone. See the staining? Where there's seepage there's usually a cistern or a crack system. We shelter here tonight, rest properly, and I re-navigate at dawn."

Sera looked at her. "You can re-navigate. In country you've never crossed. After a storm redrew all of it."

"Yes."

One word. No elaboration. Sera's jaw did the thing it did when she was deciding whether to fight, and I did what I'd gotten good at doing, which was finding a reason to be busy elsewhere. I busied myself exploring the back of the cave, which is how I found the fire ring.

Old one. Stones blackened, arranged neat as bookkeeping. And beside it, half-buried in drift sand, a tin cup with a name scratched into the bottom: *H. BRINE.*

I called the others over. Sera picked it up with both hands, careful, the way you handle something that might still be alive.

"My father's cup," she said. "He had a set of these. Marked them himself. Said if a man's gear has his name on it, he can't be entirely lost."

"He camped here," Ivo said quietly. "On the way in."

"On the way in," Sera repeated. She turned the cup over. "So he made it this far. He stood right here." Her voice did something complicated and then shut itself off, and she set the cup down on the old fire ring like she was setting it on an altar, which, I suppose, she was.

That night we lit a small fire — Ivo allowed it, small and screened, since the cave mouth would hide the glow — and ate the last of the decent food, and the wind outside moaned its aftershocks through the stone. And it was Sera, not me, who broke the rule.

"You knew him," she said to Ivo. "My father. Before I did. Before any of us knew where he'd gone."

"I met him once. Briefly."

"Once." Sera laughed, and it wasn't a laugh, it was a wound wearing a laugh's coat. "He spent one evening with you and gave you his life's work. I got nineteen years and a string of letters that stopped coming." She poked the fire. "What did he see in you?"

I waited for Ivo to deflect. Deflecting was her native language; she spoke it more fluently than anyone I'd met, and I include professional liars in that assessment. Nine days I'd watched her answer questions by answering different questions, give away nothing while appearing generous, keep herself folded up tight as a map in a wet climate.

Instead she was quiet for a long time. Long enough that the fire popped twice. Then she said:

"He saw a woman whose name was worth nothing. That's why."

I sat up straighter. Doss attention: fully engaged.

* * *

Here's what I knew about Ivo Amberdale going into that cave, which is to say here's what she'd let me know. Desert ranger, ex-branch, good with a rifle, better with a compass, allergic to settlements, paid in advance, slept facing the door. She'd hired me on at the last town — or rather, she'd declined to turn me in for the theft she'd caught me committing, and then informed me that if I was going to be a criminal in her vicinity I could at least be useful, and somehow by the end of that conversation I was employed. I'd assumed she was running from a husband. Or debts. Something ordinary. People who won't say their own names are usually hiding something embarrassingly human.

She fed the fire a stick and told us.

"I had a family holding," she said. "Small. Dryland orchard, east side of the Verrell basin. My father's, then mine. It was dying — everyone's was, that whole district, ten years of wells failing, springs going salt. And I was angry about it in the way young people are angry, which is loudly and in public." A ghost of a smile. "I said things at a land commission hearing. Named names. Said the grant revocations didn't add up, that somebody was engineering the drought to force sales. I was twenty-two and I had a receipt for everything I claimed, because I'd spent a winter pulling survey records."

"What happened?" Sera asked, though her voice said she'd already guessed the shape of it.

"The records disappeared. All of them. And three weeks later a magistrate named Ovellan convicted me of falsifying survey documents — the very documents I'd cited. Tampering with official records, fraud against the land office. My family's grant was revoked as part of the penalty. The orchard went to a speculator's consortium within the year." She said it evenly, the way you recite something you've recited so many times alone that it's worn smooth, like river stone. "I ran before they could sentence me to the work camps. I've been running for eleven years."

The wind sighed against the cave mouth. Sera had gone very still.

"They erased the evidence," Ivo continued, "and then they erased me. There's a version of events, in the official record, where I'm a forger and a fugitive. It's a tidy story. Tidy stories are expensive. Somebody paid a lot for that one."

"And Halvard Brine knew this," Sera said slowly.

"He found me at a dry well with a day left in him, and he offered me a map in exchange for an oath." Ivo looked into the fire. "I thought I was doing him a kindness. Taking a dying man's burden off his hands. It took me until the third marker to understand he'd chosen me deliberately. He knew exactly whose land his summit sat inside. He knew exactly who'd been ruined by the same men he was about to expose." Her jaw tightened. "He didn't need a ranger. He needed someone with a reason."

Sera stared at her. And I watched something happen in her face — the armor, the beautiful bitter armor she'd worn since the pass, developing its first real crack. Because here was the thing Sera had been carrying: her father had chosen strangers over her, chosen the desert over home, and she'd filed it under *he didn't love us enough to stay.* And now here was Ivo, telling her: no. He stayed away because staying meant dying small, and he loved you enough to die large instead, and he spent his last months arranging for the truth to reach you through whatever hands he could trust.

"He was building a case," Sera whispered. "All this time. He wasn't prospecting for water. He was prospecting for *proof.*"

"Water and proof," Ivo said. "Out here they're the same thing. Water is proof. Of what the land was promised and what was taken from it."

I kept my mouth shut, which for me is a genuine athletic achievement, because my whole body wanted to say: *and you, Ivo Amberdale, you've been carrying this for eleven years and you just handed it to two near-strangers in a hole in a rock, and do you know what that cost you?*

But I didn't have to say it. I could see it. For the first time since I'd met her, I was actually looking at her instead of at the efficient, closed-off surface she presented to the world, and what I saw was a woman who had traded her name for her life and had been living on the difference ever since, spending it carefully, alone, in places where nobody could ask her who she used to be. Every flat answer, every deflection, every time she'd given me nothing when I'd asked for something — it wasn't coldness. It was rationing. She had exactly as much self as she could afford to show, and she'd been showing us the interest, never the principal.

Until tonight. Tonight she'd paid out principal.

Vulnerability, I've learned, works like currency in the desert. Nobody spends it unless they expect something back, and what Ivo expected back, I think, was simply to be believed. Not pitied. Not absolved. Believed. And Sera — stiff, grieving, principled Sera Brine — reached across the fire and put her hand over Ivo's wrist, brief and hard, and said, "I believe you."

Two words. Ivo nodded once, the way you nod when accepting a delivery, and looked away fast, and pretended to check the screen over the cave mouth, and her eyes were bright in the firelight and I generously decided not to notice.

* * *

Later, when Sera slept, I sat up on watch beside Ivo. The stars were out in their usual obscene quantities. Somewhere out in the dark, the desert was rearranging itself again, settling the storm's leavings into new shapes.

"Doss," she said, after a while.

"Yeah."

"Why did you steal the deed box?"

Ah. Straight to it. That's the thing about vulnerability — it's contagious, like yawning. She'd opened a vein, so now she wanted mine.

I could have lied. I'd been lying professionally since I was twelve. But lying to a woman who'd just told me her whole ruin seemed ungrateful, somehow, like spitting in a well you'd drunk from.

"It was my brother's deed box," I said. "Or it should have been. Our landlord sold our tenancy papers to a land agent while my brother was sick — fever, couldn't stand, couldn't sign anything, which didn't stop the sale. By the time he was well enough to argue, the papers were three owners deep and legally bulletproof. So I stole the box. Originals, copies, correspondence. Figured I'd find the flaw, get it reversed." I shrugged. "There wasn't a flaw. There never is. The paperwork's always clean when the theft happened years earlier, in a room you weren't invited to."

Ivo was quiet a moment. "And the ranger who caught you."

"Wasn't a ranger. Was you." I grinned at the fire. "Worst luck of my life. Second worst. First was being born honest-adjacent in a country that isn't."

She almost smiled. Almost. On Ivo's face that's practically a parade.

"Your brother," she said. "Is he—"

"Alive. Working the agent's own fields now, if you can believe the joke of it. Sends me letters to towns I've already left." I poked the coals. "One day I'll stop moving long enough to read one."

She looked at me then, really looked, the way she'd let herself be looked at earlier, and I understood that we'd just completed some kind of transaction. She'd shown me the exile; I'd shown her the thief. Neither of us was fixed by it. But we were, I don't know. Counted. Tallied among the living, each with our losses declared at the border.

"Get some sleep," she said. "Dawn comes early, and I have to find a river that's been buried for a hundred years."

"You'll find it," I said. "You're the most stubborn person I've ever been arrested by."

And that was when she heard it — or I did, a heartbeat after she went rigid, her hand up, palm flat: the far-off sound of hooves, or boots, or something rhythmic and deliberate, out on the storm-scoured flats where nothing should be moving at night.

Ivo killed the fire with one motion and was at the cave mouth before I'd finished standing up, pressed to the stone, listening.

"Company," she breathed. "And not the friendly kind."
