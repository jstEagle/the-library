## 10. Corvane's Ledger

There is a particular kind of silence that comes off the flats at night, after a sandstorm has spent itself and the world is still deciding whether to exist again. I have stood in it maybe two hundred times in my career with the ranger-corps, and I have learned to read it the way a priest reads scripture — which is to say, I find in it whatever I'm most afraid of.

That night it said her name.

Ivo Amberdale. Say it three times fast and it sounds like a curse, which is what half the settlements in this territory believe it is anyway. The woman who burned a granary ledger. The woman who fled her own trial. The woman whose face I had pinned above my desk for six years until the ink faded and I could no longer tell whether I was looking at a criminal or just at the last honest thing I'd ever failed to understand.

I sat my horse on the ridge above the flats and listened to the storm's aftermath settle, and I did not move for a long time, because moving would mean admitting what I already knew.

She was alive. And she was ahead of me.

* * *

You should understand something about how I came to be out here, alone, without sanction, following a scrap of paper that my own superiors ordered destroyed nine years ago.

The corps keeps ledgers. That's the whole architecture of the thing — ledgers of patrol routes, ledgers of supply, ledgers of names. When a ranger dies in the field, his effects are logged. When an expedition fails, its maps are logged. And when a magistrate's office sends down an order marked *suppress*, well. There's a ledger for that too, though it isn't called one. It's called the deep file, and it lives in a locked drawer in the quartermaster's annex, and for nine years I was the officer who signed the receipt for Halvard Brine's expedition materials.

I signed because signing was my job. I signed because Wexler Magistrate Aldous Crane's seal was on the order, and you do not refuse a magistrate's seal unless you enjoy poverty and disgrace in equal measure. I signed, and I told myself the story every junior officer tells himself: *the old man got lost, the desert took him, the paperwork is just paperwork.*

Then, four years into carrying that story around like a stone in my boot, I made the mistake of actually reading what I'd locked away.

It was a slow night in the annex. Rain — actual rain, a rarity worth celebrating — drumming on the roof, nothing to do but inventory. And there was Brine's field journal, logged as *destroyed per directive*, sitting intact in its oilcloth wrapper because some clerk a generation lazier than me had filed the destruction order without performing the destruction. I opened it the way you open anything you've been told not to: with your heart going and your hands telling you they're only doing their duty.

Halvard Brine wrote like a man who talks to himself when he thinks no one's listening. Half survey notes, half complaints about his boots. But page forty-one stopped my pen mid-inventory.

*Found it. Not a seep, not a spring — a river. Running under the summit like a vein under skin. Enough water to green the whole eastern basin. Sent coordinates to the survey office at Redstone. Awaiting confirmation of grant status before we sink the first shaft.*

Awaiting confirmation of grant status. I knew what that meant. Every ranger did. It meant the land had been parceled out decades back under the settlement acts — grants that were supposed to guarantee water rights to any claimant who developed them. Grants that had, one by one over the years, quietly lapsed, been revoked, been bought up by men whose names appeared on donation lists and magistrate appointment letters.

And then, tucked behind the journal, a single sheet in a different hand — clerical, official, stamped with a seal I recognized because I'd sworn an oath to it:

*Survey finding: hydrological assessment negative. No viable water source identified in the Sunken Summit region. Expedition terminated. Grant claims void for lack of development.*

No viable water source. In the same file as a journal describing a river.

I sat with those two documents for a long time while the rain came down, and I felt something shift in me that I have never fully shifted back — the specific vertigo of realizing that the institution you serve is not merely fallible but *interested*. That the ledger doesn't record the truth. The ledger decides it.

A river didn't stop existing because a clerk wrote that it wasn't there. But a hundred settlements went thirsty because he did.

* * *

I reported none of it, obviously. Let's not pretend I'm braver than I am. I relocked the drawer, I finished my inventory, and I carried the knowledge home like contraband, where it sat in me and fermented.

But I kept the map fragment.

That's the part that would hang me if it ever came to light — and given where I'm standing tonight, perhaps it will. When the suppression order finally came down hard, two years ago, with instructions to physically destroy all remaining expedition materials, I pulled the western quadrant of Brine's route map from the pile and let the rest burn. My report says *materials incinerated, witnessed by Quartermaster Vess.* Vess witnessed nothing; Vess was drunk. The fragment went inside my coat, and it has lived there through three postings and one marriage that didn't survive my hours, and I have taken it out more nights than I care to count and looked at the penciled line of Brine's route running toward the summit, and asked myself the same question every time:

Why do you still have this?

For years the answer was vanity. I liked knowing something the corps didn't know I knew. Then the answer became fear — because a man who holds evidence of a magistrate's fraud is a man who sleeps badly. And then, eight months ago, the answer changed, and the change had a name.

Amberdale.

They brought me the report from a way-station east of the flats: a woman matching Ivo Amberdale's description, older, harder, traveling under no name anyone could pin down. Most officers would have shrugged. Fugitives drift through the territory like sand through a sieve; you can't chase every grain. But I had spent six years with her face above my desk, and I will confess something unprofessional: I had never entirely believed the case against her. Not the way you believe a thing in your bones. The granary ledger she supposedly burned — the fire that conveniently erased the records of grain shipments that would have shown where the drought-relief stores actually went — it had always fit too neatly. A woman fleeing a wrongful conviction burns the one document that might have cleared her? Criminals are many things, but they are rarely that tidy.

So when the report came in, I did something I hadn't done since the annex night. I took out the map fragment. And I laid it beside everything I remembered of Amberdale's old case file, and I saw it — plain as a brand on a steer.

Her family's land. Her father's grant. The eastern basin. The same ground the speculators swallowed when the grants were revoked, the same ground the drought hollowed out, the same ground that sat square in the middle of Halvard Brine's penciled route to a river that officially did not exist.

She wasn't running anymore. She was going home by the long way, and she didn't even know it yet — or maybe she did, and that frightened me worse.

* * *

Which brings us to tonight, and the storm, and the flats saying her name.

I picked up the party's trail two days ago at the last settlement — Sera Brine's departure was noted in the waystation log, and where Halvard's estranged daughter goes, I reasoned, the map goes. Three travelers. One of them moved like a ranger, weight forward, eyes doing constant arithmetic with the horizon. I didn't need a sketch artist. Six years of looking at a face does something to you.

I should have radioed it in. That was the procedure, and procedure has been my religion for twenty years — it's what separates a ranger from a man with a horse and opinions. But I knew what reporting it would mean. It would mean Crane's office dispatching enforcers, and enforcers do not bring people back for trial when the people know things. It would mean the map fragment's existence becoming a question someone might eventually ask me. It would mean the river — if there was a river — being written off a second time, deeper this time, in blood instead of ink.

So I broke procedure. First time in twenty years. I told the corps I was conducting a solo reconnaissance of the eastern passes ahead of dry season, which was even true, if you squinted. And I rode out alone with a fragment of a dead man's map in my coat and a doubt in my chest that has been growing so long it's less a doubt now than an organ — something vital, something that would kill me to remove.

My superiors would call it betrayal. I've had time to think about the word, riding alone across country where thinking is the only entertainment, and here is what I've concluded: betrayal requires loyalty to betray. And I find, examining the ledger of my own service the way I once examined Brine's file, that my loyalty was never actually to the men holding the seals. It was to the oath. Protect the settlements. Serve the land and the people on it. Somewhere along the way the institution and the oath diverged, and I stayed with the institution because staying is easier, and I am tired of easy.

Duty curdles. Nobody warns you about that. They tell you duty is milk, pure and sustaining, and you drink it every morning for twenty years, and then one day you look in the cup and it's turned, and the question is whether you pour it out or drink it anyway and call it the same thing it always was.

I'm not drinking it tonight.

* * *

Here is what I know, laid out straight, the way I'd want it laid out for me:

One. Halvard Brine found flowing water beneath the Sunken Summit, sufficient to sustain the eastern settlements through the drought cycles. His finding was officially declared nonexistent by a survey document I believe was fabricated or coerced, under the seal of the magistrate's office.

Two. The revocation of the eastern basin grants — including the Amberdale grant — preceded and enabled the consolidation of that land into holdings controlled by Crane's associates. The drought did not create the land grab. The land grab required the drought, and the drought required the water to stay buried.

Three. Ivo Amberdale was convicted of destroying records that, had they survived, would have documented the diversion of relief supplies — supplies that trace, through three intermediaries, back to the same holdings. She may have burned that ledger. Or she may have been the only person in the granary that night who refused to sign off on the shipments, and burning was the story that suited everyone with power. I have never determined which. I have had six years to determine which, and the fact that I couldn't is itself an indictment of everyone involved, myself included.

Four. She is now, with two companions, somewhere ahead of me in the broken country south of the flats, following Brine's route toward the summit. Toward the river. Toward the proof.

Five. I am the only officer of the corps who knows all of this, and I am standing alone on a ridge at midnight, and the wind is coming down off the high country smelling of stone and old rain, and I have to decide — tonight, now, in the dark — which side of my own ledger I die on. Because make no mistake: this ends with someone dying. Men who write off rivers do not hesitate to write off rangers.

* * *

I found their camp an hour ago. Or rather, I found where their camp had been — a cave in the storm-cut rock, fire pit still warm, three bedrolls, boot prints heading away south and west with the unhurried confidence of people who think they've lost their shadow.

They haven't. I'm better than they think I am. It's the one professional vanity I've allowed myself to keep.

I could take them. That's the ugly truth sitting next to me on the ridge. Three against one, but I have surprise and they have civilians, and I could put irons on Amberdale tonight and be a hero by week's end — commendation, reinstatement to favor, maybe even the promotion I was denied the year after her trial, when asking too many questions about a granary fire made me inconvenient.

I sat with that future for a long while. It fit me like my old uniform fits me — snug in the shoulders, wrong everywhere else.

Because here's the thing I keep circling, the thing that's been eating through my duty like rust through iron: if I arrest her, the documents she's carrying — and she must be carrying them, or going to fetch them — go into an evidence locker in Crane's jurisdiction, and I have seen what happens to evidence in Crane's jurisdiction. It develops holes. It develops amnesia. It develops a tendency to burn, much like granary ledgers.

And if I don't arrest her — if I do the other thing, the unthinkable thing, the thing that has no procedure and no precedent and no clean exit — then I become what she became. A name on a warrant. A fugitive with a cause. I'd be betting twenty years of service, such reputation as I have, and quite possibly my life on the testimony of a dead prospector's pencil marks and a convicted arsonist's good character.

Some bet. Some odds.

And yet.

I thought about the settlements east of the basin. I've patrolled them. I've watched children queue at cisterns that hold less every year, watched farmers sell land their grandfathers broke, watched the whole eastern country bleed out one dry season at a time while men in the capital spoke gravely about scarcity as though it were weather and not policy. A hundred settlements, Brine wrote. Enough water to green the whole basin. It's been under the stone the entire time, running in the dark, patient as truth, waiting for somebody to dig.

I got down off the horse. My knees objected; twenty years of riding collects its debts. I stood at the cave mouth with the fire pit's warmth still rising faint as a ghost, and I looked south along their trail, and I said aloud, to nobody, the sentence I suppose I've been rehearsing for nine years without knowing it:

"Halvard Brine found water, and they buried the finding. I'm going to go help dig it up."

It sounded less mad out loud than in my head, which is either encouraging or a symptom.

I went back to the horse and took out the map fragment one more time. Brine's penciled route ran down off the flat country and into the canyonlands below the summit, and there, at the edge of the surveyed section, his handwriting simply stopped — a line trailing off into blank paper, the way a life trails off. Below it, in different ink, someone had written a single word: *Staircase.* Underlined twice.

The drowned staircase. Whatever door the old man had found into the earth, it lay a day and a half ahead of me, and a few hours ahead of them.

I swung back into the saddle. Ahead, the storm-scoured flats gave way to broken ground, black ridgelines stitching the horizon, and somewhere out in all that dark, a woman I once arrested was leading two strangers toward the proof that would either save the eastern country or get every one of them killed — and I realized, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that I no longer knew which outcome I feared more.

I turned the horse south and rode down off the ridge, alone, off the ledger, into the country where the map ran out.

Behind me, far off, a coyote started up — or something that sounded like one, if a coyote could laugh at the shape a man's life takes when he finally stops lying to himself.

Ahead, the summit waited, and under it, the water. And between us and both of them, I suspected, waited men with better horses and worse intentions, sent by the same seal I'd spent twenty years saluting.

I touched my spurs to the horse's flank and went to find out which of us the desert had decided to keep.
