## 11. The Drowned Staircase

The canyon didn't announce itself. That was the first thing I noticed, and the last thing I liked about it.

For three days we'd followed the map's dotted line across country that got meaner by the mile—first the flats, then the shattered basalt where Doss turned an ankle twice and complained about it eleven times, and now this: a seam in the earth so narrow you could stand on its lip and throw a stone into shadow without seeing where it landed. Halvard Brine had drawn the entrance as two parallel strokes with a small mark between them, and beneath it, in his cramped surveyor's hand, a single word: *Staircase.*

"He means stairs," Sera said. She'd been quiet all morning, which from Sera was worse than shouting. "In a canyon. In the middle of nowhere."

"Prospectors don't draw things that aren't there," I said. "They draw things they wish weren't there, and things they found anyway."

"And which is this?"

I looked down into the dark. "Ask him tomorrow."

We made camp on the rim because I wasn't taking three exhausted people down a vertical crack in the world at dusk. Doss built the fire small, the way I'd taught him—screened, low, dead out by midnight—and sat picking at his bootlace like it owed him money.

"You've been down here before," he said. It wasn't quite a question. Doss had learned that questions got you less than statements; you stated a thing and waited to see if I corrected it.

"No."

"But you knew what we'd find."

"I knew what *he* said we'd find." I fed a twig to the fire. "Halvard Brine wrote like a man who'd stopped caring whether anyone believed him. That either means he was lying or he'd given up on being believed. Both kinds of men die in canyons. Only one kind leaves maps."

Sera had her back to us, facing out at the dark, the way she always slept—or pretended to sleep—with her father's compass open in her palm. I watched her thumb move over the glass cover, back and forth, a small circular argument she'd been having with herself since the first camp. *He found water. He left me. He found water. He left me.* The compass didn't answer. Compasses never do.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, and we went down.

The staircase was real. Of course it was real. Halvard hadn't carved it—the canyon had, some ancient flood cutting steps into the sandstone in its hurry to get somewhere else—but someone had widened them, chiseled handholds into the walls, driven iron pins that were rusted orange but still held. Someone had come this way planning to come back.

"His expedition did this?" Doss asked, wedged against the rock, breathing hard.

"Some of it. The rest is older than any of us." I tested a pin, put my weight on it. "Water does most of the work in this country. People just take the credit."

The descent took the better part of two hours. The light went from gray to blue to nothing, and then Doss lit the lanterns and the canyon walls came alive in ways I didn't care for—mineral streaks running down the stone like old tears, white and green and a strange bruised copper, all of it glistening. The air changed too. Cooler. Damper. And underneath the dust smell, faint but unmistakable:

Water.

Not the promise of water. Not the memory of water. Water, actual and near, moving somewhere below us in the dark, and I felt it in my chest before my ears confirmed it—a low, vast hush, like the canyon itself was breathing.

Doss heard it too. He stopped on the stairs and grabbed the wall. "What is that?"

"The map," Sera said behind him, and her voice had gone strange. "The name. Sunken Summit. He wasn't naming a mountain. He was naming what's under it."

Nobody said anything after that. There wasn't anything to say that the sound wasn't already saying better.

* * *

We found the second camp an hour past noon, on a shelf of flat stone where the canyon widened enough to hold a sky's worth of shadow.

I saw it first and called a halt without explaining why, which earned me one of Sera's looks—the ones that filed complaints for later. But I wanted a moment to read the scene before they walked into it, because I'd stood over enough dead camps to know the difference between the ones that tell a clean story and the ones that don't.

This one told a messy story.

Tents collapsed inward, rotted to lace. A cook fire long cold, its stones still stacked in a careful ring—someone tidy, someone who kept discipline even dying. Crates pried open, their contents sorted into neat piles: tools here, instruments there, rations gone to nothing but tins swollen with rot. And everywhere, over everything, the mineral crust. The same white-and-copper streaking from the walls had crept across the camp like frost, coating the crates, the tent poles, the stacked tin plates, giving the whole site a strange preserved shine, as if the canyon had decided these things mattered and dipped them in glass.

"It's been years," Doss whispered. He'd gone pale under his tan. "Nobody's touched it."

"No," I agreed. "Nobody has."

Sera moved through it like a sleepwalker. I let her. Some things you have to walk into yourself or they never take. She lifted a canvas flap, set it down. Turned a tin plate over with her boot toe, gently, the way you'd turn a sleeping child. Then she crouched at the edge of the camp, where a bedroll lay half-unrolled, and picked up something small—a clay pipe, blackened, its bowl crusted white.

"He smoked this," she said. "He swore off. After Mother. He swore—" Her voice did something I recognized and hated hearing, the place where grief hits a locked door and starts looking for windows. "He swore he'd quit."

"Sera."

"He *quit*. He told me he—" She pressed the pipe against her sternum like she could push it inside herself, keep it somewhere safe, and then she was crying, hard and graceless, the kind of crying that comes from a well nobody's drawn from in years. Doss hovered near her with his hands half-raised, wanting to help, having no idea how, and I understood him completely because I didn't either.

I gave her the only thing I had. Work.

"We search the camp," I said, loud enough to reach her without looming over her. "Everything catalogued. Journals, instruments, letters—anything with writing goes in the oilskin. Anything personal goes in the second pack. We're not leaving this shelf until every scrap of Halvard Brine's expedition is accounted for, because above us is a canyon that floods and behind us is a dry season, and when we walk out of here, we walk out with all of them or none of them. That was the oath."

She looked up at me, face wrecked, eyes furious with tears. "You're giving me chores."

"I'm giving you a job. Chores you do for other people. This one's yours."

Something shifted in her face—not softening exactly, more like a load finding a better place to sit. She wiped her face with her sleeve, stood up, and picked up the pipe again, deliberately this time, wrapping it in cloth before it went into the personal pack.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. Start with the tents. He numbered everything. If there's a ledger, it'll be in the strongbox, and if there's a strongbox it'll be—" She stopped. Stared at a heap of canvas near the canyon wall. "There. Under the red fly. He always—"

Her voice went again, but her feet kept moving, and that was the whole trick of it, the thing I'd learned in seven years of running: grief will follow you anywhere, but it walks slower than purpose. You just have to keep your feet moving faster than your heart wants to.

* * *

We found the ledger in the strongbox, right where she said. We found four journals, a brass theodolite wrapped in oilcloth, a bundle of letters tied with wire, and a surveyor's field kit with its instruments lovingly maintained despite everything—which told me Halvard had kept working, right up to whatever happened here. A man who stops maintaining his instruments has stopped believing in tomorrow. These were clean, oiled, calibrated.

And then Doss, poking along the shelf's far edge where the crust grew thickest, said, in a voice with no wry left in it at all:

"Ivo."

I knew before I turned. You always know. Seven years of deserts teaches you the specific quality of silence around a body, the way even the wind seems to step aside.

He was sitting.

That was the worst of it, at first—that he was sitting upright against the canyon wall, legs extended, hands resting on his thighs, in the posture of a man resting between tasks. The mineral crust had taken him completely. It sheathed him head to foot in that white-copper glass, thick on his shoulders, delicate as lace across his face, and through it you could see him—the shape of him, the beard, the closed eyes, the calm. The canyon had mummified him in mineral, layer on patient layer, until he looked less like a dead man than a statue of one, quarried whole from the living rock, waiting to be finished.

His jacket was buttoned to the throat. On his chest, pinned where a badge would go, was a folded square of paper, also crusted, its edges furred white.

"That's not how he died," Sera said. Her voice came out flat and terrible. "People don't die sitting up. People don't die *neatly*."

"They do," I said quietly, "if they know they're dying and they've made peace with where."

She rounded on me. "How would you know?"

"Because I've planned my own death a hundred times. Sitting down is what you choose when you want to be found properly." I crouched beside the body, keeping my hands off it—I wanted to touch the paper, and wanting to was exactly why I wouldn't, not yet, not with her watching. "Look at the camp. Fire ring intact. Instruments maintained. He wasn't trapped here. He stayed. He sent the others ahead or they left, and he stayed with whoever this is, and when it was done, he sat down against the wall facing the way he came, so anyone following the map would see him first."

"See him first," Doss repeated. "Like a marker."

"Like a message."

Sera knelt. Up close, the crust over the dead man's face was thin enough to show features—a young man, younger than Doss, jaw smooth under the sparse beard. She reached out and laid two fingers against the crystallized cheek, the way you'd check for fever, and I watched her understand, all at once and forever, that her father had spent his last months kneeling beside a dead boy in the dark, keeping him company, keeping the camp in order, writing in his journals, waiting either for rescue or for the end, and choosing dignity over panic the entire way down.

"This is Rennick," she said. "The youngest one. Father's letters—he mentioned a Rennick. He said he was *promising*." She laughed, one broken note with nothing funny in it. "Promising. He was twenty."

"Maybe twenty-two," Doss offered uselessly, and then shut up when he heard himself.

I looked past the body, deeper into the canyon, where the darkness went down and the sound of water rose up, vast and patient. Four names on Halvard's expedition roster. One body here. Which meant three more camps, or three more shelves, or three more places where the canyon had done its slow glasswork—and somewhere below us, if the map told true, the river itself, and whatever Halvard had hidden there worth killing a magistrate's reputation over.

"Sera." I kept my voice level, forward-leaning, the voice you use to point people at the horizon. "Rennick doesn't travel today. We wrap him, we cache him here, dry and sealed, and we carry him out on the return trip with proper rigging—we're not hauling a body down wet stone and dropping him. Same for the others, when we find them. Every one of them goes home. That's the oath, and oaths are paid in full, not in installments."

She stared at me. Grief and fury and something else fighting it out behind her eyes, and I braced for her to call it cowardice, logistics dressed up as mercy.

Instead she nodded once, sharp, and started pulling the spare canvas from her pack.

"Blankets won't survive the moisture," Doss began.

"Then wax them," Sera snapped. "You're the one who steals things. Steal us some wax."

And God help me, Doss grinned—grinned, down in a tomb, next to a glass-covered dead boy—because she'd sounded like her father just then, or like what I imagined her father sounded like, and sometimes the only comfort available in a canyon is discovering the dead left echoes.

* * *

We worked the rest of the day. Wrapped Rennick in waxed canvas, cached him in a crevice high on the shelf away from the seep-line, stacked the crates and covered them with the red fly weighted by stones. Catalogued the journals. By lantern light, while the others slept, I finally unpinned the folded paper from the dead man's chest and read it by the smallest flame I could make.

It wasn't a letter. It was a page from a survey log, in Halvard's hand, dated eight days after the last entry in the camp journal. Eight days Rennick had been dead, and Halvard had still been surveying.

*Day 41. R. gone in the night, peaceful, thirst took him gentle at the end. Buried him as best the canyon allows — the stone takes what the stone takes, and maybe that's a kind of grave. The others went up on Day 30 with the last of the water. I told them I'd follow. I lied. Someone has to stay with the instruments, and someone has to be the reason they send help back down. I am the reason. I have always been willing to be the reason.*

*Below, the river runs. I can hear it from here, louder each night, like it knows we've come. Tomorrow I go down the drowned staircase alone. If I find what I think is down there, I will hide it where only a stubborn person would look — because help, when it comes, will be stubborn, or it will not come at all.*

I sat there a long time with the page in my hands, listening to the water breathe in the dark below.

*Only a stubborn person would look.*

I folded the page, pinned it back where it belonged, and woke the others before dawn.

"Pack light," I said. "Today we go down to the river. And Sera—bring the strongbox key. Whatever your father hid, he hid it deep, and I intend to be stubborn enough to find it."

But when we reached the head of the drowned staircase—the final flight, slick and black, descending into a glow of phosphorescent mineral light and the roar of hidden water—we found the last iron pin sheared clean through, fresh-broken, the metal bright where it had snapped.

Someone had come down before us.

Recently.
