## 12. Water Under Stone

I heard the river before I saw it.

That's the part I keep returning to, in the nights since. Not the sight of it—though God knows the sight of it—but the sound, arriving through forty feet of stone like a rumor through a locked door. A low, insistent hush, the way a crowd sounds from outside a walled courtyard. Doss heard it first. He stopped so abruptly on the ledge that I walked into his back, and he didn't apologize, and neither did I, because we were both listening to the impossible.

"Is that—" he started.

"Water," Ivo said behind us. She said it the way other people say *landslide*.

We had come down the drowned staircase for the better part of two days—switchbacks cut into the canyon wall by hands that had been dead a decade, each step furred with salt bloom, the whole descent lit by Ivo's lamp and my father's map, which she'd stopped pretending she needed. The deeper we went, the stranger the air became. Warmer. Damper. It smelled of wet stone and something green and long-buried, the ghost of a garden. My father had breathed this air. That thought kept arriving and I kept setting it down, the way you set down a stone too heavy to carry and too heavy to drop.

Now, on a ledge no wider than a wagon bed, with the dark below us breathing, I picked the thought back up.

*He heard it too. He stood where I'm standing, and he heard it.*

"Rope's down to forty feet," Ivo said, all business, because Ivo was always all business, because feeling things in public was apparently a crime she'd already been convicted of once. "We descend the last of it in the dark. Nobody touches the walls without testing first. The mineral crust is sound stone until it isn't."

"And after that?" Doss asked. "What do we do if it's real?"

Ivo was quiet a moment. In the lamp light her face did the thing it always did—shut like a ledger closing. "Then we do what we came to do," she said, "and we don't celebrate in a hole in the ground."

But her hands, checking the rope knots, were not steady. I noticed. I'd gotten good at noticing her, the way you get good at noticing weather.

* * *

The last of the descent took an hour and cost us most of our nerve. The rope ran through a crack in the crust, down past shelves of stone that glittered where the lamplight touched them—gypsum, selenite, whole walls of the stuff, grown over centuries by water that wasn't supposed to exist. Ivo went first. Doss went second, chattering the whole way down, which was how Doss prayed. I went last, which meant I descended alone in the dark with the sound growing around me, and I will be honest with whoever ends up reading this: I cried. Just a little. Just from the noise of it. Ten years my father had been gone, and everyone in three settlements had agreed he was a fool, a dreamer, a man who'd chased a fairy story into the sand and gotten what dreamers get. My mother had agreed loudest of anyone, because grief makes liars of the people who loved you best.

And the whole time, the river had been down here. Roaring, almost, in its stone throat. Waiting for someone to stop calling him a liar.

My feet found stone floor. Ivo took my arm to steady me—briefly, impersonally, the way you'd steady a crate—and then the lamp came up, and I stopped being a person with a father and became a person standing at the edge of the world.

The cavern was a cathedral that no one had ever prayed in. It ran farther than the light could argue with, ribbed with flowstone, its ceiling lost in a haze of mineral glitter. And across its floor, filling a channel wide as a mill race, ran the river.

Not a seep. Not a trickle. A *torrent*—dark, glass-smooth where it ran deep, white-maned where it broke over stone, moving with the confidence of something that had never once doubted itself. The sound of it was everywhere at once, in the floor, in my teeth, in the hollow of my chest. Doss dropped to his knees at the edge and put his hand in it and laughed, actually laughed, a sound I hadn't heard out of him in three hundred miles.

"Cold," he said. "It's *cold*. Sera, it's cold, do you understand what—"

"I understand."

Ivo crouched at the bank a little downstream, apart from us, as she was always apart, and dipped two fingers in the current. She held them up to the lamp. Then she did something I had never seen her do. She closed her eyes. Just for a breath. Just long enough.

When she opened them, she was Ivo Amberdale again, and the moment was over, and I understood that I had just seen something she would deny to her grave.

"It's real," she said. "It was always real. Your father found it, and they called him a liar, and they let a hundred settlements go thirsty rather than admit it." She stood. Her voice had gone flat and careful, which I had learned to read as *furious*. "Now we find what else he left down here."

* * *

We found it an hour later, and I want to record, for accuracy, that none of us found it. The river found it for us.

We'd followed the channel downstream, Ivo in the lead with the lamp, looking for the second camp my father's map promised—a shelf of stone above the high-water line where the expedition had made its last stand against the desert above. We found the shelf. We found the cold ring of stones where they'd cooked. We found a rusted tin plate and a boot, and I had to look at the ceiling for a while, and nobody said anything, and I loved them a little for that.

And then Doss, who had gone wandering along the bank the way Doss wanders everywhere, shouted something garbled and triumphant, and we found him pointing at a seam in the flowstone where the water had cut back a curtain of mineral during some forgotten flood—and behind the curtain, dry as the day they'd been placed there, wrapped in waxed canvas and bound with tarred cord: my father's survey documents.

Ivo cut the cord. I couldn't. My hands had decided to be useless, and I let them.

There were eleven folios. Maps, drawn in my father's fast, confident hand—the hand from the letters, the hand from the margins of my childhood books. Cross-sections of the canyon. Flow measurements, dated, initialed, witnessed by two expedition members whose names I knew from the memorial lists. And then the documents that made Doss go silent, which was how I knew they mattered, and made Ivo go very still, which was how I knew they were dangerous.

Land grant abstracts. Certified copies, the kind you could only get from a magistrate's own archive. Grant registrations for the Amberdale valley and six neighboring watersheds, dated across a decade—and revocation orders stacked against them, each revocation bearing the same signature, the same seal, the same neat clerk's annotation in the margin: *water rights reserved to the crown pending survey.*

Pending survey. The survey that had been declared impossible. The survey my father had been laughed out of three assembly halls for proposing. The survey that had, apparently, been *completed*—by the magistrate's own office, years before my father ever walked into this canyon—and then buried, because a river that belonged to the crown on paper was worth a great deal more to certain men than a river that belonged to the people who were dying of its absence.

"He knew," Doss said. He was holding a page like it might bite him. "He didn't just find the water. He found out *why*—"

"He found out who," Ivo said.

She was reading the last folio, and her face had gone somewhere I couldn't follow. I stepped closer. The page was a letter, unfinished, undelivered, the ink faded to the color of weak tea.

*To the assembly of the settlements, from Halvard Brine, surveyor. I write this knowing it will likely be read by no one, and knowing also that the men who revoked the grants have read everything else I have ever written, which is why I write it here, forty feet under the stone they declared barren—*

I stopped reading out loud in my head, because my father's voice had started saying the words, and that was more than I could stand up under.

"They'll want this," Ivo said quietly. "The magistrate. The men behind him. The moment word of this river reaches the low country, there will be people on their way here whose job is to make sure these folios never reach an assembly hall. You understand that. Both of you. Look at me—*understand it.*"

"I understand it," Doss said. He sounded younger than he had all journey.

I looked at the river instead of at her. It went on breaking white over its stones, indifferent, magnificent, patient. Ten years it had run down here in the dark while the world above went thirsty, and it had not needed anyone's permission, and it had not needed anyone's belief, and it had outlasted every signature and every seal that had tried to erase it.

My father had found that. And they'd called him a fool. And I had, in my worst and quietest hours, agreed with them.

"We take everything," I said. "All of it. The abstracts, the revocations, the letter. We take it up and out and we put it in front of every delegate in the settlements, and we read the magistrate's signature out loud until the walls shake."

Ivo turned to look at me, and for once she didn't shut the ledger of her face. What was in it instead was something older and wearier—the look of someone who has stood exactly where I was standing, holding exactly what I was holding, and paid for it.

"You sound like your father," she said.

I almost thanked her. Then I heard what she'd left unsaid, hanging in the dripping dark between us: *and you remember what it cost him.*

Above us, faint through forty feet of stone, came a sound none of us could name at first—a knock, rhythmic, deliberate. Then again. Then a third time.

Ivo's hand went to the lamp and shuttered it, and we stood together in the roar of the hidden river, blind and listening, while somewhere on the surface, someone was knocking on our world.
