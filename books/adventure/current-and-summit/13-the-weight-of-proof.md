## 13. The Weight of Proof

I've been in a lot of arguments in my life. Most of them were about money, and most of them I lost, which is how you learn to stop arguing about money. This one wasn't about money. It was about a river that didn't officially exist, a magistrate who very much did, and whether the three of us were going to be heroes or corpses — and if corpses, whether we'd be useful ones.

It started, as bad ideas usually do, with Sera Brine reading aloud.

We'd made camp in the lee of the drowned staircase, back up on solid stone where the mineral crust didn't crunch underfoot like walking on beetles. The documents from Halvard's cache lay spread across a bedroll between us: survey folios, grant records, correspondence with seals I recognized because I'd once been paid to forge worse ones. Sera held a page up to the firelight and read it out in a voice that started steady and didn't stay that way.

"'By order of Magistrate Ellery Voss, land grants 44 through 61 are hereby revoked pending reassessment.'" She looked up. "Pending reassessment. Eleven years ago. There's no reassessment. There's no file. They revoked the grants and let the settlements dry out so the speculators could buy the land for nothing."

"Voss," I said. "That's the magistrate? That's the name?"

"You know him?"

"I know the name." I turned my hands toward the fire. "Everyone in the low country knows the name. He's the one who signs the drought relief orders. Same hand that takes the water away brings it back in barrels and charges by the cup." I shrugged. "I thought he was just greedy. Turns out he's thorough."

Sera was already sorting pages into piles with the fierce economy of someone who'd grown up around her father's expedition ledgers. Survey notes. Grant records. And then a third pile, thinner, that made her go quiet.

"What's that?" Ivo asked.

She didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice had changed. "Correspondence. Between Voss's office and the Ranger Corps." She held one out. "This one's signed by a Captain Corvane."

The fire popped. Ivo took the page without touching Sera's fingers, the way you take something that might bite.

*Re: Sunken Summit survey expedition, H. Brine commanding. Corps resources unavailable to support or recover. Recommend the matter be closed as a natural loss.*

"Dated four months after your father went missing," Ivo said quietly.

"He knew," Sera said. "Corvane knew Dad was out there and told the Corps to write him off." Her jaw worked. "Your people left him to die."

"My people," Ivo repeated. There was something in her voice I couldn't name yet. She set the page down with exaggerated care, like it was made of eggshell. "Yes."

And that's when the argument started, though none of us called it an argument at first. Arguments have rules. This was more like a flood finding the cracks in a wall.

"We take this home," Sera said, sweeping her arm over the piles. "All of it. We put it in front of the delegates at Copperwash and we read every word out loud until Voss is dragged off his bench in chains."

"And then what?" Ivo asked.

"Then justice."

"Justice needs a courtroom. Courtrooms need witnesses. Witnesses need to be alive." Ivo folded her hands. "You're proposing we walk three hundred miles of open desert carrying documents that ruin the most powerful man in the territory, past ranger patrols whose captain is named in those same documents, and we announce ourselves at the end of it. Is that about right?"

"It's about being *right*—"

"It's about being dead," Ivo said. "There's a difference, and the difference matters to me personally."

I want to be fair here. I've spent enough time with Ivo Amberdale now to know that she doesn't say things to wound. She says them the way a surgeon cuts — because the alternative is letting the rot spread. But fair doesn't make it comfortable to watch, and Sera Brine was not built for comfort. She was built the way her father built wells: deep, narrow, and under pressure.

"Cowardice," Sera said. "Dressed up as caution. You've had years of practice at running, Ranger. I can see why hiding comes easy."

The words landed. I'll give Sera that — she aimed true. I saw it hit Ivo somewhere below the ribs, in the place where she keeps everything she doesn't talk about. For a long moment the only sound was the fire and the wind moving through the canyon mouth above us, a low moan like the desert clearing its throat before it says something we won't like.

Then Ivo said, "Maybe," and picked up another document.

That's the thing about her nobody expects. She doesn't defend herself. She just absorbs the blow and goes back to work, and somehow that's more unnerving than shouting would've been.

* * *

Here's what I know about secrets, since apparently I'm the resident expert on taking things that don't belong to me: a secret is only worth what someone will pay for it, and the price is always higher than you think. I stole a magistrate's seal once — not Voss's, a lesser one, a customs man in Saltmere — because a broker promised me forty crowns and I was nineteen and hungry. What I got was a manhunt, a scar, and a lifelong education in how much powerful people will spend to keep a thing buried.

So when I looked at that bedroll full of papers, I didn't see justice. I saw a bounty with a ribbon on it.

"Let me say the ugly part," I said, when the silence had gone on long enough. Both women looked at me. "Nobody's said it yet, so I will. These papers are worth more than all three of our lives. To Voss, I mean. If he finds out they exist, he doesn't send a constable. He sends men who don't ask questions, and he pays them well, because the alternative is losing everything. We're not carrying evidence. We're carrying a price on our heads that hasn't been written up yet."

"Thank you, Doss," Sera said coldly, "for that inspiring contribution."

"I'm not done. The question isn't whether we expose him. The question is *when*, and *from where*. Because there's exposing a magistrate, and there's getting found dead in a ditch with a satchel of papers missing, and everyone says 'tragic, bandits, moving on.' Voss has done it before." I nodded at the thin third pile. "He did it to your father. 'Natural loss.' Four months, and the Corps closed the file. How many other expeditions closed like that?"

Sera opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked down at the correspondence again, and I watched the fight go out of her shoulders by degrees, not because she agreed but because she couldn't disprove it. Grief does that. It makes you want to burn everything down, and then it makes you do arithmetic.

"So we bury it," she said finally. "We carry it home like cowards and sit on it until it's safe. And when is it safe, exactly? Next year? Ten years? My father died for these pages. He starved in a hole in the ground protecting them, and you want to wait for a *convenient season*?"

"No," Ivo said.

Both of us looked at her. She'd been reading while we fought — of course she had; the woman reads like other people breathe — and now she laid two documents side by side on the bedroll. One was a grant record. The other was a map fragment, the same one Halvard had pressed into her hands at the dry well, the one that had started all of this.

"We don't bury it and we don't shout it. We *file* it." She tapped the grant record. "Water claims in this territory don't go through magistrates. They go through the delegate assembly — old law, older than Voss's office. A claim filed with proof of source and beneficial use binds every settlement upstream and down. If we file the Summit's water in the expedition's name, with Halvard's surveys attached, then the river becomes public record. Voss can't revoke what he never owned. And once the claim exists, the documents aren't a secret anymore. They're a *foundation*. You can't murder your way out from under a filed claim without the whole territory noticing."

Sera stared at her. "You've thought about this."

"I've had a lot of nights," Ivo said, "and very little to lose."

"But the truth about Voss — the revocations, the letters—"

"Travel with the claim. Attached. Read into the record when the assembly sits, with witnesses present, with the claim already protected. Not before. Not on open ground with three hundred miles behind us and his riders ahead." She met Sera's eyes. "I'm not asking you to hide your father's work. I'm asking you to build walls around it before you light the beacon. A beacon on a hill gets shot at. A beacon inside a fortress starts a war you win."

It was good. I'll admit it even though admitting it cost me something, because I'd been quietly on Sera's side and Ivo had just taken the ground out from under both of us. It wasn't cowardice. It was the difference between throwing a punch and building a fist first.

Sera sat with it for a long time. The fire burned down. Somewhere above us, water sang in the dark — the buried river, still going about its business under a hundred feet of dead stone, indifferent to all of us.

"If we wait," Sera said at last, quieter, "and anything happens to these papers — if we lose them, if someone takes them — then he died for nothing. That's what waiting means to me. Every day we carry this and don't use it is a day his death buys nothing."

"That's not what waiting means," Ivo said. "Waiting means his death buys everything, once, permanently, instead of something, briefly, and then nothing forever. I know the difference. I've lived on the wrong side of it." She paused, and for a second the mask slipped and I saw how tired she was, bone-tired, years tired. "I ran from a conviction for seven years because I wanted to clear my name *now*, every single day, and wanting it fast nearly got me killed a dozen times. The times I survived were the times I waited. I hate waiting. I'm the worst waiter you'll ever meet. And I'm telling you: wait."

* * *

We didn't resolve it that night. Arguments like that don't resolve; they just go dormant, like snakes in cold weather, and you keep your boots on around them.

Sera took first watch, which meant she took first watch alone and angry, sitting at the canyon mouth with her back to us, which was its own kind of statement. I banked the fire and lay down and discovered that sleep had no interest in me whatsoever.

After a while I heard movement. Ivo, moving quietly along the wall of the camp, checking the packs — checking *her* pack, specifically, where the documents now lived wrapped in oilcloth against rain and sweat and worse. She moved like a woman counting her own heartbeat.

"Doss," she said, without turning around. "You're awake."

"I'm a criminal. We sleep light. Occupational habit."

"Mm." She crouched by her pack, adjusting straps that didn't need adjusting. Then, to the fire, or to the dark, or possibly to me: "She's right, you know. About the cowardice."

I propped myself up on an elbow. "You just spent an hour arguing the opposite."

"I argued the smart position. Doesn't mean it's the honest one." She tied off a strap. "Every year I waited to clear my name, someone else got convicted the way I was. Someone else lost their land the way my family did. Waiting protects the thing you're carrying. It doesn't protect anyone else." She looked over at me, and the firelight caught the lines of her face, and she looked older than any ranger I'd ever met and younger than her own eyes. "But dead witnesses expose no one. So we wait. And we move fast, and we get home, and then we stop waiting all at once. Can you live with that?"

"I can live with lots of things," I said. "It's my best skill."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face and was gone. She settled back against the rock, and I thought she was done talking, but then she said, quietly, "Halvard chose me for this. Out of everyone on the flats. I've been wondering all week why, and tonight I think I stopped wondering." She didn't explain, and I didn't ask, because some doors you can hear locking from the outside.

I lay back down and stared at the stars through the canyon gap and did my own arithmetic. Three of us. Three hundred miles. One satchel worth more than the territory. One captain named in the letters who was, at that moment, presumably somewhere behind us or ahead of us — and knowing rangers, knowing Corvane by reputation if nothing else, I'd have bet my last stolen coin on *ahead*.

I was almost asleep when Sera's voice came down from the canyon mouth, tight and low.

"Doss. Ivo. Get up."

I was on my feet before the second word finished, which is another occupational habit. Sera was standing at the rim, pointing east across the dark, and I followed her finger to a point on the far ridge where — for just a moment, maybe two — a light had shown. Small. Steady. Deliberate. The kind of light a man shows when he wants to be seen by exactly one person and no one else.

It blinked twice. Paused. Blinked twice again.

"Ivo," Sera whispered. "Is that a signal?"

Ivo stood very still, watching the ridge, and when she answered, her voice was flat and calm in the way I'd learned meant the opposite.

"Yes," she said. "And it's mine."
