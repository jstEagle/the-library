## 15. Corvane Catches Up

I had been a ranger for nineteen years, and in all that time I had learned exactly one universal truth: the desert does not care about your paperwork.

The magistrate's orders did, though. The magistrate cared very much about paperwork—about which documents existed and which had been, in his phrase, "resolved." I had carried his resolution order in my breast pocket for eleven days now, the way a man carries a stone he means to throw and cannot quite bring himself to throw. *Burn the survey materials on recovery. No copies. No testimony. The Summit file is closed.*

Closed. As if a river could be closed. As if a hundred dead settlers could be closed.

I sat my mule on the ridge above the canyon mouth and watched the three of them make camp below—Amberdale, the Brine woman, and the thief Doss, whose name I'd pulled from a warrant list so old it was nearly an antique. They moved like people who had been moving too long. Even from this distance I could read the exhaustion in the way they pitched the shelter: fast, ugly, functional. Nobody arguing about tent placement anymore. That's what the flats do to you. They argue on your behalf until you stop having opinions.

I should have brought my men. That was the correct procedure. Six riders, sealed exits, the whole liturgy of arrest. I had done it before—I had done it to *her*, nine years ago, with a warrant in one hand and a bad conscience I refused to examine in the other—and it had worked exactly as procedures work, which is to say it had produced paperwork and destroyed a life and changed nothing that mattered.

Instead I had sent my men east on a false trail I'd fabricated myself, told them the fugitives had cut toward the salt sinks, and ridden out alone with a mule loaded with water and a head full of things I was not authorized to know.

My superiors would call this treason by dinnertime tomorrow. Assuming there was a tomorrow where anyone bothered to ask after me.

* * *

Let me tell you how a man like me arrives at a canyon mouth alone, because it did not happen all at once. It happened in ledgers.

Rangers keep ledgers. Water drawn, grain issued, miles patrolled, incidents logged. It is the spine of the corps—everything witnessed, everything weighed. Nine years ago I entered into my ledger a single line regarding Ivo Amberdale: *Apprehended at Duncastle ferry. Charge: falsification of land grant records. Evidence secured.* Clean line. Tidy handwriting. What I did not enter—because there was no column for it—was that the evidence had been handed to me already bundled, already sworn, already summarized by the magistrate's own clerks, and that I had been too young and too ambitious to ask why a land clerk's daughter with no history of forgery had suddenly developed one overnight.

What I also did not enter, four years later, when I made senior tracker and got access to the deep files, was the memo I found stapled inside the Sunken Summit survey—the original survey, Halvard Brine's survey, the one that mapped flowing water under a mountain everyone official had declared dry. The memo was two sentences long. *Survey findings inconsistent with grant valuations. Recommend reclassification of region as non-viable. File under drought assessment.*

Non-viable. I have thought about those two words more than any scripture. A man finds a river—a *river*—and some clerk upstream decides the finding is inconvenient to men who had bought up the dry land cheap, and so the river becomes a rumor, the rumor becomes nothing, and the settlements that might have drunk from it spend twenty years scraping lichen off stones and burying their children small.

And then, last month, a dead prospector's map fragment crossed my desk with Amberdale's name attached to a supply purchase in a border town, and I understood that the past had not finished with any of us. It had merely been waiting for us all to be in the same canyon.

* * *

I came down off the ridge at dusk, when the light goes copper and every shadow lies about its length. I wanted them to see me coming. There is a difference between an ambush and an approach, and I had spent nine years on the wrong side of that distinction without knowing it.

Doss saw me first—he was on watch, badly, chewing something and staring at the middle distance instead of the approaches—and he came up out of his crouch with a knife and a yelp that I will charitably describe as a warning cry. Then Amberdale was there, and I will say this for her: she did not reach for a weapon. She stood between me and the camp with her hands loose at her sides and her eyes doing arithmetic.

"Corvane," she said.

"Amberdale."

"You're alone."

"I'm alone."

"That's either very brave or very stupid."

"In my experience they're usually the same thing wearing different hats." I held my hands wide, away from my sidearm, and let her look at me. Let her look as long as she needed. She had spent nine years being hunted by my signature; she had earned a long look at the actual face. "I have water. Four days' worth, and feed for your mule if you still have the mule."

"We have the mule," said Sera Brine, emerging from the shelter with a rifle held in the casual-but-not-casual way of someone who has recently discovered what rifles are for. "You can put the water down and back up the ridge."

"Sera," Amberdale said quietly. Not a command. An assessment. She was deciding whether to let me live, and she was letting her dead father's daughter weigh in, which told me something about how far that particular thaw had progressed since the second camp.

"I rode past the place where you burned your supplies," I said to Amberdale. "Clever. I had trackers convinced for two days that you were dead. Then I found the boot print under the overhang, left foot, heel worn outer edge, and I stopped being convinced and started being ashamed, because I taught half the corps that trick and I should have looked sooner."

"Why are you here?" Amberdale asked. No warmth in it. I hadn't earned warmth. But no fear either, and that was new—that was nine years newer than the last time we'd stood facing each other, when she'd been nineteen and furious and I'd been the law arriving on schedule.

So I told her the truth, which is a thing I recommend trying once, late, when all the alternatives have failed.

I told her about the memo. I told her about the resolution order in my pocket, and I took it out and held it up so she could see the seal, and then—to her visible disbelief—I walked forward and set it on a rock at the edge of their firelight and stepped back. "That's the order I was given. Burn whatever Brine found. No copies. No testimony. You'll notice it doesn't say *arrest the carriers*. Dead couriers don't need arresting. My superiors assumed I'd understand that. They were right, for nine years. Then I read Halvard Brine's original survey, and I stopped understanding things on purpose."

Sera had gone very still. "You've seen my father's survey."

"I've seen the parts they didn't burn in the capital archive. Someone there—someone with access—pulled pages before the purge and hid them in a mislabeled box. I found them looking for something else. Isn't that always the way. You go hunting one crime and trip over a bigger one wearing its coat."

"The magistrate," Amberdale said. Not a question.

"Aldous Veyne. Yes." I watched her face when I said the name. Nine years ago she had screamed it in a courtroom while they took her away, and everyone had treated it as grief talking. I had treated it as grief talking. I had written it up as *defendant became emotional*. God. Ledgers. "He revoked the grants. He wrote the region down as dead. And then, through four intermediaries I can name, he and his partners acquired the same 'dead' land for less than a tenth of its value with water rights attached—water rights to a river the official record says doesn't exist. Your family's case, Amberdale. The records you supposedly forged? I've read the originals now. They weren't forgeries. They were corrections. Somebody in your father's office noticed the grant dates had been altered and fixed them, and the fixing was the crime, because the alteration was the profit."

The fire cracked. Somewhere up-canyon, wind moved through stone with a sound like slow breathing. Sera Brine lowered the rifle maybe two inches, which in her language was an embrace.

"You knew," Amberdale said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone handling something sharp. "How long?"

"Four years. Knowing and knowing are different animals. I *knew* the way you know a load-bearing wall is rotten—you adjust around it, you don't lean on it, you tell yourself the building isn't yours to condemn. I closed cases. I filed reports. I used the word *non-viable* myself twice, in writing, because the phrasing came down from above and I—" I stopped. There was no elegant version of the next sentence. "I did my duty, is what I'm telling you. That's the confession. I did my duty for nineteen years and it turns out my duty was a shovel."

* * *

We sat at the fire because sitting is harder to lie during than standing. Doss cooked—turned out the thief could make a passable stew from dried roots and shame—and ate with the wary speed of a man who has never fully trusted a meal and isn't starting tonight. Sera sat with her father's journal in her lap, closed, one hand flat on its cover like a blessing or a restraint.

"So here's my offer," I said. "And I want to be clear that I've thought about every angle of it for eleven days of riding, and every angle ends with me ruined, so understand that I'm not negotiating from strength. I'm negotiating from the only position that matters, which is that I'm right and I can prove part of it."

"Part," Amberdale said.

"Part. I have the memo. I have the resolution order—which is itself evidence, an order to destroy a federal survey signed and sealed. I have the chain of intermediaries on the land purchases, names and dates, from the archive pulls. What I don't have is the survey itself, the field originals, Brine's own hand. Copies can be called forgeries. That's the beautiful thing about what they did to your family—it established the playbook. Any document that threatens them becomes a fake. Unless it comes out of the ground with the bodies that died carrying it, in front of witnesses nobody can disappear." I nodded at the journal. "Unless it comes home the hard way."

"And your testimony," Sera said slowly.

"My testimony is worth something and they know it. Senior tracker, twenty years' service, the man who arrested Amberdale originally. When I stand up in front of the delegates and say *I was used, here's how, here's the paper trail*, it lands differently than when a convicted forger says it. That's not fair. Fairness has never once shown up to one of these proceedings. But it's true, and true is useful." I turned my cup in my hands. "In exchange, I want what you have. Everything. The documents, the journal, the samples. And I want to walk it in beside you—not ahead of you, not behind you with a weapon. Beside."

"Why should we believe you?" Doss asked. First thing he'd said all evening. "No offense, Ranger, but you're the villain from the ballad, showing up alone at the fire. In the ballads this is where somebody gets knifed."

"No offense taken. In the ballads the villain also has better boots." I pulled off one boot and tossed it into the firelight between us, sole up, showing the worn heel. "Left boot. Matches the print under the overhang. If I'd meant to bring the corps, I'd have brought the corps; they're two days east chasing a trail I invented, and by the time they figure that out, we'll either be at the assembly or dead, and either way my career is a smudge. I disobeyed a direct order to be here. There's no version of this where I go back to being what I was. That's my collateral. I've burned my own supplies, you might say."

Amberdale almost smiled. Almost. It was there and gone like fish in shallow water.

"It's not enough," she said. "Your word. Your papers. I spent nine years learning that men like you arrive with papers and leave with people. Why are you really here, Corvane? Not the ledger answer. The real one."

And that was the question, wasn't it. I looked into the fire and let myself answer it honestly, because I had come too far and ruined too much to do otherwise.

"Because I arrested you," I said, "and I have thought about it every day for nine years, and I finally understand why. Not because I doubted the conviction—shamefully, I didn't doubt it for years. Because of how you looked at me. Everyone else in that courtroom looked at me like I was the weather. You looked at me like I was a *choice*. Like somewhere behind the warrant there was a man who could have asked one question and didn't. And you were right, and I hated you for it, and then I forgot about it, and then I found that memo, and I remembered, and I haven't slept properly since." I set the cup down. "I'm here because I owe a debt to a woman I helped ruin, and because a hundred settlements are drinking dust while rich men sit on a river, and because I am fifty-one years old and I would like, before I die, to have done one thing in this uniform that I'd have done even if nobody was watching. That's the real answer. It's not noble. Nobility would have come earlier."

Silence. The wind breathed in the rocks. Sera's hand had come off the journal and was gripping her own knee instead.

Amberdale studied me for a long moment, and I recognized the look—she was doing what I had done with the rotten wall, testing where to lean. Then she said, "If we take you, you follow my lead. Not the corps' procedure. Mine. We move at night through the Narrows, we split the evidence, and if anyone springs a trap, you stand between us and it. Those are the terms."

"Those are acceptable terms."

"And Corvane—if you're playing us, if there are riders over that ridge—"

"There aren't. And if there were, I'd deserve whatever you do about it." I paused. "But there's something you should know, since we're trading truths. I didn't come entirely clean. Veyne didn't just issue the burn order. He sent his own man to confirm the destruction. A fixer named Hale Marrow, ex-corps, dismissed for cruelty, currently employed as a 'land consultant.' He's in the territory. He was a day behind me on the east trail before I bent it south. He doesn't negotiate. I've read his file. He resolves things."

I watched the word land on all three of them. *Resolves.* Sera's jaw tightened. Doss stopped chewing. Amberdale went very still, the stillness of someone recalculating every mile between here and the pass.

"How far behind?" she asked.

"When I bent the trail, a day. He's smart, but he's smart like a hammer is smart. He'll straighten out my bend within a day of reaching it. So call it—we're two days ahead, maybe less, and the pass closes in four."

Amberdale looked at the fire, then at Sera, then at the dark mouth of the canyon behind us, where her promise and my shame and a dead prospector's river all lay buried together. When she spoke again, her voice had the flat calm I remembered from the courtroom, but aimed differently now—aimed forward.

"Then we break camp in an hour," she said. "And Ranger Corvane—you'd better hope the delegates believe in redemption."

"Ma'am," I said, "I'm betting my life that they believe in paperwork. Redemption's above my pay grade."

* * *

We broke camp in the dark, four travelers and a mule and a dead man's truth, moving toward the Narrows with a hammer named Marrow somewhere behind us and the dry season closing like a fist ahead. As I tightened the mule's girth, I took out my ledger—one last habit, one last entry—and by firelight I wrote beneath twenty years of tidy lines:

*Day 19. Disobeyed orders. Joined the fugitives. Evidence intact. No regrets recorded.*

There was no column for regret anyway. There never had been.
