## 16. The Long Way Home

My father weighed nothing.

That was the first thing I understood on the morning we left the Summit, and it was the cruelest. Halvard Brine had been a big man—shoulders like a doorframe, hands that could crack geodes open the way other men cracked eggs—and the desert had spent weeks reducing him to something I could lift alone if I had to. The mineral crust had preserved him, yes, but preservation in the deep desert is just a polite word for drying out. We wrapped him in canvas and his own bedroll, and when Ivo and I lifted him together for the first time, I felt the whole of my father through the wrapping, and he was light as kindling.

"He'd have hated that," I said.

"He'd have hated being carried at all," Ivo said. "He'd have wanted to walk."

"Then we'll walk fast enough for both of us."

She looked at me then—a long look, the kind she rationed out like water—and nodded once, and that was the closest thing to a eulogy anyone gave him. My father would have appreciated the economy of it. He was a man who believed sentiment should be carried the way you carry water: only as much as you need, and never spilled.

We were eleven souls leaving the canyon mouth that morning. Ivo. Me. Doss, walking easier now than he had any right to, given everything. Corvane, who still moved like a man expecting to be arrested by himself. His four rangers, who had spent two days deciding whether they were our captors or our escort and had mostly settled on neither. And four of Halvard's old crew—the ones whose remains we hadn't found, because there were no remains to find. Veyra, Osk, Tam, and a silent, enormous man called Bracken who had been Halvard's powder-man back when there was powder to manage. They had come down into the canyon following Corvane's trail and Corvane's conscience, and they had found us standing over their captain's body with a river roaring under our boots and a satchel full of paper that explained why their expedition had been written off as a failure before it ever left.

Veyra had cried exactly once, briefly, and then started issuing orders about rope anchors, and I understood immediately why my father had trusted her with his life.

Now we had three days of hard country between us and the Narrows, and beyond the Narrows, the high pass—and the pass did not care about grief or justice or the weight of paper. When the dry season came down off the peaks, it came down like a slammed door. Ivo had stood at the canyon rim that first morning and read the sky the way other people read letters from home, and whatever she saw there had put a number in her voice: *four days, if the wind holds.*

So we walked.

* * *

You learn things about people on a forced march that you couldn't learn in ten years of polite acquaintance.

I learned that Doss talks when he's frightened and goes quiet when he's calm, which meant that by the second day, his silence was the most encouraging sound in the column. I learned that Corvane counted steps under his breath—not pacing himself, but punishing himself, some arithmetic of penance only he knew the sum of. I learned that Bracken hummed when he climbed, tuneless and low, and that the sound made Veyra's jaw tighten every time, because apparently my father used to hum the same way, and grief will ambush you through your ears when it can't get at you any other way.

And I learned—I was learning it still, hour by hour, mile by mile—what Ivo Amberdale looked like when she wasn't running.

It was a strange thing to witness. Everything about her had always been angled toward departure: the way she slept in her boots, the way she never fully unpacked, the way her eyes went to the horizon every few minutes like a woman checking on a debt. But somewhere between the buried river and the canyon mouth, between Halvard's journal pages and Corvane's white-knuckled offer of testimony, she had stopped checking the horizon quite so often. She still led from the front. She still gave orders like she was handing out rations—measured, no waste. But there was a new stillness under it. Purpose does that, I suppose. A person running from something moves one way; a person moving toward something moves the same direction, but differently. You can feel the difference in the ground.

The documents rode against my chest. That had been decided without discussion, which is to say Ivo had looked at me and I had looked at Ivo and the matter was settled. The survey papers, the grant revocations, the correspondence between Magistrate Ellery Vance and the land consortium that had bought up a hundred dry homesteads for pennies once the water was taken away—all of it, sealed in oilcloth, strapped flat against my sternum under my shirt. If we were attacked, I was to run. Not fight. Run. I had agreed to this with my jaw set, and privately resolved to do no such thing, and I suspect Ivo knew it, and suspected that was why she chose me. There is a particular kind of trust in giving the most important thing you have to the person most likely to die defending it.

The water samples rode with Doss. Four sealed flasks of river water, wrapped in wool, packed in sand in a padded case he wore across his back like a hunchback's burden. He complained about it constantly, cheerfully, the way he complained about everything, and I think the complaining was how he held onto it. Doss had stopped a saboteur's knife with his forearm back at the second camp—no. No, that hadn't happened yet. Forgive me; the days ran together out there, and memory in the deep desert is a dishonest companion. What I mean to say is that Doss guarded those flasks like a man guarding his own pardon, which in a sense he was. Every settlement delegate who tasted that water would be tasting the end of the drought, and the end of the drought was the end of the world that had made thieves necessary.

* * *

The injury happened on the afternoon of the second day, and it happened to the last person any of us could afford to lose.

We were crossing a shelf of broken rock above a dry wash—scree country, the kind of ground that shifts underfoot like a lie—when the shelf shifted. Not dramatically. No cinematic roar of falling stone. Just a soft settling, a whisper of gravel, and Tam, who had been ranging ahead to pick the line, dropped half a stride and caught himself wrong.

He didn't cry out. That was the first bad sign. Men who cry out are men whose bodies still believe help is coming. Tam made a sound like a man biting down on a coin, sat down very carefully on a rock, and began unlacing his boot with the deliberate calm of someone performing a task rather than facing a truth.

His ankle was wrong. Not broken—Ivo's hands went over it with a field medic's economy and pronounced it "sprained, badly, ligaments torn or stretched to uselessness"—but in the deep desert, on scree, at forced-march pace, the distinction was academic. A broken ankle sets. A ruined ankle simply declines, day after day, until the man attached to it cannot walk at all.

"How far to the Narrows?" Tam asked, while Veyra splinted it.

Ivo looked at the sky, then at the sun, then did the arithmetic she always did, lips barely moving. "A day and a half. At our current pace."

"And at what pace?"

Nobody answered him. Nobody had to. We all knew what pace we were making, and we all knew what pace the pass required, and the difference between them was the width of a closing door.

Here is the brutal arithmetic nobody spoke aloud, so I will speak it now, because somebody has to: eleven people move slower than ten. An injured man slows a column not just by his own halting steps but by the others who shorten their strides for him, who steady him on the bad ground, who take turns bearing his pack. And the pass did not care about our decency. The pass had a schedule written in snowmelt and wind, and it intended to keep it.

"We leave him," Corvane said.

It was not cruelty in his voice. It was worse than cruelty—it was procedure. He said it the way he must once have said it writing reports, flat and clean, a sentence shaped to survive review. "We leave him with water and shelter at the wash, mark the location, and send back for him once the documents are delivered. It's the correct decision."

"The correct decision," Veyra repeated, in a voice that could have tanned hides.

"It's the correct decision," Corvane said again, and here is the thing—he was right. By every measure a ranger corps uses, by every ledger of lives weighed against outcomes, he was right. One man's ankle against a hundred settlements' water. One man's comfort against the closing of the only pass. Any officer I had ever heard of would have made the same call and slept fine afterward.

Ivo crouched beside Tam's splinted leg and said nothing for a long time.

I watched her, and I want to be honest about what I saw, because what I saw changed how I understood everything that came after. I saw a woman doing the same arithmetic Corvane had done. I saw her arrive at the same answer. And then I saw her look at Tam—at his face, at the careful blankness he was wearing, the same armor I had worn at my father's funeral—and I saw her set the answer down and pick up a different question entirely.

"What can you walk on?" she asked Tam.

"Anything," Tam said. "Slowly."

"Slowly isn't a speed. It's a direction." She stood. "Bracken. How much does the captain weigh?"

Bracken blinked. "Wrapped? Forty pounds. Maybe forty-five."

"And the case of samples?"

"Twenty," Doss said, patting it. "Twenty-five if you count the wool."

Ivo turned a slow circle, looking at each of us in turn, and I realized she was weighing us—not our loyalty, not our courage, but our actual physical capacity, the way a mule driver weighs a load. "Then we redistribute," she said. "Tam carries nothing but his own water and his sticks. His pack splits between Bracken and Osk. The captain—" and here her voice did something small and strange, a hitch she covered instantly, "—the captain rotates. Two carriers at a time, hour on, hour off. Everyone else takes a share of the difference."

"That slows us anyway," Corvane said. "Carrying him costs more than the packs did."

"Yes," Ivo said.

"That's not an argument."

"No," Ivo agreed. "It's a fact. The argument is that we don't leave people." She looked at him then, straight on, and I saw Corvane receive that look like a man receiving a bill he'd known was coming. "You wrote men off once. All of them. On paper, in a report, with a signature. It was the correct decision too, wasn't it? Approved at every level. And it bought a hundred settlements a decade of thirst and made a magistrate rich." She let that sit. "We are not doing the correct decision. We're doing the right one. They cost different amounts, and I know which one I'm willing to pay."

Corvane held her gaze for a long moment. Then he did something I did not expect: he unbuckled his own pack, pulled out the heaviest thing in it—a coil of climbing line no one had asked him to bring—and slung it across his shoulders next to his rifle.

"I'll take first hour with the captain," he said.

Nobody cheered. Nobody thanked him. Veyra just looked at him for a moment with an expression I couldn't read, and then began re-rigging the carrying harness with Bracken, and that was that. That was the whole ceremony. But something had changed in the column's spine, some vertebra slipping quietly into place, and we moved out faster than we had moved all day, and I am certain—I remain certain to this day—that it was because we were no longer eleven people going the same direction. We were eleven people going one direction.

* * *

We made the mouth of the Narrows as the light failed.

The Narrows is a misnomer, the way so many desert names are gentle lies. It is not narrow. It is a throat—a two-mile corridor between walls of red stone where the entire watershed of the high country squeezes through on its way down, and where, in wet years, the flash floods come through with enough force to move boulders the size of wagons. In dry season it was merely a defile, shadowed early, cold early, the wind funneled through it with a low continuous moan like the country clearing its throat before saying something unpleasant.

But it was also the only way through to the pass, and the walls on either side were perfect ambush country, and everyone in the column knew it, and nobody said it.

We camped just short of the entrance, close enough to see the first hundred yards of corridor glowing dull red in the last light. Ivo set watches in pairs—her rules, no argument—and took second watch herself, and I lay next to my father's wrapped body with the documents pressing flat and familiar against my chest, listening to the wind moan in the stone, and tried to sleep.

Sleep declined the invitation. So instead I listened to the camp breathe. Doss snored like a man with a clear conscience, which after everything was either inspiring or alarming. Bracken hummed in his sleep, tuneless, and this time Veyra, on watch, didn't tighten her jaw—she listened to it with her eyes closed, letting it ambush her on purpose. Corvane sat awake at the edge of the firelight, cleaning a rifle that was already clean, and I understood that he was not keeping watch on the dark. He was keeping watch on himself, making sure the man who had said *we leave him* stayed buried.

Ivo appeared beside me sometime in the black hours. I hadn't heard her cross the camp—that was the thing about her; she moved like a debt coming due, silently and inevitably. She crouched, checked the oilcloth binding at my chest with two fingers, professional as a saddle check.

"You should sleep," I whispered.

"So should you."

"I'm busy having a conversation with my father. He's not much of a talker. Takes after me."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. On Ivo Amberdale's face, it qualified as laughter. Then it faded, and she looked past me, at the Narrows, where the wind was moaning its long note.

"Sera." Her voice was very quiet. "If anything happens in there tomorrow—if we're separated—you don't wait for us. You don't come back for the captain, you don't come back for me. You go through, and you keep going, and you deliver."

"I know the assignment."

"Do you accept it?"

I looked at her. In the dark her face was all angles and patience, and behind the patience was the thing I had finally stopped resenting and started recognizing: she asked because she meant it. Because she had learned, in whatever hard school had graduated her, that orders given without consent breed mutiny, and consent freely given breeds something stronger than obedience.

"I accept it," I said. "But I want it on the record that I'll hate every step of it."

"Noted for the record," she said, and rose, and paused. "For what it's worth—your father chose you. Out of everyone he might have chosen. He read people the way I read weather. Whatever he saw in you, it was real."

She melted back into the dark before I could decide whether to thank her, which was probably the point.

I lay back down. Against my chest, the documents rose and fell with my breathing, patient as a heartbeat that wasn't mine. Somewhere north of us, snow was failing to fall on a pass that was already beginning to close. Somewhere south, a magistrate who did not yet know his empire was folded into oilcloth against a dead prospector's daughter was sleeping soundly in a house with good water.

And somewhere below us, at the mouth of the Narrows, in the last gray hour before dawn, a lantern flickered.

Not our lantern. Our fires were banked to coals. This light came from inside the corridor itself—one small flame, bobbing, moving. Coming toward us. Or waiting for us.

I was on my feet before I'd finished deciding to stand, and by the time I reached Ivo's side at the camp's edge, she was already there, already watching, her hand resting easy on her rifle in a way that managed to be both an offer and a warning.

"Well," Doss murmured, appearing at my elbow with the sample case already strapped to his back, because even asleep, apparently, the man kept his appointments. "Either that's a friend, or it's the worst-lit ambush in the history of banditry."

The lantern bobbed closer. And in its glow, I caught the shape of whoever carried it—and felt the bottom drop out of the night.
