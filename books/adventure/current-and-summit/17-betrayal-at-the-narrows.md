## 17. Betrayal at the Narrows

I've been robbed twice in my life. Once by a magistrate's clerk who took my father's deed and called it "administrative correction," and once by a woman with a crossbow on the Salt Flats who turned out to be Ivo Amberdale. Of the two, I respected the second more. At least she was honest about what she was taking.

So you'd think I'd know a thief when I saw one.

You'd be wrong. That's the thing about the Narrows — it narrows everything. Your sightline, your options, your judgment. The canyon walls came together there like two hands closing around a throat, close enough that Sera could touch both sides at once if she stretched, and the light went from gold to a thin gray ribbon overhead, and every footstep echoed three times before it died. Perfect country for an ambush. Also perfect country for a man to fall behind without anyone noticing.

Which is exactly what happened to me, because I was the one falling behind. My boots had been giving up for two days — sole flapping like a dying bird on the left one — and I'd taken to walking at the rear so nobody had to watch my gait. Pride is a luxury. I'd learned that young. But it turns out even a man without pride likes to limp in private.

That's how I ended up twenty paces behind the others when I heard the whisper.

Not a word. A sound like paper being handled carefully. And in that canyon, where a pebble shifting sounded like a landslide, careful paper-handling was the loudest thing in the world.

I stopped. The others kept going, their echoes braiding away ahead of them — Ivo's steady tread, Sera's quicker one, Corvane's deliberate march, the mule's disgruntled clopping. On the mule's back, wrapped in oilcloth inside a saddlebag, rode the only reason any of us were still alive: Halvard Brine's survey documents. The proof. The whole rotten architecture of the drought, written down in a dead man's careful hand, with seals and signatures and dates going back eleven years.

The whisper came again, from ahead of me this time. From beside the mule.

Now, here's what I should have done: shouted. Ivo was thirty paces off. One good yell and it was over. But shouting in the Narrows brings down rock, and Ivo had drilled that into us until we all dreamed about it, and so instead of doing the sensible cowardly thing, I did the stupid brave thing, which is apparently what happens to a man after enough weeks in the desert. He stops calculating. He just moves.

I moved.

* * *

Let me tell you what I saw, because I've gone over it a hundred times since, lying awake under stars or canvas, and the picture never changes.

Corvane's man — Vessick, his name was, though none of us used names much among that lot — had drifted to the mule's flank the way he'd been drifting for days. Quiet man. Competent. The kind of soldier who checks his own bootlaces twice and never complains about rations. When Corvane had caught up to us at the canyon mouth and offered his deal — his testimony for ours — he'd brought Vessick and one other, and Ivo had accepted them the way you accept a snake into your bedroll: carefully, and with your eyes open.

She'd watched Vessick. I'd seen her watching him. But watching isn't the same as stopping, not when you need every pair of hands to get the mule through scree fields, and Vessick's hands were very good.

He had the saddlebag open. He had the oilcloth folded back. In his other hand was a flask of something that smelled wrong even from where I stood — sharp, chemical, the smell of a lamp shop burning. Lamp oil, maybe, or worse. He was pouring it into the documents with the tenderness of a man watering a garden.

And here's the detail that has stayed with me, that I keep turning over like a stone in my pocket: he wasn't hurrying. A panicked man slops and spills. Vessick poured like he was following instructions. Which meant somewhere behind us, beyond the pass, beyond the magistrate, someone had given him instructions. You don't write careful orders for a small crime. Small crimes are improvised. This had been planned by people who planned things.

I didn't think any of that at the time, mind you. At the time I thought approximately nothing. What I did was grab the nearest object — a length of broken tent pole we'd been lashing to the pack frame — and swing it at the back of his head like a man swatting a fly the size of a horse.

I missed. Or rather, I hit his shoulder, which spun him around, which meant the flask went wide and splashed across the canyon wall instead of the papers, which was lucky, but luck is just fate showing off, and fate wasn't finished with me.

Vessick was fast. Faster than me, faster than anyone I'd met except Ivo. He dropped the flask and had a knife out before the flask finished hitting the ground, and then we were grappling in the dark of the Narrows, two fools wrestling over a saddlebag while the most important pieces of paper in the territory soaked in lamp oil ten inches from our elbows.

I got my hands on his knife wrist. He got his free hand on my throat. Neither of those facts improved my situation.

"You don't know what you're carrying," he said, quiet as a prayer. Not threatening. Almost sorry. "You don't know whose names are in those margins."

"I know mine's not," I wheezed, "which puts me ahead."

He almost laughed. I swear he almost laughed. And then he twisted, and the knife came free of his wrist-grip, and everything after that happened very quickly and mostly to me.

* * *

Pain is a strange narrator. It doesn't describe; it exclaims. So let me give you the calm version, reconstructed later, the way Ivo does it when she debriefs us around the fire with her arms crossed like a schoolmistress.

Vessick cut me twice. Once across the forearm — shallow, defensive wound, the kind you get reaching for a blade instead of away from one. Once low in the side, deeper, the kind you get when you're stubborn and won't let go of a man's wrist even when letting go would be smarter. I didn't let go. I don't fully know why. Partly because the documents were right there. Partly because I'd spent my whole life being the man who lets go — of deeds, of dignity, of other people's secrets when holding them got expensive — and I was tired of being that man.

Mostly, though, I think it was because of what Ivo said to me on the salt flats, the night Doss-that-was confessed what he stole and why. She hadn't given me absolution. She hadn't given me anything, that woman, not a kind word, not a soft look. But she'd listened, and afterward she'd said, "A man's past is a debt. Doesn't mean he has to keep borrowing against it." And some fool part of me had decided, somewhere between the flats and the Narrows, that Ivo Amberdale's opinion of me was the only credit rating that mattered anymore.

So I held on. And when Vessick wrenched back to finish the job, I threw my whole weight sideways — wounded side first, which tells you how much thinking I was doing — and took us both down onto the scree, and my elbow landed on the oilcloth bundle, and I shoved it, shoved it hard, sliding it along the stone away from us, away from the spilled oil, into the dust and grit that would soak up the lamp oil before it could find a spark.

Then Vessick's knee found my ribs and the world went white and I heard running footsteps and I thought, distantly, well. Here it is. The bill comes due.

Except the footsteps were Ivo's, and Ivo didn't bring a bill. She brought a crossbow bolt into the rock six inches from Vessick's ear, which is the Amberdale way of saying *stop*, and Vessick stopped, because he was a professional and professionals recognize a warning shot that wasn't a warning.

What happened next took maybe four seconds and felt like an hour. Sera arrived with her father's old prospecting pick actually raised over her head, murder in her eyes, grief wearing armor and armor wearing bloodlust. Corvane arrived last, and I will say this for the man: he looked at his own soldier kneeling in the dust with a knife in his fist and an oil flask empty beside him, and I watched something die in his face. Not surprise. Confirmation. He'd suspected. Now he knew.

"Whose order?" Corvane asked. Just that. Three syllables, flat as the salt flats.

Vessick said nothing. Professional to the end. But he looked at Corvane the way you look at a man when the answer is *yours, and you know it, and we both know why you're asking anyway* — and then he looked past Corvane, up the canyon wall, toward the direction we'd come from, toward the world, toward whoever had sent him.

That was the moment Ivo understood it too. I saw it land on her like a dropped pack. Her jaw did that thing it does when she's swallowing words instead of saying them.

"It's not the magistrate," she said quietly. "It was never just the magistrate."

* * *

They bound Vessick with the same rope we'd been using on the mule's lead, which he bore with a dignity I grudgingly admired, and they debated what to do with him while I sat against the canyon wall trying not to bleed impressively.

Sera wanted to leave him tied for the desert to judge. Corvane argued for bringing him — a witness, he said, and the word had a bitter taste in his mouth, like he was chewing on his own career. Ivo listened to both with her arms crossed, and then she crouched down next to me, which surprised me more than the knife had.

"Let me see," she said.

"It's fine."

"Doss."

It was the first time she'd said my name like it was a name and not a category of nuisance. I let her see. She peeled back the shirt from my side, and her breath went in slow through her nose, the way it does when she's deciding how worried to let her face admit.

"Sera," she called. "Needle and the boiled thread. And the good bandages, not the ones that have been mopping the mule."

While Sera rummaged, Ivo stayed crouched there, her hand pressed flat against my side above the wound, keeping pressure, her eyes on mine. Desert-light made her face all planes and shadows. Up close she looked tired in a way she never allowed at a distance.

"You slid the packet clear," she said. "Before you went down. I saw where it landed."

"Took me a moment to remember doing it."

"I'm not surprised. Most men would've grabbed for their knife. You grabbed for the papers." She paused. "Why?"

And here's where I should have said something noble, something about justice or truth or Halvard Brine's legacy. Instead, bleeding against a canyon wall with a madwoman's fingers pressing my guts in, I told the truth:

"Because you'd have carried them out yourself if I'd failed. And you've carried enough."

She didn't say anything for a long moment. Somewhere above us, the gray ribbon of sky went on being indifferent. Then she said, "That was a stupid thing to do," and squeezed my shoulder once, hard, and stood up, and that was the closest thing to a medal I ever expect to receive, and I'll tell you honestly, I'd trade the magistrate's pardon for it.

Sera stitched me up sitting right there in the dirt, no anesthetic except her conversation, which consisted entirely of telling me what an idiot I was in increasingly inventive terms while her hands stayed perfectly steady. Halvard's daughter. Steady hands ran in the family. Around stitch forty she ran out of insults and just said, quietly, "Thank you," and I pretended the sweat in my eyes was from pain.

* * *

We camped that night in a wider bend of the Narrows, a pocket of sand between the walls, and I lay on my uninjured side listening to the arguments I was too spent to join.

Corvane sat apart, turning his ledger over in his hands — the little book where he'd recorded, years ago, the orders he'd obeyed and the questions he hadn't asked. He'd shown it to Ivo at the canyon mouth as his pledge. Now he stared at it like a man rereading a letter from a lover who'd turned out to be a con artist.

"The flask," he said finally, to the fire more than to anyone. "Regimental issue. Sealed stores. That oil doesn't come from a magistrate's armory. It comes from a garrison requisition chain that runs through the Surveyor-General's office." He closed the ledger. "I reported to the Surveyor-General when I started tracking you. Which means—"

"Which means you filed our route," Ivo finished, "with the people who want the route buried."

Silence. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"They'll know where we are," Sera said. "Or close enough. They'll send more than one man next time."

"They'll send men to the pass," Ivo said. "It's the only way through before dry season closes it. They don't need to find us. They just need to be standing at the exit when we arrive." She fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "So we don't arrive."

"We go over?" Sera's voice climbed. "Through the high country? With a body, a mule, and a man who can't walk straight? That's a week added, minimum, and the water holes up there are guesses, not certainties—"

"A dead certainty at the pass," Ivo said, "is worth more than a live guess over the top." She looked around the fire, at each of us, and last at me, and something in her expression had changed since the Narrows. Not softer. Settled. Like a woman who'd been carrying a question for years and had finally gotten her answer, even if the answer was ugly. "Vessick wasn't trying to steal the documents. Thieves take things to sell. He was trying to erase them. There's a difference, and the difference has a name, and we don't know it yet. But those papers scared somebody enough to plan this before we ever left the last settlement." She paused. "Maybe before we ever found the Summit."

I lay there with my stitches pulling and turned that over. Before we found the Summit. Before Halvard died. Which meant—

"Corvane," I said, and my voice came out rougher than intended. "Your superiors suppressed the map fragment years ago. You said so. Who gave the suppression order?"

He didn't answer right away. He opened the ledger instead, slowly, and I watched him read his own handwriting like a man reading a confession he'd forgotten writing.

"The same office," he said at last. "The same signature."

Above us, the ribbon of sky had gone fully dark, and somewhere out in that darkness, past the walls of the Narrows, past the pass, past the edges of every map we carried, somebody with a seal and a signature already knew we were coming — and had known for eleven years.

Ivo banked the fire down to coals and took first watch, crossbow across her knees, facing not the way we'd come, but the way we were going.

"We break camp early," she said. "We climb."
