## 18. Dry Season's Edge

The wind changed at dawn, and I knew before anyone said a word.

Desert wind has a vocabulary. There's the dry morning breath that comes down off the high stone, clean and indifferent. There's the afternoon gust that carries dust and the smell of hot rock. And then there's this—the flat, heavy stillness that comes before the passes seal, when the air stops moving through the mountains the way a door stops moving through a frame. The land was closing its throat. We had hours. Maybe fewer.

I stood on the last rise above the Narrows and did the arithmetic I'd been doing in my head since the betrayal, except now the numbers had stopped being polite about it.

Doss was feverish. The knife wound in his side had gone angry overnight, the skin around it hot and striped, and every mile we'd pushed since had been a mile bought with his blood. He walked because Doss walked—he'd have walked on a broken leg to keep from being carried—but he walked at two-thirds speed, and he swayed like a fence post in wind, and he'd stopped making jokes, which in Doss was the loudest alarm he had.

Halvard Brine weighed what dead prospectors weigh, which is more than living ones, wrapped in canvas and strapped to the travois. We were not leaving him. I had sworn an oath over a dying man's map, and whatever else the desert had made of me, I kept my oaths. It was the one currency I'd never counterfeited.

And the documents—the grants, the surveys, Halvard's final pages, the whole rotten architecture of a manufactured drought—were wrapped in oilcloth against Sera's chest like a second heartbeat.

Three things. One wounded man. One dead one. One packet of paper worth more than every settlement from here to the coast. And a pass that would shut like a trap by nightfall.

The math was ugly. It was always going to be ugly. I'd just been hoping it would at least have the decency to wait until we were closer to the answer.

"You're doing the face," Sera said behind me.

"What face?"

"The one where you've already decided something terrible and you're deciding how to tell us." She came up beside me, and I didn't look at her because looking at her would have made the next part harder, and the next part was already going to be hard enough. "I've known you for a month, Ivo Amberdale. I know your faces."

"That's a month too many."

"Ivo."

I made myself look. Her father's map was folded in her pocket now; she carried it the way I once carried my ranger's badge, which is to say like a thing that had cost too much to be worth what it was. Her eyes were Halvard's eyes, and I'd sworn an oath to those eyes without knowing it at the time.

"The pass closes tonight," I said. "Maybe sooner. The wind's already turned."

"I can read a sky, ranger."

"Then you can read this one. We don't all make it through at this pace. Doss can't hold the pace, and the travois slows us on the scree, and the scree is where the pass gets narrow." I kept my voice level, the way you keep a level hand when you're pouring the last of the water. "So we split."

Sera went very still. In my experience, stillness is worse than shouting. Shouting means someone thinks arguing will help.

"Say the rest of it."

"The evidence goes ahead. Fast party, light load. You, Corvane, and two of his men who can still run. You take the documents and you don't stop for anything—weather, rockfall, wounded strangers, wounded friends. You get to the assembly before the delegates scatter for the dry season, and you put that paper in front of them while it's still warm from your chest."

"And you?"

"I take Doss and your father and the slow road."

"And you," she repeated, in the tone of someone who has caught a card sharp palming a card. "That's not a plan, that's a eulogy with extra steps."

"It's arithmetic."

"It's cowardice wearing arithmetic's coat." She said it flat, without heat, which stung worse than heat would have. "You did this at the Summit, too. 'We stay quiet until we're safe.' You've been erasing yourself since the day I met you. You burn supplies to fake your own death. You give away the credit. You hand me the truth and keep the danger. When does it end, Ivo? When do you get to arrive somewhere?"

The question sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

"I get to arrive," I said, "when everyone else does first."

"That's not arriving. That's a rear guard's fantasy of a funeral."

I almost laughed. Sera Brine had a gift for eulogizing me while I was still breathing, and the terrible thing was how much I'd come to rely on it. Everyone else in my life had been content to let me vanish quietly. She was the first person in eleven years who'd stood in the doorway of my disappearing and refused to step aside.

"Doss can't run," I said. "That's the whole of it. And I won't leave him, and I won't leave the body, and I won't hand the documents to a fevered man who might drop them in a river. So the choice isn't who's brave. It's who's slow. And I've been slow my whole life. I'm practiced at it."

"You're practiced at being last out of burning buildings," she said. "There's a word for people who are always the last one out."

"Careful."

"Arsonists, Ivo. The word is arsonists. You keep setting yourself on fire and calling it duty."

* * *

Doss heard the plan the way Doss heard everything now—half from the world and half from the fever. I crouched next to him under the lean tarp while the others struck camp, and he looked up at me with eyes too bright and a grin that had gone crooked at the edges.

"Let me guess," he rasped. "You're staying with the invalid and the corpse. Very romantic. Very you."

"You should be flattered. Most people don't get a ranger as a nursemaid."

"Most people don't get a ranger as anything. They get a warrant." He coughed, winced, kept the grin up by main force. "Go with the papers, Ivo. I'll manage. Sera can—"

"Sera carries the truth. That's a heavier load than you are, and she's the only one who can carry it. You're stuck with me."

"Stuck," he repeated, tasting the word. "You know what I stole, back at the beginning? Before you caught me? Before all this?" His voice dropped, fever-soft. "I stole a coat. Off a line. It was cold, and I'd been sleeping in a drainage ditch, and there was this coat, this beautiful gray coat, and I told myself I'd only borrow it. That's the whole of my criminal career. A borrowed coat." His hand found my sleeve, gripped with more strength than I'd credited him. "And you—you stole eleven years of your own life and you still haven't figured out how to give it back. We're not so different, you and me. Except I got caught."

"You're feverish."

"I'm clear as glass. You're staying behind because part of you thinks this is how the story ends. Ranger dies in the pass, holding the line, and everyone finally says her name kindly." He patted my sleeve, twice, like a man sealing a bargain. "Well. I refuse. I refuse to be the reason. So here's the deal: you get me through that pass, and I'll testify. Me, a known coat-thief, standing up in front of the whole assembly, telling them about the magistrate's men, about the Narrows, about the man with the knife. Corvane's testimony is clean. Mine's dirty. Dirty testimony sticks, Ivo. Nobody suspects a thief of lying for the other side."

I looked at him for a long moment. The fever burned in him, but underneath it was the same stubborn, thieving, surviving spark that had kept him alive in ditches and deserts, and I realized I'd been wrong about him since the flats. I'd thought he followed me out of fear of hanging. He'd followed me the way a stray follows the first person who doesn't kick it—and then he'd bled for the party like it was his own.

"All right," I said. "You come with the slow party, and you testify, and you live long enough to regret it."

"Knew you'd see reason." He closed his eyes, grinning. "Wake me when it's terrible."

"It's already terrible. That's why I'm waking you."

* * *

We split at the mouth of the Narrows, under a sky the color of unpolished copper.

Corvane stood with the fast party, and for once he didn't argue with me. He'd spent the whole trek from the canyon mouth in a state I recognized too well—the state of a man whose duty has cracked down the middle and shown him something ugly inside. He'd disobeyed his orders to burn the evidence. He'd given me his testimony. And now he looked at the slow party—me, a fevered thief, a corpse on a travois, and old Marta, the expedition's cook, who had refused with terrifying mildness to leave Doss's side—and he said only:

"Send a signal when you clear the scree. Two fires. We'll hold the far side of the pass until dusk tomorrow. Not past that. Not even for you."

"Wouldn't ask you to."

"I know." He hesitated, and then did the thing I'd have bet a year's wages he'd never do. He put out his hand. I shook it. His grip was dry and hard and honest, and neither of us said anything about the last time we'd stood face to face, when he'd put irons on me and I'd sworn I'd die before I wore them again. Some debts don't get spoken. They just get paid in different coin.

Sera hugged me. She did it quickly, fiercely, the way you grab something you're afraid the wind will take, and she said into my shoulder, low enough that no one else heard:

"Come through that pass, Ivo Amberdale. Not because the story needs it. Because I do. I just got a father back and lost him in the same week. I'm not doing it again with you."

Then she was gone, up the Narrows with Corvane and the oilcloth packet, three figures shrinking against the copper sky, and I stood and watched until the rocks took them, because someone should watch people leave for safety instead of watching them leave for exile. It felt like a new thing to do. I decided I liked it.

Then I turned around, and there was the rest of it: the travois, the fevered man, the old woman, the scree, and the closing sky.

"Right," I said to no one in particular. "Let's go be slow."

* * *

The scree was exactly as bad as I'd promised Sera it would be.

Loose stone the size of a man's fist, stacked at the angle of a lie, giving way under every third step with a sound like slow applause. The travois runners chattered and jammed. Marta walked beside Halvard's wrapped body with one hand on the canvas the whole way, murmuring to it—I caught fragments, recipes mostly, and a name I didn't know, and once, unmistakably, *you always were a stubborn old mule, Halvard Brine, and I'll tell them so at your funeral.* Doss leaned on my shoulder on the steep stretches and pretended he didn't need to, and I pretended to believe him, and between the two of us we maintained a fiction sturdy enough to walk on.

We made the first mile in an hour. The pass needed six.

The wind came down through the gap ahead of schedule, carrying the first grit of the season, stinging like a handful of thrown sand. Above us the clouds had begun to organize themselves into something with intent. I'd seen this sky twice in my life. Both times, men who hadn't respected it were found in the spring, when the passes opened, looking surprised.

"Talk to me, ranger," Doss said, when I'd hauled him over a shoulder-high shelf and set him down gasping. "Tell me something true. Fever's making everything feel like a story. I want one true thing."

I crouched beside him, one hand on the travois rope, and considered what true thing I owned that was fit to give away.

"I spent eleven years," I said, "making sure no one knew my name. Changed it twice. Slept facing the door. Left good jobs, good water, one—person, once, who deserved better than a woman who flinched at her own name." The wind pulled at my collar like it wanted the rest. "And then a dying man handed me a map and a promise, and I took it, and I told myself it was duty. But it wasn't. It was the first time in eleven years anyone had asked me to walk *toward* something instead of away. I've been running toward my own arrest for a month now, Doss, and it's the freest I've felt since I was twenty-two years old. That's the true thing. It doesn't make any sense, and it's true."

He looked at me for a while. The fever had receded enough in his eyes to leave something steady behind.

"You're going to make it through this pass," he said. "Because I've decided. And I'm a thief. Once I decide something's mine, I keep it. Your survival is mine now. Consider it stolen."

Above us, the first thunder rolled down the Narrows like a dropped barrel, and the light went the color of old brass, and I put my shoulder back under the travois rope and pulled.

Six miles. Five hours. One pass.

I had walked away from everything I was for eleven years, and it had kept me alive and kept me nothing. Today I walked toward it—toward the arrest, the trial, the name spoken out loud in front of strangers—and for the first time I could see the far side of it, the place where the running stopped and the arriving began.

The wind rose. The pass narrowed. And somewhere behind us, faint under the thunder, I heard the sound I'd been listening for since the ridge: hoofbeats, coming fast, and not ours.
