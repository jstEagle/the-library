## 19. The Summit Speaks

The pass had a name on no map worth trusting: the Throat. A slot of stone between two shoulders of the Summit's outer ridge, wide enough for two mules abreast and narrow enough that a man with a rock and patience could hold off an army. Halvard's map marked it with a small annotation in his cramped hand—*wind sings here*—and he'd been right about that much. The wind came through all night with a low moan, like the mountain clearing its throat before it spoke.

I sat up through most of it, listening to Doss breathe.

He was worse. The wound in his side had gone from angry to quiet, which is never a good sign; fever has a way of going polite right before it turns murderous. Sera had done what she could with the last of the clean cloth and a paste of desert willow bark that tasted bitter enough to strip paint. He slept in fits, muttering apologies to people who weren't there. Once he said my name, and once he said *the ledger*, and I decided not to ask which dream he was having.

Sera sat across the fire from me, sharpening a knife she didn't need to sharpen. She'd been doing it for an hour. The blade could have split a hair by now.

"You should sleep," she said, not looking up.

"So should you."

"I'll sleep when my father's body is out of this canyon." She tested the edge against her thumb, winced at nothing, went back to it. "You should still sleep."

"I don't, much." It wasn't an apology. She didn't take it as one.

Below us, wrapped in canvas and lashed to the travois we'd built from a broken pack frame, Halvard Brine waited to go home. Beside him, sealed in oilcloth inside a saddlebag I hadn't let out of arm's reach in six days, lay the rest of him: the survey documents, the revoked grants, the ledger of names that turned a manufactured drought into a land grab worth killing for. Corvane's testimony was folded in among them, written in a hand that shook only slightly, which for a ranger-corps officer confessing to years of complicity I considered admirable composure.

And ahead of us, past the Throat, the dry season was coming down the mountains like a door swinging shut. Two days, maybe three, before the high route iced over and the low route became a furnace. We'd been racing both.

What we hadn't counted on was a third thing racing us.

* * *

It was the mules that told me. They went silent—not spooked, just *stopped*, ears forward, the way animals get when they hear something they've decided isn't worth panicking about yet. I'd learned to read that silence years ago, back when reading it wrong meant a cell.

I put out the fire with sand and moved to the lip of our little shelf of stone. Below, where the trail bent around the shoulder before entering the Throat proper, there were lights. Lanterns, shuttered low. Four of them. Moving in formation.

Not prospectors. Prospectors don't march.

"Company," I said softly.

Sera was beside me before I finished the word. She looked down, and I watched her do the arithmetic—the lanterns, the spacing, the way none of them stumbled—and arrive at the same sum I had.

"Enforcers," she said. "His?"

"Who else sends men into the dry season with lanterns burning? Men who want to be seen." I settled back against the rock. "That's the magistrate's style. He doesn't ambush. He *arrives*. Wants you to understand, right up until the end, that resistance was always theater."

"Then we run the other way."

"The other way is four days of open ground with no water and a wounded man. And even if we made it, they'd have riders on the flats by noon." I checked the oilcloth bag's knots for the fourth time that hour. "No. This is where we stop."

She stared at me. "Stop. At the pass. With five armed men coming up it."

"Four lanterns. Probably six men—two hang back with the mules. Maybe eight if he's feeling generous with other people's lives, which, historically—" I caught myself. "Yes. Here."

"You have a plan."

"I have a pass, a wounded man, a dead one, and a bag full of the truth. That's not a plan, that's a hand of cards. But I've played worse."

Doss stirred at the fire's cold ring. "Played worse than what?" he asked, in the slurred voice of a man surfacing from fever, and Sera told him, gently, to go back under, and he did, because by then he trusted us enough to obey.

That trust sat in my chest like a coal. I'd spent seven years making sure nobody trusted me enough to obey. Trust is a debt, and debts are how they find you.

But that was the old arithmetic. Tonight I was doing new math.

* * *

They came up the trail an hour before dawn, when the dark is thickest and men's nerves are thinnest. I heard them before I saw them: boots on scree, the creak of leather, and one voice—low, bored, giving orders like a man reading a grocery list. The voice belonged to a broad shape in a hooded coat, and when the first lantern swung its light up toward our shelf, I stepped out of the shadow with my hands empty and my heart, remarkably, quiet.

"Hold there," I called down. "State your business in the Throat."

The lanterns stopped. There was a pause with laughter buried in it.

"Ranger Ivo Amberdale." The broad shape pushed back his hood. He had a face like a closed ledger—all columns, no commentary—and a scar through one eyebrow that I recognized, because I'd given it to him, eleven years ago, in a courtyard in Vell's Crossing, the day they took me in chains. Sergeant Mace Ferrow. Of course it was Ferrow. The magistrate kept a stable of old dogs, and I was the bone they'd all been promised.

"Sergeant," I said. "You've come a long way to die of thirst."

"Come to bring you home, Amberdale. Magistrate Ondrell's orders. You're wanted for escape, and now—" he gestured upward, lazily, at the whole dark mass of the canyon behind me, "—for whatever you're carrying out of the Sunken Summit. Hand over the documents and the dead man's effects, and maybe the tribunal shows you mercy."

"Maybe," I echoed. "You know, in seven years I've learned exactly one thing about that word."

"Don't preach."

"It's not preaching. It's inventory. *Maybe* is what men like Ondrell keep in the place where their promises should be." I shifted my weight, casual, letting him see me alone on the ledge. Letting him count one woman, one silhouette, one problem. Behind me, in the black, Sera was already moving—Halvard's travois, Doss's bedroll, the mules, everything sliding away from the fire ring and up the goat track on the far side of the shelf, the one Halvard's map called *the stair, mind your knees*. I'd found it yesterday and hated it immediately, which is how I knew it would work.

Ferrow's eyes narrowed. He was counting too, and not liking his total. "Where's the girl? Where's Brine's daughter?"

"Gone ahead."

"Gone ahead *where*?" He laughed, short and ugly. "There's nothing ahead but the pass, and the pass is ours."

"That's the trouble with you garrison men," I said. "You think geography belongs to whoever's standing in it."

He sighed, the sigh of a man whose patience is a budget item, and made a small motion with two fingers. The lanterns began to move again, fanning out along the base of the shelf. Six men. I'd have bet on eight, but six was flattering.

"Ferrow," I called, as the first man reached for the scramble up to my ledge. "One question, professional courtesy. Did Ondrell tell you what's in these documents? Or did he tell you it's sedition and leave it at that?"

The climber paused. Ferrow didn't.

"He told me enough," Ferrow said. "Told me a dead prospector's been forging land grants. Told me you're the forger's courier."

"And you believed him." I shook my head, sorrowful as a sermon. "Mace. Eleven years ago you arrested a nineteen-year-old girl for a fire she didn't set, on testimony that was bought and paid for, and you slept fine afterward. I used to hate you for that. Then I read Halvard Brine's survey notes, and I learned that the well outside Vell's Crossing didn't fail—it was *decommissioned*. The grant that fed it was revoked the same season I was convicted. Same clerk. Same signature." I let that sit in the cold air between us. "You didn't arrest a arsonist, Mace. You arrested a loose end. So did I, in a way. Difference is, mine talks."

Something moved behind Ferrow's face. Not belief—belief takes longer—but the first hairline crack of doubt, which is all anyone ever gets on a night like this, and sometimes it's enough.

"Pretty story," he said, but flatter than before.

"It's not a story. It's a ledger. And ledgers don't care who reads them first." I spread my arms. "So here's my offer, sergeant. Stand aside, and when the delegates convene, your name appears nowhere. Stop me here, and every document I carry names every man who tried to bury them. Ondrell's clerks keep records. So do I."

"You're bluffing."

"Eleven years ago, maybe. I used to be good at vanishing." I smiled, and I felt it reach my eyes, which surprised me. "I'm out of practice."

He gave the order anyway. Of course he did. Men like Ferrow don't turn on a ledge in the dark; they turn later, in daylight, when the arithmetic is public. The first climber came up over the lip of the shelf with a knife in his teeth and a rope in his fists, and I did the thing I had sworn, for seven years, never to do again.

I stood my ground.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fighting: it is mostly geometry. Angles, footing, the length of a blade versus the width of a ledge. I had spent seven years avoiding geometry entirely—running flat country, open sightlines, always the long way around. But the Throat was mine in a way no ground had ever been mine. I knew where the loose stones were. I knew the shelf dropped three feet on its far side. I knew the wind sang through the narrows loud enough to drown a shout, and that a man climbing in the dark trusts his ears more than his eyes.

The first man came at me and I gave him the loose stones. He went down hard, and the sound he made coming down was louder than any alarm, which served me fine—I wanted the others cautious. Cautious men climb slowly.

The second was smarter. He waited, feinted left, came right, and got my elbow across his throat for his trouble. We went over the shelf edge together, and I let the drop break us apart, rolled, came up with a fistful of his rope, and used it to introduce him to the third man arriving below. Geometry.

Somewhere in there a blade opened my forearm, shallow, hot, unimportant. Somewhere in there I stopped being afraid, and noticed that I'd stopped, and understood—with a clarity that felt almost like grief—that this was the first time in seven years I had chosen a fight instead of fleeing one. Not because I couldn't run. Because running was the one thing I had left to give up, and I was giving it up gladly, here, in the wind that sang, while above me on the goat track a dead man's daughter carried a living truth toward morning.

That was the whole strategy, in the end. Not victory. *Time.* Every minute Ferrow's men spent wrestling a desert ranger on a ledge was a minute Sera spent gaining the ridge. Every eye on me was an eye off the trail. I had spent my whole life making myself invisible, and now I stood in the open with lantern-light on my face and made myself the loudest thing in the canyon.

"Is that all Ondrell sent?" I shouted, backing along the shelf, bleeding, laughing—I was actually laughing, the wind taking the sound and singing it through the narrows so it seemed to come from everywhere. "Six men for a woman and a bag of paper? Tell him the river says hello!"

"Take her!" Ferrow roared, and lost his composure entirely, which was the second-best thing that happened that night.

The best thing happened above us. A whistle—one note, high, thin, Sera's signal, the one we'd agreed on at the last camp. *Clear of the ridge.* She was over. She had the documents, the samples, the testimony, and her father's promise riding in a saddlebag against her spine, and she was moving east toward the delegate assembly at Coldharbor Ford with two days of hard ground and a head start on every man in this canyon.

Ferrow heard the whistle too. I watched him do the arithmetic—watched the columns collapse—and saw him understand, all at once, that whatever happened on this ledge tonight, the war had already left the building.

"You're too late," I told him. I meant it kindly, or as kindly as I've ever meant anything. "You were too late eleven years ago. You just didn't know it yet."

He came at me himself then, furious, sloppy, and I gave him the shelf's edge and the three-foot drop, and while he climbed back up I gave the whole canyon something better than a fight.

I gave it my name.

"Ivo Amberdale!" I shouted into the wind, into the narrows, into the faces of six men who had hunted a ghost for seven years. "Daughter of the Amberdale grants! Witness for the Sunken Summit! Remember the name—you'll be swearing affidavits about me by spring!"

Because that was the truth underneath all of it, the thing Halvard must have seen in me from the start, dying at his dry well with a map clutched to his chest: I had spent seven years erasing myself, and erasure had kept me alive but it had never once kept me *free*. Freedom wasn't disappearing. Freedom was standing in the light with your name in your mouth and daring the world to write it down wrong.

The wind carried my name up the canyon and sang it back, and for a moment even Ferrow's men hesitated, because there is a kind of stillness that stops men faster than a blade—a woman standing bloody and unbowed on a ledge, wanting nothing, fearing nothing, asking only to be witnessed.

Then Ferrow's boot found the loose stones, and the moment broke, and the fight came back on—

—and so did the dawn, grey and sudden over the eastern ridge, and with it, faint but unmistakable, carried down the wind from the far side of the pass: hoofbeats. Many. Riding hard.

Ferrow froze, head cocked, listening. His men froze with him.

"Not yours," I said. "And not his."

The hoofbeats grew louder, and the first light touched the Throat, and I stood very still in the middle of it all—stillness chosen at last, at the exact center of the storm—to see who had come.
