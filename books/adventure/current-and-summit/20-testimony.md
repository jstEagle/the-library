## 20. Testimony

The assembly hall in Meridian's Rest had been a granary before it was a courthouse, and it hadn't entirely committed to the change. Grain dust still ghosted down from the rafters when the wind came through the vents, settling on the delegates' shoulders like the building itself was reminding them where their power came from. I sat in the second row, behind a table stacked with my father's documents in oilcloth bundles, and I thought: *you would have hated this room, Da. All this dust. All these people talking instead of digging.*

Then I thought: *you would have loved what's under it.*

There were eleven delegates. I counted twice, because my hands were shaking and counting was the only thing I could make them do. Eleven men and women from eleven settlements, some of whom hadn't shared a well in a decade without an armed escort. They sat in a horseshoe at the front with the particular stiffness of people who had dressed up for an occasion they didn't trust. Behind them, on the wall, someone had hung a faded charter seal and, next to it, a map of the territory that was so wrong it was almost a work of art. The Sunken Summit wasn't on it at all. Just a blank stretch of ochre with the surveyor's old notation: *ARID — NO VALUE.*

No value. I'd read that notation a hundred times in the last three weeks, usually by firelight, usually while Ivo Amberdale watched me read it with that flat ranger patience of hers, waiting for it to land. It had landed. It kept landing, like a stone dropped down a well that never quite hit bottom.

The hall filled slowly. Farmers, well-wardens, a delegation of drovers who smelled like they'd ridden hard to get there. And at the back, standing, because he'd refused a seat—Corvane.

He looked worse than when we'd left him at the pass. The ride to Meridian's Rest had cost him something, and not just the miles. He wore his ranger coat brushed and buttoned, but it hung on him like a borrowed opinion. When our eyes met he gave me a nod, small and formal, the nod of a man reporting for a duty he'd assigned himself. I didn't nod back. I wasn't ready to forgive him for the years he'd spent hunting the wrong person, even if he was about to spend the rest of his career paying for it. Forgiveness, I was learning, was a well you had to dig yourself down to. It took time.

The presiding delegate was a woman named Ostrava, gray-braided, with a voice like a gate hinge that had been oiled just often enough. She called the session open and said the words I'd been carrying across two hundred miles of hostile country to hear:

"This assembly convenes to hear a claim of discovery, and a petition of inquiry. The claimant is the Brine Expedition. The petitioner—" she looked down at her papers, and I saw her jaw tighten, "—the petitioner is the late Halvard Brine, by testimony of his daughter."

*Late.* It was the first time anyone had said it out loud in a room like this, and it went through me like a pick through shale. My father had been "missing" for two years. "Lost." "Presumed." Words that let everyone off the hook of saying what they meant. *Dead.* He was dead, and he was here, in these documents, in the survey seals and the water samples and the journal I'd read so many times I could recite the last entry in my sleep.

*If you're reading this, the water is real, and so is the theft. Bring both home.*

I stood up. My legs held, which surprised me. I'd expected them to have opinions of their own.

* * *

I won't pretend I gave a great speech. I'm a prospector's daughter, not a pleader. I laid the documents out on the table the way my father laid out core samples, oldest first, and I walked the delegates through them in the order he'd taught me to read strata: what's on top lies the most.

The first bundle was the original land grant surveys, dated eleven years back, bearing the seal of the Office of Grants and Settlement—Magistrate Aldous Renwick's office. The second bundle was the revocation orders, issued four years later, withdrawing water rights from the eastern aquifer table "pending reassessment." The third was the reassessment itself, which had never happened, because the reassessment funds had been redirected—here I let the ledger speak, with its neat columns and its neat initials—to a land consortium called the Vell Company. A company, Corvane had told me on the ride down, whose founding shareholders included two of Renwick's brothers-in-law and, in a smaller hand that the magistrate had presumably believed was invisible, Renwick himself.

The eastern settlements had gone dry because a man in an office had decided they should. The drought that killed my father's first expedition, that emptied three villages, that sent rangers chasing desperate thieves across the flats—none of it was weather. It was paperwork.

I heard the sound the room made when that landed. It wasn't a gasp. It was quieter and worse than a gasp: it was eleven people not saying anything at all, while grain dust drifted down through the light, while somewhere outside a mule complained about the heat, while the ordinary world went on having no idea it had just changed shape.

"Where did these come from?" Ostrava asked. Her hinge-voice had gone dry.

"From my father," I said. "He found the revocation orders in a records office he had no business being in, and he ran. He ran because he knew what Renwick would do to keep them buried. He ran all the way to the Summit, and he died there, and he left them where only someone desperate enough to follow a dying stranger's map would ever find them." I put my hand flat on the oilcloth. "I was that someone. Ranger Ivo Amberdale was the stranger."

A murmur went through the hall at the name. Of course it did. *Amberdale* was a story they all knew—the ranger who burned the grant records, who fled her conviction, who'd been a cautionary tale at settlement councils for six years. I let the murmur run its course.

"The ranger you've heard about," I said, "carried these documents out of the desert at the cost of her own freedom, and she's not here to defend herself, because she's walking into custody as we speak so that this assembly can hear the truth without her shadowing it. So I'll say what she won't: the records she was convicted of burning were copies. Renwick burned the originals himself, four days before her arrest, and let her take the blame for a fire he set. Corvane can confirm it. He's spent six years confirming it, one piece at a time, in the one place a man like him could do it—inside the corps that was lying to him."

I sat down. My hands had stopped shaking, which was its own kind of shock.

Ostrava turned to the back of the room. "Officer Corvane. You stand accused by association of six years of pursuit under false pretense. Why should this assembly hear you?"

I expected him to defend himself. That's what men like Corvane do; I'd watched him do it for three weeks, armor first, honesty only when the armor cracked. But he just walked forward, slow, and stood in the aisle where everyone could see him, and said:

"You shouldn't. You should hear me anyway."

* * *

He testified for the better part of two hours, and I'll give him this: he didn't make it about himself, even though it was entirely about himself. He'd been the arresting officer six years ago. He'd carried the conviction like a stone in his pack ever since, because the details had never fit—the fire's origin, the timing, the way Renwick had pushed the case to conclusion before the ash was cold. He'd filed objections. They'd been buried. He'd requested the original grant records for review. They'd been "lost in transit." He'd kept his own ledger—of course he had, a man that rigid keeps ledgers the way other men keep breathing—and in it he'd written down every anomaly, every deflection, every order that made no sense unless the order-giver wanted the eastern aquifer forgotten.

"Three years ago," he said, "I was given a fragment of a survey map and ordered to suppress it. I was told the Summit region had been assessed at no value. I obeyed. That obedience is the worst thing I've done in twenty years of service, and I include in that the arrest of Ivo Amberdale, which I performed with full ceremony and no doubt, because doubt was a luxury I'd been trained out of." He looked at the delegates, and his voice didn't waver, which somehow made it worse. "Doubt is not a luxury. I understand that now. It's the whole job."

He laid his ledger on the table next to my father's documents. Two men who had never met, dead and living, testifying side by side in different hands. My father's writing had been cramped and practical, numbers first, feelings only if they fit in a margin. Corvane's was precise, ruled, almost judicial. Between them, the truth sat on that granary table like something excavated—crusted over, but intact.

Ostrava read the revocation order aloud. All of it. The hall was so quiet I could hear the vents breathing.

When she reached the clause transferring water rights to the Vell Company, a drover in the third row stood up without being called on and said, in a voice like a rockslide starting, "My village drank mud for two winters." And then he sat down, because there was nothing else to say that the document hadn't already said better.

* * *

The filing took less time than the testimony, which is the way of the world: truth takes hours, paperwork takes minutes. Ostrava entered the discovery claim in the name of the Brine Expedition—"in the names of Halvard Brine, and of the six who died with him at the Summit, whose remains are being carried home even now"—and the hall did something I hadn't expected. People stood. Not all of them, and not with any choreography to it, just one and then another and then most, the way a wind moves through a field. For my father. For the men whose bodies Ivo was bringing down the pass at this very hour, if the pass was still open, if the enforcers hadn't—

I didn't finish that thought. I'd spent three weeks not finishing that thought. It was a discipline, like my father's surveying: mark what you know, leave the rest blank until the instruments catch up.

The instruments caught up at dusk.

We'd broken for the evening—delegates whispering in knots, the drovers arguing cheerfully about who'd get the first pipeline rights, Corvane standing alone by the wall with the expression of a man who had set down a stone and didn't yet know how to walk without it—when the doors banged open.

A rider. Dust to the eyebrows, horse lathered gray, one boot missing its stirrup leather. She was one of ours, one of the fast party that had gone ahead with the evidence escort—Tamsin, a well-warden's daughter who rode like weather. She stood in the doorway sucking air, and the whole hall turned, and I was moving before I'd decided to, my body apparently done waiting for my permission.

"Report," Ostrava said.

Tamsin found her voice in pieces. "Pass held. Evidence delivered. The delegates' escort met us at the Narrows, all secure." A breath. "But the magistrate's men hit the rear party at the dry fork. Ivo Amberdale held them off so the body and the wounded could get clear." Another breath, and this one cost her. "She didn't come out. Last anyone saw, she was standing in the mouth of the pass with her back to them, and the enforcers took her."

The hall went loud. I didn't hear it. I was looking at Corvane, and Corvane was looking at the floor, and I understood that we were both doing the same arithmetic: she had known. She had known since the dry fork, since she split the party, since the day she'd told us at the canyon mouth, in that flat ranger voice, *evidence goes fast, I go slow.* She had traded herself the way she traded everything—quietly, without receipts, without ever once asking the rest of us to watch her do it.

Well. She'd made one mistake. She'd sent me ahead with the documents, and I was her receipt now.

"Ostrava," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to, "your assembly just filed a claim on the strength of testimony that a convicted woman carried out of the desert in chains of her own choosing. She's in Renwick's custody by now, or his men's, and we both know what happens to inconvenient witnesses in magistrate holding cells." I picked up my father's journal from the table and held it where everyone could see it. "The truth got here. Now we go get the woman who brought it."

Corvane was already moving for the doors, and behind me, one by one, the drovers started reaching for their hats.
