## 21. Homecoming, With Chains

They found me at dawn on the third day, and I want it on the record that I did not cry. My eyes watered, yes, because after three days in a rock shadow with no water worth mentioning and one wounded man breathing against my shoulder, a person's eyes will do that. But crying implies surprise, and I had known they'd come. Sera Brine does not break promises, and I had made her make three.

The rescue party was eight riders and a mule train, led by a woman named Okonwe who had once shared a mess tent with me at Fort Alder and had since learned to look at me like a ledger entry. She took in the scene—me sitting upright, Doss unconscious but breathing, Halvard Brine wrapped in canvas on a travois I'd built with hands too numb to feel the rope—and she said, "Amberdale. You look terrible."

"I've been told I photograph worse."

"Is he—"

"He's alive. The wound went through the meat of his shoulder. He'll complain about it for years, which is how you know he'll live." I stood, and my knees registered their formal objections, which I overruled. "The others came through the pass two days ahead of me. Sera Brine has the documents. Corvane is with her—he surrendered his sidearm to her somewhere around the Narrows, which I'd have paid money to see."

Okonwe looked at the travois, at Doss, at me, and then at the pass behind us, where the dry season was already chewing the high trail into something a goat would refuse. "We heard there was trouble at the Summit," she said carefully.

"There was truth at the Summit. Trouble just attended."

She dismounted. And here is the thing I had rehearsed for eleven days while keeping Doss alive and Halvard's body from the scavengers: the moment when I would say it out loud, before anyone could say it for me. Because I had spent nine years letting other people tell my story—the magistrate, the warrant, the wanted posters with my face aging in increments from twenty-three to thirty-two. Every version of me out there in the world was drawn by someone else's hand. This part, at least, I would draw myself.

"I'm coming back with you under charge," I said. "Ivo Amberdale, convicted in absentia of theft of grant documents and obstruction of survey, nine years ago, Magistrate Ellery Voss presiding. I'm surrendering myself. I'll answer for it in front of the assembly, whatever they decide."

Okonwe stared at me. Behind her, one of the younger rangers—a boy who couldn't have been born when the warrant went out—shifted uncomfortably, as if the name had arrived in the camp without luggage and nobody knew whether to feed it.

"You could just ride in as the woman who found the river," Okonwe said. "Half these people are already calling you that. It would be easier."

"Easier isn't the same as true. And if I walk in clean and the old charge surfaces later—which it will, because men like Voss keep their knives sharp—it poisons everything. The water, the documents, all of it. They'll say the woman who brought the proof was a thief all along." I shrugged, or attempted to; my shoulders had opinions too. "Better they hear it from me first, standing up, than find it later and use it to bury the river with me."

Something moved across Okonwe's face—not quite respect, but its cousin. "You always were stubborn past the point of sense."

"It's the only point I've ever been able to find."

* * *

They bound my wrists. Not roughly—Okonwe used a soft cord and apologized with her eyebrows—but bound them nonetheless, because procedure is procedure and I had asked for exactly this. What I hadn't anticipated was what the binding would do to my hands' memory. Nine years of keeping them free, of pulling them out of reach of every handshake, every offered cup, every moment where someone might notice the calluses of a woman who worked for a living instead of hiding. Now they were tied, and tied meant seen, and seen meant—

Meant I walked into Harrow's Ford in chains and discovered that the sky did not fall. That was the lesson, I suppose. Nine years of dread, and the sky just hung there, doing nothing, the way skies do.

The settlement had grown since I'd last seen it, back when I was a rumor passing through at night. There were new roofs on the east row, a proper well house, a school with actual walls. Word travels faster than mules, so by the time we crested the last rise, half the town was in the street. I had imagined this moment perhaps four hundred times over nine years, and in most versions I was running, or hooded, or dead. In none of them was I walking upright between two rangers with my wrists corded in front of me and a dead man's body riding beside me on a wagon draped in expedition canvas.

The crowd didn't shout. That was the strange part. I'd braced for shouting—for the old arithmetic of guilt, the arithmetic that had condemned me in an afternoon without ever hearing my full testimony. Instead they went quiet, the way people go quiet at a funeral, and then I understood that this *was* a funeral, or the front end of one. They weren't looking at me. Or rather—they were looking at me, but through me, at the wagon, at the canvas shape beneath it, at the man who had gone into the deep dunes with eleven companions and a promise and come home as cargo.

An old woman stepped out of the crowd. I knew her vaguely—Marta, who kept the drying sheds. She reached up and touched the wagon wheel, not the body, as if the wheel were closer to holy. Then she looked at me, at the cords on my wrists, and her face did something complicated.

"That's the ranger," she said, not to me, to the crowd. "That's the one who brought him down off the mountain."

"I'm not—" I started, and stopped, because the sentence I was about to say was *I'm not a ranger anymore*, and I realized, with a sensation like a floorboard giving way, that I didn't know if it was true. Nine years of insisting I was nothing, nobody, a nameless tracker on the flats. And here was a stranger handing the name back to me like it was a coat I'd left somewhere.

Sera found me at the guardhouse door. She looked like she'd slept maybe four hours in three days and like each of those hours had been argued over. Her eyes went to the cords on my wrists and her jaw set in a way I recognized now as inherited—Halvard had done the exact same thing, jaw first, words second.

"They said you turned yourself in." Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"I did."

"You could have waited. The documents are being read tonight. Corvane is testifying. By morning Voss will be finished—you wouldn't have needed to spend one hour in a cell."

"Yes, I would." I leaned against the doorframe, because my legs had unionized. "Sera. If I wait for the verdict to clear me, then I'm cleared by other people's courage. If I stand up first—if I say *here is my name, here is my charge, judge us both*—then whatever happens to the river happens to me too, honestly. I spent nine years making sure nothing happened to me honestly. I'm done with it."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she did something I did not expect, which was to reach out and straighten the collar of my ruined shirt, the way you'd tidy someone before they walked into a room that mattered.

"My father chose you," she said quietly. "He read your case file. He told me once—I thought he was rambling, near the end—that the only person he trusted to finish the survey was a woman the law had already thrown away, because the law throws away exactly the people who see clearly." Her voice caught, and she pushed through it. "So. Fine. Chains. But you walk in there like you're arriving, Ivo. Not like you're being delivered."

"That," I said, "is the single most useful piece of advice anyone has given me in nine years, and I resent how much it cost you to say it."

The ghost of a smile. Gone fast. But present.

* * *

They put me in a cell that had recently held a drunk sheep farmer, judging by the smell, and I lay on the cot and listened to the town assemble itself around the assembly hall. Carts. Voices. The particular murmur of a community deciding its future, which sounds like any other murmur until you remember what's stacked on the table.

I should have been afraid. Some part of me noted this with clinical interest: *you should be afraid now, this is the fear-shaped hour.* But the fear never came, and eventually I understood why. Fear requires a future you're trying to protect. For nine years my entire self had been organized around avoidance—avoid the name, avoid the towns, avoid the questions, avoid the mirrors. Tonight there was nothing left to avoid. Whatever happened in that hall would happen to the whole of me, unedited, and there was a cleanliness in that that felt almost like rest.

Doss came to the bars in the evening, arm in a sling, moving like a man whose body had been invoiced for the Narrows and was still reviewing the charges. He sat down on the floor across from my cell without asking permission, which was very Doss.

"Sera says you're going to let them try you," he said. "Before the evidence even lands."

"I'm going to let them hear me. Trying is their idea."

"You know what I stole? Back on the flats, the day you caught me?" He picked at the sling's knot. "Ledger pages. From Voss's own clerk. Copies of the grant revocations. I'd been carrying them for two years looking for somebody who'd know what to do with them, and every time I got close to somebody, I'd think—they'll hang you for possessing these, and then who carries them? So I'd run again. Steal food, steal horses, keep moving." He laughed, short and bitter. "You caught me stealing paper I'd already been hanged twice over for. And you didn't even blink. You just looked at me like you were reading a map."

"I was. You were badly labeled terrain."

"And now you're in a cell." He shook his head. "For the same crime they invented for you. While the real thieves get read aloud in a hall full of delegates." He stood, wincing. "For what it's worth—when they ask you what you did, tell them about the flats. Tell them you caught a thief and gave him water instead of a rope. That's the whole case right there. That's who you are when nobody's watching, which is the only testimony that counts."

He limped toward the stairs, then paused. "Also, Marta sent a pie. For the prisoner. Don't tell the rangers, they'll tax it."

After he left, I ate the pie, which was excellent, and I thought about what he'd said, and I corrected him silently: the pie was also testimony. A town that feeds its prisoner is a town that has already begun deliberating in your favor.

* * *

Near midnight, boots in the corridor. I expected the turnkey. I got Corvane.

He looked worse than Doss, which was saying something—the desert had taken its toll on him too, and something else besides, some internal expenditure that showed around the eyes. He stopped outside the bars and stood there a moment, and I let him, because I owed him the silence. He'd disobeyed orders for me. He'd put his career on a table next to mine. A man deserves a pause before speaking to someone he's done that for.

"The reading starts at dawn," he said. "Voss's advocates arrived an hour ago. Three of them, riding hard from the capital. Which means he knows."

"Good. Let him watch."

"He's brought a motion." Corvane's voice was flat, careful. "To exclude the expedition documents entirely—on grounds that they were recovered through the agency of a convicted fugitive, and are therefore tainted fruit. Everything Sera read tonight, everything I'm prepared to testify to—his argument is that none of it entered the record legally, because it passed through your hands." He met my eyes through the bars. "Your conviction isn't just about you anymore, Amberdale. It's the lever he's using to bury the river. Clear you, and the documents stand. Leave you condemned, and he can argue the whole chain of custody is poisoned at the source."

I sat with that. Somewhere outside, a dog barked at the assembled carts, and the murmur of the town went on, unaware that its tomorrow was being weighed in a cell that smelled of sheep.

"So it comes down to me either way," I said. "Nine years ago he erased me to steal the land. Tomorrow he needs me erased to steal the water. Consistent man. You have to give him that."

"What will you do?"

I looked at my hands, freed of the cords now, resting open on my knees. Callused. Honest. They had built a travois, held a dying man's promise, carried a stranger's secret across three hundred miles of hostile country, and never once, in nine years, stolen anything except time.

"What I've always done," I said. "Tell the truth and keep walking. Except this time—" I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like a tool I'd forgotten how to hold. "This time everyone's watching. Get some sleep, Corvane. Tomorrow we both testify."

He nodded slowly and turned to go, and I called after him.

"Corvane. When they read the grants aloud—when they get to the part about the Amberdale parcel—listen to the room. I've waited nine years to hear my family's name said in a public hall without shame. I intend to enjoy it."

He paused at the stairwell, and for the first time since I'd known him—since the day he'd put cuffs on a twenty-three-year-old girl and read her a charge she didn't understand—he almost smiled.

"Dawn, then," he said. "Wear something they'll remember."

Outside, the eastern sky had not yet begun to pale, but the town was already awake, gathering, and I lay back on the cot and listened to the sound of my own history assembling itself in the square—ready at last to be heard in full.
