## 22. Current and Summit

### Ivo

They cleared my name the way a river clears a canyon: slowly, and by wearing away everything that had been built on top of it.

Corvane's ledger did most of the work. I'd imagined vindication as some grand moment—a hall full of people gasping, the magistrate weeping, trumpets, maybe. What actually happened was a clerk in a gray coat reading entries aloud in a flat voice for six hours while delegates fanned themselves and someone's baby cried. Entry after entry after entry. Payments to land speculators. Dates on which water rights were revoked and re-granted to men who'd never seen the country they now owned. A line item, dated three days before my trial, marked simply *Amberdale — settled*. My conviction had cost forty crowns. I'd spent eleven years running from something cheaper than a good horse.

I sat in the gallery and listened to my own ruin itemized, and I found I couldn't summon the anger I'd been carrying like a canteen all this time. It wasn't gone. It was just—spent. Used up on dunes and dry wells and a dying man's oath. There was nothing left in it to drink.

When the reading ended, the assembly voted to refer the magistrate to formal tribunal, filed the water claim in the name of the Brine Expedition—all of them, the living and the mineral-crusted dead—and adjourned for lunch. Justice, it turns out, keeps market hours.

Afterward, in the corridor, Corvane found me. He looked older than he had on the ridge, or maybe just tired in a way that fit him better than his old certainty had.

"They'll offer you your commission back," he said. "Ranger-corps. Full rank, back pay, the whole ceremony."

"I know."

"You're going to refuse."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. He nodded slowly, the way a man nods at weather he's decided not to argue with.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the ledger has an entry about me too. Page nine. I signed off on the search that should have caught Halvard's map before it ever reached you. I was ordered to let it burn." He looked at the floor. "I kept it instead. Eleven years in a lockbox under my bunk, telling myself I'd use it when the time was right. The truth is I was afraid of it. You weren't afraid of anything, Amberdale. That used to infuriate me."

"It still might," I said.

He almost smiled. Then he saluted—not the crisp parade version, but the loose one rangers give each other across a valley when they mean *I see you*—and walked away down the corridor, a man finally going home to a house he'd have to rebuild from the foundation.

The offer came the next morning, exactly as predicted. Full rank. Back pay. A posting of my choosing.

I stood in the assembly hall holding the commission in my hands and thought about every night I'd slept in a hollow with my boots on, one ear open, because stopping meant questions and questions meant my name. I thought about how good it would feel to say yes. To be *Ranger Amberdale* again, official, stamped, real.

Then I thought about the river.

"Thank you," I told them. "But no."

The wardenship of the new route—the Sunken Road, they were already calling it, though Sera hated the name—paid less, commanded no one, and came with a badge made of stamped tin instead of silver. It also came with a hundred miles of canyon that needed someone who knew where every handhold lied and every seep ran honest. Someone who'd promised a dying man she'd bring his people home, and intended to keep every other promise along the same road.

Belonging earned isn't given back by a signature. I'd learned that the hard way, at the price of eleven years. I took the tin badge.

### Sera

My father's funeral was the largest gathering the settlements had seen in a generation, which would have made him laugh until he choked. Halvard Brine had spent thirty years being asked to leave places. Now four hundred people stood in the sun outside the new cistern works, and the delegates gave speeches, and the surveyors argued quietly about flow rates because surveyors can't help themselves even at funerals.

I spoke last. I hadn't planned to. But standing there looking at the box that held what the canyon had left us—most of him, wrapped in the canvas he'd carried across half the desert—I realized I'd spent my whole life rehearsing things I never said to him, and if I didn't say at least one of them now, out loud, in front of everyone, I'd carry the rehearsal to my own grave.

"He was a difficult man," I said. "He was wrong about nearly everything except the one thing that mattered. He looked at a dead mountain and saw a river underneath it, and everyone—including me—told him he was a fool. He went anyway. He died there. And the water he found will outlive every person in this crowd."

Then I told them what I'd decided, and watched their faces do the thing faces do when a name lands right.

The spring itself—the first break-out where the buried current surfaces through the limestone and runs open to the sky for a quarter mile before diving under again—we named Brine's Rest. Not for the family. For him. For the rest he never got anywhere else.

I visited it once before taking up my own post at the cistern works. Sat on the bank with my boots off, feet in water cold enough to ache, and talked to him for an hour the way I'd never managed to talk to him alive. I told him about the journal pages. About how he'd known who Ivo Amberdale was when he handed her that map—how he'd picked her, deliberately, a wronged woman and a stranger, because wronged strangers don't quit. I told him I understood now why he'd done it, and that I still hadn't entirely forgiven him, and that I suspected he'd have found that funny.

The water didn't answer. Water never does. But it kept moving past me, patient and certain, the way he used to walk ahead of me on trails when I was small and too proud to ask him to slow down.

I slowed down eventually. He waited. That was the whole marriage of our lives, compressed: he went too fast, I caught up late, and somehow we always arrived at the same place.

I'm the expedition's registrar now. Officially it means I keep the records of the water claim. Unofficially it means I read every document that crosses the assembly's desk with a thief's eyes, looking for the next page-nine entry, the next *settled*, the next quiet theft dressed up as paperwork. Somebody has to. I learned from the best—both of them.

### Doss

People ask me sometimes—young ones mostly, kids who've heard the story thirdhand—what it felt like, stopping that man at the Narrows. The enforcer with the knife, going for the documents. They want it to sound heroic.

It felt like getting stabbed, is what it felt like. I've still got the scar; it aches when rain's coming, which in the new settlements is becoming a rare and precious complaint. Ivo tied off the bandage with a knot she probably learned from a horse doctor, told me I'd live, and then stood there a second longer than she needed to, looking at me like she was filing something away. Later I figured out what. She was deciding I belonged.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about stealing: you get good at seeing what people value, and terrible at believing you could ever have any of it. I spent years lifting purses and ledgers and once, memorably, a taxidermied owl, and every time I told myself the same story—*they have plenty, you have nothing, the math is simple.* The math was never simple. The math was just lonely.

The work I do now is honest, which sounds boring until you've done dishonest work and know exactly how heavy it gets. I run the supply trains on the Sunken Road. Four wagons, six mules, a schedule, and a manifest I sign with my actual name—Doss Merrow, which turns out to be my actual name, though I'd used it so rarely for so long that writing it felt like breaking into my own house. Every load that goes down the canyon carries water gear, mail, seed stock, medicine. Nothing stolen. Nothing hidden. When the wagon master at the far end checks my manifest against my cargo and finds it true, he nods at me the way you nod at a man, not at a problem.

Some nights, camped under the canyon walls with the mules hobbled and the river audible somewhere below the stone—that low, constant breathing the ground does now, everywhere along the route—I take out the little brass compass Ivo gave me when she hired me on. She said it was payment for services rendered. We both knew it was something else. It doesn't point north worth a damn; the ore in the canyon walls sees to that. It points wherever it wants. I've decided to find that encouraging.

Sera says I watch her father's spring like a man guarding a grave. Maybe. But graves are for what's finished, and nothing down here is finished. The water's still finding its way. So am I.

Turns out that's all belonging ever was: showing up tomorrow, same place, same name, and having it matter that you did.

### Ivo

A year, almost, to the day.

I stood at the head of the Sunken Road this morning with the tin badge on my collar and a party of pilgrims behind me—settlers come to see the water, the way people once traveled to see miracles and now, blessedly, only have to travel to see plumbing—and I did the thing I still can't quite stop doing. I checked the horizon.

Old habits. Somewhere out there, logically, there might still be a man with a warrant. Logically, the tribunal finished months ago, the magistrate's assets are frozen, my record reads *vacated* in ink I've touched twice and then stopped touching because it seemed indecent, like patting a scar that's finally closed. But the body keeps its own ledger, and mine still expects to run.

Except.

Except that when I checked the horizon this morning, what I saw was Sera riding down from the cistern works with dispatches, and Doss's lead wagon creaking up from the flats with its honest cargo, and Corvane—of all the improbable people—walking the lower trail with a surveyor's kit, having taken the wardenship of the southern reaches when I told the assembly the route needed two pairs of eyes and I trusted exactly one other pair. The horizon used to be where trouble came from. Now it's just where my people come from, sooner or later, all of them, circling home along the water.

Halvard Brine bought that with his life, and paid it gladly, and never met most of the people drinking from it. I think about that a lot, standing watch over his river. I think about a dying man at a dry well who looked at a wanted woman and saw not a criminal but a delivery system—for a map, for a promise, for the truth. He chose me the way you choose a tool: not because it's clean, but because it fits the work.

I used to think my story was about outrunning my name. Then I thought it was about clearing it. Both wrong, or both only chapters. The story was about the current the whole time—the thing underneath, moving in the dark, patient, wearing through stone, surfacing where it must. Everything on top of it, all the grants and verdicts and lies, was just the summit. And summits erode.

The pilgrims are asking questions now, pointing at the seeps, wanting to know how deep it goes and whether it's safe and whose water it is. I smile and give the answer I've settled on, the one that fits in a sentence and took eleven years and one desert to earn:

"It's everybody's. That's the whole point. Come see."

* * *

And that's the end of it—or near enough. The river runs, the road holds, the names on the markers stay legible. If you want to know more, come down the Sunken Road yourself. Ask for the warden. I'll be the one checking the horizon, but these days, only to see who's coming home.
