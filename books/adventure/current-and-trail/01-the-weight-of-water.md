## 1. The Weight of Water

The river was lying about its depth again.

Astrid Hartwell could tell from the color — that pale, milky green the Mekong wore in the dry months, when it went shallow and sly and hid its sandbars like a card sharp hiding aces. In flood season the water ran brown and honest, deep enough to forgive a careless helmsman. But this was March, low season, and the river between Chiang Khong and the rapids had shrunk to a braided ribbon of channels, each one promising passage and most of them lying.

She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* with one bare heel hooked over the rail's base plate, reading the water the way other people read letters. A smooth stretch of surface meant nothing. The truth was in the texture: the faint dimpling where current slid over a submerged bar, the V-shaped wake pointing upstream that marked a rock's shoulder just beneath, the place where two currents met and stitched themselves together in a long seam of ripples — safe water there, usually, if you trusted it.

She trusted it. She'd been trusting it for eleven years, since her father put the wheel in her hands and said, *She'll talk to you if you shut up long enough to listen.* He'd been dead six years now, and the boat still talked, and Astrid still listened, because listening was the only conversation she'd never walked away from.

"Third channel," she murmured to no one. "Port side of the bar. Don't argue."

The *Kestrel* argued anyway, her old diesel thumping out its complaint below decks, but she came around the sandbar's tail with a meter and a half to spare, and Astrid allowed herself the small satisfaction of being right. It was a thin currency out here. She spent it carefully.

Ahead, the river widened toward the fueling dock at Ban Sai, a sprawl of tin roofs and drying nets on the Thai bank. She could see the fuel barge moored in the lee, and she could see — because the universe had a sense of humor she'd never appreciated — the black-hulled launch tied alongside it, its twin engines idling, its paint too clean for working water.

Sawatdee Syndicate. Or whatever name they were filing their paperwork under this year. Everyone on the upper river called them what they were: the people who owned your papers until you paid, and owned you after.

Astrid's debt lived in a folder in an office in Chiang Saen — forty thousand dollars against the *Kestrel*'s title, rolled over twice, interest compounding like mold in the bilge. Her father had borrowed the first twenty to replace his engine when she was nineteen, back when the syndicate's terms had seemed like a lifeline instead of a leash. He'd died owing them. She'd inherited the boat, the debt, and the compass, though only the last of those had ever felt like a gift.

It sat on the shelf beside the wheel now, brass-cased, its glass clouded at one edge where salt air had gotten in decades ago. Her father's compass. He'd carried it through thirty years on this river, through the bad years and the worse ones, and he'd told her once, drunk on cheap whisky and nostalgia, that a compass didn't point north so much as it pointed home — that the needle always knew where you belonged, even when you didn't. She'd thought it sentimental nonsense at the time. She thought about it more often lately, which was its own kind of warning.

She brought the *Kestrel* alongside the fuel barge with a touch of reverse and a creak of old fenders, and before her deckhand — she had no deckhand; before her own hands — had the lines half-made-fast, the man in the white shirt was already walking down the barge's planking toward her.

Krit. That was the name he used. Collector for the syndicate's northern routes, soft-spoken, immaculate, with the patient smile of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice because everyone else raised it for him. His shoes were loafers. Actual loafers, on a fuel barge on the Mekong. That was the message: I don't have to dress for this work. This work dresses around me.

"Captain Hartwell." He stopped at the rail, hands clasped behind his back, and looked up at her with mild interest, as if she were weather. "You're late."

"The river's low."

"The river is always low in March." He smiled. "You're always late in March."

Astrid tied off the spring line and wiped her palms on her trousers. "I made payment last month."

"You made a payment last month. There's a difference your accountant would explain, if you had one." Krit produced a slim ledger from inside his jacket, opened it against the rail, and turned it toward her with two fingers, the way a dealer turns a card. Numbers marched down the page in tidy columns. Interest. Fees. A line item for *administrative review* that hadn't existed three months ago. "Forty-one thousand, six hundred. By June."

"By June," she repeated.

"Or we take the boat. You know how this works, Captain. We've been very patient. My employers feel patience has been mistaken for weakness." He closed the ledger. "They're not wrong."

Astrid looked past him, at the black launch, at the crewman lounging on its stern who was watching her with the flat attention of a man counting things. Rifles weren't visible. They never were, on the upper river. They didn't need to be.

"I'll have it," she said.

Krit tilted his head, genuinely curious, the way a scientist might be curious about a moth flying into glass a fourth time. "How?"

"That's my business."

"It is. For now." He stepped back from the rail and adjusted his cuffs. "There are opportunities, Captain. Cargo that pays better than rice and engine parts. Cargo that doesn't ask questions. Men like you — capable, desperate — do well in that trade, right up until they don't." The smile again, brief and warm as a heat lamp. "Think about it. Desperation makes excellent employees. It makes terrible debtors."

He walked back along the planking without waiting for an answer, and Astrid stood at the wheel and watched him go, her jaw tight enough to ache. Below her, the *Kestrel*'s engine idled on, faithful and filthy and mortgaged to its rivets. Everything she had in the world floated under her feet, and none of it was hers.

* * *

She took on fuel and fresh water and a sack of oranges from the dock woman, who chattered about the coming rains while Astrid counted bills twice. Then she pushed off into the afternoon current and let the river carry her north, alone, the way she preferred it. The way she told herself she preferred it.

The light went gold and then copper, laying long shadows off the teak forests on the Lao bank. She ran the *Kestrel* on memory and instinct, one hand on the wheel, eating an orange in segments and tossing the peel to the current, watching each piece spin away downstream. Somewhere south of her, everything she discarded ended up in the same water. There was a philosophy in that, probably. She wasn't in the mood for it.

Her father had run this stretch with a crew of four and a radio full of voices. She ran it with silence. It had happened gradually — crews leaving for steadier pay, friends drifting ashore, the radio conversations shortening to weather reports and then to nothing — until one day she'd realized the only voice she heard regularly was her own, narrating the river to herself like a madwoman, and she'd decided that was fine. Independence. Self-sufficiency. All the words lonely people use for loneliness when they're not ready to say it.

At dusk she anchored in the lee of a shoal island, mid-channel, where the mosquitoes couldn't find her and neither could anyone else. She cooked rice and dried fish on the little deck stove and ate sitting on the coil of anchor line, her back against the wheelhouse, watching the last light drain out of the western hills.

The compass sat on its shelf inside, catching the stove-light. She got up eventually, mostly to escape the mosquitoes, and stood at the wheel in the dark cabin, and picked it up.

Brass worn bright at the pivot where her father's thumb had rested ten thousand times. The needle swung, settled, held steady on north with the small trembling insistence of something that believed in its work absolutely. She turned it in her hand and the needle stayed true, patient, certain, and she felt the familiar tightness in her chest that she refused to call anything by its real name.

"Point home, then," she said quietly. "Go on. Point."

The needle pointed north, upriver, toward Chiang Saen and the syndicate's office and the folder with her life in it. She set it back on the shelf harder than necessary.

Sleep came slow. The river talked all night against the hull — that low, ceaseless mutter, water worrying at steel — and somewhere in the gray hour before dawn she finally dropped off, and dreamed, as she often did, of her father standing at this wheel, looking back at her over his shoulder with an expression she could never quite read. Disappointment, she'd always assumed. Lately she wondered if it might be worry.

She woke to engine noise.

Not her engine. Her engine had a rhythm she knew in her bones, a loping uneven thump like a heart with a murmur. This was different — high, fast, multiple — and it was close, and it was cutting across the channel toward her anchorage with running lights blazing at four in the morning, which no legitimate boat did on this stretch of river.

Astrid was up and at the wheel before her mind finished waking, keying the starter, letting the *Kestrel*'s diesel cough and catch. Through the windshield she watched the fast launch swing wide, then turn, then come on again — not toward her, exactly. Toward the shore just downstream, where a figure was stumbling along the mudbank in the pre-dawn dark, waving both arms, falling, getting up, waving again.

A man. Alone. Dressed for the jungle and losing the argument with it.

He collapsed at the water's edge just as the *Kestrel* gathered way, and Astrid swore under her breath, and turned her wheel toward him — because whatever else she was, whoever else she owed, she had never yet left a man to drown or die on this river's banks, and she was not going to start for the sake of a good night's sleep.

The compass trembled on its shelf. North waited. She steered for the stranger instead.
