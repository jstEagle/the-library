## 2. A Stranger's Bargain

The fueling dock at Ban Pak Khwang was a crooked finger of blackened teak reaching into the river, and by the time Astrid Hartwell brought the *Kestrel* alongside it, the sun had already begun to cook the tar out of the planks. She could smell it — that sweet, blistering stink of a hot season leaning into its full weight — and beneath it the diesel and fish rot that clung to every dock between here and the border.

She tied off with hands that knew the knots better than her own name, then stood a moment at the rail, looking down at the water. Low season. The Mekong had pulled back its skirts and shown its bones: sandbars lying in wait like sleeping animals, channels narrowed to threads, the whole river gone shallow and mean. Piloting now was a matter of memory and nerve. You learned where the deep water hid the way you learned where a friend kept his temper — carefully, and over years.

Her father's compass sat in her breast pocket, as it always did, a small cold weight against her sternum. Brass-cased, glass cracked in one corner, the needle still true after everything. He'd told her once that a compass didn't point north so much as it pointed away from everywhere you'd already been. She hadn't understood what he meant until he was dead and she was alone on this boat with a debt that grew like river weed.

"Fuel," she called down to the dock boy, holding up two fingers. "And whatever news is cheap."

The boy grinned and held up one finger back — one drum of diesel, and the news would cost her nothing because it was bad. Bad news traveled free on the Mekong. It always had.

She went aft to check the bilge while the hose ran, and that was when she saw him.

He was slumped against the pilings at the head of the gangplank, a foreigner gone the color of old rope, dressed in clothes that had once been good and were now only stories about being good. His pack lay beside him like something he'd been crawling toward and hadn't reached. One hand gripped a canvas roll wrapped in oilcloth, clutching it to his chest the way a drowning man clutches driftwood.

Astrid had seen dying men before. The river made them, the jungle made them, the mines and the mountains made them, and they all had the same look — not fear exactly, but a kind of furious bookkeeping, eyes moving as if they were counting what they had left and finding the sum short.

This man was doing that arithmetic with her boat.

"You're a captain." His voice came out cracked, English flattened by too many years in too many mouths. "That's a working boat. Cargo, passengers, anything."

"I carry freight. Sometimes people who can pay." She stayed on the deck above him, keeping the height. It wasn't cruelty; it was habit. "I'm not running a hospital."

"No." He laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing bent him double. When he straightened, there was blood-bright spittle on his lips and something wilder in his eyes than fever alone could explain. "No, you're not. But I'll pay. I'll pay more than freight."

*Everyone says that,* she thought. *Everyone who has nothing.*

The dock boy finished fueling and drifted off, uninterested. Astrid should have cast off. Every instinct she'd built in ten years on this river said the same thing: strangers at docks are weights tied to your ankle, cut them loose. But the man hauled himself upright using the piling, and there was something in how he moved — deliberate, even ruined — that stopped her. He climbed the gangplank one step at a time, breathing like each rung cost him money, and stopped at the rail and looked at her with eyes the pale gray of river fog.

"Elias Vane," he said.

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to." He smiled, and for a second under the ruin of him she saw the man he must have been — quick, charming, the kind who talked his way into mountains and talked his way out again. Then the smile collapsed and he swayed, and Astrid caught his arm before she decided to.

His skin burned through the sleeve. Not warm. *Hot.* The kind of heat that came out of wounds or bad water, and neither kind forgave you for waiting.

"Below," she said. "Before you fall off my boat and I have to explain a corpse to the district office."

* * *

She laid him on the bench in the wheelhouse cabin, the one she slept on when the mosquitoes drove her from the deck, and gave him water with a pinch of rehydration salts stirred in. He drank like a man who'd been arguing with himself about whether to keep living, and won the argument for now.

The canvas roll he set on his knees and never let go of, even while drinking.

"Fever's going to take you somewhere ugly tonight," Astrid said, crouching to look at his pupils. Blown wide, but tracking. "There's a clinic two hours downstream. I can have you there by dark."

"No clinics." He said it fast, flat, final. "Clinics ask names. Names get written down. Written things travel." He looked at her then with sudden, terrible clarity, the fever receding like a wave pulling back before it broke. "You know what I mean, Captain. You've got that look. The look of someone who keeps her name small."

Astrid straightened. "You don't know anything about me."

"I know your boat's papers aren't in your pocket. I watched you glance at the wheelhouse shelf twice since I came aboard — that's where people keep documents they don't love." He almost smiled. "I spent thirty years reading rivers and the men on them. It's the same skill either way. You're in debt to somebody, and the somebody isn't gentle."

The engine ticked below them, filling the silence. Outside, the heat shimmered off the dock planks and a kingfisher stitched blue across the current. Astrid found herself wishing, absurdly, that she'd cast off when she'd wanted to.

"Say what you came to say," she said.

Elias Vane unwrapped the oilcloth.

Inside was a map. Hand-drawn on waterproofed linen, dense with ink — contour lines, river bends, elevations marked in a cramped sure hand, and annotations in three languages, some of them crossed out, some circled, one entire section written in what looked like code or shorthand or simply a private language he'd invented to keep the mountain honest. At the top corner, a single word in Lao script, and beneath it coordinates rendered to a precision that made Astrid's breath catch. She knew coordinates like that. They weren't guesses. They were the product of years.

"There's a valley," Elias said quietly, "northwest of here, past the last marked channel. Monsoon country. Nine months of the year it's jungle like any other jungle. But three months — high water, when the streams rise — there's a cave mouth that opens under the falls. Nobody finds it dry-season. Nobody survives trying wet-season unless they know the line." His finger traced a route on the map, and his hand shook, but it shook along the correct path; she could see that even without understanding the marks. "Jade. Imperial green, some of it. Enough to buy a fleet of boats. Enough to disappear forever."

Astrid looked at the map and felt the old hunger open in her chest, the one she kept fed on small things — paid fares, cleared debts, another season survived. This was not a small thing. This was the whole meal.

"What's it cost me?"

"A promise." He leaned back against the bulkhead, and the effort of the last hour landed on him all at once; she watched it happen, watched the color drain and the tremor come back into his hands. "Not to me. To my daughter. Lin. She lives in Chiang Saen — works the market, near the old customs house. I haven't seen her in eleven years. She has every reason to hate me, and she'd be right to." His voice caught, steadied. "When I'm done — and I'm nearly done, Captain, don't waste kindness pretending otherwise — you bring her this. You put it in her hands yourself. You tell her the coordinates are real and the choice is hers. That's all. That's the fare."

"And the jade itself? If she wants it recovered—"

"Then she hires you." He closed his eyes. "Or doesn't. Her father's ghost shouldn't get a vote."

Astrid sat back on her heels. Through the wheelhouse window she could see the river, low and glittering and indifferent, and beyond it the hills where men vanished chasing exactly this kind of story. She thought about the syndicate's collector, Somchai, with his soft voice and his ledger and his way of saying *next month, Captain* like a knife laid on a table. She thought about the number in that ledger, and the interest that grew on it like silt, and the fact that the *Kestrel* — her father's boat, her whole world, hull and heart — belonged to that number more than it belonged to her.

A map like this, sold to the right buyer — a mining concern, a collector, even the syndicate itself, God forgive her — would clear the ledger twice over. Maybe three times. She wouldn't even have to find the jade. She'd just have to find a man in Chiang Saen willing to pay for paper.

One promise to a dying stranger. One promise she could break after he was past knowing.

"I'll do it," she heard herself say. "Chiang Saen. Your daughter. In person."

Something eased in his face — not relief exactly, but the loosening of a man setting down a load he'd carried so long his spine had grown around it. "Thank you," he said, and then, because fever is honest in ways people aren't: "I hope I chose right. I hope you're the woman I think you are."

"I'm a captain with a fuel bill," Astrid said. "Rest."

He slept within minutes, the map rolled in his fist, and Astrid stood in the doorway of her own cabin feeling like a thief who hadn't stolen anything yet.

* * *

Evening came down the river the color of bruised fruit. Astrid cooked rice and dried fish on the deck stove and ate alone while Elias slept, and she told herself the story she was good at telling: that promises to dead men were wind, that a daughter who hadn't seen her father in eleven years had already grieved him, that selling the map might even save *Lin Vane* from a fool's expedition into flood-country caves. There was mercy in it, if you squinted. There always was. That was the trick of pragmatism — it wore guilt's clothing until you couldn't tell which was which.

She took out her father's compass and turned it over in her fingers. The needle swung, settled, pointed north with the dumb faithfulness of a thing that had never lied to anyone.

"He'd have taken the promise," she said aloud, to no one. "That's why he died poor."

But her thumb kept moving over the brass case, and the guilt had already rooted, she realized — already sent down its fine white threads through the practical soil of her, tangling with the debt and the diesel bill and the memory of Elias Vane saying *I hope I chose right*. Guilt wasn't the opposite of greed. It grew in the same pot.

Below, the fever took hold properly. She heard him talking in his sleep — fragments, English and Lao and something older, and once, clearly, a girl's name: *Lin*, said the way you say a word you've been chewing for eleven years.

Astrid banked the stove and went to the wheel, meaning to sleep at anchor and run downstream at first light.

She was checking the mooring lines when she heard it: a soft scrape from the cargo hold, forward, where the tarpaulins lay over empty crates.

She froze, one hand going to the boathook. Rats, she told herself. River rats come aboard at dusk.

But rats didn't hold their breath. And whatever was under that tarpaulin was holding its breath so hard she could hear the silence straining.
