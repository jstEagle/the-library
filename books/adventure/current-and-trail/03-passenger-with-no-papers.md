## 3. Passenger with No Papers

The Kestrel smelled of diesel and mildew and old wood, and Lin Naree Vane loved her instantly.

She'd watched the boat for two days from the teak house across the channel — watched the tall woman in the salt-faded cap walk her decks at dawn, check her lines like a surgeon counting instruments, curse at her port engine in three languages. A working boat. A solitary captain. The kind of vessel that slipped through checkpoints without cargo manifests worth reading.

Lin climbed aboard at 3 a.m., when the river fog lay thick as wool over the mooring lines, and she brought nothing but a canvas satchel pressed flat against her spine and the knowledge that if she was caught here, she would be handed to men who had already killed twice for what was inside it.

She found the gap behind the spare fuel drums in the aft hold — a coffin of a space between hull planking and stacked tarpaulins, warm from the engine room bulkhead. She folded herself into it the way she'd learned to fold herself into small places: completely, patiently, breathing through her mouth until the diesel sting stopped making her eyes water.

*Three days,* she told herself. *Chiang Saen is three days upstream if the current's kind.*

Her father had taught her that phrase — *if the current's kind* — back when he was still a man who came home, back before the maps and the debts and the long silences between postcards. Elias Vane had been gone eleven years, and now he was dead, and all he'd left her was a stranger's promise and a name whispered by a dying man's courier: *the Kestrel. The captain owes us nothing yet. Find her before they do.*

Too late for that last part. They'd found her first. That was why she was hiding behind fuel drums instead of standing on a dock with a ticket.

The engine woke beneath her an hour before dawn — a shudder through the hull plates, then the deep uneven thrum of a boat running on one good heart and half of another. Lines splashed free. The deck tilted. And the Kestrel slid out into the current carrying a stowaway who had not slept in forty hours.

Sleep took her anyway, eventually, the way sleep takes the desperate — sudden and total, like falling into dark water.

* * *

She woke to sunlight knifing through a gap in the tarps and the boat moving wrong.

Not the steady lean of a turn. Something worse — a slowing, a hesitation, the engines dropping to idle while the current still pushed. Then footsteps on the deck above her, unhurried, deliberate, stopping directly overhead.

"Whoever you are," said a voice like gravel dragged through smoke, "you've been breathing on my tarps since the Chiang Khong bend. You breathe loud."

Lin's hand went to the satchel strap. Her heart slammed against her ribs hard enough that she was sure the woman could hear that too.

"I'm coming down," the voice said. "You can be sitting up when I get there or you can be hauled up. Your choice. I don't much care either way, so don't bother performing."

Boots on the ladder. Lin made herself move — made herself sit up, unfold from the coffin-space, and face the ladder with her chin level and her hands visible, because her father had also taught her that the first ten seconds of any encounter decided everything.

Astrid Hartwell filled the hatchway. Up close she was younger than her voice — early thirties, sun-cured, a white scar splitting one eyebrow — and her eyes were the flat gray-green of deep water, taking inventory of Lin in a single pass: the satchel, the mud on her boots, the three-day shadow under her eyes, the way her weight sat ready on her back foot.

"Well," Astrid said. "You're not a thief. Thieves run."

"You didn't give me anywhere to run."

"No," Astrid agreed. "I didn't." She leaned against the hatch frame, arms crossing, and Lin recognized the posture — not aggression, arithmetic. The woman was calculating what Lin cost her and what Lin might be worth. "Name."

"Does it matter?"

"It matters whether you lie about it. Names always come out on a river. Better yours comes out true."

"Lin." She let the surname go and watched the captain notice the omission.

"Lin." Astrid tasted it. "And you boarded my boat in the night because—"

"Because every other boat out of Chiang Khong this week has syndicate eyes on it, and yours doesn't."

Something flickered in the gray-green eyes. Not surprise. Recognition. "Syndicate," Astrid repeated, very evenly. "That's a heavy word to throw around in a stranger's hold."

"I'm not throwing it. I'm telling you why I'm in your hold instead of on your deck buying passage like a civilized person." Lin kept her voice low and level, matching the captain's register. Two women testing each other's edges, each waiting for the other to flinch. "I have money. Not much. Enough for fuel and food to Chiang Saen. I'll work if you want — I can patch fiberglass, I can cook, I can read water well enough not to be useless."

"I don't need crew."

"Everyone needs crew. Your port engine misses on the third stroke."

For the first time, something almost like humor moved behind Astrid's eyes and died there. "You've got ears."

"I've got ears and no papers and no interest in being put off this boat mid-river. So let's skip to the part where you decide what I am." Lin lifted her chin. "Am I a passenger, or am I cargo you deliver to whoever pays?"

The silence stretched long enough for Lin to hear the current slapping the hull, the tick of the cooling engine, her own pulse. She held the captain's gaze and did not blink, and understood suddenly that this — this exact moment, this staring contest over a fuel drum — was the whole negotiation, and everything after was just terms.

"A passenger," Astrid said finally, "buys a ticket."

"I'm offering."

"And a passenger tells me, before we reach the next checkpoint, exactly who's looking for her. Because someone is. I can smell it." Astrid pushed off the hatch frame. "You board like a woman running from something specific. So. Who?"

Lin weighed it. Every instinct said keep it close, keep it vague — but she'd seen the collector on the dock at Chiang Khong, a soft-bellied man with a ledger and two hard-eyed friends, and she'd seen him stop at the Kestrel's mooring line and look at her too long. If the syndicate was already circling this boat, the captain deserved to know what she'd pulled out of the water.

"My father," Lin said. "Elias Vane. He died nine days ago in a clinic outside Ban Houayxay. Before he died, he sent word to me through a man I'd never met. Told me to find the Kestrel." She paused. "He also told me the people looking for me wouldn't stop at questions."

"Elias Vane." Astrid said the name slowly, and Lin saw it land — saw the captain's jaw tighten, saw her glance once toward the wheelhouse where, somewhere, Lin would have bet anything, a hand-drawn map lay hidden.

"You know him," Lin said. It wasn't a question.

"I met him." Astrid's face had closed like a door. "Once. Recently."

"What did he want?"

"To make a deal." The captain turned toward the ladder, done with the conversation, or performing done with it. "Deck's forward of the drums. There's coffee if you make it yourself. And Lin—" she stopped, half-turned, and the morning light caught the scar through her eyebrow, "—whatever you're carrying in that satchel, whatever your father gave you, you'll tell me before we hit the checkpoint at the temple bend. Not because I'll take it. Because I don't steer blind."

Then she was gone, boots receding up the ladder, leaving Lin alone in the diesel-smelling hold with her hammering heart and the growing certainty that she had just made a terrible mistake or the only right choice left to her — and no way to know which until the river decided.

* * *

The coffee was terrible. Lin drank it on the foredeck with her knees drawn up, watching the northern Mekong unspool gold and brown in the early light — fishing platforms on stilts, water buffalo hip-deep in the shallows, the far bank dissolving into haze where Laos became everything else. Behind her, Astrid Hartwell stood at the wheel saying nothing, steering with one hand and watching her passenger with the other, and the silence between them had the texture of a rope pulled taut: not slack, not snapping, just holding.

Lin catalogued her. The way she corrected for the current without seeming to think about it. The compass mounted at eye level above the wheel, brass-cased, ancient, polished by decades of thumbs — the only clean thing on the whole boat, the only thing that looked loved. The way her eyes kept returning to Lin like a man checking a wound.

"You knew him," Lin said at last, into the middle of the river's noise. "My father. You knew him, and you took his deal, and you didn't say a word about it while you were deciding whether to throw me to the bank."

"I was deciding whether to throw you to the bank the whole time," Astrid said. "I just hadn't finished."

"What did he pay you?"

"That's between me and a dead man."

"He was *my* dead man." The words came out sharper than she intended, edged with eleven years of postcards that never came, birthdays that passed unmarked, a girl in a boarding school in Vientiane learning to stop expecting the sound of his boots on the stairs. "Eleven years, Captain. Eleven years I got four letters and a Christmas card. Whatever bargain he struck with you, whatever he was running from or running toward — I earned the right to hear it before a stranger does."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. The river ran. Somewhere ahead, thunder muttered over the mountains — the season turning, the sky loading itself with rain.

"He was dying," Astrid said finally, not looking at her. "Fever, lungs full of fluid, the works. He couldn't stand. He could barely talk. And he talked for two hours anyway, because he had something he needed delivered and only one boat at that dock stupid enough to take the job." Her jaw worked. "He made me promise."

"Promise what?"

"Chiang Saen." Now the captain looked at her, and Lin saw that the flatness in those gray-green eyes wasn't coldness at all — it was control, expensive and effortful, the look of a woman holding a door shut against something with weight behind it. "There's a daughter in Chiang Saen. That's what he said. *Find my daughter.*"

The words went through Lin like cold water through a cracked hull.

She thought of the courier's whisper — *find the Kestrel before they do* — and of her father's last letter, arrived three weeks ago, four sentences long, unsigned: *If you ever want to know what I really did, follow the river north. I'm sorry is not enough. I know. Go anyway.*

"You," Lin said. Her voice came out strange. "He sent me to you. And you were going to—" She stopped, recalculated, and felt the ground shift under everything. "What exactly did he give you, Captain? What am I sitting on this boat with?"

Astrid Hartwell turned back to her wheel. For a moment Lin thought she wouldn't answer at all — and then, ahead, where the river bent past the first green shoulder of the gorge country, a white patrol boat sat at anchor beside the checkpoint pontoon, its flag snapping in the hot wind, and a figure on its deck raised binoculars toward them.

"There's your answer coming to meet us," Astrid said quietly, and reached for the throttle.
