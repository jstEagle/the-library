## 4. What the Syndicate Wants

**Astrid**

The checkpoint at Ban Houayxay announced itself the way all bad news did on the river: a flag first, then a boat, then men.

Astrid saw the yellow pennant snapping above a concrete pier half a kilometer out and felt her stomach drop through the deck plates. She'd run this stretch of the Mekong eleven years and had never once been waved down at Houayxay in low season. The syndicate didn't waste collectors on empty water — which meant they were looking for something specific, or someone.

She cut the Kestrel's throttle to an idle and let the current carry her in, buying herself thirty seconds of thinking time. Behind her, below her feet, wedged into the bilge beneath the engine housing where the fuel smell was thickest and no inspector with a working nose would linger, Lin crouched in the dark with the map pressed flat against her chest like a second heartbeat.

Astrid had given her a bucket, a bottle of water, and one instruction: *don't cough.*

The collector's launch was tied alongside the pier, black-hulled, twin engines, too clean for this river. And standing on the concrete steps with his hands folded behind his back like a man waiting for a train that would never be late was Dane Rook.

She knew him by reputation before she ever knew his face. The syndicate's enforcer. The man who'd put a fisherman through his own propeller in Luang Prabang for shorting a shipment. The man who never raised his voice because he'd never needed to.

Astrid brought the Kestrel alongside the pier with textbook precision, killed the engine, and stepped down to tie off. Her hands were steady. She made sure of it. Fear was a thing you could pilot around, same as a sandbar, if you kept your eyes on the channel and your hands moving.

"Captain Hartwell." Rook's voice was mild, almost courteous. He was smaller than she'd imagined, neat in a gray shirt, with the stillness of deep water. "Papers."

She handed over the Kestrel's registration, her license, the cargo manifest listing forty sacks of rice bound for Pakbeng. He read them without hurry, and while he read, Astrid did the arithmetic she'd been doing since dawn: debt outstanding, four thousand two hundred dollars. Days until the syndicate lost patience, maybe nine. Distance to Chiang Saen, two hundred kilometers of narrowing gorge.

"You're light," Rook said.

"The rice is compacted. I can open any sack you like."

"I'm not talking about the rice." He looked up, and his eyes were the color of old ice. "You're light on crew. Running solo?"

"Solo's cheaper." She kept her face bored. Boredom was armor. "You know what fuel costs."

"I know what debts cost." He handed the papers back, and for a moment his fingers held hers against them — not gripping, just resting, the way a man rests his hand on a door he intends to close later. "We've been patient with you, Captain. That patience has a season, same as the river. It ends when the monsoon does."

"I'll have your money."

Rook smiled faintly, as if she'd said something charming in a language he'd stopped speaking years ago. Then he stepped past her onto the deck.

"I'm going to look at your boat."

* * *

**Lin**

In the dark under the engine, Lin counted her breaths the way her father had taught her to count stars — slowly, and without expecting them to last.

The bilge smelled of diesel and rust and old river water, and every vibration of the hull came through the plating against her spine like a second pulse. Above her, boots crossed the deck. Two sets now. One heavy, one measured. She heard Astrid's voice, level as a spirit gauge, saying something about the fuel lines, and another voice answering in tones so soft she had to strain — which frightened her more than shouting would have. Men who shouted wanted you to know they were angry. Men who spoke quietly already knew how it would end.

Lin pressed the map tighter to her sternum. Oilskin wrapping, twine, her father's cramped handwriting visible even through the layers if she let herself imagine it: *the daughter, if there is one.* There was one. She was the one, crouched in an engine bilge with diesel soaking through the knees of her trousers, and she hated him for it and would have died before letting go of it.

The boots stopped directly overhead.

"She's a slow boat," the quiet voice said. "Old hull. You keep her seams caulked?"

"Every dry season," Astrid said. "Can't afford not to."

"Mm." A pause. Lin heard a knuckle rap twice against the deck plating, inches from her head, and she went absolutely still, breath held in her chest like a swallowed stone. "Hollow spaces under here?"

"Bilge. Fuel residue. You're welcome to kneel in it."

Another silence, long enough that Lin's lungs began to burn. Then the quiet voice said, "I don't think I will," and the boots moved on toward the bow.

Lin exhaled through her teeth, soundless, shaking. Through a gap in the housing she could see a sliver of gray shirt, a hand turning over a coiled rope, setting it back exactly as it lay. A careful man. A man who noticed things. When the hand paused at the rail — where three days ago a woman's wet footprints had dried in the sun — Lin felt her heart stutter.

But the hand moved on. And the boots moved aft. And the measured voice said, from somewhere near the stern, "Your manifest says Pakbeng. You'll pass the gorge narrows tomorrow."

"If the weather holds."

"It won't." Almost gently. "Storms coming down from the north. Big ones. Water will be high and fast and full of things that used to be boats." A pause. "Be careful, Captain. I'd hate for anything of yours to end up somewhere you couldn't find it again."

Then he was stepping down onto the pier, and Astrid was casting off, and only when the Kestrel had swung into the current and the checkpoint was a yellow smudge behind them did Lin crawl out of the bilge, filthy and gasping, into the grey afternoon light.

Astrid stood at the wheel and didn't look at her.

"He knew," Lin said.

"He suspected. There's a difference, and right now it's the width of this river." Astrid finally turned, and Lin saw that her face was pale under the windburn, her jaw set hard enough to crack teeth. "He left a mark on the rail. Did you see it? Chalk. Small white X, starboard side, just aft of the cleat."

"What does it mean?"

"It means we're flagged. Every syndicate man between here and the border will know this boat." Astrid wiped her palms on her trousers, and Lin watched her do it — twice, three times, as though the chalk dust were something alive. "It means I lied to Dane Rook's face today. First time in eleven years I've lied to that outfit, and I did it with my papers in his hand."

"Why?" The question came out of Lin before she could stop it, raw and real. "Why risk it? For me? You don't know me. You were going to sell my father's map — don't think I haven't figured that out. So why lie?"

Astrid looked at her for a long moment. The river ran loud around them both.

"Because he asked about you," she said finally. "Not the map. Not the money. He said, *is there a girl aboard.* And when I said no, he believed me, and I realized I'd rather hang my whole life on that lie than hand him the truth." She turned back to the wheel. "I don't fully understand it myself yet. Ask me again when we're past the narrows."

* * *

**Astrid**

That night she anchored in a side channel behind a screen of flooded bamboo and did not sleep.

She sat in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low and her father's compass open on the chart table, its brass case warm under her palm, the needle swinging lazy circles before settling north-northwest. Toward the gorge. Toward whatever waited there.

Rook had been polite. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the way her tongue kept finding a chipped tooth. In eleven years of hauling cargo for men who owned her papers, she had learned the grammar of the syndicate's violence, and politeness was its future tense. Rook hadn't threatened her. He had *scheduled* her. *Patience ends when the monsoon does.* The chalk X on her rail wasn't a warning; it was a bookmark. He would come back to this page, and soon, and he would expect to find her exactly where he left her.

Below deck, she could hear Lin moving — the small sounds of a person trying to be quiet in a space too small for it. Folding the map. Unfolding it again. Astrid understood that rhythm. She'd spent eleven years doing the same with her father's compass, opening it, closing it, as if the next time might reveal something new.

She thought about the moment on the pier. Rook's fingers resting on hers over the papers. The way she had wanted, for one hot instant, to pull free — and hadn't, because pulling free was information, and information was blood in the water. Instead she had stood still and let him hold her there, and hated him, and hated more the small obedient animal in her own chest that had learned this stillness so well it no longer felt like fear. It felt like habit.

Eleven years of habit. Four thousand two hundred dollars of habit.

Astrid closed the compass and looked out at the black water sliding past the anchor line, fast and swollen with the first northern rains. Somewhere upstream, Rook's clean black launch was moving too, or would be by morning. He had marked the Kestrel, but marks ran both ways. She knew now what he looked like up close. She knew he wouldn't kneel in bilge water. She knew he counted things — ropes, footprints, days — and that a counting man could be beaten by a woman willing to uncount everything she had.

Her eyes drifted to the engine hatch, and to what slept beneath it: a dying man's map, a stranger's daughter, and the first lie she'd told in a decade, still sitting in her mouth like river stone.

Let him come back to his bookmark, she thought. Let him find the page torn out.

She banked the lamp and went below, past the bilge where the diesel smell still clung to everything, to the narrow galley where Lin sat wrapped in a blanket, the oilskin packet open on her knees, studying her father's handwriting by candlelight like a woman learning a language too late.

"We run the narrows at first light," Astrid said. "Before the storm. After that, we stay ahead of him or we don't stay at all."

Lin looked up. In the candle flame her eyes were steady, and scared, and done with being only scared.

"Together, then," she said.

Astrid nodded once, and outside, far upstream, thunder rolled down the gorge like something being dropped from a great height — and the rain began.
