## 5. Monsoon Rising

The rain came in the night like something with intent.

Astrid woke to the sound of it hammering the Kestrel's deck, a drumming so heavy it sounded less like weather than like siege. She lay in her bunk listening, cataloguing by instinct — wind from the southwest, steady, no gusting yet; water slapping the hull higher than it should; and beneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, the wet slap of water where water had no business being.

She was up before she'd finished the thought, bare feet on cold planking, lamp lit one-handed. In the main cabin, Lin was already awake, sitting upright on the bench where she'd slept for three nights now, wrapped in a blanket that had belonged to Astrid's mother. Her eyes were wide and dark in the lamplight.

"It's coming through the seams," Astrid said. "Forward bilge. Get the pump."

Lin didn't ask questions. That was the thing about her Astrid kept noticing — she moved like someone who'd learned that questions cost time, and time cost blood. She was at the pump in seconds, working the handle in long even strokes while Astrid dropped into the engine bay with a torch clamped in her teeth.

The old seams had opened like mouths. Forty years of heat and cold and load had worked the caulking loose along the port side, and the river — fat, brown, furious now — was pushing through in thin silver threads that thickened as she watched.

"Talk to me," Lin called over the noise.

"Three seams weeping. One's honest about it." Astrid spat out the torch. "Keep pumping."

* * *

By dawn they'd driven the Kestrel into the flooded village of Ban Huay Khon and tied her off against a temple wall, the water so high the village streets had become canals and the temple steps vanished three treads down. Monks moved through the shallows in orange robes rolled to the knee, unhurried, carrying sandbags as though the flood were a guest who would eventually remember its manners.

The upper river was closed. Astrid had heard it on the radio at midnight — gorges shut, traffic suspended, the season's first real closure. Nothing would move north for days. Maybe a week. Maybe longer.

So there was nothing to do but fight the boat.

They worked through the morning in a rhythm that surprised Astrid. She'd expected to work alone, the way she always did — expected to hand Lin a bucket and receive resentment back. Instead Lin stripped to a tank top, tied her hair back with a strip of cloth, and asked where the caulking mallet was as if she'd been born on a deck.

"You've done this before," Astrid said, watching her drive oakum into a seam with short, precise blows.

"Fishing boats. My uncle's, in Nong Khai." Lin didn't look up. "Before everything."

"What everything?"

Lin's mallet paused, just for a beat. "Everything," she said again, and drove another length of oakum home.

Astrid let it go. There was a whole country of things she wasn't asking yet — why the syndicate wanted this woman badly enough to send Rook, what Elias Vane had really carried besides a map, why Lin flinched at the word *father* the way other people flinched at gunfire. The rain gave them both permission not to talk. It fell on the deck above like a conversation neither of them had to join.

* * *

The seam forward was the worst, and it couldn't be reached from inside. Astrid went over the side in the afternoon, into water warm as bathwater and brown as tea, working the hull by feel while Lin held the line and talked her through what she could see from above — a blister here, a weep there. When Astrid hauled herself back aboard, streaming, Lin was waiting with a towel that was almost dry and an expression Astrid couldn't read.

"You didn't have to go in yourself," Lin said.

"Boat's mine."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got."

Lin laughed — a short, startled sound, as if it had escaped custody. And Astrid found herself smiling back before she could stop it, which was more alarming than any bilge leak. She took the towel, scrubbed at her hair, and turned away to hide whatever her face was doing.

* * *

That evening the rain eased to a steady whisper and the two of them sat in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low, sharing a pot of rice porridge Lin had made with fish sauce and too much chili, the way her uncle used to. The Kestrel rocked gently against her lines. Somewhere across the flooded street, a dog barked twice and gave up.

"My father built this boat," Astrid said. She hadn't planned to say it. The words just came out, the way water finds a seam.

Lin looked up from her bowl.

"He was a surveyor. Ran cargo and instruments up and down this stretch for thirty years. Taught me to read water before I could read words." Astrid turned her father's compass over in her hands — brass, dented, the needle still true after everything. "He died four years ago. Heart, in his sleep, on this very bench you're sitting on. I found him in the morning and steered us home myself, because there was nobody else."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. He'd have hated dying anywhere else." She meant it as a joke. It came out sounding like grief, because it was. "After he was gone I owed money everywhere. Boat papers, fuel, mooring fees. The syndicate bought up my paper piece by piece until they owned most of me. That's the debt. That's what Rook holds."

"And if you don't pay?"

"They take the Kestrel." Astrid said it flatly, the way you say a thing you've rehearsed saying so it won't hurt. "And then I'm a woman with a compass and no current."

Lin was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked on the glass.

"My father left when I was nine," she said finally. "I used to think he died. It was easier. A dead man can't choose to stay away." She wrapped both hands around her bowl. "My mother never told me what he did. Just that he was bad at being a husband and worse at being gone. Then last month a letter came. No return address. Just coordinates and a sentence: *If anything happens to me, find the woman who runs the Kestrel.*"

"So you came looking for me."

"I came looking for him." Lin's jaw tightened. "He's alive somewhere, and he sent me to a stranger instead of coming himself. Twenty years, and his apology is a set of coordinates."

The anger in her voice had a cracked place in the middle of it, and Astrid recognized it — the sound of someone grieving a person who was still breathing. She knew exactly how much worse that was than the clean kind.

"Elias is dying," Astrid said quietly. "Was dying. Fever, coughing blood. He traded me the map for a promise because he couldn't make the trip himself."

Lin stared at her. "You've known since the dock."

"I've known since the dock."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because you looked at me like I was the only solid thing in a river full of lies, and I didn't want to be the one who—" Astrid stopped. Started over. "Because telling you felt like taking something from you. And I've had enough taken."

The lamp flame shivered. Outside, the rain thickened again, and the river rose against the hull with a patience that felt personal.

Lin set down her bowl. For a moment Astrid thought she'd get up, walk out, stand in the rain rather than sit in this. Instead she reached over and touched the compass in Astrid's palm — one finger, light as a moth landing.

"He trusted you," Lin said. "A stranger. With his last thing."

"People trust me with cargo. It's different."

"No." Lin shook her head slowly. "It isn't. Cargo is just promise with weight."

Astrid looked at her — at the rain-shadowed face, the stubborn mouth, the hands that had spent all day saving Astrid's boat without being asked — and felt something shift in her chest like ballast sliding. Dangerous. Unaffordable. Warm.

"We should sleep," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended.

"Yes," Lin agreed, and neither of them moved for a long moment, the compass warm between their hands, the river rising around them like a held breath.

* * *

Astrid woke near midnight to silence — the particular, wrong silence of an engine compartment that should be dripping and wasn't. She slipped forward with the torch and found the forward bilge dry, the new caulking holding, and Lin asleep sitting up beside the pump handle, her head tipped against the bulkhead, one hand still resting on the wood as if she'd fallen asleep mid-vigil.

Astrid stood in the doorway a long time, watching her breathe.

Then the radio crackled behind her, low volume, the night band she kept tuned out of habit. A voice, clipped and official, reading advisories. Flood levels. Closed gorges. And then, buried between bulletins, a notice repeated twice:

*All vessels report sightings of a white-hulled riverboat, forty feet, name Kestrel, believed traveling northbound. Reward offered. Report to Golden Triangle Navigation Authority.*

Golden Triangle Navigation Authority. Astrid knew exactly whose payroll wore that letterhead.

Behind her, Lin stirred in her sleep and murmured something — a name, maybe, or a place. Astrid clicked off the radio, stood alone in the dark wheelhouse with the rain beginning again on the glass, and understood that the storm outside was the least of what was coming for them.
