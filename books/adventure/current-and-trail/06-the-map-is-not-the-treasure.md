## 6. The Map Is Not the Treasure

Lin had never known her father to be a careful man, but she had always known him to be a deliberate one. There was a difference. Careful men checked their knots twice; deliberate men tied them in a way that meant something only to the person who'd been taught the tying.

She sat cross-legged on the Kestrel's deck in the blue hour before dawn, the map spread across her knees, weighted at the corners with four river stones Astrid had collected without being asked. The paper was soft as cloth now, worn along its folds into creases that felt like scars. Elias Vane had drawn it in pencil and ink, and he had drawn it twice — once for the eye and once, Lin was beginning to understand, for someone who knew how to look.

The first map was simple enough. A stretch of the upper Mekong rendered in a prospector's confident hand: the gorge narrows, a tributary mouth shaped like a heron's beak, a shelf of limestone where the water ran green even in dry season. An X, of course. Prospectors never resisted an X. It sat in a fold of hills east of the river, and beside it, in Elias's cramped lettering: *jade — all of it — enough.*

It was the second map that kept her awake.

She'd noticed it three nights ago, by lamplight, when the rain came down so hard the boat seemed to travel through a single unbroken sheet of silver. She'd held the page up to the flame and seen what the light revealed: ghost lines beneath the ink, pressed hard enough into the paper to leave grooves, then covered over with hasty strokes. Her father had written something on this sheet before he'd drawn the map on top of it. He hadn't erased it. He'd buried it.

For two nights she'd traced the grooves with her fingertip, letting the lamp throw shadows into them, building the words one letter at a time like a woman reconstructing a face from a skull. And this morning, with the sky still dark and the river muttering against the hull, she had finished.

*They will come for it,* the hidden writing said. *Let them. Every man who ever cheated me will follow this map into the flood country, and the river will do what I could not. Tell my daughter I am sorry the gift is a trap. Tell her the jade is real. Tell her I chose her over it, in the end, because the trap needs a courier no one would suspect — someone with nothing to lose and a debt to pay.*

Lin read it four times. Then she read it again, and each time the last line landed harder, like a stone dropped from a greater height.

*Someone with nothing to lose and a debt to pay.*

She looked up. Across the deck, under the tarp strung between the wheelhouse and the rail, Astrid Hartwell slept sitting up, as she seemed to do half the time — boots on, one hand loose near the compass that lived in her jacket pocket, her head tipped back at an angle that spoke of years of sleeping in places where sleep wasn't safe. Even unconscious, there was nothing soft about her. She was all angles and weathering, a woman carved down to function.

And she owed the syndicate. Lin had known that since the checkpoint, since Rook had stood on this deck and spoken about papers and interest the way other men talked about weather. A captain drowning in debt, desperate for money, handed a treasure map by a dying stranger. Of course she'd considered selling it. Any sane person would.

But her father had *counted* on it.

That was the part that made Lin's hands go cold despite the wet heat of the morning. Elias hadn't just predicted Astrid might sell the map — he'd built his whole design around it. A map changing hands quietly, sold to some broker or syndicate middleman, would draw the old partners out of their fortified territory, out onto the river, into monsoon country where their gunboats couldn't maneuver and their money couldn't protect them. Her father was dead and he was still setting a snare, and the bait wasn't jade.

The bait was Astrid.

*Someone with nothing to lose.* Her father had looked at a stranger on a fuel dock and seen exactly what Lin saw now — a woman so cornered by circumstance that she could be relied upon to act in her own interest. He'd used that. He'd wrapped his revenge around a drowning woman and called it a gift.

Lin folded the map slowly, along its old creases, and found she was shaking. Not with fear. With something closer to grief, and grief's ugly twin, rage.

"You're up early."

Astrid stood at the wheelhouse door, hair damp, a tin mug in each hand. She moved like the boat did — economical, nothing wasted. She set one mug within Lin's reach without ceremony. Coffee, cut with condensed milk, sweet enough to be an apology for something.

Lin didn't take it.

"We need to talk," she said.

Astrid's eyes narrowed a fraction. She'd spent enough days with Lin by now to know that tone. She lowered herself onto the bench across from her, unhurried, but Lin watched her hand drift — casually, deliberately — to the pocket where the compass lived. A habit. A tell. "Talk, then."

Lin unfolded the map and laid it flat between them. "Hold this up to a flame."

"I've seen it in every light there is."

"Not this one." Lin's voice came out flatter than she intended. "There's writing underneath. Pressed into the paper before he drew the map. He wrote it first, then covered it." She struck the match herself, held it low behind the page, and watched the ghost lines rise out of the fibers like drowned things surfacing.

Astrid leaned in. Lin watched her read it — watched the stillness come over her the way fog comes over water, total and silent. Watched her lips move slightly on the last line. Watched her jaw tighten.

"He used you," Lin said.

Astrid didn't answer immediately. When she did, her voice was carefully level. "He used both of us."

"No." Lin shook her head. "He *used* you. He *warned* me. That's what the hidden writing is — it's addressed to whoever deciphers it, and he knew I would, because he taught me to look for things under things when I was nine years old. He knew I'd find it. He wanted me to know what he'd done, so I could decide what to do about it." She pulled the map back. "You weren't supposed to know. You were supposed to be exactly what he needed — broke, desperate, willing to sell."

Something flickered across Astrid's face. Not guilt, exactly. Something older and more tired.

"Say what you're actually saying," Astrid said quietly.

"I'm saying you were going to sell it." Lin heard her own voice rise and hated it. "Before I told you who I was. Before any of this. You took a dying man's promise and you were already pricing it in your head. Don't lie to me, Captain. I've been on this boat long enough to know your tells too."

The silence stretched. Somewhere downstream a village dog barked at the gray light. Astrid turned her mug slowly in her hands, and when she finally spoke, she didn't dress it up.

"Yes," she said. "I was going to sell it. Before I knew you existed, I stood on a dock in Chiang Khong and priced that map at forty thousand baht to the first broker who'd take it, and I'd have taken thirty and thanked him." She met Lin's eyes. "I owed eleven months of payments on this boat and the collector had already come aboard once. You want me to apologize for surviving?"

"I want you to admit it wasn't survival. Not after. After you learned about me, after we—" Lin stopped. The word she almost said sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. *After us.* Whatever they had become during the flooded weeks — the shared work, the histories traded in lamplight, the way Astrid had started leaving coffee for her without being asked — it had a weight now, and the weight had changed everything. "After you knew whose map it was. After you knew there was a daughter. You could have told me you were thinking of selling it. Instead I find out from a dead man's handwriting."

"And what would you have done if I'd told you?" Astrid's voice stayed low, but there was steel under it now. "Asked nicely? Told me the truth about what my own promise was worth? Because here's what I see, Lin: your father lied to me from the first breath. He dressed a trap up as a treasure and handed it to the one person on this river desperate enough to carry it. And you sat on that knowledge for three days. Three nights, watching me steer us toward syndicate water, and you said nothing. So don't stand there talking about betrayal like it only flows one direction."

Lin opened her mouth and found nothing in it. Because Astrid was right, and the rightness of it stung worse than the accusation. She had deciphered the message and then hesitated — not to protect Astrid, though she'd told herself that, but because she hadn't known what she'd feel, or what she'd want, once the words were out.

"The difference," Lin said finally, "is that I was trying to find a way to tell you that wouldn't break this. You were going to sell my father's last thing without telling me at all."

"Your father's last thing is a weapon pointed at both of us." Astrid stood, restless, and paced to the rail. The river slid past, brown and swollen, carrying branches and whole islands of hyacinth. "You think the syndicate doesn't already know about the cache? Rook boarded this boat looking for it. Your father's old partners are sitting inside those coordinates like spiders, and Elias meant to drag them out by making them chase this map — chase *us* — into flood season. We're not couriers, Lin. We're the lure. And lures don't usually survive the strike."

"Then we change the plan. We go around—"

"There is no around. Look at the map again. The only approach to that valley is the gorge, and the gorge is Rook's territory, and Rook has marked this boat." Astrid turned back, and in the growing light Lin saw how exhausted she was, how much of her strength was performance. "Every choice we make now runs through people who want us dead or owned. Your father knew that. It's why he picked a captain with debts instead of a daughter with choices."

The words landed like a slap. Lin stood, the map crushed in her fist. "Don't you dare talk about him like you knew him."

"I don't have to know him. I've read his handwriting for two hundred kilometers." Astrid's voice cracked, just barely, on the last word, and Lin saw — suddenly, unbearably — that Astrid wasn't angry. She was hurt. Hurt in the specific way of a woman who had started, against every instinct, to trust, and had just been shown the bill for it. "I trusted you on this boat. I hid you under my engine bilge while a man with a gun searched six feet away. I lied for the first time in ten years, and I told myself it was for the promise. But maybe I did it because I didn't want to be alone on this river anymore. Maybe that's the real price of your father's map. It found the one soft place I had left and put a hook in it."

The morning had fully arrived now, merciless and bright, and the flooded village they'd sheltered in was waking around them — smoke rising from cooking fires, children shouting somewhere in the shallows. Ordinary life, going on, indifferent to the wreckage happening on the deck of the Kestrel.

Lin looked at the map in her hand, at her father's careful double-drawing, at the X that was real and the trap that was realer. *Tell her the jade is real.* He had chosen her over it, he'd written. In the end. As if the end were a place you could stand and wave from.

"Astrid." Her voice came out rough. "What do we do? Right now. Today. Forget him, forget the debt, forget all of it. What do *we* do?"

Astrid looked at her for a long moment, and whatever was breaking between them hung in the air, unfinished, neither of them willing to name it or bury it. Then Astrid's gaze went past Lin's shoulder, downstream, and sharpened.

"Decide fast," she said quietly. "Because that's Rook's gunboat coming around the bend, and it's running straight at us."
