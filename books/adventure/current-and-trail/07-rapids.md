## 7. Rapids

**Astrid**

The gorge announced itself an hour before they reached it, the way grief does — a narrowing you feel in your chest before your eyes confirm it. The Mekong, fat and brown with monsoon runoff, funneled between walls of black limestone so close that Astrid could have thrown a line to either bank. The water inside ran white and furious, braided with rocks that showed themselves only as seams of foam.

She stood at the wheel with her father's compass open on the binnacle beside her, though it was useless here. No course but forward. The Kestrel shuddered beneath her boots, her old engine pushing hard against a current that wanted them backward, or worse, sideways.

"Tell me again why we didn't wait for the water to drop," Lin said from the rail. She'd tied her hair back and wedged herself against the deckhouse, arms crossed, watching the gorge swallow the river ahead of them.

"Because Rook's gunboat is anchored at Ban Houayxay, and every day we wait is a day he finds another way to board us." Astrid eased the throttle a hair wider. "The narrows are dangerous. He knows they're dangerous. Men like him don't like danger they can't control."

"And we do?"

"We don't like him more."

Lin almost smiled. Almost. It was strange, Astrid thought, how quickly she had learned to read this woman — the small tells around her mouth, the way fear made her stiller instead of louder. Ten days on the river together and Astrid could chart Lin's moods the way she charted sandbars: by what broke the surface and what lay hidden under it.

The first rock shelf hit without warning. The Kestrel lurched, her hull grinding over something submerged, and Astrid threw her weight against the wheel, correcting, feeling for the boat's response through the wood like a pulse.

"Down!" she shouted. "Grab something!"

They entered the narrows proper and the world became noise. Water climbed the banks on both sides, churning through slots no wider than the boat itself. Spray came over the bow in cold sheets. Astrid worked the wheel in small, savage movements — never big corrections in white water, her father's voice in her head, *you don't fight the river, you negotiate* — threading foam seams, reading the dark glassy tongues of water that meant deep channels.

For four minutes, there was nothing but this. Nothing but the boat and the current and her hands. She had missed this, she realized. God help her, she had missed being exactly this alive.

Then she saw the smoke.

A thin gray thread rising from the far end of the gorge, where the river bent left around a spur of rock. A campfire? A village?

No. Diesel exhaust.

Rook's gunboat sat in the eddy below the last rapids, engine idling, positioned precisely where any vessel surviving the narrows would be spat out into calm water. Waiting. He hadn't chased them. He'd simply known the river better than she'd given him credit for — known that the narrows were a funnel, and all rivers pour their cargo out eventually.

"He's there," Lin said. She'd seen it too. Her voice was flat, but her knuckles were white on the rail.

Astrid's mind ran the channel ahead faster than the boat did. The main chute went straight to the gunboat. But there — starboard side, half-hidden behind the spur — a secondary slot, narrower, uglier, dropping through standing waves toward a back channel she couldn't see past the bend. Unmapped. Possibly suicide.

Possibly the only door.

"Hold on to something that loves you," Astrid said, and swung the wheel hard to starboard.

* * *

**Lin**

The world turned to white.

Lin had grown up inland, among tea terraces and her father's long absences, and she had never understood until this moment why sailors talked about water the way other people talked about gods. The Kestrel dropped into the standing wave and the bow buried itself, green water exploding across the deck, and for one endless second Lin was sure the river had simply decided to keep them.

Then the old boat clawed up and out, shedding tons of water, and they were through the first gate and into the second, and Astrid was shouting something Lin couldn't hear over the roar, and the wheel was turning, always turning, the captain's whole body leaning into it like a dancer mid-fight.

Lin held the rail and watched her.

It was a foolish thing to do in a place like this, but she couldn't stop. Astrid Hartwell at the wheel was a different creature from the wary, debt-haunted woman who counted fuel cans twice. Here she was certain. Every movement economical, every decision already made before the river could ask. Lin had spent her whole life running from men who wanted to own things — her father's map, her father's secrets, her father's mistakes — and she had never once seen anyone look as free as Astrid looked right now, wrestling a dying boat through a gorge full of murder.

The second wave took them broadside.

Lin felt the hull lift under her feet, tilt, keep tilting — and then the world inverted. Cold closed over her head. The river wasn't water anymore; it was a fist, a landslide, a thing with intent. Something struck her shoulder. She grabbed for the rail and found only spinning brown chaos, and then she was no longer on the boat at all.

The Mekong pulled her down.

Cold punched the air from her lungs. She tumbled, struck rock with her hip, tumbled again, and the surface was a rumor somewhere above her, a lighter darkness she couldn't reach. Her father's face floated through her mind — absurd, unfair, his handwriting on the map, *my Lin, if you're reading this* — and she thought, with strange clarity: *so this is how he felt, drowning in everything he wouldn't say.*

Her hand broke the surface. Someone seized it.

* * *

**Astrid**

She'd gone in after her without deciding to. That was the thing about love, Astrid would think later — it doesn't ask permission. One moment she was lashed to the wheel fighting to hold the channel; the next, the Kestrel was grinding herself onto the back-channel shoal with a scream of tortured planking, and Astrid was over the side with a coiled line around her chest, diving into water cold enough to stop a heart.

She caught Lin's hand on the second dive. The girl — the woman, she corrected herself even now, even here — was limp, dragging, and the current wanted them both. Astrid got an arm around her chest, locked her grip, and hauled on the line hand over hand while the river tried to peel them apart like it was peeling fruit.

Her shoulders burned. Her lungs burned. Somewhere above, the Kestrel groaned against the rocks, and Astrid thought with perfect detachment: *there goes my boat, there goes my life, and I don't care, I don't care, just let me reach her.*

They broke the surface together in the eddy behind the shoal. Lin coughed, convulsed, vomited half the Mekong, and clung to Astrid with a desperation that had nothing guarded in it at all.

"I've got you," Astrid gasped. "I've got you. Breathe."

"You jumped," Lin rasped, when she could speak. "You jumped off your own boat."

"She was already wrecked." Astrid hauled them both toward the gravel bar, one arm around Lin, the line biting her ribs. "Boats can be argued with later. People can't."

They collapsed on the shingle together, soaked and shaking, while twenty meters away the Kestrel settled against the rocks with a long, mournful list, her rudder torn away, hanging by her stern line like a wounded animal tied to a post.

Lin rolled onto her back, chest heaving, and started to laugh — a wild, cracked sound with terror still in it. Astrid propped herself on an elbow and looked down at her, water streaming from her own hair, and the laughter died in both of them at once.

They were very close. Closer than they had ever been. Lin's lips were blue-tinged and trembling, her eyes huge and dark, and there was a cut above her brow that Astrid wanted, with a sudden ferocity that frightened her, to touch. To press her palm against and claim, somehow, as hers to protect.

Lin's gaze dropped to Astrid's mouth. Came back up.

The gorge roared around them, indifferent. Astrid leaned in — or Lin did, or the earth tilted, it was impossible to know — close enough to feel breath, close enough that the next inch would be a country neither of them could come back from—

The Kestrel groaned, shifted, and knocked loose a cascade of shale down the bank.

They broke apart like guilty children. Astrid sat up, scrubbed a hand through her wet hair, and found something urgent to do with the line coiled at her feet. Lin stood, wobbled, and busied herself wringing out her shirt with tremendous concentration.

"The rudder," Astrid said, too loudly. "I might be able to jury-rig something. If I can find the spare sweep below."

"Right," Lin said. "Yes. The rudder."

Neither of them moved for a moment. Downstream, around the bend, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of an engine opening its throttle — Rook's gunboat, giving up its ambush position, circling to hunt the back channels.

Astrid looked at the listing wreck of her home, then at Lin, then at the narrow strip of gravel between the cliff and the flood.

"Well," she said quietly. "We'd better move."

* * *

**Lin**

They dragged the spare steering sweep up from the flooded bilge while the light went gold and then gray, and Astrid worked with a fury Lin recognized because she wore it herself — the fury of people who cannot afford to feel what they are feeling, so they hammer it into something useful instead.

The sweep was a bad fit and a worse solution, lashed to the ruined rudder stock with rope and prayer. Astrid tested it, swore, re-lashed it, tested it again. Lin held the lantern and watched the muscles of the other woman's forearms work, and hated herself a little for noticing, and noticed anyway.

"You should have sold the map," Lin said suddenly. It came out before she could stop it. "Back at the dock. You planned to. You told me."

Astrid's hands went still on the lashings. "That was ten days ago."

"And now you jump off your boat into a flood for a stranger carrying it." Lin's voice shook, and not from cold. "Why? What changed?"

Astrid didn't answer right away. She tightened one last knot, harder than the knot required, and when she spoke she didn't turn around.

"Ask me again when we're clear of this gorge."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Astrid agreed. "It isn't."

Above them, on the cliff road that wasn't a road, headlights swept through the trees. Once. Twice. Then the growl of engines, several, moving downstream along the bank — parallel to them, patient, keeping pace.

Lin's blood went cold. "He's tracking us from shore. There'll be a checkpoint at the gorge mouth. There's always a checkpoint."

Astrid finally turned. In the lantern light, soaked and exhausted, she looked less defeated than Lin had ever seen her — she looked decided, the way she'd looked at the wheel, threading the impossible channel.

"Then we don't go to the gorge mouth," Astrid said. "There's an old logging slide, three kilometers down. Steep, ugly, drops straight into the pool below the rapids. Boats used to run timber down it before the war." She glanced up at the Kestrel, heeled against the rocks, taking water she couldn't shed. "She'll never make it through a drop like that intact."

"So we walk?"

"So we run her down the slide," Astrid said, "and we aim her at whatever Rook loves most."

Lin stared at her. "Which is what?"

Astrid picked up the compass from where it sat dripping on the thwart, closed the lid, and slid it into her shirt pocket, over her heart.

"His leverage," she said. "Come dawn, we're going to take it away from him."
