## 8. Debt and Promise

Rook came aboard at dusk, alone, which was how Astrid knew it was a negotiation.

He stood at the rail of the *Kestrel* while the river went copper and then dark beneath him, hands folded behind his back like a man admiring the view. He'd tied his gunboat off downstream, out of sight of the village, and come up the bank in a longtail with two of his men waiting in it — visible, deliberate. A courtesy. *I could have brought six.*

"Captain Hartwell." He didn't turn when he spoke. "You've been hard to catch this season."

"The river's been hard to catch."

That earned her a thin smile. He turned then, and the last light caught the scar that ran from his ear to his collarbone — a story she'd heard three versions of, none of them his. "May I?" He gestured at the wheelhouse without waiting for permission, and stepped inside as though the boat were already his.

Astrid stayed by the door. Old habit: keep the exit at your back, keep your hand where the flare gun lived.

"You're carrying something for me," Rook said. "A map. And you're carrying someone who belongs to my employer."

"She's a passenger."

"She's a debt." He said it mildly, the way a man reads figures off a ledger. "As are you. Forty thousand baht, Captain, plus interest, plus the cost of my time chasing you down three provinces." He let that sit. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere across the water a dog was barking at nothing. "Here is what I'm authorized to offer. You give me the map and the girl, and every paper you owe on comes back to you clean. Signed, stamped, no liens. The *Kestrel* becomes yours outright — free and clear, first time in her life and yours."

The words landed in Astrid's chest like ballast dropped into still water. Free and clear. She'd been indentured to those papers since her father died owing on the hull; she'd piloted eleven years knowing that one bad season, one seized cargo, one collector's whim, and the syndicate would take the only thing she had ever loved that couldn't leave her. Every sandbar she'd threaded, every monsoon she'd outrun, she'd done it as a tenant on her own deck.

And all it cost was a dead man's promise and a woman who slept in her engine room with a knife under her pillow because she'd learned to sleep that way young.

"I'd want it in writing," Astrid said.

Rook's smile widened a fraction. "Of course."

"And I'd want the girl delivered somewhere safe. Not turned over at some checkpoint. Chiang Saen. Her father wanted her there."

Something flickered behind Rook's eyes — not surprise exactly. Recalculation. "Her father," he said slowly, "is the reason any of us are standing here. Elias Vane stole from people who do not forgive stealing. What he wanted stopped mattering the day he died."

"It matters to me."

"Why?" Genuine curiosity, almost gentle. That was the worst thing about Rook — he was never cruel when patience worked better. "You've known her three weeks. You knew your boat eleven years. I'm offering you the arithmetic any sane captain would take."

Astrid looked past him, out the wheelhouse window, to where Lin sat on the foredeck mending a torn tarp, needle flashing in the blue dusk, hair tied back with a strip of red cloth. Three weeks. Lin had learned the sound of the engine the way Astrid had — could hear a fouled intake before the gauge told her. Lin had laughed, once, actually laughed, when the river threw them sideways in the gorge and Astrid had sworn in three languages. Lin had held her wrist in the black water and not let go.

Three weeks against eleven years. It wasn't arithmetic at all. Arithmetic was what you did when the things being weighed were the same kind of thing.

"I'll need until tomorrow night," Astrid said. "She doesn't travel well. If I grab her sudden, she'll go over the side with the map, and you'll fish both out of the flood yourself."

Rook considered her for a long moment. The dog across the water had stopped barking. "Tomorrow night," he said. "The old temple landing, below the bend. One boat, no crew on your deck but you." He moved toward the door, then paused beside her, close enough that she could smell cigarette smoke and river mold on him. "Captain. I've watched you lie twice now. You're bad at it. Don't practice."

He went down the ladder and his longtail pulled away into the dark, running lights off, and Astrid stood in the wheelhouse with her heart going like a piston and her face doing absolutely nothing at all.

* * *

Lin found her an hour later in the bilge, headlamp between her teeth, hands elbow-deep in the guts of the engine.

"He offered you the boat," Lin said. Not a question.

Astrid spat out the headlamp and sat back on her heels. "How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough." Lin crouched at the edge of the light, arms on her knees. In the glow her face was half shadow, half something too steady to be fear. "Say it anyway. Say what he offered."

"Clean papers. No debt. The *Kestrel* mine outright." Astrid wiped her hands on a rag that was already beyond saving. "For you and the map."

"And you said—"

"I said I needed until tomorrow night."

Lin was quiet. The engine dripped. Out on the river, the flood muttered against the hull like something large turning over in its sleep.

"That's not an answer," Lin said finally.

"No," Astrid agreed. "It isn't."

"You could take it." Lin's voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone walking a plank they'd built themselves. "Anyone would. God, Astrid — anyone *sane* would. I've been running from these people since I was nineteen. I know what they are. I wouldn't even blame—"

"Stop." Astrid reached up and took the headlamp off the engine block, set it down. "Listen to me. I have spent eleven years paying other people's interest so I could keep a boat that was never really mine. My father signed those papers because he thought he was giving me a future, and instead he gave me a leash, and I've been telling myself for a decade that the leash was a lifeline." She stood, joints cracking, and faced her. "Elias Vane handed me a folded piece of paper in a fuel dock and asked me for one thing, and I shook his hand on it. That's the whole ledger. That's everything. I don't get to trade a handshake for a title deed just because the handshake got expensive."

Lin stared at her. In the headlamp's spill her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Then why did you ask Rook for until tomorrow?"

"Because if I'd said no tonight, he'd have taken the boat tonight." Astrid allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "I lied badly on purpose. He thinks I'm thinking about it. Men like Rook can't imagine anyone choosing worse odds than the ones on the table — so he'll wait, and he'll feel clever, and while he waits we're going to be gone."

"Gone where? He'll have the bend covered. He'll have the whole river covered."

"Not the whole river." Astrid picked up the rag, the headlamp, eleven years of knowing this boat the way she knew her own pulse. "Nobody covers the whole river in flood. Come on. We have work to do, and less than a day to do it."

* * *

It was near midnight, wedged under the aft bunk pulling out the spare sea-cock wrench, that Astrid's knuckles found the loose panel she'd never noticed in eleven years of owning — of *living on* — this boat.

The panel swung open on a cavity lined with oilcloth. Inside: a tin box, rusted at the corners, and inside the tin, a bundle wrapped in waxed paper. Letters. Dozens of them, unsent, addressed in a cramped, educated hand to *L.* — and on top, a single envelope with her own name on it.

*Astrid Hartwell.*

She sat down hard on the bunk frame. Lin appeared in the doorway, drawn by the silence, and read the name upside down and went very still.

"He hid them on your boat," Lin whispered. "He hid them on *your* boat."

"He never came aboard." But even as she said it, Astrid remembered — the fuel dock, Elias's fevered grip on her forearm, the way his eyes had tracked the *Kestrel*'s lines with something more than a passenger's appraisal. *You keep her well, Captain. She'll keep you better.* She'd taken it for a dying man's sentiment.

She opened the envelope. The paper was soft with damp, the handwriting looser than the rest, written close to the end.

*Captain —*

*If you're reading this, you found the hiding place, which means you were looking for something to save the girl with, which means you've already decided. Good. I won't insult you by pretending I left the map with you by accident. I chose you because Rook's people watch the roads and the checkpoints and the ferry landings, and none of them watch a woman working her own boat through low water. And I chose you because my daughter runs from kindness like it's a bill collector, and I judged — correctly, I think — that you don't offer any you don't mean.*

*You should know what the cache is. It's real. It's also poisoned bait — I salted those coordinates years ago to draw my old partners into the open, and I never stopped meaning to warn whoever carried the map. Consider yourself warned. The jade is a story. The story is worth killing for. That's the trap, and it works on everyone except people who care about something more than the prize.*

*I know what they'll offer you. They offered me the same once — my debts gone, my daughter safe, my silence bought. I took the money instead and lost twenty years to that choice. Don't be me, Captain. Be the thing I couldn't be.*

*One more thing. Whatever you decide, decide it as yourself. Not as your father's daughter paying his notes. Not as a debtor buying free. The river doesn't ask whose name is on the papers. It only asks if you can read water.*

*You can read water.*

*— E.V.*

Astrid read it twice. Then she folded it along its worn creases and held it a moment, the way you hold a compass you're trusting to bear true.

Below the letter, the other envelopes waited — twenty years of unsent words for a daughter who'd grown up believing herself abandoned. Astrid gathered the bundle and the map and Elias's letter together into the oilcloth and stood.

Lin hadn't moved from the doorway. "What does it say?"

Astrid crossed the small cabin and put the bundle in her hands. "It says your father was a coward for twenty years and brave for one day, and he spent the brave day choosing you." She watched Lin's fingers tighten on the paper. "And it says the coordinates are a trap. The jade's a lie, Lin. The whole cache is bait he set for the syndicate — and Rook knows the map's real, which means Rook will follow it straight into the gorge."

Lin looked up slowly. Understanding moved across her face like weather.

"So we don't run," Astrid said. Outside, far downstream, the flood boomed against the bar at the river's throat, deep and patient as a drum. "We give him exactly what he's coming for. Tomorrow night, the temple landing — one boat, no crew but me." She smiled, and for the first time in eleven years it didn't cost her anything. "Let's go show him what this boat can carry."
