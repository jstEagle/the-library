## 9. High Water

**Astrid**

The river had never looked so much like an enemy, or so much like the only friend she had left.

Astrid stood at the Kestrel's wheel with her father's compass open in her palm, watching the needle tremble as if it knew what she intended. Below her feet, the old boat groaned through brown water swollen past every marker she'd known for fifteen years. The banks were gone. The trees along them stood waist-deep, their leaves trailing in the current like drowned men waving goodbye. Somewhere behind her, close enough now that she could hear the low diesel thrum of its engines over her own, came Dane Rook's gunboat.

She had told him yes. That was the poison of it. She'd sat across from him in the flooded customs house at Ban Sok with rain hammering the tin roof and said, *I'll give you the map and the girl,* and she had watched him believe her — because he wanted to believe her, because a woman drowning in debt will always say whatever keeps her head above water one more day. He'd even smiled. Rook's smile was like a knife being wiped clean.

*Tomorrow,* he'd said. *Dawn. We'll run down together to the narrows. You lead. I follow.*

So she would lead.

"Course is set," Lin said beside her, and Astrid felt the girl's shoulder press against hers — not leaning, just present, a warmth she was already learning to grieve. "Bilge pumps are running. She'll float until we don't need her to."

"She'll float until I tell her to stop," Astrid said. Her voice came out steadier than her hands. "After that, she can do whatever she likes."

Lin didn't answer right away. When Astrid glanced over, the younger woman was looking at her with that expression she still hadn't learned to hide — the one that made Astrid feel seen all the way down to the rivets.

"You're really going to do it," Lin said quietly. "You're going to sink her."

Astrid turned back to the water. Ahead, the gorge walls rose on either side, black stone streaked white with twenty years of flood lines, and between them the Mekong ran fast and high and furious, a single braided muscle of current. The narrows. In low season it was a chute you threaded with respect. In monsoon, at this volume, it was a place where the river stopped negotiating.

"My father used to say a boat isn't yours," she said. "You're just the part of it that makes decisions. The rest of it belongs to the water." She closed the compass. "Rook thinks the Kestrel is leverage. My debt, my papers, my home — all of it something he can hold over me. But you can't hold a man's throat underwater and expect him not to bite."

"And after?" Lin asked. "When there's no boat, no papers, no debt — when there's nothing?"

Astrid found she was smiling. It surprised her, the way grief sometimes does when it turns a corner into something else.

"After," she said, "there's you."

She saw Lin swallow. Saw her look away toward the gorge, jaw tight, eyes bright. Neither of them said anything else, because there wasn't time, and because some things survive better unspoken — packed away dry, like matches.

The gunboat's horn sounded behind them. Two short blasts. *Keep your pace.* Rook wanted her slow, obedient, predictable.

Astrid opened the throttle instead.

* * *

**Lin**

The Kestrel leapt forward like something young again, and Lin grabbed the rail and laughed — actually laughed, a wild sound torn out of her before she could stop it — because for one absurd moment the dying riverboat moved like freedom itself.

Behind them, the gunboat answered. Its engines deepened to a growl, and in the mirror of choppy water Lin watched it come: gray hull, mounted gun covered by a tarp that fooled no one, Rook standing at the bow with his hands clasped behind his back like a man inspecting orchards.

"He's not shooting," she shouted over the wind. "Why isn't he shooting?"

"Because he needs us floating!" Astrid called back, hands dancing across the wheel. "He needs the map intact and me cooperative! He thinks we're leading him to Elias's cache!" Her grin was ferocious. "He's about to be very disappointed about the second part."

The gorge swallowed them. Walls of wet stone closed in and the light went green-gray and the sound became enormous — water thundering off rock, the boat's hull slapping wave-tops, the engine screaming at a pitch Lin had never heard from it. Spray came over the bow in sheets. Astrid flew the boat through it like a woman reading scripture, every move certain, every correction half a heartbeat ahead of the river's temper.

Then the first rapid hit.

It came out of the gorge's throat without warning — a standing wave taller than the wheelhouse, brown and roaring — and Astrid took it at an angle, and the whole world tilted. Lin was thrown against the rail. Water crashed across the deck, tore the tarp off the spare fuel drums, ripped one overboard. The Kestrel shuddered, wallowed, and then Astrid hauled her straight and they were through, plunging down the far side in a chaos of foam.

Lin dragged herself back to the wheelhouse. Astrid's hair had come loose and plastered across her face; she looked like something the river had dreamed.

"The narrows," Astrid said. "Two hundred meters. When I give the word, you jump."

"I'm not leaving you—"

"You're not *leaving* me." Astrid caught her wrist, hard, and her eyes were blazing. "You're getting to the raft. Starboard side, behind the wheelhouse. The moment I turn her, you go over the side and you paddle for the eddy below the bend and you *wait for me*. That's an order, Lin. I've been captain of this wreck for fifteen years. Let me have my last command."

Lin stared at her. The river roared. And somewhere beneath the terror she understood what Astrid was actually saying — *live, live, live* — and that arguing would cost them both.

"Say it back," Astrid demanded.

"Eddy below the bend. I wait for you." Lin's voice cracked. "But you'd better come."

Astrid leaned in and pressed her forehead against Lin's, just for a second, spray and rain and river between them.

"Always," she said.

Then she spun the wheel hard to starboard, and the Kestrel began to die.

* * *

**Astrid**

She had planned it a hundred times in her head since Ban Sok, and it still felt like tearing out her own spine.

The gunboat was closing — fifty meters, forty — Rook's crew scrambling at the rails now, sensing something wrong, Rook himself leaning forward with his smile finally gone. Ahead, the narrows funneled the entire flood into a channel barely wider than two boats abreast, and the current there ran like a freight train falling downstairs.

Astrid cut the Kestrel's engine to idle. Let the gunboat eat the distance. Thirty meters. Twenty-five. Close enough to see the rivets, close enough to see Rook shouting something she refused to hear.

"Goodbye, old girl," she whispered.

And she threw the wheel hard to port and slammed the throttle full open, straight across the gunboat's bow.

The collision, when it came, was almost gentle — a grinding kiss of steel on steel as the Kestrel's hull met the gunboat's flank amidships, exactly where the old boat's seams had been weeping for years, exactly where Astrid had spent last night loosening every plate with a wrench and a prayer. The impact tore the Kestrel's bow open like a letter. Black water came in the way truth comes in — all at once, undeniable.

The gunboat fared worse. Rook's pilot panicked, overcorrected, and the current did the rest, swinging the heavy vessel broadside across the narrows where the river hit it like a fist. Astrid heard the crack of the hull taking the full weight of the flood, heard men screaming, heard Rook's voice cutting through it all, no longer commanding anything.

She was already moving. Over the rail, into water cold enough to stop her heart, swimming for the eddy while the Kestrel settled beneath her — slowly, almost dignified, the way an old animal lies down. She got one arm over the gunwale of the raft where Lin waited, and Lin hauled her up, and together they turned to watch.

The Kestrel went under stern-first, her bow rising once, dripping, like a hand raised in farewell. Then the river folded over her and she was gone. Fifteen years of her life, gone in ninety seconds.

The gunboat lasted longer. It slewed, grounded on the submerged shelf, rolled, and the flood peeled it apart piece by piece — the tarp, the gun mount, the gray plates — scattering Rook's empire down the Mekong like ash. Whether anyone survived, Astrid couldn't say and wouldn't pretend to mourn. The syndicate's claim, the papers, the debt — all of it signed to vessels that no longer existed, held by men who might never surface.

Lin gripped her hand as they swept downstream in the raft's lee, past the bend, into calmer water.

"It's gone," Lin said. "Your home."

Astrid watched the whirlpool where her boat had been, already smoothing itself flat, keeping no record, holding nothing.

"No," she said. She squeezed Lin's fingers and found, to her astonishment, that she was laughing — breathless, soaked, unburdened, alive. "My home is right here. Everything else was just the hull."

Ahead, the river widened toward Chiang Saen, gold with evening, carrying them on.
