## 10. Current and Trail

The river changed its voice in the last hundred kilometers.

Lin noticed it from the bow of the slow cargo barge they'd hitched out of the flood country — how the Mekong's northern growl softened into something older, wider, almost patient. The water spread itself thin over drowned fields and half-submerged temples, carrying the monsoon's leavings south: branches, roof thatch, a child's plastic boat spinning in an eddy like a toy forgotten by a giant. Chiang Saen announced itself first by smell — woodsmoke, drying fish, the sweet rot of fruit stacked too long at market — then by sound, temple bells and long-tail engines arguing across the water.

Astrid stood beside her at the rail, arms crossed against the spray, saying nothing. She had said very little for three weeks. Not the silence of anger or grief, Lin had learned to tell the difference now, but the silence of a woman taking inventory of herself and finding the ledger strangely empty. No boat. No debt. No syndicate collector waiting at any dock with her papers fanned out like a threat. Nothing owed, nothing owned. Astrid Hartwell, captain of nothing, standing on borrowed deck with a compass in her pocket and salt-cracked hands she kept flexing as if they still held a wheel.

Lin had watched her do it a hundred times since the Kestrel went down — reach for the helm that wasn't there, fingers closing on air, the small correction of a wrist that would never again matter. She never said anything about it. Some losses you let a person grieve privately, even when you're standing close enough to share the wind.

The barge grounded at the old ferry landing below town, where the mud was the color of strong tea and boys waded out to catch mooring lines. Lin shouldered her pack — her father's pack, patched at both straps, heavier than it looked because of what rode flat against its spine. The map. Elias Vane's map, soft as cloth now from handling, its ink lines traced and retraced until they seemed less drawn than grown, like veins in a leaf.

"You want me to carry it?" Astrid asked.

"It's mine to give." Lin adjusted the strap. "You carried it far enough."

Astrid's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling — one corner lifting, eyes going somewhere private. "Far enough," she agreed, and followed her up the bank.

* * *

Chiang Saen in late flood season was a town holding its breath between weathers. Market stalls perched on stilts above brown water; monks crossed the temple courtyard on planks laid end to end; children raced paper boats down the flooded lane outside the guesthouse where Lin had once lived, in another life, before her father disappeared into the hills and the letters stopped and the silence started meaning something different every year.

They found Mai on the second day.

Not because she was hard to find — because Lin made herself walk past the house twice before she could make her feet stop. It was the same house she'd left at nineteen: blue shutters gone gray, her mother's frangipani tree grown huge and leaning over the wall, dropping white flowers into the floodwater where they turned like small pale boats. A woman was on the porch sorting dried chilies, hands quick, hair streaked early with silver. When Lin pushed open the gate, the woman looked up with a stranger's polite caution, and then the chilies scattered across the porch boards and neither of them moved to pick them up.

"Lin." Her sister's voice cracked on her own name, as if she'd practiced it so many years alone it had worn through. "Lin, you're — he said you wouldn't—"

"I know what he said." Lin's throat was tight. "He was wrong about most things. He wanted to be wrong about that one."

Mai came down the steps fast, mud splashing, and held her hard enough to hurt, and Lin let herself be held, and over her sister's shoulder she saw Astrid turn away politely toward the river, giving them the privacy of her back, and something in Lin's chest that had been clenched since the fueling dock, since the stowaway dark of the hold, since Rook's flashlight sweeping the bilge — unclenched.

Inside, over tea that Mai's hands shook making, Lin laid the map on the table between them.

"He sent this," she said. "Before he died. He made me promise to bring it to you."

Mai didn't touch it at first. She looked at it the way people look at a grave. "I got letters," she said finally. "For years. Then nothing. I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I thought he'd chosen the river over us. That's what Ma always said. That the water took him a little more each year until there wasn't enough left to come home."

"He wasn't taken," Lin said. "He went looking. And near the end, I think he understood what the looking cost." She unfolded the map fully, weighted its corners with teacups, and told her sister everything — the cache, the coordinates, the annotations in Elias's cramped hand, the truth Lin had worked out alone on the Kestrel's deck while the rain hammered down: that the jade sat inside syndicate territory like bait on a hook, that her father had spent his last months not hunting treasure but setting a trap for the men who'd ruined him, and had died knowing his daughters might pay the price of springing it.

"And instead," Lin said quietly, "a woman I barely knew scuttled her own boat to keep him from winning that way. To keep it from costing anyone else."

Mai stared at her. "Her boat? Her whole—"

"Everything she had." Lin glanced toward the window, where Astrid stood talking to the neighbor boy about engine parts, gesturing with those restless hands. "She gave it up to break their claim on the map. On me. On all of it." She slid the map across the table. "It's yours now. Do what you want with it. Burn it, if that's cleaner. But read the back first."

Mai turned it over with careful fingers. There, in the last corner, in handwriting gone thin and wavering near the end, Elias had written a letter he'd never sent — Lin had found it only after the rapids, after the cold, after Astrid pulled her gasping from the water and held her until her shaking stopped. It was short. It was not an apology; Elias Vane had never been built for apology. It was an accounting. What he'd taken from the river, what the river had taken from him, and what he hoped his daughters would take from neither: *Don't follow my trail. Make your own current. I loved you both more than the finding, and I'm sorry it took me dying to say it in ink.*

Mai read it three times. On the third pass she began to cry, silently, the way their mother used to, and Lin reached across the table and covered her sister's hand with her own, and outside the frangipani dropped another white flower into the water, and somewhere downriver a boat horn sounded long and low, like the river itself acknowledging a debt paid.

* * *

They stayed four days. Long enough for Mai to feed them until they ached, for the flood to drop a hand's width off the temple steps, for Lin to sleep without dreaming of white water. On the fourth evening, walking back from the market through lanes that smelled of rain on hot stone, Astrid finally spoke the thing she'd been circling for weeks.

"There's a cargo run leaving Friday," she said, eyes fixed straight ahead. "Downstream. Teak and tin, two months' circuit, maybe three. They need a first mate who knows the upper reaches." A pause, the length of a heartbeat held underwater. "They don't need two."

Lin walked three steps before she answered. "Then it's lucky I'm not applying for the job."

Astrid stopped in the middle of the lane. A monk passed them, sandals slapping, unbothered. "Lin."

"I'm not asking to crew your boat, Astrid. You don't have a boat." She watched the relief and terror fight across the other woman's face and decided, as she had decided once already in flood-swollen rapids with her lungs full of river, to jump anyway. "I'm telling you where I'll be. Same deck, whatever deck that is. You can spend the whole run pretending it's coincidence if it makes you feel more like yourself."

"That could take months," Astrid said.

"I've got time." Lin started walking again. "Current and trail, remember? My father's words. He spent his life thinking they were opposites — that you either drifted or you searched. Took me twenty-six years and one sunk boat to figure out they're the same thing. You just have to pick your company well."

Astrid caught up in four strides. She didn't take Lin's hand — not yet, not here, though her little finger brushed Lin's once, deliberately, and left a warmth that lasted the rest of the street. At the corner where the lane met the levee road, she stopped and pulled the compass from her pocket, the brass one, her father's, its glass clouded but its needle true. She turned it over in her palm, north swinging lazy beneath the crystal, and Lin realized she was watching her decide something.

"My father gave me this and told me a compass doesn't tell you where to go," Astrid said. "Only which way you're facing. I spent ten years facing debt." She closed her hand around it. "Friday, then. First mate and stowaway. Old habits."

"Not a stowaway," Lin said. "Crew. Papers and everything."

"Papers," Astrid repeated, tasting the word like something exotic. She smiled — actually smiled, both corners, brief as lightning — and looked out at the river running wide and gold in the last light, running south, running on, carrying its freight of branches and flowers and small lost boats toward a sea neither of them had ever seen.

"Come on," she said, turning back toward Mai's house, toward dinner, toward Friday. "We've got a current to catch."

And Lin followed, easy as water finding its level, down to the river that had taken everything and given back more than either of them had dared to ask for — down to the trail, and the woman walking it beside her, and the long bright miles still ahead.
