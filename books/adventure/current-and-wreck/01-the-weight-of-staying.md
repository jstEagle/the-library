## 1. The Weight of Staying

The trench gave up its dead slowly, the way it always did. Rosalind knelt in the wet sand at the base of the cut, trowel moving in short, patient strokes, and let the work do what the work always did: make her small, make her quiet, make her into nothing but a pair of hands and a problem to solve.

Three ribs. A cranium, fractured along the parietal suture—perimortem, not post. A pewter buckle gone green as sea glass. She'd been on the burial for two days now, and by tomorrow afternoon she'd have it drawn, photographed, lifted, and boxed. By Friday she'd have her report filed under *R. Calloway, contract archaeologist*, and by Saturday—

By Saturday she'd be gone.

That was the shape of every season. Arrive in September with one duffel bag and a forged-enough CV, dig until the money ran out or the ground did, then fold herself back onto a ferry like smoke poured into a bottle. No attachments. No real names. No more than one season anywhere. Three rules, older than some of the students she'd trained, and she'd never once broken them.

She sat back on her heels and flexed her fingers. The wind off the harbor carried salt and diesel and the fryer-smell from the chippy two streets over, and somewhere behind her the site hut radio was playing something tinny and cheerful that nobody was listening to. The town was called Pellow's Reach, population eleven hundred, and it had taken her exactly four days to learn where everyone bought their bread and exactly zero days to decide she would never need to know anything else about it.

"Roz." Dev, the site supervisor, stood at the lip of the trench with his clipboard hugged to his chest like a shield. He was twenty-six, earnest, and had a crush on her so transparent it was almost sweet. "Client's here. Early."

"Tell him the bones aren't going anywhere."

"He wants to talk to you specifically. Something about the report." Dev hesitated. "He's brought someone."

Rosalind climbed out of the trench, brushing sand from her knees, and followed Dev across the site toward the parking area where a silver Range Rover sat crooked across two spaces, engine still ticking. The client was Aldous Merrick, retired shipping magnate, owner of half the coastline they were surveying ahead of a marina development. She'd met him once at the start of the season, shaken his soft hand, and given him forty-five seconds of polite conversation before escaping to the finds tent.

Beside him stood another man. Sixty-ish, silver-haired, wearing a Barbour jacket that cost more than Rosalind's entire kit. He had the particular stillness of someone who spent his life being listened to.

"Miss Calloway," Merrick said, beaming. "This is an old friend of mine. Edmund Voss. He's been following your work with great interest."

*There it is.* The cold arrived in her stomach before her brain caught up, the way it always did—a split second of animal warning before thought. *Following your work.*

"Professor Voss," she said, extending a hand. "I don't think we've met."

"No," he said, and his grip was dry and brief. "But I knew your father."

The world did not stop. That was the first thing she noticed—the gulls kept screaming, the radio kept playing, Dev kept shuffling his clipboard—and that made it worse somehow, the sheer indifference of everything while a stranger said four words and dismantled thirteen years of careful construction.

"I'm sorry," Rosalind said. Her voice came out level. She'd practiced this voice in mirrors, in bathrooms, in the backs of taxis. "You must be thinking of someone else."

"Rosalind Coldharbour," Voss said gently. "Daughter of Elias Coldharbour. You have his hands—I noticed it in the photographs from the Orkney report. The way you hold a trowel. He held it the same way, like he was apologizing to the soil." He smiled, and there was no cruelty in it, which was somehow more terrible. "I sat next to him at a conference in Bergen in '09. He talked about you. Said you could read a section face better than anyone he'd taught."

Behind her, Dev had gone very still. She could feel him recalculating—*Calloway, Coldharbour, the famous name, the woman who'd been quietly competent all season was actually royalty*—and she hated him a little for it, which was unfair, because he hadn't done anything except exist within earshot.

"You're mistaken," she said. "My father drove lorries. He died when I was young."

"Of course," Voss said, and the gentleness in his voice was unbearable. "Forgive me. It's just the resemblance. Remarkable, really." He turned to Merrick. "Shall we look at the marina plans?"

They walked off toward the site office, Merrick talking, Voss listening, and Rosalind stood in the trampled grass with the wind pulling at her hair and understood, with total clarity, that the season was over.

* * *

She packed that night.

Not frantically—that was the old way, the way she'd left Cork and Caithness and the island off Brittany, slamming drawers, burning bridges. Now she packed the way she dug: methodically, in layers, oldest at the bottom. Field clothes rolled tight. Trowel wrapped in a sock. The false passport in the lining of the duffel, the real one buried deeper. She'd learned to pack in fifteen minutes flat, and she could do it without thinking, which meant she did it while thinking about other things entirely, which was dangerous, because tonight the other things were Voss's face and the word *Bergen* and the image of her father holding a trowel like an apology.

Elias Coldharbour. Sir Elias, after the knighthood. The man who'd found the Halcyon hoard and rewritten three chapters of the Bronze Age and never once, in Rosalind's memory, come to a school play. She'd spent her twenties excavating his reputation the way other people excavated tells—layer by layer, finding the same thing every time: a brilliant man who loved the past because it couldn't disappoint him, and a daughter who'd learned early that the fastest way to be invisible to Elias Coldharbour was to stand directly in front of him.

So she'd become invisible everywhere else too. It had seemed elegant at the time. A solution.

She zipped the duffel and sat on the narrow bed in her rented room above the chippy, listening to the fryers cooling below, the pipes ticking, the sea doing its endless slow arithmetic against the harbor wall. In six hours the first ferry would leave. In seven she'd be a different person in a different county with a different surname, and none of this would have happened.

That was the deal. That had always been the deal. Freedom in exchange for everything else.

Her phone buzzed on the nightstand. Dev.

*heard voss is some big deal archaeologist. did u know him?*

She stared at it for a long time. There was a version of her—she could see her clearly, a ghost in the room, sitting cross-legged on the bed with her boots still on—who typed back *yes, sit with me, I'll tell you everything*. That version had friends. That version stayed through winters. That version signed her real name to things.

Rosalind typed: *no. leaving tomorrow anyway. good luck with the marina.*

Then she turned the phone face-down, and lay back, and looked at the ceiling, and felt the loneliness arrive the way it always did—not like a wave but like a tide, gradual and total, filling up all the space the packing had cleared. This was the price. She'd known the price when she set it. Nobody got to be free and also held. She'd chosen, thirteen years ago, in a hospital corridor in Athens with her father's name trending on every screen, that she would rather be no one than be his daughter, and she had honored that choice every single day since, and honoring it felt like this: a rented room, a packed bag, a name already dying in her mouth.

*R. Calloway.* She said it silently, testing it, and found it already weightless. Gone. Another shell left on another beach.

She slept maybe three hours.

* * *

The harbor at dawn was gray and beautiful and completely indifferent to her, which she appreciated. She liked places that didn't pretend to care.

The first ferry wasn't for two hours, but she walked down anyway, duffel on her shoulder, because there was nothing left to do in the room and standing still had started to feel like sinking. Gulls argued over the fish gutting tables. An old man hosed down his deck. The fleet of small boats knocked gently against their moorings, patient as dogs.

And out past the breakwater, riding at anchor in the deep channel, sat a vessel that did not belong.

It was a tender—sleek, white-hulled, modern, the kind of ship that carried survey equipment and men in expensive waterproofs. Even at this distance she could read it the way she read a section face: the crane arm folded on the aft deck, the ROV housing, the towed sonar array stowed but present. Not a fishing boat. Not a yacht. A working salvage platform, parked off a town whose only industry was crab and tourists, and it had not been there yesterday.

She stopped at the harbor wall and looked at it for a long moment, wind flattening her jacket against her body, and felt that same cold arrive again—the animal warning, the split-second before thought.

Somewhere behind her, on the quay, an old man was arguing with a younger woman about crab pots. Their voices carried across the water, sharp and ordinary, and the woman said a name—*Vane*—and the man said it back, angrier, and Rosalind didn't yet know why the name snagged in her ear like a hook.

She shifted the duffel higher on her shoulder. The ferry terminal lights were on. All she had to do was walk through those doors, buy a ticket, and be no one again by lunchtime.

She took one step toward the terminal.

Then, out in the channel, the white tender's engines turned over—low, purposeful, wrong for the hour—and began to move, slow and deliberate, toward the open water beyond the point. Toward the cove she'd walked past a dozen times this season without a second glance, where just last week she'd found an old prospector camped among the rocks, half-starved, talking about a ship.

Toward whatever Tomas Ede had been trying to tell her before anyone else could hear it.

Rosalind Coldharbour stood on the harbor wall with her whole life in one bag, watching the boat go, and did not move.
