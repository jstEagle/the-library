## 2. A Dying Man's Ledger

The cove had no name on any chart I owned, which was exactly why I liked it. I'd been walking the tideline since dawn, following a rumor of midden deposits exposed by winter erosion, and the morning had given me nothing but kelp and gulls and the particular silence of a coast that had decided to keep its secrets.

I almost walked past him.

He was folded against the base of the sea wall like a bundle of old netting, oilskin slicker gone gray with salt, one hand still gripping a canvas satchel as if it were the last thing holding him to the earth. My first instinct—the trained one, the one that had kept me solvent for eleven years—was to keep walking. Dead or dying, he was someone else's problem. Getting involved meant names. Names meant records. Records meant my father's name surfacing like a body in a harbor, bloated with other people's curiosity.

But his chest was moving. Barely. And his eyes opened when my boot crunched the shingle three feet from him, and they found me with an accuracy that made my skin prickle.

"You're the digger," he said.

His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. I crouched despite myself. Up close he smelled of tar and old tobacco and something sweeter underneath—sickness, the kind that lives in a man for months before it takes him.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Gray's Point. The contract dig." He coughed, wet and long. "Watched you work two days. You read ground like your father reads it."

Every muscle in my back locked. "You've got the wrong woman."

"I've got the right one." He tried to smile and didn't quite make it. "Rosalind Coldharbour."

The name hit the air between us and just sat there, undeniable. I glanced up the beach—empty—and down it—empty—and hated myself for checking, because checking meant it mattered.

"Who are you?"

"Tomas Ede." He said it like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy. "Prospector. Forty years of it. And I'm dying, girl, so let's not waste the tide arguing about who knows what."

He wasn't wrong about the dying. His lips had gone the color of the slate underfoot, and there was a tremor in him that wasn't cold—or wasn't only cold. I'd seen men go like this on digs, heatstroke and heart failure both, and the window you had to do anything useful was narrow and closing.

"There's a clinic eight miles up the coast," I said. "I can flag a boat, or—"

"The clinic can bury me." He patted the satchel with the tenderness people usually reserve for children. "What I need is a witness. A promise. You're the only soul on this coast who might actually keep one, because you're the only one who doesn't want anything from anybody. That makes you honest. Or the next thing to it."

"I want to be paid," I said. "That's not nothing."

"No. It isn't." Another coughing fit bent him nearly double, and when he straightened there was blood on his lip, a thin bright thread of it. "Good. Then we understand each other. Everything has a price. Even this."

* * *

I got him propped against the sea wall where the wind couldn't sandblast him, and I sat on my heels in front of him, and I told myself I was only waiting to see if he'd stabilize enough to move. That was the lie I told. The truth was that some part of me—a part I kept starved and small, the part that had once been a daughter before it learned better—wanted to hear what a dying man thought was worth his last hour.

He worked the satchel open with fingers that shook like rigging in a blow. Inside: a tin cup, a stub of candle, a journal swollen with damp, and a single sheet of heavy paper folded into eighths, soft as cloth at the creases from handling.

"Meridian Star," he said.

The name landed somewhere in my chest. Every wreck diver on this coast knew the story the way they knew tide tables: the Meridian Star, lost 1912, outbound with a cargo manifest that never quite added up. Rumors of gold dust and uncut stones. Rumors, mostly, and salvage claims filed and abandoned by men who died broke.

"She went down in the tidal basin past Gray's Point," Ede said. "Not deep water. Shallow water that hides itself. Six hours out of every twelve she's under ten feet of sea, and the rest of the time she's under sand and nobody's looking. I found her in '89. Took me thirty years to find her, and another thirty—" he caught himself, corrected, "another five to work out why nobody else ever would."

"You're telling me you have the location of a legendary wreck." I kept my voice flat. Flat was safe. "And you're giving it away?"

"I'm trading it." His eyes were fever-bright now, fixed on mine. "There's a condition. One condition, and it's not negotiable, and if you won't swear it I'll find another digger, though God help me I don't have the time to find another one."

"Say it."

"The cargo isn't treasure." He said it slowly, like a lesson for a slow student. "It's wages. Crew's pay, held back by the owners for 'safekeeping'—that's what they called theft in those days—and it never reached a single widow. The Vanes. The Vane family lost four men on that ship and every coin those men earned, and the company buried the ledger and called it weather. I've spent thirty years knowing that and doing nothing, because I was a coward with a map." His breath whistled. "I'm done being a coward. You take her to the surface, and you take what's in her to the Vanes. Not to a museum. Not to an auction house. Not to whatever salvage syndicate comes sniffing. To the Vanes. Swear it."

The wind came off the water and pushed my hair across my face, and I was glad of the excuse to look away from him for a second.

Here is what I should have said: *I can't promise that. I don't know you. I don't know this story. I'm a contractor, not a crusader.*

Here is what I actually said, because he was dying and because the map was already glowing in my mind with its resale value—a private wreck location, authenticated by the finder himself, worth more than five contract digs:

"I'll see it gets to them."

"That's not a swear."

"It's what I've got."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger. Recognition, maybe. He knew exactly what kind of creature he was dealing with, and he took the deal anyway, and I have thought about that mercy often since, and it has never once felt like kindness.

"Close enough," he said. "Diggers. Always hedging your bets in the dirt."

He pressed the folded paper into my hands. It was warm from his grip, which felt obscene. I unfolded it just far enough to see ink lines—coastline, bearings, depth soundings in a cramped antique hand, and a cross-section sketch of a hull broken at the third rib, drawn with the loving precision of a man who had spent decades inside his own obsession.

"Logbook's in the hold," Ede said. "Under the master's berth. Company ledger, too—they hid it aboard thinking no court would ever look at a sunken ship. Proof, girl. The paper proof. Wrecks are just rumors until you pull the paper out of them."

"Why me?" I asked, and I genuinely wanted to know. "You watched me two days. That's not trust. That's desperation."

His laugh turned into another cough, longer than the others, and when it ended he slumped back against the stone and looked at the sky like he was reading something written there.

"My daughter died waiting on me," he said. "Waiting on money I kept meaning to send. Waiting on a visit I kept meaning to make. I was always going to be rich next season. Always going to come home next year." He turned his head and looked at me, and his gaze had gone clear and calm in a way that frightened me more than the blood had. "You remind me of me. Running. Digging holes in other people's ground so you never have to stand still in your own."

"Don't," I said.

"So I'm leaving my last job to someone who runs fast. She'll get there quicker than I ever did." His hand came up, trembling, and closed around mine—dry, hot, shockingly strong. "Sworn, then. Witnessed by the sea. She remembers everything, that one. She'll hold you to it even if I can't."

"Ede—"

"Tomas," he said. "Dead men shouldn't be formal."

"Tomas." The name felt strange in my mouth, a real name spoken aloud for the first time in months. "Let me get you to shelter. There's time."

"There isn't." But he smiled then, properly, and for a moment he looked like a young man, some prospector forty years gone, sitting on a beach with a whole life ahead of him instead of twenty minutes of it. "This is good, actually. This is better than the hospital. Sea air. A witness. A promise." His grip tightened. "Tell me the words back. So I can let go."

I told him the words back. *The Meridian Star's cargo goes to the Vane family.* I said it looking him in the eye, and I told myself the hedge was still in there—I'd *see it done*, I hadn't sworn to dive the wreck myself, hadn't sworn to refuse a buyer, hadn't sworn anything that would cost me more than a phone call.

He heard all of it anyway. Dying men hear everything; it's the living who learn to skim.

"Good girl," Tomas Ede said.

His grip loosened by degrees, like a knot easing, and I sat there on the freezing shingle holding the hand of a stranger while the tide turned below us, and I counted his breaths the way you count seconds between lightning and thunder, and somewhere past the twentieth count there weren't any more.

* * *

I stayed until the light went long and gold, which I told myself was respect and knew was paralysis. Then I closed his eyes, arranged his slicker over him decently, and walked back along the tideline with the map against my ribs like a coal.

At the top of the beach I stopped and took it out and read it properly, all of it, standing in the wind. Bearings. Soundings. The tidal arithmetic in the margin—Ede had worked it out to the minute, the narrow windows when the basin drained enough to reach her. And at the bottom, in fresher ink, a note that had nothing to do with navigation:

*If anyone asks how you found her, tell them a dying fool loved a debt too late. Let the Vanes hear that part. They've waited long enough to hear somebody say it out loud.*

I stood there doing the math I'd been refusing to do since he put it in my hands. A location like this, sold quietly to the right broker, would set me up for a decade. No more contract digs. No more client's son squinting at me across a trench saying *haven't I seen you somewhere*. No more packing before dawn.

One sale, and I could disappear permanently. Wasn't that the dream? Wasn't that the whole architecture of my life?

The map went back inside my jacket, against my heart, where I could feel it like a second pulse.

I was halfway to town, already composing the approach to a broker I half-trusted, when I passed the harbor board and saw the notice pinned behind glass, fresh ink, official stamp:

*SURVEY VESSEL TENDER ANCHORED OFF GRAY'S POINT. ALL UNAUTHORIZED DIVING PROHIBITED PENDING SALVAGE ASSESSMENT.*

Below it, the fleet's name. Below that, the operator's name, and I read it twice because the first time my eyes must have lied.

Hale Marine Salvage.

Marcus Hale. My father's partner. The other half of the famous duo, the man whose face had hung in our hallway next to my father's, both of them grinning out of a photograph taken on some dig thirty years ago, before the falling-out I was never told the shape of.

Ede had said *whatever salvage syndicate comes sniffing*, and I'd pictured strangers.

They weren't strangers. They were family, or the wreckage of it.

I stood in front of the notice board with the wind pulling at my coat and the map burning through my shirt, and I understood—with the flat, cold clarity that arrives only after a death—that the six tides Ede had penciled into his margin were not just a diving window.

They were a countdown.
