## 3. Six Tides

The map was drawn on the back of a tide table from 1987, and it was the best map Rosalind had ever seen.

She'd spread it across the desk of her rented room above the chandlery, weighted at the corners with a coffee mug, a coil of paracord, a dive light, and the brass compass she'd stolen—borrowed—from a client in Palau three years ago. The paper was soft as cloth from handling. Ede had worked it with a pencil stub and, later, a ballpoint that skipped, so that some lines were graphite and some were ghost-grooves he'd pressed too hard for ink to follow.

It took her most of the night to read it properly, and she resented every minute of that, because reading it meant believing it, and believing it meant she wasn't already on a bus south.

*Not a treasure map,* she told herself, tracing the coastline he'd rendered in short, certain strokes. *A salvage claim. Paper. Paper sells.*

But Ede hadn't drawn like a man selling anything. He'd drawn like a man confessing.

The cove was there, the one where she'd found him—the little hook of shingle below the collapsed sea cave, marked with an X so small she'd missed it twice. From the X, depth soundings ran out in his cramped hand: *4 fathoms. 7. 11.* Then nothing, just a blank space on the paper, and in that blank space, written once, hard enough to tear: **THE STAR LIES WHERE THE SEA FORGETS HER.**

Rosalind sat back and drank cold coffee and hated him a little for the poetry.

Because she knew what that meant. Every coastal kid knew what that meant. There were places where the tide didn't come and go so much as arrive and evacuate—shallow basins behind reef walls or sandbars, cut off from the deep water except at the top of the flood, and drained to bare wet sand at the bottom of a big spring ebb. Fishermen called them dry harbors, or dead bays, or, in the old charts around here, *the forgetting grounds*, because a boat caught inside one on a falling tide settled into the mud like a thing buried alive.

A wreck in a tidal basin wasn't hidden by water. It was hidden by *schedule*. You couldn't dive it whenever you liked; you could only reach it when the sea pulled its skirt back, and only then if you knew which pull, which night, which moon.

Ede's soundings made sense now. Four fathoms at high water over the outer bar. The basin itself barely two at the flood, draining to mud and six inches of channel at the ebb. And along the margin of the page, in letters gone brown and shaky:

*Spring tides only. New moon. Six hours of exposure if the weather holds. She has been down there forty years because nobody waits for the right night. Nobody is patient. Be patient.*

Rosalind looked up the tides.

That was the moment the night turned. She'd found the current almanac folded into Ede's coat pocket along with the map—he'd been planning this himself, she realized, right up until his heart quit—and now she opened it under the dive light and ran her finger down the columns and felt something in her chest go tight and cold.

New moon. Spring tides. The next extreme low came in four days, and after that the sequence ran: another low, another, each one a door opening onto the basin floor for a few hours before it slammed shut again. She counted them the way you count money you don't have. Six viable tides. Six windows. After that the moon fattened, the range shrank, and the Meridian Star would sink back beneath two fathoms of grey water and stay there until autumn.

Six tides to raise a fortune—or to prove a theft—or to sell a map and be three hundred miles away before anyone was the wiser.

She got up and paced. The room was eight feet wide; pacing it took four steps and a turn, four steps and a turn, like something in a cage that hadn't decided yet whether it was caged.

*You don't owe him anything,* said the voice she'd polished over fifteen years of leaving places. *He was a stranger. He chose you because you were standing there. That's not a debt, that's proximity.*

But she could still feel his hand. Not metaphorically—she could feel it, the specific weight of it, the tendons slackening under her fingers in stages, like rigging coming loose one line at a time. He'd held on for eleven minutes after he stopped being able to speak. She'd counted. Counting was how she kept panic in its box, and she'd counted his pulse down from something frightened to something finished, and the whole time his other hand had been flat against the map, holding it down against the wind, making sure she took it.

Making sure she *promised*.

"I'll see they get it," she'd said. "Whatever it is. I'll see it gets to them."

And Tomas Ede, who had spent forty years keeping a secret worth more than the town he died beside, had smiled with half his mouth and said, clear as a bell, the last words he ever spent: *"Then you're rich, girl. Just not in coin."*

Rosalind stopped pacing.

Here was the problem, laid out clean: the cargo of the Meridian Star wasn't specie. She'd assumed gold when he first pressed the map into her hands—everyone assumed gold—but Ede had talked in fragments while she worked to keep him conscious, and one fragment kept surfacing now, over and over, like something drowned that wouldn't stay down. *Wages,* he'd said. *Pay chest. They took the crew's own wages out with them, and then the owners wrote the men off as lost with all hands and never paid a widow a cent.* A ship's pay chest, sunk with the crew whose money it held, while the company collected the insurance and called it weather.

That wasn't treasure. That was evidence. Evidence didn't fence. Evidence didn't sell quietly to a collector in Singapore. Evidence had exactly one honest market, and it was the descendants of the men whose names were in that chest.

*I can't do this,* she thought, with real surprise, because she'd built her whole life on knowing exactly what she could and couldn't do, and this fell squarely in the second column. Returning stolen wages meant finding the families. Finding the families meant names in newspapers. Names in newspapers meant her father's people would find her inside a week, and then the long careful architecture of her anonymity—fifteen years of pseudonyms and cash contracts and moving on before the drinks were bought—would come down around her ears.

She sat on the bed and put her head in her hands.

Outside, the harbor breathed. Rigging ticked against masts. Somewhere down the quay a dog was complaining about the weather, and beyond the breakwater the open sea made its long indifferent sound, the same sound it had made over the Meridian Star for forty years, the same sound it would make over her bones if she let this kill her, which it might, because everything about this was wrong for her skill set. She was a digger-for-hire. She found things for people who paid and asked no questions. Questions were expensive. Questions got you followed.

Then she saw the lights.

Through the gap in the curtains, past the breakwater, well outside the harbor moorings, a white running light hung motionless on the black water. As she watched, a second light kindled beside it, then a third, strung out in a line like beads on a wire. Not fishing boats—fishing boats moved, worked, showed deck floods and net lights. These were stationary, spaced, disciplined. Anchored. A tender and her consorts, sitting fat and patient in the deep water beyond the bar, exactly where a vessel would sit if it were waiting for something shallow to become reachable.

Rosalind went very still.

She knew hull configurations the way other people knew faces, and even at this distance, even at night, the silhouette was wrong for local traffic. Too much superstructure aft. Cranes. She'd bet the compass in her pocket those were cranes.

Salvage fleet. Already here. Already anchored over the approach to the forgetting grounds, burning diesel and waiting—with the patience Ede had warned about—for their own calculation of spring tides.

They had the almanac too. Of course they did. Which meant they had the same six tides she did, minus however many it took them to stop waiting and start cutting.

Her heart was doing something complicated, part fear and part fury, and underneath both, unwelcome as a splinter, a third thing she refused to name yet.

She thought about walking away. Genuinely, methodically, she thought about it: pack now, bus at five, map sold in a city where nobody knew the coastline, done. Fifteen years of practice said this was the move. Fifteen years of practice had never once had to look at a dying man's face while it happened.

"Damn you, Tomas," she said softly, to a room with no one in it.

Then she pulled the almanac close, took out her notebook, and began to work.

Low water, day one: 0412, height 0.3 meters above chart datum. Day two: 0521, lower. Day three: 0633, the lowest of the series—*that* was the window, the big one, the morning the basin would lie open like a wound. Days four, five, six, diminishing but usable. She wrote the times in a column, and beside them she wrote what she'd need: a boat that drew less than a meter, someone who knew the reef passage, air or no air, tools, lights, and luck, in roughly that order of scarcity.

She had none of it. She had a rented room, a dory with a seized outboard, and a promise.

She looked at the anchored lights again, and the fear in her chest shifted shape—not smaller, but harder, the way loose sand becomes stone under pressure. Let them wait. Let them count their tides. She'd counted hers too, and she'd spent fifteen years learning how to move fast and quiet through other people's territory, and unlike them, she knew what was in the hold, and unlike them, she wasn't going to sell it.

Six tides. One promise. No plan worth the name.

Rosalind Coldharbour—whoever that was, whoever she'd have to be again—opened her bag and began, for the first time in years, to unpack.

* * *

At dawn the tender's lights were still there, and so was the answer to her worst question, because as the light came up grey and thin over the water, she saw a skiff put out from the fleet's side and run a slow circuit of the outer bar, pausing where the channel entered the basin. Taking soundings. Measuring the door.

And standing in the stern of that skiff, binoculars raised, sweeping the shoreline with the unhurried thoroughness of a man looking for something specific—a map, maybe, or a witness, or a woman—was a figure she recognized from photographs twenty years old, from dinner-table stories she'd spent half a lifetime trying to forget.

Marcus Hale. Her father's partner. Her father's friend.

Hale lowered the binoculars and looked directly at her window across the water, and though she knew—knew—it was impossible, that glass and distance and dawn made her invisible, Rosalind stepped back from the curtain anyway, heart hammering, one hand pressed flat against the wall.

Six tides. And now, suddenly, five felt like too many.
