## 4. The Vane House

Rosalind rehearsed it three times on the walk up from the harbor, and all three versions sounded like a con.

*I have something that belongs to your family.* No. That was how grifters opened doors in coastal towns, and Isla Vane would know it—everyone here knew everyone, and a stranger with a promise was just a stranger with a hook.

*Your great-great-grandfather's wages are at the bottom of the cove.* Worse. That was a claim on grief from someone who hadn't earned it.

She settled on the truth, stripped bare: *A dying man gave me a map and a debt. I'm here to pay it.* And if the door closed in her face, she'd deserve it.

The Vane house sat where the road gave out—a low stone cottage with a slate roof furred green at the edges, nets hung to dry along one wall like the ribs of some beached animal. A dory was hauled up on rollers, hull freshly scraped. Someone worked with care here. Someone planned to stay.

That alone made Rosalind's chest tighten. She hadn't stayed anywhere long enough to scrape a hull.

She knocked.

* * *

Isla saw the woman coming before she reached the gate—wrong coat for the coast, city boots crusted with salt mud, walking like someone who'd already mapped three exits. Isla had spent thirty years reading weather off the water and people off the shore path, and this one read as trouble wrapped in politeness.

She let her knock anyway. Curiosity was a weakness she'd never fully caulked.

"You're not selling anything," Isla said through the gap of the half-opened door, "because I'd have shut it by now."

"No." The woman hesitated. "My name is Rosalind. I need to talk to you about the Meridian Star."

The name landed like a gaff under the ribs. Isla kept her face still, the way you do when a fish thrashes—you don't let it see what moves you. "There's nothing to talk about. She went down in 1911. Everyone who cared is dead or wishes they were."

"Everyone who cared is why I'm here."

Isla looked at her properly then. Younger than her voice, tired around the eyes, hands that had done real work but not water work—the nails short, no callus ridge across the palm where a line would sit. A land diver, maybe. A treasure hunter, more likely. They came through every few years, drawn by the story like gulls to gutting tables: the Meridian Star, lost with eleven crew and a season's pay owed them, somewhere in the tidal basin past Cutter Reef. Her family's whole inheritance, sunk and salted and spoken of in the past tense for four generations.

"I'll say this once," Isla said. "Whatever you're selling—salvage rights, documentary rights, a share of whatever you think is down there—I don't want it. My grandfather chased that wreck his whole life. It cost him his boat, his marriage, and most of his mind. I fish. That's my whole relationship with the sea. It gives me haddock and takes nothing I can't afford to lose."

The stranger didn't flinch. "Tomas Ede died eight days ago," she said quietly. "He held my hand while he did it. He asked me to bring what was taken back to the Vanes. I didn't come to sell you anything. I came because I owe a dead man."

Isla's hand tightened on the door. Ede. Old Tomas, who used to buy mackerel off her father before the father was gone too, who talked to the tide like it answered. Dead. Something moved in her chest that she refused to name.

"That's a sad story," she said. "It's not mine."

She closed the door.

Then stood there, heart going hard, listening to boots retreat down the shingle—and hating herself for counting the steps until they faded.

* * *

Rosalind made it as far as the harbor wall before she stopped, furious at herself.

You had one job. One conversation. And you led with a dead man and a debt, like a bill collector at a funeral.

She leaned on the cold granite and watched the fleet tender riding at anchor beyond the point—Hale's people, patient as sharks, waiting out the tides. Six left, maybe five now. She didn't have time to nurse a bruised approach. But there was no other way; Ede's map named the Vane family, and the promise wasn't paid to a grave. It was paid to whoever carried the name.

She turned back up the hill.

This time she didn't knock. She walked around to where the nets hung and found Isla Vane mending one, seated on an upturned crate with a shuttle moving fast and sure through the mesh, not looking up.

"I said what I said."

"You did." Rosalind sat down on the shingle nearby—not close, not invited, just present. "So I won't ask you for anything. I'll tell you what I know, and you can decide whether it's worth your time. If it isn't, I'll leave and never come back. That's a promise I'm actually good at keeping."

The shuttle paused, resumed.

"Ede's map puts the Meridian Star in the basin past Cutter Reef, exposed only at extreme low water. There's a salvage fleet anchored offshore right now—they've been cutting into her hull already, modern marks on old iron. In six tides they reach the hold. Whatever proof exists that your family's wages were stolen rather than lost goes down their winch cables and disappears into lawyers' offices, and the Vanes get nothing but another generation of stories."

Silence. The shuttle had stopped entirely now.

"How do you know about the cutting marks?" Isla said at last. Flat. Careful.

"Because I've been down there."

Something shifted in the older woman's posture—not softening, exactly. Recalculating. "You dove Cutter Reef alone?"

"On the last spring tide. Badly. I nearly didn't come back up." Rosalind looked out at the gray water and decided to spend the last coin she had. "I'm good at leaving, Isla. I've been doing it my whole life. Towns, names, jobs—one season, gone before anyone asks questions. Ede handed me the one thing I can't run from, because running means your family loses everything twice. So I'm asking you not for trust. Just tell me one thing. Anything. What did your grandfather know about where she lies? Because his notes might be the difference between three dives and ten, and I don't have ten."

Isla set down the shuttle. For a long moment she studied Rosalind the way she'd study a strange current—looking for the thing underneath.

"My grandfather's papers burned when the house caught fire in '89," she said. "What survived is a box of charts he marked up, in a trunk under my bed, that I haven't opened since we buried him. Because opening it meant becoming him."

She stood, joints cracking, and looked down at Rosalind with something that wasn't warmth yet but had stopped being frost.

"One hour," Isla said. "You look at the charts, you tell me everything Ede told you, every word, and if I smell a lie I put you on the morning boat myself. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And your name. Your real one. I'll not trade truths with a woman hiding behind a first name."

Rosalind felt the old reflex rise—the itch to invent a surname, a history, a clean disposable self. She let it pass.

"Coldharbour," she said. "Rosalind Coldharbour."

Isla's eyebrows lifted a fraction. "Coldharbour. That name was in my grandfather's rants. Some archaeologist, decades back, helped bury the Meridian Star inquiry—said the salvage reports were fixed, said the government man and the divers were all bought. He swore the Coldharbour name belonged at the bottom of the sea next to the ship."

The harbor wind seemed to drop away. Rosalind heard her own pulse.

"That would be my father," she said.

Isla picked up the shuttle again, slow, eyes never leaving her face. "Well then," she said. "Now you've got my full attention. Get inside before the rain does. You've got more than one hour's worth of explaining to do—and you're going to start with why the daughter of the man who buried my family's claim is the one carrying its map."
