## 5. What Father Knew

The harbor library opened at nine and smelled of mildew and old varnish, the way every coastal library in the world smelled, as if salt got into everything eventually. Rosalind had been there since eight-forty, waiting on the steps with two coffees she didn't need, because the librarian was a woman named Dot who remembered her from three years ago under a different name and would ask questions if she lingered.

She'd come for tide tables. She left with a name.

It happened the way it always did—sideways, in the margins. The Meridian Star was in the shipping registers, of course: a coastal freighter, lost with all hands in '71, wreck location never confirmed, insurance paid, case closed. Case closed was the phrase that snagged her. Wrecks that stayed lost for fifty years usually had reasons, and reasons were usually people.

So she pulled the newspaper archive instead, microfilm hissing through the reader like something alive, and worked backward from the sinking. The *Coastal Sentinel* had covered it for eleven days straight before dropping it cold on day twelve. Eleven days of headlines—*FREIGHTER DOWN OFF CAPE MERIDIAN*, *SEVEN CREW MISSING, WAGES ABOARD*, *VANE FAMILY DEMANDS ANSWERS*—and then nothing. Not a follow-up, not an editorial, not even a correction. A story that size didn't die naturally. It was killed.

And on day nine, buried below the fold, a photograph she almost missed: four men on a courthouse steps in gray suits, one of them gesturing toward the doors. The caption read: *Salvage interests represented by attorney Marcus Hale (left) and consulting archaeologist Edmund Coldharbour (second from right) assure the public the matter is settled.*

Her father's face was younger by forty years and thinner, but she knew the set of his jaw the way she knew her own hands. He was smiling at someone off-camera, that particular smile he kept for cameras and clients, the one that never reached his eyes. Second from right. Consulting archaeologist. Assuring the public.

Rosalind sat very still while the microfilm reel turned empty past her, clattering.

*Consulting.* Her father hadn't consulted on anything in his life without understanding exactly what he was consulting on. He could read a site like other men read weather. If Edmund Coldharbour stood on courthouse steps saying the Meridian Star matter was settled, then he knew where the ship was. He had always known.

And now Marcus Hale's salvage fleet sat anchored off Cape Meridian, six tides from a hull her father had helped bury.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she'd spent her whole adult life doing: not being found. She read Hale's fleet registration out of the maritime directory in a café two towns over, paying cash, sitting with her back to the wall. *Meridian Recovery Group LLC. Principal: M. Hale.* Twenty years of permits, most of them legitimate, some of them audited into silence. And threaded through the early filings, the same name appearing again and again in the technical reports—*site assessment by E. Coldharbour, PhD.*

They'd been partners once. She'd known that much growing up, the way you know weather from childhood—a man named Marcus who came to dinner sometimes, who brought her shells and spoke to her father in a shorthand she wasn't allowed to learn. Then one year he stopped coming, and her father never said why, and she'd learned not to ask questions in that house the way she'd later learn not to leave them anywhere.

Now she understood the shape of it. Two men had known where the Meridian Star went down. One of them had spent decades quietly buying up salvage rights to the cape. The other had taken whatever they were paid and built a career on respectable excavations and a daughter who ran from him.

Ede's map burned against her ribs like a coal. She'd copied it twice and hidden the copies separately, and still she felt it there, the way you feel a splinter.

The promise she'd made was to a dying prospector about a family named Vane. But standing in that café with Hale's filings spread flat under her forearm, she understood the debt had grown teeth. This wasn't just stolen wages anymore. It was a theft maintained across fifty years by two men—one of whom she shared blood with.

* * *

She called him from the payphone outside the gas station, because payphones were anonymous and her father answered unknown numbers the way he answered everything: warily, on the fourth ring, as though the world owed him an apology for interrupting.

"Who is this?"

"It's me."

A pause long enough to hear him decide whether to hang up. "Rosalind." Not her name—never her name on a phone—but close enough. "Where are you calling from?"

"A place you'll never find." Old script. It used to be a game between them when she was small, running away to the end of the garden. "I have a question."

"I imagine you do."

"The Meridian Star."

This time the silence had texture. She heard him set something down, heard a chair, heard the specific quality of a man making himself comfortable for a conversation he'd been dreading for fifty years.

"Where did you hear that name?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously." His voice had gone flat, professional, the voice he used on grad students who'd made errors in their trench notes. "Whatever you've gotten yourself into—"

"I'm not *in* anything. I asked a question. Did you know where the ship sank?"

"Rosalind."

"*Did you know?*"

"Yes." No hesitation. That was the thing about her father; he lied beautifully but he never lied about facts, only about meaning. "Yes. I knew."

The word went through her like a hook through soft tissue. She'd known, going in—she'd read the photograph, done the arithmetic—and still. Still. Somewhere under twenty years of estrangement lived a girl who believed her father dug things up because he loved what was buried, and hearing him say *yes* killed that girl a second time.

"You helped bury it."

"I helped *settle* it."

"Don't—" She pressed her forehead against the cold metal of the coin box. "Don't you dare use their language. Seven men went down with their pay aboard. Their families spent fifty years not knowing. You stood on courthouse steps and smiled for a camera and called it settled."

"And what would you have had me do?" For the first time his voice cracked its casing, and underneath was something rawer than defensiveness. "I was thirty-one years old. I had a reputation that existed entirely on paper, a wife, a baby, and Marcus Hale holding the only copy of my site survey—the survey I'd done *for him*, Rosalind, believing we were documenting a hazard to navigation. When I understood what he intended, my choices were ruin or complicity. I chose complicity. I've chosen it every day since, every time I let sleeping wrecks lie. You think I don't know what I am?"

"I think," she said, and her voice surprised her by shaking, "that you had fifty years. Fifty years, and you waited until your daughter found it first."

"That is not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. Tomas Ede died holding my hand asking me to fix what you helped break, and he deserved better, and so did the Vanes, and so did I."

"Ede." Very quiet now. "Tomas Ede was on that survey crew. He was the one who took soundings. He's been trying to give that map away for thirty years—I know because Marcus has been looking for him for thirty years." A breath. "If you have what Ede had, then you are not merely *involved*. You are carrying the one object Marcus Hale has wanted longer than you've been alive. Do you understand what that makes you?"

"Free," she said. "Finally free. I get to choose which side of this I stand on. You never gave yourself that."

"Rosalind—"

"Hale's fleet is anchored off the cape. Six tides. Maybe fewer. I'm going to open that hold, and I'm going to put the logbook and the pay chest in front of every camera on the coast, and when they ask how the scandal was buried, your name will be in the answer unless you get ahead of it." She fed the phone another coin without knowing why; the call was already beyond saving. "Testify, Dad. Or be testified about."

"Wait." And there it was—the thing under fifty years of formality, cracking through his voice like ice underfoot. "Rosalind, wait. Don't do this alone. Tell me where—"

"You taught me better than that."

She hung up before he could hear what the words cost her, and stood a moment in the gas station's fluorescent hum, watching moths batter the light above the phone. Her hand was steady on the receiver. Her eyes were not.

* * *

She walked back along the harbor road in full dark, past the fleet's tender riding at anchor, its deck lights burning like a held breath. Somewhere on that water were men paid to find what she carried in a waterproof roll against her spine.

At the top of the sea wall she stopped, because a figure was leaning there in the dark, arms crossed, watching the tender with an expression Rosalind recognized from her own mirror—someone counting tides.

Isla Vane didn't look over. "Your father," she said. "That call took eleven minutes. I timed it."

Rosalind went cold from the boots up. "You followed me."

"I asked around about the stranger asking about the *Star*." Isla finally turned, and her face was half lamplight, half shadow, and entirely unyielding. "You've got until we reach my door to tell me everything. After that, I go talk to Hale myself, and you can keep your secrets and your promises and your famous name all to yourself."

The tide was turning. Rosalind could feel it in the dark water below them, patient and enormous, pulling the whole coast toward the truth.

"All right," she said. "Walk with me."
