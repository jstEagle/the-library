## 6. First Dive

Isla Vane had spent forty-one years learning the moods of that water, and she knew before the sun cleared the headland that the tide would be a liar. A big drain, the kind that pulled back past the kelp line and left the reef standing naked as ribs. The kind her grandmother called a *bared-teeth tide*. The kind that showed things.

She told herself she wasn't going.

She'd told herself that at midnight, when Rosalind Coldharbour—*Coldharbour*, like something out of a shipping ledger—had stood in Isla's kitchen dripping rainwater on the floorboards, holding Ede's map like it might bite. She'd told herself that at four in the morning, lying awake listening to the harbor slap against pilings. She told herself again now, pulling on her old neoprene vest anyway, because the truth was Isla Vane had never once in her life been able to leave a door unopened. Her grandmother had been the same. It was in the blood: the Vanes went toward things.

The Meridian Star had gone down with her great-great-grandfather's wages in its hold. Four months of crew pay, sealed in an iron chest, promised and never delivered. The company had called it an act of God. The Vanes had called it theft for three generations, quietly, the way you curse weather.

And now a stranger with her father's eyes and no last name she'd admit to wanted to walk out to it at low water, and Isla was supposed to just let her drown alone among the cuttlefish and the urchins.

Not likely.

* * *

Rosalind was already at the cove when Isla came down the path, standing at the tideline in a wetsuit too new for her, checking a dive watch like she didn't trust it. The map was rolled in a waterproof tube strapped across her back. She looked up when Isla's boots crunched on shingle, and there was something in her face—not surprise exactly. Recognition of a debt being collected.

"You said you wouldn't come," Rosalind said.

"I said I hadn't decided." Isla dropped her gear bag and started checking Rosalind's rig without asking, because if the woman was going to die in Vane water, she'd do it properly kitted. "Your regulator hose is chafed. Swap it."

"It's fine."

"Swap it or swim alone." She dug a spare out of her own bag and held it out. Rosalind took it after a moment, and Isla read that moment correctly: the woman wasn't used to people doing things for her. Good. Neither was Isla.

The tide was already falling fast, the channel between the outer rocks running hard and glassy. Beyond it, the basin lay exposed in long brown terraces of weed and rock pool, steam rising off the wet stone where the morning sun hit it. Somewhere out past the reef line, maybe two miles, the salvage fleet's tender sat at anchor—a white speck with a crane arm folded like a sleeping insect. Isla had watched it through her binoculars since dawn. It hadn't moved. That bothered her more than if it had.

"They're waiting for the same window," Rosalind said, following her gaze.

"Then we'd best be quicker." Isla shouldered her bag. "Stay behind me. The reef has opinions about strangers."

* * *

The crossing took them forty minutes over ground that should have taken twenty, because Isla made them go slow. This was her country and she walked it like a landlord: around the blowhole that sucked air with a sound like breathing, past the pool where the octopus lived, over the shelf of black rock where the swell still reached even at low water and could take an ankle if you got greedy. Rosalind followed well, Isla admitted. Careful feet, patient, watching everything. She didn't chatter. She looked at the reef the way Isla imagined she looked at dig sites—like it owed her answers.

At the far edge of the basin, where the weed gave way to bare scoured rock, the water pooled dark in a long trench. And below the surface of that trench, dim through six feet of clear cold water, lay the shape.

Isla had seen it twice in her life, both times from above, both times refusing to feel what she felt. Now she stood at the rim of the trench with her heart going like a gaff hook in her chest.

Timbers. Great black ribs of them, fanned across the trench floor, furred with weed, one section of hull planking risen like the keel of an overturned boat. Copper sheathing still clung in patches, gone green as old bottle glass. It was bigger than the stories said. Stories always shrink things; the sea doesn't.

"There she is," Isla said, and hated how her voice cracked on *she*.

Rosalind had gone very still. Then she was kneeling at the edge, mask down, face in the water, and Isla watched her shoulders change—the set of a woman who had spent years feeling nothing on purpose, forgetting how, for one breath.

"We dive it," Rosalind said, coming up. "Now, while it's shallow enough to work the exterior."

"It's a bared-teeth tide. Slack won't last."

"Then we don't waste it."

They kitted in silence, and Isla gave the briefing she'd given no one in thirty years: entry at the north end, follow the trench, hand on the guide line she'd rigged from a boulder, out before the flood turned the trench into a washing machine. Rosalind listened, nodded once, and did not argue. Isla decided she could almost like her for that.

* * *

Underwater, the world went green and gold and quiet.

They swam the length of the wreck with the sun coming down through the trench in slanted columns, and Isla forgot, for whole minutes at a time, to be afraid. Fish hung in the rigging of the dead ship—pollack moving like a single thought. Anemones covered the rail in orange and plum. A conger eel watched them from a broken hatchway with the flat patience of a landlord. The Meridian Star had become a cathedral, and Isla, who believed in nothing much, felt her throat close behind the regulator.

Her great-great-grandfather had stood on this deck. Had eaten at this galley, slept in this hammock, signed articles for wages that were in the hold below their fins right now, maybe, sealed in iron, waiting a hundred and thirty years for someone with his blood to come collect.

Then Rosalind grabbed her arm. Pointed.

Forward of the main mast step, the hull planking had been opened. Not storm-broken, not worm-eaten—*cut*. Three clean lines, straight as surveyor's chalk, framing a removed panel roughly a meter square. The edges were bright metal-bright even under the weed, sharp enough that Isla put her glove near them and felt the burr of fresh scoring.

Modern. Weeks old, not decades.

Rosalind ran her light along the cuts slowly, and Isla watched her face do something complicated behind the mask. When they surfaced in the shallows by the trench rim, dragging themselves onto the warm black rock, Rosalind pulled her hood back and spoke flatly, the awe burned off her like fog off a window.

"Plasma cutter or exothermic lance. Recent. They've already been into the hull."

"The tender's been sitting there three days," Isla said. "I thought they were surveying."

"They weren't surveying." Rosalind was wringing out her gloves, not looking at her. "They've taken something. Or they've looked and marked the rest for later. Either way—" She stopped.

"Say it."

"Either way, whatever's left in that hold, we're not the only ones who know about it anymore. And they've had a head start." Rosalind looked out at the white speck on the horizon, and Isla saw her jaw set, saw the calculation running behind her eyes like a tide table. "Six tides until the fleet can bring heavy equipment over the bar. Six tides, and they've already cut the hull open."

Isla sat down hard on the rock, the adrenaline draining out of her and leaving something colder underneath. She thought of the logbook her grandmother had described—crew articles, wage lists, names in faded ink. Names. If the fleet had found papers instead of coin, they might burn them. No chest, no proof, no claim. Just another act of God.

"Then we go back in," Isla said, "on the next tide. And the one after. Until we've got it all."

Rosalind turned and looked at her then, really looked, and Isla realized she'd just done the thing she'd sworn not to do. She'd joined. She'd put her name on it, or near enough.

"Your grandmother's stories," Rosalind said carefully. "Did she ever say where the captain kept the ship's papers?"

Isla stared at her. "Why?"

"Because if Hale's men found the chest empty of documents, they'll be looking for them somewhere else. And so will we." Rosalind nodded back toward the wreck, dark under its trench of green water. "There's a second compartment under the stern cabin. I saw the seam when we passed it. Somebody built that ship with a hiding place, and captains who steal their own crews' wages tend to need one."

The flood was starting. Far out in the channel, the water had begun its slow turn, sliding green over the rocks they'd crossed an hour ago. Two miles away, the tender's crane arm unfolded, joint by joint, like something waking up.

Isla got to her feet, and found she wasn't afraid anymore. She was furious, which was better. Fury kept.

"Next tide's at four," she said. "Be at my slip at three. And Coldharbour—" she used the name deliberately, watching the woman flinch, "—bring your own regulator this time."

Rosalind almost smiled. Almost.

"Isla," she said, as they packed up. "The man who sold me that map. Ede. He drew the stern cabin on it. Marked it with an X." She paused. "He knew about the hiding place. Which means he'd been inside the wreck. Recently."

The tide slid in around their boots, cold and insistent, hurrying them home.

"And whoever showed Tomas Ede the way in," Isla said slowly, "is probably the same person who taught your father's partner where to cut."
