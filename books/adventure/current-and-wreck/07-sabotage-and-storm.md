## 7. Sabotage and Storm

The smell woke her before the sound did.

Burning diesel and tar, thick enough to taste, seeping under the door of the harbormaster's office where she'd rented a cot for the week. Rosalind was on her feet with the map already in her hand—she'd slept with it zipped inside her jacket since Ede died, a habit she hadn't examined too closely—and by the time she reached the window, the whole marina was lit orange.

Her boat was a torch.

The *Kestrel*—not her boat, boats never were, just the one she'd bought with cash in Port Merrow three seasons back—stood at her slip with fire pouring out of its cabin windows like something alive. Flames climbed the mast and bent sideways in the wind, and the heat reached her even across two hundred meters of black water. Nobody was fighting it yet. The harbor crew would come, but they'd come slow, because somebody had paid them to come slow or frightened them into it, and either way the *Kestrel* was going down to its fittings.

She did the math without wanting to. Diesel in the bilge. Accelerant, probably, poured through the deck plates while she'd been asleep forty meters away. Which meant they'd known which boat. Which meant they'd known where she slept. Which meant—

Which meant the room behind her wasn't safe either.

Rosalind moved. Not fast—fast drew eyes—but with the flat efficiency of someone who'd packed under worse pressure than this. She'd kept the bag ready since the night of the tender's arrival; that was rule one, the oldest rule, the one her father had taught her without ever meaning to: *leave nothing you can't carry, love nothing you can't burn.* She swept the room in ninety seconds. Clothes, cash roll, dive knife, the waterproof case with her instruments. The map went against her spine in its oilskin sleeve, flat and warm from her body.

The door opened as she reached for the window.

Not Hale himself—one of his men, big through the shoulders, wearing the patient expression of a person doing a job. Behind him, in the corridor, another silhouette. They weren't running. That was the worst part. They had the unhurried air of people who owned the building, the town, the tide schedule, and the night.

"Ms. Coldharbour," the big one said. "Mr. Hale would like a word."

So much for anonymity. She catalogued the failure later—later, when there was time to bleed over it. In the moment there was only the window at her back, the drop to the loading dock, and the fact that they'd used her real name, which meant the pretense was already dead and she owed it nothing.

"I'm not dressed for company," she said, and went out the window.

The drop was eight feet onto stacked crab pots. She rolled off them, came up running, and took the alley behind the chandlery at a dead sprint, boots hammering wet planking, the shouts behind her arriving a half-second late. She knew these towns—the geometry of them, the service cuts between buildings, the way harbors all grew the same tumors of shed and net-rack and rusted winch. She'd been running through towns like this since she was nineteen. It turned out all those years of leaving had been training for exactly one skill: not getting caught.

She cut through the fish market, still smelling of the morning's catch despite the hour, vaulted a rail, and dropped onto the beach below the seawall. The sand was cold and gave badly. She ran north along the tideline until the lights of the town shrank to a smear, then into the dune grass, then stopped, hands on knees, breathing like a bellows.

Behind her, the *Kestrel* burned. Ahead of her, out past the point, the salvage fleet's tender rode at anchor with its deck lights burning serene and indifferent, a small floating city of other people's money.

Rosalind sat down in the grass and made herself think.

They'd torched the boat but not come for the map directly—they'd come to search the room, which meant they didn't have it, which meant they didn't know if she had it at all. The name, though. *Coldharbour.* Someone had talked. The client from the contract dig, maybe, or the harbormaster, or any of the dozen small leaks a town like this sprang when enough money pressed against it. Her invisibility hadn't been pierced. It had been sold, cheaply, the way everything in a poor coastal town got sold.

And Hale wanted a word. Not violence—not first. A word. Because Hale was careful, and careful men preferred to buy what they couldn't take, and only took what they couldn't buy. He'd torch a boat as an opening bid. An announcement: *I know who you are, I know why you're here, and I can reach you while you sleep.*

Fear sat in her chest like swallowed stone. She let it. She'd learned long ago that fear wasn't the enemy—fear was information, same as a soil stain or a sonar return. You read it. You didn't obey it.

The stone transmuted, slowly, into something with edges.

* * *

The storm announced itself the way storms do on that coast: as a rumor, then a certainty. By the time Rosalind reached Isla Vane's house above the cove—knocking because she had nowhere else, hating herself for needing somewhere else—the western sky had gone the color of a bruise and the wind carried salt and iron in equal measure.

Isla opened the door halfway, shotgun leaning against the jamb within easy reach, and looked at Rosalind's smoke-stained jacket and empty hands.

"They burned my boat," Rosalind said. "They searched my room. They used my name." She heard her own voice and was surprised by how level it came out. "I need to tell you the tide math has changed."

Isla studied her for a long moment. Whatever she saw made her step back and open the door the rest of the way. "You look like a woman who's been counting."

"I've been counting since Ede put that map in my hand." Rosalind laid it out on the kitchen table, weighting the corners with cups, and Isla leaned over it with her arms crossed like a woman inspecting a lie. "Six tides. That was the number. The basin drains enough to work the hull six more times before the fleet can bring equipment in over the bar. But—" she tapped the window, where the first gusts were already worrying the shutters, "—that front kills the low-water window. Storm surge stacks on top of the tide. The basin won't drain. Not properly."

"How many?"

"Three." Rosalind held up three fingers. "Maybe. If the front moves through fast. Three tides, starting tomorrow night, and after that the fleet sits over the wreck until they've stripped it to the keel."

Isla was quiet. Outside, the wind found its voice properly, a long rising moan through the rocks below the house. When she finally spoke, it wasn't about tides.

"My great-great-grandfather's wages went down with that ship," she said. "Eleven families. The company paid itself back out of the insurance and called the crew lost with all effects. My grandmother kept a ledger of what each family was owed until the day she died. Kept it like scripture." Her jaw worked. "And now some man sends boys to burn your boat over it."

"Hale isn't sending boys anymore. He knows I'm here. He knows about the Vane claim—he'll have known longer than I have." Rosalind met her eyes. "Which means he also knows the logbook might still be in the hold. Paper proof. Wages, cargo manifest, the theft itself, written down by the man who did it. That's not treasure to him. That's evidence."

"So we get it first."

"*We*," Rosalind repeated, and heard the question in it.

Isla uncrossed her arms. "You came to my door twice now. First time with promises, second time with smoke on your jacket. Promises I don't trust. Smoke I do." She almost smiled; it didn't quite land. "Tomorrow night. First of the three. We dive at slack water or not at all."

* * *

Rosalind didn't sleep. She sat in Isla's kitchen while the storm built overhead, listening to the house groan like a ship, and did what she always did with unbearable things: she took them apart piece by piece until they were just parts.

Hale had her name. That was done; un-done-able. For eleven years she'd traded on being nobody, and the account was spent, and the strange thing—she tested it honestly, turning it over in the dark—was how little grief there was in the loss. What she felt instead was lighter than relief and harder than anger. Something with direction.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since Ede died holding her hand: running had never once saved anything. It had only preserved her, the way wax preserves a specimen—intact, detailed, useless. Every town she'd fled, every name she'd buried, every season abandoned at its midpoint, and the world was exactly as unjust as she'd found it, except now a dying prospector's promise was sitting on her conscience like ballast, and a fleet was anchored over eleven families' proof, and a woman who smelled of honest fish and distrust had opened her door twice.

Ede had chosen her precisely because she was nobody. Fine. Then let nobody be the thing that ruined Marcus Hale.

The wind screamed. Down in the cove, waves detonated against the reef with a sound like distant artillery, and Rosalind thought about the wreck lying under all that violence, patient as it had been for sixty years, holding its dead men's ledger against the day someone came with clean hands.

Tomorrow night, the basin would drain for the first of three times. Tomorrow night she'd swim through the cut with Isla Vane beside her, into a hull that Hale's cutters had already marked, and pull the truth out of the dark before the dark closed over it again.

She checked the map one last time, folded it into the oilskin, and strapped it against her spine where it belonged.

Outside, the storm was peaking—and through a tear in the cloud, far out past the point, she could see the tender's lights. And beyond them, faint but unmistakable, a second set of lights moving toward the fleet through the rain. Boats coming in ahead of the weather. Hale was reinforcing.

Whatever window remained, it was closing faster than three tides.

It would have to be tomorrow. It would have to be the very first drain—or there would be nothing left down there but Hale's divers and an empty hole where the truth used to be.
