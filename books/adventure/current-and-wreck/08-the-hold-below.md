## 8. The Hold Below

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and I was already in the water.

I'd told myself I wouldn't come. Told Rosalind Coldharbour—whatever her real name was—that I'd take her as far as the reef line and no farther, that the wreck was hers to rob and the risk hers to drown in. I'd said it standing on my dock with my arms crossed like a wall, and she'd looked at me with those gray eyes that gave away nothing, and she'd said, "Fine. The reef line, then."

She knew. That's what galled me most, looking back on it. She knew I'd follow her down before her feet left the ladder.

The water off Vane Point runs cold even in summer, cold enough to squeeze your chest like a fist, and I'd been diving these reefs since I was nine years old, since Grandad put a mask on me and showed me where the kelp opened into sand channels. I knew every rock by its shadow. But I had never once swum past the outer hull of the Meridian Star. Nobody had. My family's whole history sat under forty feet of green water, and we'd spent four generations pretending it wasn't there, because looking at a drowned ship is one kind of grief and looking at a robbed one is another.

Rosalind swam ahead of me, fins kicking in an easy rhythm, her dive light cutting a cone through the murk. She moved like someone who'd done this a thousand times in a thousand cold places, alone, for money. I hated how much that steadied me.

At the reef gap she stopped and hung in the water, waiting, and when I caught up she pointed her light down the slope to where the seabed shelved away into darkness. The hull rose out of it like a whale's ribcage, furred with weed, crusted white with barnacles older than my grandmother.

Then she swung the light along the plating near the stern, and I saw what she wanted me to see.

Cuts. Clean, straight, machine-made cuts through eighty-year-old steel, their edges still bright where rust hadn't yet claimed them. Somebody had been here with plasma torches or exothermic lances—somebody modern, somebody equipped—and they'd started opening this ship like a tin.

I felt it in my stomach first, a drop like an elevator falling. Then anger, hot and strange, rising up through the cold water until my whole body felt lit from inside. That was *ours*. Not money—I'd never believed in the money—but ours. My great-great-grandfather's wages were in there. Eleven men's wages, eleven families' futures, sunk and stolen twice over: once by the sea, once by whoever decided the Vanes had waited long enough.

Rosalind looked back at me. Even through the mask I could read her face: *Now you understand.*

We went in through the cut.

* * *

Inside the hold, the water was black as ink and thick with silt. Our lights made two small worlds, and beyond them was nothing. The ship groaned around us—not dangerously, just settling, the way old houses talk at night—and every sound came through the hull like it was being whispered directly against my skin.

Rosalind worked methodically, checking her slate map against the reality of collapsed beams and shifted cargo. She'd spent two days decoding Ede's drawing, cross-referencing tide tables and the ship's known layout, and now she moved through the drowned dark like she was walking rooms she'd memorized from a floor plan. Past the forward bulkhead. Along the portside channel where the cargo netting had rotted into lace. Down toward the aft compartment where, according to a dead prospector's last arithmetic, the pay chest had been lashed when the Meridian Star went down.

I followed, hating her a little for how good she was, hating myself more for being grateful.

Her light snagged on something and stopped.

A strongbox, maybe three feet long, iron-strapped, half-buried in silt and the collapsed remains of a wooden crate. It wasn't locked—it had been *opened*. The lid stood propped against the box like a door left ajar, and the interior was bare. Scoured clean. Whoever had cut into the hull had found this place first, years ago, and taken whatever was worth taking.

My heart did something complicated. Relief and devastation in the same beat. If the chest was empty, then the story was over, and I could go back to my life, and I would never have to know whether the promise meant anything—

But Rosalind wasn't looking at the strongbox. She was looking past it, deeper into the compartment, where the hull curved up toward the deck above. Her light found a second shape wedged into the curve: a smaller box, copper-sheathed, its corners dented but intact, sealed with a hasp gone green with age.

She pulled it free and brought it close, and I saw the stencil on its side, faded but legible under the crust:

**MERIDIAN STAR — SHIP'S RECORDS**

"Logbook," she said, and her voice came through the regulator muffled and strange, like a ghost talking underwater. "Ede's map marked it separate from the pay. He must have figured out why."

"Why?"

"Because a pay chest can be explained away. Salvage rights, disputed ownership, decades of legal fog." She turned the box in her light. "A logbook is testimony. Wages entered, wages drawn, signatures. Proof of who was owed what, and proof it was never paid."

She stowed it in the mesh bag at her hip, checked the seal twice, and pointed upward. Time to go. The tide window was short and getting shorter, and somewhere offshore Hale's fleet was riding at anchor, waiting for the same low water we were using.

But I didn't move. Not yet.

I reached into the empty strongbox instead, into the silt at its bottom, and came up with a handful of small, round shapes. Coins. A dozen, maybe fifteen, missed by the torch men because they'd sifted into the seam where the box floor met the hull. I held them up in my light, and the gold caught orange through the grime, and eighty-three years fell away all at once.

My great-great-grandfather had held coins like these. Had earned them hauling nets and standing watches, had signed for them in a book that was now zipped into a stranger's dive bag. They'd never reached his hands. They'd never reached mine. And some man with a torch had come in the night and tried to make sure they never reached anyone's.

I put them in my pocket. All of them. My hands were shaking so hard I could barely work the drawstring.

When I turned, Rosalind was watching me. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. Something had changed between us down here in the dark, in the flooded belly of the thing that broke my family. She hadn't come for the treasure—I'd seen enough of her by now to know that. She'd come because a dying man had handed her a debt, and she was the kind of person who paid her debts. And I had come because I couldn't not.

We weren't strangers anymore. We were witnesses.

* * *

We surfaced in the cove as the sky went from charcoal to ash, the tide already creeping back up the rocks. I stripped my mask with numb fingers and sat down hard on a boulder, and Rosalind climbed out beside me and unzipped the mesh bag with the unhurried care of someone handling evidence, which is exactly what it was.

The copper box came open with a flat bar from her tool kit, prying the corroded hasp without cracking the lid. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job across eight decades, lay the logbook. Leather cover, swollen with damp but whole. She opened it on her knees and angled her headlamp across the pages, and I leaned in close enough to smell the sea-rot and the old iron gall ink.

Entries in a clerk's careful hand. Dates. Names. *Wages accrued.* And then, month after month, the same notation in the margin, growing more frequent, more cramped, more desperate: *payment deferred.* *Payment deferred.* *Payment deferred.*

"They knew," I heard myself say. "They knew before she sank."

"The company was bleeding cash," Rosalind said quietly. "They kept the crew working on promises. Then the ship went down—whether by accident or arrangement, the log doesn't say—and the promises went down with her." She turned a page, and there it was: a full accounting, eleven names, final balances owed. The last name on the list was *E. Vane*, and next to it, in figures I will remember until I die: *£47 12s 6d.*

Forty-seven pounds, twelve shillings, sixpence. That was my family. That was everything they were owed, written in a dead clerk's neat hand, sitting in the dark for eighty-three years while the world called us unlucky and the company called it weather.

I didn't cry. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here: I did not cry. But my breath came out of me in a long shudder, and Rosalind put her hand flat between my shoulder blades and left it there, and let me look as long as I needed to look.

When I finally straightened up, the sun was clearing the headland, and the water in the cove had turned from black to bottle-green, and I felt like a different person wearing the same skin.

"So what happens now," I said. "You sell it. The logbook, the location, the whole story. That was the plan, wasn't it? That's why you took Ede's map."

"That was the plan," she agreed. She closed the logbook and held it a moment, weighing it. "Plans change."

"How?"

"Because a salvage fleet doesn't send torch men into a wreck for pocket change. Hale's people have been stripping this site for years—which means he knows exactly what's down there, which means he has lawyers and permits and a paper trail saying the Meridian Star is his." She looked out at the horizon, where the fleet's tender sat anchored like a patient gull. "Evidence in a bag isn't evidence. Evidence has to be *public*. Loud. Undeniable. Before he gets to it first."

"And you're going to do that. Go loud." I almost laughed. "You've spent your whole life making sure nobody knows your name."

"I have." Something moved behind her eyes—fear, maybe, or its cousin. "Ask me again tomorrow."

But I wasn't going to wait for tomorrow, and I think she knew it. Because while we sat there drying in the thin morning sun, a boat rounded the breakwater—a harbor patrol RIB, running slow, and standing in the bow with binoculars raised was a lean gray-haired man in a fleet jacket, scanning the shoreline. Scanning *us*.

Rosalind went very still.

"That's not patrol," she said. "That's Hale's spotter. He's seen the dive lights. He knows we've been down."

The RIB altered course toward the cove.

"Get the logbook dry," Rosalind said, already moving, pulling the bag tight against her chest. "And Isla—" she glanced back at me, and for the first time since I'd met her, she looked less like a woman running than a woman deciding where to stand. "How many people in this town owe the Vanes a favor?"
