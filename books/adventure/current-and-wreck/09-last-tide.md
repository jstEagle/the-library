## 9. Last Tide

**Rosalind**

The tide went out like a held breath finally released.

I stood at the basin's edge with the pay chest between my feet and the logbook sealed in a waterproof bag against my ribs, and I watched the water peel back from the Meridian Star's bones for the last time. Three hours of exposed hull. Maybe less, if the storm surge came early. Behind me, Isla crouched over the chest, running her thumb along the Vane markings her great-great-grandmother had cut into the lid—proof, carved in brass and oak, that the wages of eleven men had never been paid.

"They'll come before the water does," she said.

"I know."

"How long do we have?"

"Long enough to be arrested. Not long enough to leave."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Then we'd better make it worth their trouble."

The fleet came around the headland at half past nine—three vessels riding low and mean, Hale's salvage tender in the lead with its crane arm folded like a mantis foreleg. I counted men on deck. Eight. Nine. Hale stood at the bow rail in a yellow rain jacket that cost more than my boat had, and even across four hundred meters of wet sand I could feel him looking at us the way a man looks at a problem he intends to solve cheaply.

"Rosalind." Isla's voice was flat. "There's a channel marker post up the beach. My grandfather drove it when I was six. It's iron."

"I see it."

"We can't outrun them carrying this."

"No," I said. "But we don't have to outrun them. We just have to outlast them until the tide turns."

She looked at me then, really looked, and whatever she saw in my face made her nod once and shoulder the chest. We moved up the sand toward the marker post, boots sucking in the wet, and behind us the fleet's tenders began grinding through the shallows toward shore.

* * *

**Isla**

They came up the beach like something crawling out of the wreck itself.

Hale walked in front. I knew his face from the harbor—the way you know a dog that's bitten someone, without ever being introduced. Two of his men flanked him, and three more fanned out along the tideline, cutting off the path to the village road. The rain had started, thin and sideways, and it beaded on the chest between Rosalind and me like the sea itself refusing to let go.

"That's far enough," Hale called. He had a voice built for engine rooms, pitched to carry over noise. "Miss Coldharbour."

Not Miss Whoever-You-Are-This-Season. He knew. Of course he knew—he'd known since the torch took her boat, maybe since before. "You have something of mine," he said.

"It was never yours," Rosalind said. Her voice didn't shake. I kept waiting for it to shake and it never did, and some stubborn part of me stood a little straighter because of it. "It was never your father's either. Eleven men drowned owing wages, Marcus. Their families starved while yours bought a fleet."

"My father bought a reputation," Hale said, "and your father helped him keep it. That makes us family, in a way. Family shares."

One of the flankers drifted left, casual, closing the angle. I shifted so the chest wasn't between me and him anymore—it was behind me, against the marker post, and my hand found the gaff hook I'd carried since I was fourteen because the water doesn't care how brave you are.

"You're trespassing on a registered claim," Hale said, closer now.

"It's not registered," Rosalind said. "You checked. That's why you're here instead of in court."

Something flickered across his face—not anger. Respect, maybe, or its ugly cousin. "Give me the logbook," he said. "Keep the chest. Sell the brass. I'll even give you a finder's fee. You can disappear again, Rosalind. Isn't that what you're good at? Vanishing? Your father said you learned it from your mother."

He said it to hurt her, and it worked—I saw it land, saw her shoulders take the blow—but she didn't move. And then one of the flankers made his mistake. He reached for the bag at her ribs, and I brought the gaff hook down across his wrist, and everything happened very fast after that.

* * *

**Rosalind**

The man screamed and the chest hit the sand and Isla swung the hook again and I got an arm around her waist and hauled her back because there were five of them and we were two and dead women testify poorly. Hale's men closed in a half-circle, and Hale himself hadn't moved—just watched, hands in pockets, letting his dogs do it.

And then an engine, high and wrong, coming down the coast road too fast.

A car I hadn't seen in eleven years slewed onto the shingle above the beach and stopped at an angle that meant the driver had stopped thinking about anything but arriving. The door opened and my father got out, and he looked older than the photograph in my head, gray to the temples, in a coat he'd owned my whole childhood, and he did not look at me first.

He looked at Marcus Hale.

"Get away from them," Edmund Coldharbour said.

Marcus laughed. It was not a kind sound. "Edmund. Twenty years, and you come down a hill in the rain for this? For a fishwife and a runaway?"

"For the truth," my father said, and started down the beach.

Hale's men hesitated—they were salvage crew, not soldiers, and old authority still has weight—and in that hesitation my father reached us. Up close he was breathing hard and his hands were shaking and he would not look at me, not yet, and I understood: he couldn't. Looking at me would break whatever he'd driven here holding together.

"I kept the survey," he said to Hale, loud enough for all of them, for the men on the tenders, for Isla, for the rain. "Nineteen eighty-eight. I mapped the Meridian Star's cargo manifest against the insurance filing. The wages were aboard. They were always aboard. Your father paid me to lose the second copy and I did, God forgive me, and I've spent thirty years telling myself the sea had taken it anyway." He pulled a folded sheaf from inside his coat, plastic-wrapped, dry. "It didn't. Here it is."

"You senile old—" Hale started forward.

"I'm testifying," my father said. "Monday. Public inquiry. Everything. And if any harm comes to these two women between now and then, everything becomes a book."

The name of it hung in the salt air. Hale stopped moving entirely. A man who has spent decades burying a thing will freeze at the sound of a shovel.

"The tide's turning," Isla said quietly, beside me. She was right. The first thin sheets of water were sliding back across the flats, silver under the storm light, reaching for the wreck like fingers remembering a shape.

Hale looked at the water, at the chest, at my father, at me. Whatever arithmetic he ran, it came out short. "This isn't finished," he said.

"Yes, it is," I said. My first words to him. "That's exactly what it is."

His men dragged their bleeding wrist-man back toward the tenders. The engines turned over, sullen, and the fleet backed off the flats into deepening water, and we stood there—three of us, four counting the chest—as the sea came home.

My father turned to me last. Rain on his face. His mouth opened, closed, opened.

"Rosalind," he said. "I'm not asking you to forgive—"

"I know what you're asking." I handed him the logbook bag. "Hold this. Both hands. If you drop it, I'll never speak to you again, and if you carry it Monday, maybe someday I will."

He took it the way you take a newborn. Like something that could die in his grip.

Behind us, the water closed over the Meridian Star's ribs, and Isla put her hand on my shoulder, and the tide that had taken everything began, at last, giving it back.
