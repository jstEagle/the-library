## 10. Anchorage

The news broke on a Tuesday, which felt wrong somehow. Catastrophes should arrive on Fridays, in the dark, with weather attached. Instead the sun came up mild and gold over the harbor, and by nine o'clock the Meridian Star was on every screen from here to the capital.

Rosalind watched it happen from the window of Isla's kitchen, a mug of tea going cold in her hands. The regional feed ran footage she recognized frame by frame: the pay chest crusted in coral, the crew's logbook spread open under museum lights, her own voice—recorded three days ago, shaky and too fast—reading Ede's name into the record.

*The cargo of the Meridian Star was stolen wages. Stolen from thirty-one families. I'm asking that it be returned to them.*

Then the anchor's voice, brisk and bright: *—and in a stunning development, salvage magnate Marcus Hale was taken into custody this morning at his estate outside Port Calloway—*

They cut to Hale being walked to a car, jacketless, one hand raised against the lenses. He looked smaller than he had on the water. Older. His mouth kept moving but no one held a microphone close enough to catch what he was saying, and Rosalind suspected no one ever would again.

She should have felt triumphant. She'd rehearsed the feeling for weeks, back when triumph still seemed like something she might be allowed. What she actually felt was lighter than that and stranger: the particular vertigo of a door opening after years of bracing against walls.

"You're doing the thing," Isla said from the stove, not turning around. "Where you go quiet and stare at nothing like you're calculating tide tables."

"I'm always calculating tide tables."

"You're doing it *sadly*." Isla set down two plates—eggs, bread fried in butter, the breakfast of women who'd been diving since dawn for a week and had stopped pretending they'd eat anything else. "Sit. Eat. The world can fall over without your supervision for ten minutes."

Rosalind sat. The kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and salt and the tar sealant they'd used on the chest. Through the window she could see the harbor, and the harbor could see her, and for once she didn't flinch from the arrangement.

Her phone buzzed against the table. Her father's name on the screen—the first time she'd saved it there, actually saved it, rather than letting it live as an anonymous number she could ignore forever. She let it ring twice before answering.

"I saw," she said.

"Everyone saw." A pause. On the line she could hear wind, gulls, the creak of a boat—he'd come north, then. He was already here. "I wanted you to hear it from me before the press got clever. They've started digging into who filed the site report."

"They'll dig for a while," Rosalind said. "I signed it Coldharbour."

Silence, long enough that she checked whether the call had dropped.

"You signed it," he said finally, and his voice did something she hadn't heard since she was small enough to ride his shoulders through museum corridors—something unguarded, almost young. "Your mother's name. Your real name."

"It seemed like the day for it."

"Roz." He cleared his throat. When he spoke again the roughness was still there, worn smooth at the edges. "I've spent forty years telling myself that burying the Star was prudence. That the families were paid off fairly, that the scandal would have hurt more people than it helped. Forty years, and it took my daughter six tides to make me see what I'd helped hide." A breath. "I'm testifying. All of it. Everything I know about Hale, about the original theft, about me. I want you to hear that from me and not from a subpoena."

"I know," she said. "Isla told me you called her first."

"She's a better person than either of us. Don't tell her I said that."

"She knows."

He laughed—a real laugh, cracked down the middle—and for a moment they were just two people who shared a name and a nose and a talent for making things worse before making them right.

"The hearing is Thursday," he said. "You don't have to come. You've done more than anyone asked."

"I'll be there."

"Of course you will." Another pause, softer. "Your mother would have—" He stopped. Started again. "She'd have liked Isla. She'd have liked who you are here."

Rosalind pressed her palm flat against the table until the grain bit into her skin, holding herself steady around the ache of it. "Thursday," she said. "Come early. I'll buy you breakfast."

* * *

Thursday came gray and clean, rain scrubbing the streets before dawn so the town shone like something newly made. The courthouse was small—wood floors, brass fittings, a gallery that held maybe sixty people and held all sixty that morning, standing room along the back wall, fishermen in their good coats, old women who remembered grandmothers who remembered the Star going down.

Rosalind testified for two hours. She laid out Ede's map with its careful inked lines and its dying man's handwriting, described the cutting marks on the hull, walked the room through the logbook page by page—the wages entered, the wages never paid, the captain's terse note that the cargo would be *secured pending settlement*, and beneath it, in different ink, the settlement that never came.

When the magistrate asked how she'd come to be involved, the room went quiet in a way she could feel against her skin.

"A dying man traded me a map for a promise," she said. "I took the map intending to sell it. I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But he died holding my hand, and somewhere between his last breath and the first low tide, the promise became mine. Not because I'm good. Because I was there, and I could, and no one else would."

She looked out at the gallery while she said it—at Isla in the front row, chin lifted, arms crossed like a woman guarding a gate she'd decided to open—and understood that she was describing the exact shape of her whole life turned inside out. All those years of being there and leaving anyway. It had only ever required staying.

Hale's lawyers fought everything and won nothing. By Friday afternoon the ruling came down: the wreck and its cargo belonged to the descendants of the crew, claim honored in full, the salvage fleet's permits revoked retroactively, criminal proceedings against Marcus Hale referred upward. The magistrate read it aloud in a flat administrative voice, and thirty-one families' worth of people stood up in a courtroom built for fifty, and the sound they made wasn't cheering exactly. It was deeper than cheering. It was the sound of a debt four generations old finally being marked *paid*.

Afterward, on the courthouse steps, an elderly woman took Rosalind's hands in both of hers. Her name was Maren Vane; Isla's great-aunt; ninety-one years old with a spine like a boat's keel.

"Tomas Ede," she said. "He was my cousin. Ran off prospecting when we were young and never wrote." Her eyes were wet and fierce. "He came home after all. Just took him fifty years and a stranger to do it."

"He'd have wanted you to have it," Rosalind said, which was true, and also not the half of it, and Maren Vane squeezed her hands hard enough to mean she knew both things.

* * *

The unpacking happened by accident, which was the only way Rosalind would have allowed it.

It was a week later. The site report was filed, the chest was in the maritime museum's conservation lab behind glass, and the harbor had gone back to being a working harbor instead of a story. She'd told herself—firmly, in the voice she used for contracts—that she'd stay through the handover, one more week, and then move on. One season anywhere. Those were the rules. She'd written the rules herself, carved them into herself over fifteen years of leaving, and rules were load-bearing.

Then Isla appeared at her rented room with a borrowed truck and a look that permitted no discussion.

"You're coming to Sunday dinner," she said. "Aunt Maren's cooking. You don't get a vote."

So Rosalind went, and ate until she ached, and laughed at stories about Isla's grandfather stealing a police boat at nineteen, and walked home along the seawall at midnight with lantern light jittering on black water, and stood in the doorway of her room and looked at her bags.

They sat by the door where they always sat. Packed. Ready. Two duffels and a field case, packed the way she'd packed them a hundred times, packed so thoroughly that unpacking had become a kind of superstition—*if I never fully arrive, I can never fully lose anything.*

She knelt down and opened the big duffel.

It took an hour. Not because there was much—there never was—but because every item came out of the bag trailing its history. The dive knife from the Java contract. The sweater bought in a market in Reykjavik and abandoned in a laundromat in Lisbon and mailed to her by a stranger who'd found her forwarding address, somehow, though she'd never learned how. The photograph of her mother she'd carried face-down in every bag for twenty years, and which she now set face-up on the windowsill, where the morning light could reach it.

Into the closet went the clothes. Onto the desk went the tools, the calipers, the notebook with Ede's map pressed flat between its pages like a pressed flower. The empty bags she folded and put on the top shelf, not thrown away—she wasn't a woman who threw things away, she was learning that about herself—but put away, demoted from furniture-of-her-life to equipment-for-trips. Trips. As in, occasions. As in, things one returned from.

When it was done she stood in the middle of the small room and felt—nothing dramatic. No swell of music. Just the absence of a weight she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for her skeleton. The room didn't feel fuller. She felt less hollow.

Downstairs, the landlady had left a note under her door: *Mail came for you. R. Coldharbour.* Actual mail, addressed to her actual name, left in an actual slot. Inside was a single sheet from the heritage office—routine, bureaucratic, a form confirming her as site archaeologist of record for the Meridian Star recovery, with a line for her signature and a date.

She read it twice. Then she sat down at the desk, picked up the pen she'd owned since university, and signed it: *Rosalind E. Coldharbour.*

Full name. Real name. First time in fifteen years she'd written it on anything that mattered.

She set the form aside to dry and looked out the window at the harbor, where the evening boats were coming in with their lights strung along the masts like low constellations. Somewhere out past the point, under forty feet of cold green water, the Meridian Star lay open to the light at last, her bones catalogued, her cargo accounted for, her people's names spoken aloud in a courtroom and written into the permanent record.

Tomas Ede, Rosalind thought. We got there. Both of us. You can rest now.

Her phone lit up on the desk. A message from her father: *Dinner next week? There's a site in the Orkneys I think you'd hate. Come hate it with me.*

And below it, a second message, from a number she didn't recognize: *Ms. Coldharbour — Dr. Okafor, National Maritime Institute. We've been reviewing the Star's survey data, and there's an anomaly on the sonar roughly two hundred meters east of the hull. Something else down there. Something bigger. Would you be available to consult?*

Rosalind read it twice. Outside, the last boat slipped into its berth, and the harbor lights steadied on the water, and she smiled at the screen like a woman who had just been offered the one thing she'd spent her whole life refusing.

She typed back: *I'm available. I live here now.*
