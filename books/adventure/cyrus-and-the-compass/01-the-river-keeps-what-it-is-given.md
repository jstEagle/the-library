## 1. The River Keeps What It Is Given

The storm came up the valley like something with a grudge, and Noor Ashgrove flew into it the way she did everything—alone, under-fueled on patience, and with her eyes moving faster than her mouth.

"Come on, old girl," she murmured to the plane. The de Havilland was thirty-one years old and held together by rivets, prayer, and a maintenance schedule only she believed in. The rain hit the windshield in flat gray sheets, and the Vessel River below had gone the color of wet pewter, swollen out of its banks and chewing at the trees along the bend.

She'd been flying medicine runs out of Kettle Hollow for six years. Insulin and antibiotics upstream to the settlements that clung to the high ground, mail and a sick child or two coming back down. Nobody asked questions in the interior, which was exactly how Noor liked it. Invisibility wasn't loneliness, she told herself. It was safety. The territory's governor had a face for the radio and a name on every government building from here to the capital, and men like Aldous Crane preferred their pilots quiet, their rivers unexamined, and their history where he kept it: buried.

Noor kept her head down and her logbook clean.

Mostly.

The turbulence hit at the bend above Sallow Cut, a hard slap that dropped the plane ten feet and rattled the cargo netting behind her seat. She corrected, swore softly, and looked down to check the river's mood—and that was when she saw it.

A glint. Brief, wrong, deliberate-looking. Sunlight through a tear in the cloud cover caught something snagged in the roots of an overturned willow at the water's edge, something that flashed like polished metal and then went dark again as the cloud closed.

Noor circled once. Then twice, lower, fighting the crosswind off the flooded flats.

It was a chest. She could see the shape of it now even through the rain-haze—a strongbox, iron-banded, wedged tight in the willow's root cage with the flood pushing against it like a dog worrying a bone. Water broke white around its corners. It wasn't drifting. It was *stuck*, as if the river had carried it a long way and finally decided to keep it somewhere she could find.

"Don't," she said aloud, to herself, to the plane, to the chest. "Don't look at it."

But she was already banking, already scanning the bank downstream for the gravel bar that surfaced every dry season—the one wide enough, on a good day, to set down on. There were no good days left this season. The monsoon's first wall was still two days south by the radio reports, but the sky ahead of her had the bruised, low-slung look of weather that lied about its schedule.

She set down anyway. Short field, into the wind, floats kissing the flooded shallows beside the bar with a jolt that made her teeth click. The engine coughed, caught, settled to an idle. Rain drummed the wings.

Noor sat for a moment with her hands on the yoke, listening to the water shove past the floats. Six years in the interior taught her three rules. Never fly into weather you can't fly out of. Never land for anything you can't see all of. And never, ever take gifts from the river.

The river gave things back the way a creditor gives change: always less than you were owed, and always with interest attached. She'd pulled drowned hunters and drowned dogs and one drowned piano out of that water over the years, and every single thing it surrendered had cost somebody something. A gift from the flood was just a debt wearing a ribbon.

She got out anyway.

The water was thigh-deep and moving with real intent, shoving at her legs, full of silt and sticks and the cold smell of things torn loose upstream. She waded to the willow with the cargo strap from the plane coiled over her shoulder, boots finding the submerged root structure by feel. The chest sat in the root cage like an egg in a fist, and up close she could see it wasn't farm salvage, wasn't mining gear, wasn't any of the ordinary junk the river coughed up after a flood.

It was built to last. Iron bands gone orange with rust, but the wood beneath them dense and dark and unswollen, oiled sometime in the last twenty years by someone who cared. And there—on the lid, half-eaten by corrosion but still legible when she scraped it with her thumbnail—a stamp. A seal pressed into a brass plate, some kind of crest she didn't recognize: a tower over crossed keys, worn nearly smooth.

Kestrian workmanship, though she wouldn't know that until later. All she knew standing hip-deep in the flood was that nobody in the interior made anything like this, and nobody honest lost something like this in a river and didn't come looking for it.

That was the part that should have stopped her. That was the part she'd remember at three in the morning for weeks afterward: the moment she understood the chest belonged to someone, and chose not to think about who.

"Nope," she said, and looped the strap under it anyway.

It weighed like sin. She got it moving an inch at a time, the flood trying to take it back with every shift, the willow roots groaning. Twice she lost her footing and went down to her armpits, and both times she came up spitting and angrier, and both times she put her shoulder back under the thing because—she couldn't have said why. Curiosity, maybe. Or the older, dumber instinct that lives in people who work alone: the certainty that if you leave a mystery floating in your river, it will follow you home.

It took her forty minutes and most of her strength. When the chest finally came free, the current grabbed it and she rode it, wrestling, toward the gravel bar, and got it dragged up onto the stones just as the cloud tore open overhead and the rain came down like a verdict.

Loading it into the cargo bay took another ten minutes and a length of netting and language that would have gotten her grounded at any respectable airfield. The medicine crates got shoved aft. The chest sat amidships, dripping, smug.

Noor climbed back into the cockpit, wrung out her sleeves, and sat with the engine idling while the rain hammered everything.

Here was the sensible move: open the cargo door, roll the thing back into the flood, and fly to Sallow Cut with the insulin and tell no one, ever, that she'd seen it. Here was what she actually did: sit there getting colder and colder while the sensible move sat across from her and waited, patient as the river.

Because she'd seen the seal. Tower over crossed keys. And underneath the corrosion, faint as a watermark, letters she'd half-recognized without knowing why—letters that itched at the back of her skull like a word on the tip of the tongue. Her mother had come out of the old border country before the maps changed. Her mother had hummed songs in a language Noor couldn't name and owned a tin cup with a crest stamped on the bottom that Noor had drunk from her whole childhood without once looking closely at.

Tower over crossed keys.

"No," Noor said firmly. She fired up the throttle and took off into the teeth of the weather, because flying was the one thing that always made sense, and because the fastest way to stop thinking about a chest was to be too busy not dying to think about anything.

* * *

The storm followed her the whole way upriver.

It wasn't a bad flight, by interior standards. Bad was when you couldn't see the water at all, when the world went white and you flew by the seat of your pants and the memory of the channel. This was merely miserable—rain in gusts, wind shoving the tail around, visibility down to a mile and closing. Noor flew it the way she did everything, hands light, eyes moving, mind mercifully empty except for the channel and the fuel gauge and the small stubborn fact of the weight sitting behind her seat that hadn't been on her manifest.

Twice she caught herself glancing back at it through the cargo netting. Twice she made herself stop.

The settlement at Sallow Cut was a scatter of tin roofs on a bluff, and the landing was ugly and short and greeted by old Tomas Ferro waving both arms from the dock like a man directing traffic in hell. She got the medicine crates into his hands and the insulin into his icebox and drank the tea he pushed on her because refusing would take longer than accepting.

"You look like something the river spat out," Tomas said.

"The river tried." She wrapped her hands around the tin mug and didn't say anything else, which Tomas, being interior himself, respected.

But as she stood in his doorway watching the rain, she found her eyes going down to the plane moored at the dock, riding the flood swell, and to the shape under the tarp she'd thrown over the cargo bay. Not hidden. Just... covered. The way you cover furniture in a house where someone died.

Tomas followed her gaze. Said nothing. Interior manners again.

"I picked up salvage," she said, before he could ask. "Off the bend below here."

"Flood's been giving things back all week." He shrugged. "Ferro boy found a canoe full of eels. Live ones. Nobody knows how."

"This wasn't eels."

He looked at her then, really looked, the way old men in the interior look at you when they've decided to give you one piece of information and no more. "Whatever it is," he said, "the government salvage men don't come up past the rapids much. But they've got a new man this year. Comes down from the capital. Young. Polite." He refilled her tea she hadn't asked for. "Polite's worse."

"Thanks, Tomas."

"Don't thank me. I didn't say anything." He turned back to his stove. "And Ashgrove—if it's got a seal on it, don't show it to anyone who asks about it. People who ask about seals aren't curious. They're counting."

She flew out an hour later, into the last of the light, with the storm breaking apart behind her and the whole western sky going the color of a healing bruise. The flight home was smooth, almost gentle, the river running gold and violent beneath her, and Noor had the sky to herself and should have felt the peace she always felt up there, alone above the water, answerable to nothing.

Instead she spent the whole flight listening to the cargo bay.

Not literally. Planes don't talk. But the chest was back there, and she knew it the way you know a stranger is standing too close behind you in an empty room, and by the time she brought the plane down on her own strip at dusk—two hundred meters of graded gravel and one windsock and a shack with a stove and a cot and eleven years of her life folded into four walls—she had already decided.

She told herself she was only going to look. Look, and then decide what to do. That was reasonable. That was due diligence. Salvage law in the territory was murky enough that even Inspector Whoever-He-Was would agree a pilot was entitled to inspect recovered property before reporting it.

Reporting it. Right.

She tied down the plane, hauled the chest inside the shack with a hand truck and a vocabulary, and set it on the floorboards under the lamp while the rain started up again on the tin roof, soft now, almost apologetic.

Up close, in good light, it was worse. Better. Worse. The brass plate with the crest was cleaner than she'd thought—someone had maintained this thing, someone had *loved* it—and the lock was a serious mechanism, not a padlock but a built-in tumbler affair with a keyway shaped like nothing she'd seen outside of a museum case. The wood was dark oak, scarred, travel-worn. Stenciled on one end, mostly faded, were numbers. A date, maybe. She tilted the lamp.

**MERROW'S BEND — 1994**

She read it three times. The lamp hissed. The rain ticked on the roof.

Merrow's Bend. Everyone in the interior knew the name the way you know the names of places that stopped existing—said quietly, if at all, usually by old people, usually followed by a silence you could land a plane in. A village on the old border, gone before Noor was old enough to remember it going. Flood damage, the official record said. A tragedy of the waters. The governor at the time—younger then, hungrier—had flown out personally to survey the devastation, and there were photographs, Noor supposed, of him standing in the mud looking grave.

Her mother had never once said the village's name in the house. But she'd gone quiet, one whole winter, in a way a child notices and doesn't understand until much later.

Noor crouched next to the chest and put her fingers on the stencil and felt her heart going hard and slow, like footsteps on a dock.

The river keeps what it is given. That was the oldest rule of the interior, older than the maps, older than the governor's family money. The river takes your dead, your secrets, your sins, holds them in the cold brown dark, and gives them back when it decides you've forgotten enough to be hurt properly.

She should burn it. She should drag it out into the rain right now and let the water finish whatever it started, and tomorrow she'd fly her routes and keep her logbook clean and stay invisible, invisible, invisible, the way she'd been invisible for eleven safe years.

Instead she went to the tool bench and came back with the pry bar.

"Just looking," she told the chest, and herself, and the shack, and the dark window where the rain came down and the river ran on somewhere below in the black, carrying everything it knew.

The lock was old and the pry bar was newer. It took her four tries and a bleeding knuckle, and then the lid came up with a groan of tired hinges, and the smell that rose out of it was paper and oil and twenty years of sealed dark.

Inside, packed in waxed canvas, lay documents. Hundreds of them. Folders, dispatches, ledgers, bound in government twine, stamped over and over with a crest she now recognized everywhere—in the corners, on the seals, in red wax and blue ink and blind embossing, tower over crossed keys, tower over crossed keys, tower over crossed keys.

And on the topmost folder, in a clerk's careful hand, a heading that stopped her breath:

*Territorial Directive 44 — Merrow's Bend — Final Disposition.*

Final disposition. She knew what those words meant in government mouths. Everybody in the interior knew, in the place where knowing lived below language.

Outside, the rain kept falling. Somewhere downriver, the flood was still rising, still carrying things toward the sea. Noor Ashgrove stood in her shack with her bleeding knuckle and her lamp and her eleven years of invisibility, and reached for the topmost folder with the hand that wasn't shaking.

Only slightly shaking.

The wilderness had taught her that gifts from the water are rarely free.

She opened it anyway.
