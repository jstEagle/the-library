## 2. A Dead Nation's Seal

The chest sat on my table like a guest who wouldn't leave.

I'd set it down between the woodstove and the window, where the lamplight could reach it and I could watch it from every angle, which was ridiculous, because chests don't move. But twenty years of flying the interior teaches you a certain grammar of things: what a snapped branch means, what a silence means, what a gift from the water means. The river doesn't give. It trades. And it always names its price later, when you're least able to pay.

I made tea I didn't drink. I cleaned my rifle, which needed cleaning anyway, and told myself that was the reason I was holding it. Outside, the rain had settled into the long patient drumming that meant the monsoon was thinking about staying. My airstrip — a scraped ribbon of gravel and grass I'd spent nine years coaxing out of the treeline — was a swamp by now. Nothing would come up that strip tonight except frogs.

The chest itself was good work. Brass corners gone green with age, oak slats swollen fat with riverwater, iron hasps crusted in rust and something darker that I chose not to examine too closely. No maker's mark. No shipping label. Just a stenciled number along one edge, half worn away: **K-114**.

I knew what the K stood for. Everybody past forty knew what the K stood for, if they'd grown up anywhere near the old survey maps, the ones with the fold lines gone soft and a rectangle of blank paper where a country used to be.

Kestria.

Erased when I was eleven. I remembered my father burning his atlas page in the stove — not tearing it out, *burning* it, like it might contaminate the rest of the book — while the radio read out the Reunification Act in its flat official voice. *All administrative records of the former territory are consolidated under provincial authority.* That was the phrase I still carried, all these years later, the way you carry a splinter too deep to dig out. Consolidated. As if a nation were a pile of firewood.

I put the rifle down. Picked up the pry bar instead.

*You should burn it,* said the sensible voice, the one that had kept me alive through engine failures and whiteout landings and one very bad winter with a man named Tomas. *Sealed chest out of a flood current, government number stenciled on the side. You know exactly whose this is. Put it back in the river and let the river keep its secret.*

But the sensible voice had never explained why I'd winched the thing aboard in the first place. In that storm, with my fuel gauge lying to me and the light dying, I'd seen brass flash in the flooded bend below Merrow's Bend — no. Below the bend. The village didn't have a name anymore. Officially it had never had a name.

I worked the pry bar into the seam.

* * *

The lid fought me for ten minutes. Water had welded it shut, and the wood groaned like a living thing before it gave, and when it gave, the smell came out — mildew and ink and something faintly sweet, like old varnish, like a house closed up for a funeral.

Oilcloth. Layers of it, wrapped around bundles, tied with waxed cord that someone had knotted with care. Whoever packed this chest expected it to be opened again. Expected it to survive.

I unwrapped the first bundle on the table, and the lamp threw my shadow huge against the far wall, and I remember thinking, absurdly, *stand straighter*, because whatever this was, I wanted to meet it on my feet.

Documents. Dozens of them. Typewritten pages going soft at the edges, carbon copies still sharp enough to read, a ledger bound in gray board with a cracked spine. And across the top of every single page, pressed into the paper in deep violet wax-and-ink, a seal I hadn't seen since I was a child staring at the space where it used to be.

A compass rose. Fourteen points. Ringed by words in the old Kestrian script that I could only half read anymore — the province had scrubbed the alphabet from schoolbooks the same year it scrubbed the maps — but the center emblem needed no translation. A river bending through hills. A bridge over it.

Underneath, in the provincial printing they'd allowed during the transition years, smaller: **REPUBLIC OF KESTRIA — OFFICE OF INTERNAL RECORDS**.

My hands were steady. I noticed that the way you notice a strange sound in an engine — not alarmed yet, just filing it away. Steady hands, reading dead paperwork, in a shack at the edge of nowhere, with rain coming down like the sky meant to finish what the soldiers started.

I turned to the ledger first. Ledgers are honest; they're built to be. Dates running down the left margin in a clerk's tidy hand. Entries. Transfers. Requisitions. And then, three pages in, a different hand — heavier pen, harder pressure — and entries that stopped being about grain tonnage and started being about people.

*Merrow's Bend. Population 412 as of last census. Disposition: total.*

*Disposition: total.*

I read the phrase four times before it gave up its meaning, and even then my mind kept trying to soften it into something bureaucratic and survivable. Total disposition of assets. Total disposition of structures. But the next line killed the soft version:

*No relocations authorized. No survivors to be processed.*

There's a kind of cold that comes off paper. I'd always thought that was a figure of speech. It isn't. It rose off that ledger like air off a glacier, and I pulled my jacket tighter around my shoulders without deciding to.

* * *

The orders themselves were in a separate folder, and they were worse, because they were so ordinary. Typed on a form. Reference numbers. Distribution list. The language of men doing a job, the way a butcher's invoice is the language of meat.

*By directive of the Provincial Security Council, and pursuant to authority delegated under Article Nine, the settlement known as Merrow's Bend is designated hostile to the reunification process. All measures necessary are hereby authorized.*

Signed.

I stared at the signature for a long time. Not because I didn't recognize it. Because I did.

**A. Crane.**

Everyone in the interior knew Aldous Crane's name. Twenty years ago he'd been a mid-level administrator nobody bothered photographing; now his face hung in every government office from the coast to the high passes, silver-haired and square-jawed, the Governor who'd "healed the former territory," who'd brought roads and clinics and quiet. They named a dam after him. Pilots drank toasts to him in the fuel-town bars because his administration subsidized aviation fuel and asked no questions about cargo.

Asked no questions. I'd flown for his government's contractors for six years. Medicine runs, mail runs, once — though I'd told myself it was survey equipment — sealed crates marked only with reference numbers, out of a depot near the old border, into the capital.

Article Nine. I'd heard the phrase my whole life the way you hear weather reports. *Pursuant to Article Nine.* It was the hinge everything swung on, the legal skeleton key, and here it was in its original lock, and the hand on the key belonged to the man whose portrait smiled down from the courthouse wall in Wrenfall.

I sat down. I don't remember deciding to sit. My legs simply reported that standing was no longer among the day's approved activities.

* * *

The rain changed pitch sometime past midnight — the monsoon arriving in earnest, a wall of it marching up the valley, and I lit the lantern because the lamp was guttering and I couldn't stand the dark just then. Not with the papers spread out around me like a card game played by ghosts.

I went through everything. It took hours. Most of it was administrative chaff — supply manifests, troop rotation schedules, a memo about road access that read like any road memo until you noticed the road was being built *to* the village and the village was scheduled to stop existing two weeks after the road was finished. They'd paved the way in so the trucks could carry the evidence out.

But some of it was worse than chaff. Some of it was names.

A census annex, clipped inside the ledger's back cover. Household by household. The clerk had been thorough — ages, occupations, a column headed *notes* that held small human details in cramped handwriting. *Ferrymaster, 61, bad leg.* *Midwife, serves three settlements.* *Schoolteacher, 24, recently arrived from the coast — verify credentials.*

Four hundred and twelve people, reduced to a column of notes by a man whose handwriting slanted left, and I caught myself wondering about him — whether he'd signed his name with relief or shame, whether he was still alive, whether he ever woke at night hearing the rain and thinking of a ferryman with a bad leg.

And then, near the bottom of the annex, a note in different ink, added later, the letters pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*Witness statements consolidated. See Appendix K. Appendix K destroyed per directive.*

Destroyed per directive. Except somebody hadn't destroyed it. Somebody had wrapped it in oilcloth, knotted the cord with care, sealed it in a chest numbered K-114, and — what? Sunk it? Lost it? Tried to float it downstream to safety and failed?

Or succeeded, and died waiting for it to matter.

I looked at the compass rose stamped in violet on the folder's cover, and for the first time all night I felt something other than cold. Anger, maybe. Or its quieter cousin, which is resolve. Hard to tell them apart at three in the morning. Both of them keep you awake.

* * *

Here is what I knew about myself, sitting in that yellow circle of lantern light: I had built my whole life on the principle of not seeing. Not seeing what the sealed crates held. Not seeing why certain passengers paid in cash and boarded at night. Not seeing, most of all, the blank space on every map I flew by — the rectangle of nothing where Kestria had been, where villages like Merrow's Bend had stood since before the province drew its first border, gone so completely that children born after the Reunification genuinely believed the interior had always been empty. I'd flown medicine over that emptiness a dozen times. Once I'd been asked to divert around weather and found myself crossing ground that the map called unpopulated, and there had been foundations down there, roofless walls, an orchard gone wild, apple trees the size of oaks. I'd noted it in my logbook as *terrain anomaly* and never thought about it again.

That's a lie. I thought about it again constantly. I just never let myself think about it *on purpose*.

The chest on my table was four hundred twelve people asking me, on purpose, to look.

I thought about Inspector-shaped consequences. About how a woman alone, nine years building an airstrip out of swamp, one plane, no family in the province, no lawyer, no name anyone important knew — how easily she could become a terrain anomaly herself. The sensible voice laid it all out, calm as a preflight checklist. Burn the papers. Bury the chest. Fly your routes. Grow old.

Then I turned over the last document in the folder, a thin sheet I'd saved for last without knowing why, and stopped breathing for a second.

It wasn't an order. It was a letter — handwritten, undated, unsigned, tucked behind the census annex as if someone had hidden it there in haste. The ink had run where water had found it, but the words survived:

*If you are reading this, then the river did what we could not. These papers are the only testimony left. The court at the capital still takes petitions — they will say otherwise, they will laugh, but Judge Rook has taken them before. Carry it. Someone must arrive.*

Judge Rook. Ansel Rook. The name went through me like current through a wire. Old Rook, retired to the capital, the one judge who'd once — years ago, at a fuel-dock dinner neither of us should have attended — looked at me across a table and said, in that dry voice of his, *Ashgrove, if you ever find something worth the risk, bring it to me. I'm old and stubborn and I owe the interior more than I've paid.*

I'd laughed. I'd thought he was drunk.

I folded the letter along its original creases, careful as handling a moth. Outside, the rain hammered the tin roof and the river I couldn't see was rising in the dark, carrying whatever else it carried, keeping what it was given.

The sensible voice had gone quiet. In its place was something older and less negotiable, the thing that makes a bush pilot turn back into weather she should fly around because someone down there signaled with a mirror.

They'd erased a country. They'd erased a village. They'd written *no survivors to be processed* in a clerk's tidy hand and paved a road to do it faster. And the man who signed it governed everything I could see from altitude, everything I'd ever fly toward, and he'd done it wearing the face on the courthouse wall.

I banked the stove low, wrapped the documents back in their oilcloth, and put the chest under my floorboards, under the spare parts crate where no inspector would look twice.

Then I got out the chart, spread it under the lantern, and put my finger on the river bend where I'd found it. Downstream from there: fishing camps, fuel docks, the small economy of people who lived off the water and noticed everything that floated past. Somebody had packed that chest. Somebody local, or somebody passing through. If there was even a chance another pair of eyes had seen it go into the water —

I told myself I was going down to the fuel dock tomorrow for diesel and salt and ordinary reasons. I almost believed it.

At dawn the rain thinned to a gray drizzle, and I walked out onto my drowned airstrip and looked south along the river, toward the camps, toward the capital beyond them, toward a courtroom and a judge and a governor's smiling face, and I understood that the woman who'd landed yesterday afternoon was not the woman standing here now. The river had given her something, and the price was already coming due.

Downstream, at the fuel dock, someone was waiting. I didn't know his name yet. But I already knew he'd recognized my engine.
