## 3. The Man Who Reads Water

The fish had stopped biting an hour before the plane came over, and Cyrus Vane had learned long ago to trust that kind of silence more than any instrument.

He sat in the doorway of the drying shed at Pell's Camp with a line slack between his fingers and his ears doing the work his hands had abandoned. Ten years of hiding taught a man to listen the way other men pray—habitually, desperately, and with a specific list of things he was asking for. He listened for boots on the boardwalk. For radios crackling in patrol boats. For the particular cough of government engines idling too close to places they had no business being.

What he heard instead was a Lycoming O-360 running rough on the left cylinder, flying low against a headwind it had no business fighting, and banking wide over the river bend like a pilot who knew every rock under the water by her first name.

Cyrus set down the fishing rod.

He knew that plane. Not this exact airframe—he'd never seen it up close—but the *kind* of it, the way interior pilots flew them, the way the sound came apart over water and reassembled a half-second later off the far bluffs. A bush plane running medicine and mail and silence into places the maps had stopped pretending to describe. And there was only one strip within a hundred miles of Pell's Camp that still operated out of a shack with no tower and no registry worth the paper it wasn't printed on.

Ashgrove. The Ashgrove woman. Noor.

He'd heard the name maybe six times in three years, always from fishermen, always the same way people say the name of weather: *the Ashgrove woman flew old Marta's insulin up through the fog when nobody else would go; the Ashgrove woman doesn't land at Wrenfall anymore since the new inspectors came; the Ashgrove woman keeps to herself.* In Cyrus's experience, people who kept to themselves in the interior were either guilty of something or paying for something, and after ten years he'd stopped believing there was much difference between the two.

He stood in the doorway and watched the plane shrink northward over the drowned willows, and he told himself it was none of his business.

Then he saw what she'd left behind.

* * *

The chest came up out of the river two hours later, and Cyrus watched it happen from the reed bank with binoculars he'd bought from a dead man's estate and cleaned so many times the lenses had gone soft as memory.

She'd come back. That was the first thing. She'd finished her run, whatever it was, and doubled back downriver through weather that would keep honest pilots on the ground, and she'd hovered her skids over the flooded bend where the current made its slow silver eddy, and she'd winched something dripping and dark out of the water like the river handing up a debt.

Cyrus lowered the binoculars. His heart was doing something embarrassing.

A chest. A sealed chest, snagged in the bend below the old Kestrian ferry crossing—the same bend, he realized, the same exact water, where the flood of '04 had torn the last archive barge loose from its moorings and scattered twenty years of a nation's paperwork into the current like ash. He'd spent four seasons dredging that stretch of river in his younger, stupider years, before the inspections got too frequent, before the falsified entry caught up with him, before Vane became a name you didn't say near government buildings. He'd found buttons. He'd found a child's shoe. He'd found forty feet of rotted rope and a ledger page so dissolved it read like a prayer in a language God had forgotten.

He had never found a chest.

And now a bush pilot who didn't know what she was looking at had winched one out of the water in an afternoon, because the river, in its infinite indifference, had decided to give it to someone else.

He told himself again that it was none of his business. He told himself that a man who had buried the truth once, with his own hands and his own pen, had forfeited any claim to dig it back up. He told himself all of this standing in the reeds with mud to his knees, already walking toward the fuel dock at Pell's Camp, because he had known for ten years exactly where the Ashgrove woman bought her fuel, and he had known it for the same reason he knew everything: in case the day ever came when knowing it mattered.

The river kept what it was given. But sometimes, if you stood downstream long enough, it gave things back.

* * *

Pell's Camp smelled of diesel, fish guts, and the particular despair of men who had chosen a life and then resented it daily. The fuel dock jutted out over brown water on pilings furred with moss, and Cyrus took his time walking it, because the walk was the last moment in which he could still be a man carrying nothing.

He rehearsed it as he went. *Evening. Bad weather coming. Heard your engine running rough—left cylinder, probably fouled plug, I used to wrench in Rennick.* True, all of it, or true enough. A man could offer mechanical help without offering anything else. A man could be a neighbor. Neighbors were the last safe species left in the interior.

Then he saw her, and the rehearsal fell out of his head.

Noor Ashgrove was crouched on the dock beside a fuel drum, and she was not pumping fuel. She was sitting very still with her forearms on her knees, looking at nothing, in the posture of a person who has recently seen something she cannot yet decide how to carry. Her flight jacket was soaked to the shoulder. Her hair was dark with river spray. Beside her, wrapped in a tarp and lashed with a cargo strap, sat a shape about the size of a sleeping dog, and every few seconds her eyes flicked to it and away, the way you check a wound.

Cyrus had spent a decade reading documents. He had learned, in the archive halls of the capital, to read the silences between entries, the ink that stopped mid-sentence, the file numbers that skipped from 447 to 449 as though a whole year had never happened. He had thought of himself, in his more honest moments, as a man who read water—the slow currents beneath official language, the eddies where things sank and circled and waited.

He read Noor Ashgrove in four seconds, and what he read was: *she knows.*

Not what it was. Not yet. But she'd opened enough of it to know it was heavy, and she was sitting on a fuel dock deciding whether to carry it or put it back in the river, and Cyrus understood—with the terrible clarity of a man recognizing his own face in a stranger—that she was losing that argument. She was going to carry it. People who doubled back through storm weather to winch a chest out of a flood were people who carried things. That was the whole disease.

"Evening," he said.

She came up off her heels fast, and there was a knife in her hand before he'd finished the word—not flourished, just *present*, held low along her thigh the way interior women hold knives, which is to say the way other people hold opinions. Her eyes did a full circuit of him: boots, coat, hands, face, the gap between him and the shore, the gap between him and the tarp.

"You're not the dock boy."

"No. Dock boy's drunk behind the net shed. It's Tuesday." He kept his hands visible and empty. "I'm camped down the bank. Fishing."

"Fishing." She said it the way a customs officer says *souvenirs*. "In this?"

"The fish don't mind the rain. It's the planes they hate." He nodded at the sky, at her plane tied down at the far end of the dock, still ticking as the engine cooled. "Left cylinder's running rich. You're fouling a plug, or you've got water in the fuel and I'd drain the sump before you fly again."

Something shifted in her face—not softening, exactly. Recalibration. A pilot hearing another pilot's diagnosis and hating that it sounded right.

"I'll manage."

"I'm sure." He let the silence sit. This was the part he'd rehearsed, and now that he was in it, he threw the script away, because he could see the script wouldn't work. She wasn't a woman you approached sideways. She was a woman you approached the way you'd approach a downed wire: directly, slowly, having already accepted you might die. "My name is Cyrus Vane."

"I didn't ask."

"No. But you're going to want to know it in about a minute, so I'm saving us both the trouble."

The knife hadn't moved. "That's a strange sentence, Mr. Vane."

"It gets stranger." He looked at the tarp. Just looked at it—one long, unhidden look, the first honest thing he'd offered anyone in ten years—and then back at her face. "That came out of the bend below the old Kestrian ferry crossing. Flooded stretch, east channel, where the current makes the slow eddy past the drowned church foundation."

Her stillness changed quality. Before, she'd been still the way prey is still. Now she was still the way a drawn bow is still.

"How would you know that."

"Because I've been dragging that bend for nine years." He said it quietly. The dock boy was drunk, the nearest boat was a quarter mile off, the rain was starting again, fine and needling, and the river itself made a noise like a crowd whispering. "Because I know what the flood of '04 took off the archive barges, and I know what the government said was lost—which was everything, conveniently, all of it, a whole nation's records destroyed beyond recovery, isn't that remarkable—and I have spent a decade of my life kneeling in that mud looking for what actually survived. And today the river gave it to you."

"Mr. Vane—"

"Cyrus."

"*Mr. Vane.*" The knife finally moved—not toward him, but away, tucked somewhere in the wet jacket, which he chose to read as progress. She straightened to her full height, and he saw that she was taller than he'd guessed and tired in a way that went deeper than the day. "I don't know you. You walked out of the reeds smelling like fish and knowing things you shouldn't, and you think I'm going to open a sealed box for a stranger because he gave me free advice about a spark plug?"

"Yes," Cyrus said.

She stared at him. Rain ticked on the tarp. Somewhere across the water a loon made its sound, that laugh that isn't laughter.

"Why?" she said finally. Not *why should I*. Why do you care. Why does a stranger kneel in mud for nine years. Why does a man's voice shake slightly on the word *Kestrian* even though Kestria hasn't appeared on a map in twenty years and saying the name out loud is the kind of thing that gets inspectors knocking on your door.

And Cyrus Vane, who had lied to tribunals, lied to colleagues, lied most thoroughly and unforgivably to himself, found that he could not lie to this woman on a wet dock, mostly because she was looking at him the way an archivist looks at a document with a forged signature—already seeing through it, already grieving it.

"Because I helped bury it once," he said. "The truth. What happened to Kestria. What happened to—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "There's a village called Merrow's Bend. There was. In 1994 it stopped existing, and the record says it never did, and I am one of the reasons the record says that. I've been trying to take it back ever since. That's why I fish in the rain. That's why I know your fuel is bad. That's why I'm standing here in front of a woman holding a knife, begging."

"Begging," she repeated. Flat. Testing the weight of it.

"Begging," he confirmed. "To see what the river gave you. That's all. Look at it yourself first—I'm not asking you to trust me, I'm asking you to look, and then decide whether you want the company of the second-most-damned man on this river."

For a long moment she said nothing. The rain thickened. Down the dock, her plane shivered in a gust, and the tie-down ropes sang their one note.

Then Noor Ashgrove did something he hadn't expected. She laughed—a short, hard, exhausted sound with no humor in it anywhere, the laugh of a person who has just recognized something.

"You know what I was doing, five minutes before you walked up?" she said. "Sitting here trying to convince myself to throw it back. Put it in the river, fly home, pretend I never saw it. That was the plan. That was a *good* plan." She looked at the tarp, then at him, and her eyes had the particular hunger he'd seen once before, years ago, in a mirror, in a young archivist who still believed documents could save people. "And then some stranger crawls out of the reeds knowing the exact bend it came from, and I realize I was never going to throw it back. I was just waiting for permission."

"I'm not permission," Cyrus said. "I'm worse. I'm a reason."

"Yeah." She crouched by the tarp. Her hands went to the cargo strap and paused there, knuckles pale. "You should understand something, Vane. Whatever's in this box, I pulled it out of the water because it was drowning. That's the only reason. I fly medicine into places the government pretends are healthy. I mind my own business. My business has kept me alive for eleven years." The strap came loose, coil by coil. "So if you're about to hand me a reason to stop minding my business, you'd better be ready to live with what that costs. Because I can tell already—you're not a man who costs nothing."

"No," Cyrus agreed. "I'm really not."

She lifted the tarp.

The chest sat in the gray light, river-dark, its brass fittings green with age, and across its lid, half worn away but unmistakable to a man who had spent his life among them, the stamped seal of a nation that officially did not exist: the compass rose and crossed keys of Kestria.

Cyrus's breath went out of him like he'd been struck. Twenty years of searching, nine years of mud, ten years of shame—and there it was, sitting on a fuel dock between a drunk dock boy and a loon, dripping.

Noor watched his face while he looked at it. He felt her watching. When he finally raised his eyes, hers were narrow and bright and afraid in exactly the way his must have been.

"All right, Cyrus Vane," she said quietly. "Tell me what I pulled out of my river."

He knelt beside the chest, and his hands—steady through tribunals, steady through disgrace, steady through a decade of deliberate smallness—were shaking as he reached for the latch.

"They're orders," he said. "If I'm right, they're the orders. And we need to open it somewhere that isn't a dock."

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, a boat with a government-gray hull slid past the far bank, moving slowly, moving upstream, its engines idling like a held breath.
