## 4. Merrow's Bend, 1994

**Noor**

The chest sat open on the table between them like a wound.

Noor had pried it apart at midnight with a flathead screwdriver and a patience she didn't feel, and now the dawn was coming up gray through the shack's one window, and neither of them had slept. The documents lay in three careful stacks—Cyrus had insisted on the stacks, dry hands, turn pages by the corner—and Noor kept looking at them the way you look at something that has already changed the shape of your life.

She'd read them twice. She understood maybe half of what she was reading. The other half was a language of file numbers and routing codes, bureaucratic weather from a country that officially did not exist.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly."

Cyrus Vane stood at the window with his back to her, watching the tree line the way a man watches for dogs. He hadn't touched the papers in an hour. He didn't need to. She could tell he knew them by heart—or knew enough of them to be sick about it.

"The seal is Kestrian," he said. "Provincial archive, Merrow district. Kestria wasn't just erased from the maps, Ashgrove. It was erased from the *record*. Twenty years ago there was an administrative dissolution—treaty, annexation, whatever word they used so nobody had to say conquest. Every document bearing the old seal was supposed to be destroyed or absorbed into the territorial registry under new numbers." He turned. His face was hollowed out by lamplight and something older than lamplight. "This chest never got absorbed. Somebody hid it. Somebody put it in a strongbox and sank it or stashed it near the river, and the river kept it until the flood gave it back."

"And the orders?"

He came to the table. He picked up the top sheet of the middle stack with two fingers, the way you'd handle a snake you were trying not to provoke.

"Directive 44-K," he read. "'Regarding the settlement designated Merrow's Bend. In the interest of territorial security, the settlement is to be cleared of inhabitants and its structures razed. Resistance is anticipated. Discretion is authorized.'" He set it down. "Discretion is authorized. That's the phrase. That's the whole massacre in three words. It means: do what needs doing, and we will deny you ever existed."

Noor looked at the signature block. She'd looked at it a dozen times already, but she looked again, because some part of her kept hoping the letters would rearrange themselves into something survivable.

*Approved and countersigned, Office of the Territorial Governor — A. Crane.*

"Aldous Crane," she said. "The governor. *Our* governor. The man whose face is on the customs posters at every dock I've ever fueled at."

"The same."

"He signed this twenty years ago. Before he was governor."

"He was deputy administrator then. This is the order that made him governor." Cyrus's voice was flat, scraped clean of anything. "You don't get to erase a nation off the map without doing one thing first. You have to erase the people who were standing on it when the map changed. Merrow's Bend was the largest Kestrian village left inside the new border. Four hundred souls, give or take. Fishing, farming, a chapel, a school. My mother bought thread there once, when I was a boy. I remember the shopkeeper's hands."

Noor waited. He didn't go on.

"Cyrus." First time she'd used his name out loud. It landed on him visibly, like a hand on the shoulder. "What aren't you telling me?"

He laughed—a single dry note with no humor in it at all.

"What am I not telling you," he repeated. "Ashgrove, I'm not telling you most of my life. But the part that matters right now—" He pulled out the other chair. It groaned under him. He sat down across from her and put both hands flat on the table, and she saw that they weren't steady. For a man who'd handled those documents all night like a surgeon, his hands shook now like a drunk's.

"I was an archivist," he said. "Territorial Registry, capital division. Ten years ago. Junior grade, records integrity section. Do you know what records integrity means?"

"It means you check the paperwork."

"It means I checked the paperwork. And one day a transfer request crossed my desk—an absorption order, a batch of Kestrian files being renumbered into the territorial system. Routine. Except one file was flagged for destruction instead of absorption, and the flag was wrong. I could see it was wrong. The cross-reference pointed to a village incident report that no longer existed anywhere in the index. A hole. A shaped hole, cut deliberately, with a file number still floating where the file used to be." He swallowed. "The number was 44-K."

Noor felt the hair rise on her arms. Outside, the river muttered against its banks, swollen and brown, carrying whatever the monsoon chose to give it.

"You pulled the file," she said.

"I tried. The physical archive said the folder was checked out. Checked out by whom? Sealed authorization. So I did the thing you're never supposed to do." He looked at his hands. "I altered the record. I created a duplicate entry—a false absorption notice—routing 44-K into deep storage pending review. On paper, the file was safe. In reality, I bought myself seventy-two hours before anyone noticed the misfile. Seventy-two hours to get into deep storage and copy what was in that folder."

"And?"

"And I got in. And I read it. And then—" His voice failed on the hinge of the sentence. He started over, quieter. "And then I sat in that basement for six hours with a photocopier I was too frightened to use, and in the end I copied nothing. I restored the entry exactly as it had been. I erased my own false trail. I walked out, went home, and told myself that a junior archivist couldn't fight a governor, that I'd get myself disappeared and accomplish nothing, that living to fight another day was a strategy and not what it actually was."

"Which was?"

"Cowardice wearing a uniform." He said it without flinching, which somehow made it worse. "I reported a records anomaly to my supervisor—a sanitized version, nothing actionable—and I requested a transfer to the provincial annexes, far from the capital, where a man with a conscience and no spine could do less damage. I've been drifting downstream ever since. Ten years. Fishing camps, seasonal work, small towns with bad memories. And everywhere I went, I asked questions I pretended were casual. Because I couldn't let it go, and I couldn't pick it up. Both hands full of nothing."

The lamp between them hissed and guttered. Noor got up, turned the wick down, and stood there a moment with her back to him, looking at the chest. Its brass fittings had gone green-black under the water; the wood had swelled and split along one seam. Inside, wedged against the lining, she'd found a small object she hadn't shown him yet—a compass, heavy, Kestrian-stamped, its glass fogged. She didn't know why she was keeping it back. Maybe because everything else in that chest was death, and this was something a person had carried.

"Why tell me this?" she asked, still facing the chest. "You could have lied. I'd have believed you. I barely know you."

"Because you pulled that chest out of the river against your better judgment, and you haven't thrown it back." Now his voice had something in it—not warmth exactly. Gravity. "Because whoever hid it died for it or killed for it, and you're in it now whether either of us likes it. And because if we're going to do what I think you're deciding to do, you need to know exactly who you're partnered with. A man who found the truth once and buried it. That's who I am, Ashgrove. I want you to decide with your eyes open."

She turned around. He was watching her with the terrible patience of a man at sentencing.

"Here's what I know about you," she said. "You identified my plane by its engine sound from a quarter mile away in the rain. You've spent ten years asking dangerous questions badly enough to stay alive but well enough to keep answers coming. And last night you organized those documents into stacks with dry hands and told me to touch them by the corner, because you were thinking about preservation while I was thinking about how fast we can run." She sat back down. "That's not a coward, Cyrus. That's a man who's been waiting ten years for someone else to find the chest so he wouldn't have to carry it alone."

His eyes closed. When they opened, they were wet, and he didn't apologize for it.

"The village," he said. "Merrow's Bend. Tell me what you know about it."

"Nothing. That's the point, isn't it? There's nothing to know. It's not on any chart. I fly medicine runs up that whole valley—" She stopped.

*Fly medicine runs.* Her own words snagged on something.

"What," Cyrus said.

"Three years ago. Flood season, like this one. I got a relay request—a ham operator upstream, garbled signal, asking for a medevac out of a settlement that wasn't on my charts. Coordinates in the old Kestrian valley. Dispatch denied the flight. Said the coordinates were invalid, no registered settlement, airspace restricted. I remember being angry about it for a week and then—I don't know. Letting it go. You let things go out here or the interior eats you." She stared at the table. "Those coordinates. They'd have been Merrow's Bend."

"There are survivors," Cyrus said softly. "There have to be. Children, in '94. People who ran. Directive says *cleared*, not annihilated—the euphemism only works if the bodies stay deniable. Which means somewhere there are people who remember it happening. Witnesses."

"Witnesses to a signed order from the man who now runs the territory." Noor leaned back. The chair creaked. "You understand what we're holding. This isn't evidence. This is a bomb with a twenty-year fuse that just finished burning."

"I understand exactly what we're holding." He reached across the stacks—carefully, by the corner—and tapped the signature page. "And I understand what it costs. If Crane learns this surfaced, we won't face inspectors and subpoenas, Ashgrove. We'll face men whose job is making sure no one ever testifies. I've seen what happens to people who ask my kind of questions. They stop asking."

"So we run to the capital. Your courts—"

"Our courts." A bitter twist. "One honest judge in that whole marble rot, if he's still alive and still honest. That's the play. That's always been the play. Get original documents, authenticated, into a courtroom, onto the public record, before anyone who matters can burn them. Once it's read aloud, it exists. They can kill us after, but they can't unread it."

Noor looked at the papers. At the neat, murderous handwriting of clerks. At the signature that had built a governor.

"My plane," she heard herself say, "is a forty-year-old bush frame held together with prayer and baling wire. My boat leaks. My airstrip is a gravel scar two hundred meters long. I have no money, no influence, and no family anybody could threaten." She met his eyes. "But I know every bend of these rivers better than the men who'll be hunting us, and I have never once been caught flying weather I shouldn't have survived."

Something moved through Cyrus Vane's face then—the first thing in ten hours that wasn't shame. It took her a moment to name it. Hope. Rusty, disbelieving, half-ashamed of itself, but hope.

"You'll do it," he said. "You'll help me take it to court."

"I'll help you take it to the truth." She stood, and gathered the stacks together with more confidence than she felt, and slid them back into the chest. "Ten years ago you had a chance to copy that file and you didn't. Fine. The river just handed you the file again. Rivers don't do that twice, Cyrus. Whatever you were—you're the man who gets it there this time. That's the only version of you I'm interested in."

He rose too. For a moment he just breathed, like a man surfacing.

Then, outside, down the valley toward the fuel dock, an engine. Not hers. Not a fisherman's putt-putt. A heavy, disciplined four-stroke, moving slow, the way government boats move when they want to seem casual.

Cyrus went rigid. Noor was already at the window, flattening against the wall beside it, one eye to the gap in the boards.

A gray hull slid around the bend below the shack. Trim, official, unmarked except for a stenciled number. At the bow stood a man in a pressed field jacket, unhurried, scanning the bank with binoculars—and the boat slowed as it passed her dock, and held there, idling, in the gray morning light.

"Noor Ashgrove," Cyrus whispered. "Is that boat here for you?"

She watched the man in the field jacket lower the binoculars and look directly at her shack—at the window, at the thin line of chimney smoke she'd forgotten to kill.

"No," she said quietly. "But it's about to be."
