## 5. The Salvage Inspector

The plane came in low over the water at half past nine, and Noor knew before she saw the markings that it wasn't hers to greet.

She was elbow-deep in the Cessna's cowling, third day of chasing an oil leak that had opinions of its own, when the sound reached her across the flats—a twin-engine government bird, the kind with a paint job too clean for the interior and a radio that talked to people she'd spent ten years not talking to. It circled her strip once. Twice. Then it settled on the water a hundred meters off her dock with the unhurried confidence of something that had never been told no.

Noor wiped her hands on a rag and did not hurry.

That was the first rule of invisibility: never look like you're waiting. Waiting meant expecting. Expecting meant you had something worth expecting. She pulled the rag through her fingers slowly, watched the floatplane's door open, and let the silence stretch until it got uncomfortable for whoever was inside it.

The man who climbed down onto her dock was not what she expected, which annoyed her, because she had prided herself on expecting everything.

He was younger than his posture. Grey suit, grey hat, grey eyes that took inventory of her dock, her shack, her plane, and filed each item somewhere behind a smile that looked like it had been issued with the uniform. He carried a leather folder under one arm and nothing else—no weapon visible, which in the interior meant either very good or very bad.

"Ms. Ashgrove." He said her name like he'd practiced it. "Inspector Hale. Territorial Salvage Authority."

"We don't get many of those out here," Noor said.

"No," Hale agreed pleasantly. "You don't. That's rather the point of you."

* * *

He declined coffee, accepted tea, and sat in the one chair in her shack as though he'd measured it first. Noor stayed standing by the stove, which gave her the height advantage and kept the cast-iron pan within reach. The chest sat three feet beneath the floorboards under the cot, wrapped in oilcloth, and she made herself think about the leaky cowling instead—the valve cover gasket, the torque sequence, anything but the weight pressing up through the planks.

"Recovered property of historical value," Hale said, opening the folder. "That's the category we're working with. Items of cultural or archival significance surfacing after the floods. The river's been generous this year."

"The river's been a bastard this year," Noor said. "Generous isn't the word I'd use."

Hale smiled as if she'd said something charming. "Three weeks ago a ferry went down at Corben's Reach. Two weeks ago, a logging camp washed out upstream. Debris fields all along the bend country. And in that debris—" he turned a page without looking at it, "—occasionally, items. Documents. Sealed containers. Things that were lost a long time ago and are, technically, state property."

"Technically," Noor said.

"You've flown that stretch. The lower bends, past Merrow's Bend."

Her hands did not shake. She'd trained them not to, years ago, over a village she'd circled twice and never landed at because the manifest said medicine, not questions, and the tower had told her the strip was closed. She thought about the valve cover gasket again.

"I fly where the work is," she said. "Merrow's Bend hasn't been anywhere since before I started."

"No," Hale said softly. "It hasn't. Has it."

He let that sit. Noor poured her own tea and drank it and gave him nothing, and understood, with a cold settling feeling, that he was letting *her* sit too—that every silence in this room was a tool and he owned the whole set.

"Here's my problem, Ms. Ashgrove." He closed the folder. "A source downstream says a bush pilot winched something out of the flooded bend two nights ago. A chest. Brass fittings, sealed, heavy. The description was specific enough that I don't have to ask whether you know what I'm talking about. I only have to ask what you did with it."

"That's not a question, then," Noor said. "That's an accusation with paperwork."

"It's an inquiry with paperwork. There's a difference, and the difference is what happens next." He crossed one ankle over his knee, entirely at ease, a man visiting. "If you recovered nothing, you say so, and I write down that you said so, and I leave, and we're both done. If you recovered something and surrendered it, you're owed a finder's fee—I'll see to it personally. And if you recovered something and didn't surrender it..." He spread his hands, apologetic. "Then you're holding state property, Ms. Ashgrove. And the penalty structure for that is not designed with your circumstances in mind."

"And what are my circumstances?"

"Alone," Hale said. "Unlicensed for charter beyond the interior registry. Airstrip built without final survey approval—there's a discrepancy in your land filing from six years back, did you know? Small thing. Fixable. Everything's fixable." He stood, brushed nothing off his trousers, and set a card on her table. "I'm staying at the fuel dock landing until tomorrow evening. If anything occurs to you—anything surfaces, so to speak—you can reach me there."

At the door he paused, and for just a moment the courtesy thinned and she saw the machinery underneath, running smooth and fast.

"One more question. Off the record." He didn't turn all the way around. "The chest, if it existed. Would it have been locked?"

"Why would that matter?"

"Because the ones that matter are always locked," Hale said. "And the ones that are always locked are always opened. Good day, Ms. Ashgrove."

* * *

She watched his floatplane lift off and bank south toward the fuel dock, and only then did she let herself breathe, and hated the breath for how ragged it came.

The lie had cost her nothing. That was the part that frightened her. He'd asked, and she'd said *nothing*, and it had come out level and easy and almost bored, and somewhere in the last ten years of flying under registries and skirting surveys and keeping her name small, she had become fluent in exactly the language men like Hale spoke. She'd built her whole life into a shield, and now she could feel it working, and working felt like rotting.

Under the floorboards, the chest waited. On top of it, in her handwriting, a list she'd made at three in the morning: *orders, signatures, Merrow's Bend.* Names of the dead in a margin. A seal she'd only ever seen on the backs of erased maps.

Invisibility had kept her alive. Invisibility had kept her fed, licensed enough, left alone enough. But Hale hadn't come out here on a rumor. He'd come because someone downstream had seen her winch, and someone upstream had decided within days what that sight was worth—and that meant the shield had a hole in it shaped exactly like a brass-fitted chest, and no amount of being nobody was going to close it.

She knelt, pried up the boards, and put her hand flat on the oilcloth.

Tomorrow evening, he'd said. Which meant tonight, someone at the fuel dock landing would be counting her fuel drums, timing her generator hours, watching for a boat launch in the dark.

Which meant she couldn't wait for dark.

Noor looked at the card on her table—*E. HALE, TERRITORIAL SALVAGE AUTHORITY*, and beneath it, in smaller print, a direct line—and understood that the life she'd built had just stopped being a place and started being a countdown.

She was reaching for the floorboards to close them when she heard the engine.

Not a floatplane. A boat, coming up the slough from downstream, running quiet against the current with the particular care of someone who knew these waters and knew they were being watched. Through the window she saw a skiff nose out of the reeds, and standing in it, soaked to the waist and holding a pole like a man who'd rather have a weapon, a stranger with river-mud to his knees and eyes that went straight past her to the fresh-scraped boards of her floor.

"You're Ashgrove," he called up. Not a question either. Everyone, today, already knowing her name. "My name is Cyrus Vane. And I think we need to talk about what the river gave you."
