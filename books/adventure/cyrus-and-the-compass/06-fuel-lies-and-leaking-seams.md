## 6. Fuel, Lies, and Leaking Seams

The boat was called *Kestrel*, which Cyrus decided was either a joke or an omen, and Noor Ashgrove did not seem inclined to say which.

She'd backed it out of the reeds behind her airstrip shack at first light—a flat-bottomed river boat older than either of them, its paint gone the gray of driftwood, its seams weeping a thin amber line of sap-like sealant that had never quite cured. It sat in the shallows like something the river had already tried to swallow once and been talked out of.

"That's what we're taking?" Cyrus asked.

"That's what there is." She was already kneeling in the bilge, sleeves shoved up, running her thumb along the port-side seam the way a doctor palpates a wound. "My plane's grounded until this weather breaks, and even if it weren't, Hale will be watching every airstrip in the territory by tonight. A man with a warrant checks the sky. He doesn't check the water."

"He'll check the water eventually."

"Then we'd better be fast." She looked up at him, and in the flat morning light he saw how little sleep she'd gotten—how much of it had been spent listening. "You've done boat work?"

"I've done archive work."

"Congratulations. You're doing boat work." She tossed him a roll of tarred canvas and a hammer. "Start at the transom and lie about your confidence while you do it. It helps."

* * *

They worked through the morning while the monsoon stacked itself on the horizon like a debt coming due.

Cyrus learned the boat the way he learned everything—by cataloguing its failures. The *Kestrel* had a two-stroke engine that Noor coaxed into life with a prayer and a rag soaked in something that smelled illegal. Its fuel tank was a repurposed drum lashed under the stern bench, and its ballast was a shallow tray of river stones beneath the floorboards, there to keep the hull honest in crosswind. Everything about it was improvised, repaired, held together by habit. It was, he realized, not so different from the fishing camp he'd lived in for three years, or from himself.

"Lower," Noor said, without looking up from the carburetor she'd scattered across a plank like surgery.

He lowered his voice. "I said nothing."

"You were about to say something about the engine."

"I was going to say it sounds like a dying animal."

"It is a dying animal. But it's *my* dying animal, and it has never once left me on the water." She blew a fleck of grit off a jet, squinted through it against the light. "Which is more than I can say for most institutions."

He let that land. It was meant to.

By noon they had the seams patched—canvas and tar where the caulking had failed, a wooden sliver driven into a split near the bow that Noor called "the scar" and spoke to by name. Then came the part that mattered: the documents.

They'd agreed on ballast. It was the one place no inspector would look twice, because no inspector looks twice at stones. Noor pried up the floorboards over the weight tray, and Cyrus knelt beside her with the chest's contents wrapped in oilcloth—the orders, the manifests, the casualty ledger, the page bearing Aldous Crane's signature in ink that had outlived everyone it condemned.

Twenty years he'd chased these papers. Now he was wrapping them like a fish for market, and his hands wouldn't stop checking the folds.

"You're doing it again," Noor said.

"Doing what."

"Treating them like they're made of glass. They're paper, Vane. They've survived a flood and twenty years of being hidden. They can survive a boat ride if you stop strangling them."

"They're the only copy."

"They're the only copy *we know of*." She took the bundle from him, and her hands—steady as a surgeon's all morning—did something he hadn't seen before. A tremor. Small, fast, gone almost before it started, like a bird crossing a window. She tucked the oilcloth between two layers of stones, weighted it, and laid the boards back down as if tucking in a child.

Cyrus watched her hands settle flat on the deck planking. Pressed there. Holding the boat still, or holding herself.

"Noor."

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything yet."

"You were going to ask why my hands shake." She stood, wiped her palms on her thighs, and busied herself coiling a rope that didn't need coiling. "So don't."

* * *

They ate standing up—hard bread, smoked fish, river water boiled in a dented kettle—and Cyrus told her about the run to the capital because planning was easier than silence. Three days downriver if the weather held, five if it didn't. Two checkpoints he knew of, both bribable, neither bribeable by people who looked like fugitives. Judge Ansel Rook's name spoken only once, quietly, like a password you didn't want the walls to learn.

"And after Rook?" she asked.

"After Rook, it stops being ours. That's the point. We hand it to someone whose job is to make it loud."

"You trust him?"

"I trusted Crane once too." He said it plainly, because hiding it would have cost more. "But Rook is the last judge in this territory who hasn't taken Crane's money, and I checked. For ten years, checking was the only thing I was good at."

Noor chewed her bread and looked out at the river, brown and swollen and moving with the patience of something that always wins. When she spoke again, her voice had changed register—lower, more careful, the voice of someone stepping onto ice.

"There's a village upstream of here. Merrow's Bend."

Cyrus went very still.

"I flew medicine runs along that stretch for six years. Vaccines, insulin, antibiotics—whatever the interior clinics couldn't get. And there was a village on the maps, right there on the bend—" she drew it in the air with two fingers, "—and the clinic listed there kept ordering supplies. Regular as clockwork. Someone signed for every delivery. I flew the route maybe forty times." Her fingers curled closed. "I never landed. The strip was marked closed on my charts—flood damage, the notation said. So I'd drop the crates by chute and turn around, and some weeks I'd look down and see smoke from cookfires and kids on the bank waving at the plane, and I'd think, good, they're fine, whoever's signing for the medicine is getting it to them."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. The river said nothing.

"And then?" Cyrus asked, though he knew. God help him, he knew exactly.

"Then one year the orders stopped. And the next time I flew over, there was no village. Just foundations. Burn-black on the stone like a shadow." She set down the bread she wasn't eating. "I told myself flood. I told myself relocation. I told myself every story that let me keep flying, because the alternative was that I'd carried medicine to four hundred people for six years and never once touched the ground they stood on. Never landed. Never learned their names. I flew over the worst thing that ever happened to them and logged it as weather."

Her hands were shaking now. Not the small tremor from before—both of them, openly, and she stared at them as if they belonged to a stranger who'd wandered into her story.

"So when I opened that chest," she said, "and saw the name—"

"Noor."

"—it wasn't shock. That's the thing I can't get past. It was *recognition*. Like finding out the noise you've heard your whole life was a scream."

Cyrus crossed the space between them before he decided to. He didn't take her hands—that felt like claiming something she hadn't offered—but he put his own palm flat on the deck beside hers, the same gesture, the same pressure, anchoring the same boat.

"I falsified an archive entry," he said. "Ten years ago. One line. I moved a file from 'pending review' to 'destroyed, routine.' It took me eleven seconds. Eleven seconds, and then I went home and slept, because Crane had taught me that order was a kind of mercy and I wanted so badly to believe my mentor was merciful." His throat worked. "You never landed at Merrow's Bend. I never even flew over it. I sat in a room with the truth and filed it into a drawer. Whatever you're ashamed of, I built mine a shelf."

She turned her head and looked at him—really looked, the assessment she'd been running since the fuel dock finally surfacing somewhere new.

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because your hands shake and mine don't, and I've been trying to figure out which of us is broken correctly." He almost smiled. "I think it might be you. I think shaking is what honesty does to hands that haven't practiced lying."

That earned him something—not a smile, but the loosening around her eyes that preceded one, in people who'd forgotten the full motion.

"The documents are under the floorboards," she said. "In the ballast. With the stones."

"With the stones."

"Good." She exhaled, long and slow, and picked up her bread. "Because tomorrow we're going to need every ounce of weight sitting low and honest, and I needed to know you'd guard stones like they were scripture."

"I would."

"I know. That's the problem with you, Vane." She bit into the bread. "You're sincere in a way that gets people killed."

* * *

They worked until dark. Fuel drums lashed and double-lashed; the decoy chest built from scrap lumber and filled with river rocks and worthless ledgers, ready to be left behind as an offering; the real compass from the old chest sewn into the lining of Noor's jacket, because some things you keep on your body. Cyrus memorized the manifest pages until he could recite dates and signature lines with his eyes shut—if the river took the papers, it would have to take him reciting them.

At dusk Noor tested the engine. It caught on the third pull, coughed, steadied into its dying-animal rhythm, and she laughed—one short, surprised note, as if the sound had escaped custody.

"She'll do," she said.

"She'll do," Cyrus agreed, and found that he believed it, and found further that believing it felt dangerously like hope.

It was full dark when he noticed the light.

Not on the water—on the ridge road above the shack, where the track climbed away toward the highway. A vehicle, parked, engine off, headlights dead. But its parking lamps glowed faint amber, the way men leave them when they want to see their map and be seen by no one.

"Noor." He said it very quietly.

She followed his eyes. Went still in the way he was learning meant danger rather than calm.

"Hale doesn't use parking lamps," she murmured. "Hale drives like he owns the road."

"Then who—"

"Someone waiting for daylight. Or waiting for us to load up." She reached over and killed the lantern between them, and the darkness came down like a lid. In the black, her voice stayed level, and her hand found his wrist and gripped it—steady now, steady as cable. "Get the documents' location fixed in your head. Tomorrow before dawn, we're gone ahead of the rain."

Below them the river ran on, carrying everything given to it, keeping what it kept.

Above them, on the ridge, the amber lights went out.
