## 7. Departure Before Dawn

The rain came first as a rumor.

Noor stood in the doorway of her shack at four in the morning and listened to it arrive over the ridge line—a sound like a freight train that never got closer, just kept getting bigger. The monsoon's first wall. She'd flown ahead of that wall a dozen times in her life, outrunning it for pay, laughing about it afterward over cheap rum with men who thought she was reckless and women who knew better.

Tonight she wasn't laughing. Tonight she was leaving everything she owned behind her, except what fit in a boat.

Behind her, inside the shack, Cyrus Vane sat at her table under the single yellow bulb, copying pages by hand. He'd been at it for six hours. His pen scratched and stopped, scratched and stopped, and every time it stopped she felt the silence press against the back of her neck like a hand.

"You don't have to do this part," she said, not turning around. "We have the originals."

"The originals go in the ballast," he said. "The ballast goes in the river if we're boarded. Copies are what survive." He said it flatly, like a man reciting a catechism he'd learned the hard way. "I've had one copy of something before, Noor. Once. I watched it burn in a filing cabinet while two men held my arms."

She turned then. He didn't look up from his work. In the lamplight his face had the gray, finished look of someone who had already said goodbye to himself and was just waiting for the rest of the world to catch up.

"Then let's make sure you get to watch these burn instead," she said. "On purpose. In Hale's face. Someday."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if a man still kept one there. "Someday," he agreed, and went back to writing.

* * *

The decoy was her idea, and she hated how good an idea it was.

Hale would come to the shack—she was certain of it. The man had stood on her fuel dock three days ago with his government coat and his courteous questions, asking about recovered property of historical value, and every question had landed exactly where a question shouldn't. He knew the chest existed. He probably knew she had it. What he didn't know was whether she understood what she had, and so he was being patient, the way patient men are when patience is cheaper than mistakes.

But patience ran out when the monsoon arrived. Rivers rose. Boats moved. A man like Hale would want to search the shack while its owner was visibly still living in it—and after that, all bets drowned.

So: leave the shack looking lived-in. Leave a fire going. Leave, in the cold stove ash where a careful searcher would look, the charred remains of paper that could plausibly be the real thing—Cyrus's copies of the copies, aged with tea water and burned unevenly, curled into black scrolls that might, in bad light, in the aftermath of a hasty fire, be read as evidence destroyed.

Let Hale find ashes. Let him report that the pilot burned them rather than surrender them. Let Crane rage at a ghost.

It would buy them hours. Maybe a day. Maybe nothing at all.

"It's a good lie," Cyrus had said when she explained it, and then, quieter: "You lie well. That worries me more than Hale does."

"I know," she'd said. "Me too."

* * *

They worked through the last dark hour in a silence broken only by rain beginning to tick against the tin roof—first scattered, like fingers testing a drum, then steady, then everywhere.

Noor packed the boat by feel and habit. Her river boat was thirty years old, cedar-planked, patched twice with canvas and once with sheer stubbornness, and she knew every seam of it the way other people knew their own hands. She pulled up three floorboards amidships, laid the oilcloth-wrapped documents in the bilge space beneath, weighted them with the tool roll, and nailed the boards back down. Then, because a nailed floorboard is its own confession, she dragged a bait crate and a coil of net over the spot and spilled fish guts near it, because no inspector alive searches a pile of fish guts twice.

The compass went in her jacket pocket. She hadn't told Cyrus she was carrying it. It wasn't a decision she'd made so much as one her hand had made without consulting her—the brass case warm against her ribs, its needle swinging lazily toward a village that no longer existed. Kestrian-made. Pointing at Merrow's Bend. She'd held it up to the lamp the night they opened the chest and watched the needle ignore north entirely, and something in her chest had gone tight and strange, like hearing your own name spoken in a dead woman's voice.

She flew by instruments and instinct. But she understood, holding that compass, why people used to believe objects could be haunted. Some things remembered harder than people did.

"You're taking it," Cyrus said from the doorway. Not a question. He'd seen her hand go to her pocket.

"It points somewhere true," she said. "Everything else I own points somewhere convenient."

He looked at her for a long moment in the lamplight, and whatever he saw made him nod slowly, like a man confirming a suspicion he'd been nursing since the fuel dock. "My mother had a saying," he said. "A map tells you where you are. A compass only tells you what you can't give up."

"Your mother sounds like she'd have liked flying."

"She'd have hated it. That's why she'd have been good at it." He shouldered his bag—one bag, ten years of a life reduced to one bag, and he carried it like a man who'd done the arithmetic long ago. "Ready?"

Noor looked around her shack one last time.

It wasn't much. She'd built the table herself from packing crates. The cot in the corner still held the shape of her body. On the wall above the stove hung the things that couldn't come: the mounted pike she'd caught the year she soloed, the photograph of her mother in front of a plane older than both of them, the tin cup with the chipped rim she'd drunk coffee from every morning for eleven years. The medicine run schedule pinned by the door—Tuesdays, Lower Marsh; Fridays, the fishing camps—with her own handwriting fading in the damp.

Eleven years she'd spent becoming invisible out here. Eleven years learning which clients paid late and which rivers lied about their depth, building a life so small and so quiet that no one in the capital ever bothered to look down at it. And now she was about to walk away from it with a bag and a boat and a dead nation's papers sewn into her floorboards, because a flooded river bend had given her a gift, and the wilderness had taught her the truth about gifts: they are never free, but sometimes they are worth the price.

The price, it turned out, was everything. She found, examining herself honestly in the last of the lamplight, that she could pay it. That surprised her. That frightened her more.

"Leave the lamp burning," she said. "And the stove. A lived-in shack burns its trash at night. An abandoned one goes dark."

Cyrus doused nothing. They stepped out into the rain.

* * *

The river had come up eight inches since midnight and was coming faster.

Noor heard it before she saw it—the changed voice of the current under the dock, deeper, hungrier, the sound of a river remembering it was an ocean once and meant to be again. Rain fell in ropes now, straight down, hammering the water flat, and out past the bend the sky flickered with heat lightning, silent, like a war happening in another country.

She got the boat moving with the engine at half throttle, running without lights, reading the channel by memory and by the white noise of rain on water. Behind them the shack shrank to a yellow smudge in the rain, lamp glowing, stove smoking, a stage set dressed for an audience of one courteous murderer.

Cyrus crouched low in the bow, watching the banks slide past. "How far to the capital by water?"

"Three days if the river behaves. Four if it doesn't." She eased around a snag that hadn't been there yesterday—the flood was rearranging the world nightly now, moving trees like furniture. "It won't behave."

"And Hale?"

"Hale thinks I'm asleep in that shack with a fire going and a guilty conscience." She risked a glance back. "By the time he figures out the ashes are theater, we'll be past Wrenfall."

"He won't take hours to figure it out. He'll take minutes." Cyrus's voice was calm in the way of a man reporting weather. "Men like Hale aren't slow, Noor. They're thorough. There's a difference, and the difference kills people."

She didn't answer, because the bend was coming up, the long sweep of it where the river turned south toward the marsh country, and she needed her eyes and both hands.

She saw the boat at the same instant Cyrus went rigid in the bow.

A hundred meters back upriver, tucked into the shadow of the cut bank where the current slowed—running lights off, engine idling, a shape at the wheel that even through rain and darkness she recognized by its stillness. Inspector Hale sat in his borrowed boat the way he'd stood on her dock: patiently, precisely, a man who had never once in his life been surprised because he'd always arrived early.

He'd never gone to the shack at all. Or he'd gone, and left, and doubled back. Either way, he was between them and the open river—or he had been, until her bow swung wide around the bend and the current took them.

For three full seconds nobody moved. Rain hammered the water between the boats. Through it Noor could see Hale clearly now, see him raise one hand—not a weapon, just the hand, palm out, almost friendly—and she understood with a lurch that he wasn't trying to stop them. He was counting them. Confirming cargo. A man checking a box on a form before the real work began downstream.

"Go," Cyrus breathed. "Go, go—"

She opened the throttle.

The old engine roared, coughed, caught—the sound of it tearing up the river like a scream she'd been holding for three days—and the bank, the bend, the idling boat, the raised hand, all of it whipped away behind them into the rain. Spray came over the gunwale in sheets. Ahead the channel unspooled gray and blind, and Noor flew it the way she flew weather: by feel, by faith, by the animal certainty that stopping was the only thing that could kill her.

Exhilaration hit her first—pure, electric, the old joy of speed and storm and a machine answering her hands—and then grief arrived right behind it, riding the same wind, because she knew what that shack behind them was now. Not a home. A prop. She'd lit the lamp herself. She'd fed the stove herself. She'd spent the last hour of her old life arranging it to be found empty and guilty, and some part of her had known, even as she struck the match over the decoy papers, that she was rehearsing.

Burning the life she built. Doing it gently, in advance, so the real burning wouldn't hurt as much.

"How long before he reports our heading?" Cyrus shouted over the engine.

"Depends how fast he can reach a radio." She squinted into the rain. "There's a relay tower at Wrenfall. If he's wired in with the garrison there, he could have boats behind us inside two hours."

"Then we don't stop at Wrenfall."

"We don't stop anywhere." She said it and heard, underneath the words, the thing she wasn't saying: *we don't stop until Crane falls or we do.* The river took the sentence and carried it south.

Lightning walked across the sky ahead of them, and this time it wasn't silent. Thunder rolled down the valley like a door slamming on the whole world behind them, and the first true wall of the monsoon hit the river all at once—a gray curtain marching toward them across the water, erasing the far bank, erasing the sky, erasing everything except the next fifty meters of channel and the two of them in it.

Noor grinned into the rain. She couldn't help it. It was terror and freedom wearing each other's clothes.

"Hold on, archivist," she shouted. "It gets worse from here."

Behind them, faint under the thunder, she thought she heard another engine wake up.

She didn't look back.
