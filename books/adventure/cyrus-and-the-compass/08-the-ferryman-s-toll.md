## 8. The Ferryman's Toll

The crossing at Tallow Reach was not on any map worth trusting, which was precisely why it existed. Cyrus spotted the signal first—a lantern hung from a dead cypress, swinging green, then dark, then green again—and touched Noor's arm where it rested on the tiller.

"There."

She cut the engine and let the current carry them in, and Cyrus watched her do it: the small economy of her movements, hands reading the water the way he had once read ledgers, feeling for the lie in a document by the weight of its paper. She'd been doing this all night—running the boat through channels that shouldn't have held water, threading between drowned fences and the tops of orchard trees, while the monsoon built itself somewhere behind them like a debt coming due.

"You trust this?" he asked quietly.

"I trust Marta." A pause. "I don't trust anyone else on this river, so let's hope she's alone."

She wasn't alone. Two men stood on the mud landing with poles, and behind them, half-hidden in the willow screen, sat a flat-bottomed ferry wide enough for the boat itself—a barge, really, with a winch and a canvas awning gone gray with salt. And standing at the barge's rail, one hand braced on a post worn smooth as bone, was a woman who could only be Marta Voss.

She was smaller than Cyrus had imagined, though he hadn't known he'd been imagining her until that moment. Seventy, maybe older, with a face like driftwood and eyes that did all the work her body no longer bothered with. She looked at Noor for a long time before she spoke.

"You fly like your mother," Marta said. "Loud. In a hurry. Certain the sky owes you something."

Noor's breath caught—Cyrus heard it, a small involuntary sound, the first unguarded thing she'd made since the fuel dock. "You knew her."

"I flew with her when she still thought she'd die old." Marta turned away from the rail. "Bring the boat up. We cross before the light does. The Reach doesn't hold after dawn—not for people carrying what you're carrying."

Cyrus went very still. "What do you mean, what we're—"

"Boy." She didn't look back at him. "You've been running since the bend like two people who found a body. You think an old woman can't smell it? Bring the boat."

* * *

They loaded onto the barge in the gray half-light, Noor's boat winched aboard and lashed down, the chest transferred to a dry locker beneath the awning because Marta said so—"Water finds paper. It's the one honest thing about it." The two polemen took their stations without a word. The crossing itself was a mile of brown flood, the far bank invisible, and Marta steered from a wheel that predated every government currently claiming this territory, humming something low and tuneless under her breath.

It was Cyrus who broke first. He couldn't help it; he never could. Curiosity was the flaw that had ended his career, his name, nearly his life, and here it was again, leaning toward an old smuggler on a flooded river.

"You said you flew with her mother. When?"

Marta's hands didn't move on the wheel. "Before you were trouble, archivist. I know what you are—I've hauled enough of your kind across this water to know the posture. You sit like the truth is a heavy bag you're tired of carrying." Her eyes flicked to him, then to Noor. "Her mother ran medicine and mail out of the interior when the interior still had names. Kestrian names. Before the maps got rewritten and everyone agreed to forget how to read them."

Noor had gone white. Cyrus saw her hand close around the rail until the knuckles stood out like knots in wood.

"My mother never talked about that time," she said.

"No. They never do. That was the whole point of it—the silence was the order. Not the burning. Burning is easy; villages burn themselves if you look away long enough. The order was the forgetting." Marta spat over the rail, into the flood. "Your mother carried Kestrian mail, Kestrian medicine, Kestrian children out of places that were about to stop existing. And when it was done, she came down off that plateau and never said one word about it for twenty years, and I loved her for it and hated it in equal measure, because silence keeps you alive and it kills you anyway, just slower."

The rain started then—not the monsoon proper but its outrider, a fine cold needle-rain that hissed against the canvas. Cyrus moved without deciding to, lifting the chest from the locker, setting it on the boards between them. He looked at Noor. She held his gaze for three seconds, weighing him, weighing the old woman, weighing the river. Then she nodded.

Marta set the brake on the wheel and knelt. Her knees cracked like rifle shots. She worked the brass locks with fingers that were surprisingly gentle, and when the lid came open and the documents showed themselves—water-darkened at the edges, the seal of Kestria staring up out of the chest like a drowned man's face—

Marta went pale.

Not metaphorically. Cyrus watched the color leave her face the way silt leaves a flooded field, top-down, and her hand came off the papers as if they'd bitten her. One of the polemen glanced over; she waved him off with a motion that was almost violent.

"Where," she said, and her voice had changed entirely, flattened into something old and afraid, "did the river give you this?"

"A flooded bend below the old Merrow road," Noor said carefully. "Snagged in the willows."

"The Merrow road." Marta laughed once, and there was nothing in it. "Of course. Of course it comes back by the water. Everything comes back by the water eventually—that's what we used to say, those of us who carried things we shouldn't have." She sat back on her heels. "Do you know how many times I've seen that seal since the erasure? Twice. Twice in twenty years, and both times it was in a stranger's hands, and both times I told myself it meant nothing, that some collector's junk had washed loose, that the past was just litter now like everything else."

"And now?" Cyrus asked.

"Now I'm telling you what I told them." She looked at him, and her eyes were terrible with age and certainty. "Both of them were carrying proof. Both of them were headed for the capital. Both of them believed someone there would listen." She paused. "Neither of them arrived."

* * *

The rain thickened. Marta made tea on a spirit stove in the corner of the awning, and they drank it scalding because there was nothing else to do with their hands, and she talked—or rather, the talk came out of her in pieces, the way water comes out of a cracked dam, not all at once but unstoppably.

"The first was a surveyor named Oduya. Quiet man, glasses, kept his documents sewn into his coat lining. Crossed with me in '09, six years ago. He had photographs. Photographs, boy—you understand what that means? Not copies, not testimony. Images. He showed me one, just one, before he thought better of it. A village square. A well. People standing near the well." Her voice went very even, which Cyrus recognized as worse than shaking. "He was going to the archive in the capital. He said the archive still had honest clerks. He said records were the one thing Crane couldn't burn, because Crane needed the records himself to prove the erasure had been legal."

Cyrus closed his eyes. "He was wrong."

"He was wrong," Marta agreed. "Or he was right, and being right was the problem. Either way, he crossed my water and walked into the capital and was never seen again. His family asked questions for a year. Then they stopped asking questions, which is how you know the answers arrived."

"And the second?" Noor asked.

"A girl. Nineteen, maybe. Said her grandmother had come from Merrow's Bend before the end. Carried a letter and a ring and a page torn from a parish register. She wouldn't tell me who sent her. She cried the whole crossing and apologized for crying, which broke my heart more efficiently than any gun ever has." Marta stared into her cup. "That was four years ago. I've crossed this flood eleven hundred times, and I remember every face, and hers is the one that wakes me up."

The barge groaned against the current. Somewhere ahead, thunder rolled along the far bank like furniture being dragged across an enormous floor.

"So you're saying it's hopeless," Noor said. Her voice was flat, and Cyrus heard what was underneath it—not despair, calculation. She was measuring the story against her own escape odds, the way she measured weather.

"I'm saying it's a toll," Marta corrected. "This crossing has a toll, and the capital has a toll, and the truth has a toll, and nobody gets to skip the payment. I'm saying two people paid it already and the river kept them. I'm saying—" she stopped herself, jaw working, and Cyrus watched seventy years of discipline fight something younger and lose. "I'm saying your mother sat where you're sitting, Noor Ashgrove, the year you were born, and she held a document with that same seal, and she looked at me and said, *Marta, if anything happens to me, burn it.* Burn it. That was her answer. She carried the truth long enough to get clear, and then she decided the living mattered more than the dead."

"What happened to the document?" Cyrus asked.

"She burned it. Right there, on my stove, over this same pot. And she flew home and raised her daughter and died in a plane crash that everyone called an accident, and I have spent nineteen years wondering whether it was one." Marta set her cup down with a click. "So no, girl, I'm not saying it's hopeless. I'm saying I've watched this exact crossing before, and I want you to make the choice with your eyes open. Because the river gives things back, but it doesn't give *people* back. Not once the capital takes them."

Silence, except the rain and the water and the far-off thunder counting down the monsoon's arrival like a fuse.

* * *

It was Noor who finally spoke, and Cyrus understood, watching her, that he was seeing something happen that he'd only ever read about in other people's archives: the moment inheritance becomes decision.

"My mother burned hers," Noor said slowly. "And it didn't save her. And it didn't save the village, because the village was already ash. All it saved was the silence." She looked down into the chest—at the orders, the signatures, the name *Merrow's Bend* written in a clerk's careful hand. "Crane has had twenty years of everyone choosing the living over the dead. Look how many of the living are left to show for it."

Marta studied her for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and brought out something wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it on her knee: a key. Old iron, river-green with corrosion, but intact.

"Oduya's," she said. "He left it with me at the crossing, in case he didn't come back. Number 44, the Arcade depository, capital riverside. He said whatever he'd already deposited there would explain itself." She pressed it into Noor's palm and closed the girl's fingers over it. "Six years I've carried this, waiting for either a ghost or a fool to give it to. Looks like I got both."

"Marta," Cyrus began, "if Crane's people are watching the crossings—"

"They are. There's a government launch patrolling above the Reach since yesterday. Gray hull, no markings, running lights off." She said it the way she'd say *it might rain*. "You'll have maybe ten minutes of dark left, and the launch swings wide at the narrows because the pilot's afraid of the snags. You go through the snags."

Noor was already moving, coiling rope, checking the winch. But Cyrus stayed a moment longer, and put his hand over the old woman's where it rested on the rail.

"Why help us? After Oduya. After the girl."

Marta looked at him, and for a second the driftwood face cracked and he saw what was underneath—not fear, exactly. Grief that had been standing watch so long it had forgotten it was grief.

"Because I'm the last one who remembers their faces," she said. "Oduya's. The girl's. Your mother's. Somebody on this river has to keep the list, and somebody has to keep sending people down it, or the list wins." She pulled her hand free and nodded toward the boat. "Go. And archivist—if you reach the capital, find the honest clerks Oduya believed in. Prove him right. It's the only eulogy left to give him."

They dropped off the barge into Noor's boat in the teeth of the rain, and the current took them, and Cyrus looked back once. Marta Voss stood at her rail in the downpour, lantern lit beside her, hanging her green signal out over the flood for whoever came next—as if she fully expected someone to.

Ahead, through the curtain of rain, the snags waited at the narrows, black and branching like something drowned reaching up. And beyond them, faint and intermittent through the storm, a gray shape was sweeping its searchlight along the far bank, patient as a creditor.

"Hold on to the chest," Noor said, and opened the throttle.
