## 9. What the Monsoon Takes

The storm found us an hour after dark, and it did not announce itself. It simply arrived, the way a debt collector arrives—already inside the door before you hear the knock.

I'd been running the boat with the throttle half-open, following the black seam of the river by the faint gray suggestion of its banks, when the rain came down like something dropped from a height. Not falling—*delivered*. A wall of water that erased the world in one stroke and left us in a drumming, roaring blindness where the only direction was the one the river chose for us.

"Kill the engine!" Cyrus shouted from the bow, both hands locked on the rail.

"If I kill it we're debris!"

"If you don't, we're a hole in something!"

He was right, which I was learning to hate about him. I cut the throttle and let the current take us, and for ten minutes that felt like a season we were nothing but cargo—two people and a chest of dead men's paper being carried wherever the water had decided to send them. Lightning turned the world white twice a second, and in those flashes I saw things I didn't want to see: a fence line underwater, the tops of trees moving like drowned men waving, a roof torn loose and sailing past us with its chimney still attached.

"We're going to hit—" Cyrus started.

We hit. Something solid under the hull, a grinding shudder, and then we swung sideways into slack water, spinning slow behind whatever had caught us. The rain kept hammering, but the violence was gone. We'd been spat into some flooded backwater, sheltered on three sides by the bulk of a building whose shape I couldn't make sense of until another flash lit it whole.

A church. Its steeple stood above the floodline like a man standing waist-deep in a river refusing to admit it.

"There," I said. "Get us to the doors."

"The doors are underwater."

"Then get us to the windows."

* * *

We tied off to what I think was a hitching post, once, now just a stub of wood breaking the surface, and waded through chest-high water into the narthex. The interior rose around us, tall and cold and echoing with rain on tin roofing somewhere above. The main floor was flooded knee-deep, maybe more toward the altar, but along the sides ran raised galleries—old country churches built their galleries high against exactly this kind of river—and we climbed to one of them and sat dripping in a pew that hadn't held a congregation in years.

Below us, drifting gently in the lamplight of the lightning flashes, were the coffins.

The flood had lifted them out of the crypt beneath the floor and set them afloat among the pews, turning slowly, knocking against each other with soft wooden sounds. Some had come open. I looked away from the first one and made myself look back, because there's a kind of fear that gets worse if you feed it, and I needed mine small.

"They came up with the water," Cyrus said quietly. "That happens, in the low parishes. Every big flood, the old graves rise."

"It's obscene."

"It's honest." He'd wedged our pack between his feet, arms wrapped around it, and he hadn't let go since the storm hit. "The ground gives back what it was lent. That's all this is."

"That's not all this is. This is a church full of dead people floating in their own nave."

"Yes." He said it without flinch, without comfort, and I realized he wasn't trying to soothe me. He was agreeing. "It's obscene. And honest. Both at once. Most true things are."

Lightning filled the windows, and for a moment every coffin below cast a shadow up the gallery wall, long and thin, like a congregation standing for a hymn.

I got the little camp stove going under the gallery overhang, out of the worst of the wet, and we ate cold rice and tinned fish and drank water boiled over a flame that sputtered every time the wind found a new way in. My hands were steady enough to light the stove. They had not been steady enough, earlier, to say the name of the village I'd flown medicine to and never landed at, and Cyrus had seen that, and neither of us had mentioned it. Some silences are a kindness. Some are just a debt deferred.

"You want to know why I do this," he said, after a while. It wasn't quite a question.

"I assumed it was guilt."

"Guilt's fuel. It isn't the reason." He was looking down at the coffins, or past them, at the black water holding them. "You want the reason?"

"We've got until the water drops."

* * *

He told it plainly, the way men tell things they've told themselves so many times the telling has worn smooth.

"When I was twenty-three I believed in the archive the way you believe in instruments. You trust the gauge because the gauge doesn't care how you feel. The National Archive was that for me—a place where the country kept its promises. Every order signed, every village registered, every debt and birth and death written down, and because it was written down, it was *real*, and because it was real, someone someday would have to answer for it. That was the whole faith. Memory as justice. Not the courts—the courts can be bought; I know, I watched them be bought—but the record itself. The record outlasts everyone who wants it gone."

"And then Merrow's Bend."

"And then Merrow's Bend." His voice didn't change. That was the terrible part. It stayed level, like a man reading his own autopsy. "Crane called me in—youngest senior archivist in the service, his protégé, his *find*—and he showed me the file. The orders. His signature. And he said, 'Cyrus, history is a tool, and right now it needs maintenance.' And he gave me a choice. Amend the record, lose my career, and watch him amend it anyway with someone more obedient—or amend it myself, keep my position, and stay close enough that someday I might undo it."

"Which is what you told yourself."

"Which is what I told myself." For the first time his voice bent. "Ten years I've said that sentence. Stay close. Undo it later. And every year 'later' cost more, and every year I amended one more thing, smoothed one more edge, and every year the man I was at twenty-three got smaller and quieter until I could barely hear him. That's how it works, Noor. It doesn't ask you to become a monster. It asks you to become a *caretaker*. Of the lie. And you get very good at it, and very respected, and very dead inside, and the file sits in your handwriting in the national archive saying a village that existed never existed, and you go home each night and sleep, because you've learned to sleep."

Below us a coffin knocked softly against a pew post, and neither of us moved.

"So no," he said. "Not guilt. Guilt would be simple. What I trust is memory. Because memory is the one thing they can't buy, only bury. And buried things—" he nodded at the water, at the risen dead, at the whole drowned church holding us—"buried things come up. Given enough water, everything comes up. Your river proved that. It handed you a chest. The flood lifted these coffins. The truth is buoyant, Noor. It's the lies that need constant ballast."

I thought about the shack burning in my mind's eye—the decoy papers going up, the fuel drums, the windsock I'd sewn myself, twenty years of a life arranged specifically so that no one would ever look at me twice. Invisibility as a philosophy. The wilderness had taught me that gifts from the water are rarely free, and I'd taken the biggest gift the water ever gave, and now everything downstream of it was flooding too.

"Why do *you* fly?" he asked suddenly. "Really. Not the medicine runs, not the charter money. Why does a woman build her whole life to be unseeable?"

The question sat in my chest like swallowed river water.

"Because I saw something once," I said. "Merrow's Bend. From the air. After. I was nineteen, flying a relief loop, and the coordinates put a village on the map and there was nothing there. Just scorch in a pattern too straight for wildfire. I radioed it in and was told my instruments were faulty and the village had been decommissioned—*decommissioned*, like it was a bridge. And I was young and scared and I let them tell me that. I landed at the next settlement and delivered penicillin and never said it again, out loud, for fifteen years. But I never flew that loop without looking down. Never once. So when I pulled that chest out of the river, I already knew what was in it. Some part of me knew. I just hadn't been given permission yet."

Cyrus was watching me with an expression I couldn't read in the lightning-dark, and I found I didn't want to hide from it, which frightened me more than the coffins did.

"Permission," he repeated.

"From the evidence. From someone else knowing. You can't carry a thing like that alone—it warps you. I warped myself around it for fifteen years and called the warp 'independence.'"

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a drowned church with a disgraced archivist and a chest of paper that could hang a governor, waiting out a monsoon, and I feel—" I searched for it, honestly, the way you search a flooded room for something that floated away. "*Unwarping.* Which hurts. Like setting a bone."

Outside, the storm leaned on the church and the church leaned back, and the water below us rose a hand's width up the gallery stairs and stopped.

* * *

We slept in shifts, which meant neither of us slept much. Somewhere in the smallest hours the rain eased from a hammering to a long steady whisper, and I took the watch while Cyrus dozed sitting up, arms still around the pack, his head tipped against the gallery rail. In the lightning flashes I counted coffins—eleven visible, turning slow—and I thought about how every one of them had a name once, and a parish record, and people who'd paid for the box, and how the flood hadn't asked any of them before raising them into the light.

Memory as justice. I was starting to understand what he meant. Not the courts—he was right, the courts could be bought; I'd find out how thoroughly before this was over. But the record. The naming. The refusal to let a scorch mark in a field be called a decommissioned bridge.

Near dawn the water began to drop. I could tell by the church steps emerging one at a time, black and glistening, like a scale being read aloud.

Cyrus woke when the light changed. He looked at the receding floodline, then at me, and something in his face had rearranged itself overnight—not softer, exactly. Lighter. A man who'd set down weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"Wrenfall's two days downstream," he said. "Sela Ivo. If she's alive, she's the last adult from Merrow's Bend, and she'll have spent twenty years keeping something safe the way we're keeping this chest."

"And if Hale's ahead of us?"

"Then we'd better hope the river likes us better than it likes him."

I went down the gallery stairs into the shin-deep water and stood among the settling coffins while the first real light came gray through the east windows. One of them had drifted against the pulpit, and on its lid, in faded paint, a name was still legible: a woman's name, a Kestrian spelling, the kind the maps had stopped carrying twenty years ago.

I read it out loud. Just once, just to the empty church. It felt like the first honest act of my life.

Behind me, Cyrus said quietly, "Wrenfall, then," and outside, far downstream, a single engine sound carried flat across the floodwater—the kind of sound you know the way you know your own heartbeat.

Hale's boat. Moving again.
