## 10. Hale Closes the Distance

The radio in the government launch crackled for a full minute before Crane's voice came through, and Inspector Edric Hale used that minute the way he used all his minutes—deliberately, without waste. He stood at the wheel of the *Meridian*, watching the brown water slide past the pilings of the Ashgrove airstrip dock, and he composed his report the way a surgeon composes an incision: one clean line, no hesitation marks.

"Sir. The chest has surfaced."

A pause on the other end. Rain ticked against the canvas canopy above him. Somewhere downstream, thunder was arranging itself into something larger.

"You're certain it's the chest." Crane's voice was dry as old paper. It always sounded to Hale like a man reading from a document he had written himself and memorized long ago.

"I'm certain it's what you're looking for. A sealed strongbox, Kestrian manufacture, pulled out of the river bend at Kilnwater by a bush pilot named Noor Ashgrove. She's been seen with it. My source at the fuel dock is reliable."

"And the contents?"

"Unknown. But she's stopped flying her medicine runs. That tells me she's read whatever's inside."

Another pause. Hale could hear, faintly, the sound of ice shifting in a glass. Ten o'clock in the morning, and the Governor of the Territory was drinking.

"Ashgrove," Crane said. "The pilot."

"The pilot."

"No record?"

"Clean as spring water. Licensed eleven years. Flies insulin and antibiotics to settlements the road crews won't reach before August. Pays her fees early. Votes, when voting matters, which is to say never." Hale turned the wheel a few degrees, easing the *Meridian* away from the dock. "She's nobody, sir. Which is a problem. Nobodies are harder to predict than somebodies. Somebodies have things they want to protect. Nobodies have already arranged their lives around having nothing."

"What do you recommend?"

It was the question Crane always asked, and it was the question Hale had learned to answer carefully, because Crane did not want recommendations so much as he wanted confirmation—wanted to hear his own intentions spoken back to him in someone else's voice, so that afterward the decision would be shared the way guilt is shared among men who all held the knife.

"Recover the property," Hale said. "Quietly. If the pilot cooperates, she keeps flying. If she doesn't—" He let the sentence go where sentences like that went. "I'll need authority to detain. And I'll need it in writing."

"In writing," Crane repeated, amused. "You always were a paperwork man, Hale. That's why I keep you. You make even the ugly things look organized."

"Yes, sir."

The radio clicked off. Hale stood in the rain-smelling dark under the canopy and looked at his own reflection ghosted in the wheelhouse glass—a lean man in a gray salvage-inspector's coat, forty-one years old, hair going iron at the temples, face arranged into the expression of a man who has never once been surprised by anything.

He let the expression drop.

For perhaps ten seconds—the length of time it took the *Meridian* to clear the last channel marker—he allowed himself to be a man standing in a boat on a flooded river, holding orders he had not yet written down, thinking about a village called Merrow's Bend.

Then the expression came back up, and he opened his logbook, and began to write.

* * *

People in the interior believed the Salvage Office existed to catalog drowned property—boats, livestock, the occasional heirloom fished out of a flood year—and Hale encouraged the belief because it was useful. An inspector who is thought to be a clerk can walk anywhere. He had walked into the fuel dock at Kilnwater that morning with a clipboard and a courteous smile, and the dockmaster had told him everything: the chest, the winch cable, the night light burning in Ashgrove's shack until three in the morning, and—this detail Hale had written down twice, underlined—the man who'd met her plane at the dock two days later, thin, gray, nervous, asking questions about the river.

Hale knew that description the way you know a scar. Cyrus Vane. Ten years dismissed from the National Archive, ten years presumed dead or drunk or both, and now surfacing in the same week as the chest, like a body and its shadow rising together.

He found Vane's trail easily enough. That was the thing about disgraced archivists—they left records everywhere. A ferry manifest signed in a hand that still bore the Archive's training, every letter disciplined, every crossing exact. A room rented in Wrenfall paid for with archive scrip that hadn't been legal currency since the dissolution. The man moved through the world leaving paper behind him the way snails leave silver. Hale almost pitied him. Almost.

By evening he had them. Not caught—tracked. The *Meridian* held station two miles below the Wrenfall crossing, engines idling, while Hale studied the ferry landing through binoculars and watched a smuggler's flat-bottomed boat take on two passengers just after dusk. One tall and stooped, moving like a man apologizing to his own skeleton. One shorter, weathered, carrying herself like the tiller was an extension of her arm.

Ashgrove and Vane, bound downriver.

Hale lowered the binoculars and made himself say the next part honestly, because honesty in private was the only discipline that kept a man from lying fatally in public.

They were heading for the capital. There was nowhere else. Every road east was washed out; every magistrate south of Rook's district owed Crane his appointment. If you had documents that could hang a governor, there was exactly one door left open in the whole territory, and it had Judge Ansel Rook's name on it.

Which meant Hale had perhaps four days.

Which meant, if he was honest, that he had perhaps four days to decide what he was actually doing.

* * *

He wrote the report that night in the wheelhouse, by lamplight, while the rain worked at the canvas overhead like a patient animal. *Property recovered by civilian party. Pursuing recovery per directive 11-K.* He kept the language clean. Clean language was armor. You could survive almost anything you described in bureaucratic nouns.

What he did not write down was the other thing, the thing that had been sitting in his chest since morning like swallowed river water.

His brother's handwriting.

He had not expected to see it. That was the truth of it—he had spent twenty years making sure he would never see it again, and then this morning, cross-checking the fuel dock's visitor log against the Archive's old personnel rolls, there it was in the margin of a supply requisition from Merrow's Bend, dated six weeks before the village ceased to exist: *T. HALE, field recorder.* Tomas Hale. His older brother, who had joined the Kestrian civil service at nineteen because he believed—God help him, he had actually believed—that a state was a machine for remembering, and that the men who kept its records were its conscience.

Tomas had been stationed at Merrow's Bend when the orders came down. Tomas's requisitions stopped in the same week the village did. When Hale had asked, twenty years ago, young and loyal and wearing his new inspector's coat, he had been told his brother had transferred north and died of fever. He had asked for the file. The file did not exist. He had asked again, in writing, properly, through channels, and had received a letter explaining that certain records of the dissolved nation had been destroyed in the interest of administrative hygiene.

Administrative hygiene. He had been twenty-three, and he had accepted the phrase, filed it, and built a career on top of it the way you build a house over a sinkhole—carefully, telling yourself the ground holds because it has held so far.

Now the ground had opened, and what came up out of it was a brass-sealed chest and a bush pilot with steady hands, and somewhere in those papers, if Crane wanted them badly enough to send an armed inspector after them, was the reason his brother's file had been the first thing fed to the fire.

Hale set down his pen.

Here was the arithmetic he could not stop performing. If the documents were worthless, Crane would not care. If they were dangerous only to Crane, then recovering them served one man and buried one truth, and the word for that work was not *salvage*. And if he refused—if he reported the chest lost, the trail cold—then Crane would send someone else, someone without a clipboard and a reputation for cleanliness, and the pilot and the archivist would die in the flood country and the papers would burn, and Tomas would stay dead twice.

There was no version of this where Hale got to be clean. He understood that now. The choice was only about which dirt he would carry.

He finished the report. He sealed it. In the morning he would transmit it, and in the morning he would also—because he was, whatever else, a paperwork man—open a second file, unnumbered, kept in his coat rather than his office, and begin recording what actually happened. Dates. Positions. Orders given and by whom. Evidence, if it came to that. Insurance, if it didn't.

A man should keep records, Tomas used to say, grinning across the kitchen table, because someday somebody will ask what you knew, and you'll want to be able to show them you knew it early.

Hale blew out the lamp.

* * *

Dawn came gray and hard, and the monsoon arrived with it—not rain but a wall of it, a moving country of water that ate the far bank of the river and came on. Most boats tied up. Hale did not. He put the *Meridian*'s bow downstream and followed the flood, because floods move faster than fugitives think, and frightened people on swollen rivers make mistakes, and mistakes leave traces: a torn tarp on a snag, a fire ring still warm, a flat-bottomed smuggler's boat hauled into the willows with its paint scraped raw.

He found the third one by midmorning. Fresh. They were close—hours, not days.

He stood at the rail in the hammering rain, binoculars useless, reading the water instead, the way the river people did, and he felt the strange double motion in himself that he suspected would define the rest of his life: the hunter's cold forward lean, and underneath it, quiet as groundwater, the other thing. The hope that they were fast enough. The shame of the hope. The refusal to put either feeling down.

Ahead, through the rain, the river bent toward the drowned lowlands, and somewhere past that bend a pilot and an archivist were running his errand for him—carrying the truth toward the last door in the territory that might still open.

Hale gave the engine more throttle.

*Catch them first,* he told himself, and did not specify, even privately, catch them for what.
