## 11. The Witness at Wrenfall

The map said Wrenfall was a town. The river disagreed.

Cyrus stood in the bow of Noor's patched and leaking boat as it shouldered through a drowned orchard, the water so high that apple trees stood to their shoulders in brown current, their fruit knocking softly against the hull like knuckles on a door. What had been a main street was a canal now. Rooftops broke the surface here and there, tiled islands with chimney stacks for masts.

"You're sure about this," Noor said. She had the tiller lashed against her hip and her eyes moving constantly—upstream, downstream, the treeline, the sky. Three days since the ferry crossing, three days since Marta's pale face and her warning: *others have carried this truth and none arrived.* "A deaf herbalist in a flooded village. That's your plan."

"She's not in the village." Cyrus unfolded the page he'd copied from the archive fragments—the one line he'd never been able to verify, the one name that had kept him alive through ten bad years. "She's upstream of it. On the ridge. Floods don't climb."

Noor looked at him the way she looked at weather she didn't trust. "And you know this how?"

"Because I tried to find her once before. Eight years ago. I got as far as the foot of the ridge and turned back." He watched the water slide past the hull, thick with silt and broken fence rails. "I told myself the trail was cold. I told myself a lot of things."

"And now?"

"Now I've got a chest full of orders signed by the man who gave them, and every day we carry it is a day Hale gets closer. If Sela Ivo is alive, she's not just a witness. She's the difference between paper and truth."

Noor was quiet for a while. The engine muttered under them, an old two-stroke she'd coaxed back from the dead with wire and stubbornness. Somewhere behind them, days back now, a boat with a government engine had been on the water. They hadn't seen it since the bend at Kettle Reach, which meant nothing and they both knew it.

"Ridge trail's past the church spire," she said finally, and put the nose of the boat toward a half-submerged bell tower rising out of the flood like a drowning man's last gesture.

* * *

They beached where the orchard ended and the land began to argue with the river—first a gravel bar, then a slope of red clay, then actual ground climbing away from the water in switchbacks overgrown with fern. Cyrus carried the documents wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, inside his coat, strapped there since dawn. He'd stopped noticing the weight the way you stop noticing a scar. It was simply part of him now.

The rain came in the way the monsoon did—not falling so much as arriving, a gray wall walking up the valley toward them. They climbed through it, soaked in minutes, saying nothing because nothing could be heard anyway. Cyrus counted switchbacks. At the ninth, according to a fisherman's rumor eight years old and probably wrong, there would be a stone wall, and past the wall, a house.

There was a stone wall. It had fallen in places and grown a beard of moss, but it ran along the contour of the hill exactly as the rumor promised, and beyond it, half-hidden by a stand of cypress, stood a low house with a slate roof and smoke rising thin and straight from its chimney.

Smoke. Someone was home.

Cyrus stopped at the gate and discovered his legs had opinions about going further. Ten years of chasing this. Ten years of imagining what he'd say if he ever found her—and finding her had always been abstract, a door at the end of a long corridor. Now the door was thirty feet away, blue-painted, peeling, real.

Noor touched his elbow. "You want me to knock?"

"I want—" He exhaled. "I don't know what I want. That's the problem."

"That's everyone's problem." She pushed the gate open. It groaned on hinges that hadn't been oiled in years but worked, which told him something: the gate was used. Someone came and went.

The garden behind the wall was the first orderly thing they'd seen in days. Rows of herbs in raised beds, labeled with wooden tags written in a careful hand. Stakes with twine. A rain barrel, a drying shed, bundles of yarrow and something silver-leaved hanging upside down under the eaves. A life built deliberately, precisely, by someone who needed the world to make sense in at least one small square of hillside.

Noor knocked on the blue door. Waited. Knocked again, harder.

Nothing.

Then Cyrus noticed the woman standing at the edge of the drying shed, and his heart stopped and restarted wrong.

She was small, gray-haired, dressed in a man's oilskin coat two sizes too big, and she was looking at them with a garden knife held low against her thigh—not threatening, exactly. Ready. Her eyes were the thing that undid him. He'd read her file a hundred times, the single surviving intake record from the Merrow's Bend relief station, *Sela Ivo, age 31, teacher,* and he had built a person out of those words, and the person he'd built had been wrong in every particular except the eyes. Dark, wide-set, watchful. Eyes that had seen the thing and were still seeing it.

She couldn't hear Noor calling out. Of course she couldn't. And she was reading their mouths, their hands, their soaked government-adjacent clothes, and the knife came up an inch.

Cyrus did the only thing he could think of. He unstrapped the oilcloth bundle from his chest, unwrapped it right there in the rain, and held up the top document so she could see the seal.

The black stamp of Kestria. The double-headed river bird. The nation that no longer existed, inked on paper that proved it had.

The knife dropped to her side. Not lowered—dropped, as if her hand had forgotten it. She stared at the seal the way people stare at ghosts, and then she stared at Cyrus, and her mouth formed a word without sound, and even deaf, even across twenty years and thirty feet of rain, he understood it perfectly.

*Finally.*

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of dried herbs and woodsmoke and lamp oil. She lit two lamps though it was barely afternoon, gestured them into chairs at a scrubbed pine table, and then did something that made Cyrus's throat close: she took down a slate from a hook by the door, wiped it clean with her sleeve, and set it on the table between them with a piece of chalk, the way you set a place for an expected guest.

The slate was worn smooth in the center. Years of conversations lived there.

He wrote: *My name is Cyrus Vane. I work—I worked—in the National Archive.*

She read it. Her face did something complicated. She took the chalk.

*I know who you are. The archive sent a letter once, years ago. Asking if I remembered anything. I wrote back eleven pages. No answer came.*

Cyrus felt the floor tilt. Eleven pages. He searched his memory of the correspondence files—he had practically memorized them during the disgrace inquiry—and found nothing. Nothing at all. Either the letter had never been logged, or it had been logged and buried, and both possibilities pointed like a compass needle at the same man.

"I believe you," he said aloud, and then remembered, and winced, and took the chalk. *I believe you. The letter never reached the public record. I'm sorry. I'm sorry is not enough.*

Sela Ivo watched his face while he wrote, the way deaf people do, harvesting meaning from the muscles around the mouth when the words themselves are denied her. When she took the chalk this time, her hand was trembling.

*You have the orders. The ones from 1994.*

*Yes.*

*Show me.*

He laid the documents out on the pine table, weighted at the corners with her herb jars, and she bent over them with the lamp pulled close, and Cyrus Vane, who had spent a decade reconstructing atrocities from carbon copies and marginalia, watched a living witness read the death warrant of everything she had ever loved.

Her lips moved as she read. Silent. But her body spoke in a language he understood too well—the stillness that isn't calm, the breath held too long, the hand that rises to the mouth and presses hard as if holding something in. When she reached the page listing the operational units, her fingers touched the names, one by one, the way you'd touch the faces in an old photograph.

When she got to the signature—*A. Crane, Territorial Governor, by order*—she went very still, and then she laughed.

It was the worst sound Cyrus had ever heard. One dry syllable, no air behind it, a laugh that had rusted shut over twenty years.

Noor's hand found his under the table and gripped it.

Sela took the chalk. Her writing had changed—larger now, pressing hard enough to snap the chalk twice before she learned to ease off.

*He came to the village himself. Before. Twice. He ate at my table. He asked about the school. He said the border would bring prosperity.* She stopped. Started again. *Prosperity. I taught that word to children. Forty-one children.*

The number sat on the slate like a stone.

*Forty-one children,* she wrote again, as if once weren't enough, as if the chalk were the only voice she had left and it needed to say it until someone listened. *The soldiers came at night in the third week of October. They said it was relocation. My brother argued. He was a surveyor; he had papers showing our land grants predated the territory charter. They shot him first, by the well, so the others would understand the terms of the discussion.*

Noor made a sound beside him, small and involuntary.

*I ran. I am not brave. I ran into the marsh with the school register because it was in my arms, the register, I had been doing inventory, and I could not let go of it. I hid in the reeds for two days. I heard it all. You do not need to hear what I heard. The fire was visible from the ridge for three days. I came back on the fourth day and there was nothing to bury, so I buried the register instead, and I buried this—*

She set down the chalk. Went to the cold fireplace, knelt, and worked loose a stone in the hearth's back wall with her fingers, practiced, the way you'd open a door you'd opened many times but hoped never to need again.

From the cavity she drew a tin box, army-green, rust-bloomed at the corners. She set it on the table between the lamps and stepped back, wiping her hands on her coat as if the box itself were unclean.

Cyrus opened it with hands that were not steady either.

Inside, wrapped in waxed linen, lay a document. Older than the ones in the chest—he could see it immediately, the paper stock, the typewriter face, the pre-war territorial letterhead. It was a memorandum, four pages, and the first line hit him like cold water:

*RE: Permanent Resolution of the Kestrian Question — Phase Two.*

Phase Two. The chest in the river, the orders signed by Crane—they had been the execution. This was the architecture. Dated eighteen months before Merrow's Bend burned. Signed not by Crane but countersigned by him, *concur, A.C.,* beneath the signature of a man whose name Cyrus knew from the deepest locked drawers of the archive: Deputy Director Hollis, Ministry of Interior. The man whose entire career had been expunged so cleanly that Cyrus had spent two years suspecting he'd invented him.

He hadn't invented him. Sela Ivo had been keeping him in a tin box above her hearth for twenty years.

"How," Noor said softly, forgetting herself, and Sela watched her lips and then looked at Cyrus, waiting.

*Where did you get this?* he wrote.

*From the well.* Her chalk moved slowly now, the tremor gentler, as if handing the burden over had steadied her. *After the fire, men came back in government trucks to fill the wells with rubble. One of them was not a soldier. He wore a clerk's armband. He was crying. He put this in my hands and said, "Someone has to survive who knows." Then he drove the truck into the marsh and I never saw him again. I think the rubble in the well was his.*

Cyrus sat back. Outside, the rain had softened to a whisper on the slate roof. In the lamplight, Sela Ivo watched them with those patient, ruined eyes, and Cyrus understood that he was looking at the reason he had spent ten years refusing to stop. Not justice—justice was a word institutions used. This. A woman on a hill with a slate and a tin box, keeping faith with the dead because no one else would.

He took the chalk one more time.

*Will you come with us? To the capital. To testify.*

She read it twice. Then she shook her head, and before his stomach could drop, she picked up the chalk.

*I will come. But not for you, and not for him.* She tapped the memorandum. *For the forty-one. And for the clerk in the well, whoever he was. Everyone else who carried this is dead or drowned. So it is my turn to be carried.*

She wiped the slate clean, and wrote one final line, and turned it to face them:

*How long until we leave?*

Noor answered aloud, forgetting again, and this time Cyrus didn't correct her, because Sela wasn't watching the words—she was watching Noor's face, and whatever she saw there, she nodded at, satisfied.

"Three days," Noor said. "Maybe four, if the river argues."

Sela began pulling herbs from her shelves with brisk purpose—feverfew, willow bark, the silver-leaved stuff—packing them into cloth squares. Preparations. Provisions. A woman who had waited twenty years and intended to lose no time now that the waiting was over.

Cyrus gathered the documents, sliding the memorandum in with the rest, and felt the bundle against his chest grow heavier and lighter at once. Paper and testimony. Architecture and witness. For the first time since the river had given up its chest, the thing he carried was no longer just evidence.

It was a case.

He was at the door when Sela touched his arm. She pressed the slate into his hands. On it, in her careful hand, one more line he hadn't seen her write:

*The inspector. The one following you. Check the name against the memorandum, page three. Some debts run in families.*

Cyrus turned to page three by lamplight, and read the list of ministry personnel seconded to the operation, and stopped breathing.

Hale. R. Hale, junior clerk, attached for records disposition.

Records disposition. The man whose job, twenty years ago, had been to make sure nothing survived.

The same job his son was doing now, on the river, behind them, getting closer.
