## 12. A Favor Called In

The river narrowed as we came into the high country, and the rain finally let up enough that I could see the hills again—real hills, not the drowned gray suggestion of them we'd been running through for two days. Cyrus sat in the bow with the chest between his knees and his arms wrapped around it like a man holding a sleeping animal he didn't entirely trust.

"Rook's landing is past the next bend," I said. "Old stone boathouse. You'll know it because it's the only building on this river that looks like somebody loved it."

"You've known him long?"

"Since I was nineteen." I eased the throttle back, letting the hull settle into the slower water. "He taught me to read weather. Not from instruments—from the way birds sit on wires, the color of the water before a squall. He said instruments lie when you need them most. People too. But the sky doesn't have an agenda."

"And he's still on the bench?"

"He's still on the bench because Crane hasn't figured out what to do with him. Too respected to remove, too stubborn to retire." I looked at Cyrus over my shoulder. "He's seventy-three years old, Vane. If you're hoping for a young man's recklessness, you're going to be disappointed."

"I'm not hoping for recklessness," he said quietly. "I'm hoping for courage. There's a difference."

I didn't answer that. The bend came up, and there it was—the boathouse, moss-green stone, its arched doors open onto dark water like a mouth about to speak. Behind it, up the terraced hill, the courthouse rose out of the mist: granite, slate-roofed, older than the republic that had tried to forget Kestria ever existed.

I cut the engine and let us drift in.

* * *

Ansel Rook was in his garden when we climbed the hill, kneeling in the wet earth with his sleeves rolled, transplanting something that looked like it had already given up on living. He didn't stand when he saw us. He just looked—first at me, then at Cyrus, then at the chest Cyrus carried—and I watched seventy-three years of judicial calm rearrange themselves around something that looked a lot like grief.

"Noor Ashgrove," he said. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy."

"I intend to." He rose slowly, wiping his hands on his trousers, and extended one to Cyrus. "Ansel Rook. And you are the archivist."

Cyrus went very still. "You know who I am."

"I knew your work before you were disgraced, young man. I cited your finding on the Harrowgate land grants in two of my own rulings." Rook's eyes were pale gray and missed nothing. "And I have known for ten years that the record was wrong. Do you know how I knew?"

"How?"

"Because I signed the warrant that opened the Merrow's Bend inquiry in 1994, and twenty years later every page of it had vanished—including my signature." He turned and walked toward the house, not checking whether we followed. We followed. "Come inside. Both of you. And bring whatever's in that chest, because I suspect it's the reason my garden suddenly has visitors who smell of diesel and fear."

* * *

The inside of Rook's house smelled of paper and pipe smoke and old wood polish. Books everywhere—not the decorative kind but the kind that had been read until they fell apart and been mended with string. He cleared a space on the long oak table, spread a linen cloth over it like a surgeon preparing a field, and nodded at Cyrus.

"Open it."

Cyrus looked at me. I nodded. He unlocked the chest and lifted out the documents—the orders, the manifests, the seal of Kestria pressed into wax that had gone amber with age—and laid them out under Rook's reading lamp.

For a long time the judge said nothing. He bent close, using a magnifying glass he took from his vest pocket, and his hands were steady in a way that made me realize mine weren't. He read every page. All of them. It took forty minutes and none of us moved.

Then he straightened, took off his glasses, and pinched the bridge of his nose.

"This is authentic," he said. "The watermark, the registry codes, the countersignatures. This is the operational order for Merrow's Bend, signed by Aldous Crane, then Deputy Minister of Interior Security." He set the magnifying glass down with exaggerated care, the way a man does when he wants very badly to throw something. "Twenty years I have waited for this to surface. Twenty years, Noor. And now that it has, I want you to understand exactly what you've carried up my hill."

"Tell us," Cyrus said.

"I'll tell you what it isn't. It isn't a case. Not yet. A document is not justice—it's kindling. What it becomes depends entirely on whether it survives long enough to be read aloud in front of people who cannot be made to unhear it." Rook walked to the window. Rain had started again, soft against the glass. "Crane will know it exists within days. Perhaps hours. His first move won't be against you—he'll barely remember your names. His first move will be against this courthouse. Because as long as I hold jurisdiction over the interior circuits, there is one room in this territory where his orders can be entered into a public record he cannot burn."

"So don't announce it," I said. "Hear it quietly. Sealed session."

"With whose clerks?" Rook turned back to us, and for the first time I saw how tired he was—not old-tired but war-tired, the tiredness of a man who had been losing politely for decades. "Noor, I have four clerks. One of them I would trust with my life. The others I would trust with my groceries, and even then I'd count the change. A sealed hearing requires staff, and staff require salaries, and salaries come from an administration headed by the man in those documents." He tapped the table. "This building runs on Crane's money the way your plane ran on fuel. And you told me once, when you were nineteen and furious at me for failing you in some small way—you don't remember—that a machine that depends on its enemy for power is already lost."

I did remember. I'd forgotten remembering it.

"So what do we do?" Cyrus asked. His voice was careful, controlled, the voice of a man who had learned to keep hope on a short leash. "You're saying we brought it here to die."

"I'm saying I will hear this case." Rook came back to the table and put both hands flat on the oak, leaning over the documents like a man sheltering a flame from wind. "Formally, publicly, on the record, with journalists in the gallery if we can get them here before Crane's lawyers arrive with injunctions. That is the only version of this that matters. Anything quieter can be disappeared, and I have watched quieter versions disappear for twenty years." He looked at me. "But hear the cost plainly, Noor. The moment I file, this courthouse becomes the most important building in the territory—and the most endangered. Crane cannot allow a reading. He will send men. He may send worse than men. Are you prepared for that?"

I thought about my airstrip shack, burned. My plane—God, my plane, sitting at Marta's crossing with a patched fuel line, the closest thing I'd ever had to a home with wings. I thought about the shack again, the decoy ashes, the life I'd torched on purpose two nights ago so that a stranger could live.

"I burned my decoy copy in my own fireplace," I said. "I've been paying costs since before I got here."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got. File it."

Rook held my gaze for a long moment, and then something in his face shifted—not softening exactly, more like a lock turning. "There she is," he murmured. "The girl who wouldn't accept no for a weather report." He turned to Cyrus. "Archivist. Your part comes next. When I file, the defense will attack provenance first—they'll say the documents are forged, that you fabricated them to rehabilitate yourself. Can you establish the chain of custody? Every hand these papers passed through, from 1994 to that river?"

"Sela Ivo," Cyrus said. "Last living adult witness from Merrow's Bend. She confirmed the massacre. She gave us a second document—her own account, written over twenty years."

"Where is it?"

The silence lasted three seconds. It felt like three years.

"The river has it," I said finally. "We lost it at the boathouse raid. Hale's men."

Rook closed his eyes briefly. "Then Sela Ivo herself must testify. Where is she?"

"Wrenfall," Cyrus said. "She's deaf. She won't travel easily, and she won't trust anyone we send."

"Then someone she trusts goes to her." Rook was already moving, pulling a ledger from a shelf, opening it across the table beside the documents. "I can file tomorrow morning. Once I do, we have perhaps thirty-six hours before Crane's response lands on this hill like weather. In that time: get word to Wrenfall, get the journalists here, and get yourselves somewhere that isn't visible." He glanced up. "There's a boathouse on my property. Stone walls, one door, and a lock I installed myself. You'll sleep there tonight, and you'll like it."

"Judge," Cyrus said, and stopped. Started again. "Why? Ten years ago I would have understood—I was useful then. Now I'm a disgraced archivist with a falsification on his record. Why risk everything?"

Rook looked at him for a long moment, and I saw the judge deciding how much truth to spend.

"Because in 1994," he said, "I signed a warrant to investigate Merrow's Bend, and three weeks later I was offered a promotion I hadn't applied for, and I took it, and the investigation died, and I told myself for twenty years that taking the promotion was the price of staying alive to fight another day." He closed the ledger. "I have been fighting other days ever since, young man. There are not many left. I would like one of them to count."

* * *

The boathouse was dry and cold and smelled of rope and varnish. Cyrus lay on the cot staring at the rafters; I sat on the stone steps outside with the door open, watching the rain come down the hill through the trees toward the river.

Rook found me there near midnight, carrying two cups of tea and no illusions. He handed me one and lowered himself onto the step beside me with the groan of old joints, and for a while neither of us said anything, because we'd never needed to.

"You're afraid," he said at last. It wasn't a question.

"I'm terrified. That's different. Fear's about the thing in front of you." I wrapped my hands around the cup. "This is about the thing after. Ansel—if we do this, if it works, what's left? The interior's built on pretending. My whole living was built on being nobody. I flew medicine to villages that don't appear on maps because appearing on maps gets them audited, or taxed, or visited by men like Hale. If Crane falls, everything underneath him comes up. Every arrangement. Every quiet deal that keeps the rivers flying."

"Yes," Rook said. "That will happen."

"You say that like it's fine."

"I say it like it's true." He sipped his tea. "Noor, when I promised to help you, all those years ago, do you recall what I actually said?"

"You said if I ever found something worth the risk, you'd help."

"I said worth the risk. Not safe from it." He set down his cup. "A favor called in is never free, child. The question you should be asking yourself tonight is not whether you're willing to pay. It's whether you've already spent the coin—and I think you have. I think the moment you winched that chest out of the river, you stopped being invisible. Everything since has just been the bill arriving."

Down the hill, the river moved in the dark, patient and enormous, carrying whatever it was given.

"There's one more thing," Rook said, standing, joints cracking. "My clerk Tam came by this evening while you were docking. Asked why I'd had visitors who arrived by boat instead of road. I told him family." He paused at the door. "Tam has worked for me eleven years. I told him family because I watched his face when he asked, and I have spent twenty years learning to watch faces, and his face was wrong, Noor. It was wrong in a way I can't prove and can't ignore."

The cold that went through me had nothing to do with the rain.

"So what do we do?" I asked. "Lock him out?"

"We do nothing," Rook said. "We let him come to work in the morning, and we file, and we watch which way the wind sits on his wire." He looked out at the black line of the hills. "Instruments lie when you need them most. People too. But faces—" he smiled, thin and tired, "—faces only lie as well as they've been paid to."

He went inside. I stayed on the steps until the tea went cold, listening to the rain, and somewhere below us, far down the flooded valley toward the capital, I imagined lights coming on in offices where men were already writing our names into their ledgers.

Tomorrow we filed. And whatever came up that river after that—

—we'd be waiting for it with the doors open.
