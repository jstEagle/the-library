## 13. The Betrayal at the Boathouse

Judge Ansel Rook's clerk was a young man named Pell with ink-stained fingers and a habit of humming when he lied, and I liked him immediately, which should have been my first warning.

He met us at the courthouse side door the morning after we arrived, carrying a tin of tea and an umbrella he never opened despite the rain coming down in gray sheets. "The judge says you're to stay off the streets," he told us, pouring from the tin into three chipped cups under the eave. "There are men asking questions at the ferry offices. Describing a woman pilot and a man who looks like a funeral."

"That's accurate," Noor said.

"He also says—" Pell hesitated, and his humming stopped, which meant this part was true— "that the hearing can't happen for four days. The flood has closed the eastern road, half the clerks haven't made it in, and Crane's people have filed eleven motions to delay. The judge is fighting them one at a time."

"Four days," Noor repeated. She looked out at the water running brown and fast through the street, high enough now that the gutters had become canals and the canals had become something with opinions. "Four days is a long time to be a funeral."

"The boathouse," Pell said. "Behind the courthouse, on the canal. The judge keeps a skiff there and the loft above it is dry. Nobody uses it since the flood took the lower door. You'll be safe until the hearing." He handed Noor a key on a loop of twine, and his fingers were steady, and his face was open as a window, and I believed every word he said because I wanted to believe that institutions still worked—that somewhere in this drowned country there was still a building where paper meant what it said.

I had believed that once before. It had cost me ten years and a woman named Ilse Varga, whose name I still could not say aloud without tasting copper.

But I said nothing. We took the key.

* * *

The boathouse loft was dry the way a coffin is dry. Narrow, low-ceilinged, smelling of tar and old rope, with a single window overlooking the canal and a second, smaller one facing the courthouse's back wall—close enough that we could see the light burning late in Rook's chambers, the judge working by lamplight like a man bailing out a boat with a teaspoon.

Noor spread our documents across the floor on a painter's tarp and knelt over them for hours, drying them page by page with a candle held at a careful distance. The chest had spent twenty years underwater before the river gave it up, and Sela Ivo's testimony—the trembling handwriting, the second document she'd pressed into my hands in her silent kitchen—had fared worse than the rest. The ink had run in places. The paper had gone soft as skin.

"Read it again," she said. Not looking up. "Out loud. I want to hear how it sounds when it isn't just us."

So I read it. Sela's words, written in a hand that shook like a branch in wind: *They came at dusk on the fourth of October. My brother Tomas ran for the church and they shot him in the doorway of God's own house. I lay under my mother's floorboards for two days and heard everything. When I came out, Merrow's Bend was smoke and flies.* And beneath it, the second document—the requisition order, the fuel allocations, the convoy manifest that proved the soldiers who burned the village had been fed and armed and paid by the territorial government, signed in a chain of signatures that ended, like all rivers end, at Aldous Crane.

"It sounds true," Noor said quietly.

"It is true."

"I know. That's not the same thing, though, is it? In a courtroom." She set the candle down and looked at me across the tarp, her face lit from below, all shadows and stubbornness. "Rook will get it entered. He promised. But promises need four days, and four days need us alive."

"We've managed so far."

"We've managed by being nobody. We stop being nobody the moment we walk into that courtroom." She folded Sela's testimony with a gentleness I hadn't seen her use on anything—not the plane, not the compass, not me—and tucked it into an oilskin pouch along with the core documents. "Which means these don't leave my body between now and then. Not for food. Not for sleep. Nothing."

"Understood."

"And Cyrus." She paused. "If it comes to it—if they corner us—you take the pouch and run, and I make the noise. You're the archivist. You're the one they need breathing."

"No."

"It's not a request."

"It's a bad plan," I said. "You fly. I drown. That's been the division of labor this whole journey and I see no reason to amend it now."

Something moved through her face—annoyance, and underneath it, unwillingly, warmth. She blew out the candle. In the dark, the rain filled the silence like a third person breathing.

* * *

I woke to the sound of oars.

Not the slap of water against hull—there was plenty of that, all night, the whole flooded city talking in its sleep—but the rhythmic, muffled knock of oars against oarlocks, moving slow, moving deliberate, coming up the canal from the direction of the market.

I put my hand on Noor's shoulder and she was awake instantly, eyes open, no transition, the way people sleep who learned to sleep in places where waking slowly could kill you. I pointed at the window. She rose without a sound and looked through the gap in the shutter.

Two boats. Lanterns hooded. Six men, maybe seven, and at the bow of the lead boat a figure I knew by his posture alone—upright, patient, hands folded like a man waiting for a train—Inspector Hale.

"How?" Noor breathed.

And then I saw it. Across the canal, at the mouth of the alley behind the courthouse, a lantern came on. Once. Twice. Three times. A signal, answered from the water.

Pell. The humming clerk with the honest face. Rook's own office, leaking from the inside.

"There's a back way," Noor whispered, already moving, sweeping the documents into the oilskin pouch and slinging it across her chest. "Down through the boat bay. The skiff. If we can reach the far channel before they—"

The front door of the boathouse below us splintered.

They hadn't waited for finesse. Boots on the lower stairs, lantern light climbing the walls through the gaps in the floorboards, and Hale's voice—courteous even now, courteous the way a scalpel is clean—rising through the loft hatch: "Miss Ashgrove. Mr. Vane. I regret the hour. The judge regrets it more, though he doesn't know yet, and I'd prefer to keep it that way."

Noor kicked open the rear window and dropped onto the roof of the boathouse's boat bay, and I followed, landing hard enough to drive the air from my lungs, the pouch slamming against my ribs. Below us, black water. The skiff was tied at the near edge, half-swamped, but floating.

She went down the drainpipe first. I went second, badly, tearing my sleeve and nearly my arm, and hit the dock planks as the first boot came through the loft above. A lantern beam swept the water. A shout went up—*"Canal! They're at the canal!"*

Noor cut the skiff's line with a knife she produced from nowhere, and we shoved off into the current just as the second boat rounded the pilings ahead of us.

"Flooded streets," she said. "We can't outrun oars in open water. Take the left fork—there's a lane that used to be Cordwainer Street, it runs shallow past the chandlery—"

The second boat swung toward us. A man stood in its bow with a hooked pole, and behind him, another with something longer and less ambiguous. Noor threw her weight against the oars and the skiff leapt, and we shot past the boat close enough to see the pole-man's face, young and frightened, before the current took us sideways into the mouth of Cordwainer Street.

The water there was waist-deep and full of the street itself—door frames, a cart wheel, a sign for a shop that sold, absurdly, umbrellas. We dragged the skiff by its painter, wading, the pouch between us like a child being passed through a crowd. Behind us the boats couldn't follow; the lane was too narrow, too choked. Ahead, the city opened into the great drowned intersection where five streets became a lake, and beyond that, the high ground of the market district, and beyond that—

A wall of rain came off the river.

It arrived the way weather arrives in this country—not gradually, but as a decision. One moment the rain was falling; the next it was horizontal, a gray freight train of water, and the lake in front of us stood up in whitecaps. Noor shouted something I lost entirely. She grabbed my collar and pointed: a stone stairway rising out of the flood on our right, the steps of some drowned church or customs house, leading up to a terrace above the waterline.

We climbed. The current pulled at our legs like hands. Halfway up, the skiff tore free of the painter and vanished backward into the dark, and neither of us reached for it, because the pouch was what mattered, the pouch was everything, the pouch was Merrow's Bend and Sela Ivo and ten years of a dead man's name.

We made the terrace. Collapsed against a stone balustrade. The rain hammered down and the flood below us seethed, and somewhere out in it, faintly, I could still hear oars.

"Where's the rest of it?" Noor gasped.

I checked the pouch with numb fingers. The core documents were there—Crane's signature, the orders, the seal of Kestria, water-spotted but legible. I counted them twice, three times, my heart slowing with each confirmation.

Then I stopped.

"Sela's testimony," I said.

Noor stared at me. "It's in the pouch. You put it—"

"I put the requisition order in. The manifest. The—" My hands were shaking now, properly shaking, and I turned the pouch upside down over the balustrade's sheltered inner ledge and shook it, and pages fell out, and I gathered them, and counted, and knew before I finished counting. "When we waded Cordwainer Street. The pouch must have—there's a seam, the stitching gave, it must have gone—"

"Cyrus." Her voice was very quiet. "Where."

I looked at the flood.

Somewhere beneath that churning gray water, in a lane or a lake or a canal, a single sheet of soft, ruined paper was dissolving even now—the last living voice of Merrow's Bend, a woman's account of her brother shot in a church doorway, written in a shaking hand because her whole body shook now, because she had survived by hiding under her mother's floorboards while everyone she loved burned. Twenty years she had kept it. Twenty years of silence, and she had given it to me because I swore—I *swore*—that this time the paper would mean what it said.

"No," Noor said. Not to me. To the water. She was already moving, back down the stairs, into the flood to her thighs, sweeping her arms through the black water like a woman searching a room in the dark. "No, no—it's oilskin, the pouch is oilskin, maybe the page stayed dry, maybe it sank slow, maybe—"

"Noor."

"Maybe it caught on something. Things catch. The plane caught. The chest caught. Everything catches eventually—"

"*Noor.*"

She stopped. Stood in the flood with her arms in the water up to the elbows, rain streaming off her, and did not turn around.

"It's gone," I said. "The river took it."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then she waded back up the stairs, and her face when she reached me was the face of a woman deciding something, and I understood, watching her, that I was seeing the exact moment when trusting institutions died in her—the same death I'd lived through ten years ago, same wound, different day. Rook's courthouse. Rook's clerk. Four days of safety, sold for whatever Hale had paid, and the price collected in a deaf herbalist's handwriting.

"Pell," she said flatly.

"Pell."

"When this is over, I want him to know it was me. Not the law. Me."

"You'll have to stand in line."

That startled something out of her—not quite a laugh, but its cousin—and she wiped the rain from her face with both hands and looked east, toward the market district, toward the high ground, toward the only road left. "The documents are enough," she said. "Barely. Crane's signature, the orders, the seal. It proves the crime. It doesn't prove the village had a name anyone remembers." She looked at me. "You told me once that memory is the only justice you trust. Well. The river just took a piece of ours. So we'd better make damn sure the rest of it gets read out loud."

Below us, out in the flood, a lantern beam swept the whitecaps, patient as ever, and found nothing. Hale hadn't gotten the pouch. Hale didn't know yet how close he'd come.

But he would keep coming. Men like Hale always kept coming, right up until the moment they didn't—and I had seen, in the boathouse lantern light, the way his eyes had gone not to Noor but to the pouch, hungry and almost afraid, like a man carrying someone else's secret. There was a crack in that man. Somewhere. I'd seen hairline fractures like it before, in archives and in governments, and I knew what usually happened to them.

They either widened, or they got papered over.

Above the drowned intersection, thunder rolled down the valley like a verdict being read, and Noor shouldered the pouch, and we started for the high ground.
