## 16. The Plane in the Flood

The rain started as a rumor on the windshield and became a fact of the world.

Noor ran her preflight by feel more than sight, the flashlight beam swallowed three feet from the cowling. The *Kestrel*—her Kestrel, twelve years of patched fabric and rebuilt pistons—sat on the flooded strip like an animal that already knew what was coming. She'd named it before she knew anything about Kestria. That felt like something now. Like the river had been writing this whole story before any of them learned to read.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Cyrus said behind her, shouting over the storm without really shouting, his voice flat with the particular calm of a man who has decided to be afraid later.

"Because the roads are gone." She checked the tie-downs, then the fuel caps, then her own hands, which were steady, which had to stay steady. "Because Hale knows every crossing between here and the capital, because Rook's courthouse burns tomorrow if we're not there first with proof, and because—" she patted the chest lashed into the cargo bay, wrapped in oilcloth and her last dry tarp, "—this doesn't survive another night at ground level. Nothing does."

"And if the engine quits?"

"The engine won't quit."

"Noor."

She looked at him. Rain ran down his face and he didn't wipe it away, just stood there holding the compass in one fist—the brass thing from the chest, needle swinging toward nothing useful—and she understood he was offering her an out. A way to say *not tonight, not in this*. Ten years ago someone had offered him an out and he'd taken it, and he'd been drowning slowly ever since. He wasn't going to let her do the same.

"If the engine quits," she said, "we swim. You can swim?"

"I grew up in a river town."

"Then you'll grow up some more. Get in."

* * *

They lifted off into weather no sane pilot would taxi through, and Noor felt the old joy anyway—that lift in the chest when the wheels leave the waterlogged grass and the world drops away and becomes, briefly, a problem instead of a grief. The Kestrel bucked the first wall of rain and climbed through it, and for eleven minutes she was only a pilot: instruments, headings, the gray churn below where the lowlands used to be.

"You should see it from up here," she said, mostly to herself. "Before. There were fields. You could follow the river like a vein all the way to the capital lights."

Cyrus leaned forward between the seats. "And now?"

"Now it's a lake with opinions."

He laughed—a short, startled sound, like he'd forgotten the machinery for it—and she thought, absurdly, in the middle of everything, *I want to hear that again when it's not raining.*

Then the engine coughed.

Not died. Coughed—the deep, wet, wrong sound every bush pilot learns in their nightmares. Noor's hands moved before her mind caught up: mixture, fuel selector, primer. The engine caught, surged, coughed again.

"Water in the line," she said. "Or worse."

"Worse being?"

"Being right about the water in the line."

She put the nose down to keep airspeed, scanning for anything that wasn't water. The lowlands were gone—not flooded, *erased*, the whole basin a single moving sheet of brown, fences and roads and the tops of trees all drowned under current. Somewhere beneath them was the ferry crossing. Somewhere was Marta's landing. Somewhere was the church with its coffins in the pews, and none of it was land anymore.

Third cough. Then silence.

The prop windmilled once, twice, and stopped, and the world went quiet except for rain hammering the wings and Cyrus saying her name and Noor's own voice, calm and far away, saying: "Brace. Now. Unlatch your door first—do it now, Cyrus, before we hit."

She picked her spot the way you pick a spot when there are no spots: a stretch where the current ran smoother, where something like a hedgerow broke the surface. Flaps. Nose up. She held the Kestrel level as long as physics allowed, whispering to it—I've got you, I've got you, old girl—and then the water took the wheels and the world became violence.

* * *

Impact is not a moment. It's several moments wearing each other's clothes.

The hull hit. The nose dug. Water came through the windshield like a wall deciding to become ocean, and the plane stood on its nose and began to go under tail-first, and Noor's harness held her upside down in the cockpit while brown water filled her boots, her sleeves, her mouth.

*Cyrus.* She couldn't see him. The cabin was black water and spinning debris and somewhere in it a man who'd grown up in a river town.

She tore at the harness release, missed, tore again, and dropped into the flood. It grabbed her instantly—cold, muscular, indifferent—tumbling her against the seat frame, the doorpost, something soft that might have been the tarp. Her lungs were screaming. She kicked for the lighter dark, found it, broke the surface gasping beside the Kestrel's wing, which floated with terrible dignity, like a bird deciding whether to admit it was dead.

"Cyrus!"

Nothing. Rain. Current dragging at her, trying to file her under lost.

"Cyrus!"

The other door burst open six feet away and he came up through it coughing, one hand locked on the wing strut, eyes wild and alive. "I'm here," he choked. "I'm here."

"The chest—"

"Forget the chest—"

"It's lashed to the frame, if the frame floats—" She was already pulling herself along the wing, hands numb, mind doing arithmetic it hated: the fuselage was sinking, the tail was down, the cargo bay would flood in seconds, and the chest was strapped where she'd strapped it because it was the safest place in the aircraft, and the safest place in the aircraft was now the bottom of a lake that used to be the lowlands.

Cyrus reached her. Together they hauled at the lashings underwater, fingers failing, oilcloth slick. The knot she'd tied—double-locked, storm-proof, the best knot of her life—would not give.

"Knife," she gasped.

"You don't have a knife."

"I have a knife."

"In your boot?"

"In my boot."

He got it. He sawed. The lashing parted, and Noor got both arms around the chest as the fuselage gave a final shudder and slid off the edge of visibility, taking her heart with it. The chest was heavy, buoyant, alive in the current—pulling her downstream, pulling her under, and she held on because it was the record of Merrow's Bend and she had burned her whole life to carry it and she would drown before—

"Noor." Cyrus's voice, very close, very level. "Let it go or it takes you with it."

"I can hold it—"

"You can't. Look at me. *Look at me.*" His face was inches from hers, streaming, gray-lipped, certain. "We survived. That's the part that matters. We're the ones who can testify. Let it go."

The current wrenched. Her grip slipped an inch, two inches, and she understood—with the cold, clean clarity that comes only at the exact border of dying—that he was right, and that being right was unbearable.

"I'm sorry," she said, though she didn't know who she was apologizing to. The village. The plane. Herself, ten years invisible, finally flying toward something.

She let go.

The chest turned once in the water—she would swear forever that it turned, deliberately, like a man looking back—and then the river folded it under and carried it away into the rain.

* * *

They washed up against a hedgerow half a mile downstream, or what had been a hedgerow and was now a strainer of branches catching debris. Noor dragged Cyrus onto the tangle by his collar, or he dragged her; afterward neither could say. They lay across the wet branches gasping while the storm spent itself around them, and the Kestrel did not come past, and neither of them mentioned it.

It came apart somewhere out there in the dark. She knew it the way you know a death in the family before the call comes. Twelve years. Every strip in the interior knew that engine sound. Marta had known it. Sela's valley had known it. She had built her invisibility out of that sound, and now the sound was gone, and underneath the grief she felt something else, something that frightened her more than the water had:

Relief. A small, shameful relief. The plane had been how she stayed hidden. The plane was gone. Whatever she was now, she would have to be it out loud.

"We lost it," Cyrus said. Not a question. His voice had gone somewhere flat and old.

"The river has it."

"The river *has* it." He laughed, and it was the worst laugh she'd ever heard, hollow as a drowned bell. "Twenty years I chased this. Ten years digging through archives, four years following rumors down rivers, and I finally hold it in my hands and I give it back to the water inside of an hour."

"You didn't give it back. I did."

"That's not—"

"It was my grip, Cyrus. My choice." She sat up on the branches, soaked to the soul, watching the current slide past in the graying dark. "So here's what I know about this river. I pulled a sealed chest out of a bend it didn't want to keep. It gave that chest to me. And now it's taken it back." She looked at him. "Rivers don't keep things forever. That's the whole lesson of my life. Everything the water takes, it gives back eventually—changed, but legible. My mother taught me that. The river taught me that. I am not going to stop believing it now, at the worst possible moment, just because believing hurts."

Cyrus stared at her. Rain dripped off his chin. And slowly, something shifted in his face—not hope exactly, but the place where hope goes to wait.

"The floodline," he said quietly. "When the water drops. Debris catches. Willows, bridges, bends."

"Willows," she agreed. "Bridges. Bends."

"And if it's destroyed? Ink runs. Paper—"

"Then we find Sela again. We find Hale's conscience. We find whatever's left and we make it enough." She stood, unsteady, and pulled him up. "But first we get off this hedge before it takes us too, and we walk until we find high ground, and at dawn we start searching the floodline. Both of us. All day if we have to."

"And if we find nothing?"

"Then we find nothing," Noor said, "and we walk into the capital anyway, and we tell Judge Rook what we read with our own eyes, and we tell it so well that Merrow's Bend becomes true again just by being spoken." She was shaking now, cold and adrenaline and grief all at once, and she let herself shake. "But we're going to find it, Cyrus. I pulled it out of the water once. I can do it twice."

They climbed down from the hedgerow and began to walk, two drenched figures along a drowned road, the storm thinning overhead to a gray, exhausted drizzle. Behind them the flood rolled on, patient and enormous, holding whatever it held.

Somewhere in that moving darkness, wedged in willow roots or riding the current toward the capital itself, a brass-bound chest drifted with the dawn coming.

All they had to do was get there first.
