## 19. The Last Honest Court

The city was burning in pieces, the way a body burns when it can't decide where to die first.

Noor saw it from the air for eleven minutes before the engine coughed and she put them down on a flooded boulevard that had once been the Avenue of Records. Smoke columns stood up out of the districts like black trees. Somewhere to the north, a munitions depot went with a sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard. The streets below were full of people moving in every direction at once—fleeing, looting, marching, singing. She couldn't tell which was which anymore.

"Fuel's done," she said. "She won't fly again today."

Cyrus had the chest strapped across his back like a pilgrim's burden. He looked up at the smoke and said nothing, which was how she knew he understood: they'd come the last four hundred miles by river, by road, by foot, and now the last mile would be on foot too, through all of this.

"The courthouse is eight blocks," he said. "Rook said the east entrance. The one facing the canal."

"Eight blocks." Noor checked the pistol Hale had left wedged under the chest's strap—a debt or an apology, she still wasn't sure—and started walking into the riot.

* * *

They moved the way water moves, finding the gaps. A mob surged past them carrying a torn banner of Crane's administration, chanting something about the truth, about Merrow's Bend, about names—the word *Kestria* in a hundred mouths at once, a word that had been illegal for twenty years and was now being screamed off balconies. Noor kept her head down and Cyrus's sleeve in her fist. At the corner of the old mint, three men were beating a government effigy with chair legs. Two blocks further, a woman stood alone in an intersection directing traffic that no longer existed, wearing a traffic warden's uniform and weeping without any change in her expression.

"You see it?" Cyrus shouted over a detonation somewhere behind them.

"See what?"

"All of it. Twenty years I thought nobody remembered. They all remembered. They were just waiting for permission."

A bottle broke against a shuttered storefront near his head. He didn't flinch. Noor pulled him around a burning kiosk and thought: *permission*. That was what the papers in the chest were—not evidence, not really, not anymore. Evidence needed a court. This was permission, and half the city already knew it was coming.

The sixth block was underwater to their knees and rising. The canal had jumped its embankment during the night, and the flood moved through the street with a low, patient current that tugged at their legs like hands. Debris rafted past them: a door, a birdcage, a child's shoe, a portrait of Governor Crane with the face cut out, floating face-down like something drowned.

Then the seventh block, and Noor stopped so fast Cyrus walked into her.

Four men in plain coats were carrying jerricans of fuel up the courthouse steps.

* * *

Judge Ansel Rook's courthouse was a gray limestone pile built by a government that had believed in itself, with columns like held breath and steps wide enough for a crowd. It had been closed officially for two years—"structural assessment"—which everyone understood to mean it had refused to rule the way it was told to rule. Rook had kept working inside it anyway, an old man keeping a lamp lit in a house the state had condemned.

Now the state had sent fire instead.

"No," Noor said, and it came out of her as a roar, and then she was running.

Cyrus caught her arm at the base of the steps. "Noor—"

"They're going to burn it. They're going to burn the *court*, Cyrus—"

"And if you run up those steps swinging, you'll be shot before the second column, and the chest goes into the canal, and everything we did was for nothing." His grip was iron. Ten years of disgrace had taught him exactly how much a man could lose in one impulsive minute; she could feel that knowledge in his hand. "Think. How do we stop four arsonists?"

She thought. Her heart was going like a piston. Then she saw it—the crowd, two streets back, the mob with the torn banner, still flowing toward the civic district like a river seeking the sea.

"We don't stop them," she said. "We give them a reason to."

* * *

It took ten minutes to find the banner-bearers, and five more to convince them, and Noor would never afterward be able to say exactly what she'd said—something about Crane's men, about the courthouse, about the last judge in the territory who hadn't sold his signature. She only knew that when she turned back toward the steps, she wasn't walking alone anymore. There were thirty of them, then sixty, then a hundred, pouring across the flooded plaza with the ugly joy of people who have finally been given a target worth their anger.

The four men in plain coats froze halfway up the stairs, jerricans in hand, doing the arithmetic every coward does when the crowd arrives early.

One of them dropped his can and ran. Another made it three steps toward the alley before the crowd folded him in. The last two set down their fuel very carefully, raised their hands, and were swallowed.

Noor took the steps two at a time, chest hammering, and hammered on the bronze doors until her fist ached.

A slot scraped open. One eye, ancient and bloodshot.

"It's Ashgrove," she said. "You promised."

The eye studied her for a long moment. Then, behind the door, bolts began to draw—one, two, three—and Judge Ansel Rook opened his condemned courthouse himself, small and stooped and dry-eyed, in a robe gone gray at the shoulders.

"I promised," he agreed. "I didn't promise it would be safe. Come in, both of you. And close the door. What's coming next is going to want in."

* * *

Inside smelled of dust and lamp oil and forty years of law. Clerks—six of them, the ones who hadn't fled—stood in the great hall clutching ledgers like life vests. Rook led them past the empty public gallery to the well of the court, a floor of worn oak worn paler in the shape of a thousand arguments.

"Tell me what you have," Rook said.

Cyrus unstrapped the chest and opened it on the bench, and even now, even here, Noor watched the clerks lean away from it slightly, the way people lean from a live wire. Water-stained paper, Kestrian seals, the orders signed in a governor's careful hand. And beside them, wrapped in oilcloth, Sela Ivo's testimony—recovered from the floodline, half its ink bloomed into ghosts, but legible. Legible enough.

Rook read for a long time. The hall was silent except for the rain on the high windows and, faintly, the crowd outside, which had not dispersed. It had sat down on the steps instead, hundreds of them, soaked and waiting, like a congregation outside a church that had finally opened.

"This is not a trial," Rook said at last. "You understand that. I cannot convene one. The writs are suspended, the registry is sealed, and by morning there may be no building here at all."

"Then what is it?" Noor asked.

Rook looked at her over his spectacles, and for the first time since the airstrip, the old judge smiled. "It is a reading. The oldest thing there is. Before there were courts, girl, there were witnesses, and witnesses spoke aloud, and everyone who heard them became responsible for remembering. That is all a court ever was—a room where the forgetting stops." He turned to his clerks. "Open the doors. All of them. If they're coming to burn a courthouse, let them hear what's in it first."

* * *

They opened the doors at dusk, and the city came in.

Not all of it—there was fighting still, and fear, and too many people who had learned that crowds attract bullets—but enough. Journalists first, three of them who'd been sleeping in a print shop two streets over, notebooks out before the doors finished opening. Then clerks from other offices, lawyers, a nurse still in her apron, dockworkers dripping canal water, the banner-bearers, the traffic warden who had stopped weeping. They filled the gallery and stood along the walls and packed the doorway, and when there was no more room they stood on the steps outside, and their voices carried the words inward and outward again.

Rook took the bench because someone had to, though he wore no authority but his own stubbornness. A clerk swore him in with a formula that hadn't been spoken in this building for two years, voice cracking on the old words.

Cyrus Vane stood in the well of the court, and Noor watched him become, in front of her eyes, the archivist he had been before the fall—spine straight, voice level, hands steady on documents that trembled only once, at the beginning.

"In the matter of the village of Merrow's Bend, Kestria," he read, "administrative order 114-C, dated the ninth of October, 1994."

He read the order. All of it. The authorization. The logistics—the trucks, the fuel allotment, the disposal instructions written in the bland language of men who burn villages between lunch and supper. And then the signature line, and he read that too, slowly, twice:

"Aldous Reeve Crane, Governor of the Territory."

In the gallery, a woman began to cry. In the doorway, a man repeated the name to the man beside him, and that man passed it backward, out the doors, down the steps, into the dark, and Noor heard it come back a moment later from a hundred throats in the plaza outside, the way a wave comes back off a seawall.

Cyrus read the second document. The third. Sela Ivo's testimony he read standing closer to the lamps, and where the water had taken the ink he said, clearly, "*here the flood has taken words, but the hand is hers, and the hand remembers*," and read on around the gaps.

When he finished, the hall was silent for three full seconds—an eternity, in that building, in that year.

Then Rook spoke from the bench, formally, in the old cadences, and a clerk wrote it down in a ledger that would survive or die with the rest of them:

"Let the record show that on this date, in open session, the destruction of the village of Merrow's Bend was entered into the record of this court, with supporting documents, witnessed by"—he paused, looking out at the packed gallery, the drenched crowd on the steps, the journalists writing as fast as their hands could move—"witnessed by the people."

He struck the bench with his palm, having no gavel, and the sound went through the hall like a shot.

"So entered. So witnessed. Let no one in this territory claim, from this hour forward, that they did not know."

* * *

Afterward there was chaos, and it was good chaos—clerks making certified copies by lamplight, journalists arguing over who got which document, the crowd outside singing something old that Noor didn't know and felt in her chest anyway. Cyrus found her by the doors, wrung out and lit up, ten years falling off him by the minute.

"You did it," he said. "We did it. It's in the record. It can't be unread."

Noor was about to answer when the singing outside changed key.

It didn't stop—it curdled. Voices dropped, scattered, reformed into shouts, and through the doors she saw torchlight moving on the far side of the plaza. Not the crowd's torches. These moved in formation, in ranks, with the discipline of men following orders, and at their head rode a black government truck with the territorial seal on its door.

Crane's men. Not four arsonists with jerricans this time. A platoon, maybe more, fanning out around the courthouse with the unhurried confidence of soldiers who know the order has already been signed.

Rook appeared at her shoulder, following her gaze, and Noor heard the old man exhale slowly, almost peacefully.

"Well," he said. "So entered, so witnessed. Now we learn whether it was enough."

The first rifle butt hit the doors as the lights inside flickered and died.
