## 21. After the Water Falls

The rain stopped on a Tuesday, and nobody believed it.

For six weeks the monsoon had owned the sky, and when it finally let go—first in grudging drizzle, then all at once, like a hand opening—the whole capital came out into the streets blinking, squinting, half-suspicious of the light. Noor stood on the tarmac of what was left of Ashgrove Field and watched water sheet off the hangar roof in bright ropes and thought: *I should be flying right now.* Then she remembered she didn't have a plane, and laughed out loud at herself, alone, which was becoming a habit.

The airstrip was a wound that was learning to close. The flood had taken the east apron entirely, peeled the fuel dock off its pilings and set it down forty meters away in a cow pasture, upright, absurdly intact. Her shack was gone—she'd burned it herself, one decoy chest inside it, but the flood finished the job with the rest—and where it had stood there was now a rectangle of clean mud and, inexplicably, someone's rowboat. The runway itself had survived, mostly, because her father had built it high and crowned it for drainage, back when he still believed his daughter would inherit more than debts.

She walked the length of it every morning anyway. Measured nothing. Just walked.

"You're going to wear a groove," Cyrus said from behind her.

He came up the strip most mornings now, from the boarding house in town where Rook's office had stashed him until the trials settled down enough for him to have an address that wasn't a state secret. He carried two cups of coffee, which was how she'd learned to tell he was staying longer than politeness required. One cup meant courtesy. Two meant intent.

"Grooves are useful," she said, taking the cup. "Pilots land in grooves."

"That's ruts."

"Grooves."

They stood shoulder to shoulder and watched the morning burn the mist off the far tree line. Somewhere beyond it, the river ran lower every day, giving back what it had borrowed—fences, a chapel bell, three kilometers of somebody's orchard, drowned and then un-drowned as if nothing had happened. Rivers were like that. They kept what they were given only as long as it suited them.

"Rook called," Cyrus said. "The preliminary hearings start Thursday. Crane's counsel filed another motion to suppress."

"Of course they did."

"It won't work. There are four hundred pages entered into the record now, plus Sela's statement—" He stopped. Corrected himself, gently, the way you'd set down something breakable. "Plus the transcript of Sela's testimony. Plus Hale."

"How is he?"

"Awake today. That's new." Cyrus turned his cup in his hands. "The surgeons say he'll walk again by spring. Whether he'll ever walk into a courtroom is another question. He says he wants to testify standing up."

Noor snorted. "Of course he does."

"He also asked about you. Specifically." A pause. "He wanted to know if you'd gotten another plane yet."

"No."

"He said to tell you that you will. He said—" Cyrus's mouth did something complicated, not quite a smile. "He said the river doesn't get to keep everything."

* * *

The memorial was in Merrow's Bend. Or rather, it was where Merrow's Bend had been, which was a different thing entirely, and everyone who attended understood the difference without anyone having to say it.

There was no village anymore. There hadn't been for twenty years—just the river bend, the terraces gone soft and green with neglect, a few foundation stones surfacing in the dry season like knuckles through skin. But the government, the *new* government, the interim council that Rook was quietly holding together with wire and stubbornness, had voted to put up a stone. And so on a bright cold morning in the first week after the rains, maybe two hundred people stood in a field that used to be a street and listened to names read aloud.

Sela Ivo's name was third from the end. Not among the dead—the dead had their own list, read by a young woman whose grandmother had died in the burning—but on a second list, a shorter list, the list of witnesses. *Survivor. Keeper of testimony.* When the reader said her name, an old woman near the front raised her hand, and Noor realized with a jolt that it was Sela herself, brought up from the coast in a wheelchair, wrapped in wool despite the sun, her hearing aids new and useless against the wind. She couldn't hear her own name spoken. She felt it instead, Noor thought—felt the shape of the crowd go still around it.

Cyrus had arranged the wheelchair. Cyrus had argued with three ministries and threatened two. Cyrus stood beside her now with his hand resting on the chair's push-handle, and when Sela reached up and patted his wrist twice—a deaf woman's applause—he bowed his head like a man receiving something he hadn't earned and intended to spend the rest of his life repaying.

Afterward, Noor found him apart from the crowd, at the edge of the field where the ground dropped toward the water.

"She stayed," he said, without turning. "Twenty years. Ten kilometers from here, growing herbs, and she never left. She said—" He exhaled. "She said someone had to stay close enough to remember where things had been. In case anyone ever came asking."

"And we did."

"We did." He looked at her then, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't pretend otherwise, which was one of the thousand small things she was still learning about him—that he had spent ten years practicing honesty and had gotten very good at it, too late for some people and just in time for others. "Thank you. For Wrenfall. For insisting on the detour. I would have—" His jaw worked. "I would have gone straight to Rook. I'd have delivered the documents and missed the point of them entirely."

"The documents are people," Noor said. "You taught me that. Or the church did. Or the river." She shrugged. "One of them."

Below them, the bend turned slow and silver in the sun, keeping its own counsel.

* * *

Hale testified on the ninth day of hearings, and the city stopped to listen.

Noor watched from the public gallery, wedged between a journalist from the coastal papers and a woman who turned out to be a Merrow's Bend descendant and gripped Noor's arm through the entire session without noticing she was doing it. Hale sat—not stood, though Noor heard him argue about it for twenty minutes with his doctors—in a wheelchair at the witness stand, his leg in a cast from the bullet wound and the fall into the flooded archive annex, looking thinner than the courteous inspector who had once sat across her table asking questions a little too precisely.

But his voice was the same voice. Precise. Courteous. Devastating.

He walked them through it the way he must have walked Crane through it, once: the chain of custody of the orders, the signature he'd verified three times, the second document from Sela's wall, the false report he'd filed, the resignation letter dated the day before the shooting. Crane's lawyers tried four times to break him and failed four times, and on the fourth failure something moved through the gallery like weather—not triumph, exactly. Something quieter. The sound of a country remembering it had a spine.

Then Crane's lead counsel made his mistake. He asked Hale why. Why turn, why throw away a career, a pension, the use of his leg for half a year—why, for a village he'd never seen and strangers he'd been sent to destroy.

Hale was quiet for a long moment. The courtroom leaned in.

"My brother wrote that name," he said finally. "Merrow's Bend. In his own hand, in a report he filed before any of us were born, before Crane signed what he signed. My brother tried to stop it and was reassigned and died believing no one would ever know. I spent my whole career telling myself the file was closed because it was empty." He looked directly at the defense table. "It wasn't empty. It was full. Someone had just decided we weren't allowed to look."

Noor's neighbor's grip went slack. Around Noor, people were crying without noise, the way you learn to cry in countries where grief has been illegal.

She slipped out during the recess. She'd heard enough, and there was a plane to think about—or the absence of one—and a man waiting outside the courthouse steps who always waited, these days, as if he wasn't sure yet whether he was allowed to simply arrive.

* * *

"I found one," Cyrus said.

They were at the airfield at dusk, walking the runway groove, because that was what they did now. It had become, without either of them naming it, a ritual: coffee, the strip, the long light, whatever each of them had carried through the day set down piece by piece between them like cargo being unloaded carefully, item by item, nothing dropped.

"Found one what?"

"A plane." He handed her a folded paper, torn from a trading gazette. "Ferry flight, out of Corvane. De Havilland turboprop, low hours, flood-damaged avionics—they're selling it cheap because nobody wants to recertify the panel. But you don't need the panel. You've never needed the panel. You flew your last one with a compass and stubbornness."

Noor read the listing twice. The price was insultingly good. The airframe was sound. The damage was cosmetic-plus, the kind of thing she could fix with parts from three junkyards and a favor owed by a man in Corvane who still owed her for a medevac in '09.

"It's not the same plane," she said.

"No."

"It won't know me."

Cyrus considered this with the seriousness he gave everything, which was one of the other thousand things. "Planes don't know people," he said. "People know planes. You'll teach it."

She folded the paper along its crease, unfolded it, folded it again. The evening was doing its gold-and-violet trick over the tree line, and the puddles on the apron held little pieces of sky, and somewhere out past the fence a frog had started up the monsoon's-retreat chorus, tentative, testing whether the season was really over.

"Cyrus." She said it to the horizon, because saying it to his face was going to require a different kind of instrument rating. "What are we doing?"

He didn't answer right away. She gave him credit for that—for not reaching for something smooth.

"I don't know," he said at last. "I've asked myself every morning for six weeks. I walk up this strip with two coffees and I ask myself. And the honest answer is I don't know what we are. I know what I did to you—I lied to you for months, and then I told you the worst true thing about myself and you didn't leave, and I still don't understand that. I know I'm not asking you to decide anything. I know I'll keep showing up with coffee either way." He turned to face her fully. "I'm unwilling to pretend otherwise. Whatever else. I'm done pretending. It's the one skill I've retired."

Noor laughed—short, surprised, real. "Retired."

"Shredded the license. Revoked."

She looked at him. Disgraced archivist, restored name, man who had drowned himself once in shame and clawed back up the riverbank of it, and who stood now on her broken airstrip in the last light with his hands in his pockets and his heart, apparently, hanging out where the weather could get at it.

"Here's what I know," she said slowly. "I spent fifteen years flying under the radar. Literally. Low passes, no flight plans, no names on manifests. Invisible was safe. Invisible was the whole strategy." She tapped the folded gazette page against her palm. "And then a chest fell out of a river and I haven't been invisible since, and the strange thing is—" She stopped. Started again. "The strange thing is I don't miss it. I keep waiting to miss it. The quiet. Nobody knowing my name. And it doesn't come."

"What comes instead?"

"This." She gestured—at the strip, the puddle-sky, him, all of it, inadequately. "Mornings. Coffee. Somebody who waits. It's terrifying. I recommend it to no one."

"Noted," Cyrus said gravely. "I'll warn the newspapers."

They walked on. The frogs got braver. Overhead, the first stars came out in the washed-clean dark, and Noor found herself navigating by them out of pure habit, triangulating her position on the earth, which was: here. Standing next to him. Unresolved and unwilling to resolve it by pretending, both of them, both stubborn as ballast, both staying.

At the hangar door she stopped.

"Thursday," she said. "When the hearings resume. Come with me?"

"I was going to."

"I know. Ask anyway. It matters that you ask."

He smiled—slow, unguarded, the smile of a man setting down a decade. "Come with me Thursday," he said. "To the hearings. And afterward, dinner. And afterward—" he hesitated, honest even in hope, "—afterward, whatever you want. Including nothing. Including just the walk home."

"Afterward," Noor said, "we talk about the plane in Corvane. Because if I'm going to be visible, Vane, I intend to be visible at altitude."

She went into the hangar to check on the tool crates she'd started inventorying that afternoon—parts lists forming in her head already, the Corvane favor, the ferry route, the first flight plan she'd file openly, under her own name, destination undecided and that being the whole point. Behind her, out on the strip, Cyrus stood a moment longer in the dark, watching the runway as if memorizing it.

And at the far end of the field, where the mud was still soft, something glinted—brass, river-bright, catching the starlight. She'd find it tomorrow, or the day after. The river, after all, doesn't get to keep everything.
