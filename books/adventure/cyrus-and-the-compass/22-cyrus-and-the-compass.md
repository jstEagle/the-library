## 22. Cyrus and the Compass

The airstrip smelled of cut grass and drying timber, and for the first time in twenty years of flying out of it, Noor Ashgrove noticed how much it smelled like beginning.

She'd rebuilt it herself—her hands, her money, her stubbornness—with help that kept arriving without being asked for. Marta's crew had hauled lumber upriver on two barges. The fishermen from the camps downstream had shown up with hammers and opinions. Even Sela Ivo had come, carried in a wheelchair by two of her grand-nephews, and sat in the shade of the new hangar all afternoon, watching the planes come and go with an expression Noor couldn't read until she realized what it was: recognition. The old woman was watching the sky the way you watch a face you thought you'd never see again.

Three weeks since the courthouse. Three weeks since Merrow's Bend had been read aloud into the record, name by name, while Crane's men hammered at the doors and the flood pressed against the windows like something trying to get in—or maybe trying to witness.

Noor stood at the edge of the strip now, morning light coming up gold behind the eastern hills, and watched Cyrus Vane walk toward her from the shack carrying something small in both hands.

He walked differently these days. She'd noticed it first at the memorial, when they'd read Sela's testimony back to her in writing because her hearing was gone but her eyes were sharp enough to catch every lie in the room. Cyrus used to move like a man apologizing for taking up space—shoulders drawn, eyes down, ten years of disgrace worn into his spine like a bend in a board. Now he moved like a man who had paid his debt and knew the exact amount. It wasn't confidence, exactly. It was more expensive than that.

"Morning," he said.

"You're up early."

"I slept." He said it like a report from a foreign country. "Actually slept. I'm not sure I remember how."

"The trials starting again?"

"Monday. Hale testifies Thursday." He looked out at the strip, at the two planes tied down against the weather, hers and the charter bird that belonged to nobody yet. "They've offered him a reduced sentence. He'll take it. He told me he intends to spend the rest of his life making sure no one ever has to appeal to a crack in their conscience again." A dry almost-smile. "His words. He's better on paper than in person."

"He's better wounded than whole," Noor said. "Most people are."

Cyrus turned his hands over. In his palms lay the compass.

She'd seen it before, of course—it had lived in the chest with the documents, wedged in a felt-lined recess like the one thing worth protecting above all the paper. Brass, Kestrian-made, its casing engraved with a maker's mark shaped like a heron standing in reeds. When they'd pried the chest open that first night in her shack, the needle hadn't pointed north. It had pointed east-northeast, steady as a sermon, and neither of them had understood why until Cyrus found the coordinates etched inside the lid and did the arithmetic with shaking fingers.

Merrow's Bend. The compass didn't point north. It pointed home—to a village that no longer existed, on a riverbend that had been plowed under and renamed, and it had been pointing there for twenty years, waiting in the dark of a flooded chest for someone to ask it the question.

"It came back from the evidence lockup yesterday," Cyrus said. "Rook signed the release himself. He said—" and here Cyrus's voice did the thing it did when he was quoting someone he respected, going quieter and more careful, "—he said some instruments aren't for navigation. They're for testimony."

Noor took it. The brass was warm from his hands, lighter than she remembered. The needle swung once, settled, and pointed east-northeast across the hills, across the territory, toward a bend in a river where a hundred and forty-one people had lived and been erased, and where last month, finally, a stone had been laid with all their names on it.

"It still points," she said.

"They built them well." Cyrus watched her hold it. "The Kestrians. Everything they made was built to outlast the people who wanted them gone. That was the whole culture, really. Persistence as craftsmanship."

"And we nearly lost it in the river anyway."

"We nearly lost everything in the river." He shrugged. "The river gave it back. That's the arrangement I've decided to believe in. You give the water your truth, eventually it gives some of it back. Not all. Never all. But enough."

Noor closed her hand around the compass and felt its edges press her palm—the same palm that had winched the chest out of the water on a storm-dark day, against her better judgment, because the wilderness had taught her that gifts from rivers are rarely free. This one had cost her a plane. An airstrip. A life built on invisibility, burned in a fuel drum outside a shack at four in the morning while the monsoon came down like a verdict.

She would make that trade again before the matches finished striking. She knew it the way she knew wind shear—not as a thought but as a fact in the body.

* * *

They spent the morning doing the small work of a rebuilt life. Noor torqued the engine mounts on the charter plane and let Cyrus hand her tools, which he did competently now—he'd learned the names of things during the weeks of rebuilding, the way he learned everything, hungrily and completely. She showed him how to read the propeller's wear pattern. He showed her a letter from the restored national archive offering him a position as chief conservator, and watched her face carefully while she read it.

"You should take it," she said.

"I haven't decided."

"It's everything you wanted. Your name cleared, your work back, the archive itself."

"It's everything I wanted *before*," he said. "Before I knew what I wanted it for." He took the letter back and folded it along its crease, precise as an archivist. "I spent ten years chasing this truth alone in fishing camps and borrowed rooms, and do you know what I actually missed? Not the archive. Not the work." He looked at her. "I missed having someone to tell things to. Someone who'd understand why a document matters. Why a name matters."

Noor wiped her hands on a rag and didn't look away. "And now?"

"Now I have a compass to give you and a speech I've been rehearsing badly for three days, and I'd rather just give you the compass."

"You already gave me the compass."

"No." He shook his head. "I put it in your hand. Giving it is different. It comes with conditions."

That made her laugh—a real one, surprised out of her. "Conditions. From the man who falsified an archive entry."

"Especially from him. I know exactly how much a condition is worth." He held up one finger. "One: you never fly without it. Two: you never fly *toward* it without telling me. And three—" he paused, and the careful archivist's precision cracked open into something younger and rawer underneath, "—three: when you land wherever you land, you say the names out loud. All of them if you can. One of them if that's all you have. That's the whole instrument, Noor. It doesn't navigate. It remembers. I need to know it's in a cockpit with someone who understands the difference."

The wind came down the valley and moved the grass in long silver waves, and somewhere beyond the hills the rivers were still running high with the monsoon's leavings, carrying whatever the water had been given toward whatever waited downstream.

Noor looked at the compass in her hand. Then at Cyrus Vane, who had drowned once, in every way a man could drown without dying, and who had swum ten years upstream to stand in front of her in the morning sun asking for three impossible conditions.

"One amendment," she said.

He braced. She could see him brace, the old reflex, expecting terms to turn against him.

"I accept one and three. Two I'm changing." She held up the compass between them, needle trembling east-northeast. "I'm not flying toward it without you. That's not a condition. That's a flight plan."

* * *

The tower at the capital field still had a cracked window from the riots, boarded over with plywood stenciled COURTESY OF THE TRANSITIONAL AUTHORITY, which everyone in the queue found funnier than the Authority intended. Noor stood at the filing counter with the clipboard in her hand and wrote, in her own handwriting, block letters, no ambiguity:

AIRCRAFT: ASHGROVE AIR, CESSNA 206, REGISTRATION PENDING REISSUE.
PILOT: NOOR ASHGROVE.
DESTINATION: MERROW'S BEND MEMORIAL SITE, FORMER KESTRIA PROVINCE.
PURPOSE OF FLIGHT: COMMEMORATION.
PASSENGERS: ONE (1).

The clerk—a young woman with ink-stained fingers who had probably been a courier during the hearings, everyone young in the capital had been a courier or a witness or both—read the destination line twice.

"Merrow's Bend," she said. "There's no airfield at Merrow's Bend."

"There's a gravel bar above the bend that dries out by mid-morning," Noor said. "I've landed on worse."

"But there's nothing *there*. Just the memorial. The stone."

"That's the purpose of flight," Noor said, and tapped the line, and the clerk stamped it because the whole country had learned in the last month what happened when you refused to stamp a true thing.

Cyrus was waiting by the plane with two bags—one his, one hers—and the particular stillness of a man about to fly for the first time in a decade. He'd confessed it to her two nights ago, embarrassed: he hadn't been aloft since before the disgrace. Ten years of watching planes pass over the fishing camp and tracking each one by engine sound, cataloguing them the way he catalogued everything, aching in a place he didn't have vocabulary for.

"Nervous?" she asked, walking up.

"Terrified," he said cheerfully. "In the useful way."

"Good. The useless kind makes passengers grab the yoke."

They loaded the bags. She ran the preflight with him shadowing her, touching each surface when she touched it, and when she got to the compass she mounted it—not on the panel, not clipped to the visor, but in a small bracket she'd welded to the base of the windshield itself, dead center, where the pilot and the passenger and the sky ahead could all see it. The needle settled and pointed east-northeast, out through the glass, toward the hills and the river and the bend where a nation that no longer existed had left one last instrument telling the truth.

"Passenger briefing," Noor said, settling into the left seat. "Seatbelt tight. Door latched. If we go down, the documents stay with the plane and we swim for the bar—there's nothing on this aircraft anyone needs to drown for anymore. We already delivered them." She looked over at him. "Questions?"

"Just one." Cyrus fastened his belt. His hands were steady. "Why here? Why the windshield? Most pilots would keep it in a pocket."

Noor took the brakes off and let the plane start to roll, feeling the gravel hum under the tires, feeling the old familiar lift of the tail as the airflow found it—home, all of it, home even after everything, especially after everything.

"Because I spent twenty years flying to stay invisible," she said, and pushed the throttle forward, and the runway poured away beneath them. "I flew low, I filed vague plans, I landed where nobody was looking. It kept me safe. It also kept me silent, and I told myself silence was the price of survival out here, and maybe it was, for a while." The end of the strip came up and she pulled back gently and the ground let go, the way it always did, the way she'd never stopped loving it. "But the river gave me a chest, and the chest gave me a name, and the name gave me a hundred and forty-one more, and I am done pretending the sky is empty."

Below them the territory unrolled—flooded fields shining like hammered tin, the rivers braided and brown and patient, smoke rising from villages where people were rebuilding, always rebuilding, the way water always returned. The compass held its bearing. East-northeast. Toward the bend. Toward the stone with the names.

"So today," Noor said, keying the radio, filing her voice into the air where anyone could hear it, "we fly loud, we fly visible, and we file the plan in our own names. First passenger: Cyrus Vane, restored archivist. Destination: Merrow's Bend. Purpose: commemoration." She glanced at him and found him watching her with an expression she intended to spend a long time learning. "Say it with me, archivist. Out loud. That's condition three."

Cyrus Vane smiled—whole, unhidden, ten years of shame finally set down—and spoke the village's name into the roar of the engine and the open sky, and Noor Ashgrove turned the plane onto the compass heading and flew toward memory, not away from it, as the morning opened wide beneath her wings.

Behind them, far to the south, the transitional government convened its second week of hearings, and Inspector Hale's wheelchair waited at the witness entrance, and somewhere in a cell Aldous Crane began drafting the statement he would refuse to read aloud.

But that was downstream. That was later.

Ahead was the bend, and the stone, and the names—and a compass that had waited twenty years in the dark to point the way.
