## 1. The Closed Lines

The trouble with mapping dead railroads, Emrys Yarrow had learned, was that the dead railroads didn't know they were dead.

They kept behaving like living things. The Kettle Creek spur, abandoned eleven years now, had grown a personality in its retirement—sullen in the shade of the cut, cheerful where the sun came through and warmed the rails until they ticked like a cooling kettle. He'd been walking it since dawn with his chain and his level and his battered leather folio, and by noon he had filled three pages of notes in a hand so small and precise it looked like it had been typeset.

*Ballast heaved at Mile 4.2, frost damage. Culvert at 4.6 holding. Birch through the deck at 5.1—two trunks, thick as a man's wrist. Nobody's coming. Nobody needs to come.*

That last line was the whole point of the exercise, though he'd never have admitted it aloud. Fifteen years ago Emrys Yarrow had been the youngest chief surveyor the government corps had ever fielded, a man whose maps went into ledgers that decided where towns would live and die. Now he was a railroad engineer of no particular distinction, employed by the Meridian & Western to inspect track nobody rode, and every line he charted was a line already closed. He mapped the past. It was the safest profession ever devised: you could not falsify what had already happened, and no one could ask you to.

He ate his lunch sitting on the 5.1 culvert with his boots dangling over the birch trunks, and he allowed himself, as he did most days, a small private satisfaction. In fifteen years, not one person had sought him out for anything more urgent than a signature on an inspection form. He had become, by careful and deliberate degrees, a man the world had misplaced—and he treasured it the way other men treasured medals.

The dog found him first.

She came up the ballast slope at a trot, all ribs and purpose, a yellowish mongrel with one ear that stood and one that had clearly lost an argument sometime in her youth. She belonged to the telegraph office at Hobb's Crossing, four miles back down the line, and she had never once in six years walked this far. Emrys lowered his sandwich.

"No," he told her.

She sat down in front of him with the gravity of a debt collector.

"You're off your route."

Her tail moved once, like a pen signing something.

He knew what she carried before he took the canvas pouch off her collar. Telegraph dogs were for telegrams, and telegrams were for other people. In fifteen years the wires had never once reached down to touch him; he'd made sure of it, routing his reports through clerks who spelled his name wrong on the envelopes. *Yarow. Yarrowe.* Once, memorably, *Yallow,* which he'd framed in his mind and hung in an invisible hallway of honors.

The envelope said **YARROW** in letters that had been written slowly, by someone who wanted them legible from across a room.

He almost didn't open it. He sat there with the dog watching him and the creek muttering under the culvert, and he thought about all the versions of himself that had worked so hard to become unreachable, and how strange it was that a dog could undo fifteen years of engineering in a single afternoon.

Then he opened it.

---

*MR E YARROW STOP MERIDIAN EXPEDITION UPRIVER MISSED THIRD SCHEDULED RADIO CHECK STOP SPONSOR REQUESTS YOUR PRESENCE CALLOWS REACH WITHOUT DELAY STOP TRAVEL AND RETAINER ARRANGED STOP THIS COMES BY HIS DIRECT INSTRUCTION NOT MINE STOP*

*CRANE*

Emrys read it twice. Then a third time, standing up now, because some messages are easier to absorb on your feet, the way bad news is easier to carry if you're already walking somewhere.

Three missed checks. The Meridian Expedition—the survey Crane had been crowing about for two years at every dinner party within wire-range of the capital, the one going upriver into the Escarpment country to chart a route nobody had ever needed or wanted. Twelve people, best part of a season's provisions, a radio set worth more than the boat that carried it. And three silences in a row, which in expedition terms wasn't worry yet but was certainly the anteroom to it.

*Your presence without delay.*

Why him? That was the question he turned over while he packed his instruments with hands that were steadier than he felt. There were surveyors closer to Callow's Reach by half. There were rescue men, river men, men whose entire trade was going places where other men had stopped answering their radios. Aldous Crane could buy any of them the way other men bought hats.

But Crane had asked for him specifically. And Crane, whatever else he was—a patron, a showman, a man who wore his wealth the way a general wears campaign ribbons—was not careless. If Aldous Crane wanted Emrys Yarrow, it was because Emrys Yarrow knew something about that stretch of river that no one else did.

Or because he owed him.

Both possibilities sat in Emrys's chest like stones in a culvert, and neither one shifted when he prodded it.

* * *

Hobb's Crossing at dusk was a water tower, a depot, a telegraph office, and a saloon that served as all three in emergencies. The operator, a round young man named Pell who treated the telegraph key with the tenderness of a musician, was waiting for him on the platform with the expression of someone about to deliver a second blow.

"Answered already, Mr. Yarrow," Pell said. "Before you even got here. Wire came in an hour after I sent yours." He handed over a slip of paper with the air of a man passing along a live coal.

*ACKNOWLEDGED STOP CAR WILL BE AT HOBB'S CROSSING 0600 STOP DO NOT DISCUSS THIS WIRE WITH ANYONE STOP C*

"Do not discuss," Emrys murmured. "Of course."

"He pays our salaries," Pell said, slightly wounded. "Half the county's salaries, near enough. You know what they say about Mr. Crane—if he sneezes in Callow's Reach, the harbor files a weather report."

"I've heard."

"And—" Pell hesitated, then leaned in, lowering his voice as though the wires themselves might be listening. "There was a second message. Private line, coded, before yours even went out. I don't read coded traffic, Mr. Yarrow, I want that understood, but—" he glanced over his shoulder at the empty tracks, at the water tower standing black against the last light, "—it was long. Longer than anything I've handled from Callow's Reach. And the return address on the acknowledgment wasn't Crane House."

"No?"

"It was a woman's name. Voss." Pell pronounced it carefully, having clearly rehearsed. "Sera Voss. Cartographer, it said, second-class, attached to the Meridian Expedition." He shrugged, embarrassed. "Probably nothing. Probably just the sponsor's clerk."

Emrys stood very still on the platform.

The name went through him the way frost goes through ballast—not loudly, not all at once, but with a deep structural shifting that he would be finding cracks in for weeks. *Sera Voss.* A name he had spent fifteen years not saying, which is a full-time occupation, and one he had gotten very good at. He had a whole interior architecture built around its absence: certain cities avoided, certain rivers untraveled, one entire decade of correspondence left unanswered on principle.

And now the name had arrived by wire, ahead of him, riding the same private line as Crane's orders, attached to an expedition gone silent in the Escarpment.

"That'll be all, Pell," he heard himself say. His voice came out level, which he counted as his finest professional achievement of the day. "Thank you for the dog."

"She found you, then."

"She found me."

* * *

He didn't sleep much. He sat in his rooms above the depot with his folio open on the table, and instead of packing—which was the sensible thing, and Emrys was a sensible man—he spent the night doing something he hadn't done in fifteen years.

He pulled out the old maps.

Not the inspection charts. The others. The ones he kept flat in a drawer beneath a false bottom that had fooled exactly no one, because the only visitor his rooms ever received was Pell, and Pell thought the false bottom was for keeping mice out. Survey maps of the Escarpment country, drawn in his own hand when he was twenty-nine years old and certain the world ran on rails and reason. River surveys. Gorge profiles. And there—one sheet, soft at the folds from being consulted too often in a former life—a line running up the east bank of the Meridian headwaters, a spur route he had charted and then personally recommended for closure, and the closure had been granted, and he had signed it, and he had never told anyone why.

He looked at that line for a long time.

Outside, the night train went through without stopping, four cars and a caboose, headed nowhere he'd ever been asked to follow. The lamp hissed. The dog—he'd let her in, because it was cold and because she had, in her terrible way, earned it—slept under the table with her good ear cocked even in sleep, listening for the next thing coming down the wire.

At 0600 the car came, as promised, a long black machine with a driver who did not give his name and did not need to. Emrys locked his rooms, put the old maps in his folio where the new ones usually lived, and stood a moment on the platform in the gray light with his breath steaming.

Fifteen years of invisibility, he thought. Fifteen years of closed lines and misspelled envelopes and a life so quiet you could hear the birches growing through the culverts. All of it balanced, apparently, on the assumption that the world would keep its side of the bargain and leave him be.

The telegram had ended that. Whatever waited in Callow's Reach—an old debt, an old sponsor, a name on a private wire—it had already reached down the line and found him, the way the dog had, with purpose and no respect for routes.

He got in the car.

The driver pulled away from Hobb's Crossing without a word, and behind them the telegraph office woke and began, faintly, to click.
