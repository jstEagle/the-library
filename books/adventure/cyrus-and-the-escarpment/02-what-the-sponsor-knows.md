## 2. What the Sponsor Knows

Callow's Reach smelled the way port cities always did: tar, fish, and money changing hands too quickly to be honest. Emrys Yarrow stepped off the coastal steamer with a single canvas bag, a hat that had seen better decades, and the deep, bone-level irritation of a man who had spent fifteen years perfecting his invisibility only to have it yanked off like a tablecloth.

He had told himself, all the way down from the hills, that he would take the meeting, decline politely, and be on the next boat home by dark. He had even rehearsed the refusal. *I'm flattered, Mr. Crane, but I map dead lines for a living because I prefer the company of the dead to the living.* It was a good line. He'd been polishing it for two days.

The Crane offices stood on Harborside Row behind a façade of pale stone that pretended it wasn't showing off. A clerk with the posture of a fence post took Emrys's name, vanished, and returned so fast it was almost insulting.

"Mr. Crane will see you now."

"He knew I'd come?"

"Mr. Crane," said the fence post, "knows most things."

* * *

Aldous Crane was not what Emrys expected, though he couldn't have said what he'd expected instead. The man behind the desk was sixty-odd, silver at the temples, dressed well but not expensively—a deliberate choice, Emrys suspected, the way some men chose their boots. He had the kind of face that made you want to tell him your troubles, which in Emrys's experience was either a gift or a weapon depending on how it was used.

"Yarrow." Crane rose and offered his hand. His grip was dry and firm, a surveyor's grip. "Fifteen years. You look—"

"Older."

"I was going to say *unfound*. Which is rarer." Crane gestured to a chair. "Sit. Drink? It's barely noon, but I find expeditions excuse most vices."

"No, thank you." Emrys sat, keeping the chair's back between himself and the window out of pure habit. Old habits were the only kind he owned anymore.

Crane sat too, laced his fingers, and studied him with an openness that was itself a kind of strategy. "You got my telegram."

"Three missed radio checks. That's not much of a telegram."

"It's everything I have. Meridian checks in every Tuesday and Friday, eight o'clock, like church. They've missed three. The last transmission was fragments—interference, then nothing." Crane's voice stayed level, but something moved behind his eyes, a current under ice. "I've sent two boats upriver already. Neither came back. Not lost—*came back*. There's a difference, and you'll understand it when you see the gorge."

Emrys said nothing. Silence was a tool; he kept his sharpened.

"You're wondering why I asked for you specifically." Crane smiled faintly. "Because you're wondering it very loudly, and I'd rather answer than let you guess wrong. Fifteen years ago you walked out of the government survey corps over a matter I won't pretend to know the details of—I know only that you refused something, and that refusing it cost you everything, and that you've never once tried to buy any of it back. Men who can't be bought back are rare, Yarrow. Rarer than unfound ones."

"That's a flattering way to describe stubbornness."

"It's the only accurate one." Crane leaned forward. "I need someone to go upriver, find my expedition, and bring them—or word of them—back. I need someone who knows closed lines, because the route they took follows old ones. And I need someone whose loyalty isn't for sale, because—" he paused, choosing the words with visible care, "—because I don't entirely trust everyone I've hired, and I'm telling you that plainly, which should tell you how badly I want them found."

"And the pay?"

"Triple whatever figure you're about to name, plus expenses, plus a bonus on return that will make triple look modest."

Emrys looked at him for a long moment. "You're paying me not to ask questions."

"I'm paying you to find people. Questions are extra."

Despite himself—the man was funny. That was the trap of Aldous Crane, Emrys began to suspect. He made you like him first.

* * *

They walked the terms down together through the afternoon, and it was nearly evening before Crane produced the envelope.

It was heavy cream paper, sealed with plain wax, and he set it on the desk between them the way a dealer sets down a card he doesn't want you to read.

"Instructions," Crane said. "For the camp. You are forbidden to open this until you've found the Meridian party alive or accounted for. Not before. Swear it however you like—I only ask that you mean it."

"Why sealed? Why not just tell me?"

"Because what's in it concerns the expedition more than it concerns you, and because I've learned that instructions travel better on paper than in memory. Memory edits itself. Paper only burns." He slid it across. "Take it."

Emrys took it.

And stopped.

The address on the front was written in a hand he knew. Knew the way you know a voice through a wall—half-remembered, wholly unmistakable. The capital S with its small impatient flourish. The V that ended in a hook, as if the letter had somewhere better to be. He had watched that hand draw contour lines across three years of shared maps, had watched it label rivers and annotate margins with jokes only he ever read. He had watched it write one letter he'd never answered, and then never watched it again.

*Sera Voss.*

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms do when your own pulse gets loud enough to fill them.

Crane was watching him. Of course he was. Emrys had the sudden, cold clarity of a man realizing the card game started before he sat down. "You knew," Emrys said. His voice came out flat and steady, which he counted as a personal triumph. "You knew she was on the expedition. You knew we—"

"I knew she recommended you." Crane's tone didn't change, didn't soften, didn't apologize. "When I asked her last spring who could be trusted to navigate the closed lines if anything went wrong, she gave me one name. Yours. She wrote it on a scrap of paper and wouldn't discuss it further. So no, Mr. Yarrow—I didn't know what you were to each other. But I knew what you were to *her*, which may be worth more."

Emrys turned the envelope over once, twice, as if the back might explain the front. Her handwriting. On orders he was forbidden to open. Addressed through a stranger's hands, traveling toward him across fifteen years of silence like a message in a bottle that had taken the long way around every continent.

"She could have written to me directly," he said. "Any time. In fifteen years."

"Could she?" Crane asked gently. "Could you?"

That landed where such things land, and Emrys let it sit there rather than dignify it.

"The rest of the details," Crane continued, mercifully returning to business, "are in the packet your boat captain holds. Departure tomorrow, dawn tide. The gorge begins forty miles upriver; past the second bend you'll want to follow the old north bank spur—it's decommissioned, which I'm told is your specialty. Yarrow." He paused, and for a moment the charm thinned and something older showed underneath, something tired. "Bring them back. All of them. Or bring me the truth about why I can't. Either of those I can live with. What I can't live with is another boat that simply doesn't come home."

* * *

Emrys walked the harbor after dark, because hotels had walls and walls had opinions, and he needed to think where nobody could watch him do it.

Lamps swung on their moorings along the quay. Somewhere a crew sang badly about a woman in Valparaíso. Gulls argued over something unspeakable near the fish market. And in his coat pocket, wrapped in oilcloth against the spray, the envelope rode against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

He took it out under a lamp. Just to look. Looking wasn't opening.

*Sera Voss* — no. The address was in her hand, but the direction line read simply: *For the engineer, at camp.* She hadn't put his name. She'd put his *trade*, which was somehow worse, or better—he couldn't decide which. It meant she'd known exactly how the envelope would reach him and had chosen, deliberately, to sign herself as function rather than feeling. Cartographer to engineer. Professional courtesy across a fifteen-year grave.

Or it meant she'd been afraid to write his name. He remembered that about her—remembered how fear made her precise. When her brother Tomas fell sick with river fever and she'd spent three nights dosing him, she'd redrawn an entire county map afterward, every letter identical, every line exact, because precision was where she put the things she couldn't say.

He wondered, walking the long curve of the quay, whether she was alive. Whether the missing radio checks meant fever, flood, rockfall, or men. Whether the sealed instructions were a plea or a warning—and if a warning, whether it was meant for Crane's eyes or for his.

He wondered, less usefully, whether she still took her coffee bitter and her jokes dry, whether she still hummed when she inked, whether she'd ever forgiven him. He suspected the answer to the last one was *no*, and suspected further that she'd have redrawn the whole question in perfect letters rather than admit it mattered.

The gull screamed. The bad singers reached a chorus. Emrys stood under the lamp a while longer, turning the envelope like a compass needle that couldn't decide on north.

Then he tucked it away, went back to his hotel, and did the thing he always did when a problem wouldn't resolve: he drew. On hotel stationery, from memory, he sketched the upper river—the gorge, the bends, the old north bank spur, the escarpment beyond it that appeared on official maps as blank green space labeled, in the government's cheerful fiction, *unsurveyed*.

Fifteen years ago he'd left the corps because powerful men wanted maps that lied, and he'd decided the safest life was one spent mapping places no one would ever fight over.

Now the place no one fought over had swallowed an expedition, and the envelope in his coat was written in the one hand that could still make him break every rule he'd built his quiet life around.

Dawn tide, Crane had said.

Emrys blew out the lamp, lay down fully clothed, and did not sleep at all—which was fine. He'd done his best work on no sleep, and besides, he had a suspicion, settling over him cold and certain as river fog, that whatever was waiting upriver, Aldous Crane knew a great deal more about it than three missed radio checks.

And that Sera Voss had known it first.
