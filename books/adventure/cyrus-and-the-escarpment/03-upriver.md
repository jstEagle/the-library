## 3. Upriver

The boatman's name was Pell, and he charged triple what the crossing was worth and told Emrys so twice before they'd even cast off.

"Triple," he said again, spitting into the brown water with the reverence of a man blessing it. "Because nobody goes upriver past the Narrows in the wet season except fools and surveyors, and the surveyors stopped coming back."

"I'm a railroad engineer," Emrys said.

"That's worse." Pell considered him over the rail of his flat-bottomed boat, a craft that had been patched so many times it was less wood than memory. "Engineers want to *change* things. Surveyors just want to look at them."

Emrys let that stand. He had spent fifteen years wanting nothing to change at all — wanting the world to stay exactly as his maps had it, every closed line closed, every abandoned spur sleeping under its blanket of brush. It occurred to him, as the gorge walls began to lean in on either side like two arguments converging, that he had never once asked whether the world agreed to the arrangement.

The river narrowed. The light went green and underwater-colored, filtered through birch and hemlock crowding down to the waterline. Somewhere above them, out of sight, a hawk complained about something. Pell hummed a tuneless song about a drowned wife, which he claimed was funny if you knew her.

They found the first cache on the second day, wedged in a rock cleft where the expedition's route left the river for an old portage trail. Emrys knew it was theirs by the stenciling: MERIDIAN EXPEDITION, CRANE TRUST, in letters so official they practically wore little hats.

The crate had been opened from the inside.

Not broken into — opened. The lid was pried up neatly from within, the nails set aside in a tidy row, as though whatever had been packed in there had grown tired of the dark and let itself out. Inside: tins of salt beef, all of them empty but unopened, their contents gone without a single puncture in the metal. A coil of signal wire, unwound and draped over the rocks like bunting after a parade nobody attended.

Pell looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked at Emrys.

"Bears don't wind wire," he said.

"No," Emrys agreed. "Bears don't."

He said nothing else, because saying anything else would have meant admitting what he already suspected: that the expedition hadn't been taken by the river or the country or bad luck. Something had happened to those people that made them leave their own supplies behind, carefully, deliberately, the way you pack when you mean to come back — or the way you leave things when you're running and can't afford the weight.

That night, moored against a gravel bar with the fire kept small, Emrys took out Sera Voss's journal.

It wasn't hers yet, not exactly. Crane had given him a copy of the expedition's field log — "for orientation," Crane had said, in the tone of a man handing you a loaded gun and calling it reading material — and the first third was ordinary enough. Soundings. Bearings. Notes on current speed and rock type. But folded into the back, copied in a different hand, were pages recovered from one of the lost caches. Pages in Sera's handwriting.

Emrys knew that handwriting the way you know a voice through a wall. Fifteen years, and the *f* still crossed too high, the numerals still leaned eastward like they were hurrying somewhere.

*Day 9,* she had written. *Tomas ate the last of the good coffee and claims it was an accident. Okonkwo has named a rapid after him in retaliation. We are calling it Voss's Folly and Tomas is furious, which means it will stick.*

Emrys laughed out loud, alone on a gravel bar in the middle of nowhere, and the laugh hurt going down. That was the trouble with Sera Voss. Even her field notes were funnier than most people's letters home.

*Day 11. Rennick has begun asking odd questions about what we intend to do with our findings. I told him we intend to do what surveyors always do: write it down accurately. He looked at me the way a man looks at a bridge he's planning to burn.*

The humor thinned as the pages turned. He read on into the night, feeding the fire sticks he didn't need, because putting the journal down felt like hanging up on her mid-sentence.

*Day 14. Strange business today. Our soundings show the river bottom dropping away beneath us — forty feet, then sixty, then nothing. The lead line runs out. Okonkwo says it's a drowned channel, some old course of the river. But the echo off the walls is wrong. Too hollow. Like shouting down a stairwell.*

*Day 15. Found a cut stone in the bank this morning, dressed and squared, half-buried in silt. There is no quarry within eighty miles of here. Tomas wants to dig. Rennick wants to move on. I want to know who dressed that stone, and why they did it beside a river that isn't on any map older than thirty years.*

*Day 16. The river makes no sense. I have charted water my whole life and I am telling you: the current runs uphill for a quarter mile below the second bend. Not an eddy. Not a seiche. Uphill, steady, like it's being drawn. Okonkwo took the bearing three times and then sat down on a rock and didn't speak for an hour.*

Emrys read that entry twice. He was a man who trusted geography the way other men trusted God — quietly, constantly, without thinking much about it until it failed. A river running uphill was not a mystery. It was a *typo*. The world did not make typos.

Unless, said a small cold voice in the back of his head, the world underneath it was doing the typing.

* * *

On the fourth day they passed the Narrows, and the gorge stopped being scenery and became architecture. Walls of gray stone rose sheer on either side, close enough in places that Pell could touch both banks with his poles, and the sky shrank to a bright ribbon overhead. The air came up cool from the water, smelling of wet iron.

"There's a tunnel mouth ahead," Pell said. "Old one. Don't know whose. My father said don't go near it, and his father said the same, and between them they never once said *why*, which in my family is how you know a thing is serious."

It appeared around the next bend exactly as promised: a black arch cut into the north wall of the gorge, its rim dressed in squared stone, tall enough to drive a locomotive through. Which, Emrys realized with a lurch, was precisely what it had been built for.

He knew this line.

Not this exact mile — but this line. It was on his maps, the ones no one else had ever seen, the decommissioned spurs and forgotten branches he'd spent fifteen years walking and measuring and keeping alive on paper long after the companies had written them dead. This was the Kestrel Branch, abandoned in his grandfather's time, officially "never constructed" according to the surveys of record. Officially a rumor. Officially nothing.

Except here it was, bored straight through the escarpment's shoulder, its masonry older than any railroad charter Emrys had ever read. The tooling marks on the stone weren't drill marks. They were chisel marks. Broad, patient, hand-cut chisel marks, the kind you find on cathedral foundations and prison walls.

"Pell," he said. "How far to the next landing?"

"Half a day, if the water behaves."

"The water's not going to behave, is it."

Pell grinned with genuine pleasure. "No."

* * *

They made the landing by dusk, a shelf of gravel below a talus slope where the expedition had cached its heavier gear. Or had cached it. The second crate stood open under a lean-to of branches, lid pried up from inside, nails in their tidy row. Emrys went through it with a lamp and found the same impossible tidiness: rations untouched, tools racked, a tin of matches full to the brim. And tucked beneath the false bottom, where no one would have hidden anything they wanted found, another bundle of journal pages.

Sera's hand again. Later days. The entries had changed.

*Day 19. We found the door. Not a figure of speech — a door, iron-strapped, set into the tunnel wall a quarter mile inside the arch. Behind it, stairs. Behind the stairs —*

She had crossed something out. Hard, twice-over strokes, the pen pressed hard enough to emboss the next page. Whatever she'd written, she'd decided the record shouldn't carry it. Emrys held the page to the lamp and read the ghost of it anyway, the way you can sometimes read a letter through an envelope if you want it badly enough.

Three words survived the ink. *They were people.*

*Day 20. Okonkwo says the tunnels predate everything. The shackles we found are colonial pattern, but the walls they're bolted to are older than the colony. Someone built down here before there was a here to build in. Rennick has stopped pretending to be bored. He counts everything we touch. Yesterday I caught him counting us.*

*Day 21. The map is wrong. My map. I've redrawn the river three times and it won't sit right, because the tunnels keep intersecting it. There are passages under the channel — I've heard the men walking on the far side of the stone, or something walking. And today, God help me, I found a survey marker. Brass, standard issue, government stamp. Dated eleven years ago. Initials E.Y.*

Emrys put the pages down on the gravel bar and stared at the fire for a long time.

E.Y. His initials. Eleven years ago — four years after he'd walked out of the survey corps, four years into his quiet life among the dead lines. He had never been upriver. He had never held a brass marker in this gorge in his life. And yet there it was in her handwriting, patient and precise and leaning eastward, describing a piece of evidence with his name stamped on it sitting in a tunnel that predated the government that issued the stamp.

Either someone was using his name, or someone was using his maps, or the country he'd spent fifteen years hiding from had been hiding something of his all along, down in the dark under the water, wearing his initials like a signature on work he couldn't remember doing.

"You've gone pale," Pell observed, from the far side of the fire. He said it the way he said everything, comfortably, as though the world were a tavern and everyone in it a regular. "Is it the ghost pages?"

"It's the initials."

"Ah." Pell nodded sagely. "Initials are worse than ghosts. Ghosts only want your soul. Initials want your *name*."

Above them, faint through the gorge walls, came a sound Emrys had been hearing since noon and refusing to hear: a low, rhythmic knocking, deep in the stone, regular as a pump. Not water. Water didn't keep time.

Pell cocked his head at it. "They start up around dark," he said. "My father said ignore them."

"And your father never said why."

"He said the *why* was down there, and he preferred it to stay there." Pell fed the fire another stick. "First sensible thing anyone in my family ever said."

Emrys lay awake most of the night listening to the knocking, and near dawn it stopped — not fading, just stopping, mid-beat, the way a clock stops. In the silence that followed he heard something worse: footsteps on the gravel above the boat, unhurried, deliberate, circling the camp once. When he got his lamp lit, the talus slope was empty. But the third cache crate, the one they hadn't reached yet, half a day further upriver —

Its lid was already open. Nails in a tidy row. Waiting for him.

And pinned to the inside of the lid, in handwriting he'd have known through a wall, a single line:

*Emrys — if you're reading this, don't trust the river. Come up the old line. — S.*
