## 4. The Escarpment Camp

The river had been lying to us for six days.

I want that on the record, in whatever ledger the Meridian Expedition keeps of its own follies: the river lied. It narrowed on our maps where it widened under our keel. It bent east where every survey since the founding said west. Okonkwo blamed the paper. "Maps are opinions," he said, tapping his copy with a pipe stem gone soft as licorice from chewing. "Rivers are facts." Tomas said nothing, because Tomas had been saying nothing for three days, which was how we all knew he'd found something.

He found it at noon on the sixth day, when the escarpment stood up out of the jungle like a wall built by someone with more ambition than patience—red stone, four hundred feet if it was an inch, seamed with shadow—and he came running down the shingle waving a page of his notes so hard I thought he'd caught fire.

"Sera. Sera. The seepage."

"The what?"

"Spring water. Coming *out* of the cliff face. Fresh water doesn't come out of solid rock unless—" He stopped, breathless, and grinned the grin he'd worn since he was nine years old and had disassembled our mother's clockwork loom. "Unless there's somewhere for it to come from."

We found the mouth of the tunnel behind a curtain of hanging moss two hours later, and I will confess here, privately, to whoever reads this: my first thought was not of history. My first thought was of Emrys Yarrow, fifteen years gone, who used to say that every landscape keeps its secrets in the same place water wants to go. He'd have loved this. He'd have hated this. With Emrys those were usually the same thing.

Tomas went in first, because he was going to go in first whether anyone sanctioned it or not, and the rest of us followed because we were not going to let him be eaten by a hole in the world without witnesses.

* * *

The tunnel did not behave like a cave.

Caves wander. Caves are accidents. This was a corridor—cut, squared, propped with timber that crumbled like biscuit when Okonkwo touched it but held where it mattered, which told us both its age and its builders' stubbornness. Our lamps found tool marks in the walls, long clean sweeps of them, hundreds of thousands of strokes laid down by hands working in shifts.

"Somebody dug this," Rennick said, stating the obvious the way he always did, as though the obvious might otherwise escape.

"Somebody dug *miles* of this," Okonkwo corrected, and he was right. We walked for the better part of an hour before the passage opened into the first gallery, and by then even Rennick had stopped pretending to be bored.

The gallery was a cathedral, if cathedrals were built downward instead of up. Our lamplight couldn't find the far wall. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of a metronome, and the air was cool and mineral and old—old in a way that got into your lungs and stayed there. I remember thinking: this is the greatest day of my professional life. I remember thinking it very clearly, very confidently, the way you think things right before they turn on you.

It was Adaeze who found the bones.

She didn't scream. She was too good a surveyor to scream; she simply went quiet in the particular way she went quiet over an anomaly, and called us in with her voice pitched low and level, and we came around a fallen pillar of rock and there they were.

Not one body. Not ten. A settlement of the dead, laid along the gallery floor in rows, some in the posture of burial and many, many more in the posture of having been left where they fell. Shackles lay among them—iron, colonial pattern, the kind stamped in the foundries of the old coastal forts. I knelt beside one and put my thumb in the lock mechanism, and it fit my hand the way tools fit the hands they're made for, and I understood, in the slow cold way understanding arrives, that these had been made for wrists exactly like the ones still wearing them.

"They built the tunnels," Tomas said. His voice had gone strange and small. "The laborers. They dug all this, and then—"

"And then," Okonkwo finished quietly, "the founding settlement decided the digging was done."

Nobody said the rest of it. Nobody had to. The bones said it, in their hundreds, in their arrangement—not scattered like flight, not stacked like burial, but *tidied*, moved to the deep galleries where no one from above would ever walk. You do not tidy evidence unless you know it is evidence.

* * *

We made camp in the second gallery that night, though camp is a generous word for six people unrolling bedrolls in a mass grave because the alternative was climbing back up the moss curtain in the dark. Adaeze cooked anyway. She always cooked anyway. There is something about rice and dried fish eaten by lamplight among the ancient dead that either breaks a team or binds it, and I am pleased to report it bound ours—we passed the pot around and told terrible jokes, because humor is how the living apologize to the dead for disturbing them.

It was during the documents that the jokes stopped.

They were in a niche near the third gallery, wrapped in waxed canvas against the damp: a clerk's strongbox worth of papers. Ledgers. Requisitions. And letters—letters between the settlement's founders and their backers downriver, discussing, in the brisk administrative prose of men who never once imagined being read, the disposal of the labor force upon completion of the works.

*Disposal.* The word appears eleven times. I counted. Counting was something to do with my hands while my mind refused the sentence around it.

The final letter, dated the autumn of the founding year, lays it out without euphemism: the workers knew the terrain, the tunnels, the approaches to the settlement. They could not be released. They could not be maintained. The cost of the alternative was itemized—rope, lamp oil, lime—and approved beneath a signature I recognized from the plaque outside the provincial archive, from the name on half the streets in Callow's Reach, from the currency itself.

Okonkwo read it aloud, once, evenly, the way you read a verdict. Then he sat down on his bedroll and looked at his hands for a long time.

"This is the founding," Tomas said finally. "This is *the* founding. The one they teach children. The harvest festival, the first winter, the letter to the Crown asking for honest governance." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it anywhere. "They asked for honest governance three weeks after doing *that*."

"So we publish," Adaeze said. No hesitation. Adaeze has never hesitated in her life; I've watched her step over a snake rather than break stride. "Every page copied, every bone photographed, every gallery surveyed. We take it downriver and we put it in front of every newspaper that can hold the type."

"That's a decision for the sponsor," Rennick said.

And there it was. I'd been waiting for it since the shackles—the pause, the pivot, the little recalibration behind his eyes. Rennick was Crane's man before he was ours; everyone knew it and nobody minded, because sponsorship buys provisions and provisions keep you alive. But there is a difference between a man who serves a patron and a man who belongs to him, and I heard the difference in Rennick's voice just then. It sounded like a door closing.

"Mr. Crane funded a survey," I said carefully. "This is what we surveyed."

"Crane funded a *railroad feasibility study*," Rennick said. "Bones and grievances are outside the scope." He smiled his reasonable smile, the one that made you feel unreasonable for disagreeing. "Consider the position. The founding families' descendants sit on every bench in the province. Half the capital is named after the signatory. You want to stand up in that city and say the whole edifice is built on a massacre?"

"I want to stand up and say what's true," Adaeze said.

"People have been hanged for less true," Rennick said, and for once he wasn't smiling.

* * *

We voted the next morning, because we were a civilized expedition and that is what civilized expeditions do, and I have thought often since about how quickly civilization becomes theater when the stakes arrive.

Five for publication: myself, Tomas, Adaeze, Okonkwo, and young Pell, who was twenty-three and had joined us to make maps and had ended up making history instead, and who voted with his chin up and his knees visibly shaking, and whom I have never been prouder of.

Two against: Rennick, and Halloway, who had been Rennick's tentmate since Callow's Reach and whose loyalty, like his hammock, was strung wherever Rennick's was.

Seven votes. It should have been settled. Majorities settle things; that is the entire promise of majorities. But Rennick received the result the way a man receives weather—unpleasant, noted, survivable—and that night I woke to hear low voices at the edge of the gallery, and saw lamplight moving toward the tunnel mouth, and heard the word *downriver* spoken twice in Rennick's flat careful voice.

I lay in the dark afterward doing arithmetic I didn't like. The radio sat smashed in its crate—I would learn later that Halloway had done it, gently, with a rock, sometime in the night—but even before I knew that, I knew the shape of our situation. Three days' boat to the nearest telegraph. One boat. Two factions. And a sponsor downriver who had paid for silence and was about to learn he hadn't gotten it.

"Tera," Tomas whispered across the dark—he'd called me Tera since he was four and couldn't manage Sera, and at thirty-one he still pretended it was affection rather than laziness. "You're awake."

"Yes."

"What do we do?"

I looked up at the gallery ceiling, at the timber props holding four hundred feet of mountain off our sleeping bodies, and I thought about how everything strong around us had been built by people who were then erased from the story, and how the erasing was still, apparently, available for purchase.

"We copy everything," I said. "Tonight. Every document, twice. One set stays visible, one set goes where only family finds it. And then we get downriver faster than Rennick's letters do."

"And if we can't?"

I didn't answer him, because I didn't have an answer, because the honest answer was a name I hadn't spoken in fifteen years and a debt I'd assumed was long expired. Somewhere south of us, past the gorges and the lying river, there was a man who mapped closed lines because he wanted nothing from the world, and who owed me a conversation we'd abandoned mid-sentence on a station platform when we were young enough to believe leaving was the same thing as freedom.

I had put his name in the sealed envelope I sent to Crane with the expedition's contingency plan—*if contact is lost, send Yarrow; he knows this country better than its own government does*. I'd told myself it was practical. Standing in that cold gallery, listening to Rennick's boots fade up the tunnel toward the world, I admitted what it actually was.

It was a message in a bottle. And I could only hope the sea still remembered how to carry things home.
