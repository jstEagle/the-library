## 5. Silence on the Radio

The camp announced itself before I saw it. That was my first clue that something had gone badly wrong.

A working expedition camp smells like woodsmoke and coffee and wet canvas, like people going about the ordinary business of being alive in an inconvenient place. This one smelled of ash and river mud and something sweeter underneath—kerosene, maybe, or the memory of it. I came up out of the gorge on the old pack trail with my rifle slung and my heart doing arithmetic it didn't like, and when I crested the last rise I understood why no one had answered the radio.

The main tent was a black ribcage. The mess shelter had been dragged half into the river, as though someone had tried to drown it. And there, bolted to its crate beside what remained of the survey shack, sat the radio set—a Meridian Mark III, government issue, a machine I could have repaired blindfolded except that someone had taken a rock to it with real conviction. Not smashed in panic. Smashed thoroughly, methodically, by a person who wanted it dead.

I stood in the wreckage for a long moment, listening. Gulls. Water over stone. Somewhere upslope, wind moving through the pines along the escarpment's shoulder. No voices. No boots.

"Hello the camp," I said anyway, because fifteen years of walking empty lines teaches you that the habit of announcing yourself is sometimes the only thing between you and a bullet.

Nothing answered. But I noticed three things in the next thirty seconds, and all three of them were wrong in ways that gave me hope. First: the fire pit had been raked clean recently, within a day. Second: the supply tent, though torn open, still held crates—and nobody abandons food unless they intend to come back for it. Third, and best of all: scratched into the mud beside the water barrel, small and deliberate, was a cartographer's symbol I knew better than my own signature. A triangle with a dot inside. Sera Voss's mark for *surveyed, verified, true*.

She was alive. She wanted me to know she'd been here, and she wanted me to look up.

I looked up. Above the camp, half-hidden behind a screen of alder, a seam of darkness ran along the escarpment's base. A tunnel mouth. Of course. Sera had never once in her life left a message she didn't also leave directions to.

* * *

The tunnel swallowed the daylight in about twenty paces, and then it went on swallowing. I walked with one hand on the wall and my lamp turned low, and the deeper I went the more wrong everything felt—not dangerous-wrong, but *geographical*-wrong. The passages were cut, not natural, and they were old in a way that made the colonial-era survey markers I passed look like children's chalk drawings. Somebody had built this place before anybody official had ever admitted to building anything here at all.

And then, around a bend where the passage widened into a chamber, a voice said, "That's far enough," and a lantern flared, and I was looking down the barrel of a very steady revolver held by a young man with Sera Voss's exact jawline and none of her patience.

"Tomas," I said. "You've grown."

He squinted. The revolver wavered, which in a Voss is roughly equivalent to fainting. "Emrys Yarrow," he said slowly, "you look terrible."

"Fifteen years will do that." I kept my hands visible and my voice level. "Is your sister—"

"Here," said another voice, from the shadows beyond him, and my whole chest forgot its job for a second.

Sera stepped into the lantern light muddier and thinner than the woman in my memory, hair tied back with what appeared to be survey twine, eyes shadowed but absolutely, ferociously awake. She looked at me the way she used to look at a map with an error in it—like she couldn't decide whether to be furious or delighted.

"You came," she said.

"Crane sent me."

"I know. He sent you because you're the only man alive who can find this place twice." A pause. Her chin lifted. "And because he thinks you'll do what he asks when you get here."

"That makes two of us who don't know if I will," I said, and heard Tomas make a small sound that might have been a laugh or a warning.

Behind them, two more figures emerged from the dark. A broad-shouldered man with a geologist's hammer hanging at his belt and the calm of someone who has personally measured how much trouble the world contains—"Okonkwo," Sera said, "our rocks-and-truths man"—and, leaning against the wall with his leg wrapped in filthy bandages and his face arranged into an expression of injured dignity, a man I recognized from Crane's photographs.

"Rennick," I said.

"The very same," said Rennick, "and might I say, Mr. Yarrow, that you took your time."

* * *

They fed me first. Cold beans and hardtack, eaten by lantern light in a side chamber that smelled of stone and old air, and while I ate they told me what had happened, each of them telling a different version, the way four witnesses always do.

The short version was this: six weeks ago the expedition had found the tunnels, and the tunnels had found them back. Bones in the deep galleries. Iron shackles sized for wrists, not ankles, arranged in rows. Ledgers in a founding settler's careful hand recording, in the bland bookkeeping language of atrocity, the disposal of the laborers who dug the escarpment's foundations—the men and women whose names never made it into any history, because the history required their absence. The settlement hadn't been built *near* a massacre. It had been built *on* one, deliberately, as a lid.

"We voted," Sera said. "Five to publish, three to bury. Rennick's faction sent word downriver. Then Rennick's friends arrived by boat with more guns than arguments, and Halloran and Deshi and Priya went over the top of the escarpment with the evidence crates, east toward the coast road, drawing pursuit off us." Her voice did not break, but it went somewhere very flat and controlled, which from Sera was worse. "We haven't heard anything since."

"They're alive," Tomas said quickly. "Priya's the best backcountry walker I've ever seen. If anyone got those crates through, she did."

"And the radio?" I asked.

Rennick shifted against the wall. "Smashed during the attack."

"During," Okonkwo repeated mildly, examining his fingernails. "By persons unknown. After we had already sent our final position report. Almost as though someone wished to ensure no further reports were possible."

"I was shot in the thigh defending the equipment," Rennick said with dignity.

"You were shot running *toward* the equipment," Tomas said, "and everyone in this room has privately concluded you were trying to smash it yourself and were interrupted."

"Gentlemen," I said, "please. We can fight about the radio after we've decided whether to fight about everything else."

Which was, I admit, not my most diplomatic moment. Sera rounded on me with the full historical weight of every argument we'd ever had, which is considerable; we once ended a courtship over the correct way to fold a contour interval.

"Don't stand there being reasonable, Emrys. You work for Crane. You carried his envelope up the river. So before anyone tells me anything else, I want to hear you say it: are you here to help us, or are you here to finish what his men started?"

The chamber went quiet. Even Rennick looked interested.

I set down my tin plate. "Fifteen years ago I refused to falsify a survey for the corps, and it cost me everything I had, and I have spent every year since mapping rail lines nobody rides because nobody riding them means nobody asking me to lie about where they go. Aldous Crane is paying me triple rate to come find you, and he sealed his instructions in an envelope I haven't opened, because the handwriting on the outside wasn't his." I looked at her. "It was yours. Which means either you wrote to him under duress, or you wrote to him hoping whoever he sent would read it and understand. I'm betting on the second. Am I right?"

Something in her shoulders let go, a centimeter's worth, which from Sera Voss was a full surrender.

"There's a note inside," she said quietly. "One line. *Ask him what he refused to sign.*"

Tomas made a strangled noise. "You put a message to Emrys in the envelope *Crane* sealed? What if he'd opened it himself?"

"He wouldn't. He never reads other people's drafts—it's a point of pride with him, like his waistcoats." She almost smiled. Then it faded. "And if he had opened it, well. Then I'd know sooner rather than later."

Okonkwo chuckled, low, like distant gravel. "Cartographers," he said, to no one in particular. "You plan an ambush for a man's conscience and call it drafting."

* * *

It was Rennick, of all people, who broke the fragile peace, because Rennick could not bear to be uninteresting for longer than an hour.

"You should know," he said, levering himself upright with a wince, "that the men who came by boat weren't all of them. There's a second party. They went after your friends over the escarpment, yes—but Crane himself is coming upriver. He told me so before I left Callow's Reach. He doesn't send for a thing like this. He comes. He likes to see the ends of things personally."

"When?" I asked.

"A week ago, when he told me. With his pace and the river's temper—" Rennick shrugged elaborately. "Days. Perhaps already past the gorges."

Sera and I looked at each other across the lantern light, and I watched her do the same arithmetic I was doing, and arrive at the same ugly total. Whatever we decided—run, publish, burn, bargain—we would be deciding it with Aldous Crane's boats somewhere below us on the river, closing the gap.

"Well," said Tomas, into the silence, with his sister's gift for finding the worst possible timing, "at least now we know why the beans taste nervous."

Nobody laughed. But Okonkwo patted him on the shoulder, and Sera reached over and squeezed my hand once, hard, the way she used to before a river crossing—*we go together or not at all*—and I understood that the question of whether I was theirs or Crane's had just been settled without a vote.

Five to nothing. Rennick abstained.

"Now," Sera said, turning to me with her survey pen already in hand, eyes bright and terrible, "tell me about these closed lines of yours, Emrys Yarrow. Because I have a feeling we're going to need one."
