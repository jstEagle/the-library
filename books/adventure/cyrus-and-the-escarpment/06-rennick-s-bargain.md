## 6. Rennick's Bargain

Rennick had made himself comfortable in the worst possible way.

He sat propped against a crate of survey instruments with his wounded leg stretched out before him like an argument he expected to win, and he'd arranged the survivors' tinned rations into a neat little inventory at his elbow—counted, catalogued, claimed. Even shot through the thigh and hiding in a hole in the earth, the man could not stop being an auditor of other people's property.

Emrys crouched across the fire from him and watched him do it. The tunnel chamber was low-ceilinged and dry, lit by two lanterns turned down to amber slits, and it smelled of stone dust and old smoke—the smoke of things burned, which told Emrys more about the last three weeks than any of them had said aloud yet.

"You're staring," Rennick said pleasantly.

"Counting," Emrys said. "You've got fourteen tins there. There were nineteen this morning."

"Tomas miscounted." Rennick smiled. He had a good smile, the kind that had opened doors for him his whole life, and he knew exactly how much light to let through it. "Or I did. Hard to say, in the dark."

Across the chamber, Sera stood with her arms folded beneath the map case slung across her chest, watching the exchange the way she used to watch river gauges in flood season—professionally, patiently, waiting for the number that mattered. Tomas sat behind her on a bedroll with a pry bar across his knees, twenty-two years old and trying very hard to look like someone who had never been afraid of anything. Okonkwo was up at the tunnel mouth keeping watch, which meant he was also, Emrys suspected, deciding whether he could drop several tons of limestone on all of them and call it geology.

The wounded man's name was Rennick. He'd come upriver with Crane's second boat, six men strong, and now he was one man weak, and the fact that Sera and her people had bandaged him instead of finishing the arithmetic the escarpment had started said something about them that Emrys found both admirable and exhausting.

"So," Rennick said. "Yarrow. Fifteen years, and they send me *you*."

"They didn't send me. Crane hired me to find a missing expedition."

"And you found us. Congratulations. Now we can both report the mission accomplished." He shifted, winced, kept smiling through it. "Except the mission isn't finding anyone. You know that, don't you? You read the envelope."

Emrys hadn't. The envelope sat in his coat pocket still, sealed, Sera's handwriting on the front of it like a hand pressed flat against glass. He'd carried it four hundred miles without opening it, which Rennick evidently found either admirable or hilarious.

"You're forbidden to open it until you find the camp," Rennick said. "Well. Here's the camp. Go ahead."

Emrys didn't move. "Tell me instead. You seem eager."

"I'm *practical*." Rennick spread his hands. "I'm lying here with a hole in my leg because four idealists wouldn't take a reasonable offer, and I would very much prefer not to repeat the experience. So let me save everyone some time." He nodded at Sera. "Voss. Your crates. Your papers. Your photographs. Mr. Crane wants every document recovered or destroyed—all of them—and the people who carried them persuaded, permanently if necessary, to forget what they saw. That's the whole order. It fits on a postcard. I've been carrying it since Callow's Reach."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke.

"The founding settlement," Sera said quietly, not a question so much as a confirmation, "murdered its own labor force. Three hundred people, maybe more. Shackled, marched into those tunnels, and sealed in when the work was done. And the documents proving it are sitting in a crate forty feet from where you're bleeding, and your employer's response is a postcard."

"My employer's response is *history*, Ms. Voss. Empires are built on worse and apologize for less. Crane isn't unusual. He's just the one holding the paper." Rennick turned back to Emrys, and here it came—Emrys felt it coming the way you feel a bridge change note under load. "But you. Yarrow. You're not an idealist. I've read your file."

"I don't have a file."

"Everyone has a file. Yours is thin, which is its own kind of interesting. Railroad engineer, fifteen years, no incidents, no publications, no society memberships, no debts. A man who has spent a decade and a half making himself impossible to find." Rennick's smile widened by one careful degree. "And yet Crane knew exactly where to send a telegram. Doesn't that strike you as strange? A man that invisible, found that fast?"

Emrys said nothing. His hands were steady, which took work.

"It struck Crane as convenient," Rennick went on. "Because Crane remembers you, Yarrow. The Meridian Valley survey. The numbers you wouldn't falsify. The town you wouldn't drown on paper." He said it lightly, conversationally, a man discussing weather over cards. "Crane covered for you. Do you understand? When you walked out, when you refused, when half the corps wanted you charged—he smoothed it over. Made it a resignation instead of a ruin. He's been carrying that favor for fifteen years like a coin in his pocket, waiting for a shop that sold what you're selling."

"And what am I selling?"

"Silence." Rennick leaned forward, and the lantern light moved on his face and made it briefly, honestly ugly. "Here's the bargain, and I'll only make it once. You walk out of these tunnels, you go back downriver, you tell Crane you found nothing but bones and bad geology. Voss keeps her mouth shut—or is persuaded to—and the past stays buried on both sides. In exchange: full reinstatement. Your old rank, your old pay, and every closed line on the map reopened under your signature. All those dead railways you've been drawing in your little notebooks—" he tapped the air toward Emrys's satchel, and Emrys felt the violation of that like a cold draft, the man knew about the notebooks, of course he knew, Crane had probably read pages of them— "you'd have them *alive* again. Trains running. Names on stations. A career instead of a hobby. Fifteen years of hiding, ended tomorrow morning."

The words did their work. Emrys hated that they did their work. He sat with them the way you sit with a hot iron, testing whether you can hold it, and somewhere in the silence he heard the thing underneath the offer, which was not reinstatement at all but *permission*—permission to come back into the world, to be a man whose maps were read instead of hidden, to stop being a ghost who haunted decommissioned track. Fifteen years he had told himself he chose the closed lines. Some nights, alone with the lamp and the graph paper, he had known better.

And Sera was watching him. He could feel her watching, and he could not make himself look back, because if he looked back she would see the iron burning his hands, and he had never once managed to hide anything from her, not even at twenty-six, not even when it mattered most.

"That's very generous," Emrys said finally.

"It's *final*. That's different."

"It usually is." He picked up a stick and nudged the fire, buying himself the motion. "One problem."

"One?"

"Several. But let's start with the practical one." He looked up. "Your boat came up the gorge. Six men, one wounded. Which means five able bodies between here and the river, and the river route back down passes the Widow's Stair—a stretch of water I scouted on the way in, and which I promise you does not forgive a loaded boat in autumn current. And your writ, your magistrate's paper, whatever Crane gave you—it gets you documents, not a road out. There *is* no road out. Not one your maps show."

Rennick's eyes narrowed a fraction. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning there's a spur." Emrys said it slowly, letting each word cost him visibly, because a lie told cheaply is a lie disbelieved. "Old line. Pre-railroad era, built for the first settlement works, abandoned when they sealed the tunnels. It runs east through the escarpment and comes out above the timber camps—two days' walk from a working junction. It's on none of your surveys because it was never on any survey. I found it eight years ago mapping a closed branch, and I've told no one, because telling people is how lines get found, and found lines get used, and used lines bring the world with them." He shrugged, a small tired shrug. "You want out of here with your leg and your orders intact? I'm the only map that matters."

The chamber went quiet except for the fire. He felt Sera's gaze change—not softer, but sharper, recalculating. She didn't know about the spur. He'd never told her; he'd never told anyone; and now he was spending his last secret like a man paying for time, and both of them knew it, and neither of them could say so in front of Rennick.

"You could be inventing it," Rennick said.

"I could. But then you'd shoot me, and you'd still be in a hole in the ground with a bad leg and winter coming down the gorge. Whereas if I'm not inventing it—" Emrys let the sentence sit. "You need me walking, Rennick. Whatever else you decide, you need that."

Rennick studied him for a long moment, and Emrys watched the arithmetic run behind his eyes—the same tidy, bloodless arithmetic that had counted the tins. Five men upriver, one of them crippled, orders that required either cooperation or corpses, and a terrain that offered no third option. Emrys had spent fifteen years learning how such men thought, because knowing how they thought was how he'd stayed off their maps.

"All right," Rennick said at last. "The spur. Tomorrow. We rest tonight, your people carry my stretcher rotation, and we all go see this miracle of yours together." He settled back against the crate, satisfied, a man who believed he had just purchased a guide. "And Yarrow—think about the offer while you walk. Coin doesn't expire. Neither does Crane's memory."

"Everyone's memory expires eventually," Emrys said. "That's rather the point of all this, isn't it?"

Rennick laughed, genuinely, and the sound bounced around the limestone like something loose.

Later, when Rennick slept and Tomas took the watch, Sera came and stood beside Emrys at the far wall of the chamber, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was the most either of them had said in three days.

"There is no spur, is there," she murmured. Not accusing. Almost admiring.

"There's a spur," Emrys said. "Just not the one I described. The real one runs west, not east, and it ends at a trestle that hasn't held weight since before either of us was born." He paused. "It'll hold once. If we're quick, and if we're lucky, and if Okonkwo can convince another passage to fall at the right moment."

Sera was quiet a moment. Then, softly, with the ghost of the old shared joke in it: "You lied beautifully. I almost married a man who couldn't lie at all."

"You almost married a man who hadn't yet learned what lies cost." He looked at her then, finally, and the lantern light was kind to neither of them and it didn't matter. "Sera—if I give Rennick his road out, he takes the crates or burns them. If I refuse, he signals his men at the river and we fight five with four and a pry bar. Either way, someone pays. So I'm going to spend tomorrow being very agreeable and very slow, and I need you to trust that the slowness is the plan."

"And the envelope?" Her voice dropped further. "You still haven't opened it."

He reached into his coat and drew it out—soft at the corners now, travel-worn, her handwriting faded to the color of old water. He held it between them.

"If I open it," he said, "it tells me what you wanted from me before you knew I was coming. And I find I'd rather hear it from you."

"Tomorrow," Sera said. "On the spur. When you show me what you really found eight years ago—and why a man who maps dead railways kept *this* line secret even from me."

Above them, faint through the limestone, came the sound Emrys had been dreading since sundown: a boat horn on the river, long and low, answered by a second. Rennick's five men weren't waiting at the bank anymore.

They were coming up.
