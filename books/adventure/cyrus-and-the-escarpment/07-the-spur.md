## 7. The Spur

The tunnels had a sound, Tomas decided, the way houses and rivers have sounds. Not silence—silence was a lie people told about caves. The tunnels breathed. Somewhere above them, water found its way through stone in long, patient drips, and somewhere below, the river ran dark and constant, like a pulse under the floorboards of the world.

Tomas Voss walked third in line, behind Emrys Yarrow's lantern and ahead of his sister's steady breathing, and he tried very hard not to think about how much rock was overhead.

"You're doing the thing," Sera murmured from behind him.

"What thing?"

"The counting thing. You count when you're frightened. You've done it since you were six and fell down the well at Meridian Station."

"I was *lowered* into that well on a rope, and I was counting the rope knots for a legitimate surveying purpose."

"You were crying."

"Surveyors can cry. It's in the manual."

Ahead of them, Emrys snorted—a small, involuntary sound, quickly smothered, as though laughter were another kind of noise you didn't want echoing off tunnel walls. But it escaped anyway, and Tomas felt something loosen in his chest that had been wound tight since the radio died.

He still didn't trust the man. That was the honest accounting of it. Fifteen years of absence didn't get forgiven by one night of lantern light and good map work, no matter how precisely Yarrow could read a passage of stone. But trust, Tomas was learning, wasn't a wall you built or didn't build. It was more like one of these tunnels—you went through it a little at a time, and you kept your hand on the wall.

Behind them, far back along the passage they'd left, came the sound they were all listening for and none of them would name: boots. Many boots. Rennick's men had come upriver in the grey hour before dawn, three boats' worth of them, and Rennick himself—wounded, furious, and suddenly no longer pretending to be a patient—had gone out to meet them like a landlord greeting tenants.

"He'll give them an hour," Okonkwo said quietly. He walked last, carrying the heaviest pack without complaint, because Okonkwo carried everything without complaint; it was his defining feature, like a mountain having a certain stony quality. "Rennick likes to make speeches first. He believes fear is a tool he owns."

"Do you think he's wrong?" Sera asked.

"I think tools rust," Okonkwo said, "and their owners rarely notice."

* * *

They stopped at midnight—or what Emrys called midnight, which was to say when his pocket watch said so and the tunnels agreed to pretend there was such a thing. A chamber opened around them, tall enough that the lantern light gave up before it found the ceiling, and the walls glittered faintly with some mineral that caught the flame like scattered coins.

Emrys set the lantern on a flat shelf of rock and unrolled his map across it, weighting the corners with four stones chosen, Tomas noticed, with the seriousness of a man crowning a king.

"There." Emrys's finger rested on a line so faint it might have been a scratch. "The spur."

Tomas leaned in. He couldn't help it. He was a cartographer's brother if nothing else, and he knew every official survey of this region the way a priest knows scripture—and this line was in none of them. It simply wasn't. A rail spur, graded and ballasted, running out of the escarpment's eastern shoulder toward the gorge, and no record of it anywhere.

"Who builds a railroad and then erases it?" he asked.

"The same kind of people who dig tunnels full of shackles and call it a founding," Sera said. There was no bitterness in her voice, which somehow made it worse. Just the flat clarity she got when she'd stopped being angry and started being accurate.

"It was never erased," Emrys said. "It was never *entered*. That's different, and the difference matters." He looked up, and the lantern light did something complicated to his face—half engineer, half ghost. "Fifteen years ago I surveyed this region for the corps. Officially, my final report covered the main line alignment. Unofficially, I spent four months mapping everything the government preferred not to exist. Old logging spurs. Abandoned grades. This." He tapped the line again. "I kept the copies myself. All of them. When I left the corps, the maps left with me, and nobody ever came looking, because officially there was nothing here to look for."

"That's not why you left, though." Sera said it gently, but she said it, and Tomas watched her brother-in-everything-but-blood go very still over his own map. "You know I read your file. Back when we were—"

"Yes," Emrys said. "I know what you read."

Tomas looked between them. "Well, I haven't read anything, and I'm carrying the gun, so I'd say I've earned the story."

Sera made a sound that was half laugh, half warning. Okonkwo settled onto a boulder with the air of a man who had been waiting all day for exactly this.

Emrys rolled the map closed, slowly, buying time the way he bought everything—with precision. Then he sat back against the tunnel wall and told it.

* * *

"There was a valley," he said. "Alderfen. Two hundred souls, a grist mill, a church with a crooked steeple that drove the bell-ringer mad. The main line alignment—the one the ministry wanted, the one Crane's money wanted—ran straight through it. Through the valley floor. Through the town."

"But there were other alignments," Sera said softly. "I remember the file. Three alternates were surveyed and rejected."

"They weren't rejected. They were *suppressed*. The southern route cost more—it followed the river, needed two extra bridges. The ministry had already promised the land grants along the direct route to men who owned the ministry. So the report that reached the minister's desk said the southern route was geologically unsound. Unstable shale. Flood risk. My signature was on that assessment." He turned his hands over, palms up, as though the lie might still be visible on them. "Except I hadn't written it. I'd written the opposite. I'd written that Alderfen's ground was the best rail bed in the entire corridor, and that the direct route would need cuttings through live landslide zones that would kill crews for a decade."

"And someone changed it," Tomas said.

"Crane's chief surveyor changed it. With Crane's knowledge—I'm sure of that now, though I wasn't sure of anything then except that my name was on a document that would bury a town under a railroad's convenience. I refused to sign the final version. Refused publicly, at the review board, with witnesses." A dry, humorless breath. "You'd be amazed how fast a career ends when you do that. You'd be even more amazed how fast a *life* ends, if the right people decide you're a problem. Someone warned me—one of the clerks, a woman whose name I still don't know—that my next assignment was going to be upriver, alone, on a stretch of river where surveys had a way of not coming back."

"So you disappeared," Sera said.

"So I disappeared. Walked out of Callow's Reach on a freight boat with my maps sewn into a coat—" he glanced at Tomas, something wry crossing his face, "—which appears to run in certain families. And I spent fifteen years mapping dead lines because dead lines have no sponsors. Nobody kills you over a track nobody wants."

The chamber held quiet for a moment. Above them, the patient dripping.

"My father worked the Alderfen grist mill," Tomas heard himself say. "Before he took the coastal survey contract. Before—" He stopped. Started again. "He used to say the railroad men came through once, all brass buttons and promises, and the whole town turned out to cheer them. He said my mother cried at the celebration, and he never understood why until years later."

Emrys looked at him. In the lantern light his eyes were tired and old and entirely without self-pity, which was somehow the most unbearable thing about him.

"I didn't save Alderfen," he said. "That's the part your sister's file doesn't contain. I delayed it. The scandal of my refusal bought the valley six years. Six years of lawsuits and re-surveys, and then the route went through anyway, on the original alignment, and the town was moved or drowned depending on who you ask. I saved them time. Time is not the same as salvation. I've had fifteen years to learn the difference."

"No," Tomas said, and surprised himself with the force of it. "No, that's not—Yarrow, you stood up in front of a review board and told the truth knowing it would end you. Most men can't do that. Most men *don't*. My father spent his whole life regretting that he cheered." He shook his head. "Six years is six years. Ask anyone who's ever been given less."

Okonkwo nodded slowly, the way he approved of things—economically, like a man rationing approval for a famine. "In my country we say: the man who plants a tree he will not sit under has understood something most men die without learning."

"That's a lovely saying," Tomas said, "and also I believe you invented it just now."

"All proverbs are invented just now, by someone," Okonkwo said serenely. "That is what makes them proverbial."

And Tomas laughed—actually laughed, in a tunnel beneath a mountain, with armed men somewhere behind them—and realized somewhere in the laughing that the thing in his chest had shifted. Not all the way. But shifted. Grudging respect settling into something with weight and grain, something you could build on.

Trust, he thought. Going through it a little at a time. Hand on the wall.

* * *

It was Okonkwo who heard them first.

He raised one hand—not dramatically, just a finger, the way he signaled everything—and the column stopped, and the tunnel's breathing seemed to hold itself.

Boots. Closer than they should have been. Rennick hadn't waited his hour after all; either he'd grown impatient or his men had grown competent, and both possibilities were bad.

"Side passage, forty meters," Emrys whispered, already moving, map folded into memory. "It loops. We can gain the spur gallery from the far end."

They ran. Lantern swinging, packs banging, Sera's hand finding Tomas's elbow in the dark and gripping hard. Behind them the boots became voices became torchlight, yellow and hungry, spilling into the chamber they'd just left.

The side passage was narrow and climbed steeply, and forty meters in, it narrowed further—to a throat of stone barely wide enough for a man, braced by old timber that groaned when Okonkwo put his hand against it.

Everyone understood at the same moment. Everyone except, perhaps, Okonkwo, who had probably understood hours ago.

"The timber's cut for pulling," he said calmly. "Two braces and the keystone above. Bring it down and the passage closes—for days, for weeks, maybe forever. They'd need charges they don't have and time they won't take." He began unbuckling the heavy pack, handing it to Tomas piece by piece: the instruments, the rations, the document case wrapped in oilcloth. "You carry the evidence now. All of it. Every gram."

"Okonkwo." Sera's voice cracked on the second syllable. "There are other ways—"

"There are slower ways. Slower ways get us killed." He smiled at her, and it was the same unhurried smile he'd worn through every disaster since the radio died. "Miss Voss. I am a geologist. Do you know what a geologist is? It is a man who has spent his whole life learning how stone falls, waiting for one day when the knowledge matters." He pressed the last strap into Tomas's hands and closed Tomas's fingers over it. "Make it matter."

"Tell me something to bring back," Tomas said, and hated how young his voice sounded. "For the publication. For the record. Something of yours."

Okonkwo considered, head tilted, utterly unhurried, while the torchlight grew louder below.

"Tell them the escarpment limestone dates to the deep sea," he said. "Tell them these mountains were ocean once, and everything—every empire, every massacre, every railroad—is a thin crust on top of water. Tell them the earth remembers being patient, and suggests we try it." He stepped back toward the timber braces, lantern raised, and his face in the glow was calm as a man reading by a fire. "Now run, Mr. Voss. Surveyors can cry. It's in the manual."

Tomas ran. He heard the first brace groan, then crack, then the deep exhalation of a mountain shifting its weight, and then the passage behind them was thunder and dust and darkness, and then it was silence, and Sera was sobbing quietly against his shoulder as they climbed, and Emrys Yarrow put one hand briefly on Tomas's back—just once, just a touch, engineer to survivor—and said, "He bought us until morning. We spend it well."

They came up out of the tunnel mouth an hour before dawn, onto the old spur grade, moonlit rails gleaming through sixty years of moss like the spine of some enormous sleeping animal. And there, parked in a collapsing shed beside the trestle's near abutment, sat a hand-car, its handles wrapped in rot-proof canvas, its wheels greased—as if someone, fifteen years ago, had maintained it against a day exactly like this one.

Tomas stared at it, then at Emrys.

"You planned this," he said. "Fifteen years ago, you planned for—"

"I planned for nothing," Emrys said, swinging the canvas off the handles. "I just never throw away working equipment."

But his eyes, when they lifted past Tomas's shoulder, toward the gorge road climbing the far bank—toward the distant, unmistakable glint of lanterns descending in disciplined formation, far too many, moving with the unhurried confidence of men carrying a magistrate's writ—

—his eyes said otherwise.

"That's not Rennick's lot," Sera whispered. "Tomas. Whose boats are those?"

Emrys went very still.

"Crane's," he said. "He's come himself."
