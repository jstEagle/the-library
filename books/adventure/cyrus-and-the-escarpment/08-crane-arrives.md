## 8. Crane Arrives

Aldous Crane had not slept on a riverboat in thirty years, and the river seemed to remember it, holding a grudge. The *Perpetual* — he'd named her himself, in a fit of optimism that now struck him as almost comic — bucked and shuddered against the current like a horse being led somewhere it did not wish to go.

He stood at the rail in the grey of morning, watching the escarpment rise ahead of them, red stone stacked in layers like the pages of an enormous ledger. He had always thought in ledgers. It was how he'd built everything: the shipping concern, the land holdings, the Meridian Expedition itself. Every column balanced. Every debt collected.

Except one.

"Sir." Rennick's man Halloran came up beside him, boots squelching on wet deck planking. "The writ's aboard. Magistrate Oduya signed it at Callow's Reach before we sailed. Declares the expedition lost, all records and findings state property."

"And the second document?"

Halloran hesitated. He was a large man with the face of a man who had been told too many things he didn't want to know. "The other instructions, sir? They're... they're with Rennick. As ordered."

Crane nodded once and turned back to the escarpment. Somewhere up there, in tunnels that predated every map his money had ever bought, four people were hiding from him. One of them was Emrys Yarrow.

That was the part that kept him awake on the bucking deck, more than the river's grudge, more than the writ in its oilskin tube. He had sent for Yarrow because Yarrow was the best, and because Yarrow owed him, and because — though Crane would have denied this under oath, and had, twice — he had wanted to see him again. Fifteen years was a long time to carry a man's secret. You began to feel less like a keeper and more like a co-owner.

"You're smiling, sir," Halloran observed carefully.

"I'm remembering," Crane said. "It happens less often than it used to. I try to mark the occasions."

* * *

The camp, when they reached it, looked like a sentence that had been interrupted mid-clause. Tents half-collapsed. A cook fire long dead, its ashes rained into gray paste. The radio mast snapped clean through, the set itself smashed with what Crane recognized, professionally, as deliberate violence rather than rage.

Rennick met them at the treeline, looking like a man who had been losing arguments with the wilderness for a week. His left arm was bound in a sling that was mostly bandage and mostly optimism.

"Mr. Crane." He managed something adjacent to a bow. "You shouldn't have come yourself."

"Nonsense. I never trust a matter I wouldn't attend personally." Crane surveyed the camp. "Report."

"The documents are gone. Voss woman has them — or had them. They went into the tunnels three days ago. I've got six men sealing the known entrances, but—" Rennick's jaw worked. "There are more entrances than there are men. The whole escarpment is honeycombed. And Yarrow—"

"Yes. Yarrow."

"He found them. He's with them." Rennick said it like an accusation, which, Crane reflected, was fair. He had hired Yarrow precisely because the man could find anything. He simply hadn't specified what Yarrow should do upon finding it. An oversight. Or perhaps not. Perhaps some part of him had wanted to see what Emrys Yarrow would choose when the choice finally arrived. There were worse ways to learn about a man.

"Where is he now?"

"Sealing himself in with them. But there's a spur line — an old one, off the books. Yarrow claims it comes out above the gorge. If they reach it..."

"They'll be gone by dark, and my writ will be worth the paper and the wax." Crane handed the oilskin tube to Halloran. "Set up my tent. And bring me Mr. Yarrow. Unharmed, Halloran. That is not a suggestion delivered casually; it is the entire point of the exercise."

* * *

They brought Emrys Yarrow to him at dusk, at the mouth of the largest tunnel, where Crane had arranged two chairs facing each other like a stage set for a play about two old friends. Which, in a sense, it was.

Yarrow looked older. Everyone did; that was the tax time levied without appeal. But he carried it differently than most men — not stooped by it, but folded around it, like a surveyor who has learned to read terrain. His hands were ink-stained. Still mapping, then. Even out here. Especially out here.

"Aldous." No title. Fifteen years, and no title. Crane found this both irritating and deeply reassuring.

"Emrys. Sit. The chair is uncomfortable; I chose it deliberately so you wouldn't linger."

"You always did think three moves ahead."

"I think in ledgers, Emrys. It's not strategy, it's bookkeeping." Crane sat in his own chair, which was comfortable, because he saw no reason to suffer merely to make a point. "You've read the writ, presumably. The expedition is declared lost. Its records are state property. I am, technically, here to recover state property."

"Technically."

"I love that word in your mouth. It always means you're about to disagree with me."

Yarrow almost smiled. Almost. "What are you actually here for?"

And there it was. The question Crane had traveled nine days upriver to answer, and which he had rehearsed in eleven different formulations, all of which abandoned him now that the man was actually sitting in front of him, patient as stone.

"The truth," Crane said finally. "Which is ironic, given the circumstances. But there it is." He leaned forward. "Do you remember the Kestrel Valley survey?"

Something moved behind Yarrow's eyes — a door closing, or opening. "I remember refusing to falsify it."

"Do you know what happened after you walked out of that office?" Crane let the silence sit for exactly as long as it needed to. "I signed the correction myself. In your name. I told the corps you'd been overworked, that your figures were sound, that the valley town stood. I buried the falsified original so deep that when the auditors came through two years later, they found nothing. Do you understand what that cost me? Not money. Money is nothing. Credibility. Standing. I spent fifteen years of accumulated standing on a junior engineer's conscience."

"Why?"

"Because you were right, and I knew it, and I couldn't stomach the alternative." Crane spread his hands. "There. Is that what you wanted to hear? That Aldous Crane, monster of commerce, architect of this entire miserable expedition, once did a decent thing?"

"It's not the decent thing that troubles me." Yarrow's voice was quiet. "It's what you built on top of it. You decided the debt ran one way. You covered for me, therefore I belonged to you. Everything since — the closed lines, the quiet work, the telegram, this—" he gestured at the ruined camp, the broken radio, the escarpment swallowing the last of the light, "—you've been collecting interest on a favor I never asked for."

The words landed harder than Crane expected, which annoyed him. He had been shot at twice in his life and had enjoyed better composure both times.

"I protected you," Crane said.

"You imprisoned me. Gently. With excellent benefits." Yarrow shook his head slowly. "Fifteen years, Aldous. I mapped dead rail lines because live ones had people on them, and people meant decisions, and decisions meant someone like you arriving with a ledger. You didn't keep me safe from the world. You kept me safe *for* yourself. For today. For this."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Down in the gorge, the river made its endless argument against the rocks, and lost, and tried again.

"You're wrong about one thing," Crane said at last, and his voice had gone soft, which those who knew him understood to be the most dangerous register he owned. "I am not the architect of what you fled. I'm the architect of what you *found*. Think about it, Emrys. Who funded the Meridian Expedition? Who chose the route? Who insisted the survey push past the falls, past the gorge, up to the escarpment itself?" He watched the realization arrive in Yarrow's face, slow and cold as frost forming on glass. "Yes. I knew what was under there. I've known for thirty years. My grandfather helped bury it. The founding settlement, the laborers, the shackles your cartographer found — none of it was lost history, Emrys. It was *managed* history. My family has been managing it since before either of us was born. The expedition wasn't a discovery. It was an inspection."

"Then Sera found what you already knew."

"Sera Voss found what three generations of Cranes paid to keep unfindable. And she wants to publish it." Crane rose, joints protesting, and walked to the tunnel mouth, where the darkness breathed cool air against his face. "Here is what I cannot make you understand, and God knows I've tried: it isn't cruelty. It isn't even greed, not really. It's arithmetic. If this comes out, the settlements collapse, the land titles unravel, the courts drown in claims from families who've been dead a century — and living ones who haven't. Thousands of people lose everything based on bones in a hole. Is that justice? Or is it just a different massacre, conducted with paperwork?"

"Some truths cost," Yarrow said behind him. "That doesn't make them false."

"No," Crane agreed. "It makes them expensive. And I have never in my life paid a bill I could avoid." He turned back. "So. The writ stands. The records are state property. Rennick's men are at every entrance you know about, and two you don't. Give me the documents, Emrys. Give me the woman's silence. And I will give you back the thing I took from you — reinstatement, full honors, every closed line in the territory opened and signed in your name. The debt paid in full. Both directions. Clean ledger."

Yarrow stood. He was not a tall man, but standing there at the mouth of a tunnel that held the bones of the country's first sin, he seemed to occupy considerably more space than his measurements allowed.

"You want a clean ledger," he said. "But you've never once written down what you owe. Only what you're owed."

"I'm offering you everything you lost."

"You're offering me a smaller cage with the door painted open." Yarrow picked up his satchel. "Sera memorized the documents, Aldous. All of them. Tomas carries copies sewn into his coat. You can burn every crate on this mountain and the truth walks out anyway, on two legs, wearing a coat."

For the first time in thirty years, Aldous Crane felt the ledger tilt beneath him.

"That's unfortunate," he said quietly. "Because I gave Rennick the other orders this morning. And Rennick, whatever his faults, follows instructions." He glanced toward the darkening gorge, where somewhere above, an old rail spur clung to the cliff face like a signature on a contract nobody had read closely enough. "If they reach that trestle before dawn, Emrys, there will be nothing left to negotiate. I'd suggest you run."

Yarrow was already running.

* * *

Crane stood alone at the tunnel mouth for a long while, listening to the river lose its argument below. Then he straightened his coat, checked his watch, and began, with the unhurried dignity of a man who had never once been late for the end of anything, to climb.
