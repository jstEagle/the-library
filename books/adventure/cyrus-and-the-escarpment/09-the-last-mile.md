## 9. The Last Mile

The hand-car had been waiting for us at the end of the spur like an old dog that had given up expecting anyone to come home. Emrys Yarrow had found it under a tarp of rotted canvas, and when he peeled the covering back, the thing gleamed—oiled, greased, its handles wrapped in fresh leather.

"Someone's been maintaining this," Tomas said.

"I have," Emrys said. "Every spring. For fifteen years."

Okonkwo was dead. That was the arithmetic of the morning, and it sat in Emrys's chest like a stone he'd swallowed whole. The geologist had gone back into the dark with a charge of blasting powder and a joke—"Tell them I finally got my name in the literature"—and brought half a mountain down behind him. It had bought them hours. Maybe three. Rennick's men were already digging through the rubble with the patience of men who were paid by the month rather than by the result.

Which meant there was no time to be careful, and every reason to be.

The spur ran four miles along the face of the escarpment before it met the old maintenance trestle over the gorge—a structure Emrys had surveyed himself, once, in another life, and had privately rated as *structurally sentimental*. It would hold a hand-car. It would probably hold a hand-car twice. He was counting on *probably*, because the alternative was staying here when Rennick arrived, and Rennick did not negotiate anymore. Whatever bargain he'd offered in the tunnels had been his last act of diplomacy. Crane's writ had arrived by riverboat that dawn: the expedition declared lost, all records state property, survivors to be detained for their own protection. The magistrate's seal looked very official. Official things, Emrys had learned, were usually the most expensive lies on the market.

"You're sure about this part," Sera said. She stood at the tunnel mouth behind him, rifle slung across her back, her hands steady in the way he knew meant they weren't steady at all.

"No," Emrys said. "I'm sure about the other part. The part where we don't stay here."

She almost smiled. Fifteen years, and she still almost smiled the same way—one corner first, like a door opening on a warm room. "Then go. And Emrys—" She took his collar in both hands and straightened it, which was absurd, because he was about to ride a hand-car down a crumbling rail line above a quarter-mile gorge, and no one would see whether his collar was straight. "The documents are in the oilskin. Double-wrapped. If you lose them, I will memorize them again from memory, and I will be insufferable about how well I remember them for the rest of your life."

"You've read them enough to recite them?"

"Tomas sewed copies into his coat lining. I read those. Then I read the originals. Then I made Okonkwo quiz me." Her voice caught on the name, just slightly, and recovered. "I can recite the shipping manifests of 1841, Emrys. There are worse ways a woman could spend a siege."

He kissed her then—not the careful kiss of the reunion two days ago, but the other kind, the kind that assumed a future and dared it to object. When he pulled back, her eyes were bright and furious and alive.

"Hold the tunnel mouth," he said. "Don't be a hero. Be a wall. Walls survive."

"Walls are boring," Sera said. "Go."

* * *

The hand-car ran sweet. That was the terrible thing about it—fifteen years of solitary maintenance, and the machine answered him like a conversation he'd never stopped having. Pump, glide. Pump, glide. The rails sang under the wheels in a voice he knew better than any human voice except one, and the gorge dropped away on his left in tiers of green shadow, the river below running thin and silver as wire.

Behind him, faint through the rock, came the first sounds of the tunnel mouth: a shot, then another, then Sera's rifle answering in its flat, unhurried way. One round. Another. A wall, not a hero. He pumped harder.

The trestle appeared around the shoulder of the cliff all at once, the way it always did—four hundred feet of timber and iron lace stretched across the gorge, built by men who had been dead longer than Emrys had been alive, held together now mostly by habit. His survey notes from nine years ago had flagged eleven compromised bents. He'd reported them. The report had gone into a drawer, because the line was closed and closed lines don't get repairs; they get forgotten, which is what Emrys had wanted for himself, and he'd understood even then that the trestle was a kind of portrait.

He rolled onto it at speed and felt the whole structure take a breath.

Timber groaned somewhere below his feet, deep in the skeleton of the thing. The hand-car bucked over a joint where the rails had spread a finger's width apart, and the crates lashed to the deck shifted—oilskin and rope and the entire buried history of the country, rattling like dishes in an earthquake. Emrys pumped on. Halfway. The wind off the river came up through the ties and smelled of water and old iron.

That was when he heard the second hand-car.

It came onto the trestle behind him with a sound like a struck bell, and Emrys turned his head and saw Aldous Crane standing upright on the deck, gripping the handle, white-haired and immaculate even now, with two of Rennick's men pumping behind him. The old man's coat streamed in the gorge wind. He looked, absurdly, like a man arriving at a garden party.

"Yarrow!" Crane's voice carried clean over the water. "You're running toward the far abutment. There is nothing there! The line ends in a cut filled with slide debris—I paid for that survey myself!"

"You paid for the survey that said it couldn't be cleared," Emrys called back, without stopping. "I did the honest one."

Pump, glide. The gap between them held steady—Crane's car was heavier, five men instead of one, but Crane's men were soldiers and soldiers pump in rhythm, and rhythm wins. The gap began to close. Forty feet. Thirty.

Emrys reached the far end of the trestle and did what he had come to do.

There was a junction bolt there—he'd oiled that too, every spring, fifteen years, a man maintaining a door only he knew existed—and beyond it the rails continued into the cut, into the slide, into the four miles of dead track that ended nowhere at all. He threw the lever. The points swung. And then he jumped, hitting the gravel of the abutment shoulder hard enough to see stars, rolling, coming up with the pry bar he'd strapped across his back for exactly this purpose.

Crane's car was sixty feet out on the trestle when Emrys drove the bar under the joint and heaved.

The rail came up with a shriek. Not gracefully—it fought him, rusted spikes screaming out of ancient wood—but it came up, and it came up angled, and sixty feet ahead of Aldous Crane the track simply stopped being a track and became a suggestion.

"Brake!" someone shouted on the car. Hands hauled at the lever. The deck shrieked, wood smoking against iron, and the car slewed sideways and stopped with its front wheels six inches from the gap and the gorge yawning beyond.

For a long moment nobody moved. The wind talked. Far away, downstream, the river kept its own counsel.

Then Aldous Crane stepped carefully over the evidence crates, walked to the front of the halted car, and looked down at the gap, and then back along the trestle—at the way he'd come, where Emrys now stood with the pry bar still in his hands and no intention whatsoever of using it as anything but a pry bar.

"You could rebuild it," Crane said. His voice had gone quiet, conversational, the voice of a man doing sums. "An hour's work. Two. My men have tools."

"And I'd be at the far end of the cut with a rifle," Emrys said, "which I also have."

Crane considered this. Behind him, his men looked at the gap, and at the crates, and at each other, and Emrys watched something happen in their faces—the particular arithmetic of hired men realizing the ledger has changed. They hadn't signed on to die on a trestle for paper. Paper burned. Everyone knew paper burned.

"You should have taken the offer," Crane said. "Reinstatement. Your lines reopened. A life, Yarrow. I was offering you a life."

"You were offering me your life," Emrys said. "Mine had a valley town in it, and you'd have paved it."

Something moved behind the old man's eyes—not anger. Recognition, maybe. Or the last flicker of a man who had spent forty years buying the world and discovering, at the end of the transaction, that he was the only thing left on his side of the counter.

Crane put one hand on the nearest crate. The oilskin wrapping gleamed wetly in the sun.

"These documents," he said, "are worth more than everyone on this river. You understand that. Everything they name, everything they prove—it comes down like a house. And houses fall on the people underneath."

"They were always going to fall," Emrys said. "We're just choosing who's standing clear when they do."

Crane smiled then, and it was the worst thing Emrys had seen in fifteen years, because it was fond. "You were the best surveyor I ever corrupted," he said, "and the only one I ever failed to."

And he ordered his men to push.

They refused. Of course they refused—six inches of gap, a gorge, a dead man's errand. But Crane had anticipated refusal; Emrys understood that a half-second too late, watching the old man's hand move not toward the men but toward the brake lever, releasing it, throwing his full slight weight against the car as it lurched forward into the gap—

"No!" Emrys was moving, dropping the pry bar, sprinting back along the ties, but the trestle was long and Crane's mathematics were short. The hand-car went down nose-first, crates tearing free of their lashings, oilskin bundles blooming open in the air like dark flowers, and Aldous Crane rode it all the way down with his hat coming off and his white hair streaming upward, and if he screamed, the river took it before the sound could climb.

The splash, when it came, was very small.

Emrys stood at the broken edge of the track with his chest heaving, looking down at the water closing over the place where the founding sin of the country had just drowned in its original packaging. Gone. All of it. The manifests, the letters, the photographs Okonkwo had taken with such patient, scholarly care. Four hundred feet down, in the deepest water on the river, in crates built to survive exactly this.

He heard footsteps on the gravel behind him and did not turn, because he knew the stride.

"The originals are gone," he said.

"Mm," said Sera Voss, coming to stand beside him at the edge, her rifle slung, her breath ragged from the tunnel mouth and the climb. "Tomas's copies aren't. Sewn into his coat, like I said. And mine—" she tapped her temple, "—are filed under 1841, cross-referenced, and insufferably complete."

Emrys stared at her. "You let me think—"

"I let you *focus*," Sera said. "There's a difference. Also, you were very dramatic just now, and I didn't want to interrupt." Her hand found his, and her grip was shaking now, all the steadiness spent, and he held on. Below them the river ran on, carrying nothing but water.

Far upstream, at the tunnel mouth, someone was shouting Tomas's name in relief, and the sound came down the cut thin and bright as birdsong.

"We should get off this mountain," Emrys said. "Before anyone else arrives with a writ."

"Yes," Sera agreed. "And Emrys? When we get down—there's a closed line I've been meaning to walk. I hear the man who maintains it is very thorough."

He looked at her, and at the gorge, and at the small bright wreckage of everything they'd lost and everything, impossibly, they hadn't.

"Every spring," he said. "Fifteen years. You have no idea how thorough."
