## 10. Miles and Years

Sera Voss had always believed that history arrived the way weather did—gradually, then all at once. She had simply never expected to be standing in a print shop when it broke over the coast like a squall.

The *Callow's Reach Courier* ran it on the front page, three weeks after she'd come downriver with a coat full of borrowed clothes and a head full of other people's names. THE MERIDIAN PAPERS, the headline said, and beneath it, in type so black it looked bruised: *Founding Settlement Built on Buried Massacre, Documents Prove.*

She read it standing up, because sitting down felt like surrender. The typesetter had gotten most of it right. He'd gotten Okonkwo's name right, which mattered more to her than anything else on the page. He'd spelled Tomas correctly too, and had rendered Rennick as "a man of divided loyalties," which was the gentlest lie Sera had ever watched a newspaper tell.

The story moved through the coastal cities the way floodwater moves through a valley—finding every low place, filling them. By the second week there were magistrates resigning in two provinces. By the third, Crane's shipping concern had suspended operations, its board issuing statements that used the word *posthumously* with the delicacy of men handling something that might still bite. Aldous Crane had been dead for a month now, lost on the trestle with the crates he'd valued more than his own life, and still his shadow fell across boardrooms and courtrooms alike. Sera found she could not mourn him cleanly. Grief, she was learning, came in grades of purity, and Crane's death was alloyed all the way through—with anger, with a terrible understanding, even, once or twice, with pity. He had spent his whole life buying things to keep the world from changing, and in the end the world changed anyway, and it hadn't cost him a thing he wasn't already willing to spend.

She published under the expedition's name. Not her own—never her own. *The Meridian Expedition*, byline-less, the way a cairn is built without a signature. The pamphlet went out in an edition of five hundred, and then two thousand, and then the printer stopped counting. In it she named every dead member of the team: Halloran, who had voted to go public first; Devereux and Marsh, gone over the escarpment with the evidence crates and never seen again; Okonkwo, who had held a tunnel down on his own shoulders so the rest of them could walk out of the dark. She wrote their names in full, with their dates, the way you carve stone. It was the only monument anyone was going to get, and she intended for it to bear weight.

Tomas helped her fold copies for mailing until his fingers were gray with ink, and teased her about becoming famous against her will. "You could put your name on it," he said one evening, folding with the brisk competence of a man who had sewn documents into his own coat and therefore feared nothing made of paper. "You earned it."

"The expedition earned it," Sera said. "I just survived it. Those aren't the same thing, and I intend to keep them separate."

"You always were stubborn."

"It's the Voss inheritance. You got the sewing. I got the mule."

He laughed, and she let herself laugh too, and for a moment the room smelled only of ink and tea instead of river water and old bones. That was the trick of the weeks after, she was finding—you didn't heal so much as you learned which hours of the day were safe to feel things in. Mornings belonged to work. Evenings, sometimes, belonged to Tomas. And one particular hour, late, when the lamp burned low and the house was quiet—that hour belonged to a man upcountry whom she had not written to, because some letters had to be delivered in person or not at all.

She knew where he was. Of course she knew. Emrys Yarrow had spent fifteen years making himself unfindable, and then had spent one night telling her, in the dark above the gorge, exactly how he'd done it. Closed lines. Decommissioned spurs. The geography nobody bothered to look at anymore. He'd handed her the map of his invisibility like a man handing over a hostage, and she had memorized it the same way she'd memorized the documents—because some things you carried whether or not you were ever asked to.

So she bought passage on the northbound freight, and then a seat on the mail coach, and then walked the last eleven miles along a road that dwindled into a track that dwindled into nothing at all, which was precisely the sort of address Emrys would choose.

* * *

Emrys Yarrow heard her coming before he saw her, which was fitting, because he had spent fifteen years listening for trains that would never run and had gotten very good at hearing things that weren't there yet.

It was the gravel first—a footfall with a cartographer's stride, even-paced, unhurried, the walk of someone reading the ground under her boots. Then the small sound of a pack strap being shifted. He was inside the section house, sorting fifteen years of maps into piles—keep, burn, burn, possibly keep, burn—when the door opened without a knock, and there she stood in the doorway with the evening light behind her, looking exactly like the last page of a book he'd been afraid to finish.

"You're burning your maps," she said. Not a question. She'd seen the smoke through the window, thin and gray against the pines.

"Some of them," Emrys said. "The ones no one should ever find." He gestured at the stove, where the corner of a survey chart was curling into ash—the old spur lines, the ghost routes, the geography of a man who had spent a decade and a half being nowhere in particular. "Turns out hiding is a lot of paperwork."

"Is it working?"

"It worked beautifully right up until a telegram arrived asking for me by name." He set down the map in his hands. It was the escarpment spur, the one they'd run the hand-car down, the line that had saved them and cost everything. He looked at it a long moment, then fed it to the stove. "I've decided I'm retiring from invisible."

Sera came further into the room, setting her pack down with the care of someone who had carried heavy things a long way and meant to set them down permanently if the house proved worthy. "I'm not here to pull you back into the world," she said. "I want to be clear about that. The world and I have concluded our business. I published. They listened. Tomas is teaching himself to sleep through the night again. It's done."

"Then what are you here for?"

"I thought," she said, and for the first time since the doorway her voice wavered, just slightly, the way a compass needle wavers before it settles, "that you might want company on a closed line. There's one I've been meaning to walk. Runs northwest out of here, forty miles, ends at a station that hasn't seen a passenger since before either of us could drive a spike. It appears on none of your maps, incidentally. I checked."

"I burned those."

"All the more reason," she said, "to go see what we're missing."

Emrys laughed—it came up out of him rough, from disuse, but it came—and crossed the small room to her, and for a while neither of them said anything at all, because some reunions are conducted entirely without words, and this one had been fifteen years and one collapsed empire in the making.

Later, over tea, she told him about the papers, the resignations, the pamphlet, the names carved in ink. He listened the way he listened to rivers—with his whole body, leaning slightly toward the sound. When she got to Okonkwo's name he closed his eyes.

"He held the tunnel," Emrys said quietly. "Four minutes. Maybe five. Long enough."

"Long enough," Sera agreed. "That's the whole epitaph. Long enough."

They sat with that. Outside, the wind moved through the pines with a sound like a distant train, and neither of them flinched at it, which was new, and good.

"I kept one map," Emrys admitted finally. He pulled a folded sheet from his breast pocket—not a survey, not a route, just a hand-drawn line with two small marks on it and nothing else. "Forty miles, northwest. Ends at a dead station. Appears on no official record whatsoever."

"That sounds dangerously like a beginning."

"It's the only kind of line I've ever trusted." He folded it away. "We'll need provisions. And you'll need to teach me the difference between easting and northing again—I've been faking it for years and no one's caught me yet."

"Everyone's caught you, Emrys. We were all just polite about it."

They left two days later, at dawn, walking the ties of a line that led nowhere on any map but theirs. The gravel crunched. The pines gave way to open country. Somewhere behind them the section house stood empty, its stove cold, its secrets ash, and somewhere ahead—forty miles, give or take, though neither of them was in any hurry to be precise—there was a dead station with a roof that probably leaked, and time enough to patch it, and years enough after that to fill with whatever they chose.

Halfway through the first morning, Sera stopped, crouched, and brushed the gravel aside from a tie stamp so faint it might have been a scar: a maker's mark, a date, a number. She copied it into her notebook, because she was who she was.

"What is it?" Emrys called back.

"A date," she said, standing. "Fifty-one years old. This line was laid fifty-one years ago, by men whose names aren't anywhere."

Emrys came back and looked at it. Then he took off his hat, briefly, the way you do.

"Well," he said, putting it back on. "Then I suppose somebody ought to know where it goes."

And they walked on, two figures on a line that ended nowhere, which was another way of saying it could end anywhere at all.
