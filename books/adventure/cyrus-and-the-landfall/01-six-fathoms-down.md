## 1. Six Fathoms Down

The tide turned at 4:17, and Cyrus Eriksen was already in the water.

They'd learned to read the Vessany tides the way other people read faces—by feel, by habit, by the small betrayals of current against the hull. The *Kestrel* sat anchored a half-mile off the cordon, engines cold, riding so low and gray in the pre-dawn water that she might have been driftwood. From the navy patrol line, she would read as nothing. A fishing skiff that had lost her nerve. A shadow with opinions about where to float.

Cyrus liked being nothing. It was the one trade they'd ever mastered completely.

The water took them like a held breath finally released. Cold first—the reef waters off Vessany never truly warmed, even in high summer—and then the slow pressure of depth settling over their shoulders like a coat they'd been fitted for. Six fathoms down, the world went green and quiet. Above, the patrol boats would be running their sweep pattern: two cutters, lazy arcs, searchlights sweeping water that was too deep and too dark for searchlights to mean anything. The navy liked its cordons visible. Visibility was the point. Nobody was supposed to look at what was underneath.

Cyrus looked.

The wreck of the *Meridian Star* rose out of the seafloor like a drowned cathedral. She'd gone down eleven years ago with all hands—or so the official record said, ninety-one souls lost to a storm the charts said hadn't been blowing—and in eleven years the sea had dressed her in coral and barnacle and drifting weed until she looked less like a ship than a reef that had once had ambitions. Her masts were gone. Her hull gaped along the port side where the storm—or whatever it had been—had opened her like a letter someone got tired of reading.

Cyrus had been diving her for three weeks now. Alone, always alone, because alone was how you stayed invisible, and invisible was how you stayed alive, and alive was how you kept the *Kestrel*, and the *Kestrel* was the last thing on this coast that belonged to them outright. Debts didn't count as belonging. Nothing else did either, really. But the boat floated, and floating was enough.

They worked by touch and lamplight, moving through the wreck's ribs with the economy of long practice. Brass first—it always paid, always moved quietly through Ondine Marrec's ledgers without questions, because brass was honest salvage and honest salvage was boring and boring things survived. A porthole here, freed with two turns of the wrench. A fitting there. Cyrus's knife slipped under a nameplate near the companionway, worked it loose, and they paused, holding it up to the lamp.

**MERIDIAN STAR**. The letters were furred with growth but legible. Someone had polished this plate once, back when polishing it mattered, back when it hung above a door men walked through on their way to work.

"Sorry," Cyrus murmured into the regulator mouthpiece, out of a habit they couldn't have explained to anyone. The words came out as bubbles. They always apologized to wrecks. It was the only courtesy the dead were likely to get from anyone anymore, given how the navy had ringed this one with gunboats and lies.

Eleven years of cordons. Eleven years of *restricted waters, unauthorized vessels will be fired upon*. For a storm casualty. Cyrus had run salvage on plenty of storm casualties, and not one of them had ever come with a patrol schedule.

That was the thing nobody in port wanted to say out loud, though everyone thought it over their beer: ships that sink get salvaged. Ships that get *guarded* get buried.

Cyrus tucked the nameplate into the mesh bag and moved deeper into the wreck's throat.

* * *

The galley still had cups in it.

That was the detail that got under Cyrus's skin every dive, the one they couldn't scrub out no matter how many times they saw it. Cups in the racks. Boots by the bunks, some of them still laced. A deck of cards fused into a single brick by eleven years of salt, lying where somebody's hand had set it down mid-game. The *Meridian Star* hadn't sunk so much as she'd been interrupted. Whatever had happened down here in the dark, it had happened fast, and it had happened to men who hadn't seen it coming.

Cyrus swam the crew corridor without touching anything. There was brass in the officers' country aft—there was always brass where the officers slept—but taking it felt different from taking it out of the engine room. In the engine room it was just metal. Back here it was someone's shaving kit, someone's letters, someone's good coat still hanging on its hook because the man wearing it had been somewhere else when the water came.

Somewhere else. Forward, maybe. Or below. Or nowhere at all, if the water had taken him before he could run.

Cyrus shook it off. Sentiment was weight, and weight was air, and air was time, and time was the whole game down here. They had perhaps forty minutes before the tide's arithmetic started working against them, and the strongbox was still twenty yards into the dark.

It lay in the purser's office, wedged beneath a collapsed shelf, exactly where the survey chart—a friend-of-a-friend document, photocopied twice and paid for in cash—said it would be. Iron-bound, brass-cornered, sealed with a lock that had rusted into sculpture. Cyrus had found it on the fourth dive and spent three more dives telling themselves not to open it. Not yet. Open it aboard, in daylight, with the hatch shut and an alibi ready. That was the discipline. You didn't open mysteries forty feet underwater with your name written all over the site in bubble-trail.

But you lifted it. That much was safe. That much was just salvage.

The box came free with a groan of shifting wreckage, and a silt cloud bloomed around them, soft as smoke. Cyrus hugged it to their chest—awkward, heavy, heavier than brass had any right to be—and kicked for the gap in the port-side hull.

That was when the searchlight swept the water above.

Cyrus froze, a habit older than thought, and let their body go neutral against the wreck's flank. The light came down through the green in a long pale blade, swinging, probing, touching the coral heads and sliding off. Through the regulator's hiss they could hear it now, faint and distorted: engine noise. Not the lazy arc of the morning sweep. Something closer. Something *stopped*.

A cutter, holding station almost directly overhead. Close enough that if anyone up there leaned over the rail and looked straight down with a glass, they might see the shimmer of exhaled bubbles rising from a wreck that was supposed to be empty of everything except fish and official secrets.

Cyrus killed the lamp. Darkness closed in, total and familiar, and they hung there in it with a stolen strongbox in their arms, listening to the navy breathe above them.

One minute. Two. The engine note changed—idling down further, or maybe just Cyrus's pulse settling enough to hear properly. Bubbles ticked up past their face, silver threads climbing toward a surface they couldn't see, toward eyes they had to assume were looking.

*You're nothing,* Cyrus told themselves, the old litany, the one that had gotten them through orphan docks and borrowed berths and eleven years of diving sites that polite divers wouldn't touch. *You're a shadow with opinions about where to float. Shadows don't get caught. Shadows don't get asked questions. Shadows go home.*

The light swung away. The engine note climbed. The cutter resumed its arc, bored again, and Cyrus counted to sixty before letting their legs move.

* * *

Surfacing was the dangerous part. Everyone knew the descent was dangerous—pressure, darkness, the wreck itself—but Cyrus had always thought the ascent was worse, because on the way up you stopped being a diver and became a person again, a person in a boat, a person with a face and a name and a mesh bag full of evidence. The water gave you back to the world whether you wanted returning or not.

They came up on the far side of the *Kestrel*, low and slow, keeping the hull between themselves and the patrol line. The dawn was doing its work now, bruising the east gray-pink, laying a long gold path across the water that pointed directly at the navy cutters like an accusation. Cyrus hauled the strongbox over the gunwale, shed the tank, and sat breathing hard in the bottom of their own boat, watching the light come up on a wreck they'd just robbed.

Or rescued. Depends who was asking.

The *Meridian Star*'s superstructure broke the swell a quarter mile south, black and weed-hung, marked by a navy buoy painted in warning colors. Cyrus looked at it while their heart rate came down. Eleven years of buoy. Eleven years of *keep away*. And in three weeks of diving her, Cyrus had catalogued enough oddities to fill a notebook they kept wrapped in oilcloth under the bunk: the hull breach that ran wrong for storm damage, inward not outward; the cargo manifest gaps in the photocopied survey; the fact that a merchant vessel lost with all hands had been cordoned within *hours*, before any family in Vessany could even charter a boat out to mourn her.

Ninety-one souls. Port records said most of the families had left the coast within two years of the sinking. Moved inland, moved away, moved on. All of them? All ninety-one families, scattered like chaff, none of them staying to argue with the navy's finding? Cyrus had grown up around grief; grief didn't scatter. Grief *anchored*. Grief built little shrines and argued with harbormasters and showed up at every anniversary with flowers it couldn't afford. Grief like that left marks, and the *Meridian Star*'s grief had left none.

Which meant someone had made sure it left none.

Cyrus pushed the thought down where it lived, next to the other thoughts they weren't having yet, and got to work. The strongbox sat in the bilge, streaming seawater, patient as a coffin. They didn't open it. Discipline. Instead they stowed it under the false floor panels with the brass, covered it, and put the *Kestrel* on a slow, aimless-looking heading toward port, the kind of heading a small-time salvager would steer after a disappointing night of finding nothing.

The sun cleared the headland as they ran in. Behind them, the cutters held their pattern, and the buoy bobbed over the drowned ship, and ninety-one men kept their silence the way they'd kept it for eleven years.

Cyrus almost believed the silence would hold.

Then they saw the harbor.

Ondine Marrec stood on the end of the customs pier with her arms crossed and her ledger under one arm, and she was not looking at the fleet, or the market, or the gulls. She was looking at the *Kestrel*. She had the particular stillness of a woman who has decided to stop waiting, and Cyrus felt the day's first real chill—not the reef's cold, which they knew how to bear, but the colder thing underneath it, the thing they'd been diving to outrun for a month now.

Debts didn't count as belonging. But debts, unlike wrecks, always surfaced.

Cyrus brought the *Kestrel* in anyway, because there was nowhere else to bring her, and because a shadow with opinions about where to float still had to float *somewhere*.

Ondine watched them the whole way in.
