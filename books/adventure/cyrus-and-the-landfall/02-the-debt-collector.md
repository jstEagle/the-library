## 2. The Debt Collector

The *Kestrel* came into Vessany harbor on the afternoon tide, riding low and honest, her hold full of brass that would sell clean and a strongbox that would not.

Cyrus took the channel slow. That was the trick of it—slow, unremarkable, the boat of a man with nowhere particular to be. The harbor at Vessany was a horseshoe of gray stone and grayer water, ringed with drying nets and the smell of tar, and by now every soul who worked it knew the shape of Cyrus Eriksen's little salvage ketch the way they knew the shape of gulls. Familiar enough to ignore. That had always been the point.

They tied off at the far end of the north quay, where the pilings were rotten and nobody moored if they could help it. Cyrus paid the extra attention anyway, checking lines twice, because a man who worked alone learned quickly that his own carelessness was the only crew he could not fire.

The strongbox sat in the bilge under a false floor, wrapped in oilcloth, cold as a drowned thing even through the canvas. Cyrus had not opened it. Had not wanted to open it, which was stranger. Out there, forty feet down in the green dark with patrol boats dragging their wakes across the surface like fingers feeling for a pulse, the box had been simple: heavy, sealed, valuable. Here in daylight, with the harbor bells ringing the hour and children running along the quay chasing a dog, it felt less like salvage and more like something Cyrus had stolen from a grave without asking permission.

*Later,* Cyrus told it silently. *You keep.*

The brass was another matter. Brass was honest work. Cyrus hauled the crates up onto the dock one at a time, shoulders burning, and began the long familiar ritual of making stolen navy property look like junk salvaged from anywhere at all. A wire brush here. A chisel to file away a stamped serial number there. Salt water in a bucket to age the fresh scratches. It was tedious, careful, invisible work—the kind of work Cyrus was best at, maybe the only kind.

"You're late."

Ondine Marrec stood at the head of the quay with her arms crossed, watching the tide come in behind Cyrus's shoulder as though she'd ordered it personally and found it adequate. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a bollard, with a face weathered down to its essential geometry and eyes the color of the water before a storm. She wore a coat that had been good twenty years ago and a ring of keys that announced her coming ten seconds before she did.

Everyone in Vessany answered to Ondine eventually. Berth fees, cargo weights, the cranes, the ice house, the rumor mill. Harbor boss wasn't an official title; the Admiralty had never granted it and never bothered to revoke it. It was simply true, the way the tide was true.

"I'm not late," Cyrus said, not looking up from the serial number. "I'm early for tomorrow."

"Tomorrow." Ondine came down the quay with that rolling walk of hers, boots loud on the planks. She stopped at the edge of the tarp, looked at the crates of brass, and did not look at the *Kestrel*, which was how Cyrus knew the *Kestrel* was exactly what she'd come about. "You know what I admire about you, Eriksen?"

"My punctuality."

"Your silence." She crouched, picked up a brass fitting, turned it over in her blunt fingers. "Four years you've worked my harbor. Four years, and I couldn't tell a soul one true thing about you except what you owe. You don't drink where people can hear you. You don't fight where people can see you. You don't love anybody I could lean on." She set the fitting down. "It's made you very easy to respect and very hard to protect."

Cyrus kept filing. The chisel whispered against brass. "I didn't ask for protection."

"No. You asked for credit. Two hundred crowns against the boat, four years back, when your engine blew and you'd have sold your own teeth otherwise." Ondine straightened, and her shadow fell across the work. "And I gave it, because your father pulled men out of the water for thirty years and never charged a family a coin, and I figured some of that was in the blood."

Something cold moved through Cyrus's chest, the way it always did when Ondine mentioned the old man. "Some of it," Cyrus said. "What's this about, Ondine?"

"This is about the season." She said it plainly, no theater, which was worse than theater. "Dry season's coming early this year. Navy's got the outer reef cordoned, which kills half the legitimate wreck work in the sound, and the other half is going to be picked over by the big fleets before the first month's out. Small divers are going to starve, Eriksen. I can't carry paper on a boat that can't earn."

"So call it due at season's end. One more season."

"One more season." Ondine nodded slowly, as if tasting the words. "That's what I came to say, so let me say it right, because you'll want to argue and I'd rather you heard all of it first." She folded her arms again. "One more season. The debt comes due the day the dry wind starts, not after. You clear two hundred crowns plus interest, or the *Kestrel* goes on my block, and I don't set the reserve price low."

The chisel stopped.

Cyrus looked up at her then, really looked, hunting for the give in her face, the softness, the loophole. There wasn't any. There never was. That was the thing about Ondine Marrec that people mistook for cruelty: she told you the exact truth, once, and then she lived inside it.

"The reef's closed," Cyrus said. "You know that. Halloway's patrols run the outer sound twice a day. Where am I supposed to earn two hundred crowns off a harbor where the only wrecks worth touching have navy buoys on them?"

"That's your problem, not mine." But something flickered behind her eyes—there and gone, quick as a fish turning—and Cyrus caught it, because catching things like that was the whole trade. Ondine knew something. Ondine always knew something. "You're resourceful. You've been resourceful for four years right under my nose, and I've pretended not to do arithmetic, because pretending is cheaper than knowing. But the pretending stops this season. Whatever you're doing out there—" she tipped her chin toward the sea, toward the buoy line, toward the wreck whose name neither of them spoke, "—do it fast, do it quiet, and be standing on this quay with my money when the dry wind comes. Or don't. And the boat's mine."

She turned to go, then stopped, half-turned back.

"For what it's worth," she said, quieter, "your father would have hated this arrangement. He thought a harbor should be a place a man could fail in." A shrug like a door closing. "Times change. Harbors too."

Her boots went back up the quay, loud and unhurried, and the tide kept coming in beneath the planks like nothing had happened at all.

* * *

The notice went up on the harbormaster's board three days later, and Cyrus heard about it before seeing it, which was itself a kind of warning.

"A reward," said Tam Wicker, the netmender, sidling along the north quay with his bundle of twine and his talent for being everywhere. He was a small man, quick-eyed, the sort who survived hard ports by making himself useful and forgettable in equal measure—which meant he and Cyrus understood each other perfectly, and had never once admitted it. "Navy money. Fifty crowns, they're saying. Maybe a hundred for names leading to a conviction."

"Conviction of what?"

Tam glanced left, glanced right, a habit so worn it had gone smooth. "Unauthorized salvage. That's the phrase on the paper. Unauthorized salvage of naval property within the restricted zone." He leaned closer, dropping his voice to almost nothing. "There's a drawing, Eriksen. On the notice. Of a boat."

The chisel in Cyrus's hand had been moving all this while, patient as tide. Now it stopped.

"They wouldn't put a drawing up," Cyrus said. "Notices get torn down. Drawings get remembered."

"Noticed that myself." Tam shrugged, elaborately casual, which from Tam meant terrified. "Whoever drew it knew the boat. Knew the patched jib, the port-side rub rail, the way your mast step sits crooked. That's not a sketch from a patrol report, friend. That's somebody who's watched you tie off a hundred times."

Cyrus walked to the harbormaster's board.

It hung outside the customs shed, a slab of weathered cork behind cracked glass, layered with tide tables and quarantine flags and the endless small bureaucracy of a working port. And there, centered, pinned with four brass nails—navy nails, Cyrus noted; the navy nailed things down like they expected the world to steal them—was the notice.

RESTRICTED WATERS — OUTER REEF STATION. By order of the Admiralty, all salvage operations within the marked zone are prohibited. Information regarding unauthorized recovery of naval property will be compensated. Report to Lt. E. Halloway, Naval Station Vessany.

And below the text, rendered in fine ink strokes by someone who had clearly stood on a quay and looked a long time: a small ketch with a patched jib and a crooked mast step.

Fifty crowns. It was a fortune to half the people who'd read that board. A winter's wages. A debt, nearly, paid off in a single act of pointing.

Cyrus stood there long enough to seem like anyone—another idler reading the news—and memorized the notice the way divers memorize a wreck: layout, exits, everything worth taking. Then, because invisibility was a discipline and not a gift, Cyrus bought a cup of coffee from the cart on the corner, drank it slowly, complained about the price loudly, and left.

Lt. E. Halloway was not difficult to find. That was the unsettling part. Navy officers in Vessany were usually ghosts—you saw their launches, their ensigns, their cordons, and rarely their faces. But Halloway had taken to walking the harbor in the evenings, alone, in a uniform that fit him badly in the way of a man who had recently lost or gained something and hadn't adjusted. Youngish. Careful. He stopped at the fish stalls and the chandlers and the board itself, and he talked to people—not interrogating, just talking, asking how the season looked, whether the herring had come in, whether anyone had seen anything unusual out past the buoys.

He was building a map, Cyrus realized. Not of the water. Of the people. Who talked, who didn't, who owed whom.

On the fourth evening, Halloway walked the north quay.

Cyrus was working the *Kestrel*'s deck seams with a mallet and oakum, hands busy, eyes down, the universal posture of a man with nothing to hide and no time to talk. The lieutenant's footsteps came along the planks, paused opposite the boat, and stayed there.

"Evening."

"Officer." Cyrus didn't look up. Kept driving oakum.

"Halloway. Lieutenant." A pause, the sound of a man deciding how much authority to spend on a stranger. "Nice boat. Older model. You dive her yourself?"

"Sometimes. When the weather allows and the wrecks aren't claimed."

"Most of them are claimed these days."

"Most of them are drowned these days," Cyrus said, and drove another seam. "Claimed is a word for paper. Drowned is a word for fact. I stick to facts."

Halloway was quiet a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had shifted—less officer, more tired man. "You know the Meridian Star station is closed."

The mallet kept its rhythm. Inside, behind the ribs, something went very still. "Everybody knows it. It's on your board."

"Do you know why?"

"I know the navy doesn't close stations over good news."

Another silence, longer. Cyrus risked the glance then—one flick of the eyes, cataloguing: the lieutenant's hands, empty; his posture, squared but not aggressive; his eyes, which were doing something complicated, looking at the boat and at Cyrus and at some middle distance where perhaps a different version of his career was playing out.

"There were ninety-one men on that ship," Halloway said, so quietly it was almost not speech. "The board says lost with all hands. Weather event. Structural failure." He straightened, and the officer came back into his spine like a man putting on a coat. "If anyone had information suggesting otherwise, the Admiralty would want it handled properly. Through proper channels. Not—" a small gesture that took in the quay, the harbor, the whole gray economy of Vessany, "—not the way things usually get handled here."

"Is that a warning or an invitation, Lieutenant?"

"It's a notice." He touched his cap brim, a gesture precise and hollow. "Read your board. Good evening."

He walked on down the quay, and Cyrus watched him go, mallet forgotten, heart working slow and heavy like a pump bringing something up from depth.

*Ninety-one men.* Everyone in Vessany knew the number; it had been said at the memorial service four years ago, said in the bars for a season, said by mothers until saying it hurt too much and they stopped. Ninety-one, and then the story had closed over them the way water closes over a sinking thing—smooth, official, final. *Weather event. Structural failure.* The navy was good at words like that. The navy had probably written the book.

Cyrus looked down through the deck boards, through the false floor, to where the strongbox lay wrapped in oilcloth in the dark of the bilge.

*Later,* Cyrus had told it. The tide tables hung inside the cabin door, and Cyrus went and stood before them now, tracing the days with a salt-cracked finger. Spring tides. Big lows. The kind of water that pulled back far enough to show things that preferred to stay hidden.

Six big lows before the dry season shut the reef down for good. Six chances.

Behind, up the quay, Ondine's office lamp burned yellow in the dusk, and somewhere beyond the breakwater a navy launch ran its evening circuit, searchlight sweeping the buoy line like a slow deliberate finger. Two hundred crowns by dry wind, or the boat. Fifty crowns for a name, posted in brass nails on a public board, with a drawing of the *Kestrel* that anyone in this harbor could collect on any morning they chose.

Invisible had always been the plan. Invisible had a price tag now, and the tag read both ways: stay hidden and drown in debt, or surface and hang.

Cyrus rolled the tide table closed and stood a long moment in the dark of the cabin, listening to the water slap the hull.

Then, quietly, almost against their own will, their eyes went to the bilge—to the box—and for the first time since hauling it up from forty fathoms, Cyrus wanted to know what was inside.
