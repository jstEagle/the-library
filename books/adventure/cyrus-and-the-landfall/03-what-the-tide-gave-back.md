## 3. What the Tide Gave Back

The tide went out wrong.

Cyrus had worked Vessany waters for eleven years and knew the sea's moods the way other people knew their own hands—the way it breathed twice a day, the way it lied about depth in the afternoon light. But this morning, standing on the deck of the *Kestrel* with coffee going cold in one hand, Cyrus watched the water fall away from the reef like something being pulled off a wound.

It had started three days ago. The almanac called it a spring tide, the moon pulling full and close, but this was more than that. The old fishermen at the breakwater had been muttering about it since dawn—*the sea's showing its bones*, one of them said, and spat over the side for luck. The water around the wreck of the *Meridian Star* had dropped nearly four meters below its lowest normal mark, and it was still going.

Cyrus killed the engine and drifted.

The navy cordon buoys sat high and dry on exposed rock now, their warning flags hanging limp, ridiculous as party decorations on a corpse. The wreck itself—forty years of silt and coral grown over a naval transport that officially sank in a storm—stood out of the water like the ribcage of something enormous that had died mid-stride. Cyrus had dived her a dozen times, always in the grey half-light before the patrol boats made their rounds, always with a plan: brass fittings here, porthole glass there, nothing that would be missed, nothing that would be traced.

Cyrus had never seen her like this. Never seen what was *under* her.

Where the hull's shadow met the seafloor, where the charts showed only sand and rubble, there was an opening. A cave mouth, black and breathing, wide enough to drive a skiff through. The falling tide had drained it like someone opening a drain in a flooded cellar, and from the *Kestrel*'s rail Cyrus could see down into it—could see stone steps, cut by human hands, descending into the dark.

Stone steps. Under a wreck that had supposedly gone down in open water during a storm.

Cyrus set the coffee down very carefully, as if sudden movement might wake the morning up and end this.

* * *

The approach took twenty minutes of careful poling through exposed reef, the *Kestrel*'s draft too deep for the drained shallows. Cyrus left her anchored in the last pocket of floating water, took the dive bag and the lamp, and waded the final hundred meters across seabed that hadn't seen air or sun in four decades. Anemones shriveled in the sudden light. A moray eel, evicted from its hole, gave Cyrus a look of profound offense and poured itself into deeper shadow.

The cave mouth swallowed sound. That was the first thing Cyrus noticed inside—that the sea, which never stopped talking, had gone quiet. The second thing was the smell: salt and rust and something older underneath, something like a closed room.

The lamp beam found the steps and followed them down.

They were naval work, those steps—cut clean, edged with iron railing mounts long since gone to orange lace. They descended maybe eight meters below the current low-water line, which meant that at any ordinary tide, the entire cavern was submerged. Which meant that whoever built them had built them to be reached by boat. From the sea. In secret.

At the bottom, the passage opened into a chamber the size of a chapel, and Cyrus stopped walking because the lamp couldn't find the far wall.

Water still pooled in the deepest part, black and mirror-still, reflecting the beam back up so it seemed Cyrus stood on the edge of a hole that went both directions. Along the near wall, stacked with a care that hurt to look at, were crates. Naval stenciling, faded but legible in the lamplight. And not just crates—

Personal effects. Sea chests, dozens of them, each one marked with a name in white paint. DAVENPORT. OKONKWO. ILLÉS. MERCADO. Names in rows, names in stacks, names going back into the dark farther than the light would reach.

Cyrus had robbed wrecks for eleven years and had a rule about it, unspoken even to himself: take the metal, leave the ghosts. Brass doesn't grieve. Portholes don't have mothers. But this—this wasn't cargo. This was a ship's company, packed away like they'd be coming back for it.

Like someone had expected them to come back.

Cyrus lifted the nearest chest's lid. Inside, folded with military precision: uniforms. A photograph in a tin frame, two young men squinting into sun that had set forty years ago. A woman's letter, the ink bloomed to brown, the paper soft as cloth. Cyrus put everything back exactly as it lay and found his hands weren't steady.

*This stopped being salvage,* said some quiet voice that sounded uncomfortably like his father. *You walked past the line somewhere on those stairs.*

He kept going anyway. That was the thing about him people had never understood—Ondine least of all. Curiosity in Cyrus Eriksen wasn't a trait. It was a tide. It came whether he wanted it or not, and it did not care what it drowned.

Beyond the sea chests, the chamber narrowed, and the crates changed. These were newer-looking, better sealed, and when Cyrus pried one open with the flat of his knife, the lamp caught the dull gold gleam of coin sacks, bank-wrapped bundles in oilcloth, and—a separate case, smaller, locked—something that made the breath go out of him slow.

A strongbox. Naval issue, the good kind, bolted to a pallet. Stenciled across its face: **MERIDIAN STAR — CAPTAIN'S EFFECTS — DO NOT OFFLOAD**.

*Do not offload.* As if the captain's effects were cargo. As if the man himself had been inventory.

The lock was old salt-water iron, and it surrendered to Cyrus's pry bar with a crack that echoed off walls it had no business reaching. Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas against forty years of damp—and dry, perfectly dry, the seal had held—lay a ledger bound in grey leather, and beneath it a bundle of papers tied with signal cord, and beneath *those*, a sealed tube of the kind used for orders that were never meant to be read outside a flag officer's cabin.

Cyrus knelt there a long time, not touching anything.

Every instinct he owned, every hard-earned scar of survival in the salvage trade, said the same thing: *take the coin, take the documents, sell them separately, tell no one, vanish.* There was enough in this chamber to buy a fleet. Enough to pay Ondine ten times over. Enough to disappear to some coast where no one asked questions and the navy's writ ran thin.

But the ledger was a captain's log. He knew the format on sight—he'd sold enough of them. And captains' logs recorded things. Times. Positions. Orders received. Orders given. And if the *Meridian Star* had a hidden sea-cave hold beneath her, then she hadn't sunk in a storm at all, and everyone who'd ever said so had been lying, and the ninety-one names painted on those sea chests belonged to men who had been—

Cyrus didn't finish the thought. He opened the log instead, right there on his knees in the dark, because his hands decided before his head could vote.

* * *

The early entries were ordinary. Loading at Corvane Naval Yard, weather notes, a cook reprimanded for watered grog. Captain Aldous Wren wrote a tight, disciplined hand, the kind drilled into officers who expected their logs to be audited. Cyrus skimmed forward through weeks of routine, the lamp burning steady, the tide pool at the chamber's heart giving back a faint reflected glow like a second, drowned moon.

Then, fourteen days before the sinking:

*Received sealed orders via launch, origin undisclosed. C/O instructed to alter course to Vessany Reefs after nightfall and await further instruction. Crew not informed. Am directed to log this entry as "routine coastal exercise."*

Cyrus turned the page slowly.

*Anchored in position. Launch returned at 0300 bearing one passenger, rank concealed, and written instructions which I am ordered to destroy upon execution. I have not destroyed them. Whatever is asked of me tonight, let it be known that I objected. Let the record show I objected.*

The chamber was very cold. Or Cyrus was. He couldn't tell anymore.

*Have been instructed to open the sea valves and scuttle the vessel in position over the cave mouth, with souls aboard. Passengers to be landed beforehand; crew to remain. I am told the families will be "seen to." I am told this is necessary to the security of the realm. I have ninety-one men aboard who think we are conducting exercises.*

*I will not order them below. I will log the truth here, seal it in my effects, and do what can be done. If any hand ever finds this, know that the Meridian Star was murdered, and her killers wear the same uniform I have worn for thirty-one years.*

*God forgive me. I am going to open the valves myself, so that no man under my command has to carry it.*

The last entry was dated the night of the sinking. One line:

*They are singing. I cannot stay in my cabin for it.*

Cyrus closed the log with both hands, gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child.

For a long while there was no sound in the chamber except the small, patient drip of water finding its level, and Cyrus Eriksen kneeling in the dark among the belongings of ninety-one dead men, holding the proof of how they'd died.

He thought about the patrol boats. About Halloway's reward notice nailed to the harbor board, fresh ink, *UNAUTHORIZED SALVAGE — INFORMANTS COMPENSATED*. About Ondine's debt, and the season running out, and Captain Renn Vosk's name already moving through the port bars like a rumor of weather coming. About how many people would burn down a town to keep this log from seeing daylight.

He thought: *I could put it back. Seal the box. Dive the outer wreck for brass until the debt takes the Kestrel, and live small, and die unknown, and never once have to look over my shoulder.*

And then he thought about the photograph in the tin frame. Two young men, squinting into sun. Somebody had loved at least one of them enough to pack it in a sea chest, and somebody had waited for a sailor who'd been told would come home, and nobody—not one person in forty years—had ever told those somebodies the truth.

The tide had gone out wrong, the fisherman said. The sea was showing its bones.

Maybe. Or maybe the sea had been holding this room shut for forty years, waiting for someone with a lamp and no sense of self-preservation to come down the stairs.

Cyrus wrapped the log in oilcloth, took the sealed tube of orders, and left the coin where it lay—all of it, every sack, though his fingers ached to do otherwise. At the top of the stairs he paused once, looking back down into the dark at the rows of names.

"I'll come back," he said aloud, and his voice cracked in the silence like ice on a pond. "All of you. I'll come back."

The tide had begun to turn. Out beyond the reef, far off toward the naval anchorage, a patrol boat's engine note rose and fell, rising again—closer than it should have been, closer than it had any business being at this hour.

Cyrus waded for the *Kestrel* with the log against his chest, moving fast through the returning water, and did not let himself run until the wreck's shadow hid him from the open sea.
