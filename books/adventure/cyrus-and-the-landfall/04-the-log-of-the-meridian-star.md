## 4. The Log of the Meridian Star

The lamp was a salvager's lamp—brass housing gone green with salt, a glass chimney I'd replaced three times in as many years—and it threw a circle of light no wider than a dinner plate across the chart table of my cabin. Outside, the *Kestrel* rocked on her mooring lines, and the harbor breathed its usual night sounds: halyards ticking against masts, the far-off clang of the fish house, water slapping hulls in that patient rhythm that had been the only lullaby I'd ever trusted.

I sat down with the captain's log of the *Meridian Star* and did not open it for a long time.

That's the truth of it. I sat there with my hands flat on either side of the book like a man at a séance, afraid to touch the thing in the middle. It had come up out of the cave wrapped in oilcloth inside a lead-lined document case, and the case had done its work—the pages were damp-stained but whole, the ink legible, the spine cracked but holding. Nineteen years underwater and the words inside it still had teeth.

I'd told myself all day that it was salvage. That was the word I used, the word I'd built a life around. Salvage meant ownership. Salvage meant brass fittings and copper sheathing and a strongbox whose contents could be weighed, valued, converted into debt payments and diesel fuel and another season on the water. Salvage was honest work for a man who wanted nothing from anyone.

But I'd felt the weight of it change in my hands coming up through the cave mouth, even through the lift bag's neutral buoyancy, even through forty feet of green water. Some things get heavier when you raise them.

I opened the cover.

The first page was a commissioning record in a clerk's careful hand: *H.M.S. Meridian Star, steam-assisted coastal freighter, 1,240 tons, commissioned [date redacted]*. The redaction was a bar of black ink, deliberate, modern—not the fade of saltwater but the stroke of a pen applied after the fact, which meant someone had handled this book since it went into the sea, or someone had prepared it before the ship ever sailed, knowing what would need to be hidden.

My mouth went dry. I made myself keep reading.

* * *

The early entries were ordinary, and I mean that as praise. Ordinary is what logs do best. Weather reports. Coal consumption. Cargo manifests—grain, machine parts, timber, the dull blood of a working coast. The captain's hand was small and precise, a schoolmaster's hand, and he signed himself *E. Vasser, Master*, with a flourish that shrank a little every week, as if the man himself were being worn down by the routine.

I read fast at first, skimming for value the way I skimmed a wreck—eyes hunting for anything that gleamed. And then, eleven days out of Vessany, the handwriting changed.

*Received sealed orders via naval cutter at anchor off Cape Morrow. Orders direct diversion from scheduled route. Destination not to be entered in log. Crew informed of "mechanical delay."*

Below that, in different ink, different pressure, a second line had been added:

*Objected formally. Overruled.*

I turned the page slowly. The paper whispered against itself, and in the quiet of the cabin it sounded like breathing.

The next entries tracked the *Meridian Star* into waters no grain freighter had business entering—a naval anchorage, coordinates given only obliquely, depth soundings that matched nowhere on any chart I owned. The cargo changed too. The manifests stopped being manifests and became euphemisms: *special freight, secured below, manifest withheld per orders.* Night loading. Armed escort aboard, officers who did not sign the guest book because there was no guest book, because officially they had never come.

And then, on the last full page of entries, the handwriting came apart entirely.

*They are going to sink us. I am certain now. The seacocks have been tampered with—I found the wrench marks myself. The escort departed at dusk without transfer of cargo. Mr. Adeyemi asked point-blank why we rode so deep and was told it was ballast. We ride deep because we carry their sin, and they mean to bury it with us.*

*I have ordered the boats provisioned in secret. There are ninety-one souls aboard counting my own. The boats will hold perhaps sixty if the sea allows. I write this log in duplicate; this copy goes in the strongbox with the pay ledger and the letters, and God forgive me, I am hiding it rather than sending it, because the wireless officer is theirs and I cannot trust a word sent aloft will survive the night.*

*If anyone reads this: we were not lost. We were spent.*

The lamp flame guttered in a draft from the companionway and I realized I was standing. I didn't remember getting up. My legs had decided something before my head caught up, some old animal instinct about distance from danger, except the danger was ink on paper and couldn't be walked away from.

I sat back down. I read the final entry.

*Flooding has begun aft. Not an accident—the rate is controlled, steady, engineered. They want her to go down slow enough to look like weather and fast enough that no boat reaches shore before dawn. I have mustered the crew. I will not tell them yet. Let them have one more hour of not knowing.*

*To whoever finds this: the names follow. All ninety-one. Write them somewhere the sea cannot reach.*

Then, in a hand gone almost childlike, cramped against the bottom margin as though the writer were running out of page and time in equal measure:

*Vasser, E., Master. Okafor, D., Mate. Ilori, T., Quartermaster. Adeyemi, S., Engineer—*

* * *

I read the names the way you take medicine. One at a time. No skipping.

Ninety-one of them. Rank, then name, then—in maybe half the cases—a detail that had nothing to do with any official record. *Married in March. New father, daughter born while we were at Cape Morrow. Owes me two silver for cards, forgive him. Sang better than any man alive. Afraid of the dark and volunteered for the engine room anyway.*

Captain Vasser had known his people. That was the part that got under my skin and stayed there. Any clerk could list a crew. This man had written them into permanence as human beings, in the last hour he had, with seawater rising somewhere behind him and his own name four lines from the end of the list.

The lamp burned low. I trimmed the wick without looking away from the page, a diver's reflex, hands doing their work while the rest of me drowned elsewhere.

There was more. Folded into the back of the log, brittle as moth wings, were the things he'd promised: the pay ledger showing wages drawn and never delivered, ninety-one signatures or thumbprints; a bundle of letters, last words home that had never gotten home; and a single sheet in a different hand altogether—typed, formal, bearing a stamp I recognized from a hundred navy documents I'd stolen past in nineteen years of diving cordoned wrecks.

It was an order. Signed. Dated three days before the *Meridian Star* went down.

*Upon completion of transfer, vessel to be disposed of at sea. No survivors' accounts to reach port. Full compensation to dependents per attached schedule. Silence is the highest service.*

Attached schedule. I flipped to it. It was exactly what it sounded like: a price list. A widow in the northern ports: forty crowns. A mother with three children: sixty-five. A line item for "discretionary dispersal," and beside most of the names, in a column headed *Disposition*, a single word repeated over and over until it blurred: *Relocated. Relocated. Relocated.*

They hadn't just killed ninety-one men. They'd paid their families to disappear, scattered them along the coast like ash, bought the silence so thoroughly that nineteen years later nobody in Vessany could even tell me where the *Meridian Star*'s people had come from. The town remembered the wreck as weather. A storm loss. Bad luck. The navy had written the story and the sea had held the pen and everyone had agreed to forget.

Except one quartermaster. *Ilori, T., Quartermaster*—the name surfaced in the list and my finger stopped on it, because the log's earlier entries mentioned him twice. *Quartermaster Ilori assisted in provisioning the boats. Ilori questioned the escort officer and was confined to quarters for six hours.* Confined. On a ship being murdered around him.

And the log said boats. Plural. Provisioned for sixty.

Which meant the math said what the official record refused to say: some of them might have lived. If the boats had been launched. If anyone had come looking before the tide took them. If the navy cutter that stood off the wreck site through the night—there was a line about it, *she shows no lights and answers no signals*—had been there to rescue instead of to witness.

Survivors abandoned. I'd heard the phrase before, in old men's mutterings over harbor rum, always about other seas, other wars, other navies. It had never had a face. Now it had ninety-one.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the cabin's salt-hazed port, and I was still at the table. The lamp had burned dry hours ago. My tea, made and forgotten, sat cold at my elbow, a skin on its surface like something dead.

I had done this before, in a way. Every salvager has. You bring up a dead man's belongings—his pipe, his razor, his lucky coin—and you tell yourself the market doesn't care where things come from, and mostly you believe it, because believing it is how you sleep. I'd sold wedding rings off drowned wrists. I'd never once let myself think about the wrist.

But this wasn't a ring. This was a confession, a murder weapon, and a funeral register bound in one. And I was sitting here calculating, because that's what I did, what I was built for—calculating what it would fetch, who'd buy it, whether the navy's reward for unauthorized salvage was smaller than what a newspaper in the capital might pay, whether Captain Vosk's people—

I stopped. Put both hands flat on the table again, the same séance gesture, and made myself look at what I was actually doing.

I was pricing ninety-one funerals.

The morning bell rang at the fish house, far off, and a gull screamed somewhere above the harbor, and none of it touched the little circle of gray light where the log lay open. I thought about the debt. Ondine Marrec's voice, flat as a slate: *One more season, Cyrus, or the boat goes.* The strongbox in the cave below the wreck—silver coin, real money, enough to clear everything, enough to vanish south where nobody knew my name and nobody needed to. That had been the plan. That had always been the plan. Work invisible, owe nothing, need no one, die someday in a warm place with my accounts balanced.

The plan required me to put this book back in the water. Or sell it quietly to whatever power had paid for its burial the first time. Either way, the same act: choosing the debt over the dead.

*We were not lost. We were spent.*

I closed the log and my hand stayed on the cover, feeling the ghost of the oilcloth, the cold of the cave, the way the water had gone utterly still in there, a held breath nineteen years long. Somewhere under that reef, ninety-one people were waiting for their names to be said out loud, and the only person in the world who knew all ninety-one was sitting broke in a leaking cabin trying to figure out how much his silence cost.

It cost everything. That was the answer. It had always cost everything; I just hadn't been handed the bill before.

I wrapped the log back in the oilcloth, but I didn't put it in the strongbox with the coin. I put it in the seabag under my bunk, the bag I kept packed for running, because some part of me already understood that running was going to be on the table soon, and whatever else happened, this book was coming with me.

Then I sat down with the harbor directory—the battered, coffee-stained volume every boat kept—and ran my finger down the civil records until I found what I was half hoping not to find.

*Ilori.* Two entries. One a fisherman, decades dead. And one living, listed under government service, of all things: *Sefa Ilori — Naval Records Office, Vessany Annex.*

A family name. A naval archivist. Someone who had spent years, maybe, asking the navy for the very files I was holding unredacted in a seabag.

I looked at the entry for a long time. Going to see her meant becoming visible. It meant the first thread tied between me and the world, the first claim anyone would have on me, and claims were debts, and debts were the one thing I'd sworn off.

Under the table, the *Kestrel* knocked gently against her fender, like a knock at a door.

I memorized the address, blew out the dead lamp, and went up on deck to watch the tide turn.
