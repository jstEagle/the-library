## 5. A Woman Who Reads Wrecks

The Records Annex smelled of salt and old paper, the way everything in Vessany eventually did. Sefa Ilori had stopped noticing it years ago, the way a person stops noticing their own heartbeat, but on this particular morning—the morning Cyrus Eriksen walked through her door—she noticed it again, sharp as a slap, because for the first time in three years someone had come into her office who was not there to reprimand her.

She was cataloguing requisition forms when he appeared in the doorway. She knew him by silhouette before she knew him by face: broad-shouldered, moving like a man accustomed to water even on dry land, with the particular stillness of someone who had learned to be quiet around other people's property. A salvage diver. She'd seen his name on the harbor manifests—*C. Eriksen, vessel *Kestrel's Wake*, single-hand permits, restricted-zone endorsements pending denial.*

"Archives close at four," she said, without looking up.

"I'm not here for the archives."

"Then you're lost."

"I'm looking for Sefa Ilori." He said it carefully, the way a diver tests a hull plate before trusting it with his weight. "Formerly of the Naval Records Office. Currently of—" he glanced around the cramped annex, the shelves bowing under decades of misfiled paperwork, the single window crusted with salt bloom "—this."

Sefa set down her pen. She had imagined this moment so many times, in so many configurations, that she had to check herself against the reality of it. In her imaginings, whoever came always came with authority—a warrant, an order, a summons back to the capital to answer for the requests she'd filed. Never like this. Never a weathered stranger with rope-burned hands and eyes that kept flicking to the window behind him.

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said. "You know my name. I don't know yours."

"Cyrus Eriksen."

The name landed somewhere in her chest and sat there. Eriksen. She pulled up the manifest from memory—she remembered most things; it was the one gift her disgrace had left her intact—and found the entries: dawn dives, solo, in waters where solo diving was prohibited. Waters cordoned by patrol boats. Waters around a wreck the navy had spent eleven years pretending was nothing but ballast and bad luck.

"The *Meridian Star*," she said.

Something moved behind his face—not surprise exactly. Confirmation. The look of a man who has carried a secret alone for long enough that hearing it spoken aloud is both relief and wound.

"I didn't say—"

"You dove the exclusion zone at first light, twice last week and once the week before, according to the patrol logs I'm no longer cleared to read but read anyway." She stood, brushing paper dust from her skirt. "And now you're standing in my annex asking for me by name, which means whatever you found down there, you couldn't carry it alone anymore." She came around the desk. "So. Did you find it?"

He held her gaze for a long moment. Outside, a gull screamed over the fish market, and somewhere below a cart rattled across cobblestones, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary while two people in a room full of dead men's paperwork stood very still.

"I need to know," he said slowly, "why they keep denying your requests."

* * *

Her office—or cell, if she was honest, though she'd never been imprisoned; the annex was simply the shape her exile had taken—held three filing cabinets, a lamp with a cracked shade, and a wall map of the Vessany approaches annotated in her own hand with depths, currents, and tide tables going back nine years. Cyrus studied the map while she made tea on the ring burner she wasn't supposed to have. He studied it the way she'd seen few people study anything: completely, hungrily, tracing the contour lines with one finger as though reading braille.

"Eleven years ago," she began, because he hadn't asked and therefore needed telling, "the naval transport *Meridian Star* went down off the Serpent's Teeth reef with all hands. Ninety-one souls. The official finding was structural failure compounded by weather. My grandfather was her quartermaster. He survived because he'd been put ashore at Vessany two days prior with dispatches." She handed Cyrus a chipped cup. "He never believed the finding. Neither did I, once I was old enough to read what he wouldn't say out loud."

"What did he say out loud?"

"Nothing. That was the point of him. He'd come home from the memorial services every year and sit in his chair and polish his service medal until midnight, and if you asked him what happened to his ship he'd say, 'The sea takes what the sea takes.' Eleven words. He had ninety-one reasons to say more and he chose those eleven every time." Her voice did something she didn't authorize it to do—cracked, briefly—and she pressed on before it could become anything worse. "When he died, I joined the Records Office. I thought: the truth is in there somewhere. Files survive. Paper is patient. All I had to do was be patienter."

"And?"

"And I filed my first request eight years ago. The *Meridian Star*'s complete engineering logs, cargo manifests, and board-of-inquiry transcripts." She laughed, a dry sound with no humor in it. "I received forty pages. Thirty-one of them redacted. Whole paragraphs blacked out so densely the pages looked like they'd been tarred. I appealed. Denied. I requested the crew roster. Received it—with six names struck through and no explanation. I requested the paymaster's records for the month preceding the sinking. I received a letter informing me that such records were 'not retained.'" She let that word hang. "*Not retained.* In an archive whose entire function is retention. I've filed one hundred and fourteen requests, Mr. Eriksen. I have received redactions, denials, and on three occasions visits from officers who used the word 'career' in sentences shaped like threats. On the fourth occasion they took my clearance. That was the disgrace. Not misconduct. Curiosity."

Cyrus drank his tea without appearing to taste it. His eyes had gone to the wall map again, to a small inked cross she'd marked years ago at the edge of the Serpent's Teeth shoal.

"They scuttled her," he said.

It wasn't a question, but she answered it anyway, because some statements are questions wearing armor. "Say it plainly. I've spent eight years assembling that sentence out of shadows. I want to hear it whole."

"The ship was flooded deliberately. From inside. There's a captain's log in a cave beneath the wreck—the real log, not the one they submitted—and it records the order coming down from above. Seacocks opened. Crew ordered below. And the last entry—" His jaw worked. "The last entry says the boats were never launched. Says the admiral's office wanted no witnesses to the cargo. Ninety-one people, Ms. Ilori. They drowned their own crew to bury a smuggling run, and then they paid off the families and called it weather."

Sefa set her cup down very carefully on the desk, because her hands had begun to shake and she would not give him the sight of it. Eight years. One hundred and fourteen requests. Three threatening visits and one stolen career, all of it spent circling a truth she had known in her marrow since she was nineteen years old and watched her mother burn her grandfather's letters rather than read them.

"The names," she said. "The six struck from the roster. Do you have them?"

"I have all ninety-one."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon with the lamp lit against the gathering gloom, and it was during those hours—cross-referencing the log's muster list against the census rolls, the pension files, the parish registers—that Sefa understood she was in the presence of something rare. Not the evidence; the evidence was a thing, inert until wielded. It was the manner of the work. Cyrus handled each photocopied page the way a diver handles a recovered artifact: with total attention, with respect, with the understanding that carelessness here meant loss forever. When she caught an error in her own transcription—a transposed date—he pointed it out gently, without triumph. When he misread her shorthand, he asked instead of guessing.

Lonely people recognize each other the way ships recognize lighthouses: not by conversation but by the quality of the light.

"My grandmother refused the money," Sefa said at some point, past dusk, when the tea had gone cold and the annex had grown too dark to read without leaning close to the lamp. "The hush payment. Everyone else in the quartermaster's family signed. There was a settlement—'compassionate disbursement,' the letter called it—signed by a lieutenant commander who spelled my grandfather's name wrong. She tore it up in front of him. He never forgave her for it, which tells you everything about what the money was really buying." She smoothed a page flat. "One of the ninety-one was my great-uncle Tomas. Grandfather's younger brother. Boatswain's mate, second class. Twenty-three years old. The struck-through roster—I checked it the day it arrived. His name was the third one blacked out."

Cyrus was quiet for a moment. "Why strike names from a casualty list? The families knew who died."

"That's the question that kept me up for a decade." She pulled a folder from the bottom drawer—the drawer she locked, the drawer that had cost her everything—and laid out her own work: charts, timelines, copies of copies. "Because the strikes weren't random. Every struck name belonged to a family that received its payment within thirty days of the sinking. No inquiry correspondence. No follow-up. The unstruck names—the ones they left visible—belonged to families that fought. Petitioned. Wrote letters for years." She tapped the page. "They didn't strike the dead, Mr. Eriksen. They struck the *settled*. The record isn't a memorial. It's a ledger of who stopped asking questions."

Cyrus stared at the chart of names and dates, and Sefa watched something settle in the set of his shoulders—the look of a person accepting a weight they'd been carrying anyway and only now naming it.

"Why did you come to me?" she asked. "Of everyone in Vessany. You must know what I am, professionally speaking. A disgraced clerk in a basement."

"Because you're the only person in Vessany who's been asking the same question for eight years." He met her eyes. "And because I read your file."

"My file is sealed."

"It was. The seal's degrading. Everything degrades, given salt and time." Almost—almost—a smile. "I dive wrecks, Ms. Ilori. I know how seals fail."

She should have been angry. Instead she felt something she hadn't felt since her grandfather died: the specific warmth of being *found*. Of existing in someone else's search pattern.

"Call me Sefa. If we're going to do this—and I can see that we're going to do this, you have the look of a man rehearsing an argument he already knows he'll lose—we should be on Christian-name terms. Or whatever terms divers use."

"Eriksen is fine."

"Eriksen, then." She closed the folder and locked the drawer, and the small sound of the key turning felt, absurdly, like a bell. "Now tell me what you're afraid of. Because you've been afraid since you walked in. You keep checking the window like the harbor itself might grow eyes."

He told her. About the strongbox. About the cave the low tide had opened like a mouth. About the ledger of payoffs he'd left hidden and the log he'd copied twice and stashed in places he wouldn't yet name. And about the fleet—three hulls riding at anchor beyond the breakwater, lights burning late, flying the colors of Captain Renn Vosk, whose reputation traveled faster than his ships.

"They're not here for brass," Cyrus finished. "Somebody contracted them to clear the site before dry season closes the reef. Six tides, maybe seven. After that, the cave goes under for good—and everything in it goes with it, or goes to Vosk."

Sefa stood at the window and looked out at the harbor, at the dark shapes of the fleet against the last grey light, and felt the old grief turn over in her chest and come up changed—harder, edged, useful. Eight years of redactions had taught her one thing above all others: the truth does not drown. It waits. It waits in caves and sealed drawers and the memories of stubborn old women, and it waits for someone reckless enough to go down after it.

"Six tides," she said. "Then we have no time to do this carefully." She turned back to him. "Tomorrow I'll show you what else I have. Letters. My great-uncle wrote home every fortnight, right up to the end—and the last letter mentions a cargo he wasn't supposed to see. Originals, Eriksen. Paper in my grandfather's hand, predating every redaction they ever stamped." She blew out the lamp. "But tonight, you should know: the last request I filed before they stripped my clearance was for the *Meridian Star*'s signal traffic. It came back yesterday. One page. Almost entirely blacked out."

"One page of what?"

"An order." She smiled in the dark, and it was not a kind smile. "Dated three days *before* the sinking. And the only line they failed to black out—the only surviving fragment—is a routing stamp. It says the order originated with the office of Admiral Corvane." 

She heard Cyrus's breath stop.

"The man who just arrived in port aboard Vosk's flagship," Sefa said, "is flying Corvane's pennant."
