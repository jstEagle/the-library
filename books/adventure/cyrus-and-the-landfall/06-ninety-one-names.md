## 6. Ninety-One Names

The archive room in Sefa Ilori's house had once been a dining room. The table was still there—long enough to seat twelve, built by her grandfather from salvaged teak—but now it held four filing cabinets, two lamps with green glass shades, and eleven years of requests denied. She cleared a space the size of a dinner plate and set the captain's log of the *Meridian Star* down on it as gently as if it were sleeping.

Cyrus Eriksen sat across from her, salt-damp hair pushed back, hands wrapped around a cup of tea they hadn't touched. They had not spoken since the fish market, since the sprint through the gutting stalls with Halloway's grey coat weaving behind them, since the moment when Cyrus had shoved her into an alley between two drying racks and pressed a finger to their lips while boots passed close enough to hear the buckle creak. Some silences were agreements. This one was.

"Names first," Sefa said. "Everything else can wait."

She opened her own ledger—the one she'd kept for eleven years, the one no archivist was supposed to keep—and turned to the pages she knew by heart without needing to read them. Ninety-one men and women had gone down with the *Meridian Star* off the Vessany reef, according to every official account. Ninety-one names on a memorial plaque that had been moved three times, each time to somewhere less visible, until it ended up bolted to the seaward face of a breakwater where the spray ate the letters faster than anyone could read them.

She had copied those names out by hand, years ago, from a photograph she'd taken before the last move. She'd done it because someone should know them, even if only her.

Now she laid that page beside the log's muster roll and began to match.

* * *

It went slowly at first, the way careful work always does. The log's handwriting changed hands twice—captain's copperplate giving way to a quartermaster's slanted script—and the names came in ranks: Able Seaman, Stoker, Cook, Bosun's Mate. Sefa read them aloud in a low voice, one by one, and ticked them against her list.

"Adeyemi, T. Present."

"Brennan, J. M. Present."

"Cole, R.—"

She stopped.

Cyrus looked up. "What?"

"Nothing. It's nothing." But her pen hovered over the line, and her hand had gone still in a way she recognized from other people's griefs, standing at counters delivering news, watching faces go the color of paper. She had never been on this side of the stillness before. It was strange how quiet it was inside.

She drew the line anyway. Cole, R. Present. Her great-uncle. Rufus Cole, quartermaster's mate, twenty-six years old, who had written her grandmother letters from every port for six years and then written nothing ever again. Who had signed on for one more run—one more, he promised, and then he'd come home and take over the chandlery like a sensible man. His last letter was in a drawer upstairs, its envelope soft as cloth from handling. *Tell Ma I'll be back before the dry season. There's talk of a bonus run.*

A bonus run. She had read that sentence a hundred times and never known what it meant. Now she looked at the log open on the table, at the entry dated nine days after that letter, and understood that the bonus run had been cargo no honest manifest would carry, and that the navy had known it, and that Rufus Cole had been worth more dead and silenced than alive and talking.

"Sefa." Cyrus's voice was careful. "Whose name is that?"

"My grandmother's brother." She kept her voice level. It took effort; the effort showed, and she let it. "Rufus Cole. He was twenty-six. My grandmother refused the compensation payment. Did you know they offered payments?"

"I didn't know anything until yesterday."

"Four hundred crowns per family. Signed receipts. And a clause—" she pulled a folder from the second cabinet, thumbed to a tabbed page, "—a standard non-disparagement rider. They couldn't have made it easier to find, once I knew what to look for. The same law firm handled all ninety-one settlements. Same clerk's initials on every receipt. Same disbursement account, routed through a naval auxiliary fund that was dissolved eighteen months later."

Cyrus set down the untouched tea. "You've been pulling this thread for years."

"I've been pulling threads that kept coming back redacted." She heard the bitterness in her own voice and didn't sand it off. "Eleven years I filed requests. Crew manifests—redacted. Board of inquiry transcript—'not retained.' Wreck survey photographs—'restricted, hydrographic purposes.' I was a naval archivist, Eriksen. I knew exactly which forms to file. That's what made it so instructive. It wasn't incompetence. It was architecture. Someone built a wall around this wreck, brick by brick, and every brick had a signature on it."

"And you got fired for it."

"I got 'restructured.'" A thin smile. "After I flagged the settlement fund in a routine audit. Apparently routine audits aren't supposed to notice things." She turned back to the table. "Let's finish the list."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon. Light came sideways through the shutters and crawled across the teak, and the green lamps came on one by one as the room dimmed. Outside, the harbor sounds faded toward evening—the fish carts, the winches, the argument that lived permanently on the quay below her window and could be relied upon like a tide table.

The pattern emerged the way patterns do: gradually, then all at once.

"It's the families," Sefa said. She stood, pressing both palms flat on the table, looking down at the two lists as though the answer might be physically visible among the ink. "Look at the disbursement addresses. Not one family stayed in Vessany. Not one. Widows paid off in Port Callow, mothers relocated inland to Ferrow's Crossing, brothers shipped out on contracts to the northern yards. Four hundred crowns doesn't scatter ninety-one families across half the coast by accident. Someone wanted them gone. Spread thin. Far from the water, far from each other, far from anyone who might start comparing notes."

"Or," Cyrus said slowly, "far from the wreck."

"Yes." She looked at them. "That too. You don't pay hush money to people who live within sight of the reef. Grief forgets. Proximity doesn't."

Cyrus turned a page of the log with the care of someone handling something explosive. "There's something else. The muster roll has annotations. Look—here, and here, and here. Little marks beside certain names. A stroke, like a checkmark but angled."

Sefa bent close. The marks were faint, pencil, the kind of thing a censor would miss or consider beneath notice. She counted. "Twenty-two marked names."

"Any pattern?"

She matched them against her files, fast now, her fingers moving through folders with the muscle memory of eleven years. When she found it, she sat down again, because her legs suggested sitting was wiser.

"The twenty-two are the ones who signed the supplemental articles. There was a second contract—a bonus run, just like my uncle's letter said. Twenty-two crew signed on extra terms for extra pay. The others were ordinary complement." She looked up. "The twenty-two weren't just aboard for the smuggling, Eriksen. They were the ones who could testify to it. And every single one of them is annotated in this log, and every single one of them is listed in the inquiry summary as lost 'with the vessel,' and not one of the twenty-two families received the standard four hundred crowns."

Cyrus went very still. "How much did they get?"

"That's the thing." She pulled the folder. "They got nothing. No receipt, no settlement, no record of next-of-kin contact at all. As far as the paperwork is concerned, these twenty-two men and women had no families. No one to pay. No one to ask questions." Her voice had gone flat and precise, which she knew from experience was what her voice did instead of shaking. "Someone erased them twice. Once in the water, and once on paper."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere below, the quay argument resolved itself or gave up for the night.

"We need to see who signed the supplemental articles," Cyrus said. "Is there a copy?"

"If there is, it's behind the wall we just talked about." She closed the folder. "But the log gives us the names, and the names give us the families, and the families are scattered but not unreachable. People remember. Eleven years, twenty years—they remember. A widow in Ferrow's Crossing remembers whether her husband signed a second contract. A brother in the northern yards remembers whether the money ever came." She straightened. "We don't need the navy's copy. We have ninety-one witnesses who were told to forget."

* * *

She worked past midnight after Cyrus left, because sleep was not going to come and because the work had changed shape in her hands. For eleven years it had been an investigation, which is a lonely word, a word for filing cabinets and form letters and the particular despair of competent bureaucracy. Tonight it had become something else. She wrote letters. Nine of them, to the addresses she had for the twenty-two families—addresses eleven years stale, but her trade was finding what records claimed was lost, and she was very good at her trade.

To the Aldous family, Port Callow. To the widow Merrin, Ferrow's Crossing. To T. Adeyemi's sister, whose last known address was a northern yard dormitory that had probably been demolished, but whose name—Bessat Adeyemi—appeared in a dockworkers' benevolent society register Sefa had indexed years ago for unrelated reasons. Everything connected. Everything always connected; that was the whole secret of archives, and the whole horror of them.

At the bottom of the ninth letter she paused, pen hovering, and then wrote the sentence she had been circling all evening:

*Your husband's ship was sunk deliberately. I can prove it. I need your help to prove it.*

She read it back and did not soften it. Softening was how the wall had been built—brick by polite brick, each redaction reasonable, each denial regretful. Whatever came next would not be reasonable, and she was done pretending otherwise.

She sealed the letters and set them by the door for morning post, and then she went upstairs to the drawer where Rufus's last letter lived, and took it out, and read it again by the light of her bedside lamp.

*Tell Ma I'll be back before the dry season. There's talk of a bonus run.*

He had been twenty-six. He had signed his letter *your loving Rufo*, a joke name from childhood that only the family used, and her grandmother had kept every letter in shoeboxes tied with string, and when the navy man came with the folder and the pen and the offer of four hundred crowns, her grandmother had looked at him and said, in a voice Sefa had never heard her use before or since: *My brother is not for sale.* And the man had left the folder on the table anyway, because that was how it worked—you didn't have to sign for the silence to be bought. The silence was bought the moment everyone else signed. One refusal among ninety was just a woman shouting into a wall.

Sefa put the letter away and sat in the dark for a long time.

Then she went back downstairs, because there was one more thing, and she had been avoiding it, and avoidance was a luxury the timeline no longer allowed. She took out the log again and turned to the final entries—the ones Cyrus had described reading by lamplight in the cave, the deliberate flooding, the order given and obeyed, the boats that pulled away. She had skimmed them earlier, professionally, cataloguing. Now she read them properly, word by word, letting each one land.

The handwriting was the quartermaster's—the slanted script, the second hand. Her grandfather's old shipmate, or so the family story went; her grandfather had served with a man named Okonkwo who survived, the sole survivor, the one whose granddaughter she was. Which meant the slanted hand writing the death of the ship belonged to a man who had been in the water after writing it, and had lived, and had carried it.

*Flooding confirmed. Captain has given the order. We are told it is necessary. May God forgive whoever decided this was necessary, because I will not.*

Below that, in different ink, added later, shakier:

*I got out. I don't know why I got out. Ninety-one did not. If anyone reads this, the order came from above the captain. Find the stenciled box. The bell knows.*

*The bell knows.* She'd taken it for a drowning man's poetry. But bells were records—every ship's bell bore its commissioning date, its yard, sometimes its patrons. And a bell could be raised. A bell could be read. A bell, unlike a logbook, unlike a redacted file, was a piece of the ship itself, and pieces of ships had a way of ending up in harborside collections and private museums where censors' pencils couldn't reach.

Unless someone had already gotten to it.

She wrote *the bell* on a slip of paper and weighted it under the lamp base where Cyrus would see it, and finally, near three in the morning, she slept—badly, in the chair, with the muster roll under her hand like a talisman.

* * *

The reply came faster than any letter had a right to.

Not by post. By knock—at dawn, two days later, a knock she answered with the chain on, to find a woman in her sixties on the step, weathered as a piling, holding Sefa's own letter in one hand like a summons.

"You're the archivist," the woman said. "The one they sacked."

"I'm Sefa Ilori."

"Ilona Merrin." The woman's eyes were dry and fierce. "Widow of Josiah Merrin, stoker, second class. You wrote that my husband's ship was sunk deliberate." She stepped closer. "I want you to say it to my face."

Sefa unhooked the chain. "Come in, Mrs. Merrin. I'll do better than say it. I'll show you his name in his captain's own hand—and then I'll show you why no one ever sent you his four hundred crowns."

Ilona Merrin crossed the threshold, and behind her, out on the gray morning water, Sefa saw a navy launch making slow rounds off the point, and further out, riding at anchor where no merchant fleet had any business anchoring, the black hulls of a salvage fleet that had not been there yesterday.

Six tides, Cyrus had said. The clock had already started, and it was running whether they were ready or not.
