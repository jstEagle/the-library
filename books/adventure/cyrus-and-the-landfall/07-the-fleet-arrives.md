## 7. The Fleet Arrives

They came in on the morning tide, three hulls abreast, flying no colors at all.

Cyrus was mending net on the *Kestrel's* foredeck when the sound reached them first—not engines, exactly, but a pressure in the water, a deep thrum felt through the hull before anything showed above the horizon. Old divers knew that feeling. It was the sound of something big displacing a lot of sea, and it made the small hairs on their arms stand up the way weather did.

Then the point came around the breakwater and there they were: a salvage fleet, riding high and empty, which meant they'd come to fill themselves. The lead ship was a converted coastal freighter with a crane arm folded along her deck like a sleeping mantis. Behind her came two support vessels, flat-decked, bristling with compressors and winch gear, and lashed to the freighter's rail were dive ladders—six of them, maybe eight. Cyrus counted twice.

Eight dive stations. Nobody put eight stations on a wreck unless they meant to strip it fast and leave nothing for gulls.

"Vosk," Ondine said from the harbor office door, and spat over the rail into the water. She'd come out without being called; she always knew when the weather or the money turned. "Renn Vosk. That's his flagship, the *Iron Meridian*. He ran the Cordova deep-salvage ten years back. Pulled a mail steamer off sixty fathoms in eleven days."

"Eleven days," Cyrus repeated. The *Meridian Star* lay in nine.

"That's what I'm telling you." Ondine's face had gone the color of old rope. She wasn't looking at the fleet anymore; she was looking at Cyrus, and there was something in it she couldn't quite hide—something between warning and apology. "He doesn't dive pretty and he doesn't dive slow. He dives like a man putting out a fire. And he didn't come all this way for brass fittings."

Cyrus coiled the net line slowly, buying time, watching the ships take their moorings with a precision that told its own story. No drift, no second attempts, no shouting between boats. Every anchor went down where someone had already decided it would go down. These weren't salvagers who'd heard rumor of a wreck. These were salvagers with charts.

With coordinates.

The thought landed cold, right at the base of the skull, where fear kept its office. Only four people alive knew those coordinates with any precision: Cyrus, Sefa Ilori, her grandson Tam, and whoever had signed the paper that sent Vosk here. And one of those four hadn't known them a month ago.

"You've been quiet lately," Ondine said. "Quiet even for you."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like an empty house. This is different." She leaned on the rail beside him, close enough that he could smell the coffee and tobacco on her, and lowered her voice to almost nothing. "Whatever you found out there—and don't tell me you haven't found anything, I've watched you come back with your eyes wrong for two weeks—you need to understand what just anchored in my harbor. Vosk doesn't work for himself. Vosk works for people whose names never appear on contracts. If he's here, somebody upstairs decided that reef needs to be *clean*. Not salvaged. Cleaned."

"Cleaned of what?"

Ondine looked at him a long moment. "That's the question, isn't it. And the fact that you're asking it tells me you already know."

She pushed off the rail and went back inside, and Cyrus sat with the net in their lap and watched the fleet settle into the harbor like a knife sliding into a sheath.

* * *

By afternoon the whole port knew the fleet's name and half its business, because Renn Vosk made sure of it. That was the thing about him, the port discovered within hours: he was not a man who hid. He bought the back room of the Saltcellar Tavern outright for the evening and threw it open, and by sundown there was beer enough floating that half the waterfront had forgiven him in advance for whatever he'd come to do.

Cyrus went anyway. That was the trick of invisibility—it worked best in crowds, and crowds were where you learned things.

The Saltcellar was thick with smoke and noise when Cyrus slipped in through the kitchen passage, took a corner table with a view of both doors, and ordered nothing but time. From there they could watch Vosk work, and Vosk was worth watching.

He was a big man gone soft in the way that strong men go soft—thick through the chest still, but thickening at the collar, silver shot through a black beard trimmed short as a hedge. He wore salvage gear the way other men wore jewelry: a brass depth gauge on a chain, a diver's knife at his belt old enough that the handle had been worn smooth by generations of grips. He moved through his own party like a host at a wedding, hand on shoulders, laughing at jokes before they finished, and every third sentence was a question dressed up as small talk.

"How long you been working this coast, friend?"

"Bad season for crawfish, I hear. Or is that just what everyone tells the buyers?"

"Now that wreck off the reef—the navy one, the old corvette—she must be picked clean by now, eh? Nothing left but rust and crabs?"

And the port, drunk and flattered and paid in free beer, answered him. Answered everything. Cyrus sat in the corner and felt each answer land somewhere behind their ribs.

Because the answers were wrong. That was the strange mercy of it. A deckhand swore the wreck had been stripped years ago. A fisherman's wife said her husband had seen patrol boats chase off a diver once, years back, some fool after propeller bronze. Nobody—thank God, thank the drowned, thank whatever looks after invisible people—nobody mentioned Cyrus Eriksen. Two weeks of dawn dives, a strongbox hauled up under a patrol boat's nose, and the port's collective memory held nothing but fog.

Invisibility, it turned out, was a currency. Cyrus had spent a lifetime earning it without knowing it was worth anything.

Then Vosk's gaze crossed the room and snagged.

It shouldn't have. There was nothing to snag on—a thin figure in a salt-stiffened jacket in a shadowed corner, a glass of untouched beer, a face you'd forget while looking at it. But Vosk's eyes stopped anyway, hung there for three full seconds, and then he smiled—the broad, generous smile he'd been giving everyone all night—and changed course across the room like a shark that had caught a scent it couldn't name.

"Eriksen," he said, arriving, hand extended. "Cyrus Eriksen. Best hands on this coast, if the rumors run true."

Cyrus took the hand. It was dry and heavy and it squeezed exactly hard enough to be a message. "I don't think we've met."

"We haven't. That's the point." Vosk dropped into the opposite chair uninvited, sprawling, owning the space the way he owned the harbor now. He signaled a barmaid without looking. "Two more. You'll drink with me. It's bad luck to refuse a salvage captain his first night in port."

"I wasn't aware of that superstition."

"There's a lot about salvage you're not aware of." He said it pleasantly, the way a man says *nice weather*, and Cyrus understood they were past the opening moves whether they liked it or not. "Here's what I know about you, Eriksen. Your father worked the Meridian Star's home port before the war. You hold a master diver's ticket nobody can quite verify. You owe Ondine Marrec more than your boat is worth. And in the last month you've burned through more compressed air than any single diver has any business burning, most of it at dawn, most of it out toward a reef the navy finds very interesting."

The beers arrived. Vosk waited until the barmaid left.

"So either you've got a lover out there on the water," he said, "or you've found something on that wreck worth dying for. And I've met lovers, Eriksen. They don't make men dive alone at slack tide in cold water six times running."

Cyrus lifted the beer and drank, slow, letting the silence do the talking. Fear was a live wire in the chest, but fear was an old acquaintance, and old acquaintances could be asked to sit quietly in a corner.

"Maybe I just like the diving," Cyrus said finally.

"Sure. And maybe I like the beer." Vosk laughed, big and warm, pitched to carry—the laugh of a man performing good humor for a room. Then, quieter, with the performance stripped away: "Listen to me now, because I'm only going to say this once and I'm going to say it friendly. In eleven days the dry season closes that reef and my contract ends, and I intend to be done in six tides. What I take off that site is mine by contract. What anybody else took off it before I arrived is—" he turned his glass, considering, "—a complication. I don't like complications. Complications cost me divers."

"Sounds like a dangerous trade."

"It is. That's why I pay so well." He stood, dropping coins on the table far more than the beers cost, and rested one heavy hand briefly on Cyrus's shoulder—the grip of a man testing a joint for give. "Enjoy your evening. We'll talk again, I expect. I remember faces, Eriksen. It's the one talent God gave me that matters in this business. Faces, and how much they're worth."

He moved off into the crowd, and within a minute he was laughing again, hand on another shoulder, asking another question, and the room loved him. That was the worst of it, Cyrus thought. Not the threat. The ease. Men like Halloway wore the navy like a uniform they couldn't take off. Men like Vosk wore menace like cologne—everyone smelled it, and everyone leaned closer anyway.

* * *

Cyrus walked the long way home, along the seawall, past the darkened chandlery, checking reflections in dark shop windows the way Sefa had taught them to check documents—for what had been altered, for what had been added. Twice they caught footsteps matching theirs and twice the footsteps resolved into drunks. The third time there were no footsteps at all, and somehow that was worse: a figure standing at the end of the pier near the *Kestrel*, not moving, not approaching, just *there*, a silhouette against the harbor lights.

By the time Cyrus reached the pier the figure was gone. On the deck of the boat, weighted with a fishing sinker, lay a piece of slate with a single line chalked on it:

**SIX TIDES. THEN THE REEF CLOSES AND SO DOES EVERYTHING ELSE.**

No signature. None needed.

Cyrus stood a long time at the rail, slate in hand, looking out at the fleet riding at anchor—three black shapes against the lights of the town, and beyond them, miles out, the low dark line of the sea where a cave full of dead men's names waited under the sand.

Six tides. Vosk needed six tides to strip a wreck. Which meant Cyrus and Sefa had fewer than that to move a captain's log, a ledger of payoffs, and ninety-one reasons why powerful men wanted this reef cleaned—out through a cave mouth that only opened when the moon and the tide agreed.

They crushed the slate in their fist until the chalk dust bled white between their fingers, then went below, lit the lamp, and began, in a notebook no one had ever seen, to plan a dive that would have to be perfect on the first try.

Outside, across the black water, the *Iron Meridian* lit her working lamps one by one—eight of them, one for each dive station—and the clock started.
