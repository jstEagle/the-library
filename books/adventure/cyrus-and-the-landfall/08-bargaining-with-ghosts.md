## 8. Bargaining with Ghosts

Cyrus hadn't slept. The log lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath the false floor of the *Kestrel's* bilge, and every creak of the hull at anchor sounded like a knock at the hatch.

They came to Sefa Ilori's rooms above the chandlery on Rope Walk an hour after dark, taking the back stairs, hood up, moving like a thief in their own town. Which, Cyrus supposed, was exactly what they were now. The distinction between salvager and thief had dissolved somewhere around the ninety-first name.

Sefa opened the door before they knocked. She'd been watching the street from behind the curtain; Cyrus could see the shape of that vigilance in her, the same shape they wore themselves. Her rooms were small and ferociously ordered: shelves of ledger books, files stacked in date order, a naval archive stamp she no longer had any right to use sitting on the desk like a relic from a dead religion.

"You brought it," she said. Not a question. Her eyes went straight to the oilcloth bundle under Cyrus's arm.

"I brought it."

She cleared the desk with one sweep of her forearm—papers sliding everywhere, uncharacteristic, her hands not steady—and Cyrus unwrapped the captain's log of the *Meridian Star* under her lamp.

For a long moment Sefa didn't touch it. She stood over it the way people stand over graves. Then she pulled on white cotton gloves—of course she had gloves, of course she'd spent years handling other people's drowned paperwork—and opened the cover with the reverence of a priest.

"Water-stained here," she murmured, turning pages. "But legible. Gods. Do you know how many requests I filed for this? Eleven. Eleven formal requests over six years. Every one came back with half the pages blacked out and a cover letter telling me the material was 'not germane to my inquiry.'" Her finger stopped on a line of ink. "And it was sitting in a cave forty feet under the wreck they cordoned off."

"They didn't lose it," Cyrus said. "They put it there."

"Yes." Sefa said it flatly, without surprise, and Cyrus realized she had known—or had suspected—for years. That was what redaction did to a person. It taught them to read the black bars as sentences.

They worked until well past midnight. Sefa read aloud in a low archivist's voice, translating navy shorthand into plain speech: the sealed orders received at Vessany Roads, the cargo manifest that didn't match the customs manifest, the captain's protest entered and withdrawn, and then the last entries—the flooding ordered, the hatches dogged from outside, the lifeboats turned away by the patrol vessel *Ardent*. Ninety-one souls. The log named them all in the muster appendix, in a clerk's neat hand, as if a list could make a thing official instead of monstrous.

"We copy everything," Sefa said finally. "Not just the log. The paymaster's annotations in the margin—see here, initials *R.V.* against three disbursement dates? We need the paper trail, not just the crime. A crime needs a criminal. A scandal only needs a rumor, and rumors drown."

"How do we get it out? Vosk's divers are already sweeping the outer wreck. Halloway's got men on the quay. And the next big low tide is—" Cyrus counted automatically, the way they counted everything now, in tides. "Two days. Dawn tide."

"Then we have two days to build a way out." Sefa pulled a chart of the reef from a drawer and spread it flat, weighting the corners with inkwells. Her finger traced the cave mouth, the channel, the shallow water beyond. "The cave floods at half tide. Anything we carry out has to survive being submerged unless we bag it properly. And anything we carry out through open water gets seen."

"So we don't carry it out through open water."

Sefa looked up. There it was again—that click, two minds catching on the same gear tooth. "The fish pens," she said slowly. "Off Gull Point. My family's old moorings. Nobody uses them since the navy took the lease for their buoy yard, but the pens are still there, and the walkway is rotten enough that no patrol walks it after dark."

"Underwater entry, surface inside the pen enclosure, hand the bags up through the slats." Cyrus was already seeing it, the geometry of it, the dark water and the rotten planking. "One swimmer inside, one topside."

"One swimmer who isn't you," Sefa said. "You've been down four times in three days. I can read your hands from across the room—you're shaking and pretending you aren't."

"It's not my hands you need steady. It's whoever swims that channel blind, at night, past a navy buoy yard."

"My grandson pilots boats. He doesn't dive."

"Then we find a third pair of hands, or we do it with two and accept the risk." Cyrus held her gaze. "I'm not asking anyone else to drown for this. I've done my arithmetic on what these papers cost already."

Sefa was quiet a moment. Then: "That's the first honest thing anyone's said to me about this wreck in six years. Everyone else wants to tell me it's safe, or it's simple, or it's worth it. You want to tell me exactly what it costs." She closed the log gently. "All right. Two swimmers. But we do the copying tonight, and we make three copies, and no single person ever carries all of them. Not even you. Especially not you."

"Why especially not me?"

"Because you're the one they're watching. Because if they take you, they take everything, and this dies in an evidence locker like my eleven requests did." She began laying out paper, carbon sheets, a copying press older than both of them put together. "We don't move documents anymore, Eriksen. We move *doubt*. Doubt is harder to drown."

* * *

Halloway had been watching Rope Walk since dusk.

He told himself it was routine—a flagged salvage diver with known harbor contacts, standard surveillance, nothing personal. He told himself the file on his desk, the one he'd been ordered to close, stayed closed because the evidence was inconclusive. He told himself a great many things, and had gotten very good at telling them, which was its own kind of drowning.

So when he saw the figure in the hooded coat climb the chandlery's back stairs at nine o'clock, and saw the light in the upstairs window burn past midnight, he did not radio it in. He wrote the time in his pocket notebook, and sat in his borrowed skiff among the pilings, and waited to see what he would do about it.

At half past one the light died. At quarter to two, two figures came down the back stairs separately, five minutes apart, moving well—too well for civilians. The first turned toward the fish market. The second drifted along the harbor wall, unhurried, a person out walking sleeplessness off, except that their eyes never once touched the water.

Halloway followed the second one.

It was a mistake and he knew it within a block. Eriksen moved through the sleeping streets like water finding a channel—never hurrying, never doubling back obviously, but every turn chosen so that sightlines broke and reformed. Halloway kept to doorways and let the distance stretch, because the alternative was admitting he was following them, and something in him flinched from that. From making it real. From becoming, officially, the man who hunted the person carrying the truth he himself had tried to flag three years ago and been slapped down for.

The fish market at two in the morning was a country of ice and shadow. The day's gutting tables stood scrubbed and empty under strings of dim bulbs; the ice house breathed cold fog across the lane; the tide smell lay over everything, thick and mineral. Eriksen slipped into it and vanished.

Halloway moved up the center of the lane, boots quiet on wet stone, and heard—nothing. The market swallowed sound. Ice machines, distant winches, the slap of water against the pier piles below the loading doors. He passed the third row of tables and the cold fog took his breath, and in that moment of blurred vision a hand closed on his collar from behind a hanging side of salted cod and spun him against the table edge.

Eriksen's knife was not at his throat. It was resting flat against the table beside his ear, point down, an argument rather than a threat. Their face, inches away, was calm in the way of deep water.

"Lieutenant."

"Mr. Eriksen." Halloway kept his voice level. His heart was doing otherwise. "Evening walk?"

"You followed me from Rope Walk. You were the only one. If you'd been navy, there'd have been two of you and you'd have been worse at it." The grey eyes searched his face with terrible patience. "So. You tailed me alone, off the record, no radio call. That's not surveillance, Lieutenant. That's curiosity. And curiosity in your position is a dangerous thing to have."

"You're carrying stolen naval property."

"I'm carrying proof that your navy murdered ninety-one of its own sailors and paid their families to forget it." Cyrus said it quietly, which made it worse. "You flagged that file yourself. Three years ago. I know what a man looks like when he's following his own conscience in a borrowed skiff. It looks a lot like what I'm doing."

The words landed somewhere Halloway had been keeping locked. He felt the lock strain. "If you're caught with that log—"

"If I'm caught, I'll say I stole it alone, and everyone else stays clean. That's the arrangement." Cyrus stepped back, knife vanishing somewhere into their coat, and the market's cold fog rolled between them. "You have a choice too, Lieutenant. You've had it for three years. Tomorrow's dawn tide, I go back into that cave. You can be there with a patrol boat, or you can be somewhere else, and we'll both know which oath you picked."

Halloway said nothing. Eriksen nodded once, almost respectfully, and melted backward into the ice fog, and was gone between one bulb and the next.

He stood a long time among the empty tables. Then he took out his notebook, crossed out the times he'd recorded, and wrote instead: *Nothing to report. Subject walked the harbor. No contact.*

It was the first lie he'd written in an official capacity. It felt less like a fall than a landing.

* * *

Dawn found them in Sefa's rooms again, wrung out, surrounded by copies.

Three sets. One sewn into the lining of Sefa's winter coat, which she would wear to the records office where no one would ever search a disgraced archivist's own clothing. One in a waterproof satchel that would go down with Cyrus into the cave and out through the fish pens at Gull Point. And one—Sefa's insistence—hidden in the one place in Vessany no one would ever look: the donation box of the Seamen's Chapel, taped beneath the tray, among coins dropped by men praying for safe passage. "Let God hold the collateral," she'd said, and for the first time in Cyrus's memory of her, she'd smiled.

Cyrus flexed their hands on the desk. They still trembled faintly. Four dives in three days, and tomorrow a fifth, through a night channel, past a buoy yard.

Sefa watched them do it and said nothing for a while. Then she got up, poured two fingers of some amber spirit from a bottle she clearly kept for occasions, and set the glass in front of them.

"For the shaking," she said. "Medicinal. Don't argue with an archivist about dosages."

Cyrus drank. It burned all the way down and settled warm in the hollow places. "Why are you doing this?" they asked. "Really. Your grandfather survived. He lived a whole life after. Why dig it up?"

Sefa sat back down and looked at the log, closed now, ordinary, deadly. "Because when my grandfather died, he was still waiting for someone to ask. Sixty years, and no one ever asked him what happened, because asking was forbidden and he was a careful man. He died with the whole story folded up inside him like a letter he could never post." Her voice stayed level; her hands did not. "I am posting his letter, Eriksen. Late, but posted."

Outside, the sky was going from black to iron. Somewhere out on the water, invisible in the greying light, Vosk's fleet rode at anchor, and divers were already going down onto the outer wreck, sweeping it clean of everything but the secret.

Sefa stood, buttoned her coat with the log's twin sewn against her heart, and became abruptly, completely, the archivist again—spine straight, eyes clear.

"Dawn tide's in ninety minutes," she said. "Go and get some rest. And Eriksen—" she paused at the door, "—when you come out through the pens tomorrow night, whistle twice. My grandson will be waiting. After tomorrow, there's no version of this where we're strangers."

Cyrus watched her go, then looked down at the waterproof satchel waiting by the door, patient as a coffin, full of names.

Ninety minutes later, lying in their bunk aboard the *Kestrel*, they heard it: engines. Not one boat—several, moving with purpose through the pre-dawn harbour, and a voice amplified across the water, flat and official:

*"This is a restricted waters notice. All vessels stand clear of the Meridian Star exclusion zone. Effective immediately, the reef approach is closed."*

Cyrus sat up in the dark, heart hammering, and did the arithmetic they dreaded.

The navy wasn't waiting for the dry season.

They were closing the cave tomorrow.
