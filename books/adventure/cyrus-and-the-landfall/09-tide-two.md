## 9. Tide Two

The tide table said the water would drop low enough at 4:17 in the morning, and Sefa's grandson had the boat idling off the reef point a full hour before that, because he was seventeen and terrified of being late to the only important thing he'd ever been asked to do.

His name was Tam. He had his grandmother's long face and her habit of looking at you like he was already composing the argument against whatever you were about to say.

"You're sure about the channel," he said for the fourth time.

"I'm sure." I crouched in the stern, checking the seals on the dry bag one more time. The log of the *Meridian Star* sat inside it wrapped in oilcloth and then in waxed canvas and then in a second layer of oilcloth, because I had decided that if the sea wanted it back it would have to work for it. "The reef opens up along the north spur when the tide's below two feet. You take us in slow, engine at idle, and you keep your hand on the kill switch the whole way."

"And if a patrol boat comes?"

"Then we're a fishing boat that took a wrong turn in the dark."

Tam looked at me the way only teenagers and cats can look at a person. "We don't have any fishing gear."

"We're bad fishermen."

* * *

Sefa came down the dock steps as the sky was going from black to bruise-colored, carrying a thermos and a canvas satchel that clinked. She moved differently than she had in her archive office, where she'd been all hunched shoulders and apology. Out here, with salt wind pulling at her gray braid, she walked like someone who had finally been given permission.

She handed me the thermos without asking if I wanted it. "Kaffa. It's terrible. My grandson made it."

"It's true," Tam said.

I drank it anyway. It was terrible. It was also the first hot thing anyone had handed me before dawn in longer than I could remember, and I held the cup a moment longer than I needed to.

The plan was simple, which is how I knew it would go wrong. Simple plans depend on everything else staying simple, and nothing in Vessany harbor had stayed simple since Lt. Halloway nailed that reward notice to the customs post. One more season or the boat goes, Ondine had told me, and now there were men in this port who would sell my name for the price of a drink, and one of them captained a fleet of four vessels anchored out past the mole with their running lights burning like a string of festival lanterns.

Vosk's fleet. Six tides, the harbormaster's boy had told me, grinning like it was good news. That was three days ago. This was tide two.

"Grandmother," Tam said, "you should stay ashore."

"I should do no such thing." Sefa settled herself on the thwart with the dignity of a woman boarding a flagship. "I have spent eleven years reading other people's redactions. I am not waiting on shore while the actual paper comes up out of the water. I'll be seasick, but I'll be seasick aboard."

* * *

The reef at slack low tide is a different country. We came in over the north spur with maybe three feet under the keel, the coral heads rising around us like the backs of sleeping animals, and Tam steered exactly as well as I'd taught him in the ten minutes I'd had to teach him, which is to say he steered better than I would have. The cave mouth opened off our port bow—a black slot in the living rock, wide enough for the boat at this tide and nothing wider, breathing out cold air that smelled of stone and old salt.

Inside, the ceiling came down close enough that I could have touched it from the gunwale. Our single lantern swung shadows across walls ribbed like the inside of a shell. And there, where the water shallowed to waist depth over the flooded threshold, the cargo hold of the *Meridian Star* waited—intact, sealed by the collapse that had hidden it for nineteen years, its timbers black with age but sound as church pews.

I'd seen it once, alone, by torchlight, and I had stood in that drowned room until my air ran low because I couldn't make myself leave. Ninety-one people had died within arm's reach of where I floated. Their coats were still hanging on their hooks.

"Anchor's set," Tam whispered, though there was no reason to whisper except that the place demanded it.

"Sefa, you hold here. Anything happens, you take the boat and you go, and you don't come back for me. That's not bravery talking, that's arithmetic—one of us has to carry what we know."

"I've done the arithmetic," she said. "It says I sit in this boat and watch the entrance and count seconds, and if you're not out in forty minutes I start screaming loud enough to wake the whole fleet. Go."

* * *

Diving the cave at low tide is a different animal from diving it at high. At high water the chamber floods deep and dark; at low, half of it stands open to the air, dripping, and you can move through the dry half like a man walking through the ribs of a whale. I went in on snorkel first, fins off, wading, because the last thing I wanted was to kick a lamp into the water where its light would show through the cave mouth like a signal fire.

The strongbox lay where I'd left it, wedged behind the fallen mast step. Beside it, the ledger.

I hadn't found the ledger on my first visit. It had been tucked inside a seabag under a bunk frame, wrapped in the same kind of oilcloth I used myself—the universal wrapping of men who didn't want things found—and when I pulled it free and shook the sand off, my hands were shaking worse than the cloth.

A paymaster's ledger. *Meridian Star*, official stamp burned into the leather cover. Column after column of names, and beside each name a sum, and beside each sum a date, and at the head of each page a notation in a clerk's careful hand: *Final settlement, family of ———, per Admiralty instruction 44-C.*

They hadn't just scuttled their own ship. They'd priced it. Ninety-one line items. A widow in Corvane got forty crowns. A mother in Vessany itself got thirty-five and a railway voucher. Somebody's handwriting got shakier toward the end of the book, as though even the clerk had begun to feel what he was writing.

I put it in the dry bag with the log. Then I sat down on a bunk frame in a dead man's quarters, in the dark, and breathed, because my chest had gone tight in a way that had nothing to do with the diving.

"Cyrus," Sefa called softly from the boat, and the sound came strange down the stone throat of the cave. "Time."

Coming out, I passed the crew coats again. In the lantern light one of them had swung on its hook, and for half a heartbeat I saw the man who'd worn it—broad shoulders, patched elbows, a quartermaster's braid at the cuff. Sefa's great-uncle, maybe. Or somebody's somebody. All of them somebodies, filed under numbers.

I climbed into the boat and showed Sefa the ledger, and she read the first page, and then she stopped reading and pressed the book flat against her sternum with both hands and looked out at the black water and said nothing for a long time. Tam watched her. I watched her. Nobody spoke. Some griefs you have to let finish their sentence.

Then she said, "Instruction 44-C. I've requested that file number six times. Six times they told me it didn't exist." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it anywhere. "It exists. It's been sitting in a dead ship under a reef, more honest than the whole archive building."

"Let's get it home," I said.

That was when Tam grabbed my arm and pointed north.

* * *

Lights. Out past the wreck site, where the *Meridian Star*'s upper works broke the surface at low water—a cluster of lights moving in a pattern I knew too well. Not patrol boats. Patrol boats run straight lines and bright floods. These lights were sweeping, methodical, side to side, the way divers' tenders light a search grid.

"They're on the outer wreck," Tam breathed. "That's—that's Vosk's people, isn't it. That's what it looks like."

"That's what it looks like." I took the glasses from under the thwart and raised them. Four hulls, riding high and empty except for deck crews. Bubbles breaking the surface in a neat ladder pattern, working up the starboard side. They weren't in the cave—they couldn't know about the cave yet—but they were stripping everything else. Brass, fittings, anything that might tell a story before we could tell it ourselves.

"They'll find the superstructure breach eventually," Sefa said quietly. "The one that led you in. When the tide comes back up—"

"When the tide comes back up, the cave mouth closes and nobody finds it by accident. But they're not diving by accident. Somebody gave them charts." I lowered the glasses. My mouth had gone dry. "Somebody who knew this wreck better than the navy's own survey."

Ondine. The thought arrived without permission and sat down heavily. Ondine, who had sold information about my dives before—who had admitted it, who had cried about it, who had sworn it was finished. Or maybe not Ondine. Maybe Halloway's reward notice had simply shaken loose some old harbor rat who'd watched me dive at dawn for years and finally found a buyer. There were a dozen ways my invisibility could have been sold, and every one of them ended with lights on the water.

"Go," I said. "Idle. Same channel. Don't run until we're clear of the spur—if they hear an engine they'll come look, and we cannot be found leaving this reef with a boatload of evidence."

Tam brought us out through the coral heads with his jaw set and his eyes flicking between the channel and the distant lights, and I will say this for him: his hands never shook. Behind us the search lights kept sweeping, patient as lighthouse beams, erasing the *Meridian Star* piece by piece.

We cleared the spur. Tam opened the throttle, and the little boat rose onto plane and ran south into the graying dawn with the cave's cold breath still on my neck.

* * *

We didn't go to the harbor. We went to Sefa's boathouse on the tidal flats east of town, a sagging structure on stilts that smelled of tar and old nets, and we stowed the dry bag under the floorboards beneath a false panel that Sefa revealed by pressing a knot in the planking. Her grandfather had built the hiding place during the border troubles, she said, for pamphlets. Families kept their secrets a long time in Vessany.

"The ledger changes things," she said, while Tam boiled water for more terrible kaffa on a spirit stove. "The log proves how they died. The ledger proves why nobody ever asked. Together—" She stopped, and I watched her choose her words with the care of a woman who had spent her career watching words get taken away. "Together they're not a scandal anymore. They're a case."

"They're also a death sentence if Vosk gets them," I said. "He's not clearing that wreck for salvage value. Brass doesn't buy a four-ship fleet. Someone is paying him to make sure nothing comes off that wreck but scrap, and we just carried the something off first."

"So he'll come looking."

"He's already looking. Those lights weren't random. Either he knows about the cave, or he knows someone's been in the wreck ahead of him, and either way—" I looked at Tam, young and solemn, stirring a pot with the concentration of a bomb technician. "Either way, everyone standing in this room is now worth money to dangerous people. I need you both to understand that. There's still time to put the ledger back."

Sefa looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the tide was turning; you could hear it in the gurgle under the floorboards, the flats beginning to fill.

"My great-uncle wrote letters," she said. "From aboard. The last one said the officers were acting strange, that they'd taken on sealed crates at night, that the crew wasn't allowed aft. My grandmother kept those letters in a tin for fifty years and never once said the word *navy* above a whisper." She closed the floor panel with her palm. "You don't put that back in the water, Cyrus Eriksen. Not for safety. Not for anything."

* * *

I left at midmorning, taking the long way along the seawall so I could look at the harbor without being in it.

Vosk's fleet rode at anchor, four hulls in a line, and on the nearest one men were rigging a decompression rack with the crisp economy of professionals. On the customs house steps, two of Vosk's crew were talking to a harbormaster's clerk, and the clerk was nodding, and money changed hands so casually it might have been change for a fare.

And moored at the fuel dock, taking on diesel, was a boat I knew. Ondine Marrec's tender, the *Kindly*. Ondine herself stood on the deck coiling a line, and she looked up as I passed along the wall—not searching for me, exactly, but not surprised to see me either. Her eyes went from me to the fleet and back to me, and something crossed her face that I couldn't read at that distance and didn't want to guess at.

She raised one hand. Not a wave. More like a warning, or a salute, or the beginning of a sentence she couldn't finish across the water.

I walked on, counting in my head. Tide three was tomorrow night. Two recoveries left after that—the bell, the strongbox with the admiral's order stenciled inside it. And somewhere between here and there, the hunt had already started, and I no longer knew which of the faces I trusted would be the ones selling me.

Behind me, faint across the water, I heard the first dive boat of Vosk's fleet puttering out toward the reef, and I understood that from this moment on, every tide belonged to whoever got there first.
