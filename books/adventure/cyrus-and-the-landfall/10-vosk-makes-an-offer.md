## 10. Vosk Makes an Offer

The note came folded inside a fish.

Cyrus found it at their usual stall in the morning market, tucked into the gills of a red snapper so fresh it still smelled of open water. The paper was heavy stock, the kind used for shipping manifests and naval correspondence, and the handwriting on the outside was a single word in a careful, expensive hand: *Breakfast.*

No name. No signature. Just a time—*eight bells*—and a place: the Salt Room, the private dining cellar beneath the Gullwing Inn where harbor magnates settled things they didn't want the fishwives to hear. Cyrus turned the paper over twice, looking for a trick, and found only the faint embossing of a fleet crest: three anchors crossed over a wave. Renn Vosk's mark. The man had been in port four days and already his stationery was better than half the town's.

"You going to eat that or read it?" asked the fishmonger, an old woman named Petra with hands like knotted rope.

Cyrus bought the snapper, note and all, and carried it home through streets that seemed, that morning, to have more eyes than usual. A boy mending a net looked up too quickly. Two men in shore clothes with the sunburn line of naval caps across their foreheads stood talking at the corner a beat too long. And at the mouth of the lane, leaning against a piling as if he'd been born there, one of Vosk's people—a big Samoan-built man with a shaved head and the patient stillness of someone paid to wait.

He didn't follow. He didn't need to. The note was the following.

* * *

Cyrus spent the morning deciding whether to go, which was to say, spent the morning pretending there was a decision. There wasn't. A man like Vosk did not send invitations that could be declined; he sent them to see how you carried the refusal, and if you didn't show, he simply came to you, and the next conversation happened on ground of his choosing anyway. Better to pick the room.

So at eight bells Cyrus walked down to the Gullwing, past the innkeeper who pretended not to recognize them, down the stairs worn hollow in the middle by two hundred years of important boots, and into the Salt Room.

It was a small vault of a place, stone-walled, lit by a single lantern and a candle on the table. The table itself was old driftwood, glassed over, and set for two: bread, hard cheese, a bottle of wine with a label from a country Cyrus had never been able to afford to sail to. Vosk sat with his back to the wall, as Cyrus would have, and rose when the door opened.

"Cyrus Eriksen." He said the name the way a man tests a knot—giving it weight, seeing if it holds. "Sit. Please. The cheese is from Vessany's own high pastures. I'm told it's the only thing this coast produces worth shipping."

"I'll stand."

"Of course you will." Vosk sat back down himself, unhurried, and poured two glasses anyway. He was smaller than he looked on his quarterdeck, Cyrus noted. Compact, gray at the temples, with the deep tan of a man who had spent forty years in weather and the flat, unblinking calm of one who had spent them giving orders that got people wet and kept him dry. His hands were the only dramatic thing about him—huge, scarred across the knuckles, diver's hands from a career he liked people to know he'd had. "I'll come to the point, because I respect your time and I know you don't trust my wine."

"Good instinct."

A smile touched his mouth without arriving at his eyes. "Three days ago, my divers were sweeping the outer wreck of the Meridian Star. Routine work. Clearing debris, mapping the site, the contract work I was hired to do. And they found dive marks on the brass fittings. Fresh marks. Cut with a cold chisel, which is a poor tool for the job—unless the diver is working alone, at night, without a support boat, and can't afford to burn air on finesse." He turned one of the wine glasses slowly on the table. "That's you. That has been you for years, if my sources are right, and my sources are very good. You've been picking that wreck like a crab picks a carcass, and nobody in this port has stopped you because nobody in this port has been paid to look."

Cyrus said nothing. Every instinct said nothing. But Vosk didn't need answers; he was building something, laying plank by plank, and he let the silence sit like a keel while he worked.

"Then two days ago," he went on, "my divers found chisel marks on the *inner* structure. Deeper. Where no lone salvager has any business going, because the inner wreck is a death trap of shifted plating and silt. And last night, one of the harbor pilots mentioned—over drinks, the way these things travel—that Ondine Marrec's ledger shows a debt against your boat. A large one. Coming due." He looked up. "Am I boring you?"

"No."

"Good. Because here is what I actually came here to say." Vosk leaned forward, and the lantern light found his eyes, which were pale and patient and entirely without malice, which was somehow worse. "You've found something down there. I don't know what. A strongbox, a hold, papers—I don't care what. I know it's valuable, and I know it's dangerous, and I know that you, being a careful person, have already worked out that valuable and dangerous together means the price is going to be paid in something other than coin. I am offering you the chance to be paid in coin instead."

"And if I'm not selling?"

"Then I'm wasting both our mornings." He slid one glass of wine across the table until it rested at the edge, an offering Cyrus could take or not. "Sit down. Hear the number. If you still want to stand afterward, stand. But hear the number first. Everyone should hear a number like this once in their life, just to know what one sounds like."

Cyrus sat. Not for the number. Because standing over a man across a table was a posture of confrontation, and Cyrus did not want confrontation; Cyrus wanted information, and the fastest way to get it was to let him talk. They took the glass without drinking it.

Vosk's smile widened by a degree. "There we are."

* * *

He talked for a long time, and he was good at it.

The number came first, because he was a professional. A sum with six figures in it, delivered without flourish, the way a surgeon names an organ. Enough to clear the debt on the boat three times over. Enough to buy a new boat, a better one, and a berth in a port where nobody had ever heard the name Eriksen. Enough to disappear, which Cyrus understood—and Vosk made clear he understood—was the only thing Cyrus had ever actually wanted. "You're not a greedy person," Vosk said. "That's what I like about you. Greedy people are unpredictable. You just want to be left alone, and there's a price on that like there's a price on anything. I'm offering it. Fair market rate."

Then the teeth.

"Here's what happens if you don't take it." He said it the way he might describe weather. "The navy has posted a reward for unauthorized salvage at a cordoned site. Lieutenant Halloway is an ambitious man with a thin file, and you are an easy arrest. I don't need to do anything at all—just mention your name in the right ear, and you spend the dry season in a naval cell while your boat is impounded and Ondine Marrec's debt gets called against a hull sitting at anchor with nobody to work it. She loses her collateral. You lose everything. That costs me nothing. That's the *cheap* version."

He let that land, then picked up his own glass and finally drank.

"The expensive version is that I don't mention your name, and instead my divers simply work the inner wreck without you. They're good divers. They'll find what you found, in a tide or two. And my contract—my *legitimate, navy-witnessed* contract—is to clear that site before the dry season closes the reef. Clear it. Do you understand what that word means in my trade? Not salvage. Not preserve. *Clear.* When my fleet is done, there will be nothing down there but ballast stone and sand, and whatever papers you're sitting on will be the only copies in the world—which means they'll be worthless, because a document with no corroboration is just a story, and stories about drowned sailors are a copper a dozen in this port." He set the glass down. "So the documents decay in your floorboards, the wreck goes to sand, the families stay paid off and scattered, and you stay invisible. Which, I'm told, you like."

The word *invisible* landed harder than the number had. Cyrus kept their face still and hated how much effort it took.

"You've done your homework."

"It's my trade. I'm a salvager. People think I salvage ships." Vosk spread those enormous scarred hands. "I salvage *situations*. A ship goes down with something aboard that someone powerful would rather stayed down, and I am the man who makes the sea keep its mouth shut. I've done it eleven times. This is number twelve. And in eleven situations, Cyrus, I have never once had to hurt anyone. Do you know why? Because everyone I've ever sat across from has understood the arithmetic the way you're understanding it right now. The dead are dead. The living can be paid. There is no version of this where the dead get anything, and there is a version where the living get rich. Only a fool or a poet chooses otherwise, and you—" he pointed one blunt finger, almost affectionately, "—are not a poet. I've watched you work. You're the most careful diver I've ever heard of. Careful people survive. That's all I'm asking you to do. Survive. Profitably."

"And the crew?" Cyrus heard their own voice come out flat and low. "The ninety-one?"

Something flickered across Vosk's face—not guilt, nothing so useful as guilt, but recognition, the look of a man hearing a familiar song. "Ah," he said softly. "So it's the log."

The room went very still. Cyrus's hand, resting on the table, had gone cold, and they knew—knew with the sick certainty of a diver feeling the current change—that they had just handed him something. Vosk watched them realize it, and had the grace not to smile.

"You didn't know what you had," he said, almost gently. "When you went down there. You were chasing brass like always, and the tide gave you a hold full of ghosts, and you thought—what? Papers? A payday? And then you read it." He shook his head slowly. "I've read logs like that. Twice. They don't get easier. Ninety-one names, and every one of them a man who trusted the navy to bring him home. I'm not going to insult you by pretending I don't understand why it sits in your chest like an anchor. It should. If it didn't, you'd be the kind of man I'd have to watch more carefully." He refilled his own glass, unhurried. "But understanding it and acting on it are different trades, Cyrus. I'm in the second one. And here is my last offer, and then I'll let you eat the cheese or throw it at me, whichever eases your conscience."

He leaned forward.

"Take the money. Take all of it—I'll add ten percent for the insult of the offer, because you strike me as a man who'd want to be paid for his pride. Sail out past the buoy at the next tide and don't come back. And I will do one thing for you that I have never done in eleven jobs." His voice dropped, and for a moment the harbor magnate fell away and something older looked out of his face, something tired. "I'll ring the bell. The ship's bell, if it's down there—I'll have it raised, and I'll have it rung once for every name in that log, here in this harbor, before my fleet sails. Publicly. Let the whole port count them. No inquiry, no headlines, no navy tribunal that ends in a drawer somewhere. Just the bell, and the names, and then the sea closes over it and everyone gets to go on living." He sat back. "That's more than the dead are going to get any other way. You have my word on it, and my word is the only clean thing I own."

For one long moment—Cyrus would remember this, would hate remembering it—the offer was *beautiful*. The bell rung ninety-one times. The names spoken over the water. The debt gone, the boat saved, the whole rotten weight of the last two weeks set down at once. It would even be *true*, Cyrus suspected; Vosk's word, within his own strange ledger, was probably good. The dead would get their bell. The living would get their silence. And Cyrus Eriksen would get a boat and a horizon and the old, warm, familiar dark of not mattering to anyone.

It was the easiest way out that had ever been offered, and it was dressed in bells.

"That's quite a sermon," Cyrus said.

"I've had practice."

"And the families? The ones who took hush money and got scattered? They get the bell and nothing else. No names in any record. No answer to *why*."

"They got paid, Cyrus. That's the answer. It's an ugly answer, but it's the one the world issued, and I don't sell the world." Vosk's patience was beginning to show its edges now, very finely, like wear on a hull. "I'm offering you the best deal that exists. Not the right deal. The best one. In my experience those are rarely the same thing, and the people who confuse them end up at the bottom of something."

"Ondine's debt," Cyrus said. "You mentioned the ledger. How does a fleet captain out of the Corvane chain know the terms of a harbor boss's private paper in a port he's worked for four days?"

The pause was small. It was also, Cyrus noted, the first pause Vosk had taken all morning that he hadn't chosen.

"I buy information," he said. "It's cheaper than divers."

"You bought it from *her*. Or you bought the port's books, which amounts to the same thing." Cyrus set the untouched wine down and stood. "How much of this harbor do you own, Captain Vosk? The pilots? The chandlers? The innkeeper who suddenly can't remember my face?"

"Enough," Vosk said, and did not smile at all. "Enough that you should think very carefully about who feeds you and who moors your boat and who looks the other way when you come home wet at dawn. I told you I own half this port. I was being modest to make you comfortable. I own the half that matters." He rose too, and for the first time the size of him arrived, the sheer mass of a man who had spent decades making the sea do what he wanted. "The offer stands until the next low tide. After that, the price changes, and not in your favor. And Cyrus—" he said the name like a man setting down a marker, "—the girl. The archivist's granddaughter who reads wrecks. She's a lovely young woman and she is in *way over her head*, and unlike you she has a grandson who pilots boats through channels I could close with a phone call. Whatever you've decided you owe the dead, think hard about what you're about to owe the living."

Cyrus stopped at the door.

"The bell," they said, without turning around. "You'd really ring it. Ninety-one times."

"I would. It's a good gesture. Gestures are cheap." A beat. "That's the whole trick of them."

Cyrus climbed the stairs out of the Salt Room into the hard white light of the market, where the fish smell and the tar smell and the noise of ordinary commerce hit like surfacing too fast. The world was still turning. Petra was still hawking snapper. Somewhere a navy patrol boat sounded its horn, twice, and the sound went through Cyrus like a hook through the gill.

The offer stood until the next low tide. Which meant Vosk already knew the tide tables. Which meant he already knew about the dive.

Cyrus walked home fast, bought nothing, spoke to no one, and went straight for the floorboards where the log slept wrapped in oilcloth—and found, sitting on top of the loose plank in plain view, a second piece of heavy cream-colored stationery with the three crossed anchors embossed at the head.

*Changed my mind about the deadline,* it read, in that careful, expensive hand. *My divers start on the inner wreck at dawn. Whatever you're going to do, do it tonight.*

*—R.V.*
