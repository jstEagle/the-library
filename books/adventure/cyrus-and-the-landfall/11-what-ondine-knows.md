## 11. What Ondine Knows

Ondine Marrec had been counting other people's debts for thirty years, and she had learned long ago that the hardest ones to collect were the ones you owed yourself.

She was in her office above the harbor when she saw Cyrus Eriksen coming up the stairs—saw him in the smoky mirror she kept propped against the wall behind the ledger shelf, a habit from the days when harbor bosses who were also widows learned to watch their own backs. He moved the way he always moved, quiet as ballast shifting, and he didn't knock. That alone told her this wasn't about money.

"Door's open," she said anyway, because saying it made it true.

He came in and stood on the worn square of floorboard where debtors stood, where captains stood, where navy clerks with their little leather folders stood. He didn't stand like any of them. He stood like a man deciding something, and Ondine had watched enough men decide things to know that the deciding was the dangerous part.

"You've been diving the Meridian Star," he said.

Not a question. She let the silence sit between them like a fish she wasn't going to touch yet.

"I run this harbor," she said finally. "I know what goes down off my reef."

"You knew before Vosk did."

That landed harder than he probably intended. She turned back to the window, hands flat on the sill, and looked out at the water—the same water that had given her everything and taken the only thing she'd ever asked to keep. The morning tide was coming in, brown-green and patient, sliding up the pilings the way it slid up everything eventually, no hurry at all.

"Sit down, Cyrus."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down," she said again, "because what I'm going to tell you is longer standing up."

* * *

She told it from the beginning, because there was no other way to tell it, and because she had rehearsed it for eleven years in the dark of her bedroom without ever once saying it aloud to another living soul.

"Your father fished with Tam," she said. "You remember that. You were fourteen when Tam took the navy contract."

Cyrus's jaw tightened. "I remember."

"Everyone remembers. Nineteen boats signed on to sweep ordnance off the shipping lanes after the war. Good money. Navy money." She laughed, and it came out wrong, dry as old rope. "Navy money. Say it fast enough and you don't hear what it means. It means they pay you to be near the thing that kills you, and they pay your widow to stop asking questions."

"Tam's boat went down in a squall. That's what the report said."

"That's what the report said." She turned from the window then, because she wanted him to see her face when she said the next part. "I read the actual report, Cyrus. Took me two years and every favor I had left in this port. Do you know what the weather log said for that day? Calm. Glass-flat. The squall arrived on paper three weeks later, when somebody needed a reason."

The floorboards creaked under Cyrus's weight, the only sound in the room.

"Nineteen boats," she went on. "Four went down. Forty-one men. And the compensation came through in four months—fast, for the navy, suspiciously fast—and every widow signed a receipt that was also a release, and none of us understood that until it was too late, because we were drowning on land the way our men drowned at sea, except slower, and nobody rings a bell for that."

"And you started selling information."

The words hung there. She didn't flinch from them. She had earned the right not to flinch; she'd paid for it in full.

"I started surviving," she said. "There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and I have spent eleven years telling myself the difference is everything."

She sat down behind her desk at last, because her legs had opinions about standing, and folded her hands so they wouldn't shake where he could see them.

"The first time was small. A navy clerk came to me—a supply officer, drunk, lonely, wanted to feel important. Told me which wrecks were cordoned and which were just forgotten. I passed it along to a salvage man I trusted, for a cut, and the cut fed my daughter for a month. That's how it starts. Not with treason. With groceries."

"And after?"

"After, it got easier, which is the horror of it. The navy has secrets the way the sea has fish. There are always more. And I learned something, running this harbor, watching every hull that comes in: everybody needs something. Every captain, every clerk, every diver. And a woman who knows what everyone needs can eat forever."

"So you sold my dives."

"Yes." No decoration on it. Just the bare timber of the word. "Three years ago, when you started working the northern wrecks alone. A man from the Admiralty's hydrographic office came through, paid well, asked careful questions. Who dives alone. Who dives at dawn. Who doesn't file claims. I gave him your name and your habits, and I told myself you'd never know, and that if you ever found out, you'd understand, because you're careful, because you're good, because nothing would happen to you."

Her voice cracked on the last words, and she hated it, and she kept going anyway.

"And then Halloway posted the reward, and Vosk's fleet came in with contracts signed by someone whose name gets redacted, and I understood that whatever I'd sold three years ago had been sitting in some drawer waiting, like a mine on a mooring line, for exactly this."

* * *

Cyrus hadn't moved. That was the thing about Cyrus—other people paced, shouted, broke things. Cyrus just became more still, the way deep water is still, and you could drop a whole life into that stillness and never hear it hit bottom.

"How much?" Cyrus asked.

"For the first sale? Eighty crowns." She almost smiled. "Eighty crowns for a diver's name. The second time, when they asked about the Meridian Star specifically—whether anyone local worked the reef—two hundred. I want you to know the number. I want you to carry it around the way I've carried it. Two hundred crowns. That's what I priced you at, and the shame isn't the price. The shame is that I needed it."

"Why are you telling me now?"

"Because Vosk burned down my office last night."

Cyrus blinked—the first real expression to cross that face since the door. "What?"

"Or tried to. My night clerk smelled the oil before it caught. We saved the building. We didn't save the records." She opened the desk drawer and took out a charred corner of paper, laid it on the blotter between them like evidence at a trial. "Every ledger I keep. Every debt, every favor, every name. Gone. Except—" she tapped the blackened scrap "—except the one page I'd pulled the day Vosk anchored, because I'm an old woman who's survived by knowing when to move things. It's the page with your name on it. The sales. Dates, amounts, the buyer's office. I was keeping it to bargain with, Cyrus. That's what I am. I was going to trade your history to save my skin, and then his men came with lamp oil, and I understood that men who burn ledgers aren't buying silence. They're erasing witnesses. And I would be next, right after you."

The harbor sounds came through the window—gulls, a winch, someone shouting about a fouled line—all of it ordinary, all of it obscene in its ordinariness.

"So this isn't a confession," Cyrus said slowly. "It's a defection."

"It's both. Don't insult me by pretending they're different." She leaned forward. "I sold you once because I was drowning and you happened to float. I'm telling you now because I'm done being the sea, Cyrus. The sea takes and takes and never once says why. I'd rather be a person for one more year than the harbor for ten."

"What do you want?"

"Nothing you'd believe." She pushed the scorched page across the desk. "Take it. It names the hydrographic office, the dates, the payments. It won't hang an admiral, but it shows the chain—shows that someone ashore was feeding information to whoever hired Vosk before Vosk ever anchored. Your archivist will know what to do with it. Ilori's girl. She's been requesting files for years; let her request this one face to face."

Cyrus picked up the page but didn't look at it. Looked at her instead, and Ondine braced herself for the verdict, the way she'd braced at Tam's funeral for the chaplain's face.

"You could have let Vosk finish the job," Cyrus said. "Burned building, dead harbor boss, clean story. Nobody would ever have connected you to me."

"I thought about it." Honest to the bone. "For about an hour, while the smoke was still in my hair. Then I thought about Tam, and how the navy wrote calm water over his grave, and I realized I'd spent eleven years doing the same thing to you. Writing calm water over a living man." Her voice went low. "I'm tired of writing calm water, Cyrus. It's the one tide I can't swim in anymore."

* * *

They sat in silence a while after that, the kind of silence that used to exist between her and Tam on the boat, when there was nothing left to say and no need to say it, and the work spoke for itself.

Then Cyrus asked the question she'd been dreading, the fair question, the one she'd have asked in his place.

"The other divers. The ones you sold before me. Did anything happen to them?"

"One lost a boat to a customs seizure he swears was tipped. One disappeared off Cordwell Shoals—drowned, officially. I don't know, Cyrus. That's the truth. I never asked what my information was for. Asking would have made it real, and I had a daughter to feed, and I chose not to know, and not-knowing is just lying with better posture." She rubbed her eyes with thumb and forefinger until she saw sparks. "You want to hate me. Go ahead. But hate me accurately. Don't make me a monster, because monsters can't warn you, and I'm about to warn you."

"Warn me about what?"

"About tomorrow." She stood, joints announcing themselves, and came around the desk. Up close she was smaller than her reputation, a wiry grey woman with rope-scarred hands, but her eyes hadn't aged a day since the funeral. "Vosk bought half this port before his anchors were down. Not with money—with fear, mostly, and a little money for the ones fear wouldn't hold. He knows about the cave. Don't ask how; ask who, and then look at whoever's been closest to you and ask yourself what they needed. He knows, and he's not going to wait for six tides. Men like Vosk don't race tides. They sink the clock."

"He'll come for the documents."

"He'll come for the people. Documents float. People sink." She put her hand on the scorched page in Cyrus's grip, briefly, like closing a coffin. "Get it to the archivist tonight. Get copies made. And Cyrus—if you're waiting for a sign that this stopped being about your debt a long time ago, consider that I just handed you mine."

At the door, Cyrus paused. Didn't turn all the way. Just enough.

"Ondine. The eighty crowns. Would you take it back, if you could?"

She thought about her daughter, grown now, teaching school in the north. Thought about the winter the firewood ran out. Thought about Tam's face in the photograph on the shelf, young forever, calm water forever.

"No," she said. "But I'd sell something else instead. That's the whole lesson, if you want it. Everyone sells something. The only choice you get is what."

Cyrus nodded once and went down the stairs, quiet as ballast, and Ondine Marrec stood in her office among the smell of old ink and fresh smoke and felt, for the first time in eleven years, lighter—not forgiven, nothing so kind as that, but unburied. Above the harbor the tide kept coming in, patient as ever, covering everything.

By nightfall, she knew, the whole port would know she'd talked. By nightfall, Vosk would know too.

She poured herself a glass of the cheap brandy she saved for bad news and drank it looking at the water, and waited to find out what kind of tide was coming for her.
