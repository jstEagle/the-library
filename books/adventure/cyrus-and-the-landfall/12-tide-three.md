## 12. Tide Three

Sefa Ilori had spent thirty years in the archive learning to read paper, and it had taught her one thing above all others: documents lie by omission. The sea did not. The sea told you everything, if you knew how to listen, and this morning it was telling her that the tide would be low at 0640 and the swell was running half a meter from the southeast and the wind had a bite in it that hadn't been there yesterday.

She stood on the deck of her grandson's boat with a clipboard she didn't need—she'd memorized the manifest hours ago—and watched Cyrus Eriksen check their gear for the third time.

"You'll want to stop doing that," she said. "It reads as nerves."

"It is nerves."

"Good. Nerves are honest." She made a mark on the clipboard anyway, because the ritual of it steadied her hands. "My grandfather used to say a diver who isn't afraid is already dead, they just haven't been informed."

Cyrus looked up, and there was something almost like a smile under all that salt-worn reserve. "Your grandfather sounds like he'd have liked the water."

"He hated it. That's why he was good at it." Sefa clipped the board to the rail. "Kofi Ilori, quartermaster's mate, Meridian Star. He survived because he was afraid of everything, properly, respectfully, the way you're supposed to be afraid of the sea. Ninety men around him were brave."

The words came out flatter than she intended. She'd rehearsed them as history, but out here, with the wreck's buoy bobbing gray against a pewter horizon, they came out as what they were: family.

Cyrus said nothing. That was one of the things Sefa had come to value about them—the silence wasn't empty. It was load-bearing.

Tobi appeared from the wheelhouse with three cups of chicory coffee and the particular swagger of an eighteen-year-old who had been entrusted with a boat and intended to enjoy it. "Captain says weather window closes by nine," he announced. "So we dive now or we write poetry about it later."

"You're the captain?" Sefa asked.

"I'm piloting, which is basically captaining."

"It is not remotely—" She caught herself, heard her own voice climbing into the register she'd used on junior archivists, and stopped. He was sixteen when his father left. He'd grown up steering boats and dodging her lectures, and he was here anyway, at dawn, doing a thing that could end badly, because she'd asked. Because she'd shown him names.

"Basically captaining," she conceded, and took the coffee.

* * *

The descent was textbook. Cyrus went down first, Sefa second, Tobi holding station above on the hook line with the boat idling shallow over the reef shelf. Visibility was better than the last tide—eight meters, maybe ten where the light came through clean—and the Meridian Star rose up out of the blue-green gloom like a cathedral that had fallen over sideways.

Sefa had dived wrecks before. She'd never dived one that belonged to her.

Her grandfather's handwriting lived in her memory the way other people kept photographs, and seeing the ship his hands had steered—the bollards his ropes had wrapped, the wheelhouse skeleton where he'd stood his last watch aboard her—made her chest tighten inside the regulator. She ran her glove along a rail furred with coral and thought: he touched this. He stood right here and the water came in and the navy let it.

Focus. Cyrus was already at the cave mouth, running the guide line they'd fixed last tide, and Sefa followed into the dark.

Inside, their work waited where they'd staged it: the ledger of payoffs sealed in its waterproof tube, the photocopied log pages, the strongbox lid with its stenciled order still awaiting proper documentation. Today's job was the paymaster's cabinet—a locked strongbox in the aft section of the cave hold that Cyrus believed held the original disbursement records. Names. Amounts. Signatures. The kind of paper that couldn't be called a forgery because it had never left the sea.

Cyrus worked the cabinet's lock with tools and patience while Sefa held the lamp steady and counted her breathing. Four counts in, six out. Her grandfather's trick, learned from him secondhand through her mother, who'd learned it from a man who'd spent forty years being afraid of water and loving it anyway.

The lock gave. Cyrus lifted out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and even through the murk Sefa could see the shape of it: books. Ledgers. Real ones, swollen with fifty years of brine but whole, the ink protected by wax and greed.

They'd done it. Tide three, and the heart of the evidence was coming up.

That was when the sound started.

* * *

Sefa felt it before she understood it—a pressure in her teeth, a flutter in her chest, the water itself seeming to shiver. Then the pinging began, rolling through the reef like a hammer on a bell, sonar sweeping the shallows in long, arrogant strokes.

Navy. A patrol boat, close, running active sonar over a cordoned wreck at slack tide, which meant either incompetence or intent, and Sefa had read enough redacted files to know which way to bet.

Cyrus's head snapped toward the cave mouth. Even in the bad light Sefa saw the calculation happen—the same one she was making. Sonar at that intensity meant they'd been found, or meant someone wanted them flushed, and either way the clock had just collapsed.

Then the pinging changed pitch, and beneath it came a new sound, deeper, mechanical: engines. Big ones. Closing.

Cyrus grabbed her arm and pointed up and out, and they moved.

The swim back through the cave mouth was the longest forty seconds of Sefa's life. The sonar pulses hit harder in open water, each one a fist against her eardrums, and through the green she could see it now—a gray hull standing in off the reef, maybe four hundred meters, too close, much too close, and a smaller tender already moving between it and the buoy line.

Tobi. Where was Tobi?

The boat was there—thank God, the boat was there—but it was moving, swinging wide off the hook, and Tobi's voice crackled through her earpiece, high and fast: "Aunt Sefa, get up, get UP, they're coming around the north side, I can't hold station, they're going to run the line right over—"

The tender. It was cutting across the reef shelf at speed, a navy RIB with two figures in it, driving straight for the gap between the wreck's superstructure and the shoal where her grandson's boat sat anchored.

Sefa kicked for the surface with everything she had. The world narrowed to ascent rate and air and the terrible arithmetic of a propeller wash hitting a swimmer versus hitting a hull. Cyrus was beside her, matching her kick for kick, and above them the surface strobed white with the tender's wake.

They broke the surface twenty meters from the boat, and Sefa got one arm over the gunwale and one look at her grandson's face, and she knew.

"They wouldn't answer the radio," Tobi was shouting, hauling Cyrus aboard, hands shaking so hard he dropped the knife twice cutting the hook line. "I hailed them twice, maritime band, they just kept coming, so I slipped the anchor and I was turning—and Aunt Sefa, they turned INTO me—"

"Tobi—"

"The wash hit us. It spun us. I think—I think we're taking water, I think it cracked something, the stern's sitting wrong—"

He was right. Even as he said it Sefa felt the deck's list under her feet, the ugly new angle, water sloshing in the bilge with a sound she'd only ever heard in other people's emergencies. The RIB was already receding toward the gray patrol hull, mission accomplished, whatever the mission had been. Nobody had fired a shot. Nobody had said a word. They hadn't needed to.

First blood, and it hadn't even required malice. Just a hull driven where it was driven, and a boy who'd chosen to take the hit rather than let it land on his grandmother in the water.

Cyrus was already below, finding the breach—a split seam near the transom where the wash had slammed them against their own anchor chain—and plugging it with a salvage kit that materialized from some compartment Sefa hadn't known existed. "We can make harbor," Cyrus called up. "Barely. Pump as you go."

Tobi stood at the wheel with both hands locked on it, knuckles white, staring at the receding gray shapes. "They didn't even slow down," he said. His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated it, she could see him hating it, eighteen years old and furious at his own throat. "We hailed them. We're allowed to hail them. It's the law."

"Yes," Sefa said. She put the waterproof tube containing the ledgers into its case, lashed it, checked the lashings twice. Her hands were perfectly steady now, which frightened her more than shaking would have. "Write down the time. Write down the bearing, the hull number if you got it, every word you said on the radio. You're a witness now, Tobi. Start acting like one."

"A witness to what? They'll say it was an accident. They'll say we were in a cordoned zone, that we shouldn't have been—"

"They will." She looked at her grandson—at the fear in him hardening into something she recognized from old photographs of her grandfather, the fear that becomes purpose or poison, no third option. "And then we make them say it in front of ninety-one families."

* * *

They limped into harbor at midmorning, riding low, the pump running the whole way, and the port watched them come in. Sefa felt the eyes on them—the fishermen pretending to mend nets, the harbormaster's clerk suddenly very interested in his window. Word traveled fast in Vessany, faster than tides.

Ondine Marrec met them at the fuel dock, took one look at the stern, and said, "Who?"

"Navy tender. Hull number 114, running off a patrol boat, active sonar over the reef at slack water." Sefa handed her the page Tobi had written, his handwriting degrading as the adrenaline drained. "You wanted to be unburied, Ondine. Here's your first receipt."

Ondine read it twice. When she looked up, her face had gone the color of old rope. "Hull 114 is Halloway's command," she said quietly. "That's his tender."

The dock went very still.

"No," Sefa said.

"I'm telling you what I know, not what I like." Ondine folded the page and put it inside her coat, next to her heart, which Sefa suspected was exactly where Ondine kept everything that mattered. "Halloway flagged the file himself, years ago. If his tender was out there today, either someone above him ordered it and he obeyed—or he's playing a game I can't see the bottom of."

Cyrus climbed up onto the dock, dripping, and stood looking out at the gray water for a long moment. "Either way," they said, "we just learned what a warning looks like. Next time it won't be a tender. Next time it'll be Vosk, and he won't bother with the radio."

Tobi sat down heavily on a coil of rope, and Sefa went to him, and for a moment she forgot about evidence and tides and ninety-one names and just put her hand on the back of his neck the way she had when he was small.

"We don't have to keep going," she said quietly. "Say the word and we stop. I mean it. You've paid enough for my obsession."

He was quiet a long time. Out past the breakwater, the sea lay flat and innocent, keeping its dead.

"Grandpa Kofi's brother drowned on that ship," Tobi finally said. "Nobody ever even told me his name until last month." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and absolutely level. "What was the cost of NOT doing it, Aunt Sefa? Because that's the number I'm adding up."

Sefa had spent her life among documents, and she knew a signature when she saw one. This was his.

She straightened, and turned to Cyrus and Ondine, and heard her own voice come out with an archivist's cold precision, because precision was all she had to fight with.

"Two tides of safe water left. So we change the plan. No more recovering things—they expect that now. Tide four, we photograph everything and scatter the copies so wide that burning one office burns nothing." She paused. "And somebody needs to find out whether Lt. Halloway sent that tender, or whether someone sent it wearing his name."

Behind them, at the head of the dock, a man in civilian clothes stood watching—not hiding, not approaching, just watching, with the posture of someone carrying a weight he hadn't decided where to set down.

Cyrus saw him first. "Sefa," they said quietly. "Don't turn around fast."

She turned slowly.

Lt. Halloway took his hands out of his pockets, and started walking toward them.
