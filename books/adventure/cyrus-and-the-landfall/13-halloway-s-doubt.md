## 13. Halloway's Doubt

The file arrived at 0700, which meant someone had carried it through the rain before dawn rather than trust it to the morning runner.

Halloway knew what it was before he broke the seal. He had requested the Meridian Star file eleven times in nine years—first as a lieutenant junior grade with a clerk's conscience, later as a lieutenant with a reputation for being careful about the wrong things—and eleven times the request had come back stamped *REDACTED UNDER MARITIME SECURITY PROVISIONS* until the pages looked like they'd been fed through a shredder and reassembled by an optimist. This one was different. This one came in a plain courier sleeve with no stamp at all, only a handwritten notation on the cover: *CLOSED BY ORDER OF CINCFLEET. NO FURTHER CORRESPONDENCE WILL BE ENTERTAINED.*

No further correspondence will be entertained. Nine years of asking, answered at last with a door shut so hard the frame cracked.

He sat in his office above the customs house and read it anyway. That was the thing about Halloway that his superiors had never quite managed to beat out of him: you could tell him not to look, but you couldn't make him forget that looking was possible. The file was thin. Thinner than it should have been for a ship that went down with ninety-one souls. An incident report dated three days after the sinking, signed by a board of inquiry whose members he'd already cross-referenced against promotion lists—all four made captain within five years, two made admiral. A cargo manifest listing baled hemp and machine parts, valued at an amount that wouldn't have paid the ship's coal bill. And a single page near the back, the one he had flagged himself, eight years ago, when he was young enough to think flagging things was how you fixed them:

*Received order 44-C via signal lamp from flagship VIGILANT, 2240 hours. Order reads: proceed to scuttling position, open sea valves, abandon without boats. Acknowledged.*

Acknowledged. Someone on the Meridian Star's bridge had acknowledged it. Someone had read those words by lamplight in the dark of the sea and tapped out *understood* because the alternative was mutiny against the entire chain of command, and then they had opened the valves on their own ship with ninety-one men sleeping below, and the navy had called it weather.

Halloway closed the folder. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Outside the window the harbor lay gray under a low ceiling of cloud, patrol boats moving along their prescribed lines like beads on an abacus, counting nothing.

There was a knock. "Sir? Commander Pell is downstairs. Says he needs your signature on the reef closure order."

"Tell him I'll be down directly."

He put the file in his desk drawer, under the spare chart of the Vessany approaches that he never used, and locked the drawer, and sat for a moment longer listening to the rain start up again against the glass. Then he went down and signed the paper that would seal the reef forever, because refusing to sign it would accomplish nothing except replacing himself with a man who wouldn't hesitate, and Halloway had learned—slowly, expensively—that the only power a careful man had inside a system like this was the power of small disobediences.

He just hadn't yet decided what form this one would take.

* * *

Commander Pell was a broad, pink man who sweated authority the way other men sweated heat. He stood in the customs house lobby with his cap under his arm and his satisfaction showing through his uniform like light through a lampshade.

"You'll be pleased to know," Pell said, "that this business is nearly concluded. Captain Vosk's fleet begins final recovery operations tomorrow. By the end of dry season there will be nothing down there but ballast stone."

"And the families?" Halloway asked. He kept his voice flat, administrative. "The pension inquiries. There are still forty-one outstanding."

Pell waved this off the way a man waves smoke. "Settled. All settled years ago. Compassionate disbursements, handled through channels." He smiled. "You did good work flagging the irregularities early, Halloway. Shows initiative. It's been noted."

It had been noted. That was the poison of it. Eight years ago, a junior officer had found an anomaly in a sinking report—an order transmitted by signal lamp instead of wire, which no legitimate fleet order used, because signal lamps could not be logged—and he had written a memorandum recommending review, and the memorandum had gone up the chain, and somewhere between decks it had stopped being a recommendation and started being a liability, and the navy did not destroy liabilities. It filed them. It attached them to the man who created them and waited to see whether he would prove useful or inconvenient.

Halloway had proven useful. He told himself every morning that this was strategy, that a man inside the building could do more than a man outside it, and most mornings he almost believed it.

"The salvage captain," he said. "Vosk. What exactly is his contract?"

"Recovery of naval property. Standard terms." Pell was already turning toward the door. "Why do you ask?"

"Because standard terms don't require a private fleet to begin work before the closure order takes effect. Because standard terms don't include a security rider authorizing 'discretionary measures against interference.' I've read the annexes, Commander. I sign things for a living."

For a moment something flickered behind Pell's pleasantness—not guilt, Halloway thought, but the wariness of a man who has been assured the fire is contained and does not enjoy being reminded that fires exist. "Captain Vosk's methods are his own affair. The ministry's interest ends with the site being cleared. Which it will be." He settled his cap. "Enjoy the rest of your tour, Lieutenant. I hear the transfer list comes out next month. A man with your record might do well somewhere quieter. Southreach, perhaps. There's a records depot in Southreach where ambitious officers go to be forgotten."

"I'll keep it in mind, sir."

Pell left. Halloway stood in the lobby watching the rain come down harder over the harbor, over the masts, over the black line of the reef six miles out where a hundred and forty tons of evidence sat waiting for the tide to give it back.

Southreach. They were offering him the choice they always offered: disappear comfortably or stay and become a problem. And problems, he now understood, were not always handled with paperwork. Vosk's fleet had burned a harbormaster's office two nights ago. Everyone in port knew it; nobody official had said a word. Discretionary measures.

He climbed back to his office and unlocked the drawer and took out the file, and this time he did not read it. He weighed it in his hands, this thin gray folder, this whole drowned crew compressed into thirty-one pages with holes punched through their names.

*You did good work flagging the irregularities.*

Yes. And then he had watched the flag go into a drawer for eight years while the men who gave the order collected their flags and their pensions and their houses on the hill. Somewhere in this city there were women who had received envelopes of money and a form letter beginning *the Navy regrets*, and some of them had spent the rest of their lives wondering why their husbands' ship had gone down in flat calm water, and none of them had ever been permitted to wonder out loud.

Halloway had a wife once. She had left him four years ago with the observation that he was a man who kept receipts for his own conscience and paid with other people's lives. She'd been unfair. Mostly. But she had been right about the receipts.

He took out a blank sheet of customs-house letterhead and began, very carefully, to copy.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon with the door locked and the blind half-drawn, copying by hand because anything that passed through a typist's room passed through the navy's memory. Page after page. The signal-lamp order. The board of inquiry's suspiciously swift conclusion of *catastrophic hull failure*. The manifest discrepancy—the cargo listed at a fraction of its insured value, the difference quietly absorbed by a shipping concern whose directors overlapped, Halloway had discovered long ago, with the names of two admirals' brothers-in-law. And the paymaster's ledger fragment, the one piece of the original file that had survived unredacted by apparent clerical accident: disbursements to forty-one next-of-kin, each annotated *final settlement — no further claim recognized*, each witnessed by the same two signatures.

He copied the signatures too. Names were dangerous; signatures were proof.

By dusk he had eleven pages in his own handwriting, and he sat looking at them and felt the strange vertigo of a man who has finally stepped off a ledge he'd been standing on for years. There was no version of this where he remained what he had been. If the copies surfaced, he would be investigated, stripped, possibly worse—if Vosk's discretionary measures extended to officers, and Halloway increasingly believed they did. If the copies never surfaced, he had committed treason in ink for nothing.

But if they surfaced in the right hands—

He thought about the archivist. Ilori. Sefa Ilori, dismissed from the naval archive three years ago for "persistent misuse of records access," which was the bureaucratic way of saying she had asked too many correct questions. He had seen her name on the request logs for years before that, always requesting the same file, always receiving the same shredded answer, always requesting again. Request number twelve had crossed his desk last month and he had denied it himself, because denying it was the only way to keep her name off the watch list. He wondered if she would ever know that. He suspected not. That was the shape of a life like his: all the decent acts invisible, all the visible ones compromised.

She was in Vessany. He knew that much from the harbor gossip that reached even the customs house—the disgraced archivist seen with the salvage diver, the diver who'd been diving the forbidden wreck since before anyone thought to forbid it loudly. Eriksen. Cyrus Eriksen. The name had appeared twice in Vosk's security reports, both times underlined.

They were going for the documents. Of course they were. Any competent person with access to that cave would go for the documents, and Sefa Ilori had spent her entire adult life proving herself more competent than the people who fired her.

Which meant the navy would be watching her. Which meant anything handed to her openly would be confiscated within the day, and its source traced, and Halloway would spend his remaining career—or his remaining anything—in a cell in Southreach explaining himself to men who already knew the answers.

It had to look accidental. It had to look like an oversight, a clerical failure, one unredacted page slipping through the shredder the way the paymaster's ledger had slipped through nine years ago. The system was full of cracks. That was the secret nobody said aloud: the cover-up wasn't a wall, it was a sieve, held together by the fact that everyone downstream had agreed to stop noticing the leaks. All he had to do was notice one leak in front of witnesses who hadn't agreed to anything.

He spent the evening thinking about where. Not her rooms—the navy had those watched, or Vosk did, and possibly both, courteously sharing. Not the customs house. It needed to be somewhere she would go anyway, somewhere public enough to be innocent and private enough to be missed. He walked the wet streets for two hours, collar up, hat down, a middle-aged officer taking exercise, invisible in the way he had trained himself to be, and it was in the fish market—at dawn, when the stalls were being set up and the whole town's eyes were on the catch—that he saw her.

Sefa Ilori stood at a tarpaulin table sorting through a crate of salvaged brass fittings, haggling with a fishwife over candlesticks that had clearly come off a wreck, and beside her, keeping watch with the loose coiled readiness of a man used to being underwater, stood the diver. Eriksen. Smaller than Halloway expected. Eyes that went everywhere and rested nowhere.

Halloway did not approach them. He bought a mackerel he didn't want from a stall across the aisle, and while the fishwife wrapped it he let his document case swing open, casually, carelessly—a tired man's mistake—and let the topmost page slide free onto the wet cobbles among the fish scales and the straw.

Then he walked away without looking back, and forced himself not to break stride, and got fifty yards before the discipline failed him and he glanced sideways into a shop window.

Eriksen had picked it up.

The diver stood in the aisle turning the page over, and Halloway watched the exact moment recognition landed—the stillness, the sudden absolute attention of a body that has stopped performing casualness. Eriksen looked up, scanning the crowd, and Halloway turned his face to the window and studied a display of rope and became, for ten seconds, the most interested man in Vessany in the subject of rope.

When he looked again, Eriksen was showing the page to Ilori, and her head had come up sharply, and she was scanning the crowd too—but not the way Eriksen scanned it, hunting for a threat. She was reading faces. Looking for whoever had dropped it. Looking, Halloway realized with a feeling he had no clean name for, for *him*.

He turned and walked back toward the customs house through the thinning rain, heart going like a hammer on a hull, and behind him he heard nothing at all, which was either safety or the sound of a trap closing, and he found—to his genuine surprise—that he could live with either.

Because the page Eriksen held in his wet hands bore, in Halloway's own careful hand, the full text of Order 44-C, and beneath it, transcribed from the paymaster's ledger, the first seven names of the forty-one settlements, and beneath those, in letters small enough to fit in the margin, the two witness signatures—and underlined once, deliberately, unmistakably, the words:

*MORE WHERE THIS CAME FROM. ASK THE RIGHT ARCHIVE.*

Tomorrow the reef closure took effect. Tomorrow Vosk sailed. One tide left, maybe two, and a lieutenant who had spent nine years being careful had just bet everything he had left on two strangers in a fish market knowing what to do with a single page of truth.

He reached his office, locked the door, and pulled the rest of the file from the drawer. Eleven pages of his own handwriting. Enough to hang them all, if it reached daylight. Enough to hang him, if it didn't.

Outside, the tide was turning.
