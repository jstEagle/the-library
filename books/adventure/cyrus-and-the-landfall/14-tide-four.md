## 14. Tide Four

We worked by lamplight in the back room of Sefa's house, where the shutters were painted shut and the walls were thick enough to swallow a whisper. The log of the *Meridian Star* lay open on her table like a body on a slab. Beside it, the ledger—ninety-one names, and beside each name a sum, a date, a signature. Hush money, paid out to grieving families the way you'd pay a fishmonger. Line by line. Receipted.

"Slow hands," Sefa said, when my fingers trembled over the page turn. "Fast hands tear."

I slowed them.

The camera was hers—a naval survey unit, brass-bodied, older than both of us put together but built the way they used to build things, to outlast their owners. We'd rigged two lamps with tin reflectors and hung a bedsheet behind the table to kill the shadows. It took four hours to photograph every page of the log, every line of the ledger, the admiral's order I'd pulled from the strongbox on Tide Five's near-drowning, still salt-stiff and smelling of the deep. Sefa logged each frame in a notebook in a cipher she'd invented herself, some archivist's habit she'd never shaken.

"Frame forty-four," she murmured. "Log, page nine. 'By order of Rear Admiral Corvane, flooding commences at 0400. Crew confined below.' Read that again."

I read it again. I'd read it eleven times now and it hadn't gotten easier. *Crew confined below.* Ninety-one men locked under hatches while the sea came in through valves opened from the outside. Not lost to weather. Not lost to war. Murdered by paperwork.

"You keep reading it," Sefa said quietly, "like it'll change."

"It keeps not changing."

"That's how you know it's true." She turned the page for me. "Truth is stubborn. Lies are flexible. That's how you tell them apart."

* * *

Dawn found us splitting the copies three ways, because Sefa trusted no single hiding place and neither did I, and because we'd learned by then that anything we both knew could be taken from us both at once.

The first set went into the hull of her grandson Tam's boat, sealed in an oilskin packet glassed beneath the floorboards under the fuel tank—the one place on any vessel no inspector wants to kneel in. Tam did the glassing himself. He was sixteen and had his grandfather's hands, quick and sure, and he asked no questions, which frightened me more than questions would have. A boy who'd learned not to ask was a boy who'd already been taught what asking cost.

"Second set?" he said, wiping resin off his palms.

"The dry stack at the mission," Sefa said. "Father Anselm owes me thirty years of favors and doesn't know it yet."

"And the third?"

Sefa looked at me. This part had been mine to arrange, and I'd arranged it the only way that made sense to a salvage diver: somewhere the sea itself kept the key. The third set went into a weighted jar, and the jar went down onto the outer reef in twelve fathoms, tucked into a coral overhang I'd marked years ago and never told another living soul about—not Ondine, not anyone. A diver could find it. But a diver had to know exactly where to look, and the reef was a mile of teeth.

"There's no version of this," Sefa said as we rowed back in the gray light, "where we all walk away clean."

"I know."

"No. Say what you mean, Cyrus. There's no version where everyone walks away *at all*. Vosk has already decided that. The navy has already decided that. All we get to choose is who we are when it ends."

I shipped the oars and let the boat drift. Out past the buoy, the water lay flat and innocent, holding its dead the way it always had.

"Then we choose loud," I said.

* * *

Vosk answered before noon.

I was at the harbor wall mending net I didn't need mended—that was the point of it, being visible and harmless—when I saw the smoke. It rose from the east end of the waterfront, black and greasy, the color of tar and paper burning together, and my stomach understood before my head did. There was only one building at the east end worth burning.

I ran. Everyone ran; half the port ran toward fires the way gulls run toward gutting tables. But I ran faster than sense allowed, and I arrived ahead of the bucket lines, and what I saw took the legs out from under me.

Ondine Marrec's harbor office was an open furnace.

The front wall had gone. Through it I could see the great ledger desk—her father's, her grandfather's, the desk where every debt in the port had been written and crossed out for sixty years—collapsed into embers. The racks of charter papers curled and flared like startled birds. The smell was ink and varnish and something underneath it, sweeter, that I refused to identify.

Ondine stood in the street in front of it, in her apron, soot-striped, holding a bucket she wasn't using. She wasn't crying. Her face had gone past crying into something flat and mineral, the face of a woman doing arithmetic.

"Ondine—" I got her by the arm and turned her away from the heat. "Are you hurt? Where's—"

"Back room was empty. I was at the market." Her voice was level as a spirit gauge. "He sent a boy first. Fifteen minutes' warning. Said Captain Vosk wanted me to have time to save what mattered."

"What did you take out?"

She looked at me then, and there was something in her eyes I'd never seen there in twenty years of knowing her: fear, yes, but under it a kind of terrible clarity, like water gone suddenly still.

"My husband's letters. Nothing else. Let it burn."

The bucket lines arrived and formed up, and the port fought the fire the way this port fought everything—too late, together, grimly. I worked beside them until my arms gave out, hauling seawater, and all the while I watched the flames eat sixty years of paper debts and thought: *he didn't burn records. He burned a message.* Every debt in that office existed in duplicate in Ondine's memory anyway; she was the ledger, not the desk. Vosk knew that. He wasn't destroying information. He was telling the whole waterfront, in language even the dullest deckhand spoke fluently:

*I can reach anyone. Even her. Especially her.*

* * *

That night I sat with Ondine on the steps of the net loft across from her ruin. The fire was dead, but the building still steamed, and a navy constable had strung rope around it and was being ignored by everyone. The whole port smelled of char.

"He'll do worse next time," I said.

"Yes."

"To you, or to someone else?"

"Yes," she said again, and almost smiled. "You always did ask precise questions, Eriksen. Worst bargainer I ever financed."

"Ondine. Get out. Take the ferry north tonight. There's nothing keeping you here except—"

"Except everything." She pulled the apron off and folded it in her lap, slow, deliberate, a woman buying time to say a thing once. "You think I stayed in this port through two wars of harbor politics because I love the smell? I stayed because Marrecs don't run. My husband ran—one contract, one navy job, one promise it'd be safe—and the sea kept him. I swore on his grave the Marrec name would stand on this waterfront until I was in the ground beside him." She looked at the smoking wreck. "Vosk knows that. That's why he burned the office instead of me. He's not trying to scare me off. He's trying to make me choose between the name and the cause."

"And which is it?"

She was quiet a long moment. Gulls argued over the wreckage. Somewhere down the quay a drunk sang something about a girl from the northern islands, badly.

"When Tam was born," she said, "my husband wrote me a letter from his last contract. Never mailed it—I found it after. He said, *if it comes down to standing straight or standing safe, stand straight, and I'll haunt whoever made you bend.*" She folded the apron into quarters now, smaller and smaller. "So. Stand straight. That's my answer, Eriksen, and you can carry it to your archivist: whatever you need—boats, money, lies, my name spoken in rooms where names matter—you have it. And it's not charity. Don't you dare call it charity."

"It's not," I said. "It's payment."

"For what?"

"For twenty years of my silence costing you things you never billed me for."

She studied me, and whatever she saw in my face seemed to settle something in hers. She stood, tucked the little apron-square into her pocket like a relic, and looked down at the ashes of her family's desk.

"They'll come for you next," she said. "Not a message this time. Vosk doesn't send messages twice. Next is the real thing."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I've watched you work six tides now, Cyrus Eriksen, and you plan like a man who intends to survive. You'd better stop planning like that. Men who intend to survive hesitate. Men who've already said goodbye don't." She patted my shoulder once, hard, the way she used to when I was young and broke and she was deciding to trust me anyway. "Say goodbye tonight. To the boat, to the money, to the life. Then go finish it."

* * *

I went down to the mooring after midnight and sat in the dark in my boat's stern, and I did what she told me.

It should have been hard. That boat was the last thing my father left me besides his hands and his silence. Every plank of her I'd replaced myself, caulked myself, cursed at and loved. Selling her had been the nightmare at the bottom of every bad season, the thing the debt was always threatening, the cliff edge I'd spent twenty years not looking over.

And now I sat there and found that the fear was gone. Not the fear of drowning—no diver loses that, and the ones who do lose it die—but the fear of losing *things*. Vosk had burned a sixty-year-old institution to ash in an afternoon to make a point about a woman's loyalty. Against that, my boat was a splinter. My debt was a joke. Everything I'd been hoarding my invisibility to protect fit in a match flame.

I said goodbye to her. Out loud, stupidly, to the dark, the way you talk to a ship because ships listen better than people. Then I went below and took out the one thing I'd never shown anyone: the original admiral's order, wrapped in oilskin, and the photographs Sefa had printed of the ledger, and I read the ninety-one names one more time by the light of a stub of candle.

*Corvane,* the order said. *Flooding commences at 0400.*

Somewhere out in the anchorage, riding lights bobbed where Vosk's fleet lay at anchor, and beyond them the navy patrol boats walked their slow circuits, and beyond them the reef held its cave and its dead and its bell. Two tides left. Tomorrow the navy sealed the reef forever, and Vosk sailed at dawn after that, and every hour between now and slack water was a door closing.

A lantern swung aboard the nearest patrol boat, and I watched it pass, and I thought about Halloway—the officer who'd posted the reward on my head, who'd tailed us through the fish market, whose file this scandal had once been before someone above him buried it. I didn't trust him. Trust wasn't the word. But a man who flags a wrong and is ordered to bury it carries it like ballast, and ballast shifts.

There was one play left, and it was mad, and it required the whole port to be watching.

I pinched out the candle and sat in the black, listening to the water slap the hull, and began, at last, to plan like a man who had already drowned.

Two tides. One chance. And tomorrow, if the tide served, I would give the *Meridian Star* back her voice—in front of the entire fleet, in water too shallow to hide in, where no one, not Vosk, not the navy, not God himself, could look away.
