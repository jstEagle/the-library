## 15. The Survivor's Granddaughter

The letters lived in a biscuit tin under Sefa Ilori's bed, and she had not opened it in four years.

She knew this the way you know a tooth that aches only when you bite down wrong. She knew the tin's weight—lighter than grief, heavier than iron—and the exact sound the lid made when it came free, a thin sigh of oxidized metal that always made her think of a held breath released. Four years since her last redaction notice. Four years since she'd stopped writing to the naval archive and started writing to no one.

Tonight she took the tin out because Cyrus Eriksen was sitting at her kitchen table with salt still drying in their hair, and because the ledger from the cave lay open between them like something dissected on a table, and because there are questions you can only answer by showing someone where you keep your dead.

"Read this one," she said.

Cyrus took the letter with the care of a diver handling something that might still be alive. The paper had gone the color of weak tea, soft at the folds. The handwriting slanted hard to starboard, as if the man who wrote it had spent his life on decks that never stopped moving.

*Dear Amma,* it began. *We make port Thursday if the weather holds. Tell Tomo I have not forgotten the harmonica. I have not forgotten anything.*

"Tomasz Ilori," Sefa said. "My grandfather's older brother. Quartermaster's mate, *Meridian Star*. He wrote home every fortnight for six years. My grandmother kept every one."

"He was aboard when she went down."

"He was aboard," Sefa said, "when they put her down."

The lamp between them hissed softly. Outside, the harbor worked its night sounds against the seawall—hull knock, hawser creak, the far bell of the channel buoy counting out its patient arithmetic. Somewhere out past all of it lay the reef, and beneath the reef the cave, and inside the cave ninety-one names waiting in the dark for someone to say them aloud.

"There were eleven letters after the sinking," Sefa said. "Not from him. From the navy. All identical. *Regret to inform.* Then a twelfth envelope, thicker, with money in it. Three hundred crowns and a card that said *In recognition of loss sustained in service of the Crown.*"

"You grandmother refused it."

"She sent it back in the same envelope with a note my mother can still recite." Sefa's voice did not rise or roughen; it went flat and precise, the way water goes flat before it freezes. "*My brother did not sustain loss. He was lost. There is a difference, and you know it, and that is why you are paying me.*"

Cyrus set the letter down. "What happened to her?"

"The same thing that happens to everyone who asks. Nothing violent. Nothing you could point to. Her husband's berth contracts dried up. Their credit at the chandlery evaporated. When she petitioned for a formal inquiry—the navy has a form for everything, did you know that? There is a form for requesting the truth about how your family died—they returned it twice for improper completion. The third time they didn't return it at all." She turned the ledger page. Names, amounts, dates. A bureaucracy of drowning. "She died asking. My grandfather died not asking. And I—" she gestured at herself, at the small apartment above the chandlery, at the archive credentials revoked eleven years ago and framed anyway, hanging crooked on the wall like a wound she'd decided to keep "—I became an archivist. Which is a way of dying asking, slowly, with a filing system."

Cyrus laughed—a short, surprised sound, quickly swallowed. It was the first time Sefa had heard them laugh, and it sounded rusty, like a winch unused for a season.

"That's what you are," Cyrus said. "That's what this is. You've been diving paper wrecks for years."

"And finding them all scuttled." Sefa closed the ledger gently, as if the men named inside could feel a slammed cover. "Every file I ever requested came back with black bars through it. Do you know what a redaction looks like up close? Under magnification? It's not ink. It's a stamp. Someone sat at a desk and pressed a stamp over the names of drowned men, hundreds of times, and then went home to dinner." She looked at Cyrus across the lamplight. "So now you know why I'm here. Why are *you*?"

* * *

It was the question she'd been carrying since the fish market, since Halloway's grey coat had ghosted them through the stalls and they'd escaped by the width of a mackerel cart, laughing afterward in the alley with the giddy breathlessness of people who had just discovered they trusted each other with their lives. She'd wanted to ask it then. She'd waited, because some questions are like dives—you don't ask them until you're sure the other person can handle the pressure.

Cyrus didn't answer right away. That was one of the things Sefa had come to respect about them: they treated words the way they treated the sea, with patience and a certain professional suspicion. They turned their cup in their hands instead of drinking from it. The lamp caught the old scar along their jaw, the one Ondine Marrec had once told Sefa came from a trawl cable, though Sefa suspected half the stories told about Cyrus Eriksen were ballast.

"When I was fourteen," Cyrus said finally, "my father's boat went down off the Skerries. Not a navy ship. A leaky coastal trader, badly surveyed, overloaded because the owner cut corners and the surveyor looked away. Seven men." The cup turned. Turned. "There was an inquiry. It lasted an afternoon. *Act of God, adverse conditions.* The owner got a small fine and bought a bigger boat the next year. Nobody ever even learned the crew's names except us. Seven families, and we all knew each other, and we all knew, and none of it mattered. The sea got blamed for what men did."

Sefa sat very still. She had expected debt. She had expected the salvage, the boat, the numbers Ondine Marrec held over them like a knife at the throat. She had built a whole architecture of motive out of practical things, because practical things were easier to trust than whatever this was turning into.

"So when I found the strongbox," Cyrus went on, "I thought: this clears the debt. This buys the boat free and clear and maybe a season's fuel besides. That was the whole shape of it. Brass and paper, converted to coin, converted to survival. I've been converting things to survival my whole life. It's the only trade I know." They looked up, and in the lamplight their eyes had the flat calm Sefa had seen in them underwater—or imagined she'd seen; she'd never dived, but she had read enough wreck reports to know what a diver's eyes look like in the descriptions of those who watched them surface. "And then I read the log. Ninety-one names. And I sat in that cave with the tide coming back in, and I realized I wasn't thinking about the debt at all. I was thinking that those men have been down there thirty years being called an act of God."

"Adverse conditions," Sefa said quietly.

"Adverse conditions," Cyrus agreed. "Same stamp. Different desk."

The buoy bell counted somewhere out in the dark. Sefa found she had to look away, toward the window, toward the harbor she had grown up reading the way other people read scripture—which ships came in heavy, which left light, which captains drank, which owners skimped on hull plate. Thirty years of watching the water and never once seeing it clearly until a stranger had walked into her archive with salt in their hair and a captain's log under their coat.

"My great-uncle signed his last letter two days before the sinking," she said. "*Tell Tomo I have not forgotten the harmonica.* Tomo was my father. He was nine years old. He waited for that harmonica his whole childhood, and then his whole life, and he never once said so. When he died I found a drawer full of harmonicas he'd collected. Forty of them. He never learned to play a single one." Her voice held. She made it hold. "That's what a cover-up does, Cyrus. It doesn't just bury the dead. It hands the living a question they carry until they die, and then it hands it to their children. Your seven families carried theirs. Mine carries ours. Ninety-one families are carrying theirs right now, scattered to three coasts, paid off or frozen out, most of them probably believing, all these years, that they're the only ones who smell the rot."

"Not anymore."

"No. Not anymore." She opened the ledger again, to the page they'd marked. "I've been writing letters tonight. Before you came. Twelve so far. There's a woman in Corvane whose grandfather was the cook. A man in Harrow Point, a lighthouse keeper's son—the Meridian Star's bosun. They don't know yet why I'm writing. By tomorrow they will."

Cyrus studied her for a long moment. "You understand what we're doing. What it costs. Vosk burned Ondine's office over photographs. When this goes public—and it goes public, that's the whole point—it won't be a fine and a shrug. People go to prison for this. Or people go to the bottom of the harbor for it. We might be those people."

"I've been dead asking for eleven years," Sefa said. "I'd rather be alive asking. It's faster."

Again that rusty laugh, briefer this time, bitten off. But something in Cyrus's shoulders changed when they laughed—some plate shifting, some long-sealed compartment opening a crack. Sefa recognized it because she'd done the same thing the first time she'd spoken her great-uncle's name aloud to another human being in a decade. Grief kept private turns to stone. Spoken, it stays flesh, and flesh can move.

"Can I tell you the truth?" Cyrus said. "The actual truth, not the version I've been telling myself?"

"I would prefer nothing else."

"I told myself it was the debt. Debt's clean. Debt's a number. You pay it or you don't, and either way it's nobody's fault but yours." The cup had stopped turning. Cyrus's hands were still around it, knuckles pale. "But I've been invisible for twenty years, Sefa. Invisible by design. No registry flag, no mooring fees, no name on any list anyone checks. I told myself I liked it that way. Free. Unowned." A pause, and Sefa heard the current underneath it. "But there's a difference between being unseen and not mattering, and I crossed it somewhere without noticing. You can go years mattering to no one and call it freedom, and then one day you're sitting in a flooded cave holding a dead man's logbook, and you realize the reason your chest hurts isn't the pressure. It's that ninety-one people mattered less than a scandal, and nobody—not one soul in thirty years—stood up and said otherwise. And you realize you've been standing on the sidelines of that your whole life. Every inquiry that lasted an afternoon. Every act of God. You were fourteen and powerless then. You're not fourteen now."

Sefa let the silence sit. Outside, a late ferry groaned against the pier, and voices drifted up, ordinary voices, people going home to dinners, unaware that the floor of their harbor held a confession written in a dead captain's hand.

"So it's not about the debt," she said.

"It stopped being about the debt in the cave. Maybe it stopped being about the debt the day my father's boat went down and I just didn't have the words for it yet." Cyrus met her eyes. "It's about mattering. Theirs. Mine. Maybe yours. I don't know how to do anything small about it. I only know how to dive. So I'm going to dive."

"Then listen," Sefa said, "because I've been doing the arithmetic while you were being honest, and honesty deserves company." She pulled a fresh sheet toward her and uncapped her pen—the good pen, the one she'd bought with her first archive salary, the one thing from that life she'd refused to surrender. "Six tides. We've used three. Tide four, we photograph everything and split the copies three ways—that's tomorrow, and it has to happen, because whatever else goes wrong, the record survives. Tide five, the bell and the strongbox order. Tide six—" she paused, pen hovering "—tide six is the one I keep failing to plan for, because every version of tide six ends with someone in irons or someone in the water, and I've run it forty ways and I don't love any of them."

"Then we'll find the forty-first."

"That's what I was afraid you'd say." But she was smiling as she said it, and she saw, with a small shock of recognition, that Cyrus was too—thinly, warily, but really. Two lonely obsessives, she thought, recognizing each other across a kitchen table. Except they weren't lonely tonight. Tonight there were two of them, and twelve letters sealed and stamped, and ninety-one names with the black bars peeling off them at last.

She wrote the heading on the fresh sheet—*TIDE FOUR — TASKS*—and the pen scratched, steady as a sounding line.

It was nearly midnight when the knock came. Three slow blows, spaced like a man trying not to wake the street. Cyrus was on their feet before the second one, hand going to the knife at their belt, and Sefa raised a palm—*wait*—because she knew that knock. Everyone in the port eventually learned Halloway's knock; it was the knock of a man who hated knocking.

She opened the door on the lieutenant's rain-grey face, and on the paper in his hand, and on the words that fell out of him before either of them could speak:

"You need to hear this tonight. The reef gets sealed at dawn tomorrow—naval order, signed, final. And Vosk's fleet weighs anchor at first light." He glanced past her shoulder at the ledger, at the letters, at Cyrus standing rigid in the lamplight. "Whatever you're going to do with all that—you have one tide left."
