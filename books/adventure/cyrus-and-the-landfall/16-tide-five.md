## 16. Tide Five

The tide chart said slack water came at 3:40 in the morning, and I was in the water at 3:12, because I had learned long ago that the sea keeps its own clock and punishes anyone who trusts the printed one.

The reef at night was a different country. By day it was work—brass, copper, the honest arithmetic of salvage—but by night it was a cathedral with the roof torn off. My dive light made a small yellow room around me and everything beyond it was black water moving like something alive. The *Meridian Star* lay below me on her starboard side, her hull furred with nine years of coral, her superstructure rising out of the dark like the bones of a drowned church.

I checked my gauges twice. Air full. Knife on the left calf, spare mask tucked against my chest where I could reach it blind. The admiral's order—the one Sefa swore was stenciled inside the strongbox lid, signed, dated, the whole rotten architecture of it in one man's hand—was forty feet down and thirty feet into a cave that would be underwater again within the hour.

And Vosk's divers were somewhere out there in the dark. We'd seen their tender's running lights go dark an hour after sunset, which meant they hadn't gone home. It meant they were waiting.

"You don't have to do this part alone," Sefa had said on the dock, and she'd meant it, but her grandson Tam was still favoring his ribs from the propeller wash at Tide Three, and someone had to run the boat, and the truth was simpler than either of us wanted to say: if two of us went down and only one came back up, I could live with the arithmetic better than she could.

So I went alone. Not because I was brave. Because I was used to it.

* * *

The cave mouth opened in the reef wall like a held breath. At this tide it stood clear of the surface by six feet of air—a black throat of a place, dripping, echoing. I surfaced inside it and killed my light for a moment just to listen. Water dripping off stalactites that shouldn't have existed over a shipwreck, off rock that the charts said wasn't there. The navy had never mapped this cave. That was the whole point of it. They'd sunk ninety-one men on top of their own crime and let the reef grow over the door.

I found the bottom by feel, finning slow so I wouldn't stir the silt into milk. The cargo hold gaped ahead, and my beam swept across it: crates burst open, bolts of cloth gone to rot and colorless lace, tin plates furred green, and everywhere the small domestic wreckage of ninety-one lives—boots, a chess piece, a child's shoe that no one had ever explained to me and that I had stopped trying to explain to myself.

The strongbox sat where I'd left it at Tide Four, wedged under a fallen deck plate, half-buried in silt. I'd photographed the ledger already; copies were stashed in three places, and Vosk had answered that with fire, burning Ondine's office to ash as a message written in somebody else's handwriting. So tonight we went for the thing that couldn't be copied, couldn't be burned, couldn't be argued with: the bell, and the order.

The bell first. It hung in the wreck's forward housing, and getting it free meant cutting the mounting bolts, and cutting bolts meant noise and sparks and time, and time was the one thing the tide wouldn't sell me on credit.

I got the hacksaw blade around the first bolt and started working it. The sound carried strangely in the cave—a thin silver scraping, almost musical—and I remember thinking, distantly, that it sounded like tuning an instrument. Like the ship was being tuned for something.

That was when the light hit me from behind.

Not mine. Harder than mine, whiter, a diver's torch with the kind of output you didn't get from harbor salvage gear. It pinned my shadow against the cargo hold wall, huge and wavering, and then a second beam crossed the first, and I understood that I had walked into an ambush with my pants literally down around my ankles, bent over a bolt, breathing through a hose.

I did the only smart thing available, which was nothing. I went still. I kept the saw moving, slow, like I hadn't noticed. Maybe they'd think I was deaf. Maybe they'd think I was stupid enough to keep working while they closed the distance, and maybe stupidity was the best disguise I owned.

They came on. Two of them, fins kicking, bubbles streaming up past me toward the cave's air pocket. Big rigs—commercial gear, hard-hat umbilicals, the kind of setup Vosk's fleet ran. One of them carried a pry bar, and I want to be honest about what went through my mind when I saw it: not fear, exactly. Arithmetic. A pry bar underwater is a weapon that doesn't run out of air.

The lead diver grabbed my shoulder and spun me around.

I knew the face behind the mask. Renn Vosk himself. Of course it was. A man like Vosk didn't send other men to do the thing he needed done most badly; he saved that for himself, the way misers save coins.

His regulator came out and he shouted through the water, that garbled half-language divers use when they're idiots, and I caught enough: *"Box. Now. Or we take it off your body."*

I pointed at my ears and shook my head. Played deaf. He grabbed my mask strap and ripped it sideways—not off, just crooked, a warning shot, flooding half my vision with cold salt sting. Then he put the pry bar against the strongbox and gestured: open it or lose the hands that opened it.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about drowning: it takes a surprisingly long time to become afraid of it. You're wet all the way through your whole life. Water in your lungs is just the last invoice.

But I wasn't ready to drown tonight, and more importantly, I wasn't ready to let him have that box. Inside its lid, stenciled in paint that nine years of seawater hadn't touched, was the signature of Vice-Admiral Corren Hale, ordering the *Meridian Star* flooded at sea with souls aboard. Sefa had matched the handwriting to three files. Halloway had confirmed the chain of command. That stencil was the difference between a story and a verdict.

Vosk wrenched the box free of the silt and hauled it toward the cave mouth, and I let him—let him carry it, because a man carrying eighty pounds of iron in zero gravity is a man who can't fight. His partner covered me with the pry bar while Vosk swam the box out through the throat of the cave, and I drifted backward, hands raised, the picture of surrender, until my shoulder blades found the hold's broken bulkhead and my fingers found the knife on my calf.

* * *

What happened next took eleven seconds and I have replayed all of them, waking and sleeping, ever since.

I cut the partner's umbilical.

Not the air hose—I'm a salvager, not a murderer, and there's a line even in the dark—but the hardline comm cable running alongside it, the one connecting his helmet to the surface tender. And then I pulled the emergency release on his weight belt, because a man who suddenly weighs forty pounds positive doesn't fight, he flies, and he flew—straight up, arms windmilling, bellowing into a dead line, toward the ceiling of the cave and the air pocket above it with all the grace of a launched torpedo.

Vosk heard the commotion, turned with the box still in his grip, and I hit him the way the sea hits a hull: low, from an angle he wasn't watching, with everything I had. We went down together into the silt and the world became brown chaos and elbows. He was stronger. I'll say that plainly—he was ten years younger and built like the winch on his own boat, and in a fair fight on land he'd have taken me apart in a minute.

But it wasn't land, and it wasn't fair, and I had spent twenty years learning how the water fights.

I got my fins between us and kicked off his chest, tore loose, and grabbed for the strongbox. His hand caught my ankle. I kicked again and felt something in his grip give—his fingers or my tendon, I still don't know which—and then I was swimming, blind, my mask half-flooded, the box hooked under one arm like a stolen lamb, following the falling slope of the cave floor toward the seaward breach I'd mapped on the last dive.

He came after me. Of course he came after me. Behind me his torch beam swung wild through the murk, slicing the brown water into blades, and once it caught me full across the back and I felt him close the distance with three kicks that should have been impossible for a man hauling nothing, because he'd dropped the box to chase me instead, and I understood in that moment exactly what kind of man I was dealing with. Not a man who wanted the evidence. A man who wanted *me*, because I was the only living soul who could testify to what the box contained.

My mask strap gave.

It had been crooked since he'd wrenched it, and the second kick of my escape finished the job—the strap snapped, the skirt peeled away, and the open ocean poured into my eyes and nose and I was swimming blind in brown water with a steel box under my arm and a killer on my back, and my spare mask was tucked against my chest where I could reach it blind, except reaching it meant letting go of the box with one hand, and letting go meant losing it, because Vosk was close enough now that I could hear his regulator rasping behind me like something breathing.

I chose the box. God help me, in the dark, with my lungs starting to burn, I chose a piece of painted iron over my own eyes.

The breach was ahead—I remembered it as a narrowing, a squeeze where the reef pinched down to barely a body's width, too tight for Vosk's big commercial rig, too tight for anything but a skinny salvager who'd been worming through wrecks since he was fifteen. I hit it at speed. The reef scraped my tank, my shoulders, the skin off both forearms, and the box jammed, and for one endless second I was stuck in a stone throat with a madman's torchlight blooming red through the rock behind me and no air in my lungs worth the name.

I exhaled everything, made myself smaller, and wriggled through like a worm through a hook. The box came with me. The reef took a strip of my wetsuit as payment and I paid it gladly.

Behind me, the torchlight stopped. Vosk's voice came through the stone, muffled, furious, wordless—just rage shaped into sound. Then, worse, silence. He was going back for his partner, or back for the tender, or back to think of some other way to ruin my life, and I didn't stay to learn which.

I cleared the breach into open water and the sea went from brown to black to blue-black, and I was maybe sixty feet down with an empty chest and a dead mask and a box full of the truth of ninety-one dead men. I reached the spare mask, wrestled it on, cleared it with the last technique my instructor ever taught me, and saw the world arrive all at once: the pale sand, the rising shimmer of the surface, my own blood unspooling from my forearms in lazy ribbons.

I kicked for the light.

* * *

Tam had the boat positioned exactly where I'd told him, which saved my life, because when I broke the surface I had maybe four breaths left in me and no strength to swim to anything. He hauled me over the gunwale by the harness, and Sefa wrapped me in a blanket and shouted at me, and I lay on the deck coughing seawater onto planks that had probably been coughed on by braver men than me, holding the strongbox against my ribs like it was the only solid thing left in the world.

"Your mask," Sefa said. She was looking at my face, at the raw stripe the broken strap had carved across my cheekbone, at the blood in the water still rinsing off my arms.

"Gave it to the reef," I managed. "Fair trade."

She laughed, and it came out wrong, halfway to crying, and then she put her forehead against mine the way you do with family, and neither of us said anything for a while. Tam knelt beside the box with a crowbar and looked up at me for permission, and I nodded, and he broke the hasp with one clean blow.

Inside, under the rotted canvas and the brass fittings I'd catalogued weeks ago, the lid waited. Sefa tilted her lamp across it, and there it was, stenciled in paint that might as well have been cut in stone:

**BY ORDER OF VICE-ADMIRAL C. HALE — FLOOD AND ABANDON — NO SURVIVORS TO BE REPORTED — 14 MARCH**

Fourteen words. Ninety-one names underneath them, invisible, the way the navy liked its work.

Sefa read it aloud, twice, her voice steady the first time and not the second. Tam said nothing at all. I lay on the deck with the sky wheeling overhead and thought about the bell still hanging in the forward housing, uncut, unfinished—one bolt left, maybe two, and dawn coming, and Vosk's fleet between us and it.

"We got it," Tam said finally. "We got the order."

"No," Sefa said softly. She was staring east, where the horizon was starting to turn the gray of old dishwater, and where, far out beyond the point, the running lights of Vosk's fleet were coming back on one by one, like eyes opening. "We got half of it. Tomorrow they seal the reef, and that bell comes up tomorrow, or it stays down there with them forever."

I looked at my hands. They were shaking now, now that it was safe to shake, and my cheekbone throbbed where the strap had torn free, and somewhere out in the dark a man who had tried to drown me was briefing his divers for morning.

One tide left. One bell. And I was going back down.
