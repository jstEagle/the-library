## 17. No Way Out That Doesn't Cost

The knock came at the third hour of the morning, three soft raps against Cyrus's cabin door, spaced like a man who didn't want to be heard but couldn't afford to be mistaken.

Cyrus opened it with a knife already palmed. Halloway stood in the gray light, out of uniform, wearing a fisherman's oilskin two sizes too big for him and a hat pulled low. He looked like a man playing a part he'd rehearsed badly.

"You shouldn't be here," Cyrus said.

"No," Halloway agreed. "I shouldn't." He glanced up and down the dock, then stepped inside without being asked, which told Cyrus more than anything he'd said so far. A man still loyal to his uniform would have waited on the threshold like a gentleman. This one had crossed it.

He took off the hat. Underneath, his hair was plastered flat with sweat, though the morning was cold. "I flagged the Meridian Star file six years ago," he said. "Did Sefa tell you that?"

"She said you were thorough."

"I was thorough enough to get reassigned to harbor patrol." He laughed, short and joyless. "Thoroughness is a disease in that service, Mr. Eriksen. It spreads until someone cuts it out."

Cyrus poured him tea from the dented kettle because there was nothing else to offer, and because watching a naval officer wrap both hands around a chipped cup like a dockhand was worth the price of the leaves. Halloway drank half of it before he spoke again.

"Tomorrow at first light, the *Vigilant* lays a permanent boom across the reef mouth. Chain, buoys, gun mounts. The reef gets sealed under Article Fourteen—hazard to navigation, environmental protection, whatever they print on the order. Nobody dives it again. Not this season, not ever." He set the cup down. "And Vosk's fleet sails at dawn. Before the boom goes in. He's been granted clearance to 'recover hazardous material' from the site—one final pass."

"Recover," Cyrus repeated. "Or destroy."

Halloway met his eyes. He didn't answer, which was an answer.

"One tide left," Cyrus said.

"One tide. Tomorrow's low water comes at 0440, and it's not much of one—the spring series is dying. You'll have maybe ninety minutes of workable slack, and the cave mouth will barely break the surface." Halloway rubbed his face with both hands. "I'm telling you this because I've run out of ways to tell myself I'm doing my job. I came here as a warning, not an offer. If you're on that reef when the fleet arrives, Vosk's divers will find you, and I won't be able to stop what happens next. If you're gone by dawn—if you take what you have and sail east past the shipping lanes—I can lose your paperwork for a week. Maybe long enough."

"You'd do that? Forge a blind eye?"

"I'd do something worse than forge. I'd believe it." He stood, put the hat back on, and paused at the door. "There's no version of tomorrow where everyone walks away clean, Eriksen. Pick the version where you're alive to hate yourself."

Then he was gone, and the door swung shut behind him, and Cyrus sat alone with the tea going cold and the arithmetic turning over and over like a fouled anchor.

One tide. Ninety minutes. And a choice that had been narrowing all along until now it was a keyhole: through it or nothing.

* * *

Sefa Ilori read the tide tables the way other people read scripture—lips moving, finger tracing the columns, finding in them both promise and judgment. She spread them on her kitchen table between the ledger photographs and her grandfather's brother's last letter, and she stared at the numbers for a long time before she looked up.

"Ninety minutes," she said. "That's what we have."

"That's what we have," Cyrus confirmed. They had gathered in her house above the chandlery because it was the only room in Vessany that felt safe anymore—Vosk's men had burned Ondine's office to its foundations two nights ago, and everyone in port understood the message even if no one would say it aloud. Her grandson Tam sat by the window with a shotgun broken open across his knees, not because anyone expected him to use it, but because a boy of nineteen needs to hold something when the grown-ups start talking about the end of things.

"Then we go public," Sefa said. "Tonight. We walk into the *Harborside Register*, we put the ledger on old Maren Kettle's desk, and we let every press boat in the archipelago race here by morning. Sunlight, Mr. Eriksen. That's the weapon nobody's used yet."

"And what happens at sunrise?" Cyrus asked quietly. "Before the presses roll. Before anyone races anywhere."

She frowned. "Explain."

"The navy seals the reef at first light regardless. Vosk sails at dawn regardless. And you—" Cyrus tapped the ledger photos, the neat columns of payments, the names crossed out like debts settled. "You're standing in the *Register* holding stolen naval documents. Where did you get them, Sefa? From a forbidden wreck. Salvaged illegally, copied illegally, held illegally. Halloway can't save us—he'll be lucky to save himself. The moment those pages hit a newspaper desk, the story stops being *ninety-one sailors murdered by their own admiralty* and becomes *three thieves caught red-handed trying to blackmail the service*. They won't hang us for the truth. They'll hang us for the theft, and bury the truth in the paperwork of our trial."

Tam shifted by the window. "He's right, Gran. I've heard how they talk at the customs house. They don't even call it a wreck anymore. They call it *the incident*."

"Incidents get investigated," Sefa said. "Crimes get punished. And they will make very sure the world sees a crime." Cyrus spread their hands over the table, over the letters and the photographs and the ninety-one names Sefa had written out in her own hand, each one verified against a missing-persons record, each one a family scattered with hush money and silence. "Look at what we actually hold. A logbook. A ledger. An admiral's signed order stenciled inside a strongbox lid, sitting at the bottom of a flooded cave. Paper proves nothing that paper can't be made to unprove. But the bell is still down there. The ship herself is still down there. Ninety-one men are still down there, and tomorrow the navy drops a chain across the door and calls it closed."

"So what are you proposing?" Sefa's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman who had spent years being disappointed by answers and had learned to brace for them. "Because if you say *run*, I will remind you that my grandfather spent forty years running from this, and it ate him alive from the inside."

"I'm not proposing run." Cyrus turned the strongbox lid photograph toward her—the stenciled order, ADMIRAL R. CALLOWAY, BY DIRECTION OF THE BOARD, FLOOD AND ABANDON, and beneath it the date, and beneath that the signature like a scar. "I'm proposing that tomorrow morning, at slack water, while Vosk's divers are sweeping the outer wreck and the *Vigilant* is steaming in to close the reef—we put everything where no one can unprove it. Lashed to the superstructure. Above the low-water line. Lit with strobes. In water so shallow that any diver Vosk sends down, any patrol launch, any press boat with a glass-bottomed hull will see it plain as a shop window. Let the evidence hang off the wreck itself like laundry. Let the *Meridian Star* testify."

Silence. Tam stopped fidgeting. Sefa stared at the photograph, and Cyrus watched her do the same arithmetic they had done—the tides, the timing, the odds—and watched her arrive somewhere different.

"They'll dynamite it," she said finally. "Vosk will blow the evidence to splinters rather than let it float."

"Maybe. Probably. But he has to do it in front of witnesses, in daylight, in shallow water, with the *Vigilant* arriving and half the port's fishing fleet already out for the morning haul. Every stick of explosive he sets becomes its own confession. And if Halloway—" Cyrus hesitated on the name, still unsure whether the man was a crack in the wall or part of the wall. "If Halloway meant anything he said tonight, he'll be standing on that deck when it happens. Men like him break eventually. We just have to make sure he breaks the right way."

"And you?" Sefa asked. "Who goes down to lash it? Because I can't dive, Tam can barely swim, and Vosk knows your face better than he knows his own crew's."

"I go down alone. Slack water, before the fleet's eyes are open. Ninety minutes to rig the lines, set the strobes, lash the log and the ledger copies and the box lid where the whole world can see them." Cyrus heard their own voice go quiet. "And then I come up, and whatever happens on the surface happens to all of us together, in front of everyone."

Sefa looked at her grandson. Tam looked back at her, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with words—an old family language, forged in forty years of refused hush money and unanswered letters.

"My great-uncle drowned believing no one would ever say his name again," she said at last. "Ninety-one men went into that water believing the same thing. And you're telling me the plan is to walk into the lion's mouth with the proof tied to our backs."

"I'm telling you the plan is to stop hiding. All the rest is detail."

She laughed then—a real laugh, ragged at the edges, the laugh of a woman who had spent six years requesting files that came back blacked-out and had just heard, for the first time, a plan shaped like hope instead of surrender. She reached across the table and put her hand flat on the tide tables.

"0440," she said. "Slack water at 0440. Tam will run you out in the *Kestrel*. I'll be on the radio by 0500 with every port frequency in the archipelago, reading names. If they want to arrest someone, they can arrest a woman reading the dead back to life on an open channel."

Outside, the wind had begun to rise out of the southeast, the first breath of the dry season's closing gales. It rattled the shutters and moaned in the chimney, and somewhere down in the harbor, the lights of Vosk's fleet rode at anchor like a string of patient, hungry stars.

One tide left. One chance, taken with the body before the mind finished consenting.

Cyrus checked the dive lamp, charged the strobes, and lay down in the dark without undressing, listening to the wind build, counting the hours the way a condemned man counts steps—not because the counting helps, but because the alternative is lying awake knowing exactly what's coming.

At 0330, the alarm rang once, and Cyrus rose, and the last day began.
