## 18. The Last Low Tide

The tide went out like a held breath finally released.

Cyrus stood on the seawall at half past three in the morning and watched the water fall away from the reef, inch by grudging inch, the way it had fallen away every night for a week—except tonight was the last night. Tomorrow the dry season would close the reef like a fist. Tomorrow the navy would anchor its buoys and string its lines and the Meridian Star would belong to them again, to whatever they wanted buried under her.

Tonight there was one low tide left, and Cyrus meant to spend all of it.

They had argued until midnight. Sefa had wanted to go with them—had wanted, in truth, to go down herself, though she'd never taken a breath from a tank in her life and said so only once, quietly, as if admitting a wound. Her grandson Tam had volunteered to run the boat. Halloway had laid out the navy's timetable on Ondine's scarred table with the precision of a man who had memorized his own betrayal: patrol rotation at dawn, Vosk's fleet weighing anchor an hour before that, the reef sealed by midmorning.

"There's no version of this," Halloway had said, "where you dive down, come back up, and hand me the evidence on a dock in daylight. The moment you surface with it, you're a thief. The moment Vosk sees you surface with it, you're a dead thief."

"So we don't surface with it," Cyrus had said.

And then the idea had arrived—not fully formed, not reasoned through, but arriving the way the tide arrives, inevitable and indifferent to whether anyone was ready for it. *Put it where everyone can see it. Put it where it can't be stolen because it can't be hidden.*

Lash the log, the ledger, the admiral's order to the wreck itself. To the exposed superstructure of the Meridian Star, standing proud of the sand now in the lowest tides of the year, visible from the surface, visible from any boat that cared to look. Rig strobes around it. Let the whole fleet anchor their lights over it and see what they had been paid to bury.

"You'd be hanging evidence on a wreck in open water," Sefa had said slowly, testing the shape of it, "in front of ninety-one families' worth of witnesses—if we can get them there."

"That's the other half," Cyrus said. "It only works if people are watching."

* * *

Now, alone in the dark at the harbor steps, Cyrus checked the gear a third time. Two waterproof cases, sealed and strapped: the captain's log of the Meridian Star, and the ledger of payoffs in Sefa's careful archival wrapping, and folded inside the second case, flat as a sin, the admiral's signed order with its stenciled authorization still legible after eleven years of salt. A bundle of marine strobes, six of them, salvaged fair over three seasons from wrecks nobody else had bothered to mourn. A spool of stainless lash wire. A knife. A tank filled to capacity and a spare bottle clipped low on the harness, because Cyrus did not intend to need it but intended very much to have it.

Tam crouched at the skiff, checking the fuel line for the fourth time. He was nineteen and had a propeller-wash bruise the size of a dinner plate fading yellow-green along his ribs, and he had not once suggested, in all these tides, that any of this was too much.

"Slack water's at four-forty," he said. "You'll have maybe fifty minutes of nothing moving before it turns. After that—" he made a small gesture toward the sea, toward everything the sea does when it changes its mind.

"Fifty minutes is enough." It was true. It had been enough for every dive so far. But something in Cyrus's chest kept insisting that this dive was different, that fifty minutes was a number and the sea did not read numbers, and Cyrus told that voice to shut up and finished coiling the line.

Sefa came down the steps carrying a lantern turned low, and she stopped a few feet away and just looked at Cyrus for a long moment, the way she looked at documents—with attention, with respect, with the particular grief of someone who reads things for a living and knows how much weight paper can hold.

"I keep thinking about my great-uncle," she said. "Emory Ilori. Quartermaster's mate. He wrote letters home for two years from that ship, and then the letters stopped, and my family got an envelope with money in it and no letter at all. My grandmother sent it back. She was the only one who did." Her hand tightened on the lantern handle. "Everyone thought she was mad. Proud and mad and poor forever. She died poor." A pause. "She died right."

Cyrus said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't smaller than the silence.

"Bring it up into the light," Sefa said. "That's all. Bring it up where it can't be put back down."

* * *

The skiff ran dark out past the buoy, engine at idle-throttle, Tam navigating by the three points of light he'd fixed in his head from shore—the customs house lamp, the chapel cross, the old signal mast on Gull Point—and by the pale seam of the reef showing where the falling tide stripped it bare. The fleet was already stirring out beyond the anchorage. Vosk's ships had their running lights on, and one of them, the big salvage crane vessel, was swinging at anchor closer than she'd been yesterday, her deck cranes silhouetted against the last stars like gallows being assembled in advance.

"They're loading charges," Tam said quietly. He nodded toward a workboat shuttling between the crane ship and the wreck site, riding low. "I watched them take crates off the pier at midnight. Crates that size, going out to a wreck site—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. Everyone in Vessany knew what dynamite crates looked like, and everyone knew what a man like Renn Vosk used them for.

"Then we're early enough," Cyrus said. "Barely."

The Meridian Star's superstructure broke the surface a hundred yards ahead, black against blacker water—the bridge wing rail, a stanchion of the boat deck, the tilted stump of the forward funnel, all of it exposed now as it had not been exposed in eleven years, standing out of the retreating sea like the bones of something that had been swallowed whole. Cyrus had seen the wreck a hundred times from above. Never like this. Never standing in the air.

Tam killed the engine and let the skiff drift the last stretch, and the silence out here was enormous—the lap of water on iron, the creak of the hull settling as the tide dropped, somewhere far off the cough of the fleet's generators. Cyrus swung the tank onto their back, shrugged the harness tight, checked the cases clipped to the D-rings one final time.

"If I'm not up in fifty minutes—"

"I know." Tam's jaw was set. "I go get Sefa, and we raise hell, and I don't leave this spot."

"Don't raise hell. Raise the families. That's better hell." Cyrus pulled the mask down over their face, bit the regulator, and rolled backward off the gunwale into water cold enough to stop thought.

* * *

Underwater, the world was simpler.

Descent along the shot line they'd rigged on Tide Four, hand over hand, the darkness thickening from grey to green to the absolute black of the deep channel, then lightening again as the reef shelf rose to meet the wreck. The strobes were already there waiting—two of them, planted on previous dives at the base of the superstructure, blinking their slow patient red. Cyrus had always thought of them as markers for their own navigation. Tonight they were rehearsal. Tonight there would be six more, and they would not be markers. They would be witnesses.

The exposed superstructure rose overhead like a cathedral that had fallen over sideways. In the slack-water gloom, with the tide drained away above it, the upper decks of the Meridian Star stood in only four fathoms of water—shallow, scandalously shallow, shallow enough that on the lowest tides a man in a skiff could look straight down and read the bridge telegraph still frozen at FULL ASTERN. Cyrus had seen that telegraph on the first dive, months ago, and had thought it strange and sad and thought nothing more about it. Now it was the whole story in brass and glass: a ship ordered full astern, a ship that never got to answer her helm, a ship drowned with her engines answering orders no one aboard had given.

Work first. Grief later.

Cyrus found the footing they'd scouted on Tide Five—the boat deck rail, bent outward where the funnel had come down across it, forming a natural cradle of steel. They unclipped the first case and began wiring it down, feeding the stainless lash wire through the rail stanchions, through the case handles, through a shackle point that had once held a lifeboat davit. The wire sang faintly under tension, a thin bright note in the water. Tight. Tighter. The case did not shift when Cyrus hauled on it with both hands. It would not shift in a storm. It would not shift under a diver's pry bar unless the pry bar man had hours and light and the patience of a saint, and Vosk's divers would have none of those things if the plan worked—if the whole anchorage woke up looking at what hung there.

The second case went opposite, balanced, wired the same way. Then the strobes, six of them, mounted high on the rail and on the funnel stump and on the bridge wing itself, angled upward, set to pulse in sequence—a slow rolling flash that would climb the superstructure and start again, climbing and starting again, all night, all morning, as long as their batteries lasted. Visible from the surface. Visible from a mile off. Visible, God willing, from the fleet's breakfast tables.

Cyrus worked with the mechanical calm of long habit and understood, distantly, that the calm was a lie of the hands only. The mind was elsewhere—running the arithmetic of the next few hours. Charges on Vosk's workboat. Patrol boats turning at dawn. Halloway burning his career on a radio call he might or might not make. Sefa on the dock with a list of names and a hundred phone calls' worth of courage. And underneath all of it, the simple brutal fact that Cyrus Eriksen was alone on a drowned ship at the bottom of a falling tide, and that everything anyone had bled for these six tides was now lashed to a railing in four fathoms of water, and that the only thing standing between it and the bottom of the sea forever was wire, and strobes, and whatever Cyrus could do next.

* * *

It was on the last strobe—the sixth, mounted on the bridge wing rail directly beside the engine telegraph—that Cyrus stopped working and started seeing.

The telegraph handle stood up out of its housing not two feet away, green-painted brass, the pointer jammed hard over against FULL ASTERN. Salt had welded it there. Eleven years of current had polished the housing smooth. And below it, bolted to the bulkhead where it had hung since the yard fitted her out, was the ship's bell bracket—empty. The bell was gone; they'd taken it on Tide Five, nearly died for it, and it sat now in Sefa's keeping wrapped in wool, waiting for tomorrow, waiting for ninety-one rings.

But the bracket was there. And bolted beside the bracket, screwed flush to the bulkhead, was a small brass plate, and Cyrus's lamp found it and the letters came up out of the green dark one at a time:

MERIDIAN STAR — PORT OF VESSANY — LAUNCHED 19—

And beneath the name, in smaller letters, the yard's traditional benediction, the one they cast into every hull that ever slid down the ways in this port:

MAY ALL WHO SAil IN HER RETURN TO HARbor.

Cyrus hung there in the water, regulator hissing, and read it twice, three times, and felt something in their chest crack along a seam that had been holding since the cave, since the log, since ninety-one names had stared up out of lamplight and refused to be cargo.

*May all who sail in her return to harbor.*

Ninety-one had not returned. Ninety-one had been ordered under, and the men who ordered it had signed their names in triplicate, and the money had gone out in plain brown envelopes, and the town had learned not to ask, and Cyrus had spent a lifetime learning not to be asked. Invisible by design. Invisible by choice. Invisible the way the Meridian Star was invisible—officially sunk, officially accounted for, officially nobody's grief.

The mind caught up late, the way it always did. The body had decided already—had decided somewhere between the fifth strobe and the brass plate, maybe had decided weeks ago in a flooded cave by lamplight. The body had chosen: not the strongbox, not the debt, not the clean vanishing life with a cut of Vosk's blood money warming some far shore. This. Wire and strobes and a name on a plate in four fathoms of water, hung out in the open where hiding was impossible, where the truth would sit in plain sight and dare the whole navy to look away.

Cyrus pressed a glove briefly against the brass plate. Salt-cold through the glove. Like touching a forehead.

"Hold on," they said into the regulator, and the words came out as a burst of silver bubbles that climbed away toward a surface they couldn't see, and it didn't matter who they were talking to. All of them. Any of them. Emory Ilori, quartermaster's mate, who wrote letters for two years. The grandmother who mailed the money back. The granddaughter on the dock right now with a lantern turned low.

Hold on. You're coming up.

* * *

The tide turned while Cyrus was still on the wreck.

They felt it before they saw it—a change in the pressure, a shiver in the water column, the dead-still slack giving way to the first faint pull of the flood. Fifty minutes gone. Maybe less. The current would build fast now, pouring back over the reef, and the swim to the skiff's mooring line would go from easy to ugly in twenty minutes if they waited too long.

One last thing.

Cyrus swam up over the boat deck to the highest point of the exposed superstructure—the corner of the bridge wing, standing barely three fathoms under the surface now, close enough to the air that the strobes' glow rippled against the underside of the swell. There, at the outermost corner, where any boat in the anchorage would pass closest, they mounted the seventh strobe—the spare, the one they hadn't planned, the one they'd carried because carrying spares was how you stayed alive in this trade. Wired it to the rail. Set it to pulse fastest of all. A heartbeat at the top of the wreck, flashing up through three fathoms of clearning water, saying *here, here, here* to anyone with eyes.

Then Cyrus took out the knife and did the last thing, the thing they had not told Sefa or Tam or Halloway because they had only understood it themselves in the last hour. On the underside of the bridge wing, facing straight up toward the sky, they cut away the growth of weed and barnacle in a rough rectangle, and beneath it the original hull paint still held, navy grey, and with the knife's tip they scored into it, deep and slow and deliberate, letter by letter, working against the building current:

91

Just the number. No accusation, no argument. Just the count. The count that would be visible from above at low water, painted in the wreck's own skin, lit by the strobes, impossible to sand off before dawn without a hundred witnesses asking why.

Cyrus looked at it for a long moment through the mask. It was ugly handwriting. It was enough.

Then the current took hold in earnest, and there was no more time for ceremony. Cyrus clipped off, found the mooring line, and swam into the flood headfirst, pulling hard, the spare bottle knocking against their hip, the whole ocean sliding past toward the land like a door swinging shut behind them.

* * *

They surfaced in the lee of the skiff gasping, and Tam's hand was there hauling them over the gunwale before their legs remembered how to work.

"Forty-eight minutes," Tam said. "I counted."

"Generous of you." Cyrus dragged the mask off, spat the regulator, and lay back against the thwart sucking air, and it was a full minute before they could lift their head and look back at the wreck.

The Meridian Star's superstructure stood out of the flooding sea, black and patient, and along its rails and funnel and bridge wing the strobes had begun their work—red pulses climbing the structure in sequence, climbing and beginning again, reflected doubled in the swelling water, a burning lattice visible, Cyrus judged, from anywhere in the anchorage. From the fleet. From the patrol boats. From the customs house roof, if anyone cared to climb it.

From everywhere. That was the point. It was in the open now. It could not be put back down.

Tam stared at it, and his young face in the pre-dawn dark was doing several things at once. "They'll see it," he said. "Vosk will see it the minute his watch-keepers wake up."

"That's the idea."

"And the charges—"

"He can't blow it now." Cyrus said it and heard, even as the words left their mouth, how much of it was hope dressed as certainty. "Not with the whole port able to see what he's blowing up. Not without explaining why a salvage contract needs dynamite aimed at a box of papers." A beat. "He'll try anyway. Men like Vosk don't rewrite the plan. They just spend more of other people's blood on it."

Tam started the engine, and they ran for shore with the sky still black but the east going a deep bruised purple, and halfway back Cyrus turned once in the stern sheets to look.

The fleet had woken. Lights were coming on along the crane ship's deck, one after another, and a workboat was putting out from her side toward the wreck, fast, riding high and empty, and Cyrus watched it go and felt no fear, or rather felt the fear and set it down beside them in the boat like a passenger they'd agreed to carry the rest of the way.

Behind the workboat, far out at the edge of the dark, other lights were moving too—small ones, scattered, unlit by any fleet's discipline. Fishing boats. Family boats. Boats with lanterns hung at their bows the way boats do when they are coming to a vigil and want to be counted.

Sefa's calls had gone out at midnight. The families were coming.

Cyrus gripped the gunwale and watched the little lights multiply across the water between the fleet and the shore, and the tide ran in beneath them, filling the reef, covering the wreck, covering the number 91 and the wired cases and the seven beating hearts of strobelight, covering it all—but not hiding it. Never hiding it again.

The workboat reached the superstructure and stopped, and a figure leaned out over her bow with a boathook, groping at the rail where the case was lashed, and the first grey light of the last day of the dry season came up out of the east and showed them all, fleet and families and one exhausted diver in an open skiff, exactly what the tide had given back.
