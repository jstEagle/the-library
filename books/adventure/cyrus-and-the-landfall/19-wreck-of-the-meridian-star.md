## 19. Wreck of the Meridian Star

Sefa heard the fleet's engines before she saw them—six boats running dark, no running lights, coming around the point at a speed no honest vessel used inside the reef line.

She was on the *Tern's Mercy*, her grandfather's old trawler, now her grandson Deni's boat and, as of three days ago, the floating headquarters of a conspiracy that had outgrown its conspirators. The radio room smelled of solder and old coffee. On the bench before her lay the ledger of payoffs, copied twice over in her own careful archivist's hand, and beside it the admiral's order—the one Cyrus had nearly drowned for—wrapped in oilcloth like a relic.

Deni stood in the doorway, silhouetted against the pre-dawn grey. "Grandmother. Lights on the water. Six of them."

"Vosk," she said.

"Cyrus is still down."

The words landed in her chest like ballast dropped from height. Cyrus Eriksen had gone into the black water an hour before slack tide to do the maddest thing Sefa had ever agreed to: lash ninety-one names and thirty years of buried orders to the exposed superstructure of the *Meridian Star*, lit with strobes, in water so shallow that no one—not Vosk, not the navy, not God himself—could pretend not to see it.

Not hide the evidence. Exhibit it. That had been Cyrus's plan, delivered flatly, the way they said everything, as if proposing a change to a mooring fee. *You can't sell truth to people who've already bought the lie. So you don't sell it. You hang it where the whole harbor has to look.*

And now six unlit boats were converging on the reef before dawn, and the diver who'd hung the truth there was still under it.

"Get us moving," Sefa said. "Slow. We're just another fishing boat going out early." Her voice came out steadier than her hands. "And wake up every radio in that rack."

* * *

The *Meridian Star* sat in twelve feet of water at slack low, and even in the grey half-light Sefa could see it from two hundred yards off: the broken superstructure breaking the surface like a whale's back, and along it, strobing white every two seconds, a line of dive lights lashed to the rail with cable ties—a string of small cold stars marking the grave of ninety-one people.

Someone had already seen it. She could hear it before she understood it: voices carrying across flat water, from the fishing boats that had drifted out at first light to gawk, from the seawall where the early market crowd stood in a ragged line. The reef that had been cordoned and forbidden for thirty years was suddenly the most public place in Vessany.

Then the lead boat turned toward it, and Sefa saw the man at the bow through her grandfather's binoculars, and her stomach went cold.

Renn Vosk stood with his legs braced wide, the way men stand when they intend to stay put through violence. Two of his divers crouched behind him. Between them, on the deck plates, sat something long and wrapped in canvas, and when one of the divers peeled the canvas back, the morning light caught metal and coiled fuse.

"Dynamite," Deni said. He'd come up beside her without her hearing, which frightened her more than the boats did. "He's going to blow the wreck."

"He's going to blow the evidence." Sefa set down the binoculars. Her hands were shaking now; she let them. Fear was information. "The wreck he can explain away. A storm-shifted hull, a collapse. But those lights—" She looked at the strobes pulsing their patient white rhythm. "He can't blow what everyone in port is looking at unless he does it in front of everyone in port."

"Then he'll wait for—"

"He won't wait." She was already reaching for the radio handset. Thirty years in the naval archives had taught her exactly one thing about men like Vosk and the admirals who hired them: they never waited. Waiting was how documents got read. Waiting was how names got remembered. They acted in the dark and apologized in the light, and the light always, always blinked first.

She keyed the transmit switch and spoke into the mouthpiece of a radio that could reach every harbor set in Vessany, every fishing skiff, every patrol cutter, every galley speaker from the customs house to the cannery pier.

"All stations, all stations. This is Sefa Ilori, formerly Archivist Third Class, Naval Records Office. I am broadcasting from position off the Meridian Star reef." Her voice didn't sound like hers. It sounded like her grandfather's—like the quartermaster who had survived when his brother hadn't, who had spent his last forty years writing letters no one answered. "I am holding the cargo manifest of the *Meridian Star*, lost with all hands save one, twenty-nine years ago this month. I am holding a ledger of payments made to the families of ninety-one dead sailors in exchange for silence. And I am holding a signed order, bearing an admiral's seal, directing that the ship be flooded with her crew aboard."

She paused. Across the water, Vosk's divers were lowering the charge over the side.

"The man about to destroy that evidence is Captain Renn Vosk, master of the salvage fleet *Kestrel*, operating under contract from the Office of the Admiral-Commodore. Everything I have said is documented. Everything I have said will be repeated until someone makes me stop. All stations, look at your reefs. Look at your wrecks. Ask yourselves what else is down there being kept quiet for you."

She released the key. The radio room was silent except for the tick of cooling solder.

Deni was staring at her. "Grandmother. They're going to come for us."

"I know," said Sefa Ilori, and keyed the mic again, and began reading names.

* * *

She started with the first page of the crew list because it was alphabetical and because alphabet felt like order and order felt like armor. "Abara, Tomas, able seaman, aged twenty-four. Adjei, Ruth, cook's assistant, aged nineteen."

On the third name, the radios began to answer.

Not voices—at first just carriers, open mics, the electronic equivalent of a held breath. Then a fishing boat called in: *"This is Petrel's Daughter, we're off the north buoy, we see the lights, we see the fleet."* Then another. Then the harbormaster's office, cautious and official-sounding, asking her to identify her source of documentation. Then a voice she recognized with a jolt—an old woman from the Salt Row widows' cooperative, a woman Sefa had interviewed four years ago and been ordered to strike from the record—saying only: *"Say the name again. Say my husband's name again."*

"Abara, Tomas," Sefa said, and her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack, because ninety-one people had died in the dark and the least their deaths deserved was a witness who wasn't smooth. "Abara, Tomas. Able seaman. Twenty-four years old."

Through the wheelhouse window she watched the fleet converge.

Vosk's lead boat had reached the shallows and stopped, hemmed in by its own draft, and the divers were working the charge down toward the superstructure on a line. The strobes pulsed. The crowd on the seawall had grown; she could see them massing even at this distance, a dark fringe along the breakwater, and more boats were putting out from the harbor—fishing boats, oyster punts, a ferry running off-schedule with its deck crowded.

And then, cutting across all of it at full speed with its running lights blazing and its ensign snapping, came a navy patrol cutter.

Sefa's breath caught. For one whole second she thought: *they've sent them to finish it.* The navy had cordoned this reef for thirty years; the navy had redacted every file she'd ever requested; the navy had paid her family's neighbors to scatter and forget. Of course the cutter was here for the same reason Vosk was.

But the cutter didn't slow at the cordon buoy. It drove straight through it, horn sounding, and swung broadside between Vosk's boat and the wreck, and a voice came over the fleet channel, loud enough that Deni's receiver picked it up and filled the wheelhouse:

"This is Lieutenant Halloway, Naval Patrol Vessel *Vigilant*. Captain Vosk, you are ordered to recall your divers immediately. You are operating in a restricted site without authorization, and you are preparing to detonate explosives over a military grave."

There was a pause. In it, Sefa heard the whole architecture of the last month tremble.

"Lieutenant," Vosk's voice came back, easy, unhurried, the voice of a man who owned half a port, "you might want to check whose signature is on your orders this morning. Because mine goes higher than yours."

"My orders," Halloway said, and Sefa closed her eyes, because she knew what was coming, had known since the night she'd found that unredacted page left in her document tray like a confession, "are the ones I took an oath on. Recall your divers, Captain. That's the last time I'll ask."

The divers kept working.

What happened next happened fast, the way reckonings do after decades of delay. Halloway brought the *Vigilant* in hard, gunwale to gunwale with Vosk's lead boat, and boarding parties went over the sides. Vosk's crew—men who bought drinks and remembered faces, men paid to be invisible in the other direction—looked at the cutter's rifles, at the growing crowd on the seawall, at the strobes blinking their patient count over the grave, and made the calculation men like them always make. Two of them dropped the charge line entirely. One dove overboard and swam for it. Vosk himself stood at his bow with the canvas-wrapped detonator box in both hands, and for a moment—one long, salt-heavy moment—he actually raised it.

"Don't," Halloway said, and it carried across the water, plain and almost gentle. "Captain. There are ninety-one families watching you from that shore. Don't give them this too."

Sefa never learned afterward what broke in Renn Vosk in that second—whether it was the rifles or the crowd or the simple arithmetic of a career spent buying silence finally coming due. She only saw him lower the box. Saw the boarding party take it from him. Saw him turn, slowly, to face the shore where dozens of boats were now arriving, packed stem to stern, flying no colors at all, carrying people.

* * *

They came from everywhere. From Salt Row and the cannery flats, from inland villages three days' travel away, from the far ports where hush money had scattered them twenty-nine years ago and where some of them had stayed, waiting, without ever admitting to themselves what they were waiting for.

Sefa stood at the rail of the *Tern's Mercy* and watched them come, and read names into the radio the whole while, because the reading was the thing, the reading was the rope thrown to drowning people: *"Bello, Anselm, engine room, aged thirty-one. Corver, Mireille, steward, aged twenty-seven."*

A boat drew alongside, an old oyster punt with a cracked windshield, and standing in it was the widow from Salt Row, the one from the cooperative, gripping the gunwale with both hands, staring at the strobing lights on the water.

"That's him?" the woman called up. Her voice was thin and it carried anyway. "That's where he is?"

"It's where they all are," Sefa called back. "Every one of them. Together."

The woman looked at the water for a long time. Then she did something Sefa would remember for the rest of her life: she took off her headscarf and held it over the side, and behind her, boat by boat, other people began doing the same—scarves, hats, caps, shirts held up in the grey morning air, a forest of fabric waving over the reef like kelp in a current, like a fleet's worth of flags for a country that had never existed until right now.

*"Daramola, Efua, quartermaster's mate, aged twenty-two."* Sefa's own family name. Her great-uncle. Her grandmother's brother, who had written letters home from every port until the letter that never came. She read his name and her voice held, mostly, and Deni put his hand on her shoulder and left it there.

Out at the wreck, divers were going down now—navy divers, under Halloway's flag, with cameras instead of cutting torches. The strobes still pulsed. Lashed to the rail, waterproofed and unmistakable, the evidence hung in the shallow water like a lantern, and above it the boats gathered, and above them the sky went from grey to the color of hammered tin to, at last, a thin and ordinary gold.

It was over. She knew it was over the way you know a fever has broken—not because anything dramatic happens, but because the fear simply isn't there anymore when you reach for it.

Then Deni touched her arm. "Grandmother. The dive float. It hasn't come up."

Sefa turned. Out past the fleet, past the cutter, past the drifting crowd of scarves and witnesses, the small orange marker buoy that Cyrus Eriksen had launched before descending—the signal that meant *diver below, clear the area*—bobbed alone in the swell, and had been bobbing alone for a very long time.

"No," she said. Not a question. A refusal.

"Their air was good for sixty minutes at that depth," Deni said quietly. "They went down seventy minutes ago."

Sefa looked at the orange buoy. At the strobes blinking beneath it, patient as a pulse. At the hundred boats full of people who had finally, finally come.

"Halloway," she said, and grabbed the radio handset, and her voice went out over every channel in the harbor, cracking and fierce and not remotely calm: "Lieutenant Halloway. Get your divers in the water. Now. One of ours is still down there—and after everything your navy took from these people, you do *not* get to lose one more."
