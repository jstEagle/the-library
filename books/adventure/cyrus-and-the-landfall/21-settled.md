## 21. Settled

The harbor smelled of pitch and rain the morning after the bells.

Ninety-one rings had gone out over the water the evening before, and the sound of them seemed to have soaked into everything—the pilings, the tarred rope, the salt-crusted windows of Ondine Marrec's office above the chandlery. Cyrus climbed the outside stairs with the particular lightness of a person carrying nothing for the first time in months. No strongbox. No ledger. No admiral's signature rolled in oilcloth against their ribs. All of it was in navy custody now, photographed a hundred times over, read aloud on port radio until the clerk at the customs house could recite the payoffs from memory.

The door to Ondine's office stood open.

She was at her desk with the ledger book—the real one, hers—open before her, and she did not look up when Cyrus came in. The room was smaller than Cyrus remembered it being, or perhaps Cyrus had simply been smaller when they'd first signed for the boat. A lamp with a cracked glass chimney. A map of the Vessany approaches pinned to the wall, corners curling. Rain ticking on the tin roof like a patient hand.

"Sit," Ondine said.

Cyrus sat. The chair was the same one from three years ago, when they'd signed the note that kept the *Kestrel* afloat and put their name in her book. They'd been invisible then, more or less. A diver nobody hired twice because nobody remembered hiring them once. Ondine had been the only person in Vessany who knew their name, and she'd used it the way you use a hook.

She closed her ledger. She turned it around so the spine faced Cyrus, and then she opened it again to the page with their name on it. Cyrus Eriksen. Principal outstanding. Interest accrued, season over season, in her small merciless handwriting. It was a longer column than Cyrus had ever let themselves look at directly.

"You rang a bell ninety-one times," Ondine said. "I counted."

"Everyone counted."

"I counted because my husband's brother-in-law was one of them." She said it flatly, the way she said everything, but her thumb pressed hard against the edge of the ledger page, whitening the skin. "Tomas Ferren. Engine room. He was twenty-six. My sister got a letter eight months after the *Meridian Star* went down saying he'd been lost with all hands in an uncharted shoaling incident, and a bank draft for four hundred crowns, and instructions not to make inquiries. She made inquiries. That's why she lives in Corvess now and doesn't write."

Cyrus said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't already ringing somewhere over the reef.

Ondine took out a knife—not the big chandlery blade she used for cutting canvas, but a small folding one, bone-handled, the kind a woman keeps in an apron pocket for forty years—and she began, methodically, to cut the page out of the ledger.

The sound of it was worse than tearing. The paper resisted, then gave, and the knife whispered along the binding gutter, and Cyrus sat very still and watched three years of debt come loose from the book.

When the page was free, she held it out.

"Take it."

Cyrus didn't move. "Ondine—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked like a line going taut. "Don't thank me, and don't call it charity, because if you do I'll put the page back and charge you another season's interest out of spite. This isn't forgiveness. This is payment." She pushed the page across the desk until it touched Cyrus's hand. "You want to know what you're paying for? You're paying for silence. Mine."

Cyrus took the page. It was lighter than it should have been.

"Four years ago," Ondine said, "after Rennard drowned on the navy contract—after they paid me off and told me the same story everyone gets told—I started selling information. Small things, at first. Which boats were running what past the customs buoy. Then bigger things. Who was diving where they shouldn't be." Her eyes came up, gray and level as the winter sea. "I sold your dives, Cyrus. Every one of them, before the cordon ever went up. Halloway's people knew about the *Kestrel* slipping out before you ever slipped out. I told them about the dawn runs. I told them about the brass. If they hadn't been so busy watching the wrong part of the reef, you'd have been boarded in your first week."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere below, in the chandlery, a customer was arguing about the price of oakum.

"Why are you telling me this now?" Cyrus asked.

"Because yesterday there were a hundred boats on that reef, and every one of them knows your name, and if I don't say it now I never will." She folded her hands on the ledger, on the raw gap where the page had been. "And because I need you to understand something about debts. I've spent four years telling myself mine was settled—that Rennard's death was paid for, that the draft covered it, that selling scraps of other people's business was just how a widow eats. It wasn't settled. It was buried. Same as those men." She nodded toward the window, toward the harbor, toward the reef beyond it. "You dug them up. And when you dug them up, you dug me up too. So we're even. Not square—there's no such thing as square—but even. You carried my secret into the water along with everything else, and you never once tried to buy your way clear with it, though Vosk would have paid you handsomely for exactly that kind of leverage. I know. He offered me some."

"He did?"

"Last week. Before his fleet sailed." Something moved behind her eyes, old and tired. "He wanted to know if you were the sort who could be bought through a third party. I told him you weren't worth the trouble." The ghost of a smile, there and gone. "First lie I've told him for free."

Cyrus looked down at the ledger page in their hands. Principal outstanding. Interest accrued. Their own name in ink, in a dead language now, a contract dissolved by a knife and a confession. They felt the strange vertigo of a man whose ballast has been cut loose—who rises too fast and has to trust the water to hold him gently.

"What happens to you?" Cyrus asked. "If Halloway's inquiry goes looking at information brokers—"

"It will. I'll be standing on the dock waiting for it." Ondine straightened the ledger, squared its corners with the desk edge, a woman putting her house in order. "I'll tell them everything. Names, dates, prices. It won't save the inquiry any time, but it might save someone else's sister a letter eight months late." She looked at Cyrus then, and for a moment the harbor boss was gone and there was only a woman of sixty-odd years sitting in a small room with rain on the roof. "Rennard used to say the sea keeps honest books. Everything owed comes due eventually. I'm paying while I can still see the page."

Cyrus stood. Folded the torn page once, twice, and put it inside their coat, over the heart, where a month ago the admiral's order had ridden.

"Thank you," Cyrus said.

"That's charity talk."

"No," Cyrus said. "That's payment received."

* * *

The naval administration building in Vessany was a squat granite block that always reminded Cyrus of a tombstone for something too large to bury. They had never been inside it. They went inside it now, past a sentry who recognized them—everyone recognized them now, that was the whole strange weight of it—and up a stair that smelled of floor wax and old paper, to a corridor of doors with brass plates.

Lt. Aldous Halloway's door was open. He was packing.

Not fleeing-packing. Resigning-packing: books squared in crates, files tied in bundles with government string, a framed certificate of commendation laid face-down on the desk like something he couldn't bear to look at. He wore his uniform, complete, every button done, as though dressing for a funeral he was also officiating.

"You shouldn't be here," he said, without heat. "This office is technically still a restricted area."

"So arrest me. You'd be the last man in Vessany with the authority to do it."

Halloway almost smiled. He set down the bundle of files he was holding and looked out the narrow window at the harbor, where the masts stood thick as reeds. "They're offering me a promotion," he said. "Did you know that? Rear section chief, Records Division, Corvess. A quiet office. A pension. And one document to sign before I transfer—the final disposition order for the Meridian Star file. Sealed. Ninety-nine years." He picked up a single sheet from the desk, held it between two fingers as though it were wet. "One signature, Cyrus. One line of ink, and everything you and Ilori bled for goes back under the water permanently. They'd have given me the bell ceremony, the commendation, the works. Halloway, the man who flagged the irregularity. Halloway, the conscience of the division."

"And you wouldn't sign."

"I drafted the flag six years ago." He set the sheet down carefully, aligned it with the desk edge, the way Ondine had squared her ledger. "Do you know what it's like to find something true in a system built to digest truth? I wrote a memo. Four pages. Irregularities in the loss of HMS Meridian Star, recommended immediate inquiry. It came back to me redacted so thoroughly that my own name was missing from it. I resubmitted. It came back with a note suggesting I consider my career prospects." He laughed, short and airless. "So I considered them. For six years I considered them. I told myself I was staying close to the file, keeping it alive, keeping myself positioned to act. And all the while I was the lock on the door, wearing a key I refused to turn."

"You turned it," Cyrus said. "The unredacted page. Sefa found it."

"After I left it where she'd find it, yes. Which is not the same thing as courage. Courage would have been turning it six years ago, when ninety-one families were still waiting by their doors instead of scattered across three coastlines." He resumed packing, but slower now, each book placed rather than dropped. "I resigned this morning. Effective immediately. Refused the transfer, refused the disposition order, and wrote a statement naming every officer above me who touched that file. It'll be in the inquiry record by tomorrow, and in the naval gazette by the week's end, and after that I expect to be a very unpopular guest at every mess hall from here to Corvess."

"They'll try to bury you with it."

"Probably. But here's the thing I learned from you people—from you, and Ilori, and that boy of hers who took a propeller wash to keep you alive—" He stopped, hands flat on the crate, and Cyrus saw for the first time how much older he looked than the man who'd posted the reward notice in the harbor office a season ago. "There's a difference between being buried and being sunk. Buried things stay where you put them. Sunk things wash up. The tide brings them back." He glanced at Cyrus. "You taught this port that. I'm just applying the doctrine."

Cyrus leaned against the doorframe. Below the window, the harbor lay bright under a rinsed sky, and among the working masts Cyrus could pick out the family boats—Sefa's people, the Ferrens, the Okonkwos, the dozens of names from the log who had come by sea to hear a bell. Some of them were still anchored out at the reef roadstead, keeping a kind of vigil nobody had organized and nobody would end.

"The inquiry," Cyrus said. "Will it go anywhere? Honestly."

Halloway considered the question with the seriousness he gave everything. "Honestly? The admiralty will sacrifice Vosk—he's a contractor, expendable, and dynamiting evidence in front of a hundred witnesses is the kind of crime even admirals can't redact. They'll sacrifice two or three middle officers whose careers were already stagnant. They will fight like drowning men to keep it below the flag rank, and they may succeed for a year, maybe five." He closed the crate. "But the file is public now. The names are public. Ninety-one families have copies of a ledger in their own hands, and ledgers multiply. Somewhere in Corvess right now a junior archivist is reading that ledger and writing a memo. Maybe she'll be braver than I was. Maybe she'll only take six years instead of sixteen." He looked up. "It goes somewhere. Slower than justice should travel, but it goes."

He picked up the face-down certificate, looked at it a long moment, and then set it in the crate with the books—kept, not discarded. Whatever it meant to him, he wasn't ready to throw it away, and Cyrus understood that better than most.

"There's one more thing," Halloway said. He reached into his breast pocket and produced a folded paper, official stock, stamped. "Your salvage claim. The *Kestrel* was seized under the unauthorized-salvage statute—my statute, my posting. The seizure is voided as of this morning; the reward notice is withdrawn. And this—" he held it out— "is a salvage award. Provisional. For recovery of materials material to a naval inquiry. It's a fiction, legally speaking. The bell and the documents were recovered under circumstances the court will spend years untangling. But the adjudicator owes the inquiry a debt of gratitude, and gratitude, in the navy, is paid in paperwork."

Cyrus took the paper. Read it. Read it again. The figure at the bottom was not large—it was, in fact, roughly the size of a modest fishing boat, a sound hull, a decent engine, nothing more—but it was written against Cyrus's own name, spelled correctly, witnessed twice.

"This clears the debt," Cyrus said slowly. "Or replaces it."

"It does neither. Debts and payments are Ondine Marrec's jurisdiction; I've read enough of her ledger to know she outranks me." Halloway allowed himself the smile he'd been withholding. "This is just the navy admitting, in its own cramped bureaucratic dialect, that you did a thing worth doing. Take it or leave it. But if you take it—" and here his voice changed, became the archivist's voice, precise and careful— "know that dry season ends in nine days. The reef closes. The cave floods and doesn't open again for half a year, maybe longer, and when it opens the inquiry may want divers on site. Certified ones. Ones the families trust."

Cyrus looked at the paper. Nine days. The *Kestrel* was still impounded at the customs dock—no, not impounded anymore. Voided. Free. A boat with a season left in her hull, maybe less, and a reef that would soon swallow the only place in the world where ninety-one men had left anything behind.

"Sefa's family sails the day after tomorrow," Cyrus said. "Past the buoy. They asked me to come."

"Then you have nine days and a decision," Halloway said. He extended his hand, and they shook, officer and salvager, across a desk full of packed-away years. "For what it's worth, Cyrus Eriksen—I flagged the wreck, and you finished the job. Between us, I think the file closes clean."

Cyrus walked down the stairs and out into the washed morning light, the provisional award folded in their coat beside the torn ledger page, two papers riding together over the heart—one a debt dissolved, one a debt acknowledged. At the bottom of the steps, the harbor opened wide, and the tide was turning, and somewhere past the buoy the *Meridian Star* waited with her bell rung silent and her dead named at last, holding her ground against the coming season the way the living had finally learned to hold theirs.

Nine days. Cyrus turned toward the customs dock, where a small boat with a familiar silhouette rode at her lines, free water under her keel for the first time in a year—and broke into a walk that was nearly a run.
