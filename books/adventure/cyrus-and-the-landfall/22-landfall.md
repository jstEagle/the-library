## 22. Landfall

The rain came back on a Tuesday.

Cyrus felt it before they saw it—a change in the way the wind sat against the harbor, the smell of wet stone and far-off green that had been absent for three months. The dry season was ending. Everyone in Vessany knew what that meant: the reef would close. The channels that ran shallow over the coral shelf would silt up with the first storms, the water would go brown and blind, and whatever the Meridian Star still held in her drowned bones would belong to the sea again until next year—if anyone was fool enough to come back at all after what had happened there.

Cyrus stood on the seawall with a cup of terrible coffee and watched the first clouds stack up over the headland like ships under full sail. The harbor was busy in a way it hadn't been in years. Boats that usually sat rusting at their moorings were out working. Men who used to look through Cyrus as if they were glass were nodding now. A woman selling fried fish at the market called out "Eriksen!" across three stalls just to say good morning, and Cyrus had stood there holding a paper of salted plantain not knowing what to do with being seen.

That was the strange arithmetic of it. They had gone into the water with everything—boat, gear, reputation, the last shreds of anonymity—and come out with nothing but a name that everyone knew. By any ledger Cyrus had ever kept, that was ruin. And yet standing there on the seawall, watching the weather come in, they could not make themselves feel ruined.

The *Kestrel* was gone, of course. Ondine's office had burned; Vosk's men had made sure of that, and though Vosk himself was in the naval brig awaiting trial and his fleet had scattered like startled fish, the fire had taken the harbor records with it—including the paper that said Cyrus Eriksen owed Ondine Marrec one more season or the boat. The *Kestrel* herself had been seized as evidence in the inquiry, hauled out onto the navy slipway where she sat under a tarp, photographed and catalogued and argued over by lawyers. She'd been a leaky old thing anyway. Cyrus told everyone that. It was mostly even true.

What stung was the gear. Twenty years of accumulated kit—the good mask, the spare regulators, the slate with tide tables scratched into it going back six seasons—all impounded in an evidence locker, tagged and bagged like contraband. Somewhere in a navy warehouse, Cyrus's whole working life sat in plastic bins waiting for a tribunal that might take two years to decide anything.

So: boatless, broke, and famous. There were worse fates. Cyrus had spent twenty years proving it.

* * *

Sefa found them at the seawall, as she always did now, as if she'd mapped Cyrus's habits the way she'd once mapped the archive stacks. She came down the steps with her coat collar up against the coming rain and a folder under her arm, because Sefa Ilori did not walk anywhere without a folder.

"You're staring at the reef," she said.

"I'm staring at the weather."

"You're staring at the reef." She leaned on the wall beside them. Out past the buoy—the old channel marker with its faded paint and its bell that no longer rang—the sea lay flat and pewter-colored, and beneath it, four miles out, the Meridian Star waited under her new coat of fame. Divers had been out to her every day since the inquiry opened. Photographers. Naval survey teams. Two weeks ago a man from the capital's newspaper had hired a boat and come back white-faced, talking about names stenciled inside a strongbox.

"They're closing it," Sefa said. "Officially. The reef goes restricted until the tribunal finishes. Could be a year. Could be three." She tapped the folder. "I have the order here. I wrote half of it myself, which is either progress or surrender, I haven't decided."

"Which is it?"

"Both." She looked out at the water. Her grandfather's face lived somewhere behind her eyes when she did that; Cyrus had learned to see it. "Halloway resigned yesterday. Did you hear?"

"I heard."

"He wouldn't sign the final disposition. The one that says the site is 'closed pending administrative review'—which is the navy's way of saying buried, again, politely, with stamps." Sefa's mouth went thin. "He left rather than put his name on it. Walked out of the building with his hat under his arm like something out of an old painting. I thought I'd hate him forever, you know. The man who redacted my grandfather's file eleven times. Eleven. I counted."

"And now?"

"Now he leaves me his unredacted pages like a man leaving flowers at a grave he helped dig." She shook her head slowly. "People are not files, Cyrus. That's the whole lesson of this year. I keep having to learn it in person."

The first drops came then, fat and warm, spotting the stone around their feet. Neither of them moved. In Vessany, the first rain of the wet season was an event; people came out of doorways and held their palms up like it was a benediction. Down the quay, someone started laughing, and a child ran barefoot through the puddles forming between the cobbles, arms out, screaming with joy.

"My grandmother stood in the rain like that," Sefa said quietly. "The day the hush money letter came. She read it, and she walked outside, and she stood in the rain until it soaked her through, and then she came back in and said, 'They think we can be paid.' Ninety-one men, and they sent her a number." She closed the folder. "She never cashed it. It's in my desk drawer. I look at it sometimes when I forget why I'm doing any of this."

"Do you forget?"

"Less than I used to." She turned and looked at Cyrus directly, and there was something in her face that Cyrus had learned to be wary of, because it usually preceded a proposal. "The *Anansi* sails Thursday."

"The *Anansi*."

"My family's boat. My grandfather's, then my mother's, then mine. Forty-two feet of stubborn timber older than both of us put together." A small smile. "You've seen her. She's the ugly blue one with the patched jib, third berth from the fuel dock."

"I know her."

"We're going out past the buoy. One last time before the reef closes and the weather shuts the door." She said it lightly, but her hand was flat on the folder, pressing it down as if the wind might take it. "My family hasn't been out to those waters since the funeral boats. Sixty years. My mother refused. My grandfather went once, alone, at night, in a rowboat, and came back and never spoke of it. I used to think that was weakness. Now I think it was a man carrying ninety-one names in a vessel built for one."

She paused. The rain thickened, drumming on the seawall, running silver down the stone.

"There's a berth on the *Anansi*," she said. "Has been since March, honestly, since before I understood why. Grandfather's old berth. Forward, port side, under the leaky hatch that we've never fixed because fixing it would mean admitting the boat isn't perfect." She shrugged, elaborately casual, entirely failing at it. "It pays nothing. The work is hauling lines and reading water and arguing with my grandson about the engine. But the *Anansi* runs charters now—survey work, mostly, honest diving for the tribunal, and teaching kids from the town to swim and read charts. We need hands. And I'd rather sail with someone whose name I trust than someone whose references I do."

Cyrus looked at her for a long moment. Rain ran off the brim of an imaginary hat they weren't wearing; twenty years of habit reached for the answer that kept them safe, that kept them moving, that kept them nobody. There was a tramp steamer loading at the far pier, bound south. There was always a tramp steamer. There was always a way to be invisible again, to let the water close over the name and sink back into the comfortable dark where no one expected anything of you.

But the dark was where the Meridian Star had lain for sixty years, and Cyrus had spent a season learning what the dark does with the truth. It keeps it. It keeps it so well that people drown trying to bury things there, and the things wait, patient as brass, for one low tide brave enough to give them back.

"When do you need an answer?" Cyrus asked.

Sefa blinked. "I—well. Thursday. But—"

"No," Cyrus said. "I mean yes. Yes. Before you talk yourself into taking it back." They stuck out a hand, rain-slick and callused. "I'll take the berth. Leaky hatch and all."

Sefa took the hand and gripped it hard, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the rain filled the silence the way it fills everything in Vessany—generously, without asking.

"You'll hate the engine arguments," she said finally.

"I've been looking forward to them all morning."

* * *

Thursday came gray and gusting, the tail of the dry season blowing itself out over the harbor. The *Anansi* looked worse and better than Cyrus remembered—worse because the patch on the jib had grown a companion patch, better because Sefa's grandson, Tam, had repainted the rail a defiant bright yellow, and because the wheel had been rewrapped in new cord, tight and clean, the kind of care that gets taken by people who intend to keep a thing.

There were others aboard. That was the part that undid Cyrus, standing on the dock with their single duffel bag—everything they owned in the world now fit in one canvas sack, and it should have felt like poverty, and instead it felt like lightness. Because coming up the gangplank behind Sefa came a dozen townsfolk. The fish seller with the loud voice. Two of the kids from the swimming classes, solemn with importance, allowed aboard as ballast and entertainment. Old Maren from the chandlery, who had once refused Cyrus credit for three straight years and now pressed a wrapped parcel of food into their hands and said, "For the passage, diver," as if it were nothing, as if it were everything.

And at the end of the line, awkward as a man at a funeral he wasn't sure he was welcome at, stood Halloway. Out of uniform. A civilian jacket that didn't fit him right, a duffel of his own.

"Sefa invited me," he said, before Cyrus could say anything. His ears were red. "I told her it was a bad idea."

"It probably is," Cyrus said.

"Yes." He shifted. "Well. I've been making bad decisions all year. This one at least has decent company." He looked out at the water, jaw working. "I flagged that file in my first year. Did you know that? Twenty-three years old, first posting, and I read the Meridian Star incident report and wrote 'recommend full inquiry' in the margin. They thanked me and shelved it. I told myself for thirty years that I'd done my duty by writing it down." He shook his head. "Writing it down isn't duty. Putting it where people can see it—that's duty. I learned that late. From a thief and an archivist and a boy with a patched jib." He almost smiled. "Anyway. I can handle a line. If the berth's taken, I'll sit on the rail and stay out of the way."

Cyrus looked at him—this man who had hunted them through the fish market, who had left pages like flowers at graves—and found that the hatred had quietly drained out of the account sometime during the winter, replaced by something harder to carry and worth more.

"There's room forward," Cyrus said. "Under the leaky hatch. You'll get dripped on."

"I've been dripped on by worse."

* * *

They cleared the breakwater at mid-morning and ran out toward the buoy with the wind quartering astern, the *Anansi* rolling easy in the long swell. Tam had the wheel and wore the expression of a boy who had been born for exactly this and knew it. Sefa stood at the bow with her grandfather's old quartermaster's telescope, sweeping the horizon out of habit. The kids shrieked at flying fish. Maren sang something bawdy until Sefa told her to stop, and then sang it quieter.

Cyrus worked the boat. That was the miracle of it, the plain ordinary miracle: there was work, and they did it. Coiling line. Trimming the jib. Reading the water ahead for Tam and calling the gusts. Nobody watched them do it with suspicion. Nobody weighed it against a debt. The work was just work, and it belonged to them, and they belonged to the doing of it.

The buoy came up on the starboard bow an hour later—the old channel marker, paint peeling, riding its mooring chain with the patience of things that outlast governments. Beyond it, the water changed color, deepening from green to slate, and somewhere under that slate lay the reef, and under the reef the wreck, sealed now behind an official order and the coming storms. The tribunal would pick her bones by remote camera next spring. Divers were forbidden. The cave, if it still existed, if the tide still made it, would fill and empty and fill again with no one to watch.

Sefa rang the ship's bell twice as they passed the buoy—two strokes, soft, the sound carrying flat across the water. For the crew, she didn't say. She didn't have to.

Then Tam brought the boat about, and they ran back for the harbor with the rain squall chasing them, and the town rose up out of the gray with its crooked roofs and its smoking chimneys and its hundred windows lit against the weather, and every one of those windows, Cyrus thought, belonged to someone who knew their name. Not the name whispered over reward notices. Not the name muttered by harbor bosses tallying debts. Just the name. Cyrus Eriksen, who dives, who argues about engines, who takes the berth under the leaky hatch.

Ondine met them at the dock. She'd rebuilt the office smaller, one room, a plank counter, a kettle. She stood there with her arms crossed and her chin up, defying anyone to pity her, and when Cyrus climbed onto the planks she jerked her head toward the harbor entrance.

"Storm's two days out," she said. "Channel'll be soup by Friday. You picked your window."

"I had help picking it."

"Hm." She looked at the *Anansi*, at the yellow rail, at the kids hauling fenders. Something moved behind her eyes that she'd have denied under oath. "Marrecs have worked this harbor for four generations. We keep accounts. Debts, favors, silences—it's all one ledger." She uncrossed her arms. "Yours is clear, Eriksen. Don't come back asking for credit."

"I wouldn't dare."

"See that you don't." But she was almost smiling when she said it, and she stayed on the dock watching until the *Anansi*'s lines were coiled and the kettle went on below, and only then did she turn back to her small office and her small ledger and her large, unsinkable life.

That night, in the berth under the leaky hatch, Cyrus lay awake listening to the drip—patient, irregular, faithful as a clock—and to the sounds of a boat full of people settling around them: Tam's laughter fading down the companionway, Sefa's lamp burning late over her folders, Halloway's careful footsteps checking the anchor rode because some habits outlive careers. Rain arrived properly around midnight, drumming on the deck above, and the hatch leaked exactly as promised, one cold bright drop finding Cyrus's forehead every few minutes.

Twenty years ago, Cyrus would have been gone by now. Would have slipped the harbor on some gray dawn with a borrowed jib and no forwarding name, and the water would have closed over, smooth and silent, and no one would ever have known to miss them.

Instead Cyrus lay still and let the drop fall, counting the beats of it against the hull's slow breathing, and thought about low tides. About how the sea gives things back when the conditions are right—not the drowned, never the drowned, but their names, their bells, their stenciled orders, their stubborn human proof. About how maybe people worked the same way. How a person could spend a lifetime underwater, invisible, intact, waiting—and how all it took was one extreme low tide, one season of pressure, one archivist with a folder and a grandmother's refusal, to drain the water away and stand revealed in the open air, blinking, exposed, alive.

The drip fell. Cyrus slept.

In the morning the storm would come, and the reef would close, and the Meridian Star would keep her secrets and her peace. But the *Anansi* would sail again on Monday—survey charter, six passengers, honest work at fair pay—and there was a berth aboard with a name chalked on the bulkhead beside it in Tam's careful handwriting, and the name was still there when Cyrus woke, and no tide in the world was going to wash it out.
