## 1. The Debt Collector Smiles

The Vane brothers arrived the way weather arrives in the basin—first a change in pressure, then the smell of something coming, then the thing itself, cheerful and total.

Ada Brightwater saw their Suburban pull into the lot of the Dusty Fork at eleven in the morning, sun glinting off chrome like a promise. She was under her truck with a wrench and a bad attitude, and she stayed there as long as she could stand it, which wasn't long. The Vanes had a way of finding you even when you weren't looking for them. Especially then.

"Miss Brightwater." Ray Vane crouched at the truck's flank, his shadow falling across her boots. He wore a linen shirt the color of cream and a smile that had never once reached his eyes. "You're getting grease on your only good shirt."

"I've got three good shirts."

"Not anymore." He said it lightly, like a joke, and it was not a joke. His brother Dell leaned against the tailgate, eating an apple he'd taken from Ada's own cooler without asking, because that was how the Vanes worked—they treated what was yours as theirs, on loan, pending interest.

Ada slid out from under the chassis and stood up slow, letting the wrench hang loose in her hand. Not a threat. Just a fact she wanted them to notice.

"You're early," she said.

"We're punctual," Ray said. "There's a difference, and it's worth money. Which brings us to the subject." He pulled a folded paper from his back pocket, unfolded it with two fingers like a man handling a snake he'd already charmed. "Forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve dollars. Principal, plus the carrying costs we discussed. Due in thirty days."

"The agreement said ninety."

"The agreement said ninety *if* the monsoon surveys came through. They didn't come through. You didn't bid the Caliente contract. You didn't take the pipeline job." Dell crunched his apple and swallowed. "We can't carry a business that won't work, Ada. It's nothing personal."

Everything about them was personal. That was the whole design. The Vanes didn't break legs out of anger; they broke them out of bookkeeping, and they did it smiling, and they made sure you understood, every step of the way, that the arithmetic was your fault.

"Thirty days," Ada said.

"Twenty-nine now. We started the clock when we parked." Ray tucked the paper back in his pocket and patted it once, fondly. "You're resourceful. You'll find it. You always do." He smiled wider, showing teeth. "Or you won't, and we'll find other ways to be useful to each other. Your truck. Your rigging gear. Your crew's contracts. Little things add up."

They drove away at 11:14, waving like neighbors.

Ada stood in the heat with the wrench in her hand and did the math she already knew by heart. The Brightwater Survey Company—two trucks, one aging drone rig, four people, and a reputation for going where the maps went blank—was worth maybe sixty thousand if she sold everything, including the trucks, including herself. Thirty days. Forty-one thousand six hundred and twelve.

She got back under the truck. The wrench didn't help. Nothing helped, but the underside of a vehicle was the one place in the world where nobody expected her to have answers.

She was still there when Cass Merrow's Land Cruiser rolled in at noon.

* * *

She heard the door before she saw the boots. She knew those boots—Danner, resoled twice, left foot dragging a half-inch from an old break—and knowing them made her angry before she'd said a word.

"Under there or dead?" Cass asked.

"Both. What do you want, Merrow?"

"A job. Triple rate."

Ada came out fast enough to crack her head on the frame, which she chose to blame on Cass. She sat up in the gravel, blinking, and there was her rival standing over her in the noon glare: tall, sun-cured, gray coming in at the temples like frost on wire. Ten years since they'd spoken. Ten years since the funeral neither of them had cried at because crying would have meant saying things out loud.

"Triple rate," Ada repeated.

"Cash on completion. Half up front." Cass crouched, forearms on knees, close enough that Ada could see the new scar splitting her eyebrow. "Guided survey run. Canyon country. Four days, five max."

"Which canyon?"

Cass held her gaze. That was the thing about Cass Merrow—she never flinched first, and she'd learned, somewhere along the line, to not flinch at all. "Devil's Throat."

The name landed in Ada's chest like a dropped anchor. Below her, the ground felt less solid than it had a moment ago, though of course that was impossible; ground doesn't care what names you say over it.

"No," Ada said.

"You haven't heard the number."

"It's not a number canyon."

Cass sat down in the dirt across from her, cross-legged, unhurried, the way she used to sit at campfires a decade ago when they were partners instead of rivals, before the Throat took Tomás and took whatever the two of them had been besides. "Three surveyors. Hydrology consultants, they say. I don't ask my clients their hobbies. They want a guided descent, full traverse, low route to the cave systems above the old dam site. You know that country better than anyone alive. Better than me. Better than Tomás knew it, God rest him."

"Don't."

"I'm not being cruel. I'm being honest, which is crueler but faster." Cass pulled an envelope from her jacket—not thick, but paper-wrapped cash has a certain density, a certain gravity—and set it on the gravel between them. "Half now. The rest on walkout. That's your forty-one thousand and change, Ada. Paid. Gone. The Vanes out of your life, the trucks stay yours, your crew keeps their contracts. One run. Four days."

Ada looked at the envelope and hated it with her whole body. Hated that it was exactly the right size. Hated that Cass knew the number—knew it to the dollar, which meant Cass had done her homework, which meant someone had talked, which meant the whole trap had been built with her measurements inside it.

"Who are these surveyors?" she asked.

"Consultants." Cass shrugged. "Water people. The state's obsessed with the aquifer feeds below the dam. Bureaucrats wanting maps."

"Bureaucrats pay per diem rates, not triple."

"Then they're rich hobbyists. Ada." Cass's voice dropped, went almost gentle, which was worse than any threat. "I watched you eat dust for ten years rather than come to me for work. I know what this costs you. But I also know what the Vanes cost, and I know you. You'll take the run. You'll hate yourself cleanly and completely, and you'll pay your debts, and in five years this will just be a story you tell over beer. That's how you survive out here. You turn the worst day into a story."

Tomás hadn't gotten to turn his worst day into anything. That was the part Cass left out, the part sitting between them on the gravel like a third person.

"My crew doesn't know that canyon," Ada said finally. "If I take this, they go in blind."

"They go in with you. Nobody alive knows it better."

"That's not—" Ada stopped. Swallowed. "That's not true anymore. Ten years is ten years. Water moves rock. The Throat I knew isn't down there anymore."

"Then you'll all learn it together." Cass stood, brushed off her jeans, and left the envelope where it lay. "Day after tomorrow, dawn, the north rim staging area. Bring your navigator—the good one, Reyes. I hear he reads water like scripture." She paused at the door of her Land Cruiser. "And Ada? Don't tell them where they're going until they're committed. You know why."

"I know why," Ada said, and the knowing sat in her mouth like copper.

Because Devil's Throat had a reputation. Because no crew on the Colorado Plateau would sign onto it, not since the accident, not since the inquiry that went nowhere and the report nobody filed and the silence the four of them had built around that week like a dam around a reservoir. Because the only way to get a crew down there was to lie to them, and the only person who'd ever asked her to do that was standing right here, holding out money with one hand and a shovel with the other.

* * *

That night she called a crew meeting at the shop, and she lied.

It wasn't hard. That was the shameful part. Jonah sat on the workbench with his charts spread across his knees, and Priya and Deke took the folding chairs, and Ada stood by the whiteboard with a marker she didn't use and told them about a private hydrology contract, canyon country, triple rate, half up front. She watched the relief move across their faces like cloud shadows—relief, because the Vanes had been circling all of them, everyone knew it, and a big payday meant survival.

"Which drainage?" Jonah asked. He always asked. He read water the way other men read faces, and he could feel a lie in a drainage the way he felt rain coming in his bad knee.

"Upper tributary system," Ada said. "North rim access."

He looked at her a beat too long. Jonah Reyes missed nothing, that was his gift and his curse, and she watched him decide—visibly, deliberately—not to press. Yet. He nodded and rolled up his charts, and she loved him for it and hated herself more.

After the others left, she sat alone in the shop with the envelope open on the bench, counting bills she couldn't look at without seeing Tomás's rope bag hanging on its hook, ten years dusty, too heavy to throw away and too heavy to touch.

One run, she told herself. Four days. In and out. People changed; canyons changed; the past was just geology, layered and buried and holding still if you didn't dig.

At midnight her phone buzzed. A message from Cass, no greeting:

*Weather service flagged a system off the Gulf. Early season. Could push north. Watch your barometer.*

Ada typed back: *Dry season doesn't start with storms.*

The reply came in eight seconds, which meant Cass had been holding it:

*Neither does a debt start with broken legs. Everything comes early when somebody wants it to.*

She sat with that until the screen went dark, and somewhere south, beyond the horizon, a storm she couldn't see yet began deciding which way to turn.
