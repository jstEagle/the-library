## 2. What the Water Keeps

Jonah Reyes had learned to read Ada Brightwater the way he read water: by what she didn't say. All morning, as the convoy rattled up the washboard road toward Devil's Throat, she'd been quiet in a way that had nothing to do with concentration. She checked her gauges twice. She answered questions with half-sentences. When Dez asked if they'd run this canyon before, she said *once*, and the word came out of her like a splinter.

He'd been her navigator for six years. He knew every version of her silence. This one was grief wearing a work shirt.

The Throat announced itself the way it always did—a seam of shadow splitting the mesa, so narrow at the rim that noon only visited for an hour. Jonah stood at the edge with his topo case open and felt the old vertigo climb his spine. The walls dropped four hundred feet in terraces of red and black, and somewhere down there, under the silt and the stone, was the place where Tomás Vidal had died ten years ago. Ada had never told him how. He'd stopped asking around year three.

"Rigging points are on my mark," Cass Merrow called out, all brisk competence and expensive gear. She'd brought three surveyors with soft hands and hard cases—Pelicans stacked like ammunition crates—and a confidence that sat wrong on her. Jonah remembered Cass from the old days, before whatever happened happened, back when she and Ada used to finish each other's route plans. Now they spoke to each other in the clipped dialect of people keeping score.

They descended through the morning, bolt lines humming, the canyon swallowing light by degrees. By midday they were walking the lower narrows, boots in ankle-deep water the color of weak tea, and the walls had closed to a corridor you could nearly touch both sides of. The air went cold and mineral. Somewhere ahead, water dripped in a rhythm too regular to be natural.

That was when Jonah noticed the surveyors weren't surveying.

Dez had flagged it first—he always noticed the wrong things fastest. "Hey, Cass. Where's your total station? Your GPS rover?" He gestured at the equipment. "You've got cameras. Four of them. And lights like a film crew."

Cass smiled the way the Vane brothers smiled. "Documentation requirements."

"For a hydrology survey," Ada said flatly.

"For a thorough one."

They rounded a bend where the canyon floor tilted downward into a flooded slot, and there it was: a cave mouth breathing cold air above the waterline, its threshold scarred with old drill marks and a rusted steel door hanging off one hinge. Stenciled on the rock beside it, half-eaten by decades of flash floods: **CALDERA ENGINEERING — SURVEY ACCESS 7 — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL**.

One of the soft-handed men already had a wetsuit on. Another was unsealing a dry tube with the reverence of a man opening a reliquary.

"Seven," Ada said quietly. "Access Seven." She said it like an address she'd been trying to forget.

Jonah watched her face do something complicated. "Ada. What is this?"

She didn't answer him. She walked past the surveyors into the cave mouth, headlamp beam sweeping across walls lined with conduit stubs and corroded shelving, and stopped at a steel cabinet bolted into living rock. The lock had been cut recently—bright metal in a dark place. Inside, wrapped in wax paper and plastic, were documents. Hundreds of them. Seismic reports. Core sample logs. A bound volume stamped in faded red ink across every page:

**SEALED — STATE OFFICE OF DAM SAFETY — DO NOT DUPLICATE**

"The Caldera Dam sits eleven miles downstream," Cass said behind them. She'd followed them in, and her voice had shed its briskness; now it was smooth as poured concrete. "Built sixty-one years ago on the strength of these reports. Except the reports lie. The bedrock under the dam has a shear zone running straight through it. The original engineers found it, documented it, and their bosses buried the findings and poured the concrete anyway."

One of the surveyors was photographing pages against a gray card, shutter clicking like a slow insect. Click. Turn. Click.

"So this is evidence," Dez said slowly. "Of what, exactly? Criminal fraud?"

"Of the fact that the flood-control project protecting two hundred thousand people downstream is sitting on a fault line its own builders knew about." Cass lifted one page toward Jonah's light. Core logs, dates, signatures crossed out and re-signed in different hands. "And that makes it worth money. To journalists. To lawyers. To the state." She paused, and the pause had teeth. "To Caldera Construction itself, which would pay a great deal to make sure no one else ever sees it."

The shutter clicks kept going. Jonah felt the temperature of the cave drop even though it couldn't have.

"You're selling it *back* to them," Ada said. "You're going to let them bury it again."

"I'm going to let the market decide," Cass said pleasantly. "Same as anyone. You came for triple rate, Ada. Don't stand in my cave acting surprised about who pays for things."

*My cave.* Something in Jonah's chest went hard and cold. He looked at Ada—not at the argument, at her—and saw her jaw set in the particular way it did when she was losing something she'd already decided not to fight for out loud.

Ten years ago. Tomás. Access Seven. It assembled itself in his mind without permission: they'd found something down here once, or been meant to, and someone had paid to keep it buried, and Tomás had been the cost. Ada hadn't taken this job despite the canyon. She'd taken it because the debt collectors left her nowhere else to stand.

"Ada," he said, low, just for her. "What happened here?"

Her eyes flicked to him, and for one second the whole decade showed in her face. Then the surveyor's camera flashed, and the moment was gone.

* * *

They made camp that evening on a gravel bench forty feet above the channel, tarps strung between boulders, the cave mouth visible upstream as a black notch in the blacker wall. The surveyors worked by generator light, photographing in shifts. Cass sat apart, cleaning lenses, radiating the calm of a woman who believed she had already won.

Jonah took the first watch because he didn't want to sleep next to his own thoughts. Around nine, Ada came and sat beside him on the cold rock, close enough that their shoulders touched, far enough that it could be an accident. Neither of them made it one.

"How long have you known what was down here?" he asked.

"A week after I signed." She turned her canteen in her hands. "Cass told me enough to make sure I wouldn't walk. Not everything. Just—the shape of it. I thought maybe I could get us in and out and never look inside the cabinet."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen the signatures." Her voice was barely above the sound of the creek. "Jonah, I knew men who poured that dam. My grandfather drove a mixer truck for Caldera for thirty years. He came home every night smelling like wet cement and never once said a word." She laughed, short and joyless. "Maybe he didn't know. Maybe that's the part that gets me. Whole towns built on a lie, and everyone downstream just—living their lives on top of it."

Below them, the channel murmured over its stones. Dry season. The gauge readings had been low for weeks. That was the whole reason Cass had scheduled the run now.

"Tomás found these documents," Jonah said. "Didn't he."

Ada was silent long enough that the silence was an answer. "We were doing a legitimate survey. His idea. He wanted to know why the seepage patterns in the lower narrows didn't match the published geology." Her thumb traced a scratch on the canteen. "He got into Access Seven. He got out with copies. And three days later his truck went off Kaibab Grade in perfect weather, and the copies went into the reservoir with him, and two men in nice jackets came to our shop and explained, very kindly, that some canyons are better left unmapped." She looked up at the thread of stars above the canyon rim. "I was twenty-six. I took the settlement. I told myself it was insurance money. I've been telling myself that for ten years."

Jonah reached over and covered her hand on the canteen. She let him.

"Cass knows all of that," he said. "And she hired you anyway. Brought you back to the exact spot."

"She needed a crew that could run the Throat. And she needed—" Ada exhaled. "I think she needed me to be the one who opens the door. So if anything goes wrong down here, it's my name on the permit. Mine, not hers."

Click. Turn. Click. The camera sounds drifted down from the cave, patient as dripping water.

Then the sounds stopped.

Jonah heard it before he understood it—a deep, sub-audible groan rolling up through the rock beneath them, felt in the sternum more than the ears. The gravel bench shivered. Loose stone clattered down the far wall. Upstream, the black notch of the cave mouth coughed a plume of dust, and somewhere in the dark beyond it, something massive settled with a grinding exhale.

Dez was on his feet. "That was a tremor. That came from *downstream*. From the dam."

They ran the fifty yards to the cave mouth, lights swinging. The news was written in fresh rubble: the exit chute they'd rappelled through that morning—the high route, the only other way out besides the narrows—had slumped. Forty tons of new talus sealed it like a cork. Dust still hung in the beams.

Behind them, faint but unmistakable, came another sound. From downstream. From the direction of the reservoir.

Water.

A lot of it, moving fast.
