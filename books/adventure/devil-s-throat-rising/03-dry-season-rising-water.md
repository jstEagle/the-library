## 3. Dry Season, Rising Water

The first drop hit the back of Ada's neck like a thrown stone.

She was standing at the canyon floor, checking the tie-downs on the survey crates, when the sky over the rim went the color of wet slate. Not a cloud building—a cloud *arriving*, shouldering over the mesa edge as if it had been waiting ten years for this exact hour. The dry season wasn't supposed to break for another three weeks. She'd built the whole schedule around that. They all had.

"Rain," Jonah said behind her. Just the word. He didn't need to say more. In Devil's Throat, rain wasn't weather. It was a countdown.

Ada looked up the slot canyon toward the exit they'd come down—the low route, the one with the ledges and the hand-line anchors her crew had set two days ago—and did the arithmetic she'd sworn off doing a decade ago. Six miles of narrows between them and open country. Flash flood in the Throat moved at twenty feet per second when it came down the tributaries. The water would reach them before they reached daylight.

"Cass!" she shouted up the talus slope.

Cass Merrow stood at the cave mouth forty feet above, silhouetted against the dark seam in the canyon wall where the old survey team had found the flooded gallery. The sealed records were already packed—waterproof tubes strapped to the surveyors' packs, everything cinched down tight and ready to run. Cass had been ready to run since yesterday. That was the thing Ada hadn't let herself see until now: you don't pack like that unless you know something's coming.

"We take the high route," Cass called down. "Northeast ledge. It's marked from the '09 survey. We'll be on the rim before this turns serious."

"The high route hasn't been climbed in fifteen years."

"It's been *rained on* even less." Cass turned to her surveyors. "Move. Single file. Nobody stops."

And that was when Ada understood—not the route, not the storm, but the shape of the whole trap. She'd spent last night turning it over: how the Vanes had waved her off at the trailhead, cheerful as ever, telling her no cell coverage out here so don't bother calling if anything goes sideways. How Denny Vane had smiled his leg-breaking smile and said, *Ten days, Ada. In and out. Don't get ambitious.* How the firm's name had surfaced in Cass's paperwork—the same construction conglomerate that had poured the dam, the same one whose falsified safety reports were sitting in waterproof tubes forty feet above her head.

The Vanes hadn't lent her money. The Vanes had *bought* her. Bought her reputation, bought her crew's trust, bought the one guide alive who could get people into Devil's Throat and—more importantly—who could be counted on to keep everyone there long enough for the monsoon to do the rest. A dead crew in a flash flood was an accident. A dead rival with the evidence still strapped to her body was a tragedy. The firm would send flowers. The Vanes would send flowers. Everyone would be very sorry, and the dam would stand on its lies for another fifty years.

"They paid them," Ada said. Jonah had come up beside her, and she said it quietly, just for him. "The Vanes. The firm hired them to bury us with the job."

Jonah looked at her, then up at the sky, then at the six-mile throat of rock that was about to become a pipe full of moving water. His face did what it always did—went calm and precise, the navigator reading a current nobody else felt yet.

"Then we're not getting out the way we came in," he said.

* * *

The rain arrived in earnest five minutes later, and it didn't fall so much as *land*—fat, violent drops hammering the sandstone, running together in threads, threads becoming rills, rills finding the grooves carved by a million years of exactly this. The sound filled the canyon like applause for something terrible.

Ada got her crew moving. Priya and Okafor, the two young techs who'd signed on for triple rate and a line on their résumés; Marcus, her rope man, who'd been in the Throat ten years ago and had gone gray around the mouth when he saw the cave mouth but hadn't asked questions because Ada told him not to. She owed him the truth now. She gave it to him in four sentences while they clipped into the traverse line above the main channel.

"So we're bait," Marcus said.

"You're crew," Ada said. "You're the only thing I'm not willing to spend."

Above them, Cass's party was already strung along the northeast ledge, tiny figures against the black streak of the old route, the record tubes gleaming dully under their rain covers. One of the surveyors slipped, swore, recovered. Cass didn't slow. Cass never slowed. That had always been the difference between them—Ada measured, Cass committed, and the world rewarded commitment right up until the moment it buried it.

The water came down the tributary canyon as a sound before it came as water: a deep, grinding roar, like the canyon clearing its throat. Then the front edge of it rounded the bend below them—brown, thick, carrying boulders the size of trucks, the flood surge standing nearly eight feet tall and climbing as the narrows squeezed it.

"High ground! Now!" Ada hauled Priya up the last pitch to a shelf above the main channel and pressed them all flat against the wall while the flood went through beneath them, a brown river where an hour ago there had been dry gravel and her truck tracks. Her truck. On the far side. On the wrong side. Everything they'd driven in on was now downstream, or would be shortly.

The high route it was, then. Cass's route. Which meant catching Cass.

* * *

They gained the northeast ledge an hour later, soaked, hands shredded from the wet sandstone, the rain still coming down in ropes. The ledge was eighteen inches wide in places, tilted outward, slick as glass. Below it, sixty feet down, the new river ran white and furious through the narrows.

They caught the surveyors at the point where the ledge narrowed to a crawl. Two of them were across. The third—a heavyset man named Brandt—was frozen halfway, arms wrapped around a knob of rock, the record tube strapped across his back like a shell.

"I can't," he was saying. "I can't feel my hands. I can't—"

"Give me the tube and go back to the wide spot," Cass ordered. She was anchored at the far side, reaching back, rain streaming off her jaw. "Brandt. The tube. Now."

That was the moment. Ada saw it whole, the way you see a rapid before you run it. Cass wanted the records across that gap more than she wanted Brandt across it. If the man fell, the firm lost a surveyor and kept its documents, and Cass collected her fee either way. Ada watched her rival's face do the calculation—watched it happen, quick and cold, behind the rain—

—and hated her, and understood her completely, because Ada had done the same math about the Vanes' money for thirty days.

"No," Ada said, and stepped onto the ledge.

She crossed to Brandt in six moves she didn't remember making, clipped his harness to hers, and put her mouth against his ear. "We go together. Look at me, not the water." Behind her, Jonah had the crew anchored and was paying out rope. She felt the line come tight. Good. Trusting Jonah with your life was the easiest decision left in the world.

They crossed. Brandt made it on legs that weren't his own, collapsed on the far side, and lay there shaking. Ada unclipped him, took the record tube off his back, and stood up facing Cass Merrow with the evidence of a decade of fraud in her hands and the flood screaming below them both.

"Give it here," Cass said. No smile now. Rain and fury.

"You're selling it back to them."

"I'm selling it to whoever pays. That's called a market, Ada. You wouldn't know—you've been too busy being *honest* at people." Cass advanced along the ledge, sure-footed even now, even here. "You think leaking it makes you clean? You think Ruth Okonkwo's story pays the Vanes? They'll break every bone you have and then some, and the dam will still be standing, because the state will bury the report and the story both. I'm offering you a cut. Last offer. Enough to walk away free."

The water rose. Ada heard it before she saw it—a change in the roar below, a deeper note—and then the surge came around the bend and hit the ledge's underside and the whole canyon shuddered, dust sifting down, a crack opening somewhere overhead with a sound like a rifle.

The ledge under Cass's feet moved.

Not much. An inch. But Cass staggered, arms windmilling, one boot sliding off the wet lip—and Ada dropped the tube, lunged, and caught her wrist as the ledge crumbled away beneath her rival's heels.

For one long second they hung there, Ada flat on her belly on wet stone, Cass's full weight pulling her shoulder out of its socket, the flood sixty feet down hungry and brown. Cass looked up at her, and there was something in her face that wasn't gratitude yet—something older, more surprised, the look of a woman meeting a version of events she hadn't priced in.

"Why," Cass shouted over the water. "*Why*—"

"Because I'm not you," Ada yelled back, and hauled.

It took everything—Jonah's anchor line, Marcus hauling her belt, Brandt sobbing and grabbing handfuls of Cass's jacket. They got her over the lip as the ledge she'd been standing on tore loose and went into the flood, swallowed without a ripple worth the name.

Ada lay on her back in the rain, arm screaming, lungs full of thunder, and laughed until it hurt. Cass crawled to her knees beside her, dripping, wrecked, staring.

"The tube," Ada said. "Where's the tube."

Cass pointed. It had landed wedged in a crack ten feet back, intact, dry inside its shell. Of course it had. Things survived in this canyon. People mostly didn't.

Then the crack overhead opened wider, and the far end of the ledge—the direction of the rim, the high route, everything—dropped away into the flood with a sound like the world closing a door.

Behind them, the only way left was back down. Into the cave. Into the dark, and whatever the water was doing to it.

Jonah appeared at her elbow, rope coiled, face pale and steady. "Tell me you have an idea," he said.

Ada picked up the tube and looked into the Throat's black throat. "I have a map," she said. "And ten years of reasons to be wrong."
