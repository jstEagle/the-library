## 4. Miles, Not Years

Jonah counted the photographs twice in the truck's cab, then a third time, because his hands wouldn't stop shaking and he didn't trust the first two counts. Forty-one images of sealed engineering reports, water-stained and warped, the ink bleeding but legible—load calculations halved on paper, inspection dates forged, a signature from an engineer who had died two years before the dam poured its first concrete.

Ada sat behind the wheel with her boots off, her socks steaming on the dashboard, staring at nothing through a windshield still beaded with canyon rain. Her hands were wrapped where the rope had burned them at the cave mouth, and she hadn't said a word since they'd climbed out of the Throat with the flood roaring at their heels like something cheated.

Behind them, Cass Merrow was in a hospital in Red Mesa with three cracked ribs and a lung full of canyon water, alive because Ada had gone back for her when every sane instinct said to run. The surveyors had scattered into the high country like quail. And somewhere between the canyon rim and the county line, Ada had made her decision—not out loud, not with any ceremony, just a quiet turn of the wheel toward a town that wasn't home and a phone number she'd torn out of a trade journal six months ago and kept folded in her wallet like a prayer she wasn't sure she believed in.

Ruth Okonkwo answered on the second ring.

* * *

They met her in a diner outside Bitter Springs, the kind of place where the coffee tasted like the pot had been brewing since the last administration and nobody asked questions because everybody had answers they were hiding. Ruth was smaller than Jonah expected, gray-shot and sharp-eyed, with the particular stillness of a person who had learned that listening was a weapon.

She spread the photographs across the table between the ketchup and the napkin dispenser, and Jonah watched her face do what faces did when a story stopped being a story and became a reckoning.

"Where did you get these?"

"Devil's Throat," Ada said. "There's a cave above the old survey line. The firm sealed it in '09 and told everyone it was unstable rock. It was unstable paperwork."

Ruth picked up one photograph, held it to the light. "You understand what happens if I run this. The state will call it forgery. The firm will call it theft. Whoever gave these to me will be named, eventually, because documents like this have a way of finding their provenance." She looked up. "I can protect the source for a while. Not forever."

"I'm not asking you to," Ada said.

Jonah felt the words land in his chest like a dropped anchor. He'd spent the drive rehearsing arguments—the Vanes, the debt, the thirty days that had shrunk to nine, the cheerful menace of men who smiled while they broke your fingers. He'd built whole speeches about survival, about paying what you owed so you could live to owe again. And here was Ada, giving it all away before he'd even opened his mouth.

"The Vane brothers hold paper on everything you own," he said, because someone had to say it. "The truck. The house. Your mother's—"

"I know what they hold." Ada's voice was level, tired, certain. "And I know who paid them to make sure we didn't come out of that canyon. You don't pay a debt to men like that, Jonah. You just teach them the price of you." She turned to Ruth. "Run it. All of it. Names, dates, the falsified reports, the buyout of my debts—everything. Make it loud enough that breaking my legs costs more than leaving them attached."

Ruth studied her for a long moment. Then she gathered the photographs with the care of someone handling evidence, or dynamite. "Nine days," she said. "That's how long until I can verify enough to print. Stay reachable. Stay alive."

* * *

The nine days were the longest of Jonah's life, and he'd once spent four of them pinned under a rolled truck in a wash waiting for a storm to pass.

They holed up in a motel on the edge of Red Mesa with the curtains drawn and the television tuned to local news, watching the world react to a story that hadn't broken yet. Ada slept in shifts, light as a bird, waking at every car door in the parking lot. Twice, a sedan with tinted windows rolled past slow, and twice it rolled on. On the fourth day, a man in a good suit came to the motel office and asked the clerk questions, and Jonah watched him through the window and memorized his walk, his watch, the way he stood like a man used to being obeyed.

On the fifth day, the story broke.

It broke everywhere at once—Ruth had spent those five days feeding pieces to three other outlets, and when it landed, it landed like the flood itself. FALSIFIED REPORTS UNDERLIED STATE'S CROWN JEWEL DAM, the headlines said. ENGINEER'S FORGERIES DATED YEARS AFTER HIS DEATH. The firm's stock fell off a cliff by lunchtime. By evening there were subpoenas. By the third morning after, the governor—who had cut the ribbon on that dam with golden scissors and a speech about progress—announced an independent commission with the grim face of a man reading his own obituary.

The Vane brothers, Jonah learned from a bartender who talked too much, had left town the same night. No violence, no parting threats. Just a shuttered office and a note taped to the door of their lending shop that read CLOSED FOR RENOVATIONS in the same cheerful lettering they'd used for ten years of smiling extortion. Men like the Vanes survived by arithmetic. When the cost of a debt exceeded its value, they walked away from it—and now, thanks to forty-one photographs, Ada Brightwater's debt was radioactive. Nobody would buy it. Nobody would collect it. It existed only as a liability to anyone foolish enough to touch it.

"They're gone," Jonah told her, standing in the motel doorway with the news still glowing on his phone. "Really gone."

Ada was sitting on the bed with her mother's compass in her lap—the one thing she'd carried out of the canyon besides the photographs, though she'd given the photographs away. She turned the compass over in her fingers without opening it.

"Ten years ago I swore off that canyon," she said. "Swore it after we lost Dev, after we couldn't even say how. I measured everything after that in miles. Miles from the Throat. Miles from the water. Miles from anything that could take another piece of me." She looked up, and for the first time since the flood, Jonah saw her eyes clear all the way down. "You know what I figured out down there? Miles are just years wearing a disguise. Same cowardice. Same running."

"Ada—"

"I'm done running distance." She closed her fist around the compass. "If something takes me, let it take me going forward."

* * *

They drove out of Red Mesa at dawn, two days later, in a truck with no liens on it and a gas tank paid for with the last cash either of them had.

The highway ran north along the plateau, and somewhere behind them, hidden in the folds of the earth, Devil's Throat was filling with its dry-season storm water, doing what it had always done—keeping what the water keeps, letting go of what it doesn't. Ada drove with the window down and her bandaged hand resting on the seat between them, palm up, an offering or an invitation, Jonah couldn't tell and didn't need to.

"Where to?" he asked, when the town had shrunk to a smudge in the mirror.

"Nowhere yet." She glanced over, and there was something new in her face—not peace exactly, but the end of a war she'd been fighting alone for a decade. "We've got a whole year ahead of us. First one I've ever had that isn't already owed to somebody."

Jonah put his hand in hers. Her grip was strong, rope-burned, alive.

Ahead, the road unspooled through country that belonged to no one—not the firm, not the Vanes, not the past—and for the first time in ten years, neither of them measured it in miles. They measured it in mornings, in seasons, in the slow accumulation of days that owed nothing to anyone.

The Throat fell away behind them, and the year began.
