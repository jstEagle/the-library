## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The supercell came up out of the west like something with an opinion.

Dominic Dunmore watched it from the shoulder of Route 9, engine idling, radar app glowing on the cracked screen of her phone, and felt the old pull in her chest—the one that had nothing to do with money and everything to do with the sky deciding to be honest for once. A storm never pretended to be your friend. A storm told you exactly what it was going to take and then took it.

If only people worked that way.

She'd been chasing this cell for six hours across the Harrow Flats, a client's contract folded soft as cloth in her jacket pocket: Halvard Agri-Cooperative, data package on hail core structure, four hundred credits on delivery. Four hundred credits was two weeks of diesel and half a month of what she owed on the truck. She'd driven through three counties of nothing—wheat stubble, fence wire, grain elevators standing like lonely teeth—and parked under a sky gone the color of a bruise healing wrong.

The cell was beautiful. She'd give it that. A striated updraft base rotating slow and deliberate, like it had all the time in the world, which, for the next forty minutes, it did. She got the probe out, got the anemometer mast up, got ninety seconds of clean wind-field data before the RFD gust front hit her at sixty knots and tried to peel the truck's doors off like tin on a sardine can.

She crouched behind the wheel well and laughed. She couldn't help it. There was something in a storm that made the rest of her life look simple by comparison.

Then the rain came sideways, the light went green-black, and she packed up and drove east ahead of the hook echo while hail the size of her thumb hammered the roof and she thought about how much a new windshield cost, which was more than she had, which was the whole story of her life lately told in glass.

* * *

Halvard Agri-Cooperative's field office was a double-wide trailer outside Merrow's Bend with a sign that had lost its second L years ago. Dominic climbed the steps at 6:40 p.m., mud to her knees, hair still wet, data drive in a ziplock bag because she'd learned that lesson the expensive way.

The man at the desk was named Pell. He had the particular expression of someone who'd been rehearsing an apology all afternoon and was relieved to finally perform it.

"Dunmore. Right. The—the weather thing." He shuffled papers he didn't need. "Look, there's been a delay on our end. Budget cycle. You know how it is."

"I know how it is," Dominic said. "I know how it is every quarter, Pell. This is the third time."

"And I appreciate your patience—"

"Patience isn't what I have." She set the ziplock bag on his desk with a small wet slap. "That's nine hours of mesonet-grade data off a supercell that killed two hundred acres of somebody's sorghum today. It's worth what we agreed. I'd like to be paid."

Pell looked at the bag like it might bite him. "Give me until Friday."

"Friday." She said the word flat, tasting it. "You said Friday last time."

"End of Friday."

Dominic stood there a moment, rainwater dripping off her sleeve onto his linoleum, and considered the geometry of the situation: him behind the desk, her in front of it, both of them knowing exactly who held the leverage and neither of them saying it. She could make noise. Noise got you noticed. Being noticed was the one thing she could not afford.

So she picked the bag back up. "Then I keep the data. When Halvard pays, Halvard gets its hail cores."

"You can't—that's breach of—"

"It's called collateral, Pell. Look it up. It's in the same chapter as *paying people*."

She walked out into the last gray light of the storm's aftermath feeling, briefly, like a person with a spine. The feeling lasted eleven minutes, which was how long it took her to do the math in the truck: no payment meant no diesel money, no diesel money meant no work, no work meant the ledger got heavier, and the ledger was not kept by anyone who accepted excuses.

She sat in the cab with her forehead against the wheel.

Her father used to say debt was just weather too—it rolled in, it rolled out, you hunkered down and waited it out. Her father had also died owing nothing to anybody, or so she'd believed, back when believing things was still allowed. Eleven years now. She kept his compass on the dash, brass casing worn to shine where his thumb had rubbed it ten thousand times, needle settling always toward north-northwest, toward the escarpment, toward whatever country he'd loved enough to die in.

*Don't chase the money,* he'd told her once, when she was small enough to sit on his knee in this same truck, before it was this truck. *Chase the storm. Money's just weather that lies.*

She turned the key. The engine caught on the second try, which was one try worse than last month.

* * *

The drive back to Merrow's Bend proper took twenty minutes along a road the hail had littered white, like someone had spilled the sky's dishware. Dominic's plan for the evening was modest and achievable: park behind the boarding house, eat whatever Mrs. Calloway had left, sleep twelve hours, figure out tomorrow at tomorrow.

The plan survived until she opened the truck door.

There was a man sitting in her passenger seat.

He sat the way people sit when they've been invited—not slouched, not tense, hands folded on his knee, hat on his thigh, a leather ledger book balanced on his lap like a hymnal. The door hadn't been forced. The lock hadn't even been scratched. He was simply *there*, occupying space in her truck the way damp occupies a wall.

"Evening, Ms. Dunmore." His voice was warm as fresh bread. "Rough day for windshields out there. I heard the hail on my way into town. Sounded like applause."

Dominic did not move. She stood with one boot on the running board and one on the gravel and ran the calculation every child of the flats learns early: distance to the door, distance to the ditch, whether the tire iron under the seat was worth reaching for.

"Truck's locked," she said.

"It is now." He smiled. He had a good smile, symmetrical, practiced, the kind of smile that had never once been surprised by anything. "Ives. Vellum Syndicate, regional collections. We've spoken before—well, I've spoken. You tend to let answering machines do the conversational heavy lifting, which I respect. Boundaries are healthy."

"How did you get in my truck?"

"Ms. Dunmore." He pressed a hand to his chest, mock-wounded. "A collector has his methods, and a lady has her mysteries, and neither of us needs to spoil the evening with specifics. May I?" He gestured at the driver's seat without waiting for permission, because the question itself was the performance; the sitting was already decided.

She got in. She didn't see what choice she had, and he knew she didn't, and they both knew he knew, and the whole architecture of the moment was built on that shared knowledge, load-bearing and invisible.

Up close, Ives smelled faintly of cedar and ink. His suit was good but not too good—a collector dressed too well made people run; dressed right, he looked like a neighbor. His eyes were pale and patient and missed nothing, cataloguing the mud on her boots, the hail dents on the roof, the compass on the dash, with the mild professional interest of a man pricing a room just by standing in it.

"Your father's?" he asked, nodding at the compass.

"That's not in the ledger."

"No," Ives agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. Sentimental items rarely are—they don't liquidate. Which is a shame, honestly. Some of the most valuable things people own are worthless on paper." He opened the ledger across both their laps, uninvited again, and turned to a page tabbed in green. "Now. Let's talk about your Tuesday."

"I don't have anything today. You can see that. You're sitting in all of it."

"Oh, I'm aware. This is—" he tapped the page, "—a conversation about trajectory, not balance. The Syndicate thinks in trajectories. Your principal stands at two thousand one hundred and forty credits, accrued across, let's see, equipment financing, a medical advance after the Tarnish Creek incident—you were very lucky there, by the way, broken ribs heal free if you're careless enough—and rolling interest at terms you signed and I'm sure remember vividly."

"I remember the pen they handed me didn't work until the third signature."

"A quirk of the ink," Ives said, entirely sincere. "Happens constantly. Humidity." He turned another page. "Here's the thing, though. Here's why I drove all the way out to beautiful Merrow's Bend instead of sending one of the boys. Your trajectory is bad. Not catastrophic—catastrophic is actually useful, catastrophic means assets—but bad. You earn irregularly. You hide your earnings—which, again, respect—and you have no collateral except a truck worth less than its lien and a data archive nobody's bought in eight months. On paper, you're a rounding error."

"Thank you."

"But." He held up one finger. "You have a skill set. Meteorological fieldwork, remote terrain navigation, salvage recovery experience from your time with the county crews. And the Syndicate has recently developed a need for exactly that skill set, in exactly the region you already live in, at a rate of pay that would clear your ledger in a single engagement." He closed the ledger and smiled at her, warm as a stove in a cold room. "I'm here to offer you optimism, Ms. Dunmore. It's what I do. I'm the optimist."

Dominic looked out the windshield at the drowned street, the gutters running loud, the streetlamp flickering over water the color of pewter. Somewhere a dog was losing its mind about the thunder. Everything ordinary. Everything fine, if you didn't count the man in her passenger seat holding her future in a book with a green tab.

"What kind of job?"

"Salvage. Government survey equipment, abandoned site, Karst Escarpment. Inventory, recover, deliver to a depot we'll specify. Straightforward. Well-marked. The Syndicate acquired the recovery rights through channels that were entirely legal, and I will swear to that on any book you like." He said it lightly, the way honest men say honest things, which was somehow worse than if he'd lied. "Nine days' work, maybe ten. Weather window's tight—monsoon season's coming up the coast—but you'd know that better than me. You being the expert."

"And if I say no?"

Ives spread his hands, regretful, reasonable. "Then nothing tonight. Nothing this week. The Syndicate is patient; patience is cheaper than force, and force attracts attention, and attention is bad for everyone's business. But the interest doesn't pause, Ms. Dunmore. Interest never pauses. And eventually the trajectory does what trajectories do." He paused, letting the silence do a little work. "I'd hate that. Genuinely. I read your file and I thought, here's a woman who pays her debts, she just needs one fair wind. And I am, as established, an optimist."

She wanted to hit him. That was the remarkable thing about Ives—she'd known collectors before, men who sweated and men who sneered, and she'd handled both. But this one offered you the knife handle-first and thanked you for the pleasure, and some animal part of her brain kept screaming that the warmth was the trap, that the smile was the whole mechanism, gears disguised as grace.

Instead she said, "I'll think about it."

"Of course." He produced a card from inside his coat—cream stock, embossed, absurdly elegant for a man sitting in a truck that smelled of wet dog and diesel—and laid it on the dash next to her father's compass. "Think fast, though. The weather window, as they say." He opened his door, stepped down into the puddles, settled his hat, and then leaned back in, one hand on the frame, and gave her the full wattage of the smile.

"For what it's worth—and it's worth very little—I think you're going to say yes. People always think they're choosing. They're really just reading the terms of their own situation and calling it a decision. No shame in it. It's the human condition." He tapped the roof twice, gently, almost affectionate. "Sleep well, Ms. Dunmore. Congratulations on the storm. The data sounded valuable."

And he walked away up the flooded street without a ripple, like a man who'd never once in his life gotten his boots wet.

* * *

Dominic sat in the truck for a long time after he was gone.

The rain thinned to a drizzle. The dog gave up. Mrs. Calloway's kitchen light came on across the yard, gold and square in the dark, and she could smell onions frying, and none of it touched her. She was somewhere else, doing arithmetic with numbers that weren't money.

Two thousand one hundred and forty credits. Nine days' work. One fair wind.

She picked up the compass. Brass, warm from the dash, lighter than it had any right to be. Her father's thumb had worn a valley into the casing and her thumb fit the same valley, which was either comfort or cruelty depending on the hour. The needle swung, wobbled, settled north-northwest. Toward the escarpment. Toward the job. Toward whatever the Syndicate wanted found so badly it would forgive a debt to find it.

Nothing forgives, her father's voice said in her head, dry as August. Forgiveness is just a price tag wearing a nicer shirt.

"Then I'll read the price tag," she said out loud, to the empty cab, to the compass, to the man eleven years dead who had left her nothing but a needle that pointed at trouble. "And I'll decide what it costs."

The needle didn't answer. Needles never did. That was the thing she'd loved about storms first and instruments second: they only ever told you the truth about direction. What you found at the end of the bearing was your own affair.

She put the compass back on the dash, face-up, needle steady, and went in to eat onions and not sleep.

In the morning, she'd call Ives. In the morning, she'd say yes, and hate herself efficiently, and pack the truck.

But that night, lying awake under Mrs. Calloway's thin ceiling while the last of the storm ticked away over the flats, Dominic Dunmore made herself one promise, quiet and absolute, the kind you don't write down because written things get read:

Whatever was waiting for her up on the Karst Escarpment, she would go in invisible, come out invisible, and touch nothing she wasn't paid to touch.

It was a good policy. She'd kept it for years.

She just didn't know yet that the mountain had other terms.
