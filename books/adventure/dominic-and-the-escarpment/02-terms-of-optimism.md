## 2. Terms of Optimism

The collector was still sitting in her truck when she came back down off the flats.

Dominic saw him from two hundred yards out, a pale shape behind the wheel of her own cab, and her first instinct—the good one, the one that had kept her solvent and alive for six years—was to keep walking. Turn around, cut through the stockyards, come back after dark, sleep in the cab of some stranger's rig. She'd done it before. Invisibility was a discipline, not a trait. You practiced it the way a pilot practices instrument work, until it held even when the weather turned.

But her truck was her house, her office, her only appreciable asset, and the man behind the wheel was already waving.

That was the worst part. Not the waiting, not the trespass, not the fact that he'd obviously defeated a lock she'd personally upgraded after the last visit. The wave. Cheerful. Two-handed, like she was a daughter coming home from a long trip instead of a debtor walking into a collection.

She crossed the gravel anyway. That was the other thing about discipline: sometimes it just meant knowing when you'd already lost.

"You're back early," the man said through the open window as she approached. "The cell said the cell said the supercell would hold together another ninety minutes. I told them you'd read the sky better than the model." He said it like a compliment. He said everything like a compliment. "Ives. Vellum Syndicate, regional accounts. We spoke on the phone—well, I spoke. You have a gift for silence."

"I've had practice."

"With your ledger, I'd imagine." He unfolded himself from the driver's seat—tall, narrow, a suit the color of wet sand that probably cost more than her radar rig—and stood aside from her own door with a little flourish, a doorman presenting a lobby. "May I? I know how chasers feel about their cabs."

"You're already in it."

"Was in it. Now I'm asking, which is different." He smiled. He had one of those smiles that had been maintained so long it had gone past sincerity into something structural, load-bearing. "Dominic Dunmore. May I call you Dominic? The file says Ms. Dunmore, but files are so cold, and I'm not a cold man."

"You're a collector."

"I'm an *optimist*." He said it the way other men said *veteran*. "That's the official title, actually. Debt Recovery Optimist, Grade Four. The Syndicate believes every account is a story that hasn't found its good ending yet. My job is to help it find one." He gestured at the cab. "Shall we? It's starting to spit."

It was starting to spit. The first fat drops of the dying storm's outer band ticked against the roof, and the wind had that turned-over feel it got when a cell collapsed and left its cold pool behind. Dominic looked at the sky for three full seconds—habit, arithmetic, the part of her brain that never stopped pricing weather—and then got in her own truck, in the passenger seat, because a man from the Vellum Syndicate was standing there holding the door.

He settled into the driver's side like he'd been fitted for it. Set a leather folio on his knees. The folio was the color of good coffee and had a brass clasp, and everything about it said *this is the most expensive object within fifty miles*.

"Your client didn't pay," Ives said.

"Your client didn't pay," she corrected. "The agricultural insurance pool. They hired me to verify hail damage across four hundred square kilometers. I verified it. They're disputing the invoice."

"Mm. They're disputing it because they're insolvent, and insolvent men dispute invoices the way drowning men grab driftwood. You'll never see that money. You know that." He opened the folio. "Which brings us to your ledger."

"I know my ledger."

"Everyone knows their ledger. Few people *read* it." He turned the folio toward her, and there it was, her whole life in columns: the rig loan, the truck loan, the radar array on a lease-to-own that had quietly become a lease-to-nowhere, the two years of interest that compounded during her father's illness and funeral because she'd stopped opening mail, the consolidation that wasn't, the fee structure that read like a ransom note written by an accountant. Numbers she could recite in her sleep. Seeing them in someone else's handwriting, in someone else's neat, patient ink, was different. It was like seeing your own house from the air after a flood.

"Total exposure," Ives said, tapping one line with a fountain pen, "including the recent service charges, sits at forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve marks. Against that, your verified assets: one truck, aging. One radar unit, leased. One reputation, which the Syndicate values—" he paused, pen hovering, and gave her the smile again, "—modestly. You've been very careful to be worth nothing on paper, Dominic. It's admirable. It's also, I'll be honest, beginning to irritate people."

"People above your grade."

"People above everyone's grade." He said it lightly, the way you'd mention weather. "The Syndicate is patient. Patience is our entire business model. But patience has a maintenance cost, and lately the regional office has been asking what maintenance on the Dunmore account is buying them. A woman who chases storms for clients who don't pay, who owns nothing, who owes everything, and who—" he turned a page, delicately, like turning the page of a love letter, "—has never once missed a payment. Not one. Six years. Through the illness, through the funeral, through the consolidation that we both know was designed to fail. You've paid every month, on time, in full."

"Because I pay my debts."

"Because you pay your debts." He nodded slowly, and for one strange moment the smile dropped and something almost like respect looked out of his face, and it was worse than the smile. "Do you know how rare that is? Do you know what the default rate is on accounts like yours? Seventy percent within four years. You're a statistical miracle, Dominic Dunmore. And the regional office looks at a statistical miracle and asks: why is she still *poor*?"

The rain had thickened against the roof. Dominic watched it run down the windshield in ropes and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a hook with her own name on it.

"So," Ives said, and the smile came back, and the folio turned to a fresh page. "The Syndicate has an opportunity. We prefer to call it a forgiveness job, though the legal department makes us call it a contingent salvage engagement with debt-offset provisions. You can see why we prefer forgiveness."

"I can see why."

"Three weeks ago, a government survey team abandoned a camp on the Karst Escarpment. Upstream country. The Karst River, the gorge country, the high benchlands—the maps mark most of it restricted, which for our purposes means *uninsured*, which for our purposes means *cheap*." He said *cheap* with relish. "The team left behind a considerable quantity of equipment. Instruments, generators, survey gear, a full meteorological package. Government spec. The Syndicate has salvage rights through a subsidiary of a subsidiary, and we would like you to go up and bring it down."

Dominic looked at him. "You have collectors with trucks."

"Collectors with trucks collect debts. This is different. This requires someone who can read weather, drive bad ground, and—forgive me—be invisible. The escarpment in monsoon season is no place for a man in a good suit." He spread his hands, presenting himself. "And the salvage value offsets your balance. Not all of it. But the instruments alone, at recovery rates, could clear a third. Perhaps half, if the meteorological package is intact. And if you complete the engagement to standard, the Syndicate is prepared to discuss restructuring the remainder. Better terms. A real path, Dominic. An *optimistic* path."

"What's the catch."

Ives laughed. It was a genuine laugh, warm, delighted, and it was the most frightening sound she'd heard all year. "Everyone asks about the catch. There's no catch. There are *terms*, and terms are just catches wearing nice clothes. Shall I walk you through them?"

He walked her through them.

The salvage was to be inventoried on-site, tagged with Syndicate seals, and transported to a depot he named, a rail junction two days south of the escarpment. She'd carry a manifest transmitter—non-negotiable, insurance requirement, he said, though his eyes said *and so we always know where you are*. The recovery window was three weeks, because after that the monsoon made the high country impassable and the Syndicate's underwriters got nervous. Any equipment lost, damaged, or unaccounted for came off her offset at replacement value plus thirty percent. And the survey camp itself—

"What happened to them?" she asked. "The survey team."

Ives's pen stopped moving. Just for a second. Just long enough.

"That," he said, "is an excellent question, and the answer is that the Syndicate doesn't know, and the Syndicate's not knowing is precisely why the salvage is available to a contractor of your discretion rather than to, say, a crew of our own. The government has been unhelpful. The families have been litigious. There are men in this region who would very much like to know what happened up there, and the Syndicate has concluded that the equipment matters more than the mystery. You're not being asked to solve anything, Dominic. You're being asked to carry things down a mountain. Can you do that?"

She could do that. That was the humiliating truth of it—she could do that, easily, and the numbers on the folio said she *should* do that, and every instinct she owned was standing at the back of her skull screaming that a debt collector did not offer a third of a debt off for carrying boxes unless the boxes were bait.

"Let me see the contract," she said.

"Of course." He passed it over—already printed, already prepared, her name already in the header in the same patient ink as the ledger. Of course. He'd written it before he ever sat in her truck. Maybe before he ever left the regional office. Maybe the day the survey team vanished, three weeks ago, whenever that had been, whenever *they* had decided.

She read it the way she read sky. Not for what it showed—for what it was about to do. The offset clause had a floor: no matter how much she recovered, her balance could never drop below nine thousand marks. Nine thousand. A number designed to keep her on the hook forever, a permanent anchor, except—she read it again—the floor waived upon "successful completion of all engagement deliverables." All deliverables. Including, clause eleven, "recovery of any documentation of survey origin, to be surrendered intact to the Syndicate prior to contractor departure from the recovery zone."

Documentation. She thought of a government camp, instruments still running, meals half-eaten—no, she didn't know that yet, she was guessing, but she'd seen abandoned camps before and abandoned camps always had paper—and a clause that said every scrap of it went to Vellum before she came down the mountain.

"Why do you want their paperwork?"

"Salvage value," Ives said smoothly. "Government documents are surprisingly collectible. There are archivists. It's a whole market."

"There's a whole market."

"There's a market for everything, Dominic. That's not cynicism. That's weather." He watched her read, and he was patient, and his patience had a maintenance cost too, and she could feel it accruing. "You're looking for the trap. I respect that. So let me say the plain thing, the thing I'd want said to me if our positions were reversed." He folded his hands on the folio. "If you don't sign, the account moves to enforcement grade within the month. Enforcement grade means the truck. Then the rig. Then the wage attachment, which for a contractor means every client you have learns, in a letter with a Vellum letterhead, exactly what you owe and to whom. Your policy of invisibility ends. Not because we're cruel—because it's arithmetic. And then, a year from now, you'll sign something much worse than this, because the alternative will be much worse than a truck."

The rain hammered. Somewhere over the flats, the collapsed storm was feeding its cold air into the evening, and the sky out the windshield had gone the color of wet slate, and Dominic sat in the passenger seat of her own truck doing the arithmetic that had no good answer.

Forty-one thousand, or nine thousand forever, or nothing and a wage attachment and a name on every client's desk. Against: three weeks on the Karst Escarpment in the teeth of monsoon season, hauling government gear down bad ground for a Syndicate that had already printed her name on the contract, with a clause about paperwork she didn't like and a manifest transmitter so they could watch her the whole way up.

And one other thing. She didn't say it. She barely let herself think it. But the Karst Escarpment was high country, and her father had worked high country his whole life—hydrology, gauging stations, river surveys, the kind of work that ended with a man's name on a memorial plaque and a closed file—and the escarpment was the last place he'd ever filed a report from, six years ago, two months before the accident that killed him. Coincidence. The world was full of them. She'd built a whole life on the assumption that they were just coincidences.

"Clause eleven," she said. "Documentation surrendered intact. Define intact."

"Unaltered, complete, and surrendered before departure from the recovery zone."

"And if I find documentation that isn't survey origin? Personal effects. Letters. A journal someone kept."

Ives's smile did something very small and very quick, a flicker like heat lightning, there and gone, and then it was the load-bearing smile again, warm as a lamp. "Then that would be personal property, and the Syndicate respects personal property deeply. It's the foundation of everything we do." He produced the pen. "Shall we?"

She looked at the signature line. She looked at the rain. She did the cornered arithmetic one last time and found, as always, that every road out of it ran through the same door.

She signed.

"Wonderful." Ives blotted the signature with a little cloth he produced from his breast pocket, tender as a man handling a relic, and slid the contract back into the folio. "You've made a good decision. I want you to know I mean that. People think collectors enjoy this. I *hate* this part. I love the next part." He passed her a card—cream stock, brass-embossed, a phone number and nothing else—and a second document, the manifest transmitter, a box the size of a cigarette pack with a lanyard. "Wear it from the moment you leave the lowlands. Check in every forty-eight hours. The depot foreman's name is on the back of the card. And Dominic—" he paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and the rain came in past him and spotted the folio, and he didn't seem to mind, "—one piece of professional advice, optimist to optimist. Whatever you find up there, however strange it looks, bring it down anyway. Curiosity is the one debt the Syndicate can't forgive."

He tipped an invisible hat, stepped out into the rain, and walked away down the gravel with his hands in his pockets, unhurried, a man with nowhere in the world he wasn't welcome.

Dominic sat in her truck for a long time after the taillights—no, he'd walked; after his pale suit had gone gray and then gone—sat in her truck for a long time, engine off, rain on the roof, the signed contract's ghost still warm under her hand.

Then she took her father's compass off the dash, the brass one with the dented rim she'd never once needed and never once left behind, and turned it over in her fingers, and looked north, toward the escarpment, where the storm she'd been chasing all day was dying over country the maps marked forbidden.

"Curiosity," she said to the empty cab, and started the engine.
