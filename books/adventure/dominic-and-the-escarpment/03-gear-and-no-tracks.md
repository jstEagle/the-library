## 3. Gear and No Tracks

The trail gave out two miles below the escarpment, which was the trail's way of telling me the government had never intended anyone to follow it.

I parked the truck where the ruts drowned in fern and hiked the rest with a daypack, the compass in my jacket pocket, and Ives's manifest folded into quarters like something I didn't want touching my skin for longer than necessary. The manifest listed what I was supposed to recover: six tents, two instrument cases, a satellite uplink, assorted field gear. Salvage value, Ives had called it, tapping the paper with one finger. Modest. Routine.

Nothing about the Karst Escarpment had ever been routine. The maps showed contour lines stacked so tight they bled into each other, a wall of limestone and cloud forest rising two thousand feet out of the river lowlands, cut through with sinkholes and caves and water that went where it pleased. Half the old survey sheets of the region were stamped RESTRICTED in faded red ink, and half of those were wrong anyway, because the people who drew them had never climbed past the treeline to check.

The survey team had. Eight names on the manifest's header page. A departure date eleven weeks ago. No return date logged.

I told myself it was weather. Up here it was always weather—monsoon season coming on, rivers jumping their banks, a chopper unable to fly extraction. People got stranded on the escarpment every year. Most of them walked out eventually, hungry and humbled and alive.

Most of them left tracks.

* * *

I smelled the camp before I saw it. Woodsmoke gone cold and sour, and under it something sweeter and worse—the chemical tang of batteries left too long in the damp, lithium cells weeping into the soil.

Then the trees opened and there it was, tucked into a shelf of flat ground below a limestone overhang: six dome tents in government gray, guy lines still taut, rain flies still rigged. A cook tarp strung between two trunks. A fire ring with a kettle sitting on the cold stones. And beyond it, on a folding table under the overhang, an array of instruments that made me stop walking entirely.

Because they were running.

A data logger blinked its green heartbeat at me from inside a weatherproof case, cable snaking out to a mast rigged against the rock. A barometer's needle trembled with the pressure drop I'd been feeling in my teeth since morning. The solar panels feeding the whole setup were angled correctly for this latitude, this season, kept clear of leaf-fall by someone who wasn't here anymore—or by no one at all, if the system was automated enough to run itself. Government gear could do that. Government gear was built on the assumption that the people assigned to it might vanish, and the equipment should carry on regardless.

I stood at the edge of camp for a full minute before I let myself cross into it. Old habit. You don't walk into a scene until you've read it from outside, whether the scene is a storm cell or a crime or just a stranger's kitchen. You watch the edges first.

The edges said nobody had panicked.

Six sleeping bags rolled or unrolled according to temperament—one of them a neat military roll, five of them the loose knots of people who expected to climb back in tonight. Boots lined up under the tent flaps. A card game abandoned mid-hand on a crate, someone's queen of hearts face-up in the mud where it had slid off the pile. A shirt drying on a line, stiff now, colonized by a fine green mold that takes about three weeks to get established. Three weeks. The math matched the missing radio calls.

On the cook table sat four bowls of something long since liquefied, spoons still standing in them. Four. Not eight.

I crouched and looked closer without touching. Two of the bowls had been pushed away, finished or refused. Two looked interrupted—a meal stopped halfway through, the way you stop when somebody says your name in a particular tone.

So: eight people, four bowls, one moment. Half the team eating, half not. Whatever happened, happened fast, and happened to all of them at once, and nobody came back for their boots.

That was the detail that put ice down my spine, more than the bowls, more than the mold. Nobody comes back for boots. You can leave a card game, leave a meal, even leave a tent—but the escarpment is cold at night even in monsoon season, and the ground is wet, and eight trained field people do not walk off barefoot into karst country full of cutting limestone and things that live in holes. Unless they left together, escorted, in a hurry, by someone who didn't care about their feet.

Or unless they went somewhere they knew they'd be back from in a minute.

I straightened up and made myself say it out loud, because saying things out loud takes their power down a notch. "Okay," I told the empty camp. "Where'd everybody go?"

The camp declined to answer. The barometer needle ticked. Somewhere above the overhang, water dripped off the limestone with a patience I envied.

* * *

I worked the camp the way Ives would have wanted, because Ives was the reason I was here and pretending otherwise was a luxury. Tents first—they'd break down small and carry heavy but carryable, and hauling them out meant I could tell myself the trip hadn't been wasted yet. I dropped each one, folded it badly on purpose, and cached them under the overhang with a tarp over the pile. If I was going to haul salvage out of these hills, I'd do it on my schedule, after I understood what I was standing in. Ives could bill me for the delay. Ives billed me for breathing.

The instrument cases were next, and that's where the camp started talking to me properly.

Field teams label everything—it's drilled into them—and this team had labeled everything twice. Case numbers in marker, contents lists taped inside lids, and then, on top of the official labels, a second set of markings in grease pencil. Codes. Little clusters of letters and numbers that meant nothing to me except that they were consistent, deliberate, and recent; the grease pencil stubs were worn to nubs in a tin on the table.

Two of the instrument cases were empty. Not stripped—empty, cleanly, their foam inserts still molded around the shapes of whatever had been lifted out. Foam holds the ghost of a shape for years. I pressed my thumb into one of the hollows: a cylinder, maybe eight inches long, and beside it a rectangular recess the size of a thick book.

Someone had taken instruments out of their cases carefully, recently, and carried them away. In a camp where everyone else had apparently evaporated out of their boots.

And then I found the gap in the inventory, and the camp stopped being eerie and became something else.

The manifest in my pocket listed, among the recovery items, item fourteen: *river gauge assembly, acoustic, w/ mounting hardware.* Item fifteen: *sample case, source series, sealed, 24 units.*

No gauge at the camp. I checked twice, then a third time, walking the perimeter, looking in the fire pit, under the tarps, in the latrine trench. Nothing that heavy, nothing with that silhouette. And no sample case either—no case at all matching the dimensions stenciled on the manifest, no rack of little sealed vials, no shipping crate with SOURCE SERIES stenciled on the side.

Everything the Syndicate wanted was here, rotting politely in the damp. Everything the survey team had actually come up the mountain to use was gone.

You don't misplace a river gauge. It's the size of a suitcase and it anchors to bedrock with bolts. You don't misplace twenty-four sealed samples of anything, least of all samples from a source—which meant samples taken at the headwaters, at whatever the river was before it became the river. Somebody had packed up the mission's entire purpose and walked it somewhere, while the mission's furniture stayed behind to keep the lights on.

Instruments running. Purpose gone. People gone.

I sat down on the crate with the abandoned card game and dealt the dead hand out of habit, queen of hearts to the mud, and thought about my father, which I had been trying very hard not to do since the tree line.

He'd worked water his whole life. Gauges, channels, flood plains—the boring heroic science of keeping rivers honest. He taught me to read a stream the way other fathers teach kids to read faces: where it slows, where it lies, where the smooth water is lying hardest. He died—or stopped, or was stopped, the paperwork said died and paperwork is usually a rumor with a stamp—eleven days before this team's departure date. I'd done that arithmetic on the drive up and hated the answer and done it again anyway, as if the numbers might get embarrassed and change.

Eleven days. Long enough to brief a team. Long enough to point them at a mountain.

I took out the compass. My father's compass, brass-cased, the lid dented where he'd dropped it off a bridge once and laughed about it for a year. It didn't point north anymore—not reliably, not up here, not with this much iron in the karst—but I opened it anyway, the way you touch a rail for luck, and the needle swung lazy circles and settled pointing nowhere useful.

"Fine," I said to it. "Be useless. Everyone else has."

But the needle's uselessness gave me an idea, and the idea had teeth. If the compass couldn't find north, the instruments probably couldn't either—not the magnetic ones, not down-slope of all this limestone. Which meant the survey team had needed something better than magnetism to navigate this terrain. Which meant their notes, their charts, their route plans were worth more than the tents I was supposed to haul. Which meant I should stop doing Ives's job and start doing mine.

* * *

The command tent was the biggest of the six and the only one with a table in it, and the table was where the team had lived its paperwork life, and the paperwork life was a massacre.

Charts everywhere, weighted down with stones. Notebooks swollen with damp, ink feathering. And torn paper—torn deliberately, methodically, shreds of map and log sheet scattered across the table and floor like the aftermath of a very tidy tantrum. Someone had gone through the documentation and ripped out specific things. Not everything. Specific things.

I gathered shreds for an hour, kneeling in the damp, fitting pieces by feel the way I used to fit puzzle pieces with my father on the floor of a house that isn't mine anymore. Most of it was garbage—supply requisitions, a duty roster, somebody's complaint about the coffee. But the pattern of the tearing told its own story. Every shred that mentioned elevations above a certain contour line had been destroyed. Every fragment referencing a place-name I didn't recognize—handwritten, always handwritten, never printed—had been torn out with almost surgical care. The printed maps were intact. The annotations were gone.

Somebody had sanitized this camp of exactly one subject, and the subject was wherever the river starts.

By late afternoon the light under the overhang had gone the color of weak tea, and I'd filled two notebook pages with my own inventory of absences: no gauge, no samples, no annotated charts, no boots gone, no tracks. Because that was the thing I kept circling back to, the thing the dread kept sharpening itself on. I'd hiked in on soft ground most of the way, and my own prints followed me like a confession. Eight people leaving this camp on foot would have churned that ground into porridge. There was one trail in—mine—and one fainter trail leading out along the riverbank, heading upstream, and it was narrow and single-file and it ended, forty yards from camp, at the water's edge.

It just ended. As if eight people had walked into the river and the river had agreed to keep the secret.

I stood at that spot while the light died, looking at the last footprint—a boot heel, size ten or so, pressed deep like the person had turned or braced—and at the black water sliding past it, and I felt the fear change shape. It had been creeping dread all afternoon, the animal kind, the kind that wants you to turn around. What replaced it was worse and better at once. Fascination. The specific fascination of a storm chaser staring at a sky that's doing something the textbooks say it can't.

Something had happened here that had a mechanism. Storms have mechanisms. Vanishings have mechanisms. Nothing simply stops, not meals, not footprints, not rivers. Something had *carried* them, or *moved* them, or *taken* them—and had done it quietly enough that four men kept eating through the middle of it.

And whatever did the taking had also, or someone had also, made sure the gauge and the samples went upstream instead of down. Away from the Syndicate. Away from the world. Toward the source.

I walked back to camp in the dark I didn't need a flashlight for yet, and I made a decision that Ives would hate and my father, wherever he was, would understand. I wasn't hauling tents down the mountain tomorrow. I was following that single-file trail upstream to where it ended, and then I was following the river, because the river knew where everything went, rivers always know.

At the edge of camp I paused at the data logger, its green light pulsing in the dark like a slow heart. On impulse I flipped open the access panel and pulled the memory card, a fingernail-sized chip holding however many weeks of readings the machine had drunk from the air. Evidence, maybe. Leverage, definitely. Ives wanted salvage; I'd give him tents and call it generous.

The logger's screen scrolled its last entries as the panel closed. Pressure falling. Humidity climbing. And a third channel I hadn't noticed before, unlabeled, its trace flatlining along zero for weeks—

—except for eleven pulses in the last two nights. Regular as breathing. Rising.

I clicked my flashlight on and read it three times, and the dread and the fascination shook hands and merged into something with forward momentum.

Upstream, then. Tonight's camp, tomorrow's river.

Whatever was breathing up there, I was done letting it breathe alone.
