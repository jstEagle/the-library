## 4. The Logbook in Her Father's Hand

The rain started an hour after I found the camp, which felt less like weather and more like commentary.

I'd spent the afternoon doing what Ives had actually paid me to do—cataloguing salvage. It was good work for keeping the mind busy and the hands honest: inventory sheets, serial numbers, photographs of everything worth photographing. The Syndicate wanted a manifest before I hauled anything down the mountain, because the Syndicate wanted a manifest of everything, always. Debt was just their word for it. What they really collected was information about what people owned, and then the people.

So I catalogued. Six dome tents, all staked properly, all dry inside. A galley fly with a propane stove that still lit on the second try. Four crates of core samples, sealed and labeled in a neat surveyor's hand. Two theodolites wrapped in oiled canvas. A shortwave radio with its antenna guyed like someone expected to be listening for a while.

And no one. No bodies, no graves, no note pinned under a mess tin. Just forty sets of gear arranged with the tidy optimism of people who believed they were coming back from wherever they'd gone.

I ate cold noodles standing under the galley fly and watched the rain stitch silver lines down the escarpment face, and I told myself the story Ives would want told: government team got recalled, left in a hurry, paperwork will sort it out. Governments did that. People walked away from camps all the time. There was probably a convoy of trucks somewhere over the ridge, and by Thursday this whole place would have a fence around it and a man with a clipboard.

Then I went into the command tent to check the instruments, because the barometer was my business even if nothing else here was, and that was where I found the logbook.

* * *

It wasn't hidden. That was the first strange thing. It sat in a waterproof map case on the folding table beside the radio, spine toward the door, like a book someone meant to pick back up after lunch. The case was scuffed at the corners—the kind of wear you get from years of pockets, not weeks of sitting on a table.

I should have photographed it and logged it and left it alone. Salvage rules. Chain of custody. Everything in that camp belonged to somebody's file cabinet now, and the somebody was Vellum.

I opened it anyway. You can call it a lot of things—curiosity, trespass, the particular stupidity of a woman who has never once left a locked box unopened—but I opened it.

Field log, Karst Escarpment Hydrographic Survey, Season Three. The early pages were standard stuff. Gauge readings. Gravel bar surveys. Notes on turbidity that read like poetry written by someone who didn't know they were writing it: *River running tea-colored after the bend storm; silt load up 40%; fish behavior odd, holding deep.* The handwriting was small and slanted-left, efficient, a hand trained to write in wind.

I flipped forward through weeks of entries. The camp's rhythm came off the page—morning gauge runs, equipment maintenance, a birthday celebrated with canned peaches and a complaint about the peaches. Ordinary. Alive. And then, maybe two-thirds of the way in, the entries changed. Shorter. Coded, almost. References to "the source" that stopped being geological and started being careful.

*Source readings confirmed again. H wants them run a third time. Told him three times is superstition, not science. He says with this client, superstition is due diligence.*

H. A client. A hydrographic survey didn't usually have a client in the sense that needed capital letters.

I turned another page and the bottom fell out of my chest.

There, in the margin beside an entry about flow rates—a different hand. Loops where the surveyor's hand was all angles. A backward-leaning script I had last seen on grocery lists and birthday cards and the margins of my mother's recipes, a hand I would have known blindfolded, underwater, dead drunk, dead.

My father's handwriting.

I stood very still under the fly with the rain coming down and the tent breathing around me, and I let myself think the reasonable thoughts. Coincidence. Similar penmanship. Someone on the team who'd known him, imitated his shorthand, some surveyor's private joke—

But it wasn't imitation. I knew the difference the way you know your own front door in the dark. My father wrote his eights without lifting the pen. He crossed his t's low and hard, like he was angry at every t individually. He dotted his i's slightly right of center, always, a habit he'd picked up drafting charts in a rocking boat and never lost. The marginal notes had all of it. Every habit. Every tilt.

Beside the entry about the third source reading, in his hand: *Don't argue with H. He's right to be paranoid. If the numbers are real, we're not doing science anymore. We're holding something.*

I sat down on a supply crate because my legs had made an executive decision without consulting me.

My father had been dead for six years. Officially, publicly, with a funeral and a closed casket and a river to blame—the same river, as it happened, though I hadn't let myself think about that until this exact moment. Flash flood on the upper Karst, they said. Body never recovered, which happens, which is a thing rivers do. I'd been twenty-three and already drowning in the first layer of the debt he'd left behind, and I had grieved him the way you grieve a man the water took: completely, permanently, with a stone in a cemetery that said his name above nothing at all.

And here was his handwriting, dated eleven months ago.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that the rain moved from the tent fly to somewhere inside my own head, a white noise that made the rest of the world go quiet and far away.

Then I did what I do when the ground moves under me. I worked.

I went through the logbook page by page, forensically, the way I'd read a sky. Dates first. His annotations ran from early spring to late autumn of last year—eleven months of marginalia, growing denser as the season went on, as if whatever was happening at the source kept pulling him deeper into the margins of other men's records. He never signed. He never wrote more than a few lines at a time. But the voice was unmistakable, and not just the letterforms. It was the jokes. Dry, sideways, aimed at authority. Beside a requisition form for additional sample containers: *Ask for double. They'll cut it in half and feel generous.*

That was him. That was so exactly him that laughing would have been possible if my throat hadn't been full of broken glass.

The content of the notes built a shape, slowly, the way pressure builds ahead of a squall line. Early entries were technical—corrections to flow calculations, questions about bedrock porosity that showed a mind worrying a bone. Then the tone shifted. Beside an entry noting that a government liaison had visited camp for two days: *He wasn't here about the survey. He was here about who else knows. Count who he talked to. Count who stopped talking after.*

Later: *The maps going downstream are wrong on purpose. Good. Wrong maps protect wrong people from finding this.*

And then, near the end, in a hand pressed harder into the paper, the ink nearly tearing through:

*They know I'm the leak. Doesn't matter. The source stays up here or nobody gets it. Tell D—*

The sentence stopped. Not faded, not smudged. Stopped, mid-word, the pen lifted, the thought abandoned—or interrupted—and never taken up again on any page that followed. The remaining entries were pure survey routine, meticulous, calm, the record of professionals continuing to do their jobs while something enormous circled their camp. The last entry was a gauge reading dated the day before the camp emptied. Routine. Neat. *All stations nominal.*

Nominal. The word people use right before the end of the world, because the alternative words won't fit in a logbook.

Tell D. Tell Dominic. He'd been writing to me the whole time, in the only way left to him, and he'd been cut off mid-sentence.

I put the logbook down on the table with exaggerated care, walked out into the rain, stood in it until I was soaked through, and screamed at the escarpment until my voice gave out. The mountain took it the way mountains take everything—without echo, without comment. Somewhere below me the river kept doing whatever it was doing, indifferent, patient, running its secret errands.

Then I went back inside, because grief is a luxury and anger is fuel, and I've never in my life had enough of the first to waste time on it when the second could move me forward.

* * *

Here's what the anger sorted out, once I let it work.

One: my father hadn't died on this river six years ago. He had died to this river—there's a difference, and the difference was a decision. He'd faked it, or someone had faked it for him, and either way the funeral had been a instrument of concealment, and I had stood at it holding a folded flag of lies I hadn't known was folded.

Two: he had come here afterward. To this escarpment, to this survey, to within—what, four hundred kilometers of the town where I was dodging collectors? Eleven months of him alive and writing, while I ate discount noodles and calculated interest. He had been reachable. Or unreachable by design. Both possibilities were unbearable, and I held them both, one in each hand, like stones.

Three: the Vellum Syndicate held my debt. The Vellum Syndicate had sent me here. Which meant either the world's most elaborate coincidence, or the Syndicate had some thread of connection to this survey—to the client with the capital letter, to the project my father had died protecting—and they had sent the daughter of the man who'd sabotaged them up the mountain to fetch their property, without telling her, because telling her was a risk and using her was free.

Ives's smile floated up in my memory, cheerful as a knife. *A simple salvage job,* he'd said. *Nothing up there but old equipment and old paperwork.*

Old paperwork. He'd practically handed me the logbook. Had he? Was that the design—let the stray daughter sniff out her father's ghost and follow it straight to the source, tracking a claim the Syndicate couldn't find on its own? Or was I building conspiracy out of coincidence because grief does that, because the mind would rather be hunted than orphaned twice?

I didn't know. That was the worst part. I sat in the command tent with the rain hammering the fly and I genuinely did not know whether I was the hunter or the hound, and both answers required me to keep moving upstream, which was convenient for whoever had designed this and necessary for me regardless.

Four—and this was the one I kept circling back to, the one that glowed like heat lightning behind everything else:

*Don't bring it down the mountain.*

His warning was there in the margins, repeated in variations across the last month of entries. Don't certify the source readings for the client. Don't transmit the data on the radio. Don't let the samples travel. Once, starkly, beside an entry about packing procedures: *Whatever leaves this escarpment in a crate gets used as a weapon. Keep it up here where it's only water.*

Only water. As if water could ever be only water. As if the man who taught me to read clouds hadn't also taught me that everything falls, eventually, and what matters is where.

He hadn't wanted the source claimed. Not by the client, not by the government, not by anyone. And the survey team—the forty professionals whose half-eaten meals I'd inventoried, whose tents I'd slept beside—they had apparently agreed with him, and then they had vanished without a footprint between them, and the gear had stayed behind like a promise everyone intended to keep.

I closed the logbook and held it against my chest for a moment, the way you hold something that might be a weapon or might be a wound.

Then I did the thing Ives would have fired me for, if Ives had been watching, and which I did anyway, deliberately, as the first act of a war I hadn't officially declared: I took it out of the map case, wrapped it in my spare shirt, and buried it in the bottom of my own pack, under the storm chaser's kit, under the clothes, in the place where I kept the things that were mine.

Not salvage. Not evidence. Inheritance.

* * *

The rain eased near dusk. I cooked a proper meal from the camp's stores—rice and the last of someone's dried fish, eaten off someone's tin plate, an apology owed to forty missing strangers—and I laid out my options on the table of the galley fly like tools.

Option one: finish the salvage manifest, haul the crates down, collect Ives's forgiveness, and never think about any of this again. Except I'd already stolen the logbook, so option one was now a lie I'd have to maintain under the eyes of the best liars in the region. Also, my father had drawn a line at the base of this mountain and the Syndicate was standing on the wrong side of it, and I had discovered, somewhere between the scream and the rice, that I was standing on the other side, next to him.

Option two: leave the gear, take the boat I'd need to arrange somehow, and follow the river up. Toward the source. Toward whatever my father had spent his death protecting and his life after death guarding. Without a manifest, without permission, with a Syndicate collector expecting me back down in nine days carrying their claim for them.

Every practical instinct I owned voted for option one. Practical instinct had kept me fed and invisible for six years, and I honored it, and I overruled it.

Because here was the thing the anger had cracked open underneath the grief, the thing I couldn't unsee once seen: I had spent six years paying a debt I inherited, on terms I never signed, to people who smiled while they measured everything I had. And my father—the man whose death had created that debt—had spent his last eleven months trying to make sure that nobody, anywhere, ever got to own the one thing on this mountain too valuable to own.

We were the same, him and me. We'd just been fighting the war from opposite ends of it, and nobody had told either of us.

I banked the stove. I checked the barometer—falling, steady, a long trough coming, monsoon season gathering itself offshore like a debt coming due. Nine days of working weather, maybe less. Bram's ferry schedule, if the gossip at the crossing held, ran day after tomorrow.

I looked upriver, into the blue-black folds of the escarpment where the water came from, where my father's handwriting said *don't*, where forty people had vanished leaving their boots behind.

"Don't bring it down the mountain," I said aloud, to the dark, to him. "Fine. Then I'm coming up."

Somewhere below me, faint under the river sound, I heard an engine—far off, on the track I'd come in by, running without lights.

Somebody was driving fast toward a camp that was supposed to be empty.
