## 5. Strays at the Crossing

The crossing at Ferrow's Bend was supposed to take twenty minutes. Pay the ferryman, float the truck across on the barge, drive on, salvage the gear, get paid, disappear again. That was the whole shape of my week.

The river had other ideas, and so, it turned out, did three people I'd never met.

I smelled the trouble before I saw it—woodsmoke and wet canvas and something frying in too much old oil. The official ferry was gone. In its place, beached on the near bank like a dead whale, sat a river boat with its engine housing torn open and a government confiscation notice zip-tied to the rail, fluttering in the wind like a flag of surrender.

A man was sitting on the bow in his socks, eating beans out of a can with a folding spoon, watching me come down the track with the expression of someone who had been expecting me for days and was mildly disappointed by the wait.

"You're not the ferryman," I said through the window.

"Ferryman's gone." He didn't stop chewing. "Took the last crossing two days ago when the water started doing things water shouldn't do."

"Doing what things?"

"Stopping." He pointed upstream with the spoon. "Then going. Then stopping again. Like a man deciding whether to say something."

Great. The river was broken, the Syndicate wanted me to fetch equipment from a camp full of ghosts, and now the only way across was a confiscated boat owned by a man who talked like weather.

"I need to cross," I said.

"Everybody needs to cross." He set down the can. "Nobody crosses free."

Here we go. "How much?"

"Not money." He stood, and up close he was built like something the river had carved—broad, patient, worn smooth in places. His eyes were the pale gray of floodwater. "You're Dunmore. The storm chaser. Salvage job on the escarpment, right? Vellum paper in your pocket?"

Every muscle in my back went tight. "Who told you that?"

"The Syndicate tells everyone everything. It's how they work. They don't need to chase you if everyone you meet already knows what you owe." He shrugged. "Name's Bram. This is my boat they confiscated, which means technically it belongs to a government office that doesn't exist anymore, which means technically it belongs to nobody, which means I'm the closest thing to an owner it has."

"And you want?"

"A ride upstream. They took my license when they took the boat. Only way to get either back is to be useful to somebody with a reason to go where I'm going." He looked at me the way a locksmith looks at a lock. "You've got a reason. I've got hands that know this river better than the river does. That's the trade."

"I work alone," I said.

"So did I." He picked up his boots. "Look how that's going."

* * *

I should have driven away. I want that on the record, somewhere, in case anyone ever asks. There was still a road option—a long one, a bad one, a day of switchbacks and washouts—but it existed, and I could have taken it, alone, invisible, the way I'd survived eleven years since my father died.

Instead I stood there doing arithmetic. Nine days of river before monsoon landfall, per the syndicate's own timetable. A truck that couldn't climb the escarpment tracks. And a pilot who knew the water the way I knew storms—which is to say, intimately, resentfully, and better than anyone sane should.

"One condition," I said. "You don't ask what I'm salvaging."

"Deal. You don't ask why my license got pulled."

"Deal."

He spat into the river, which I chose to interpret as a signature.

We spent the afternoon getting the boat running—he worked with a wrench the way other men pray—and I'll admit the sound of that engine catching, rough then smoothing out, did something warm and unwelcome to a part of me I'd kept locked since the funeral. It sounded like company. I drowned the feeling in fuel checks.

That's when the second stray found us.

She came down the far bank at dusk, wading the shallows with her pack held over her head, a tall woman in a survey team jacket with the insignia half torn off. She moved like someone who'd been moving for days without stopping—efficient, joyless, running on something darker than calories. She saw the boat, saw us, and stopped dead in the water like she'd hit glass.

"Don't shoot," she said. "I'm worth more as a witness."

"Nobody's shooting," Bram said. "But we are curious."

"Sefa," she said, coming ashore. "Hydrographic surveyor. Third class." She said her own rank like an apology. "I was with the Karst survey team."

Bram went very still. So did I, though I hid it better, or thought I did.

"The team that vanished," Bram said.

"The team that didn't vanish." She dropped her pack and sat on it, suddenly out of strength. "The team that was made to disappear. There's a difference, and the difference is why I ran."

I crouched by my truck and pretended to check tire pressure while she talked, because looking directly at her felt dangerous. She described lights in the gorge at night. A sound like the whole river inhaling. Orders from their lead to erase every record, wipe every instrument, and then—her voice flattened here—the lead himself going quiet mid-sentence on the radio, and the camp going wrong in a way she couldn't name but had been fleeing from for six days.

"Why run downstream?" I asked, not turning around. "Everyone's downstream."

"Everyone's downstream," she agreed, "but not everyone's going *up*. You are. I can read your route off your gear from here." She nodded at the survey-grade cases strapped to my rack. "And whatever you're chasing up that river, I'm the only person alive who can tell you what you're actually looking at when you find it."

"I don't need a guide."

"No," she said quietly. "You need a witness. There's a difference, and you'll figure it out around day four."

I hated her a little for that. Mostly because she was right, and we both knew it, and neither of us said so.

* * *

We crossed at first light, because Bram refused to run the bend in the dark—"the river lies differently at night"—and because Sefa insisted the far bank was safer than sleeping where we'd been seen. Three strangers loading a confiscated boat with my salvage gear, none of us asking the questions that mattered, all of us pretending this was a business arrangement.

It was almost working until the tarp moved.

I'd stacked the last of the crates aft, under a tarp, and when Bram gunned us off the shallows the tarp shifted, and a hand came out of it. Small. Filthy. Attached to a boy of maybe twelve with a shaved head growing out unevenly and eyes that had already done the math on all three of us and liked none of the answers.

"What," I said.

"He was on the dock," Bram said, unbothered, steering with one knee. "Since Sefa came aboard. I figured he was yours."

"I figured he was yours."

"I assumed he was attached to the woman," Sefa said. "There was a lot happening."

The boy sat up in the pile of crates, brushed off a coil of rope he'd apparently been using as a pillow, and looked at me with the flat patience of a cat that has decided your house is his house now.

"Name's Tuck," he said. "Turn the boat around and I'll jump. I swim better than any of you."

"That's not a negotiation tactic, that's a hostage situation," I said.

"It's a fact about me." He shrugged. "Village downriver. Lowbank. You're going past the places the company men have been buying out, and I want to see their faces when they find out somebody got past them."

"Past them to where?"

"Wherever you're going that they don't want anybody to go." He said it simply, the way kids say true things. "Everybody on this river is scared of them. You're the first people I've seen who aren't. Well. Who pretend less badly."

I opened my mouth to order him off the boat at the next landing, and Bram cut the engine.

We drifted. The current took us gently sideways, and the escarpment rose ahead of us out of the morning haze, green and gray and enormous, hiding whatever it hides. The river made its small sounds against the hull. Nobody spoke.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about staying invisible: it works fine until it doesn't, and then you look around and realize you've been alone for eleven years and the aloneness has gotten so comfortable it feels like a personality instead of a wound.

"Next landing," I said finally. "I put him ashore at the next landing."

"Sure," Bram said, in the tone of a man agreeing to weather.

"Sure," Sefa said, in the tone of a woman agreeing with Bram.

Tuck just smiled, small and crooked, and helped himself to the beans.

* * *

We made the next landing by midafternoon, and I want to state clearly that I tried. I pulled in at a mudflat with a fish-drying rack and a dog and exactly nothing else, and I told Tuck to get off, and he got off, and he stood on the bank with his hands in his pockets while I idled there like an idiot waiting for him to walk away.

He didn't walk away.

"There's a company man comes through Lowbank every month," he said. "Measures the water. Writes numbers in a book. My gran says the numbers are about us—about whether our village is worth flooding or worth keeping. Nobody knows which." He kicked at the mud. "You're going to wherever those numbers come from. So either I go with you and find out, or I stay here and wait to find out. Same information. One of them takes longer."

"That's not—"

"My gran says a debt you don't understand is the worst kind." He looked at me straight. "You've got debts. Everybody can see it. Mine's just mine."

The dog barked once, decisively, as if seconding the motion.

I looked at Bram. Bram looked at the sky. Sefa was suddenly very interested in the depth sounder.

"Back of the boat," I said. "You touch nothing, you steer nothing, and when this stops being safe, you do what I say the first time I say it."

"Yes," Tuck said, climbing aboard with the speed of a boy who knows better than to give adults time to reconsider.

"And that's not a yes to anything else later."

"It's a yes to everything else later," he agreed cheerfully, and settled into his spot among the crates like he'd been born there.

* * *

We ran the river through the late afternoon, four of us on a boat built for cargo and silence. Bram piloted like a man reading aloud from a book only he could see—little corrections, little murmurs, his hands never still. Sefa spread charts on the deck plates and marked them with a grease pencil, frowning at the river as if it had misspelled something. Tuck watched everything, asked questions about everything, and touched nothing, mostly.

And I sat amidships with my father's compass in my pocket, thumb on the cracked brass, and let myself feel the thing I'd been refusing to feel since Ferrow's Bend.

It wasn't safety. Safety was a word for people whose creditors didn't smile. But it was something adjacent to safety—four sets of eyes instead of one, four reasons for the Syndicate to hesitate, four silhouettes on a boat instead of one lonely target on a road.

They make you easier to find, the smart part of me said.

They make me harder to destroy, the tired part answered.

Upstream, the light was going copper on the water, and somewhere ahead the gorge waited with its missing footprints and its breathing river and whatever my father had been doing in the years after he taught me to read clouds. I turned the compass over in my hand. The needle swung, settled, pointed home the way it always did.

Only these days, I wasn't sure which direction home was. Maybe that's what a crew was. Not a destination—a direction.

"Sandbar ahead, port side," Tuck called out, having somehow learned the channel in four hours.

"Good eye," Bram said.

I didn't say anything. I just didn't tell him to stop looking, either.

Behind us, far downstream, a smudge of dust hung over the track we'd left—dust that hadn't been there an hour ago, hanging where no truck should be, moving the way traffic moves when it isn't in a hurry because it doesn't have to be.

Sefa saw it too. She folded her chart slowly and said nothing, and I decided not to decide anything about it until morning.

The river carried us north, toward the source, four strays and a stolen boat, and the escarpment swallowed the sun behind us like a secret being kept.
