## 6. What Sefa Saw

The night the survey team vanished began with a sound Sefa would spend the rest of her life trying to describe.

She was on second watch at the lower camp, the one tucked into the fold of the escarpment where the river bent hard around a shelf of black limestone. Eleven people asleep in their tents. The generator humming its tired hum. Rain coming down in that patient, businesslike way the monsoon had of reminding everyone who owned the season.

Then the river stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped. The way a sentence stops when someone puts a hand over your mouth.

Sefa stood up out of her chair so fast it went over backward behind her. She'd grown up on this water—her mother's family had fished the Karst for four generations—and she knew every voice the river had. The low mutter of drought. The flat roar of flood. The strange glassy hush before a landslide dam gave way upstream. She had never heard silence like this. It wasn't the absence of sound. It was suction. It was the whole river, a hundred meters wide and running deep as a church, being pulled backward into the gorge below Widow's Bend like breath drawn through teeth.

The sound that came with it—the inhale itself—wasn't water at all. It was metal. Vast sluices somewhere under the escarpment grinding open, a groan she felt in her sternum more than heard, the mountain clearing its throat.

And then the lights.

They came up in the gorge floor, which should have been impossible, because the gorge floor was forty meters down and reachable only by rope. Green-white banks of them, arranged in rows, illuminating nothing but mist. Machinery lights. Sefa's mind supplied the word before she wanted it to: *infrastructure*. Somebody had built a cathedral under the river and never told anyone.

She got Halvard up first because he was team lead and that was the protocol drilled into all of them—wake the lead, let the lead decide. He came out of his tent already dressed, which she noticed later and understood even later than that. Nobody sleeps dressed on second watch night unless they've been expecting something.

"Stay here," he said.

"Sir, the river is running *backward*—"

"I said stay here." His voice had a flatness to it she'd never heard from him. Halvard Osei was the kind of lead who remembered your daughter's name and carried the heaviest pack on portage days. This man speaking through his mouth sounded like a memo. "Nobody goes to the rim. That's an order."

He took Renn and Okonkwo and the two junior techs whose names Sefa could never afterward fully recall, which haunted her, and they went down toward the gorge with headlamps bobbing, and Sefa stood in the rain and did the worst thing she ever did in her life.

She obeyed.

* * *

Here is what guilt does, if you feed it long enough. It stops feeling like a weight and starts feeling like fuel. Sefa learned that over the following hours, standing at the edge of camp with the generator humming and the river still holding its stolen breath, doing arithmetic she couldn't stop doing.

Eleven people. Five went down to the gorge. Six remained, counting her.

The lights burned for three hours. The inhale-sound cycled twice more—open, hold, close, open, hold, close—like something enormous practicing. And then, near what the camp clock called 0300, everything shut off at once. Lights, machinery, generator noise from somewhere below that she hadn't even registered until it died. The river came back all at once, a great exhaled shove of water that lifted the level two meters in a minute and sent driftwood and one very confused tapir past the camp at speed.

At dawn, Halvard came back up alone.

He was dry. That was the detail that broke something in Sefa and never let it heal. Forty meters down into a flooded gorge in monsoon rain, after a night of whatever that had been, and Halvard Osei climbed back into camp without a spot of mud on him. As if he hadn't gone down at all. As if he'd been somewhere else entirely—somewhere covered, somewhere *finished*, somewhere with stairs.

"Where are the others?" Sefa asked.

"Gone ahead," he said. "Relocated. The survey's concluded."

"The survey isn't concluded. We're missing six weeks of downstream gauging. The source samples haven't—" She stopped, because Halvard was looking at her, and his eyes were doing something terrible. They were kind. They were the kindest she'd ever seen them, and kindness from a man that dry was worse than anger.

"Sefa." He said her name gently, the way you speak to someone standing on ice you know is thin. "I need you to help me erase everything."

* * *

The orders came in pieces over that morning, each one reasonable, each one small, and that was the trick of it—she saw the trick even as it worked on her, and it worked anyway.

The data drives first. "Redundant copies are held centrally," Halvard said. "Field copies create liability." Fine. Standard. Government surveys did clean up their field copies; she'd done it herself on two previous contracts.

Then the paper logs. "Shred the working notes, keep the official record." Also standard, also fine, except the working notes were where the real numbers lived, and Sefa had spent nine months on those numbers. The gauge calibrations at Widow's Bend showing flow reversals no natural karst system produced. The piezometer readings from the monitoring wells—wells nobody had authorized them to drill—that showed pressure differentials consistent with active pumping beneath the escarpment. Her own handwriting, page after page, describing a river that breathed.

"Halvard," she said, "these readings mean there's a machine under the mountain."

"These readings mean we did our job," he said, "and our job is done," and he smiled, and it was the smile of a man reading words someone else had written for him.

Then the instruments. Then the tents were to stay—"salvage crew will handle the physical teardown"—and Sefa felt the shape of it finally, the whole cold architecture. A salvage crew. Someone had already contracted strangers to come up the river and make the camp look abandoned rather than evacuated. Abandoned camps raised questions for a season. Evacuated camps raised questions forever.

"And the team?" she asked. "Renn? Okonkwo? The juniors?"

"Relocated," Halvard said again, and this time the word had a seam in it, and Sefa heard what was underneath.

She was not a brave woman. She wants that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts like hers. She was a careful woman, a precise woman, a woman who double-checked knots and calibrated twice. Bravery would have stayed and fought. Bravery would have confronted Halvard, radioed out, done *something* in the moment when five people were still maybe alive and one word from her might have mattered.

What Sefa did instead was what careful people do. She waited for the midday supply run. She packed one bag with things Halvard had ordered destroyed—a duplicate logbook, a set of falsified survey maps she'd found in his footlocker marked with corrections in a hand that wasn't any of theirs, and a single photograph of the gorge lights taken on Okonkwo's camera, printed in camp, the only image that existed outside the erased drives. She told no one. There was no one left to tell. The other five remaining members had spent the morning shredding with the obedience of people who trusted the structure more than their own eyes, and by noon two of them had gone down to the gorge too, "to assist with decommissioning," and had not come back up.

At 1400 she walked into the treeline with her bag and her guilt and she ran.

* * *

She ran for three days.

Down the old hunting trails on the escarpment's eastern flank, the ones the maps politely declined to show, sleeping in overhangs, eating what the forest offered and what she'd stuffed into her pockets from the camp stores like a thief robbing her own house. Twice she heard helicopters. Once, on the second night, she saw a boat go upriver through the rain with its running lights off, riding low, and she pressed herself into the mud of the bank and watched it pass and thought: *that's how they move people now. Dark boats, low water, no wake.* She thought about flagging it down. She thought about it for a long time, long enough that the boat was gone, and she has never decided whether that was cowardice or the only smart thing she did that week.

On the fourth day she reached the crossing at Tarn Ford, half-starved, feverish, smelling like the inside of a boot, and traded her father's watch to a ferryman for passage and hot food, and sat in the ferryman's kitchen listening to a woman talk about storm cells on the flats like other people talk about weather, and thought: *there's someone who doesn't owe anything to anyone.*

She envied her. She'd learn later how wrong she was.

* * *

Now, weeks later, sitting in the stern of Bram's confiscated boat while it rocked against the pilings at the flooded crossing, watching the woman with the compass argue with a boy who refused to get out of her boat, Sefa did the arithmetic again. She did it every day. Some mornings it was penance. Lately it had started to feel like navigation.

Five people went down into the gorge and did not come back up. Two more followed them the next morning. Halvard was relocated too, presumably—or promoted, or silenced, or standing in some office in the capital signing the paperwork that made the whole survey never have happened. Eleven names on a roster that officially read *contract concluded, personnel reassigned.*

Officially. But Sefa had the duplicate logbook sewn into the lining of her pack, and she had the photograph, and she had the maps with the corrections, and every day she kept them was a day the eleven weren't finished being erased.

The problem—the problem that had kept her moving, kept her alone, kept her from going to authorities or journalists or anyone with a badge and a filing cabinet—was that she didn't know who to trust. The survey had been government. The erasure had been government-shaped. The salvage contract that would tidy the camp had been signed by somebody with reach, and reach like that didn't stop at the escarpment. It came down the river looking for loose ends. She was a loose end. She'd known it since the third day of running, when she'd realized the ferryman asked one question too many about where she'd come from, in a tone too casual, and she'd paid him extra to forget her face and hadn't slept since.

So she'd stayed small. Stayed moving. Stayed a rumor in the river towns, fixing gauges for cash, sleeping in boat hulls, telling no one. Guilt as fuel, burning slow, going nowhere.

Until tonight.

Until the woman with the compass said the words that rearranged everything: *survey camp, Karst Escarpment, salvage job.* Said them casually, loading gear, not knowing what she was saying. And Sefa had looked at her—at the worn brass compass clipped to her jacket strap, at the way she checked the sky without seeming to, at the debt-collector's shadow Sefa could smell on her like woodsmoke—and understood that the Syndicate's salvage crew wasn't coming to tidy the camp anymore.

It was coming because the camp had led somewhere. Because the river was breathing again. Because whoever owned the machine under the mountain needed it found, claimed, and buried deeper than before, and they'd hired a stranger to do the finding.

Which meant the stranger was either their instrument or their obstacle.

Sefa looked at Dominic Dunmore—tired, cornered, visibly drowning in a ledger she didn't understand yet—and made the decision she'd been running from for weeks. Not confession. Not yet. Confession could wait until they were on the water, committed, past the point where the woman might reasonably put her ashore. Careful people survived by choosing their moments.

But she stepped onto the boat. She picked up a line and coiled it correctly, the way a surveyor coils a line, and watched Dominic notice that.

"You've handled rope," Dominic said.

"I've handled rivers," Sefa said. "Hire me."

Dominic's eyes narrowed, and above them the first true thunder of the season rolled down off the escarpment like something vast clearing its throat, and far upstream, though none of them knew it yet, the river at Widow's Bend paused between beats—inhaling—waiting to see who was coming.
