## 7. The Syndicate Follows

Dominic smelled the smoke before she saw the fire.

She was two ridges down from the survey camp, following the river's noise through the trees, when the wind shifted and brought it to her—woodsmoke, but under it something chemical and wrong, like burning plastic and burnt coffee. Campfire smoke didn't smell like that. Vellum Syndicate field rations did. She'd learned that smell in her first year of debt, back when Ives still pretended their meetings were friendly lunches.

She went still on the slope, one hand flat against wet stone, and let the forest hold her.

Below, through a break in the canopy, she could see the bend of river she'd been aiming for—a gravel bar, a rope-and-plank crossing some farmer had strung up decades ago, the far bank dissolving into karst country where the maps went vague and apologetic. And on the near bank, pitched neat as a showroom, were three tents she didn't own, a boat trailer with no boat, and a portable generator humming its small arrogant hum into the wilderness.

Four figures moved around the site with the unhurried efficiency of people who expected to find exactly what they found.

She counted gear instead of faces. Two long-range radios. A drone case, closed. A satellite uplink mast already raised. And laid out on a folding table under a tarp, being photographed by a man with gloved hands, was equipment she recognized down to the scratches—the survey team's backup instruments, the ones that hadn't been running when she'd walked through the camp yesterday. The ones that had been packed for travel.

They'd come up the same trail she had. Hours behind her, maybe less. Which meant—

Her stomach did something cold and slow.

Which meant they'd known where to look. Not the escarpment generally. Not the coordinates in the salvage contract, which pointed at the camp itself. They'd known her route. Her pace. The way she cut switchbacks when she thought no one was watching.

*You're not the salvager,* she thought. *You're the bloodhound.*

She made herself breathe. Made herself think it through properly, because panic was just interest accruing on fear, and she'd sworn off borrowing years ago.

The contract Ives had given her was clean on its face. Recover salvageable equipment from an abandoned government survey site. Standard clause about Syndicate ownership of anything recovered. Standard clause about her fee being applied against principal. She'd read it twice in the truck while Ives watched her with those bright patient eyes, and she'd signed because the alternative was a collections process she'd seen happen to a storm-chaser named Okafor—truck impounded, gear auctioned, name entered into a ledger that followed him across three provinces until he stopped using his real name at all.

But there had been a clause. Clause eleven. She'd skimmed it, told herself it was boilerplate.

*The Contractor agrees to pursue the recovery objective by the most direct practicable route and to make reasonable efforts to locate associated materials of value.*

Associated materials. Of value.

She'd assumed that meant the missing samples, the river gauge, the loose ends any honest salvage would chase. She'd even planned to chase them, because finding the full kit meant a bigger credit against her debt.

That was the beauty of it, she realized now, watching the men below photograph a water-stained instrument case. It hadn't been a trap she could have caught. It was a trap built out of her own competence. Follow the trail, Dominic. Be thorough, Dominic. You're so good at reading terrain, at reading weather, at reading what people leave behind. Do what you do best, and you'll lead us straight to whatever my father's survey team died hiding.

*My father's.*

She still couldn't say it flatly. It kept catching in her chest like a bone in the throat. The logbook sat against her spine in its waterproof sleeve, and every hour it got heavier, the way grief does—not lighter with carrying but denser, settling, becoming part of her center of gravity. His handwriting. Dated fourteen months after the flood took him. Annotations in the margins of a government survey he'd supposedly never touched, referencing a source the maps marked forbidden, warning in that cramped leftward slant she'd know anywhere: *Don't bring it down the mountain.*

And here she was, bringing herself down the mountain's flank with four Syndicate contractors between her and the river.

One of the men below laughed at something. The sound carried up the slope, easy and unbothered. They weren't searching. Searching was for people who didn't know. These men were *collecting*—moving through the abandoned camp's outer caches with a checklist, bagging things, tagging things. Somewhere in that operation was a document with her name on it, probably, and a set of instructions, and a line item labeled *asset tracking* or something worse and more polite.

Ives. She could see him so clearly it hurt—leaning against her truck door that first morning, ledger balanced on one palm like a waiter's towel, saying, *We're optimists, Ms. Dunmore. We believe everyone can pay.* He'd said it kindly. That was the thing about Ives that nobody who hadn't owed him money could understand: the kindness was real. He genuinely liked her. He genuinely wanted her to succeed. And every ounce of that genuine warmth was aimed at getting her to do exactly this—climb into the wild places, sniff out value, and hand it over with a smile of gratitude for the privilege.

She'd been so relieved, signing. That was the worst part. She'd felt *lucky*.

A branch cracked somewhere above her and her whole body went to iron.

She held it—held everything, breath, blink, heartbeat—for ten seconds, twenty, listening past the river's mutter. Rain-wet forest settling. A bird working the bark somewhere upslope. Nothing human. She unclenched one finger at a time.

But the message was received. She couldn't stay on this ridge, and she couldn't go back the way she'd come, and the crossing below was occupied.

So she did what she'd done her whole adult life when the sky turned ugly. She read the situation and moved before it moved first.

* *

The detour cost her half a day.

She climbed instead of descended, angling away from the river through karst country where the ground opened without warning into sinkholes and the limestone drank the rainwater straight down, leaving the surface dry and treacherous and empty of trails. Nobody followed her into that. Nobody sane. She picked her way along a ridge the locals called the Teeth, testing every hold, the logbook pressing against her spine like a second vertebra, and all the while her mind ran the numbers the way it ran storm cells—speed, direction, probability of rotation.

Here's what she knew. Here's what she made herself lay out, fact by fact, on the gray slab of rock where she finally stopped to eat cold noodles:

Fact: the survey team vanished fourteen months ago. Tents intact, meals half-eaten, instruments running. No tracks leaving. Sefa—that was the name stitched into the spare jacket she'd found folded in the supply tent, the only personal item anyone had bothered to leave—Sefa, whoever that was, had either vanished with the rest or run without stopping.

Fact: her father's handwriting in the logbook, dated after his death. Which meant his death was a story someone had needed told, and he—or whoever wrote in his hand—had needed it told badly enough to fake a body, a flood report, a funeral she'd stood at in the rain at nineteen with an urn that weighed nothing.

Fact: the Syndicate bought her debt eight months ago. Not gradually, not piecemeal—they'd bought the whole bundle from three smaller creditors in one week, quiet, efficient, the way you buy something you've already decided what to do with. At the time she'd thought it was bad luck. Debt got sold all the time. Now the timing sat differently. Eight months ago was roughly when the survey's parent ministry had quietly reclassified the upper escarpment as restricted pending review. Roughly when somebody upstairs had started asking what happened to Survey Team Karst-Four.

Fact: Ives had handed her a salvage job pointing at the exact place all those threads knotted together, and then sent a second crew up her trail hours behind her.

Conclusion, and she hated it, hated it with her whole tired heart: they hadn't needed her to find the equipment. The equipment was trash to them—cameras, tents, sample kits. What they needed was the *claim*. Whatever the survey had found upriver at the source, whatever her father had spent his fake death protecting, it existed as a legal object somewhere—a filing, a map, a set of rights—and the Syndicate wanted it badly enough to burn a full field crew on retrieving it. But claims required locations, and locations required proof, and proof required someone who could read terrain the way Ives read ledgers.

Someone like her.

*I'm not your employee,* she said silently to Ives, to the gray sky, to the ghost in the logbook. *I'm your instrument.*

And instruments, she thought, could be dropped. Could be broken. Could be left on a mountainside when they stopped returning useful data.

She finished the noodles. She checked the compass—her father's compass, brass-cased, the needle settling north with a small patient confidence she envied—and she noticed, not for the first time, that the bezel had a second ring of markings etched around it, fine as hair, numbers that matched nothing on any compass she'd ever owned. She'd always assumed it was a manufacturer's quirk.

Now she turned it slowly in her fingers and wondered if it was a key.

Later. One problem at a time. The sky to the southwest had begun doing something she didn't like—a bruised, organized darkness stacking up over the lowlands, the monsoon arriving early and mean, and between her and any kind of safety lay a river crossing currently staffed by men paid to be thorough.

She gave them until dark. Then she went down.

* *

She reached the edge of the crossing site at full dark, rain just starting, a fine needling rain that softened sound and blurred light, and she lay in the mud at the lip of the bank with the river talking below her and watched.

The crew had lit lanterns. Through the rain-haze they glowed warm and domestic, and the men moved between them with the looseness of an evening winding down. One sat under the tarp speaking into a radio, and though the words shredded in the wind, she caught the cadence—reporting, not requesting. Regular check-ins. A schedule.

Then the radio man said her name.

It came through clear between gusts—*"Dunmore"*—and the word went through her like a hook through cloth. The listener nodded. Said something short. And the man with the radio laughed, a bright easy laugh, and repeated a phrase she caught whole because the wind chose that moment to carry it perfectly down the bank:

*"No, she's good. Best tracker we've ever rented."*

*Rented.*

She lay in the mud and let the rain hit the back of her neck and felt something in her chest rearrange itself—not breaking, exactly. Setting. Like a fracture knitting into a different shape than the bone started with. All these years she'd believed her invisibility was a skill, a discipline, a craft she'd perfected: stay small, stay mobile, stay unremarkable, owe quietly, pay quietly, never give the world a handle to grab you by. And the whole time, the Vellum Syndicate had simply been waiting for the day her particular talents became worth more than her silence.

Today was that day. She was worth more tracked than free.

The radio crackled again. This time the reply came through almost whole, and the voice on the other end was cheerful and familiar and made her teeth ache:

*"—tell her the offer stands. Tell her we're optimistic."*

Ives. On the line, live, personally invested. She filed that away too. Men like Ives didn't take personal interest in routine recoveries.

The crew began banking their lanterns for the night. She counted them again—four, plus a fifth she hadn't spotted earlier, asleep in a tent with boots still on. Five. Against one, in the dark, in the rain, on terrain she knew better than they ever would.

She could take the crossing tonight. Slip wide, use the flooded side channel, get across upstream of them and put the karst between herself and their radios. Risky. The river was rising—you could hear it, a new bass note under the usual voice, the monsoon feeding it from the high country—but she'd crossed worse in worse.

Or she could go back up. Follow the river to its source ahead of them, ahead of the second crew, ahead of everyone, and find out what her father had died pretending to die for. Find it first, understand it first, and decide—her, alone, with no ledger and no leash—what happened next.

The compass sat in her palm, rain beading on the brass. The needle held north. The strange fine-etched ring held its secrets.

*Don't bring it down the mountain,* her father had written.

Fine, she thought. Then I'll bring myself up it.

She slid back from the bank, silent, mud to the elbows, rain erasing her tracks behind her almost as fast as she made them. Behind her the lanterns glowed and the radio murmured and five men slept beside a river they thought they understood.

Ahead of her, upriver, in the dark, something waited that had swallowed a whole survey team and left no footprints.

Dominic Dunmore turned her back on the crossing and started climbing.
