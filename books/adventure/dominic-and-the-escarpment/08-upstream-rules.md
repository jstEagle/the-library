## 8. Upstream Rules

Bram Oduya had run the Karst Escarpment's rivers for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned one rule above every other rule: the river tells you what it is going to do, if you're willing to listen. Most people weren't willing. Most people looked at water and saw a road. Bram looked at water and heard a conversation, and the conversation the Kessarin was having this morning was not a friendly one.

He stood on the deck of the *Marrow*, his confiscated boat, his seized boat, his boat that some clerk with a rubber stamp had taken from him eight months ago over a licensing fee he'd paid twice and couldn't prove — and he watched the river come down from the escarpment in long brown ropes of current, and he did not like what it was saying.

The others were still asleep. The boy was curled against the cargo netting like a cat who'd decided ownership by occupation. Sefa had taken the wheelhouse bench and hadn't moved all night; she slept the way deserters sleep, he imagined, which was to say badly, one hand closed around a canvas bag like it might be taken from her. And Dominic Dunmore —

Dominic Dunmore was already awake. She came down the bank through the mist with mud to her elbows and rain in her hair, moving quiet as a rumor, and Bram decided in that moment two things: first, that she had been up all night doing something she hadn't told them about, and second, that he didn't care, because anyone who moved like that through wet karst at dawn was someone worth standing next to when things went wrong.

"You found my boat," she said.

"Requisitioned," Bram said. "There's a difference. Legally."

"There's always a difference legally. That's what makes it useful." She stepped aboard, and the *Marrow* rocked under her weight, complaining. She was a thin woman, weather-cut, with the kind of eyes that measured everything and trusted almost none of it. "How much trouble are we in for stealing it?"

"None yet. Some later. The harbormaster at Lowbridge sold me the papers back for less than they cost him to take them, if you follow me."

"I follow you."

"Then we're not thieves. We're customers."

She almost smiled. It didn't quite land, but it got partway there, and Bram counted it. He put the kettle on the spirit stove because there was nothing else to offer a woman at dawn except hot water and honesty, and he gave her both.

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse while the mist burned off — Dominic, Sefa, the boy Tuck sitting on an oil drum with his knees drawn up like he owned the drum, and Bram himself, standing at the chart table with his hands flat on the only map of the upper Kessarin that wasn't a lie.

He knew it wasn't a lie because he'd drawn it himself. Nineteen years of running cargo, contraband, and occasionally people up and down these waters, and every official chart between the crossing and the escarpment crest was wrong in the same polite way: depths fudged, channels shifted, whole side-canyons left off as if the cartographers had been paid to forget them. Which, he suspected now, watching Sefa's face, they had been.

"So," Bram said. "You want to go up."

"We want to go up," Dominic said.

"The boy wants to go up. The surveyor wants to go up. I want to stay here and drink tea until the monsoon makes the question moot." He tapped the chart. "But since we're apparently doing this, let me tell you what 'up' means, so nobody's surprised when it tries to kill us."

He laid it out the way he laid out every route: plainly, without decoration, because decoration on a river got people drowned.

"Nine days. That's the number. Nine days from this crossing to the high water above Widow's Bend, assuming the monsoon holds off, which it will not, but let's pretend. Day one and two, easy water — broad channel, decent depth, we make good time and I stop being angry about the boat. Day three we hit the Narrows, and the Narrows don't care about your schedule. The channel pinches down to forty meters between limestone walls, the current runs four knots against us, and there's exactly one place to winch through. If anything's waiting for us there, we're fish in a barrel with a motor."

Sefa spoke for the first time that morning. Her voice was rough from not sleeping. "Nothing's waiting. Nobody knows we're going."

Bram looked at her. He was a patient man, but he was not a stupid man. "Nobody knew your team was going either, and look how that ended."

She flinched. He regretted it immediately, but he didn't take it back, because on this river kindness that lied would kill you faster than cruelty that told the truth.

"Day five," he went on, "we reach the terraces. That's where the river starts climbing in earnest — a staircase of drops, three meters, five meters, seven. We portage the worst of them. Portage means everybody out, lines on the boat, hauling two tons of steel uphill over wet rock while the water laughs at us. Twelve hours a day minimum. Your hands will bleed. That's normal. Don't complain about it, it wastes breath."

Tuck raised his hand like they were in school. Bram stared at him. The boy lowered it slowly.

"Day six and seven, the gorges. Narrower, deeper, and the water does something up there I've seen twice in nineteen years and never gotten used to." He paused, choosing his words the way you chose footing on slick rock. "It runs backward. Not all the time. In pulses. Like something under the escarpment is breathing in, and the river gets pulled toward it, and then it lets go and everything surges back down. When that happens, you get out of the water or the water takes you. No exceptions. No heroics."

"That's not possible," Sefa said quietly. But she said it the way people say things they know aren't true.

"It's not possible," Bram agreed. "And yet. Day eight, we come off the main channel entirely and take the floodcut — a side passage through the karst that only runs in high water, which means we need the monsoon's first rains to use it, which means we need to arrive just as the sky decides to drown us. Too early, no water in the cut. Too late, and the cut's a death chute. There's a window maybe six hours wide, and we have to find it blind."

"And day nine?" Dominic asked.

"Day nine," Bram said, "you're above the maps. Whatever's up there — whatever swallowed your survey team, whatever your father was hiding, whatever the Syndicate thinks is worth killing for — day nine is when you meet it." He straightened up. "That's the route. Nine days of water that doesn't forgive. Now the other half of the truth."

He held up one finger.

"The monsoon makes landfall in ten days. Maybe eleven. I've watched these skies my whole life and the pressure's wrong, the birds are flying low, and the river's already carrying more silt than it should for this season. So we don't have nine days of margin. We have nine days of margin minus whatever the sky decides to steal. Every hour we stand here talking is an hour the storm takes off the end."

He looked around the wheelhouse. Mist through the windows, brown water below, the escarpment a gray wall in the north with its head in cloud.

"So that's the deal. One boat, too small. Four people, too many. A route with no room for error and a deadline set by weather we can't argue with. Anybody who wants off, gets off now, and I'll say nothing, because wanting off is the smart move and I've rarely met anyone who took it."

Silence. The kettle ticked on the stove. Downstream, far off, thunder muttered like a creditor clearing his throat.

* * *

It was Tuck who broke it, because it was always going to be Tuck.

"I'm coming," the boy said. "My village is downstream of whatever that machine is. If it floods, I'm the one who loses everybody. So I'm coming, and you can't stop me, and also I can cook."

"You cannot cook," Bram said.

"I can cook rice."

"He can cook rice," Sefa confirmed, and there was something in her voice — the first thing resembling warmth Bram had heard from her. "Badly. But he can."

Dominic hadn't spoken. She stood at the window with her father's compass open in her palm, needle steady, and Bram watched her the way he watched water before committing the boat to a channel. She was reading something. Not the compass. Something behind it.

"Sefa," she said finally. "Your team. They went up this route?"

"Some of it. We came in from the east trail with mules. But the gauge data — the samples — they were pulled from the upper gorge. Someone ran the water route." She hesitated. "Your father's annotations reference it. The floodcut. He marked it 'the honest way in.'"

"The honest way in," Bram repeated. He laughed once, short and without humor. "Nineteen years I've run that cut in flood and never once thought of it as honest. Dangerous, yes. Suicidal, occasionally. Honest is new."

"Maybe that's why they needed a pilot," Sefa said, looking at him directly. "Maybe that's why the logbook keeps mentioning finding one."

The wheelhouse went very quiet. Bram felt the sentence settle onto his shoulders like a wet tarp. He thought about saying no. He thought about it seriously — about walking off his own boat, catching a ride downstream, letting the Syndicate and the government and the ghost of some dead surveyor fight over their mountain without him.

Then he looked at the boy on the oil drum, who had nowhere else to go. At the surveyor with her dead colleagues and her living guilt. At the storm chaser with mud on her elbows and a dead man's compass in her hand, who had climbed out of a river in the dark last night and come back changed, harder, aimed at something.

And he looked at the river itself, running hard for the sea, carrying the mountain's secrets down to the delta one grain of silt at a time.

"Well," Bram said. "Damn it."

He pulled the chart closer, took out his pencil, and began marking the route in his own shorthand — winch points, portage camps, the pulse-timing at the gorge, the six-hour window at the floodcut. His hands moved steadily. They always did. That was the whole trick of Bram Oduya: hands that stayed steady even when the man behind them wanted to run.

"Rules," he said, not looking up. "Upstream rules. You don't argue with the water. You don't overload the boat past what I say, and I'll say less than it can hold. You do exactly what I tell you in the gorges, instantly, without discussion, because discussion happens at four knots per second and drowning happens faster. And nobody—" he glanced at Tuck, "—nobody touches the throttle but me."

"Agreed," Dominic said.

"All of you? Say it out loud. On this river, a promise is ballast. I want to hear it."

Sefa said it. Tuck said it, solemn as an oath at a hanging. Dominic said it last, and she said it looking at the north wall of cloud where the escarpment waited, and her voice had something in it Bram recognized from men he'd crewed with before bad runs — not fear, exactly, and not courage either, but the thing underneath both, the thing that decides.

"Then we leave on the next tide," Bram said. "Which is now, because I said so."

* * *

They cast off into the brown morning water, and the *Marrow* — his boat, his stolen, bought-back, resurrected boat — turned her blunt nose upstream for the first time in her life. The current took her stern and swung it, grumbling, and Bram fed power in against it, feeling the hull answer, feeling the old argument between engine and river begin again.

Behind them the crossing fell away: the lanterns, the Syndicate collectors' camp, the whole soft world of debts and ledgers and men who smiled while they ruined you. Ahead the escarpment stood up out of the earth like a wall built by something with opinions.

Tuck stood at the bow with his arms out like a figurehead, pretending to steer. Sefa sat amidships with the canvas bag in her lap, watching the water with the expression of a woman counting ghosts. Dominic came aft to stand beside Bram at the wheel, close enough that he could see the compass riding in her jacket pocket, needle swinging gently with each course correction, always finding north.

"Nine days," she said.

"Less, if the sky has opinions."

"The sky always has opinions." She watched the gray wall grow. "I've spent my whole life chasing storms, Bram. Running after them, measuring them, writing reports nobody read. This is the first time I've ever driven straight at one on purpose."

"How's it feel?"

She considered it honestly, which he respected. Most people answered fast with something brave.

"Like the moment before a chase breaks loose," she said. "When the air goes still and heavy and you know something enormous is coming and you can't stop it and you wouldn't. You just point yourself at it."

Bram nodded and eased the throttle forward. The *Marrow* dug in. The river pushed back. Somewhere ahead, past the Narrows and the terraces and the breathing gorges, the floodcut waited with its six-hour window, and beyond it the source, and beyond the source whatever truth had eaten a survey team whole.

"Then hold on to something," he said. "We've got nine days of water that doesn't forgive, and I intend to introduce it to a crew that doesn't ask."

The bow lifted. The current thickened. Upstream, faint under the wind, Bram thought he heard it again — that deep, slow sound he'd heard twice in nineteen years and told no one about.

Like a breath drawn in, somewhere under the mountain.

And held.
