## 9. The Gauge at Widow's Bend

Widow's Bend announced itself before we saw it, the way bad places do.

Bram cut the engine a half mile out and let the current carry us, and I understood why when the sound arrived—a low, grinding hush, like gravel being swallowed somewhere underground. The river bent around a shoulder of black limestone in a horseshoe so tight the water piled up against its own outside edge and stood there, confused, two feet higher on one side of the channel than the other. Old-timers named it for the women who'd lost boats here. The name was generous. It had probably taken more than boats.

"There." Sefa pointed with her chin.

The gauge stood on an iron piling driven into the rock shelf at the bend's apex, maybe thirty yards off our line. A squat steel cabinet bolted to a stanchion, solar panel canted south, antenna whip leaning like a tired man against a doorframe. Government issue. The same make as the instruments still humming away in the abandoned camp, still logging data for nobody.

"The missing gauge," Sefa said quietly. "It didn't walk off. It was installed here."

"Installed," Bram repeated. "By whom? The team went upriver. This is downstream of everything."

"Which is exactly where you put a gauge," Sefa said, "if you want to measure what comes *down*."

I looked at her. She was doing that thing she did—holding information behind her teeth like water behind a dam—and for once she let some through.

"A source sample tells you what the water is. A downstream gauge tells you what the water is *doing*. If you're studying a river honestly, you instrument both ends." She paused. "If you're studying something dishonestly, you only instrument the end people can see."

Tuck was already moving to the bow, coiling a rope like he'd been born to it, which he hadn't—two days ago he'd been cargo. "I can swim it."

"You can not," I said.

"I swam worse than that in the millrace back home."

"You swam the millrace because your cousin dared you and the millrace is forty feet wide. That's a hundred yards of standing waves into a rock shelf."

He gave me the look boys give when they've decided a thing is decided and are just waiting for the adults to catch up. Bram cut the argument off by simply turning the boat, threading us along the slack water inside the bend with a touch on the tiller so light it looked like luck. We came alongside the piling and Tuck had the rope around the stanchion before I'd finished standing up.

* * *

The cabinet door was locked. It took Sefa four minutes and a flathead screwdriver, during which time she narrated nothing and apologized less, and then it swung open on a dry, warm interior that smelled faintly of ozone.

Warm. That was the first wrong thing. Every other piece of government equipment we'd found had been dead or dying, batteries swollen, screens fogged. This cabinet was running hot, and the battery bank inside showed a charge level no solar panel this size could sustain.

"It's got auxiliary power," Sefa said. She'd gone very still, the way she did at the camp when the instruments were still ticking. "Hardline power. There's a cable." She traced it with one finger: out through a grommet in the cabinet floor, down the stanchion, into the water.

"Runs to the far bank?" Bram asked.

Sefa shook her head slowly. "It runs *down*."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The river ground its gravel somewhere beneath us.

The data logger's screen glowed a soft patient green. Sefa pulled up the record with three keystrokes—she knew the firmware, same suite as the camp instruments—and the last ninety days of readings scrolled past in columns too small to read from where I stood. She changed the display to a plot instead, stage height over time, and the screen filled with a line I will see when I close my eyes for the rest of my life.

Rivers have a signature. I've spent my whole adult life reading them from the sky. A healthy river's hydrograph is a language: rain makes it rise, drought makes it fall, snowmelt gives it a slow springtime breath. Smooth curves. Cause and effect. The river answers the sky.

This line didn't answer the sky.

It pulsed.

Regular as a heartbeat—no, not regular, that was the thing, that was the *wrong* thing. Regular would mean tide, or a dam release, or some dumb mechanical cycle. These pulses came in clusters. Three quick rises, a long fall, a pause, then two more. The rises were small—centimeters, mostly—but they all went the same direction.

Every pulse was a rise at the downstream gauge.

"Rise means water arriving from upstream," Sefa said, and her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone reading aloud something they don't yet believe. "Except—" She switched views, cross-referencing the flow direction sensor, and made a sound low in her throat.

"What?" Bram said.

"Flow direction reads negative during every pulse. Negative means upstream. The water is flowing backward toward the source while the stage is rising." She looked up at us, and her face had the expression of a person watching a magic trick they can't unsee. "Water can't do that. Water doesn't pile up at the bottom and run home uphill. Unless something downstream of the gauge is pulling."

"Pulling how?" Tuck asked.

"Or pushing," Sefa said. "From below."

* * *

We ate lunch on the rock shelf because nobody could think of a reason to leave and nobody could think of a reason to stay, and both of those facts sat in the boat with us like extra passengers.

Bram smoked half a cigarette and stared at the bend. "My grandfather ran freight up this river for thirty years. He told me once the escarpment drank. Said in dry years the river above the gorge would drop a foot overnight, no weather anywhere, come back just as fast. Everyone called it karst. Limestone does funny things—sinkholes, springs, underground channels. Whole rivers vanish into it and come out miles away."

"That's natural," Tuck said.

"That's natural," Bram agreed. "But natural doesn't install gauges with hardline power running down into the bedrock."

Sefa hadn't touched her food. She had the logger's raw export loaded onto her handheld now, scrolling through ninety days of pulses, and I watched her find the pattern the way I watch storm cells organize on radar—that moment when noise becomes structure and structure becomes intent.

"Dominic," she said. "Come look at this."

I knelt beside her. She'd isolated the largest pulse event—eleven days ago, per the timestamp—and expanded it. Stage rise of nearly half a meter in under four minutes. Flow reversal sustained for six. And underneath the main pulse, visible now that she'd zoomed, a fine tremor in the record. Not turbulence. Turbulence is chaotic. This was rhythmic. Slow oscillation, period of about ninety seconds, decaying evenly after the main event.

"That's not water," I said.

"No," Sefa said. "That's machinery. Big machinery, starting and stopping. The river's just what it moves."

I thought about the camp. Meals half-eaten, instruments running, no tracks leaving. I'd filed it under dread and moved on, because dread was easier than thinking. But now the picture reassembled itself with the gauge in the middle of it. A survey team doesn't vanish over a natural spring. A survey team vanishes over a *project*. Something worth installing hidden instrumentation for. Something worth powering from below the riverbed. Something whose owners could afford to erase eight professionals without leaving a footprint.

Something my father had annotated a logbook about, dated eleven days after his funeral.

"Widow's Bend," Bram said suddenly, and laughed, but it wasn't a laugh. "You know why they call it that? My grandfather said the old pilots swore the bend breathed. Boats would feel a suck go through the eddy, regular as sleep, right at dusk. He said the widows' husbands drowned on nights the river inhaled."

Tuck had gone quiet in a way that made me turn around. He was crouched at the waterline, hand flat on the wet rock, and he wasn't pretending anymore—he was scared, and trying to hide it, and failing.

"My gran says the river has a heartbeat," he said. "She says it in the stories, the ones the old people tell. Everybody thinks it's a story." He looked up at me. "It's not a story, is it."

"No," I said. "It's not a story."

And here is the part I keep coming back to, the part I'll be honest about even if I'm not honest about much else: some cold, greedy, debt-ridden corner of me was *thrilled*. Because a machine under the escarpment meant a claim. A claim meant value. Value meant leverage, and leverage meant the Vellum Syndicate's ledger finally tilting my way instead of pinning me flat. Ives's cheerful voice was already in my ear—*salvage rights, Dominic, salvage rights on something like this*—and I hated myself for hearing it, and I heard it anyway.

Then I looked at Tuck's hand on the rock, and at Sefa's face, which held the particular grief of someone who'd run from whatever killed her colleagues and had just learned the killing had an engineering budget. And I understood that the thrill and the horror were the same discovery, viewed from opposite banks, and that sooner or later I was going to have to pick a side of the river to stand on.

* * *

We recovered the gauge properly, because that was the job and because Sefa insisted the data had to survive even if we didn't. She backed up the full record to two separate drives, wrapped one in wax cloth and sewed it into the lining of her pack, and gave the second to Tuck, who took it with the gravity of a boy being handed a live coal.

"If we get separated," she told him, "you get that to the lowlands. Any telegraph office, any newspaper, anyone with a press. You don't come find us. You deliver."

"I'm not getting separated," Tuck said.

"Nobody plans to," Sefa said. "That's what separation means."

Bram winched the cabinet free of the stanchion—it fought us, bolts rusted to lace, and for one ugly moment the whole assembly leaned out over the channel and I saw all four of us going into the bend together, a package deal for the widows—and then it came loose and we lowered it into the boat's belly like ballast, which is exactly what it became.

The hardline cable we couldn't cut. Sefa tried; the insulation was armored, some composite none of us recognized, and the blade skated off it like it was laughing at us. So we left it running down into the dark, attached to whatever was down there, still powered, still breathing its slow ninety-second breath.

As we pushed off, the bend did it. I felt it through the hull before anything—a shudder, a gathering, the water around us tightening like a fist closing halfway—and then the eddy at the apex turned, visibly turned, and began to spin the wrong way. Three seconds. Four. The surface dimpled inward, drawn toward the center of the river as if a plug had been pulled in the sky. Then it released, all at once, and the current resumed its manners as though nothing had happened.

None of us spoke until we were a mile downstream.

"Eleven days since the big pulse," Sefa said finally, eyes on her handheld. "The intervals between major events are shortening. Nine days, seven, five, and the amplitude's growing each time." She did the arithmetic out loud, because saying numbers makes them real. "If the trend holds, the next full event is—"

She stopped. Looked upriver, toward the escarpment, toward the source, toward the place the maps marked forbidden and my father's handwriting had marked *don't bring it down the mountain*.

"When?" Bram asked.

"Tomorrow night," Sefa said. "Maybe the night after. And Dominic—the next one won't be centimeters."

I stood at the stern with the tiller in my hand and the current pulling us toward the escarpment, and I felt the whole sky change pressure the way it does twenty minutes before a cell drops. Everything we'd been running from and everything we'd been running toward had just collapsed into the same point on the map, and the point was breathing, and it was due to inhale again by tomorrow night.

"Tuck," I said, "get the tarps. Bram—how fast can this boat climb in high water?"

He looked at the black shoulder of the escarpment rising ahead of us, and then at me, and his grin had no joy in it at all, only appetite.

"Faster than sense," he said. "Which is the only speed that gets us there first."
