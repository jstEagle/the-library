## 10. Tuck's Bargain

Tuck had been counting the days since he'd climbed into the supply crate at the crossing, and he'd decided that if they were going to throw him off the boat, it would happen tonight. That was the arithmetic of stowaways: every hour you stayed hidden was an hour borrowed against a debt you couldn't pay, and eventually somebody came to collect.

He sat on the deck of Bram's boat—the *Marrow*, which was a terrible name for a boat and Bram defended it with the kind of stubbornness Tuck recognized from his own grandmother—watching the fire on the bank where the others had made camp. Dominic sat closest to the flames, turning her father's compass over in her fingers like she was trying to read something written on the back of it. Sefa had spread the survey maps out on a flat rock and was staring at them the way Tuck stared at the river when it was too high to cross. Bram was drinking something from a dented flask and pretending not to watch all three of them.

Nobody had thrown him off yet. But nobody had said he could stay, either.

That was worse.

* * *

He'd learned about being bought out the way most kids in Lowmark learned things—overheard, half-understood, and then understood completely all at once, the way floodwater comes up through your floorboards slow and then all at once.

It had started in spring, when the men in gray jackets came down from the escarpment road. They weren't Syndicate men; Tuck knew Syndicate men by their smiles, which were too wide and arrived too early. These men had clipboards instead of ledgers, and they smiled only when they thought it would help. They called themselves Vellum Field Acquisitions, which sounded official until you noticed the paper they wanted signatures on was the same cream-colored stock the Syndicate used for everything else. Same watermark. Tuck had held enough of those papers in his hands—his mother had made him hold them, made him look at them, so he'd know what was coming for them—to know the watermark.

*Vellum Field Acquisitions,* offering fair market value for every parcel in Lowmark. Every house, every dock, every net shed, every grave plot along the waterline. Fair market value for a village that wouldn't have a market anymore once the water changed.

Because that was the part nobody would say out loud, the part Tuck had pieced together from the gauge readings his uncle kept and the way the fish had moved upstream two seasons early and the survey crew that had come through in autumn asking questions about flow rates and never come back down.

Something upstream was going to change the river. And when it did, Lowmark would either be underwater or be worth nothing, depending on which way the sluices opened. Either way, the people holding the paper won. They'd buy the village cheap now, before anyone could prove what was coming, and if the villagers refused—well. Rivers drowned villages all the time. Accidents happened. Paper didn't get wet.

His mother had signed. That was the thing that still sat in Tuck's chest like a swallowed stone. She'd signed because the alternative was nothing, because the offer expired, because Tuck's little sister needed medicine that cost more than the whole dock brought in during a good season, because she was tired in a way Tuck had never seen before and hoped he never would again. She signed and then she cried in the kitchen with the door shut, thinking he was asleep, and Tuck had stood in the dark hallway listening to the sound a grown woman makes when she's sold the ground under her own feet, and he had decided something.

He'd decided that somebody from Lowmark was going to find out what was actually happening upriver. Not the version on the clipboards. The real one.

And then the survey camp had appeared in his life like an answer to a question he hadn't asked out loud—a salvage job, a woman with storm-gray eyes and a ledger-shaped shadow behind her, a boat heading up the escarpment instead of down. He didn't plan to stow away. Planning was for people with options. He just saw the crate being loaded at the crossing, saw the woman arguing with the pilot about weight limits, and did the math: a boy weighs less than a coil of rope, and nobody counts rope twice.

* * *

The fire cracked and spat. Tuck pulled his knees up and rehearsed it one more time, the speech he'd been building in his head for six days, the one that would make them let him stay.

Except every version of the speech was a lie, and he knew it. If he told them he could cook, fine, but Sefa cooked better. If he told them he knew the river, fine, but Bram had forgotten more about the river than Tuck would ever learn. If he told them he was strong, they'd laugh, because he was twelve and built like a heron.

There was only one true thing he had, and it was the thing he'd been afraid to say, because saying it out loud made it real, and because once they knew, they might decide he was a complication instead of a passenger. Complications got left at portages.

But the deadline was here. Tomorrow they'd reach the bend where the channel split, and Bram had already said the east fork was the fast way up and the west fork was the smart way, and whichever they picked, Tuck knew this was the last night he could speak without it costing more than silence.

He stood up. His legs felt like someone else's legs.

Dominic looked at him first. She always looked at him first, like she'd known since the crate, like she was keeping a running tally of him somewhere behind her eyes. "You should be sleeping," she said. Not unkind. Not kind, either. Dominic's voice lived in that country between.

"We need to talk," Tuck said. His voice cracked on *talk*, which was humiliating, but there was no fixing it now. "About why I'm really here."

Bram lowered the flask. Sefa looked up from the maps.

"I heard that," Dominic said quietly. "In the crate. Day two. You talking to yourself." She turned the compass over once more and set it down on her knee. "I figured you'd tell me when you were ready."

"You knew?" The word came out too loud. "You knew and you didn't—"

"Throwing you off a moving boat is how boys drown," she said. "I wasn't going to do that. I was going to put you on the first downstream barge with money for the fare." Her eyes didn't move from his face. "Unless you gave me a reason not to."

This was it. The door was open and the drop was right there and Tuck stood at the edge of it with the stone in his chest and the words in his throat, and he thought about his mother crying behind the kitchen door, and he stepped off.

"It's Vellum," he said. "Your Syndicate. The same company. They're buying my whole village."

* * *

He told it badly and then he told it well, the way truth works—you stumble through the front of it and then suddenly you're inside it and it comes out of you in order, everything, the gray-jacketed men and the cream paper and the watermark and the medicine and the door shut in the kitchen, and the survey crew that came through in autumn measuring the river like undertakers measuring a body, and how none of them came back down, and how the men with clipboards stopped pretending after that, stopped smiling even, because they didn't need to smile anymore. Whatever was going to happen to Lowmark was already decided. The offers were just courtesy.

"They keep saying fair value," Tuck finished. His voice had gone steady somewhere in the middle, which surprised him. "Fair value for a drowning. How is that fair? Nobody asked us if we wanted the river changed. Nobody even told us. They're buying us quiet so we can't complain about whatever it is you're—" he gestured upstream, at the dark gorge, at all of it, "—whatever it is you're all chasing."

The fire popped. Downstream, far off, the river made its long constant sound, the sound Tuck had slept to his whole life, and for the first time he heard it as something that could be taken away.

Sefa spoke first. She'd gone very still, the way she went still whenever the survey came up, like a person standing very close to a wound. "Lowmark," she said. "Downstream of Widow's Bend?"

"Past it. Last village before the delta flats."

Sefa looked at Dominic, and something passed between them that Tuck couldn't read but could feel, like a change in air pressure before weather. "The team took baseline samples at Lowmark," she said carefully. "Flow, sediment, seasonal levels. Standard survey work. Except—" she hesitated, and Tuck watched her decide to tell the truth, watched it cost her something, "—except we also surveyed every structure on the floodplain. Every house. Every foundation depth. You don't do that for a hydrology study. You do it when you need to know what a flood will destroy."

"That's what I said," Tuck said. "Undertakers."

"Tuck." Dominic's voice had changed. It was quieter and harder at the same time, the way it got when she talked about the Syndicate. "The buyout papers. Do you have any? Did your mother keep copies?"

"She kept everything. She said paper's the only weapon poor people get." He dug into the inner pocket of his coat, where he'd carried them since the crossing, folded small and wrapped in oilcloth because the river takes paper and his mother had taught him that too. Three pages. Offer letters, a map of the parcels, and a schedule—one page of dates, running through autumn, each date with a number of households beside it, counting down the village like a fuse.

Dominic took the pages and held them close to the firelight, and Tuck watched her face while she read. He'd spent six days learning that face. It mostly showed you weather, not feeling—storm light, all movement and no confession. But this broke through it. This came up through the floorboards slow and then all at once.

"This schedule," she said. "The final date. The full buyout completion." She turned the page toward the firelight so they could all see it. "That's three weeks after monsoon landfall."

"So?"

"So whoever wrote this schedule knows when the landfall is coming. Knows it down to the week." She looked up at Sefa. "Monsoon timing isn't public knowledge. Chasers like me guess at it. Governments estimate it. Nobody *schedules* around it unless they're the ones who'll be deciding when the water moves."

"The reservoir," Sefa said. Barely above the fire's crackle. "If the sluices run at capacity during landfall, the surge downstream—" She stopped. Tuck watched her do the arithmetic she didn't want to do. "Lowmark is in the surge path."

The stone in Tuck's chest turned to ice and then to fire, all in about four seconds. Because he'd known—of course he'd known, some part of him had known since the men with clipboards—that the village was in danger. But knowing a thing as a shape and hearing it said out loud by a woman who had helped build the machine that would do it were different animals entirely.

"My sister," he said. "My mom. There's four hundred people in Lowmark."

"I know," Dominic said.

"They signed because they thought the water was a maybe. A risk. Something they could take the money and run from." His voice was climbing and he let it climb. "It's not a maybe, is it. It's a schedule."

"No," Dominic said. "It's not a maybe." She folded the buyout pages along their old creases and handed them back to him, and she did it carefully, the way you hand something back when you understand what it weighs. "Tuck, I'm going to tell you something, and I want you to listen, because it matters." She picked up her father's compass and held it flat on her palm. "Six days ago I took a job because I owed money and the job was the only way to pay it. My reasons started and ended with me. That was the whole world—my ledger, my neck." She looked at the compass, then at him. "Then I found a logbook in my dead father's handwriting, and I thought that was my story too. Grief. Mine. Then Sefa's team. Hers. Then Bram's boat. His." She shook her head slowly. "I keep finding out the story is bigger than the person carrying it. And now there's a village."

"So what does that mean?" Tuck asked. "For me. Are you taking me back down?" He hated how young his voice sounded. "Because I'm not going back down. I'll jump and swim if I have to. I'll—"

"You're not going back down," Dominic said.

Bram made a sound from the shadows near the boat—not quite a laugh, not quite a groan. "Dom."

"Bram."

"You realize what you're saying. A boy. Monsoon season. Upstream of a surge path, on a river that breathes backward, with collectors behind us and Corricks somewhere on our flank, and now we're doing it *for* something." Bram took a long pull from the flask. "Doing it for yourself, you can cut losses. Doing it for a village, there's no cutting loose. You've just tied us to four hundred people we've never met."

"Yes," Dominic said.

"And you're sure?"

Tuck held his breath. He'd have sworn the fire held its breath too.

Dominic looked at the compass in her palm for a long moment. Then she looked at Tuck, and whatever she saw in his face—the heron bones, the oilcloth packet, the whole stubborn insufficient weight of him—she nodded once, like closing a ledger column.

"My father wrote something in the logbook," she said. "*Don't bring it down the mountain.* I've been trying to figure out what *it* means since I read it. The samples. The data. The claim." She closed her fist around the compass. "Maybe it means all of it. Maybe *it* is anything worth owning, and the warning is that ownership is how the mountain kills people." She stood up. "Either way, a company that schedules a village's drowning around a monsoon doesn't get to finish its purchase. Not while I'm on this river. That's not charity, Tuck. That's just math. Four hundred people is a bigger number than my debt."

"It's not about numbers," Tuck said, before he could stop himself.

Dominic blinked. "No," she admitted. "It's not. Bad math habit. I'm working on it."

* * *

Later, when the others had rolled into their bedrolls and the fire had burned down to a red eye, Bram came and sat next to Tuck on the deck rail without asking, which was how Bram did everything.

"Pilot's opinion," Bram said. "You're a fool for hiding in a crate. Crates shift. Cargo gets thrown overboard when it's overweight, and boats jettison cargo without checking inside it first. You could've died six times before day two."

"I know."

"But." Bram turned the flask in his fingers. "My boat got confiscated by the same paper. Cream stock, watermark and all. Took eight years off my life and my father's berth rights with it." He looked out at the black ribbon of the river. "So I understand wanting to fight paper with your body. It's a bad strategy. It's also the only one that feels like anything."

Tuck didn't say anything. Beside Bram, silence was allowed.

"Get some sleep," Bram said finally, standing. "Tomorrow we take the west fork. Smart way." He paused at the cabin door. "And kid—if we're doing this for your village, you're done being cargo. You eat at the fire, you stand watches, you learn lines. Crew doesn't hide in crates."

Tuck lay back on the deck boards after Bram went below, the oilcloth packet under his coat, over his heart, where it had ridden since the crossing. Above him the clouds had come up the valley all day, stacked and bruise-colored, and the stars were nearly gone now, and the air had that thick green taste it got before real rain.

Crew, he thought. He turned the word over like a coin he'd never owned before.

He was almost asleep when he heard it—Dominic and Sefa at the fire, voices low, the words drifting up to him in pieces. *...the schedule... landfall... if the sluices run at capacity...* And then Sefa, sharp with something that sounded like fear wearing anger's clothes: "Then we can't just outrun it, Dominic. We have to reach the source before the test. We have to reach it in five days, not nine."

A pause. The fire cracked.

"Then we'd better find out what this river can really do," Dominic said.

Tuck stared up at the bruised sky and pressed the packet against his chest, and for the first time since he'd climbed into the crate, he wasn't afraid of being thrown off.

He was afraid of what was ahead. But it was a clean fear, a fear with a shape and a reason, and it belonged to all of them now.

Five days. He closed his eyes and started counting.
