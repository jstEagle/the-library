## 11. The First Collector

The portage at Kettle Narrows was the kind of place where you learned what your crew was made of.

Two hundred meters of slick limestone between one stretch of river and the next, the water dropping through a chute so violent it looked like the escarpment had torn open and was bleeding white. No way through in the boats. Everything came out—crates, fuel, the gauge lashed under canvas in Bram's skiff—and went over on shoulders and backs, then down into the calm pool below.

Bram went first with the heavy end of the skiff. Sefa carried the instrument cases like they were sleeping children. Tuck hauled crates half his size, refusing help with the ferocity of a boy who'd been told too often he was small.

Dominic carried the gauge herself.

She hadn't let anyone else touch it since Widow's Bend. The data on it—the pulses, the backward breathing of the river—was the closest thing she had to her father's handwriting, and she carried it the way you carry something that might break or might bite.

Halfway across the portage path, she stopped.

Not because of anything she saw. Because of what she didn't hear.

* * *

She'd spent eleven years listening to storms the way other people listened to music, and somewhere in that time she'd picked up a second sense for the escarpment itself—the way it spoke in creaks and drips and the far-off grumble of water working through stone. A healthy gorge hummed. This one had gone quiet.

"Hey." She set the gauge down on a flat shelf of rock. "Hold up."

Bram eased his end of the skiff onto its runners and looked back upriver, squinting against the midday glare. "What?"

"The narrows. Listen."

They stood in the green shade of a fig tree gone sideways out of the cliff, four people holding their breath over two hundred meters of dead air. Tuck said the obvious thing first: "It stopped."

Rivers didn't stop. Not rivers like this one, running fat with pre-monsoon melt. Rivers like this roared or they died, and this one had done neither—it had simply paused, the way a man pauses when he hears someone coming up behind him.

Sefa's face did something complicated. Dominic watched her go from confusion to recognition to a fear so old it had calluses on it. "I've heard this before," Sefa said quietly. "The night the camp—" She cut herself off. "Something upstream is opening."

"Opening what?" Tuck asked.

"That's the question," Dominic said, and then the question answered itself, because the river came back.

Not gradually. All at once, like a held breath released, the narrows refilled with a sound that wasn't a roar so much as a shove—a wall of water slamming down the chute with debris riding its crest, whole trees cartwheeling, the pool below their feet surging up a meter in seconds and swallowing the lower third of the portage path.

And riding ahead of the flood, downstream of it, moving fast against all logic—

A boat.

* * *

It was a Syndicate boat. Dominic knew it before she could read a single letter on the hull, knew it by the geometry: low, black, over-engined, built by people who thought of rivers as debts to be collected. It came around the bend below the narrows running the flooded chute like it had been launched rather than piloted, and it swung hard toward the base of the portage path, and standing in the bow with one hand on a rail and the other shading his eyes was a man with binoculars and a Vellum Syndicate windbreaker worn open over civilian clothes, because even hunters liked to pretend they weren't hunting.

"There," Bram said. "That's a scout. That's not Ives."

"How can you tell?"

"Ives smiles. That one's working." Bram was already moving, shoving the skiff's runners toward the waterline, and his voice had dropped into the flat register he saved for decisions. "We launch now, in this, or we're caught on land with everything we own in crates."

"In *that*?" Sefa pointed at the chute still vomiting floodwater.

"In exactly that." Bram looked at Dominic. "Your call. You're the one who reads weather."

She looked upstream instead of down. The flood pulse was already cresting past them—the narrows were loud again but the water level had peaked and begun its slow surrender, which meant whatever sluice had opened upstream was closing, which meant she had maybe minutes of high fast water before the river settled back into something a scout could work in comfort.

Minutes of ugly water. Or hours of being cornered on a limestone shelf with a man who had a radio.

"We launch," she said. "Now. Everyone in. Tuck, you're ballast—sit low and hold on and don't be brave."

"I'm never brave," Tuck said, which was a lie, and everyone let him have it.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about running flooded water in an overloaded skiff with a boy and a defector and a pilot who talks to his engine like a horse he's trying not to spook:

It is the most alive you will ever feel, and you will hate yourself for feeling alive.

Bram took them into the tail of the flood pulse at an angle that made Sefa swear in three languages, and then the river took over the argument. The skiff surfed. There was no better word for it—they came down the throat of the narrows on the shoulder of the last of the surge, hull singing, spray going over the bow in sheets, and Bram stood at the tiller with his weight forward and his eyes doing the thing Dominic recognized from her own mirror: reading water the way she read sky, seeing the next five seconds everywhere at once.

"Left channel!" he shouted. "Rock garden—do *not* touch anything—I want the seam between the eddy and the main flow, it's narrow, trust me or pray, either works!"

They threaded it. Of course they threaded it; Bram had been born in a boat if the stories were true, and the stories were true because no one could have invented a man who steered like that and also folded his socks.

Behind them, the scout's boat came off the bank.

He was good. Dominic gave him that much. He read the same seam Bram had found, ran the same rock garden, and stayed with them through the first kilometer of chaos, close enough that she could see the radio handset clipped to his collar and the expression on his face, which was not anger or excitement but concentration—the clean, patient concentration of a man who believed the outcome was a formality.

"He's gaining," Sefa reported. She'd turned around in the bow despite every instruction ever given on a boat, watching the black hull eat the gap between them. "He's lighter. He's faster. He's going to run us down."

"Not here," Bram said. "But below the confluence, where it widens—" He didn't finish, because finishing would have meant saying *he catches us*, and Bram didn't say things like that out loud.

Dominic looked upstream.

This was the part she never told clients about, the part that made them think storm chasers were adrenaline junkies. It wasn't the chase. It was the arithmetic. Her whole career was arithmetic done at speed: how fast is the cell building, how long is the road, how many seconds between the lightning and the thunder and what does that gap buy me. She had once driven a client's car down a farm track with a wall of hail behind her and calculated, calmly, the exact moment to bail out and lie flat in a ditch, and she had bailed at that moment, and the car had survived, and the client had not paid her, and none of it had ever felt like fear. It felt like math.

So she did the math.

Flood pulse, decaying. Confluence, two kilometers. Below it, the valley opened and the scout's horsepower won forever. Unless.

"Sefa," she said. "The gauge data. The pulses. How far apart?"

Sefa understood instantly, the way she understood everything that came in numbers. She had the gauge case open before Dominic finished the sentence, laptop shielded under her body, rain jacket spread over both of them like a tent. "Nineteen minutes between the first two. Fourteen between the second pair. It's accelerating—or the machine's cycling faster."

"Which means the next pulse is due in—" Dominic checked her watch against the timestamps. "Eight minutes. Give or take."

"You want to outrun a flood using the flood," Bram said. Not a question. His grin arrived like weather.

"I want to be exactly where the flood is and exactly where he isn't, and those are the same place if we stop fighting the current and start scheduling it." She pointed at a bend ahead where the river kinked against a bluff—a natural choke point, a place where any pulse would pile up and backwater before breaking loose. "There. We eddy out above the bluff, we let him close the distance, and when the pulse comes through, we go out on its shoulder and he goes into it broadside, because he'll be looking at us and not at the water."

"And if the timing's wrong," Sefa said, "we die."

"If the timing's wrong, we get wet and he gets wet and we try again tomorrow," Dominic said. "I've been wrong about storms exactly enough times to know the difference between dangerous and fatal. This is dangerous."

Tuck, soaked to the ribs and glowing like a lamp, said, "Do it."

* * *

They tucked into the eddy above the bluff and killed the engine, and for ninety seconds Dominic experienced the strangest sensation of her life: hiding from a hunter inside a trap she'd set for both of them.

The scout closed. He was disciplined about it—no shooting, no shouting, just the steady approach of a man securing an asset. When he was fifty meters out, he raised a hand, palm out, and called across the water in a voice pitched to carry and calibrated to soothe.

"Dunmore! Vellum Recovery Services! Nobody needs to get hurt today—I just need you to heave to and show me your manifest!"

*Show me your manifest.* As if they were cargo. As if she were cargo. Something in Dominic's chest closed around that phrase and kept it, the way a splinter stays in.

"We're a salvage crew under contract!" she called back. "Check with your office!"

"I did!" And there it was—the smile, faint at this distance but unmistakable, the family resemblance to Ives that all these people seemed to share. "Your contract has a rider, Ms. Dunmore. Page nine. Everything you salvage belongs to the Syndicate. Including information." He gestured, almost gently, at the gauge case. "Hand that across and we'll call it a misunderstanding, and I'll write you up as cooperative, and cooperators do fine in this business. Ask anyone."

The water behind him began to rise.

Dominic saw it first—not the rise itself but the change in the scout's wake, the way his boat lifted slightly at the stern as the river beneath him tensed. She saw the bluff-side current reverse. She saw a log the size of a canoe come around the upstream bend standing on end, vertical, like something surfacing.

"Bram," she said.

"Seen it." Bram's hand was already on the starter cord. "Ten seconds. Tuck, down. Sefa, hold the cases. Dom—call it."

The scout had seen it too, finally, but he'd spent his life on Syndicate water, scheduled and dredged and polite, and he misread it. He thought it was a wave. He turned his bow to take it head-on, which was correct, except that a pulse in a choke point wasn't a wave—it was a door swinging open, and the river came through it sideways.

"Now," Dominic said.

Bram hit the cord and the engine caught on the first pull, because of course it did, and they slid out of the eddy onto the shoulder of the pulse at the precise instant the pulse decided to become a wall. The world went white and loud. The skiff climbed—actually climbed, bow up, Tuck whooping with a joy that would have horrified his mother—and for three full seconds they were flying on the crest of something the escarpment had exhaled, and then they were down the far side in the broken water beyond, spinning, shipping half the river over the gunwales, alive.

The scout's boat met the pulse broadside.

Dominic got one look at it through the spray: the black hull lifted and rolled, the scout in the water, his radio handset bobbing free, his boat pinwheeling away into the foam. Then the flood took even that view away, and they were through, and the river below the bluff was theirs.

"Man overboard," Sefa said. "Do we—"

Bram was already turning. They came back around in the slack water behind the pulse and found the scout clinging to a half-sunk crate, coughing, one hand still locked around a waterproof case he'd clearly chosen over his own grip on the boat. Dominic looked at him for a long moment. He looked back. Water ran off his chin and he didn't wipe it.

"Cooperators do fine," he said, and there was no smile left, only arithmetic of his own. "Runners get flagged."

Then he let go of the crate and swam for the bank, strong and unhurried, and Dominic understood that he'd never been in danger at all. Men like him didn't drown. Men like him were the current.

* * *

They ran hard for two hours, until the valley widened and the light went bronze and Bram finally eased off, and only then did anyone speak about the thing they were all not speaking about.

"He radioed," Sefa said. "Before the flood. I saw him key it twice."

"I know."

"So they know where we are. Where we were. They'll triangulate our heading, and every collector between here and the source is going to get a description of this boat and a very cheerful phone call from Ives."

"I know," Dominic said again.

Tuck, wringing out his shirt, asked the question with a child's terrible directness: "Does that mean we can't hide anymore?"

Dominic thought about it honestly, because the kid deserved honesty. For eleven years her entire strategy had been invisibility—no contracts in her own name, no fixed address, no photographs, a woman who chased the loudest things in the sky while making no sound at all. It had worked because she was small and the Syndicate was busy. But she'd signed Ives's paper, taken his job, followed his bait up a forbidden river, and led a parade of witnesses—Sefa, Bram, Tuck, a scout who'd seen her face from ten meters in good light.

"No," she said. "It doesn't mean we can't hide. It means hiding is over as a plan. From here on, hiding is just a tactic." She looked at each of them in turn. "There's a difference. A tactic you use when it helps. A plan is something you build your whole life around so you never have to risk anything. I did that for eleven years and it bought me a truck I don't own and a debt I didn't choose." She tapped the gauge case between her boots. "We're visible now. So we'd better be worth looking at."

Bram snorted. "That's the most inspiring thing anyone's ever said while bailing out a boat."

Sefa didn't laugh. She'd gone quiet in the way that meant her mind was somewhere else, and when Dominic glanced over, she had the survey maps unrolled across her knees despite the damp, her finger tracing the falsified contours, stopping at the blank spaces the government cartographers had filled with polite nothing.

"These zones," Sefa said slowly. "Every place the map says *survey restricted*. Every place marked forbidden." Her finger moved from blank space to blank space, and Dominic watched her line them up against the river's course, against the pulse timings, against the breathing of the escarpment. "They're not scattered. They're a shape."

"What shape?" Tuck asked.

Sefa looked up, and her face was pale in the bronze light.

"A reservoir," she said. "The biggest one nobody's ever built. And I think your father drew the real map, Dominic. I think it's hidden inside these lies."

Upstream, faint as a heartbeat, the river pulsed again—right on schedule, fourteen minutes, the machine keeping its own time.

Dominic put the throttle down and aimed them at it.
