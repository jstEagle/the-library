## 12. Maps That Lie Politely

The rain started an hour before dawn, the polite kind, the kind that asks permission instead of taking. Sefa sat in the bow of Bram's boat under a tarp rigged between two poles and spread the survey maps across a crate of dried rice, weighing the corners with river stones because there was no wind worth trusting and she had learned, over four years with the Directorate Survey Corps, that trust was a thing you extended to objects before you extended it to people.

There were eleven maps. Official issue, Directorate cartography stamp, the blue-black ink that never smudged even when you sweated on it. She had drawn three of them herself.

That was the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed hook. She had drawn them, and she had known, even then, that they were lies. Not sloppy lies. Not the lies of a lazy draftsman. Polite lies. The kind a government tells when it wants you to feel informed rather than deceived. Every contour was accurate. Every elevation was true. The river was the river. The escarpment was the escarpment. And threaded through all of that honest geography, like veins of something dark in good marble, were the zones marked in the pale hatched red that every surveyor in the country was trained to read as one word: *forbidden*.

Restricted hydrological interest. That was the phrase. She had written it herself on Map Seven, in her neat, upright hand, the hand her mother had drilled into her with a ruler and a switch. *Restricted hydrological interest. No survey access without Directorate authorization.*

She had believed it meant something. A fragile cave system. A sacred site the government wanted to keep off-limits to salvage crews and tourists. Maybe a contaminated aquifer. Governments always had reasons, and the reasons always sounded like reasons, and that was the trick of it — a lie with a good explanation attached is more durable than any truth, because people will defend it for you.

She had believed it right up until the night in the gorge, and then she had believed nothing, and she had run, and she had been running for eleven days when a storm chaser with tired eyes and a compass she kept touching like a rosary had looked at Sefa's Directorate credentials and said, *You're either useful or you're a liability, and I'd rather you be useful.*

So. Useful. She spread the maps and made herself useful.

Tuck crouched at the edge of the tarp, dripping, pretending not to watch. The boy had a genius for pretending not to watch. He had been pretending not to watch since the crossing, when Dominic had found him wedged under the tarpaulin in the cargo hold with his knees to his chest and a look on his face that dared her to make him leave. Sefa had argued for leaving him. She still wasn't sure she'd been wrong. She was only sure that the argument had been lost, and that the boy ate like a starling and asked questions like a surveyor, and that both of those things were starting to feel less like problems.

"Those are the ones from the camp?" Tuck said.

"Some of them. Some are mine." She said it flat, and she watched him decide not to ask which were which. Smart boy. She pointed at the red hatching on Map Seven, then Nine, then Eleven. "You see how much of the upper escarpment is forbidden?"

"Half of it."

"More than half. Add them up." She made him do it, made him trace the boundaries with a wet finger and total the area in his head, because she wanted him to see it the way she was seeing it, which was slowly, the way a bad smell becomes undeniable. "Sixty-one percent of the catchment above the gorge. Restricted. Now ask yourself what kind of cave system needs sixty-one percent of a river's catchment to itself."

Tuck frowned. His village was downstream, past the Bend, in the green country where the river went wide and slow and generous. She watched him do the arithmetic that every downstream kid learns before they learn their times tables: what happens upstream happens to you.

"Nothing natural," he said.

"Nothing natural," Sefa agreed. "Now watch."

She took out her knife — Directorate issue, another thing she'd carried out of the camp on the night she ran, another thing she'd told herself was just equipment and not evidence — and she held Map Seven up against the gray light. And she did the thing she had done once, drunk, in the survey tent at Karst Camp, as a joke, and had never been able to stop seeing afterward.

She folded it.

Not in half. Along the red boundaries. The hatched zones were shaped strangely, irregular, like spilt things — that was part of the politeness, the deliberate ugliness of them, the way they looked like bureaucratic afterthoughts rather than designs. But fold along the boundaries, and bring the zones together, and the irregularities canceled. The spilt shape became a shape. A long, branching, engineered shape, following the high ground above the gorge, with a spine and ribs and a head.

"A reservoir," Tuck said.

"A reservoir system." Sefa's voice came out steadier than she felt. "Plural. Terraced. Look — the spine runs here, along the karst shelf, and each of these forbidden zones is a basin. They aren't hiding a place on the map, they're hiding a *machine* on the map, and they're hiding it in pieces so no single zone looks like anything." She set the folded map down. "Every forbidden zone is a lie told politely. The truth is only visible if you refuse to read the map the way they printed it."

Bram came up from the stern, wiping his hands on a rag that had started life white. The pilot had the gift of arriving exactly when a silence needed filling or breaking. He looked at the folded map for a long moment, and Sefa saw him do what river pilots do, which was translate geometry into water.

"That's a lot of storage," he said. "That's — " He stopped, and started again, and Sefa understood that Bram Halvard was a man who almost never said a number he hadn't checked twice. "That's enough water to drown every village from the Bend to the delta. If it all came down at once."

"If it all came down at once," Sefa said, "it wouldn't come down as water. It would come down as a decision."

The rain thickened. Under the tarp, the three of them looked at the folded map, at the shape of the thing that had eaten a survey team, and nobody said the obvious thing, which was that the Syndicate wanted this, that Ives's cheerful salvage job had been a leash with Dominic on the other end of it, and that they were rowing toward the machine instead of away from it.

*

Dominic came back from the treeline with wet hair and that way she had of moving like someone who expected the ground to be an ambush. She'd been checking the backtrail again. She'd been checking it every hour since the camp, since the second set of boat wakes they'd found at the crossing, since the word *Ives* had started meaning *behind you* instead of *behind me*.

"Talk to me," Dominic said.

Sefa unfolded the maps and showed her.

She watched the storm chaser read them, and it was a different thing from watching Bram read them or watching Tuck read them. Bram read for water. Tuck read for home. Dominic read the way Sefa had once read, before — looking for the author. Looking for the hand.

"Show me that again," Dominic said. "The fold."

Sefa folded Map Seven along the red lines. Dominic took it, turned it over, held it up to the light, and Sefa saw her jaw change.

"There's writing," Dominic said. "In the margin. Under the fold."

"There's nothing — " Sefa began, and then stopped, because there was. There was writing in the margin under the fold, in a fine, dry, slanted hand, in pencil, the kind of pencil that doesn't photograph and doesn't smudge and doesn't officially exist. She had handled this map a hundred times. She had *drawn* this map. She had never seen the margin, because the margin only existed when the map was folded the wrong way, folded into truth, and no one at the Directorate had ever folded it that way because no one at the Directorate had ever been looking for the lie.

She took the map back with hands that were not quite steady.

The pencil writing ran along the inside of the fold, following the spine of the reservoir system like a path following a ridge. It wasn't labeled. It wasn't signed. It was a route — bearings, distances, notes on water level and footing, a warning about a channel that floods at dusk — and threaded through the route, at intervals, a small symbol Sefa knew from a hundred field documents.

A compass rose. Tiny, precise, drawn freehand with the north point elongated, like a needle.

She looked up. Dominic had gone very still, in the way of people standing at the edge of something they've been falling toward for a long time.

"That's his," Dominic said. Not a question.

"You're sure."

"He drew that rose on everything. My homework. Grocery lists. The back of my hand, once, when I was six, so I'd always know which way was home." Dominic's voice was level in the way that ice is level. "He's been dead for six years. Officially."

Sefa looked down at the margin, at the route penciled through the machine's spine, and did the dating in her head, and hated herself for doing it, and did it anyway. The ink of the printed map was Directorate stock, batch-coded, and she knew the batch codes the way she knew her own name. Map Seven had been printed in the spring of the survey. The annotations were on top of the print. The annotations were *newer than the print*, newer than the forbidden zones, newer than the lie — which meant they had been drawn by someone who had seen the lie and known it for one, and had chosen, quietly, in pencil, to leave a thread through it.

"He wasn't just backing the survey," Sefa said slowly. "He was inside it. He saw what they were building. And he couldn't stop it, so he — " she touched the tiny compass rose — "he left a way through. For whoever came after."

The rain drummed on the tarp. Tuck had gone quiet in the way of a boy who has just watched two adults grieve and doesn't know the etiquette yet, because there is no etiquette.

Dominic sat down on the rice crate. She took out her compass — the brass one, the one she touched like a rosary — and held it next to the penciled rose in the margin, and the two needles pointed the same way, and something in her face closed and opened at the same time, which Sefa had not known a face could do.

"He knew I'd come looking," Dominic said. "Someday. He didn't know when. So he wrote it where only someone looking for the lie would find it." She laughed, one dry note with no floor under it. "He hid the truth inside the forgery. That is the most him thing I have ever seen."

"Your father was a surveyor?"

"My father was a *hydrologist*. Before the debts. Before he started taking money from people who smile." Dominic folded the map along the lines herself, once, twice, committing it. "The Syndicate thinks they sent me up this river as their tracker. They think I'm following their salvage manifest." She looked up, and Sefa saw the tiredness burn off her like fog off water. "I'm following him."

*

They spent the day on the maps, all four of them, and it was the first day since the camp that Sefa did not feel like a woman running. She laid them out in sequence, Seven through Eleven, and folded each one along its red boundaries, and the machine assembled itself out of polite lies piece by piece: the terraced basins, the sluice channels marked as *unstable karst*, the monitoring stations marked as *abandoned homesteads*, the great buried spine of it running up toward the source the official maps simply declined to depict, as if the river began nowhere, as if water came from accounting.

Dominic's father's route threaded through all of it. At each junction there was a rose, and beside each rose a few words in that fine dry hand, and the words were never instructions, exactly. They were the things you'd say to someone walking behind you on a trail. *Loose shale, keep to the left wall.* *Water rises fast here after rain — don't camp.* *If the sluices are open, wait. It breathes. Let it breathe.*

*It breathes.* Sefa read that one four times, and each time the sound came back to her, the sound from the night in the gorge, the sound like the river inhaling, and her hands went cold on the map, and she let them go cold, because the cold was true and she was done flinching from true things.

Tuck copied the route onto a scrap of oilcloth in his careful, unformed hand, and asked questions that were better than most Directorate juniors' questions. Bram studied the water notes and muttered about draft and current and the nine days of unforgiving river between them and the source, and once, quietly, looking at the note that said *don't camp below the third basin*, he said, "Whoever he was, he piloted," and Sefa thought that was the closest thing to a eulogy the man was going to get, and that it was a good one.

Toward evening the rain stopped being polite. It came in sideways off the escarpment, and they bundled the maps into oilskin, and Sefa held the folded seventh map a moment longer before she gave it up — the lie and the truth folded into one another, the red hatching and the pencil thread, the machine and the way through it.

She had drawn that map. She had written *restricted hydrological interest* in her upright hand and believed it meant something. She could not undo that. But the pencil in the margin had undone it for her, and there was a strange, uncomfortable mercy in that: the dead man had corrected her work, six years after she'd made it, and the correction was a road.

"Dominic," she said, as the storm chaser banked the cookfire against the wet. "The notes. They stop."

Dominic looked over.

"The route goes all the way to the source, but the annotations thin out past the fifth basin. The last one is — " Sefa unfolded the corner one more time, though she knew it by heart now, though she had read it until the pencil gleamed. "The last one says: *If you're reading this, they've started the test. Don't follow my route. Follow the river. And whatever you find at the top — it was never meant to be owned.*"

The fire hissed. Downstream, somewhere in the dark, the river ran on toward all the villages that didn't know what was being built above them.

"And after that?" Dominic asked.

"After that, nothing. The handwriting just stops." Sefa folded the map away and looked up at the escarpment, black against the raining sky, at the hidden spine of the machine sleeping somewhere in it. "Which means either he stopped writing —"

"— or someone stopped him," Dominic said.

Nobody answered that. Nobody had to. In the morning they would row upstream, along a dead man's pencil line, toward the place where the handwriting ended.
