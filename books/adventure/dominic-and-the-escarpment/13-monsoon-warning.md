## 13. Monsoon Warning

Dominic felt it before she saw it, the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck before you hear footsteps.

She was standing knee-deep in the shallows at the base of the third portage, wringing river water out of a rope that didn't need wringing, when the light changed. Not darkened—changed. The green of the gorge walls went silver at the edges, and the air, which had been pressing down all morning like a wet blanket, lifted half an inch and went still.

Stillness was worse than wind. Stillness was the sky taking a breath.

"Everyone out of the water," she said. "Now."

Tuck looked up from where he was skipping stones off a flat rock, his face doing the thing it did when he was deciding whether to obey or negotiate. He read her voice instead of her words, which was more than most adults managed, and dropped the rock and waded for shore.

Bram was already moving. That was one of the things Dominic had decided to trust about him: he didn't ask why when the weather spoke first. He had the boat hauled up the bank and tied to a root system thick as a fist before Sefa even looked up from the survey maps she'd been cross-referencing against her father's annotations.

"What is it?" Sefa asked.

Dominic didn't answer right away. She climbed onto the flat shelf of limestone above the landing and stood there with her face up, letting the sky talk to her the way it had talked to her since she was nine years old and her father put a barometer in her hands and said, *This is just a machine that agrees with what your skin already knows.*

The clouds were wrong. That was the whole of it, and the whole of it was bad. Monsoon systems came up the escarpment in layers—you could watch them build for days, a slow gray staircase climbing the horizon, and everyone downstream planned their lives around that staircase. Ten days, Bram had said at the last crossing. Ten days of water left before landfall, maybe eleven if the high pressure held over the coast. They'd been budgeting their ascent against that number like coins in a pocket.

But the clouds overhead weren't building. They were arriving. A deck of them, low and fast-moving from the southeast, moving against the wind at ground level—the classic lie, the signature she'd chased across a hundred miles of flats for clients who paid late. Upper-level flow outrunning surface flow meant the storm's engine was already spinning somewhere offshore, already organized, already feeding. And underneath the deck, those towering cumulus heads going up like smoke columns, hard and vertical, not the lazy afternoon variety but the kind that meant convection with an agenda.

"How long have we got?" Bram asked. He'd come up beside her without her hearing him, which should have been impossible for a man his size, and stood with his arms crossed, watching her watch the sky.

Dominic did the math she didn't want to do.

Six days. Maybe five and a half. Landfall in six days, not ten, and every hour after that the escarpment channels would start eating themselves—flash floods in the narrows, portages underwater, the whole route Bram had mapped turning into a chute with them inside it.

"Six," she said. "Maybe less."

* * *

They argued about it around a fire they almost didn't light, because smoke was visibility and visibility was Syndicate scouts, but the evening had gone cold enough and wet enough that morale required flame, so they built it small and shielded in a hollow behind a fallen karst slab, and they argued by its orange inch of light.

"We turn back." Sefa said it first, and she said it like someone setting down something heavy. "We are four people with two boats and a boy, six days from anything, and the biggest storm system in three years is about to sit on this escarpment like a lid. Every professional instinct I have says go down."

"Your professional instincts signed off on a survey team that walked into a gorge and vanished," Tuck said.

Sefa flinched. It was small, but the firelight caught it, and Dominic saw her swallow whatever she'd been about to say back. The boy had a talent for finding the soft place under the armor. Nobody had taught him that. Some kids just grew up where the soft places showed.

"That's not fair," Sefa said quietly.

"No," Dominic said. "It isn't. But he's not wrong either."

Bram fed a stick into the fire. He was a man who spoke about once per argument, and everyone had learned to wait for it. "The route above Widow's Bend has two sections I wouldn't run in flood," he said. "If we're on them when the monsoon hits, we die in the water. If we're sheltered when it hits, we wait it out and lose days we don't have. Either way, turning back doesn't fix it. Turning back just means we die closer to home."

"So we push on," Tuck said.

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't say anything."

"I said turning back doesn't fix it." Bram looked at Dominic then, and she understood that he was handing her something, and that she could refuse it, and that refusing it would cost all of them. "You're the one who reads the sky. Sky says six days. River says nine. Somebody has to decide which number we live by."

And there it was. The thing she'd spent thirty-four years avoiding—the exact weight her father had carried, the reason he'd faked his own death rather than keep holding it. Leadership wasn't a title. It was being the person everyone watched when the numbers disagreed.

"I'm not your captain," she said.

"No," Sefa agreed. "You're worse. You're the one who's usually right."

* * *

Dominic took the first watch alone, because she needed to be alone with the sky, and because she'd learned long ago that decisions made in company were really just decisions made by whoever talked loudest, and she refused to let that be how they died.

She sat on the limestone shelf with her father's compass open in her palm. The needle swung lazily, unbothered, pointing at nothing useful. She thought about him up here somewhere—years of him, in a room she hadn't found yet, writing letters he never sent—and she thought about the version of him who must have sat exactly here, on some shelf exactly like this one, reading a sky exactly like this one, and chosen to stay.

"Why," she said out loud, to no one. "Why would you stay."

The sky didn't answer. The sky never answered questions, only gave data.

Data: the cloud deck had thickened since sunset. Data: the temperature hadn't dropped the way it should have; the air was holding its heat like a held breath. Data: somewhere far to the southeast, lightning flickered inside the cloud mass, silent from this distance, sheet-light strobing the underside of the deck like a pulse under skin. The storm wasn't coming. The storm was *here*, in its early form, and everything between now and landfall was just the opening act.

She ran the options the way she'd run chase intercepts, cold and fast.

Option one: turn back. Nine days down, six days of margin. They'd be in the flooded lowlands when the monsoon hit, which meant the crossing at Widow's Bend in full spate, which meant drowning with better scenery. And even if they made it—even if they came down the mountain alive—they came down with nothing. No source. No truth. Tuck's village bought and drowned on paper, Sefa's team erased forever, her father's letters rotting in a room nobody would ever find. The debt unpaid. The knot untied.

Option two: hold here. Shelter in place, wait out the storm, move after. Safe-ish. Also dead in the water, because Ives knew where they were, and the Corrick brothers knew roughly where they were, and six days of sitting still was six days of gift-wrapped targets. Besides, the monitoring station her father's annotations pointed toward was upstream, not here. Holding meant abandoning the only shelter worth having.

Option three.

Option three was insane, and she kept circling back to it anyway, the way your tongue finds a broken tooth.

Go forward. Hard. Faster than they'd ever moved. Nine days of river compressed into six, running sections Bram called unforgivable, portaging through the night, gambling everything on reaching the monitoring station—a buried structure, solid, storm-proof, built by engineers—before the sky came down. Her father had lived there. Engineers built things to survive exactly this. It was the only strongroom on the mountain, and it was upstream.

The storm had collapsed the deadline. Fine. Then the plan had to collapse too, and rebuild itself smaller and harder.

She went back down to the fire. Three faces turned toward her—Bram's unreadable, Sefa's guarded, Tuck's wide open, the boy unable to hide a single thing he felt, which was either his greatest weakness or his greatest protection, she hadn't decided.

"Here's what I know," she said. "Turning back puts us in the worst water on this river at peak flood, with the Syndicate behind us and no shelter ahead. Staying here makes us a target with a deadline. Going forward gets us to my father's station—a concrete bunker built to ride out exactly this—before landfall, if, and only if, we move like we mean it. Night portages. No rest days. Everyone pulls."

Silence. The fire cracked. Somewhere up the gorge, thunder muttered, the first real voice of it, still hours away but audible now, like a dog that can hear a whistle nobody else can.

"If we miss the window," Sefa said, "we're in the narrows when it hits."

"Yes."

"And if we make the window, we're trapped in a hole in the ground with a Syndicate enforcement detail somewhere behind us."

"Also yes."

Tuck raised his hand, which was such a schoolboy gesture in such an unholy place that Bram actually snorted. "I vote forward," the boy said. "My village is downstream of that reservoir. If the machine floods it, I want to be standing next to the off switch when it happens."

Nobody laughed. It wasn't funny. It was also, Dominic thought, the cleanest moral arithmetic anyone had offered all night.

"Bram?"

The pilot looked into the fire for a long moment. "I've run water that didn't forgive," he said finally. "Lost a boat doing it. Lost a crewmate doing it." His jaw worked. "But I've never lost one running away from something that mattered. Forward."

"Sefa?"

She wrapped her arms around her knees. When she spoke, her voice was steady, and Dominic recognized the steadiness for what it was—the same forged metal her own calm was made from, guilt hammered until it held an edge. "The team I ran from never got to choose. I'll spend the choice they didn't get. Forward."

Four votes, and the last one was hers, and everyone knew it, and everyone waited.

Dominic looked up at the sky one more time. The deck had swallowed the stars. Lightning pulsed inside it, patient, rhythmic, a heart beating somewhere above the world.

Her whole life she'd been the woman who read the storm and told other people where it was going, and then went home alone. Reading the storm was safe. You could love the weather from a distance. But her father had taught her the other lesson too, the one written in the margins of every logbook page: *the sky doesn't care what you choose, so choose like it matters.*

"Forward," she said. "We break camp at first light. We run the narrows tomorrow while the river's still low, and we don't stop until we see concrete."

Tuck grinned. Sefa closed her eyes like someone accepting a sentence. Bram nodded once and started kicking dirt over the fire, because in his language, a decision made was a fire out.

Dominic stayed up another hour after the others slept, watching the lightning walk closer across the southern sky, counting seconds between flash and thunder. Twenty. Fifteen. Eleven.

The storm was coming faster than any of them could move.

But so, starting at dawn, were they.
