## 14. The Rival Crew

Bram counted them by their wake before he ever saw the hull.

Two boats, running dark, a half-mile back in the flooded timber where honest pilots wouldn't push. They'd been there since midafternoon—Bram had felt it first as a wrongness in the current, that faint stutter of displaced water when another prop turns somewhere upstream of you. Thirty years on the escarpment rivers and his skin still knew it before his eyes did. Somebody was reading their water.

He didn't say anything to Dominic right away. She was up in the bow with the gauge crate open across her knees, Sefa leaning over her shoulder, both of them arguing happily about pressure pulses like a pair of schoolgirls with a dead bird. Tuck was asleep against the fuel drums, mouth open, one hand still curled around the towline he refused to let go of even unconscious. It was the best hour they'd had since the crossing at Widow's Bend, and Bram figured he'd give them the whole hour before he poisoned it.

But the wake didn't go away. That was the thing about shadows—they kept your schedule better than you did.

He eased the throttle down a notch, just enough to change their rhythm, and watched the water behind them. A long count. Then the stutter came again, matching him, correcting. Not following the river. Following *him*.

"Dom," he said quietly.

She looked up, and whatever she saw in his face made her close the gauge crate without argument. That was one thing he'd learned about her in nine days of water that didn't forgive: she could read a man's silence faster than most people read print.

"Two boats," Bram said. "Back in the timber. They've matched my throttle twice."

"Syndicate?"

"Wrong shape." He nodded toward the stern, where the second boat rode low under its tarp. "Ives's crews run Vellum gray, government registration stickers, all very tidy. These are running stripped. No lights, no plates, no registration." He spat over the side, an old habit that meant he was thinking. "That's salvage money. That's men who bought their own boats because nobody reputable would insure them."

Sefa had gone very still. "The Corricks," she said.

"You know them?"

"Everyone from the survey knows them." Her voice had flattened into something Bram hadn't heard from her before—not fear exactly, but the memory of it, worn smooth like a stone she'd carried too long. "Rennick and Ode Corrick. They do contract work the ministries don't want on any paperwork. When our team's permits got pulled in the third month, the rumor was the Corricks were the reason. Somebody wanted us watched. Somebody wanted to know what we were finding before we reported it."

"And now somebody wants to know what we're finding," Dominic said.

"Same somebody," Sefa said. "Whoever inside the government made my team vanish."

Nobody said anything for a while. The river talked instead—that low escarpment mutter it made when the channel narrowed and the karst underneath started pulling at the keel. Bram worked the wheel through a bend he knew by the sound of it, a bend that would look like suicide to anyone who didn't have his map memorized in his spine, and felt a small mean satisfaction when the shadow boats fell back rather than follow.

They wouldn't fall back far. Men like the Corricks didn't lose you; they just let you think you'd lost them, so you'd relax, so you'd lead them somewhere worth going. Bram had done the same trick himself, once, in another life, for another employer whose name he didn't say anymore either.

*Enemies as mirrors,* his old pilot master used to call it. *Every man hunting you is hunting the version of himself he couldn't afford to be.*

* * *

They made camp at dusk in a limestone pocket off the main channel—a slot canyon mouth wide enough for both boats, walled high enough that a fire wouldn't show from the water. Bram ran the anchor lines double and set Tuck on first watch mostly to keep the boy from arguing about being sent away from it. The kid had a way of hearing things in the dark that Bram trusted more than any instrument.

Dominic cooked. She cooked the way she did everything—efficiently, silently, like someone paying down a debt even to a pot of rice—and they ate in the firelight while the escarpment stood black against a sky bruising purple with monsoon buildup.

"We can't outrun them tomorrow," Bram said finally. "Not loaded, not in this water. They know these channels or they wouldn't have found us. So we don't run."

"So what do we do?" Sefa asked.

"We talk to them."

Dominic's head came around fast. "Talk to the men who've been hunting us?"

"I've been hunted before," Bram said. "And I've been the hunter. And I'll tell you what I learned: hunters are tired too. Rennick Corrick has been on this river as long as I have. He's got brothers to feed and a payroll that only pays if there's a payout, and whoever hired him—" he tapped the gunwale, "—whoever made Sefa's team disappear isn't the kind of employer who pays survivors. Ask me how I know."

Sefa flinched. Bram pretended not to see it, which was its own kind of courtesy.

"The point is," he went on, "men like that aren't loyal. They're *employed*. There's a difference, and the difference is negotiable."

"They tried to run us into Widow's Bend," Dominic said flatly. "Tuck almost drowned."

"No," Bram said. "Somebody *signaled* at Widow's Bend—I've been thinking about it. Those lantern flashes on the bluff weren't Syndicate. Wrong color, wrong rhythm. Salvage code, old-style. They were marking the hazard for us." He shrugged. "Or marking it so we'd think they did. Can't tell yet. But a crew that wanted us dead doesn't warn you about the water that'll kill you. Dead clients don't lead you to the source."

"That's comforting," Sefa said dryly.

"It's not supposed to be comforting. It's supposed to be *useful*." He stood, stretched his bad knee, and looked at each of them in turn. "Here's how it goes tonight. I take the skiff up-channel alone, no weapons showing, and I hail them. Rennick and I have drunk out of the same bottle before, years back, before either of us was owned. He'll hear me out. What comes after that—" he spread his hands, "—that's the river's business."

"You're not going alone," Dominic said.

"It's safer alone."

"Everything's safer alone. That's why we're not doing it." She said it without heat, which somehow made it harder to argue with. "You taught me the rules of this river, Bram. Rule one: nobody runs a channel solo that two can run together. This is a channel."

He looked at her for a long moment—the storm chaser who'd come aboard invisible and debt-ridden and had somehow, day by day, become the person the rest of them arranged themselves around without ever voting on it.

"Rule two," he said, giving in. "If shooting starts, you get behind me, because I'm wider."

* * *

The parley happened at moonrise, mid-channel, in the dead water below a bluff the locals called the Deacon's Teeth.

Bram brought the skiff out with a single white lamp burning—parley light, older than any government on the escarpment—and the Corrick boats came in from the timber with their own lamps lit, which was answer enough for now. Three men in the lead boat. Rennick Corrick at the wheel, big and gray-bearded, with a face like something the river had rejected twice. His brother Ode beside him, younger, thinner, watchful in the way of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. A third Bram didn't know, young, hands too near the rifle laid across his knees.

"Bram Hollis," Rennick said. "Thought you were dead."

"Everybody thinks that. It's useful." Bram held his lamp steady. "You've been on my wake for two days, Rennick."

"Been on the river two days. River's public."

"River's public. Your employers' business in it isn't." He let that sit. "Who is it this time? Ministry? Land office? Some deputy director who needed a survey team to stop existing?"

Something moved behind Rennick's eyes—quick, quickly buried. Ode shifted on the thwart.

"You always did ask questions with answers in them," Rennick said.

"Because I already know them. I just need to hear which lie you've chosen." Bram leaned forward on the oars, keeping his voice level, conversational, the voice of a man with nowhere else to be. "Here's what I know, Rennick. You were hired to shadow a salvage job. Fine. Old work. But the job changed. The woman running it found something upstream that your employer killed six people to bury—including, I'd wager, the surveyor who signed your original briefing. And your employer hasn't called you off, which means they intend for this to end the same way the last one did. And I'll tell you something else for free." He paused. "Vellum Syndicate enforcement is already on this river. You've seen their boats. You're not partners to them, Rennick. You're *witnesses*. And witnesses to Vellum business have short careers."

Silence, except the river working at the bluff below.

Ode spoke first, quiet, to his brother. "He's not wrong about the gray boats. We clocked them at the second portage. Enforcement detail, full kit. Nobody told us Syndicate muscle was in play."

"Nobody tells us anything," Rennick said. "That's the arrangement."

"Then maybe the arrangement's expired."

Rennick turned on his brother with a flash of real anger—family anger, the kind that has nowhere else to go—and Bram watched the two of them and understood the whole shape of the Corrick operation in that one exchange. Rennick was the loyalty; Ode was the ledger. Rennick kept faith with employers because faith was all he had left of being an honest man. Ode kept accounts, and the accounts had stopped balancing.

"Your boss," Bram said gently, into the middle of it, "is the same kind of boss mine was. The kind that pays in full only once."

The young one's hand drifted toward the rifle. Rennick put two fingers on the barrel and pushed it down without looking, the way you'd calm a dog.

"Say what you came to say, Hollis."

"I came to say: truce. We run ahead, you run behind, nobody shoots at anybody, and when this ends—however it ends—you'll have seen exactly what your employer is worth. Then you decide who you work for. That's all. I'm not buying you. I'm not flattering you. I'm telling you the water ahead is worse than anything behind us, and the monsoon's coming early, and dead men make poor witnesses."

Rennick stared at him for a long time. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—one short bark, no humor in it, all exhaustion.

"You always were a sermon in a boat." He rubbed his beard. "Truce. But hear me, Hollis, and hear me plain. I don't trust you. I don't trust your storm-witch, I don't trust the deserter, and I especially don't trust the boy, because children on rivers mean trouble that hasn't happened yet. If it turns out you're sitting on something worth selling, I'll sell it out from under you and sleep fine."

"Wouldn't expect less," Bram said. "And if it turns out your employer offers you more to sink us than we're worth standing, you'll take it. Also fine. That's not betrayal between men like us, Rennick. That's just weather."

Ode almost smiled. Almost.

"One condition," Rennick said. "When you find whatever it is up there—if you find it—we get eyes on it first. Before Syndicate, before ministry, before anybody. My brothers eat either way, but we eat *informed*."

"Done," Bram said. "Informed and alive. Best terms on the river."

They backed water apart, lamps swinging, and the last thing Rennick called across the black water was:

"Hollis! The gray boats turned upriver past the Teeth an hour ago. Heading for the gorge. Whatever your dead man built up there—they're already going to it."

* * *

Bram rowed back to camp slow, letting the news settle in his arms before he had to settle it in his voice.

They took it the way he knew they would. Dominic went quiet and started packing before he finished talking, because that was how she processed fear—into motion. Sefa sat staring into the fire with the logbook in her lap, whispering coordinates. Tuck, awake now, listening from the dark the way he did, asked the question none of the adults had asked:

"If the Syndicate gets there first, does it matter what we do?"

Bram looked at the boy a long moment. "It matters what we do," he said. "That's the only part that's ever ours."

They broke camp by lamplight. As Bram cast off the second line, he glanced back once at the Deacon's Teeth, where the Corrick lamps had already gone out—both boats vanished into the timber like they'd never been, running dark again, running behind them, running *with* them, and no map on the escarpment could tell you which.

Ahead, far up the black gorge, thunder rolled with no lightning attached. Bram had lived on this river long enough to know that sound wasn't weather.

It was machinery.
