## 15. The Inhaled Gorge

We smelled the gorge before we saw it.

That was the first wrong thing. Rivers smell like rivers—silt and green rot and the mineral cold of stone—but this was something else, something metallic and faintly warm, like the air over a struck match that hasn't caught yet. Bram noticed it at the same moment I did. He cut the engine to a low burble and stood in the bow with his head cocked, listening the way other people look.

"Smell that?" I asked.

"Been smelling it for an hour." He didn't turn around. "Thought it was my boat."

"It's not your boat."

"No," he said quietly. "It's not."

Sefa came forward from the mid-deck where she'd been hunched over the survey maps all morning, cross-referencing my father's annotations against the official charts until her eyes went red-rimmed. Tuck trailed her like a shadow she'd stopped trying to shake off. The boy had been quiet since the portage—since the scout's radio crackle that meant every hour of our invisibility we'd bought and bargained for was spent.

"That's it," Sefa said, pointing ahead to where the river bent north into a wall of grey limestone. "The gorge. What we called—" She stopped herself. Whatever they'd called it on the team, she wasn't ready to say it out loud yet.

"Take us in slow," I told Bram.

"I was going to take us in slow anyway." He eased the throttle down to a crawl. "I don't take fast water fast. That's how you die polite."

The walls rose around us as we entered, sheer and dripping, close enough in places that Tuck could have touched them from the gunwale if he'd dared lean out. The light went green-grey, filtered through mist that hung in the gorge like breath held too long. My father's compass sat open in my palm, needle steady, pointing not north but upriver—the way it had been doing, off and on, for two days. I'd told myself it was a local anomaly. Iron in the karst. A surveyor's trick of terrain.

I was running out of ways to tell myself things.

* * *

The sound started as a pressure change more than a noise. A fullness in the ears, a shifting in the chest, like the moment before lightning hits close enough to taste. Then the river did something my eyes refused for three full seconds to accept.

It stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped. The whole surface of the Karst River, all that brown muscle and momentum we'd been fighting and riding for nine days, went glass-still between the limestone walls, as if someone upstream had put a hand flat against it and said *wait*.

Then it began to move backward.

Tuck made a small sound, half word, half prayer. Sefa gripped the rail with both hands and whispered, "There it is. There it is, oh God, there it is," and I understood she wasn't seeing it for the first time. She was seeing it again, and the first time had never left her.

The current reversed. Slowly at first, then with gathering purpose, eddies spinning in reverse, foam sliding back upstream toward its own source. Our boat, engine idling, began to drift stern-first downriver, gently, like a leaf returning to the branch. Bram killed the engine entirely and let it happen. His face was unreadable, which for Bram meant he was reading everything.

"How deep does this go?" he asked no one in particular.

"The river inhales," Sefa said. Her voice had gone flat, recitation-flat, the voice of someone who'd rehearsed this testimony in the dark for months. "That's what I wrote in my report before I burned my copy. The river inhales. Every ninety seconds or so, give or take. It pulls back—God knows how much water, thousands of tons—and holds. And then—"

She didn't finish because then it happened.

The exhale.

The river *surged*. Not downstream—upward. In the center of the channel, maybe two hundred meters ahead, the water domed up like something enormous rising beneath it, a swell without wind behind it, and from somewhere under the escarpment came a sound I will hear for the rest of my life: a vast, grinding exhalation, mechanical and geological at once, the groan of sluice gates the size of cathedral doors swinging through their arcs. Spray lifted off the dome and hung in the air. The boat rocked in a swell that had traveled from nowhere.

Then the gates closed, the dome subsided, and the river remembered which way it was supposed to flow and resumed, sheepish, as though nothing had happened.

Nobody spoke. The mist drifted. Somewhere high above us a bird complained once and thought better of it.

"Again," Tuck said finally. "It'll do it again. Right?"

"Ninety seconds," Sefa said. "Give or take."

We waited. I counted in my head, watching the second hand on my father's watch, the one thing of his besides the compass I'd ever owned. At eighty-eight seconds, the river stopped. At ninety-one, it began to pull backward. At two minutes and six seconds, the gorge exhaled, and the dome rose, and the deep gates ground through their cycle, and the spray hung, and fell.

"It's a machine," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, small in all that stone. "The source is a machine."

"Not the source," Sefa said. "Under it. Around it. Dominic, the entire upper escarpment is hollowed out. Reservoir galleries, penstocks, control chambers—it's been built over decades, hidden inside the karst itself. The survey wasn't mapping the river. We were mapping the plumbing."

"And nobody knew?"

"The right people knew." Her knuckles were white on the rail. "The maps lie politely, remember? Every forbidden zone on those charts is a wall hiding a room. And what you just watched—that's not an accident. That's not nature hiccupping. Those are intake gates cycling on a test schedule. Somebody is running this thing."

* * *

Bram backed us into an eddy behind a spur of rock where the reversal couldn't drag us, and we tied off to a root thick as a man's leg, and we watched. Three more cycles. Four. I timed each one. Ninety-one seconds. Eighty-nine. Ninety-three. Regular as a pulse, regular as anything alive, and that regularity was somehow worse than chaos would have been. Chaos you can read. This was intention.

On the fifth cycle, Tuck spotted the marks.

"Look." He was hanging halfway over the side, pointing at the limestone wall just above the flood line. "Look at the stones."

At first I took them for natural staining—mineral darkening in the seams. Then the next surge came and the water climbed, and when it fell back, the stones were wet in a pattern. A band of darker blocks, mortared, running along the wall for thirty meters and disappearing into the rock on both sides. Old work. Decades old, by the lichen on it. But work.

"They built it into the cliff," Bram said. "All of it. So from the air, from the river, from anywhere a person might look—you see karst. You see nothing."

"Who has that kind of patience?" Tuck asked.

"Anyone who expects to be paid forever," I said.

And there it was, the thought arriving like cold water finding a seam: *forever*. Water rights. The phrase had been drifting through my father's logbook annotations, through Sefa's fragments, through the Syndicate's hunger, and I'd been holding it at arm's length because holding it close meant understanding what it weighed. But watching the river breathe on schedule, watching the escarpment swallow and release it like a lung, I understood. Whoever controlled this machine controlled every drop that came down the Karst. Every village. Every field. Every season. Not a dam you could see and argue about and vote down—a dam you couldn't even prove existed. Debt, I thought, is just water rights with paperwork. The Vellum Syndicate had taught me that much. They smile while they ruin you, and they ruin you slowly, and they call the interest optimism.

My hands were shaking. I told myself it was the chill off the water.

* * *

Sefa talked while we watched. It came out of her in pieces, the way ice comes off a roof in thaw—one sheet at a time, each one landing hard.

"The night it happened, we were camped at the mouth of this gorge. Team lead was a woman named Okafor—smart, careful, the kind of scientist who triple-checks a negative result. She'd been getting strange orders for weeks. Survey this grid, ignore that one. Log the flow data but don't archive it. And then that night the lights came on in the gorge—not flashlights, *lights*, mounted lights, industrial, coming on section by section down the cliff face like a runway illuminating. And the river did what you just saw. Only harder. The whole riverbed pulled back and for about ten seconds there were things visible down there in the wet stone. Intake mouths. Gratings big enough to drive trucks through. And the sound—" She pressed her lips together. "Like the world inhaling through its teeth."

"What did Okafor do?" I asked.

"She stood at the edge of the water for a long time. Then she turned around and told us to erase everything. Wipe the instruments, burn the notes, forget the coordinates. She said, 'Whatever this is, we were never here.' And most of them started doing it, because she was Okafor and you did what Okafor said." Sefa looked down at her hands. "I ran. I took the sample case and the gauge logs and I ran, and I've been telling myself for eight months that running was the brave version, that staying would've gotten me buried in some unmarked gallery down there with the rest of them."

"Was it?" Tuck asked. He asked it without cruelty, the way children ask things, which is the only way the question can be survived.

"I don't know," Sefa said. "But I know this: they didn't vanish. People who vanish leave tracks. These people were *erased*. And erasure takes an organization, and organizations cost money, and money—" she looked at me, and finished it for both of us, "—money leaves ledgers."

The Vellum Syndicate. My debt. My father's name in a dead man's logbook. A machine breathing under a mountain. I'd come up this river thinking I was following a salvage job into a mystery. I was following a thread, and threads don't care what they're attached to—they only care about being pulled.

* * *

On the ninth cycle, I saw the platform.

It was during the inhale, when the water dropped nearly a meter below its normal line, that the base of the far wall exposed itself: a ledge, man-made, maybe four meters wide, running back toward a dark opening in the rock. On the ledge sat equipment. I couldn't make out details at that distance, but the shapes were legible the way gear is legible to anyone who's lived around it—cable drums, a generator housing, and something tall and thin that my gut identified before my brain agreed: a mast. A radio mast. Freshly guyed, the wires catching what little light reached down there.

"Somebody's using it now," I said. "Recently."

Bram shaded his eyes. "Maintenance crew?"

"Maintenance crews don't need masts." I dug the binoculars out of the deck bag. Through the lenses, the platform sharpened: the generator was new, the paint bright; the mast carried a whip antenna; and stenciled on a crate beside the opening, in letters I could read clear across the gorge, was a logo I knew better than my own signature.

A stylized leaf. Folded like a page.

Vellum.

The binoculars lowered slowly, as if my arms already understood what my mind was still assembling. "They're testing it," I said. "The Syndicate isn't chasing the source. They're *operating* it. Or their client is. The survey team found it, refused to run the test, got erased—and now the same money that owns my debt owns these gates, and they're cycling them on a schedule to see what the river does."

"To see what it can do," Sefa corrected softly. "A test isn't curiosity, Dominic. A test is rehearsal."

Rehearsal. For what? For the day the sluices open and don't close. For the day the water becomes a product, metered and priced, flowing only where it's paid to flow. For the day every farm and village on the lower Karst learns what I learned in a truck cab with a smiling man and a ledger: that everything you thought was free was only yours until someone filed the claim.

Anger arrived the way the surge arrived—not gradually, but all at once, from underneath, lifting everything I'd been keeping level. My father hadn't died in some accident I could grieve cleanly. He'd found this. He'd tried to stand between it and the rest of us. And the same machinery of paper and patience that erased his survey team had spent years quietly buying up his daughter's future, one small debt at a time, so that if I ever came looking—which they'd apparently bet I would—I'd arrive already leashed.

They hadn't sent me to salvage equipment.

They'd sent me to find the handle.

"Well," Bram said into the silence, in the tone of a man consulting the weather, "we can go around. There's a portage trail on the east bank, according to your map. Adds two days."

"We're not going around," I heard myself say.

"Dominic." Sefa turned, wary. "What's on that platform is a Syndicate operation. Armed, probably. Radioed-in, definitely. Going toward it is—"

"Going toward it is the job." I folded the binoculars and found my hand had stopped shaking. That was the strange part. The anger hadn't burned the fear off; it had organized it, given it somewhere to stand. "They think I'm their tracker. Fine. Let me track. But we do it on our terms—we watch, we learn the cycles, we find where that tunnel leads. Because whatever's at the end of it, it's the reason my father is dead, the reason Sefa's team is erased, the reason Tuck's village is getting bought out with paper smiles. It's all one thing. It's been one thing the whole time."

The river inhaled again. Held. Exhaled. Ninety-two seconds. I watched the water climb the mortared band of ancient stones and fall away, patient as interest accruing.

"One more thing," Tuck said. He was staring at the platform through the binoculars now, and his voice had gone carefully casual, the way voices go when they're carrying something fragile. "There's a boat tied up down there. Two men loading crates. And Dominic—the crates have the same stencil as the ones on the barge that came through my village last month. The buyout men."

Of course they did. Everything had the same stencil. The whole world, it turned out, was one shipment.

Bram looked at me across the idling engine, waiting, the way he always waited—for the decision, not the speech. Sefa's jaw was set. Tuck's knuckles were pale around the binoculars.

"Get us closer," I said. "Behind that spur, engine off, paddles only. I want to know what goes in and out of that tunnel before the next cycle."

"And after the next cycle?" Sefa asked.

"After that," I said, "we stop watching the machine."

I picked up a paddle. "We start planning how to shut it down."
