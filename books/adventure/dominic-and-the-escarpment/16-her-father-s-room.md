## 16. Her Father's Room

The door was where Sefa said it would be, which was the first impossible thing.

Behind the third sluice gallery, past a junction of pipes thick as tree trunks, there was a wall that wasn't a wall—a seam in the concrete so fine you'd miss it if you weren't looking for it, and Tuck found it because he was always looking for things nobody else thought to look for. He ran his palm along it like he was petting something.

"Here," he said. "It sounds hollow."

Bram put his ear to it and knocked twice. The sound came back wrong—empty, resonant, a drum instead of stone.

"Pressure door," Sefa said quietly. She'd gone pale somewhere back in the gallery and hadn't recovered. "Standard survey issue. There'll be a manual release."

There was. A wheel, stiff with eleven years of disuse, that took all three of them and Bram's whole temper to turn. It gave all at once, the way stuck things do, and air breathed out of the dark beyond it—cool, dry, smelling faintly of machine oil and paper and, underneath everything, something Dominic couldn't name until she could, and then it hit her like a fist under the ribs.

Tobacco. Pipe tobacco. Black Virginia, the cheap kind, the kind her father had smoked on the porch every night of her childhood until her mother made him take it outside, and even then he'd stand at the edge of the steps in the rain rather than give it up.

Nobody had smoked in this room for years. But the smell had soaked into the walls and stayed, patient as a held breath.

"Dominic?" Bram said.

She went through the door first. Nobody stopped her. Maybe they knew better. Maybe they just understood that some doors you have to walk through alone even when everyone's watching.

* * *

Lights came up in stages as they moved—old motion sensors, still loyal after a decade—and what they revealed was not an abandoned station. It was a home.

A bunk, made. A desk with a lamp and a mug with a tea ring dried into the bottom. A shelf of books: hydrology manuals, yes, but also novels, dog-eared, and a battered atlas of the escarpment with colored pins in it. A second chair pulled up to the desk at an angle, like someone had been sitting in it recently, talking to someone behind the desk. On the wall above the bunk, a photograph in a cheap frame.

Dominic stood in the middle of the room and did not move for a long time.

The photograph was of her. Age nine, gap-toothed, standing on the tailgate of her father's truck holding up a fish she'd caught herself and was clearly unbearably proud of. Her mother had taken it. It had hung in the hallway of the house on Delmore Street until the bank took the house, and Dominic had assumed it went into a box, and the box into storage, and the storage unit sold for arrears, the way everything they'd ever owned eventually got sold.

It was here. Eleven hundred kilometers from anywhere, on the wall of a dead man's room, hanging over his bed so it was the last thing he saw at night and the first thing he saw in the morning.

"He lived here," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange, like it belonged to someone else. "He didn't visit. He *lived* here."

Sefa was very still by the door. "The team lead talked about a consultant. An outside expert who reviewed the source data. He never came to camp meetings. He had his own quarters, off-limits." She swallowed. "We called him the ghost."

Dominic turned around slowly. "Describe him."

"Tall. Gray coming in early. Smoked a pipe he wasn't supposed to have down here. Kept to himself, but—" Sefa frowned, reaching. "But he was kind. When I botched a gauge calibration my first month, everyone laughed, and he stayed after and showed me how to do it right. Never told me his name. Just said, 'Measure twice, apologize once.'" She looked at Dominic's face and stopped talking.

"Measure twice, apologize once," Dominic repeated. Her father's saying. His one piece of wisdom, deployed at every disaster of her childhood—the flooded basement, the wrecked car, the night she'd told her mother about the dropped scholarship. Measure twice. Apologize once. Do the work carefully, own the mistake fully, move on.

She walked to the desk. Sat down in his chair. The wood creaked under her the way it must have creaked under him ten thousand nights, and that small familiar complaint undid her more than the photograph had.

The drawer was unlocked.

Inside: a pipe, a tin of tobacco gone stale, a folding knife with a broken tip, and paper. Reams of it. Notebooks, loose sheets, envelopes. And on top of the stack, squared neatly, addressed in handwriting she would have known blindfolded, in the dark, underwater—

*Dominic.*

Her name. Forty-one envelopes. She counted them without meaning to, the way you count anything when your mind is trying not to feel. Forty-one unsent letters, each one dated, the dates running backward through eleven years like a countdown or a confession.

The earliest was dated three weeks before his death.

Before the bridge. Before the rain-slicked curve and the collapsed guardrail and the closed casket and the thirteen-year-old girl in the front row who decided, from that day forward, that the world was a place where good men drowned and nobody asked why.

"Dominic." Bram's voice, low. He'd come up beside her without her hearing, which was its own kind of skill. "You don't have to read those now."

"Yes, I do."

"You're shaking."

"I know." She picked up the top letter. The most recent. Dated four months ago. Four months ago her father had sat in this chair, under her gap-toothed photograph, and written to her, and sealed it, and put it in a drawer, because there was no address in the world he could send it to that wouldn't get her killed.

Four months ago she'd been hiding from Vellum collectors in a motel outside Corren Flats, eating crackers for dinner, telling herself her father's death was just bad luck, just weather, just the ordinary cruelty of a wet road.

She broke the seal.

* * *

*My Dominic,*

*I've started this letter nineteen times. I keep them all. Your mother used to say I never threw anything away, and she was right, and I'm glad of it now because maybe someday these will reach you and you'll understand why I chose to be dead.*

*Not died. Chose. There's a difference and the difference is everything and I will spend whatever time I have left trying to earn it.*

*You were thirteen. You are twenty-four now, though by the time you read this you may be older, and I am sorry for every birthday I've missed, and I am not sorry for the choice, and I need you to hold both of those truths at once because holding two true things that hurt is most of what being an adult is.*

*They came to me with money, at first. Then they came with contracts. Then they came with the kind of offer where you understand that no is a word other people say. The Syndicate doesn't buy projects, love. It buys people's obligations, and an obligation is just a debt wearing a suit. They bought the survey's funding, and the survey's funding bought the engineers, and the engineers built something extraordinary under this mountain, and then they asked the extraordinary thing to lie about what it was.*

*A reservoir that could water the whole escarpment. Or a weapon against everyone downstream, priced per village. Same machine. Depends entirely on whose hand is on the sluice.*

*I said no. Not loudly—I'd learned by then that loud men drown first. I said no the way water says no: slowly, everywhere, patiently. I faked the accident because a live opponent gets managed and a dead consultant gets forgotten, and I could protect this place better as a ghost than as a grave.*

*I know what it cost you. I have spent eleven years costing you things from inside a locked room. If you are reading this, then either I am finally brave enough to send it, or you found your way here on your own, which means you inherited the stubbornness I prayed you'd be spared.*

*Either way: the compass is yours. It was always yours. It points at nothing useful and everything important. That was the joke, and also the inheritance.*

*Forgive me. Don't forgive me. Come find out what I was protecting, and then decide.*

*All my love, longer than the river,*

*Dad*

* * *

She read it twice. Then she set it down on the desk, faceup, and pressed both hands flat against the wood, and breathed, and did not cry, because the crying was somewhere below the anger and the anger was closer to the surface and easier to carry.

Eleven years. Eleven years of her mother working doubles. Of the house going, then the truck, then the boat. Of Dominic learning to sleep in her car and call it freedom. Of every collector's smile, every red notice, every night she'd lain awake doing arithmetic that always ended in the same number: not enough.

And the whole time, he'd been alive. Alive and forty-one letters deep and drinking tea in a room with her picture on the wall, choosing, every single day, to let her believe he was dead.

"Furious," Bram said softly. He was leaning against the doorframe, arms crossed, watching her the way you watch weather. "That's the word I'd use."

"You don't get to psychoanalyze me."

"No. But I get to stand here while you do it to yourself." He nodded at the letter. "Go on. Say it."

"He left me." Her voice cracked down the middle. "He left *me*. Not the project. Me. He let me stand at a funeral with an empty box and grow up poor and angry and alone, and for *what*—so some machine under a mountain could keep running? So his precious public trust could—"

"So you'd be alive to hate him for it," Bram said.

She rounded on him. "That's not—"

"It is, though." He said it without heat, which was worse. "Think like a chaser, Dom. You read a storm cell. Read this one. A man who fakes his death to hide from the Vellum Syndicate knows exactly what they do to the families of people who cross them. They don't kill you. They buy you. They buy your debts and your neighbors' debts and your daughter's debts, and they squeeze until you sign whatever's put in front of you. A dead man's daughter is a loose end. A living traitor's daughter is leverage." He shrugged. "He didn't leave you. He made himself worthless to them so you'd be worth nothing to take."

The words landed and kept landing, echo after echo, and Dominic hated every one of them because they were true, and she could see it now, the whole shape of it, the way you see the full rotation of a supercell only when it's already on top of you.

The debt. Her debt. The one Ives smiled about across her own truck seat. She'd assumed it was hers—her failures, her bad seasons, her signed line. But her father had known. Had probably watched it happen from inside this mountain, helpless, writing letter after letter he couldn't send: *they've found her, they've bought her paper, they're using her to draw me out.* Except she'd never been drawn out, because he'd never surfaced, and so the debt had just... grown. A leash attached to a collar on a dog tied to a post outside a house where nobody lived.

"They weren't collecting my debt," she said slowly. "They were fishing."

"Now you're reading it right."

Tuck appeared in the doorway, quiet as a cat, eyes huge. Behind him Sefa stood with her hand on his shoulder, keeping him half-back, giving Dominic the room she needed while refusing to leave her alone in it. Found family, Dominic thought. Strays at a crossing. Her father had written *come find out what I was protecting,* and she had, and she hadn't come alone, and she suddenly understood that this too might have been part of what he'd hoped for—that she'd arrive here with people, because a person alone is easy to bury and a crew is hard to argue with.

"How many letters?" Tuck asked.

"Forty-one."

"Are you going to read them all?"

"Not tonight." She looked at the stack. It would take days. It might take the rest of her life. Somewhere in those envelopes was the story of eleven years—of the machine breathing beneath them, of the survey team who'd vanished, of the exact shape of the trap that had caught them all. Somewhere in those envelopes, probably, was the reason the river ran backward.

But tonight she had one letter, and one photograph, and one room that smelled of pipe smoke and patience, and a fury in her chest that had nowhere to go and therefore, she realized, was going to become fuel. Her father had spent eleven years protecting this place with silence. She had a different temperament.

"Ives offered to forgive my debt," she said to the room, to the photograph, to the empty chair. "For the engineering documents. He thought he was being generous." She picked up the letter, folded it along her father's creases, and put it inside her jacket, over her heart, where the compass rode. "He has no idea what he actually owes this family."

Bram pushed off the doorframe. "That sounded like a plan forming."

"It's not a plan yet."

"What is it, then?"

Dominic looked around the room one last time—the bunk, the books, the atlas with its colored pins marking every gauge, every sluice, every secret vein of the escarpment her father had mapped in the years he was supposed to be dead. On the desk, under the lamp, half-buried under the envelope stack, she noticed a slim black notebook she hadn't seen before, its cover stamped with a government seal and a word she recognized from Sefa's falsified maps.

*Sluice Control — Master Index.*

Her father had kept a copy. Of course he had. Measure twice.

"That," she said, reaching for it, "is a map of everything Ives wants—and everything he's afraid of."

Down the gallery, faint through the concrete, the river inhaled again.
