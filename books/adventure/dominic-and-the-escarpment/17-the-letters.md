## 17. The Letters

The room was small enough that her shoulders nearly touched both walls when she turned around. A cot with a blanket folded to military corners. A tin cup on a shelf, stained brown at the waterline from years of the same tea. A photograph pinned above the desk—her mother, laughing, squinting into sun that had stopped existing eleven years ago.

And in the desk drawer, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with surveyor's twine: forty-one letters.

Dominic sat down on the cot because her legs told her to, not because she'd decided anything. The station hummed around her—old generators two floors up, the buried machinery beneath them breathing its slow tidal breath through the rock—and the sound filled the silence where her own breathing should have been.

Her father's handwriting covered the first envelope. *For Dominic. When she's ready.* The same words on every one. Forty-one envelopes, forty-one versions of the same impossible sentence.

*Ready for what?* she thought. *Ready for you to be alive? Ready for you to be dead again?*

She opened the first one anyway.

* * *

She was nine years old, sitting on the tailgate of his field truck while rain hammered the canvas canopy, watching him calibrate his compass by lamplight. He'd let her hold it once—the brass case warm from his pocket, the needle trembling like something alive.

"It doesn't point north," he'd said.

"Everyone says it does."

"Everyone's mostly wrong about everything important." He'd tapped the glass. "It points at what matters. You just have to know how to read it."

She'd asked him what mattered. He'd looked out at the rain coming off the flats in gray sheets and said, "Water. Where it goes. Who it belongs to." Then he'd laughed, embarrassed, and ruffled her hair and said never mind, finish your homework.

She'd spent twenty years thinking that was a joke.

* * *

The first letter was dated three weeks after his funeral.

*Dominic —*

*I don't know how to write this, so I'll write it badly and often, and maybe one of these will come out right.*

*You're reading this, which means I'm dead in every way that counts. I want you to understand that I didn't die. I disappeared. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole story, and I've been a coward about telling it because telling it would have put you in danger, and I chose your safety over your trust. I'm sorry. It's the worst thing I've ever done and I did it every day for years.*

*You deserve the truth in order. So: order.*

*Twelve years ago I was hired as a hydrologist on a government project called the Karst Initiative. On paper: flood control for the escarpment villages. That part was true. What wasn't on paper was the rest of it—a reservoir system inside the mountain itself, sluices and galleries carved into the karst, capable of holding an entire monsoon's worth of water and releasing it on schedule. Clean power. Year-round irrigation. Water rights for every settlement from Widow's Bend to the sea.*

*It was meant to be a public trust. That phrase is written into the original charter—I helped draft it. The water belongs to the people who drink it. Not to a company. Not to a government ministry that can be bought. To them.*

*Then the money got complicated. Money always gets complicated. And when it did, the people who owned the complication were the Vellum Syndicate.*

Dominic stopped reading. Her thumb pressed flat against the page as if she could hold the words still.

She read the name again. *Vellum Syndicate.*

Ives's ledger lived in her truck outside, under the seat, along with the salvage contract she'd signed in a room that smelled of lemon polish and menace. Forty thousand owed, plus interest calculated in a way no honest mathematician would recognize. She'd inherited the debt at nineteen from a father who died owing it—or so the Syndicate's collectors had told her, with smiles, over seven years of monthly visits.

*He didn't die owing it,* she thought. *He died fighting it. And they billed me for the war.*

* * *

Memory came sideways, the way it always did—not scenes but fragments, sensory shrapnel.

The smell of graphite and wet canvas. The way he whistled through his teeth when he was concentrating. One winter evening—he'd been gone on "survey contracts" for months at a stretch even then—she'd found a document on his desk, half-tucked under a blotter. A letterhead she didn't recognize. Words like *galleries* and *capacity* and a signature block with a name she'd later see again in nightmares: Vellum Holdings, subsidiary interests.

She'd been fifteen. She'd put the paper back. Some instincts you have before you have reasons.

* * *

The second letter was shorter. Angrier.

*They bought the debt today. Not funded it—bought it. The Ministry's budget collapsed in committee the way budgets do when three committee members have accounts in places committees can't look. Vellum now holds the paper on the entire Karst Initiative. Every engineer, every contractor, every village easement—all of it payable to them.*

*Do you understand what that means? It means the public trust has a landlord. It means the charter we wrote is worth exactly what they decide it's worth, which is nothing, unless the water becomes more valuable sold than given away. And it will. Of course it will. Drought is coming—they know it, I've seen their projections, they've known for a decade. They aren't building a reservoir. They're building a tap. And whoever owns the tap owns everyone downstream.*

*I argued. I want you to know that, whatever else is true. I stood up in a room full of men in good coats and said the word "trust" until it lost all meaning, and they smiled at me the way creditors smile, and the project director—who is not a bad man, which is worse—told me quietly that I had a daughter to think of.*

*That's when I understood the shape of the trap. My debt to them was already on their books. My expertise was already irreplaceable. And my daughter was already, in their arithmetic, collateral.*

*I'm going to disappear. I'm going to stay here, inside the mountain, and keep the machine honest as long as I can—one man with a compass and a conscience against a syndicate with lawyers. If I do it right, they'll never find me, and they'll never be sure the source works without me, and that uncertainty will be the only leash left on them.*

*If I do it wrong, you'll get this letter and hate me. Hate me if you need to. But then read the next one.*

Dominic set it down. Her hands weren't steady.

Outside the monitoring room, faintly, she could hear Tuck asking Sefa something about the gauges, and Bram's low laugh, and the ordinary sounds of people being alive near her. It felt obscene. It felt like the only thing keeping her in the chair.

* * *

She'd hated him. That was the truth she'd never said aloud at the grave, never said to anyone. Eleven years old when he died—officially died—a bridge inspection, a swollen river, a closed casket. And by thirteen she'd learned the other thing: that he'd died insured for less than he owed, and that the debt hadn't died with him, because debts to the Vellum Syndicate didn't die, they *migrated*, the way weather migrates, the way her whole life had migrated from one cheap room to another while men in good coats arrived monthly to smile at her and update a number.

She'd built her entire self around that hatred. Invisible. Unattached. Chase storms for clients who wouldn't pay, because clients who wouldn't pay couldn't own you. Keep the truck gassed and the bags packed and love nothing that couldn't be abandoned in ninety seconds.

And the whole time—

The whole time he'd been thirty kilometers upstream, alive, writing to her.

* * *

Letter nine. Dated four years ago. His handwriting had changed—tighter, faster, a man writing against something.

*They tested the sluices today without authorization. Full draw-down. The river at Widow's Bend ran backward for eleven minutes and the villages downstream lost their shallows—fish stranded, boats grounded, a boy broke his leg falling into a channel that shouldn't have been empty. I logged it. I filed the report through channels that go nowhere, and then I copied it, and hid the copies, because channels that go nowhere are how Vellum likes its information.*

*Here is what I've learned in eight years underground: the Syndicate doesn't want the reservoir finished. They want it *contested*. An unfinished project is a debt engine—it generates obligations forever, and obligations are their crop. Every year the Initiative runs over budget, they buy more of the paper. Every village that signs a water easement to survive signs away a little more of itself. They are farming us, Dominic. Patiently. Legally. With excellent manners.*

*The survey team that arrived last month—young, idealistic, mapping the upper gorge—they think they work for the government. In six months they'll either work for Vellum or they'll vanish. I'm trying to teach them which questions to ask before it's too late. I don't know if it's working. Idealism has a short half-life down here.*

*Your mother would tell me to stop writing in circles and say the thing. Fine. The thing:*

*I have a plan. It's a bad plan. It involves the master key, the claim documents, and eventually you, though God willing not soon and not like this. The charter still exists. The public trust clause is still legally binding—if the project were ever completed honestly, the water would belong to the escarpment, all of it, worth more than every debt Vellum holds in this province combined. They know this. It's the one outcome their lawyers can't survive. So they will never complete it honestly, and they will never let anyone else.*

*Unless someone burns their version of the future down and hands the truth to the river.*

*I'm tired, sweetheart. I'm so tired. But the needle points at what matters, and I taught you what matters, and I am so proud of you that it makes the dark hours bearable.*

*Read the next one tomorrow. Grief keeps better in installments.*

*—Dad*

Dominic laughed. It came out broken, one syllable, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the pressure turned into something else.

*Grief keeps better in installments.* He'd known she'd read them all in one night. He'd known her at nineteen, at twenty-five, at however-old-she-was-when-she-found-them, and he'd known she'd tear through the oilcloth like a storm through a tent flap. He'd written to the woman she became from inside the man he was, across a gap of years and death, and gotten it exactly right.

* * *

She read until the generator cycle changed and the lights dipped and steadied. Letters about the machine—valve tolerances, seepage rates, the particular song the deep gallery made when the sluice gates cycled, a sound Sefa had described as *the river inhaling*, and there it was in her father's hand, *it sounds like breathing, like the mountain inhaling, I've started to think of it as alive and I'm not sure I'm wrong.*

Letters about the team. The young surveyors. He'd liked them. He'd taught them night navigation and bought illicit candy from a supply runner for the youngest one, a girl named—Dominic checked the spelling—*Sefa*, who reminded him, he wrote, *of you at that age: too stubborn to freeze, too curious to leave.*

Sefa. Who'd run. Who'd survived. Who was two floors above her right now, keeping watch, carrying guilt like ballast.

Letters about Ives—or not Ives by name, but *the collector they send now, young, cheerful, genuinely believes he's doing kindnesses. Beware that one most of all. Cruelty that knows it's cruel can be fought. Kindness in the service of the ledger is a fog you walk into smiling.*

Letters about her. So many letters about her. Her first chase, which he'd tracked in the station's weather feeds, terrified and proud in equal measure. The debt, which he'd discovered they'd transferred to her, and which had made him write pages of pure fury that ended mid-sentence and resumed three days later, calmer, colder: *I cannot pay it from here without revealing myself, and revealing myself ends the only protection I can offer you, which is my absence. Do you see? Even my ghost is collateral. That is what it means to owe Vellum. Everything you love becomes a line item.*

*But listen. Listen, because this is the important one:*

*A debt is a story about the future. That's all it is—an agreement about what someone owes tomorrow. Vellum understands this better than anyone alive. They don't collect money; they collect tomorrows. And a story about the future can be rewritten by whoever holds the pen.*

*I'm leaving you the pen, sweetheart. It's hidden where a compass would point, if compasses pointed at what matters instead of north. Find the source. Find the vault. Read the charter. And then decide—not as their debtor, not as my daughter, but as the only Brightwater they never got a signature from—what the river owes, and what it's owed.*

*Whatever you choose, I'm proud of you. Whatever you choose, the water remembers.*

*—Dad*

* * *

The last letter was different. No date. The handwriting slower, older, a man who knew his time was being measured by something other than calendars.

*If you're reading this, then either they found me or the machine found me, and either way I ran out of tomorrows before I ran out of things to say. Forgive the mess. I've tried to leave it orderly.*

*One last thing, and then I'll let you sleep:*

*Don't bring it down the mountain. I wrote that warning in the team logbook and I write it here. The moment proof of the source exists below the escarpment, it becomes a document, and documents can be bought, and Vellum buys everything. The truth is only free while it stays in the stone. I know what I'm asking. I know you came looking for something you could carry out, something that would settle the ledger, buy back your life, make the last ten years mean something.*

*So did I. For twelve years.*

*The answer isn't downriver, Dominic. It never was. Come up here. Stand where I stood. And then decide what kind of Brightwater you are.*

*The needle points at what matters.*

*—Dad*

* * *

She came back to herself slowly, the way you surface from deep water. The lamp. The cot. The tin cup. The photograph of her mother laughing at a sun that no longer existed, above a desk where a dead man had written himself alive for twelve years.

Down the corridor, boots on metal grating. Sefa's voice, tight: "Dominic? We've got movement on the lower approach sensors. Vehicle, maybe two."

Bram, quieter: "How far?"

"Hours. Maybe less, if they push."

Tuck: "Is it the Syndicate?"

Nobody answered him, which was answer enough.

Dominic folded the letters along their old creases, all except the last one, which she kept in her hand. She stood. Her legs worked. Something behind her ribs had shifted—some load redistributed, some weight converted to fuel.

Twelve years of collectors. Seven years of her own. Smiles and ledgers and interest compounding in rooms that smelled of lemon polish. She'd thought she was paying for her father's failures.

She'd been paying for his defiance. They'd billed her for the fight, month after month, and called it kindness when they offered forgiveness in exchange for the river.

"They're farming us," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the photograph, to the man who wasn't in it. "Yeah. I read that part."

She tucked the final letter inside her jacket, over her heart, where the compass used to ride, and went to see who had come up her father's mountain.

The sensors counted four vehicles now. And the lead vehicle, according to the thermal overlay flickering on the old monitor, was carrying passengers who traveled light and smiled heavy—and Dominic knew, with the certainty of a needle finding north, that somewhere in that convoy was a cheerful man with a ledger, coming to offer her everything she'd ever wanted at the exact price of her soul.
