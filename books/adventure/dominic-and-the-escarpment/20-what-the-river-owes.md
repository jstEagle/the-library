## 20. What the River Owes

Tuck had never told anyone the stories. That was the rule in Lower Vessel — the stories stayed in the village, told by grandmothers to children too young to know they were being given a map.

He was eleven when he'd decided he was too old for them. He was thirteen now, sitting on a wet rock above a river that wanted him dead, and he would have given anything to be small again, back on his grandmother's floor, listening like the stories were just stories.

"They'll follow us," Sefa said. She stood at the lip of the gorge where Ives's crew had left them, looking down at their confiscated boats tied off below with Syndicate padlocks on the throttles. "They have our supplies, our boats, and six days before landfall. They don't need to chase us. They just need to wait."

"Then we don't wait," Dominic said.

Bram laughed, but it wasn't a happy sound. "On foot? Through the escarpment? In monsoon season? That's not an escape, that's a funeral with extra steps."

Tuck looked at the cliff face across the water. He'd been looking at it since they'd been dumped here, because he knew what was behind it, and every minute he didn't say so felt like a kind of lying.

"There's a way," he said.

Everyone turned. He hadn't meant to say it out loud yet. His mouth had gone ahead of his courage, the way it always did.

"A way through?" Dominic asked.

"A way under." He pointed at the dark seam in the limestone across the river, half-hidden by ferns, the kind of crack you'd walk past a thousand times and never see. "The Hollow Ways. My grandmother called them that. The old villages used them before the reservoir people came. Before your survey people came. Before anybody with a ledger came."

Sefa frowned. "Karst caves? Tuck, those systems flood. One rain event and—"

"These ones don't. Not all of them." He got up, because standing made it easier to talk, the way it always did. "There are two kinds down there. The low ways fill with water — my grandmother said never go in anything that drips. But the high ways run along the spine of the escarpment. Dry even in the big wet. The old people kept grain in them. Kept whole families in them during the floods, back before the government built the levees and then stopped maintaining the levees."

"And you know the route," Bram said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know the stories." Tuck swallowed. "I've never been past the first chamber. Kids aren't allowed. But I listened. I always listened. Everybody said I didn't, but I did."

Dominic was looking at him in a way nobody looked at him — not like a stowaway, not like a burden, not like a boy who'd cost them a trade with Ives. Like a person holding something valuable.

"How far does it go?" she asked.

"To the monitoring station. Maybe farther. There's a story about a door of iron under the mountain, and a room where the river is kept like a goat in a pen." He hesitated. "I thought it was a story about a monster."

"It's a story about a machine," Sefa said quietly.

* * *

Crossing to the cave mouth took an hour of Bram's rope work and everyone's nerves, and Tuck went last because Dominic wouldn't argue about it. The water at the crossing ran fast and brown with the first spit of the storm's outriders, and halfway across his foot slipped on the slick rock and Bram's hand closed on his collar like a crane hook and held until his heart stopped trying to leave through his ribs.

"You're heavier than you look," Bram grunted.

"Everyone says that."

"Everyone's right."

The cave mouth breathed cool air at them, and Tuck felt the old childhood shiver — the one that said *dark places eat people* — and pushed through it into the first chamber, where Sefa's lamp found walls ribbed like the inside of a shell.

Then he stopped, because the chamber wasn't empty.

There were marks on the stone. Old ones, smoke-blackened, in shapes he half-recognized from the lintels of the oldest houses in Lower Vessel: spirals, and river-lines, and little clustered dots that his grandmother had called *the counting marks*. Below them, newer and cruder, someone had scratched letters.

Sefa raised her lamp to them and read aloud: "'High way dry to the third fork. Keep left where the stone sings.'" She lowered the light slowly. "That's survey notation. That's my team's shorthand."

"The vanished team came through here," Dominic said.

"Some of them did." Sefa touched the scratched letters with two fingers, careful, like touching a wound. "This is Oyelaran's hand. She was our route specialist." A pause. "She was one of the ones who argued against the test."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The cave dripped somewhere out in the dark, patient as a clock.

"So they ran this way," Bram said. "When they were erased."

"Or they were sent this way," Sefa said. "And what happened next happened underground, where nobody could see it."

Tuck's stomach turned over. He thought about saying the thing he was thinking — that maybe they shouldn't be walking into a place where people had disappeared — but Dominic was already moving toward the fork, and her voice came back to him steady as a handrail:

"Then we find out what happened next. And we finish it differently."

* * *

The high way was everything the stories promised and nothing like them at all.

In the stories it was a corridor of wonders: galleries where the stone sang, chambers of standing water so still they mirrored the stars, passages wide enough to drive a barge through. In truth it was mostly stooped walking over broken flowstone, single file, lamps throwing shadows that moved wrong, the air tasting of mineral and old rain. But it was dry. Tuck checked constantly, palm flat against the ceiling, the way his grandmother had taught him in the story that wasn't supposed to be instructions — *dry stone overhead means the mountain holds its breath; dripping stone means it's about to speak.* Every ceiling came back dry.

At the second fork, the stone sang.

It was real — he'd forgotten it was real, or some part of him had filed it away with the monsters and the iron door. Wind moving through a fissure somewhere made the hollow rock hum, a low note that rose and fell like breathing. Bram stopped dead and stared up into the black.

"That's not wind," Bram said.

"It's pressure," Sefa said. Her voice had gone thin. "Air displaced by water. Something below us is cycling — filling and emptying, filling and emptying. We're hearing the machine breathe."

"The sluices," Dominic said.

"The sluices," Sefa agreed.

Tuck put his hand flat on the humming wall. It buzzed faintly against his palm, like a sleeping animal. His grandmother's voice in his head, telling the story he'd thought was about a monster: *and the mountain swallowed the river and gave it back, and gave it back, and the people learned to read the swallowing, because the mountain only ever took what it meant to return.*

Except now somebody else had taught the mountain to swallow. And Tuck was pretty sure the somebody hadn't learned to give things back.

They walked on.

* * *

They camped in a gallery where the ceiling arched up beyond the lamplight, and Tuck lay awake listening to the others talk in low voices, the way adults did when they thought the kid was asleep.

"—can't be more than four hours' walk to the station, if the boy's bearings hold," Bram was saying.

"If they hold," Sefa said. "Tuck, I'm sorry, I know you're awake, your breathing changes when you listen."

Caught, Tuck sat up. "My grandmother said the counting marks show distance. Dots between the spirals. One dot per hundred paces, she said, from the crossing to the iron door."

Sefa was quiet a moment. "Your grandmother counted it out herself?"

"She walked it. During the flood year, when the levees broke. Half the village lived down there for a month." He picked at a seam on his sleeve. "She said the mountain fed them. Springs in the deep chambers, fish that swim blind. She said—" He stopped, because the next part was the part that mattered, and saying it out loud made it heavy. "She said the mountain doesn't owe anybody anything. But people owe the mountain. And the people who took the river and locked it up—" He gestured vaguely upward, toward the weight of stone and storm above them. "They're stealing from everybody downstream. From my village. From yours, probably. From people who don't even know there's something to steal."

The lamps made three circles of light and one long shared shadow, and in the quiet after his voice stopped, Dominic said, "How old are you, Tuck?"

"Thirteen."

"When I was thirteen I already owed money I didn't borrow." She said it plainly, no self-pity in it, which somehow made it worse. "My father signed things. People always sign things for other people. The debt gets handed down like a coat." She looked at him across the fire-ring of stones they'd made. "You didn't choose any of this either. But you're here. And you knew the way when none of us did. So I'm asking, not telling: tomorrow, you lead. All the way to the station. Can you do that?"

Tuck's throat went tight. Nobody had ever asked him to lead anything except the goats.

"The stories say the last stretch has a trick," he managed. "A chamber where the way splits and both sides look the same, and the wrong one goes down into the wet. You have to count the dots. Nineteen spirals, then take the passage that smells of iron."

"Smells of iron," Sefa repeated softly. "Steel. Rails. Equipment." She looked at Dominic. "His grandmother mapped a government installation by smell, decades before the government built it. Or the stories are older than the project, and the project was built into something that was already here."

"Does it matter which?" Bram asked.

"Yes," Sefa and Tuck said together, and then, awkwardly, both stopped.

"It matters," Tuck said, quieter, "because if the old people knew about the water first, then it was never the company's to lock up. It was never anybody's. That's what my grandmother said. Water isn't property. Water is a promise. The mountain promises it downhill, and everybody drinks, and that's the whole law."

Dominic nodded slowly, and Tuck saw her hand go to her pocket — the compass, the one she carried everywhere, her father's. She didn't take it out. Just touched it, the way you touch a promise.

"Then that's the law we're walking under," she said. "Sleep. Tomorrow we move."

* * *

The nineteenth spiral was exactly where the counting marks said it would be, and the passage beyond smelled of iron — cold machine-smell threading through the wet stone smell, getting stronger with every step, until Tuck's nose told him what his eyes confirmed an hour later: a seam in the rock too straight to be natural, and set into it, a door.

Not iron, exactly. Steel, painted institutional green, gone scabbed with rust at the hinges. A wheel-lock in the middle, and above it a stenciled number that Sefa read out in a voice like she was reading a headstone.

"This is a service access," she said. "Into the monitoring station. From underneath." She turned, and her lamp caught all their faces in turn. "Oyelaran's notes said keep left where the stone sings. She brought them here. To this door. And then—"

"Then whatever happened, happened," Dominic said. She stepped forward and put her hand on the wheel. "Tuck. Your stories end here?"

Tuck shook his head slowly. "The stories end inside. There's a last one. About the room where the river is kept." He hesitated, then gave them the ending the way his grandmother gave it, word for word, because some things deserved to arrive intact. "*And the man who kept the room grew old keeping it, and the people forgot his name, but the mountain remembered, and when the thieves came for the water, the mountain showed his daughter the way in.*"

Silence. Even the distant hum of the machine seemed to lean closer.

"My father's name was Aldous Dunmore," Dominic said at last, very quietly. "And I am so tired of finding out who he was from other people."

She spun the wheel. It resisted, groaned, then gave all at once, and the door swung inward onto darkness that smelled of dust and paper and long years of one man living alone inside a mountain.

Tuck followed the lamps through, last as always, and paused with his hand on the cold steel to say the thing his grandmother said at the end of every story, the words he'd rolled his eyes at his whole life:

"What the river owes, the river pays."

From somewhere far below them, through miles of stone, the machine inhaled — and held its breath.
