## 21. The Corrick Brothers' Choice

Bram heard them before he saw them, which was the only reason any of them were still breathing.

He'd been running the night watch at the mouth of the secondary channel, a half-mile downstream from the monitoring station, where the river split around a shelf of black limestone and went quiet enough to hear a paddle knock against a gunwale. Three days of monsoon buildup had turned the water brown and restless, and Bram had spent thirty years learning what that restlessness sounded like when something was moving through it that wasn't water.

It was the oars. Nobody ran silent oar-stroke in flood season unless they didn't want to be heard, and nobody didn't want to be heard on this stretch of river except people with bad intentions or worse employers. He counted four sets. Then a fifth, dragging — someone tired, or someone who didn't care anymore.

He slipped back up the trail to the station without lighting his lamp and woke Dominic by putting one hand flat over her mouth and one finger to his own lips, which earned him an elbow to the ribs that would've doubled over a smaller man.

"Company," he breathed. "Five boats. Coming up the channel."

She was awake all the way through before he finished the sentence. That was the thing about Dominic Dunmore that Bram had stopped being surprised by: there was no fog of sleep on her at all. She came out of it like a diver surfacing, already looking at the sky, already reading the wind.

"Syndicate?"

"No." He'd listened harder on the walk back. "They're not moving like collectors. Collectors travel loud — they want you to know they've found you, it's half the job. These ones are quiet and they're tired and one of them is cursing about fuel in a voice I recognized."

"Recognized how?"

Bram hesitated. He'd been turning it over since the channel, and he still didn't like the shape of it.

"Corrick," he said. "The younger one. Denny. We ran salvage together out of Marrow's Landing, years back, before his family bought their first confiscation boat and decided honest hauling was for people without ambition."

Dominic sat up in the dark. Sefa was already awake across the room — she slept like a cat, in short suspicious intervals — and Tuck was pretending to sleep the way boys do, eyes shut too firmly, listening with his whole spine.

"The rival crew," Sefa said. "The ones who've been shadowing us since the portage."

"The same." Bram rubbed a hand down his face. "Which means either the Syndicate's second wave caught up with them, or—"

"Or whoever hired them cut them loose," Dominic said.

That was the sentence, and everyone in the room felt it land. Because if the Corricks had been abandoned by their employers — the same shadowy corner of the government that had made an entire survey team vanish — then the Corricks were no longer hunters. They were strays. And strays, Bram knew better than anyone on this river, were dangerous in exactly two directions: at you, or beside you.

* * *

They watched from the station's upper gallery, behind a slit window crusted with twenty years of mineral stain, as the five boats came around the bend below.

Even in the dying light Bram could read the story in them. The lead boat rode low, overloaded, its canvas rigged wrong by somebody who'd lost the crew member who usually rigged it. The second had a cracked thwart lashed with wire. The third and fourth were running dark, paddles only. And the fifth — the fifth was barely moving, listing hard to starboard, with two men bailing in shifts.

"They've been in the water a long time," Sefa murmured. She had the survey binoculars, glass older than any of them. "No resupply. Look at the gear — they're down to personal kits. That's not a hunting outfit anymore, that's a retreat."

"And they're coming here," Tuck said, "because we're the only lit window on the mountain."

Nobody had lit a window. But the boy wasn't wrong in spirit, and everyone knew it.

The Corricks grounded their boats on the gravel shelf a hundred yards below the station and did not approach. That was the interesting part. Old Man Corrick — Hollis, though nobody had called him anything but Old Man in a decade — stood at the waterline with his hands visible at his sides, an old smuggler's courtesy, and waited. The younger brother, Denny, stayed with the boats. The other three hung back among the rocks, and Bram noted with professional approval that they'd posted a lookout on the high stone within ninety seconds of landing. Sloppy outfit, but never a stupid one.

"He wants to talk," Bram said.

"Of course he wants to talk," Dominic said. "The question is whether what he's selling is worth the price of listening."

"It always is with Hollis," Bram said. "The price is the problem. Never the goods."

She looked at him for a long moment. "You knew him."

"I drank with him. I hauled with him. I once watched him talk a customs officer out of impounding forty crates of untaxed engine parts by convincing the man the crates were full of church bells bound for a dedication ceremony." Bram shrugged. "He's the best liar on this river, which is why everything he says that happens to be true hits twice as hard."

"I'll go down," Dominic said.

"Alone?"

"With you." She was already pulling her jacket on. "He'll respect that you're vouching, and he'll behave differently with a woman standing next to a man than facing a man alone. It's a rotten fact about men like him, but it's a usable one."

Sefa started to object and then didn't, because there was nothing to object *with*. They couldn't run — the channel above was already rising with the pre-landfall surge, and the station was the only shelter on this stretch rated for what was coming. They couldn't fight — three of them plus a boy against six river rats in their own element was arithmetic, not combat. So it was going to be talking, and Bram had learned long ago that when talking was inevitable, you wanted to be the one who set the table.

* * *

The meeting happened at the gravel shelf, in the last grey hour before true dark, with the river talking loudly enough behind them that any eavesdropping lookout would have to work for it.

Hollis Corrick was sixty if he was a day, built like a dock piling, with a white beard trimmed short and hands that looked like they'd been assembled from spare winch parts. Up close, Bram could see what the binoculars hadn't shown: the man was hollowed out. Not sick — emptied. The look of someone who'd been carrying a cargo for months and had just been told to drop it in the river.

"Dunmore," Hollis said. "Heard you were dead. Glad the river's a liar, same as everybody else."

"You have me confused with my father."

"Do I?" Something moved behind his eyes — quick, guarded, gone. "Maybe. Maybe not. Doesn't matter tonight." He nodded past her, up the slope, at the station. "You found it, then. The old man's hole in the mountain."

"We found a camp," Dominic said, level as a spirit gauge. "We're salvaging. Contracted job."

Bram nearly smiled. She lied exactly well enough to be believed and exactly badly enough to be disbelieved, which was a skill he'd seen maybe three people possess, and none of them had learned it honestly.

Hollis looked at her the way a card player looks at a hand he can't quite read. Then he laughed — one dry bark, no humor in it, just air escaping.

"Contracted. Right. Here's mine, then, since we're trading stories." He turned and spat into the current. "Three weeks ago our employer stops answering the radio. Two weeks ago the payment schedule dries up. Ten days ago we get one final transmission, unsigned, telling us to return to base and stand down, and when we ask *which* base, because we've got four hundred miles of river between us and anywhere, the frequency goes dead. Permanently." He held up one thick finger. "We are nine hundred miles from home, Dunmore, with no pay, no orders, no extraction, and a monsoon sitting offshore getting fat. Somebody used us as a leash and then cut the leash off at the collar."

"And you think I can fix that."

"I think you're the only other soul on this mountain who knows what's actually up here, and I think whatever your father buried in that station is worth enough that somebody paid to make a whole survey team disappear over it." He stepped closer, and Bram shifted his weight forward without meaning to, an old habit. "I'm not asking for charity. I'm offering a trade."

"There it is," Bram said quietly.

"Here it is." Hollis didn't even flinch; he'd expected the interruption. "We know where the Syndicate's staging. Has known for eleven days, back when we were still on their side of the fence — because don't fool yourselves, Dunmore, the people who cut us loose and the people holding your paper drink from the same well. There's a supply depot at the old quarry works, two bends below the gorge. Boats, fuel, radios, and a dozen collectors waiting on the weather to break so they can come up after you in force. Ives himself is supposed to arrive when landfall clears."

At the name, Dominic went very still. Bram watched her do it — watched the stillness move through her like cold water through a net.

"You want passage," she said.

"I want to live. Passage, provisions, and one share of whatever comes out of that mountain that can be carried in a boat." Hollis spread his enormous hands. "That's the whole offer. No claim, no documents, no rights. My brothers and I aren't fools — paper's worthless up here anyway. Just cargo. Enough to start over somewhere the Vellum name doesn't reach."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we wait right here until the storm makes the decision for all of us, and afterward, whichever group is still standing takes what it needs from whichever isn't." He said it without heat, without threat, almost gently, the way you'd report the weather. "I'd rather it not go that way. I'm too old to drown and too honest to enjoy it."

"You're neither of those things," Bram said.

"No," Hollis agreed pleasantly. "But I'm the closest thing to both that you've got."

* * *

They conferred ten feet away, in shouting distance, which Hollis permitted with the confidence of a man who already knew the answer.

"He's lying about something," Sefa said when they'd gathered. She'd come down uninvited, arms crossed. "Watch his left hand. Every time he mentions the depot, he closes it."

"He's lying about *something*," Bram agreed. "He's always lying about something. The question is whether the lie is load-bearing."

"What does that mean?" Tuck whispered. The boy had followed them down despite explicit instructions, and nobody had the energy to send him back up.

"It means," Bram said, "that a liar's lie tells you what he's protecting. If the lie protects the trap, we walk away. If the lie protects his brothers — say, one of them's hurt, or they've got less fuel than he claims, or there's a sixth boat he hasn't told us about — then the lie is just pride, and pride we can work around."

Dominic was quiet through all of it, watching the older man across the gravel. Hollis stood patiently, hands at his sides, letting them deliberate like it cost him nothing, and Bram had been on rivers long enough to know that patience like that was the most expensive thing a desperate man could buy.

"He knows about my father," Dominic said finally. "Did you catch it? *Maybe I have you confused.* He knows Aldous Dunmore was up here. Which means his employers knew, which means the Syndicate knew, which means—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "Which means my whole life, every debt they ever held over me, might have been collateral on this mountain. On him."

"That's a hole you can fall into later," Bram said. "Right now it's a ladder. A man who knows that much is a man who thinks he needs us more than we need him. Desperation talks straighter than honesty ever will."

"So we take the deal."

"We take the deal," Bram said, "and we sleep in shifts, and we keep our boats keyed separate from theirs, and we tell them nothing about the vault, the letters, or the lever. We let them believe the station is all there is." He glanced at Sefa. "And we put Tuck somewhere else whenever maps come out."

"I'm standing right here," Tuck said.

"You're standing right there," Bram agreed, "and staying there."

Dominic exhaled, long and slow, and walked back across the gravel.

"One share of portable cargo," she said. "Passage and provisions. You beach above the gorge, not below. And Hollis — the first time I catch one of your brothers near our boats at night, the alliance ends at the waterline, and I mean that as a geographic description."

Hollis grinned, and it transformed his whole ruined face into something almost young. "Spoken like a Dunmore. Done." He spat in his palm and held it out.

She looked at the hand for a moment. Then she shook it, once, firm, and wiped hers on her jacket without apology, and Bram thought: *there's the daughter. There he is, alive after all.*

* * *

They made camp that night in the station's lower gallery, two crews separated by a barricade of stacked instrument crates that fooled no one and satisfied everyone. The Corricks' third man — the one who'd been bailing — turned out to have a rope burn down his arm from a capsizing two weeks back, and Sefa cleaned and dressed it without a word, and the man thanked her without a word, and something passed between those two silences that Bram pretended not to see.

He took first watch on the gallery steps, and an hour past midnight, Denny Corrick climbed up and sat beside him uninvited, passing a flask that Bram accepted because refusing it would have been a conversation.

"Marrow's Landing," Denny said. "You remember the flood year? When the whole waterfront went under and we ran people out on the flatboats for three days straight?"

"I remember."

"My father talks about that like it was the last year anybody on this river was worth a damn." Denny turned the flask in his hands. "Then he bought the confiscation boat, and we started taking things instead of carrying them, and I never figured out exactly when the carrying stopped mattering." He looked out at the black water. "Your pilot friend says you're headed for the source."

"Your pilot friend should sleep more."

"Yeah." Denny almost smiled. "Well. Whatever's up there — my father wants a share, and I want out, and those aren't the same thing, and one of us is going to be disappointed." He stood, stretched, and paused at the top of the steps. "For what it's worth: the depot at the quarry works is real. Twelve collectors, maybe fourteen now. And Ives doesn't wait for the weather to clear. He waits for the weather to *peak*. Says it thins the field."

He went down into the dark, and Bram sat with the flask going warm in his hand, doing the arithmetic he hated.

Because here was the truth of it, plain as a depth sounding: the alliance was real, and the betrayal was real, and both sides had signed for both. Hollis would sell them the moment the cargo changed hands. Dominic would cut the Corricks loose the moment they became ballast. Everyone on this mountain knew it, and everyone had agreed to defer it, because the storm gave them no better option and the river never asked anyone to be good — only to be useful.

Pragmatism over pride. His father had called it the fisherman's marriage: two hooks in the same water, each hoping the other catches the smaller fish.

Below the gallery, through miles of stone, the machine cycled — the deep tidal groan of the sluices opening, holding, closing — and Bram realized the sound had become familiar. Comforting, almost. That was the most dangerous thing a river could do to a man: make its menace feel like home.

Dawn came up grey and bruised. And with it, from the direction of the quarry works, faint under the wind, came a sound Bram had hoped not to hear for another three days.

Engines. Many engines, idling in convoy.

The Syndicate wasn't waiting for the peak.

It was coming up to meet it.
