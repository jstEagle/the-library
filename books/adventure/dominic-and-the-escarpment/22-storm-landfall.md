## 22. Storm Landfall

The sky told me first.

I'd been reading weather since I was nine years old, crouched on the tailgate of my father's truck with a barometer in one hand and a thermos of terrible coffee in the other, and I had never once been wrong about the shape of a storm's intent. The monsoon wasn't supposed to make landfall for four more days. Every forecast Sefa could pull on the station's ancient receiver said four days. But the sky over the escarpment that morning was the color of a bruise going bad—green-black at the base, stacked so high it bent light—and the wind had turned and come back around from the southeast carrying a smell I knew better than my own name: rain with weight behind it. Rain with a schedule.

"It's early," I said to Bram, who was already looking at it.

He didn't answer right away. He stood at the edge of the monitoring station's loading platform with his arms crossed, watching the cloud wall build over the high country like a man watching a debt collector walk up his driveway. "How early?"

"Tonight. Maybe sooner."

"That's not what the forecasts said."

"The forecasts are written for people who can afford to be wrong." I nodded upriver, toward the drowned gorge where we'd watched the river inhale itself two days ago. "Whatever they're doing under this mountain, it's changing the water before the storm even arrives. The air's feeding off it."

Bram chewed on that. He was a man who trusted rivers the way other men trusted banks, and a river doing something unnatural offended him at a level below language. "Then we're not leaving," he said. It wasn't a question. The descent route through the flooded channels was suicide in clear weather; in a full monsoon landfall it was a closed door. We were on the mountain until the storm spent itself, and everyone knew it, and nobody said the rest out loud: the machine beneath us would be awake the whole time.

* * *

We spent the day making the station ready, and I'll give the survey team this much—they built to last. The monitoring station sat half-buried into the escarpment's shoulder, its face poured concrete stained with a decade of runoff, and behind three feet of that concrete were generator rooms, a comms room, bunks, a galley, and a data hall full of equipment that hummed even now, drawing power from some source Sefa refused to speculate about on an empty stomach. Tuck hauled supplies down from the boats while Bram and I stripped the Corricks' donated tarps into storm shutters. Sefa lived in the data hall. She hadn't come out except to eat since we'd found my father's quarters, and when she did surface, she moved like someone carrying something breakable in both hands.

"Talk to me," I said, finding her at the master console, surrounded by printouts she'd made because she didn't trust the screens not to lie. Fair. Everything about this place lied politely.

"Two things." She didn't look up. "One: the sluice cycle has shortened. When we watched the gorge, the intake pulses came every forty minutes. They're coming every eleven now. Something is ramping toward capacity."

"Testing."

"Practicing." She finally looked at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before—not guilt exactly, guilt was old news on her, but dread with a deadline attached. "Full-capacity tests aren't scheduled casually. They take weeks of preparation. Flow modeling, downstream warnings, evacuation coordination if you're honest people. This one—" she spread the printouts across the console, "—this one looks improvised. Like someone decided suddenly that they needed to know what the machine could do, and soon."

"Someone who knows the monsoon's coming early too."

"Or someone who wants the monsoon to do their testing for them." Sefa tapped a line of gauge data. "Free inflow. No pumping costs. Run the reservoir to the brim, open everything, measure the damage, and call it an act of God." Her voice went flat and careful. "Dominic, if they run a full-capacity discharge without controlled release, every village on the lower river gets a flood wave. Not rising water. A *wave*. Tuck's village sits in the first thirty kilometers."

I thought about the boy upstairs, hauling crates with that stubborn cheer he wore like armor, and I felt the cold move through me that I usually only got from storms—the cold that means the sky has stopped negotiating.

"Second thing?" I said.

Sefa hesitated. Then she pulled a single sheet from under the others and laid it flat. It was a power distribution schematic, and one line ran off the edge of the page toward a substation marked with a code instead of a name. "This feed has been active continuously for six years. Same draw, same cycle, never varying. Whatever it powers doesn't sleep, doesn't surge, doesn't fail." She met my eyes. "It's the kind of load profile you get from a vault door that's been locked and sealed since the day it was installed."

My father's key sat against my sternum on its cord, warm from my skin. Six years. He'd faked his death eight years ago. Two years of building, then six years of silence, and somewhere in that silence a door had closed that hadn't opened since.

"After the storm," I said.

"After the storm," she agreed, and neither of us believed it, and both of us pretended to.

* * *

The storm arrived the way debts arrive—all at once, after months of pretending it wouldn't.

It hit at dusk. There was no gentle leading edge, no polite drizzle to announce it; the pressure dropped so fast my ears popped, and then the front wall of the monsoon came over the escarpment crest like a waterfall falling sideways. The sound was total. Rain on concrete, rain on the tarps we'd lashed down, rain on the river—a white roar that erased the world past arm's length. Inside the station the lights flickered twice and steadied, and Tuck whooped from the bunkroom like it was a festival, and Bram put his hand flat against the wall as if taking the building's pulse.

"She'll hold," he said. "She held through worse than this, whoever built her."

"Whoever built her built her to hide things," I said. "Hiding things and holding things turn out to be the same skill."

We ate hot food for the first time in days—Sefa found the galley stores intact, decades of canned calories packed by a government that had planned for its own vanishing—and for about an hour it was almost cozy. Tuck told a long, rambling story about the flood of '09 in his village, how the water came into the schoolhouse and the teacher made them all stand on desks and sing songs until it went away, and how he'd decided that night he would never again be somewhere the water could reach without him knowing first. That was why he read gauges, he said. That was why he'd stowed away. Bram listened with his eyes half-closed. Sefa smiled at her tray.

Then the lights died.

Not flickered—died, all of them, and the generator didn't catch, and in the sudden black the sound of the storm seemed to double. Tuck's story stopped mid-word.

"Backup grid," Sefa said in the dark, calm in the way of someone reciting facts to keep panic at arm's length. "The station should switch automatically. Unless—"

Unless the power was being drawn elsewhere. Unless whatever was ramping beneath us needed every watt the mountain could produce.

Emergency lighting came up after ninety seconds that lasted a year—dim amber strips along the floor, the color of caution—and Sefa was already moving toward the data hall, Bram behind her, and I took the stairs two at a time because the console in the data hall was the only window we had into the machine, and I needed to see what it was doing.

* * *

What it was doing was breathing.

The main flow display filled the wall screen, and I watched the numbers with the particular horror of someone who understands what they're looking at. Intake sluices: cycling at four-minute intervals now, each pulse dragging the upper river down by nearly a meter before releasing it. Reservoir level: climbing past the red line Sefa had annotated in her neat surveyor's hand. Discharge gates: sealed. All of them. Sealed while the reservoir filled like lungs filling.

"It's holding everything back," Sefa whispered. "Why would it hold everything back? You never run a reservoir to absolute capacity, the margin exists so the structure can—"

"So the structure can survive the test," I finished. "Unless the test isn't meant to be survived."

Bram had gone very still. River pilots live their whole lives one flood away from ruin, and I watched him do the arithmetic we were all doing. "When it lets go," he said quietly, "how fast?"

Sefa's fingers moved across the console, pulling the reservoir's engineering specs, the ones the falsified maps had been drawn to hide. She read the number and closed her eyes. "Downstream villages have between twenty minutes and forty minutes of warning, depending on where the wave concentrates in the gorge narrows. If anyone's watching the gauges."

"Tuck's village watches nothing," Bram said. "Tuck's village is asleep."

The amber emergency light made everything look like a photograph from a war. I stood in front of the screen and felt my father in the room—not the ghost I'd carried for years, the absent drunk genius who'd chosen a river over his daughter, but the man from the letters, the one who'd written *they will use it, Dominic, they will always use it unless someone stands in the room when the decision gets made*. He'd stood in this room. He'd watched these same numbers climb these same walls. And somewhere downstream of here, in a future he couldn't see, I was standing where he'd stood, watching the same machine fill its lungs.

"No," I said.

Everyone looked at me.

"We don't wait for it to decide. Sefa—you said the shutdown sequence needs the control vault. How far?"

"Through the spine galleries. In good weather, four hours' climb. In this—" she gestured at the roar beyond the concrete, "—six, maybe more, if the flooding hasn't cut the route."

"Then we leave now, tonight, in the teeth of it, because tomorrow the route won't exist." I heard my own voice come out level and hard and realized I sounded like my father's letters. "Because if that gate opens at full capacity, it isn't just Tuck's village. It's every crossing, every ferry, every family on the low river. It's the Syndicate's collectors and the Corrick brothers and every soul who never signed anything and never got a vote. The machine doesn't care whose side you're on. Water never does."

Bram pushed off the wall. "Boats are useless in this."

"We're not taking boats. We're taking the spine." I turned to Sefa. "Your father's route maps. The real ones."

"They end at the vault antechamber. Past that—" she shook her head. "Past that, only your father's annotations go further, and half of them are riddles."

"He wrote them for someone stubborn," I said. "I qualify."

Tuck appeared in the doorway, soaked to the shoulders—he'd been outside checking the mooring lines, of course he had, the boy treated weather like a personal acquaintance—and read the room the way kids read rooms, instantly and completely. "We're going up," he said. Not a question either.

"We're going up."

Outside, the monsoon leaned on the mountain with its whole weight, and beneath our feet, patient as a creditor, the machine breathed in and did not breathe out.

* * *

We geared up in the loading bay by emergency light. Rope, headlamps, the waterproofed logbook, my father's letters sealed in oilcloth against my chest next to the compass. Sefa printed the spine route and laminated it with tape. Bram broke open the emergency cache and distributed flares with the gravity of a man handing out last rites. Tuck tried to slip a fifth flare into his pack and caught my look and returned it, mostly.

At the bay door we paused, the five of us—four of us, and the memory of a fifth who'd walked this threshold years ago and never come back down—and the storm screamed at us through the gap like it had opinions.

"For the record," Bram shouted over it, "I think this is insane."

"Noted," I shouted back.

"And I'm still coming."

"I know."

I put my hand on the door release and felt, through the soles of my boots, a vibration rising out of the mountain's bones—deep, rhythmic, mechanical, the pulse of sluices cycling faster than any natural thing. The machine beneath us was winding toward its moment, and somewhere above us the monsoon was winding toward its own, and between those two converging forces stood a buried vault, a dead man's riddles, and four strays with headlamps and no permission from anybody.

I opened the door, and the storm took us in.
