## 23. The Test

The water came for them at three in the morning, and it came politely.

That was the thing Sefa couldn't stop thinking about as she stood in the monitoring station's main gallery with her hands flat against the cold glass of the observation wall. Below her, the gorge that had swallowed a survey team whole was filling like a bathtub. Not violently. Not yet. The river simply stopped being a river—stopped moving downstream—and began to rise, smooth and silent and patient, the way water rises in a lock. The sound she'd heard on the night everything ended, the sound like the river inhaling, was happening again, and this time she understood what it was: sluices. Enormous ones, buried in the karst beneath her feet, opening to swallow the flow.

"Level's up four meters in twenty minutes," Bram said from the console bank. He'd been awake all night anyway; river pilots slept like cats, in short suspicious naps. His voice was steady but his knuckles weren't. "That's not rain. That's not even close to rain."

"It's the test," Sefa said.

Dominic turned from the far console where she'd been reading her father's notes by lamplight. "The full-capacity test? I thought the survey team refused to run it."

"They did." Sefa kept her eyes on the water. It was easier than looking at Dominic. "They refused. And then they vanished. And now it's running anyway."

* * *

She had been the one who found the schedule.

It was two hours earlier, when the storm had begun its first real assault on the escarpment and the station had shuddered like something alive. Tuck was asleep in the bunkroom, curled around his pack the way he curled around everything he owned, which was very little. Bram was on watch. Dominic had gone down the corridor toward her father's quarters again—she'd been going there every few hours since they'd found them, and none of them had said anything about it, because there are kinds of grief you don't interrupt.

Sefa had taken the opportunity to do what she'd been avoiding for eleven days: open the survey team's operational archive.

It wasn't hidden. That was what bothered her most, later, when she let herself think about it later. The files were right there in the station's records system under a folder labeled ROUTINE MAINTENANCE, because the people who built this place had never imagined that anyone would come looking who wasn't supposed to be looking. The Vellum Syndicate's people had scrubbed the obvious things—the claim documents, the personnel files—but they'd left the engineering schedules, because to them the schedules were worthless without the claim. Paper about valves meant nothing unless you owned the valves.

Sefa had spent six years as a hydrographic surveyor. She knew exactly how much paper about valves was worth.

The file was titled CAPACITY VERIFICATION — PHASE III and it was dated fourteen months ago. She read it twice, because the first time her eyes had slid off it the way your eyes slide off a word you don't want to read.

*Full-capacity sluice verification. All nine gates. Projected downstream discharge at maximum gate release: 340,000 cubic meters per minute over ninety-minute window. Note: downstream channel capacity at current season flows is 61,000 cubic meters per minute. Discrepancy to be addressed via managed village relocation prior to test window.*

Discrepancy. That was the word. Two hundred seventy-nine thousand cubic meters per minute of difference between what the machine would release and what the river valley could hold, and somewhere in an office a man with good handwriting had called it a discrepancy, to be addressed via managed village relocation.

Tuck's village. The Widow's Bend settlements. Every community strung along the lowland river like beads on a string that someone intended to snap.

She had sat down on the floor of the records room, back against the cabinet, and read the rest. The correspondence trail was short because it didn't need to be long. The Syndicate's project office had ordered the test run before monsoon season—before landfall, when the villages could still be cleared, when the displaced could be processed as a logistics problem instead of a casualty count. The survey team's lead, a careful, decent man named Ondrej Vasik whose handwriting Sefa still recognized in her sleep, had responded in writing, which was his mistake and his monument:

*We will not certify this test. The gates have never been cycled above forty percent. Running Phase III without a certified structural review is not a test, it is a gamble with the entire valley. Our recommendation stands: independent review or no operation.*

And below that, in a different hand—one Sefa hadn't seen before, clipped and administrative, the kind of hand that signed things—two words:

*Noted. Proceed.*

Fourteen months ago. The team had refused, and within a week they had vanished, and their camp had been left like a stage set, meals half-eaten, instruments running, because whoever erased them wanted the world to believe they'd simply walked into the river. And now, with the monsoon arriving early and no one left to refuse, the machine was running the test anyway. The storm was their cover. Any deaths downstream would be attributed to weather. The perfect crime needed only one ingredient, and the sky was providing it for free.

Sefa had walked out of the records room and stood in the corridor for a long time, listening to the station breathe around her—the deep subsonic pulse of the sluice machinery transmitted through the karst, slow as a sleeping animal's heart—and she had thought about the night she ran.

* * *

Here is what Sefa had never told any of them, not Bram, not Dominic, not the boy:

On the last night, she hadn't just run. She had been sent away.

Vasik had pulled her aside at dusk, while the others argued in the mess tent about the order they'd received—a new directive, unsigned, instructing them to prepare the gates for cycling. He'd put both hands on her shoulders, and he'd said, "You're the youngest license holder. You're going to take the field copies of our refusal and the gauge data downriver tomorrow. Officially you're doing a routine calibration run at Widow's Bend."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially," he'd said, and smiled the tired smile she'd trusted more than any certification stamp, "you're going to be somewhere else when whatever happens next happens. If we're wrong about these people, someone has to survive with the evidence."

She'd protested. She remembered protesting—she'd said she wouldn't leave, that the team stayed together, that she wasn't some courier to be shipped out of danger. And Vasik had looked at her with such gentleness and such finality that she'd understood, even then, that the decision had already been made and her job was to carry it out.

So she'd gone downriver at dawn with a waterproof case strapped inside her jacket, and she'd reached the crossing, and she'd heard the lights and the inhaling sound behind her in the gorge, and she'd known—even before the news came, even before the empty camp was reported—that she was the only one who'd walked out. The evidence case had been lost at the crossing when she went into the water. She'd told herself for fourteen months that losing it wasn't her fault, that the river took it, that surviving was the mission.

But she had never once opened the case afterward to check whether the copies inside were duplicates. She had never gone back upstream. She had built a small quiet life downstream and told herself the story where running was the same as surviving was the same as doing her part.

Then Dominic Dunmore had walked into her life at a river crossing with her dead father's compass and a logbook full of annotations, and every story Sefa had built came apart at the seams.

* * *

Now she stood at the observation glass and watched the gorge fill, and the guilt that had been fueling her since the crossing finally burned clean.

"We can't stop it from here?" Dominic asked. She'd come up from the corridor quietly, and Sefa wondered how long she'd been standing there, how much of the silence she'd witnessed.

"Not from here. This gallery is monitoring only." Sefa tapped the schematic on the wall display—the one her father's annotations had matched, the one that showed the control vault at the source, nine kilometers upstream through flooded channels. "Shutdown authority is at the source vault. Manual override, physical key, per the original trust specifications. The builders designed it that way on purpose—they didn't want the gates controllable remotely, in case anyone who owned the debt tried to own the water."

"The builders," Bram said dryly, "sound like they expected us."

"The builders expected exactly this," Sefa said. "A private owner running an unsafe test during a disaster. They just expected the public trust to prevent it. Instead—" she gestured at the glass, at the rising black water, "—instead we got the Syndicate."

"How long until the release phase?" Dominic asked.

Sefa pulled the schedule back up. Her voice came out flat, which was how she knew she'd passed through fear into something more useful. "Ninety minutes to full gate pressure. Then the release window opens. Ninety minutes after that, if nobody shuts it down, the downstream channel gets two hundred seventy-nine thousand cubic meters per minute more than it can hold. Every levee at Widow's Bend overtops inside the hour. Tuck's village has maybe three hours' warning, and half of it is asleep, and the roads flood first."

Nobody spoke. Above them, the storm hit the station roof like a fist, and somewhere in the machinery below, the inhaling sound deepened.

"Tuck's asleep," Bram said finally. "Somebody should—"

"I'll tell him," Sefa said. "I'm the one who should."

* * *

Waking him was the worst thing she'd done since the crossing, and she had done some bad things at that crossing.

He came awake the way river kids did—all at once, no grogginess, already reaching for his pack—and she told him straight, because he'd earned straight. The machine upstream was going to release a flood. His village was downstream. There was a way to stop it and it was nine kilometers away through drowned tunnels during a monsoon, and she was going, and he was staying here where it was safe.

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "My grandmother's house is the third one past the ferry steps. Blue door."

"Tuck—"

"You're going to need someone who knows the caves under the escarpment. Everyone knows the surface floods. Underneath, the karst drains east, away from the release channel. My uncle fished eels in those caves. I've been in them since I was six." He slung his pack over his shoulder. "So either I come and show you, or you drown in the dark trying to prove a point to a twelve-year-old."

"He's twelve?" Bram asked from the doorway.

"Thirteen in spring," Tuck said, not turning around.

* * *

They gathered in the main gallery at 4:40, and Sefa laid it out the way Vasik would have laid it out—clean, numbered, no decoration, because decoration was for people with time.

"One. The shutdown is manual, at the source vault, and it needs the master key, which means it needs Dominic, which means all of us go or none of this works. Two. The surface route is underwater by now and getting worse; the karst caves drain east and stay above the release channel until the last approach. Tuck knows them. Three. We have roughly eighty-five minutes before gate pressure peaks, and the release window opens ninety minutes after that. Four." She paused, and made herself say it. "Four. Even if we shut it down mid-release, some flooding happens downstream. The villages get water, but not the killing volume. Which means somebody also has to warn them. The gauge at Widow's Bend still transmits. If we can reach its relay, we can push an emergency broadcast on the civil band."

"I can rig that," Bram said. "If I live long enough."

"Five," Sefa said. "After tonight, none of us come back down this mountain as the people we were."

"That's not a plan point," Dominic said.

"It's the only honest one I've got."

Dominic looked at her across the lamplit gallery—looked at her the way nobody had looked at her in fourteen months, like a person carrying something instead of a person who'd dropped something—and nodded once.

"Then we go now," Dominic said. "While the machine's still inhaling. Before it learns to exhale."

Sefa picked up her pack. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, was the one thing she'd carried since the crossing: Vasik's field notebook, water-stained, half-destroyed, the pages with his refusal still legible. She'd kept it the way you keep a stone in your shoe—to remember the cost of walking away.

She wouldn't need it for remembering anymore.

Above them the storm screamed against the escarpment, and below them the machine drew another long breath, and Sefa Brightwater—who had run once, at dawn, with the evidence lost and the living to think of—turned and led them toward the caves, into the dark, to finish what the dead had started.
