## 24. Climbing the Spine

The storm arrived like a debt collector—no warning, no negotiation, just the whole sky leaning on them at once.

Dominic stood at the mouth of the flooded channel below the monitoring station and watched the escarpment disappear. One minute there had been a spine of gray limestone running up toward the source, stitched with switchbacks her father had marked in his own hand; the next, the rain came down so hard and so sideways that the mountain simply stopped existing. She'd chased storms for eleven years. She had never been inside one that felt personal.

"Say it again," Bram shouted over the roar. He was lashing the last of their gear to the boat's frame, turning it into something between a skiff and a stretcher. "Slowly."

"Two miles of channel," she said. "Then the stairs cut into the spine. Then four hundred feet of exposed rock to the vault door. Sefa's map says the stairs flood above level nine." She looked at the gauge strapped to Bram's wrist, its needle trembling past a number she didn't want to say out loud. "We're at seven already."

"Then we climb fast or we swim slow." Bram grinned, because Bram grinned at everything, but his hands never stopped moving. "I've run water like this on the lower Vell. It doesn't forgive, but it does negotiate. You give it your fear and it lets you keep your boat."

Tuck appeared out of the rain, soaked through, hauling the coil of rope he'd refused to let anyone else carry since the caves. The boy was eleven years old and had spent three days leading grown men and women through karst tunnels by feel, calling turns from village stories his grandmother told him, stories about a river that breathed. He looked up at Dominic now with an expression she recognized from mirrors: someone deciding not to be afraid.

"The Corricks?" he shouted.

"Gone an hour ago," Dominic said. "Downstream, loud, and lit up like a carnival."

* * *

The Corrick brothers had come to them in the dark two nights before, hands visible, weapons slung, offering the only currency they had left: themselves. Their employers had cut them loose when the Syndicate's enforcement detail moved into the gorge—the rival crew had been hired to find the survey team's claim, and now that Ives had found it first, they were expendable. Rafe Corrick had laid it out flat, no charm, no apology.

"You need a distraction. We need to not be shot. Both of those problems have the same solution."

So here was the plan, such as it was, scrawled on waterproof paper in Sefa's careful surveyor's hand: the Corricks would take their boat down the flooded main channel, running lights, running engines, broadcasting on every frequency the Syndicate's scouts used, making themselves the loudest thing on the river. Ives's detail would chase noise. Dominic's crew would go up the drowned back-channel in silence, dark hulls, paddles where possible, engine only when the current demanded it.

It was a plan built entirely on other people's courage.

"They'll hold," Sefa said, reading her face. "Rafe wants the Syndicate hurt more than he wants to live comfortably. That's the most reliable kind of ally."

"And if they don't hold?"

Sefa folded the map away. "Then we're the loudest thing on the river, and we climb anyway."

* * *

The channel took them like it had been waiting all monsoon for exactly this.

Bram steered by memory and instinct, reading the water the way Dominic read sky—a flicker of white here meaning a submerged fence line, a smooth green tongue there meaning depth enough to breathe. The rain hit the river so hard it bounced. Twice the current grabbed the boat and dragged them sideways toward a strainer of downed karst oak, and both times Bram leaned into the turn with his whole body and talked to the river under his breath, low and constant, words Dominic couldn't catch and suspected were negotiations.

Tuck bailed water. Sefa held the compass—her father's compass, the one thing she'd kept from the burning that hadn't happened yet—and called headings. And Dominic sat in the bow with the master key wrapped against her chest and did the one thing she was worst at: nothing. She let Bram pilot. She let Tuck bail. She let the plan be bigger than her pride.

Every debt called in at once, she thought. Her father's silence, paid forward as this route. Sefa's guilt, spent as these maps. Tuck's village, staked on their speed. Bram's confiscated boat, wagered on flooded water it was never rated for. The Corricks' betrayal-turned-truce, cashed tonight. Even Ives—even the Syndicate itself—had a claim on this moment, because everything they'd stolen and squeezed and smiled at had pushed this boat up this channel in this storm.

Fine, she told the river. Collect.

* * *

The stairs appeared out of the rain like a rumor.

Cut into the spine's eastern face, forty steps, then a landing, then forty more, each tread worn concave by decades of boots that official maps insisted had never existed. Water sheeted down the rock face in braided ropes, and above them, somewhere in the whiteout, the vault waited with its steel door and its lever and its answer.

They tied off the boat in the lee of the first landing, and Bram shook Dominic's hand like he was handing something over.

"I can't take the stairs with this shoulder," he said. "Not carrying gear, not in this wind. I hold the boat. If you get the sluices shut, the river drops, and I bring her down to Widow's Bend to meet you. If you don't—" He shrugged. "If you don't, none of us are meeting anywhere."

"Bram—"

"Dominic." His grin faded into something older and steadier. "You burned nothing yet. You owe nobody a speech. Go close the door your father kept open."

She went.

* * *

The spine in a monsoon is not a place. It is an argument the wind is having with the rock, and you are the punctuation.

Four hundred feet of exposed traverse, handline rusted but holding, the drop on their left falling away into a gorge that was no longer visible—only audible, a vast wet breathing below them, the machine beneath the escarpment cycling its sluices in unscheduled, panicked test pulses. Each time the river inhaled, the spray rose up the cliff face like cold applause. Each time it exhaled, the whole spine seemed to lean.

Sefa went first, clipping and unclipping, moving with the economy of a woman who had spent years on government survey lines and had decided, somewhere in the last week, that she would never run from another one. Tuck came third, roped to Dominic, small and fierce, shouting measurements of the wind gaps between gusts because he'd learned the rhythm faster than either of them.

Halfway across, the wind took Sefa's map.

It tore free of her jacket, cartwheeled once against the rock, and was gone into the gorge, and Sefa stared after it for one long second—and then laughed, short and hard, and kept climbing.

"Good," she shouted back. "Now we do it from memory. Like the team should have."

* * *

The vault door was smaller than it had any right to be.

A steel slab set flush into the limestone, unmarked except for a hairline seam and a keyhole worn bright by use. Her father's key fit like it had been cut yesterday. The mechanism turned with a sound like a held breath releasing, and the door swung inward onto dry air, lamplight, and the hum of machinery that had been running, unattended and unowned, for years.

Dominic stood on the threshold with rain streaming off her and Tuck pressed against her side, and she understood that the last step of the climb wasn't behind her.

It was inside.

Somewhere below, through the rock, she could hear the sluices cycling again—faster now, the breathing gone ragged, the full-capacity test tearing loose from anything resembling schedule. Somewhere downstream, villages that didn't know yet were sleeping beside a river being taught to misbehave. Somewhere behind her, the Corricks were being loud and brave and possibly dying for it.

And somewhere in this vault, her father had left the lever, the claim, and the choice.

Dominic wiped the rain from her eyes, pulled Tuck inside, and sealed the door against the storm.

The hum from below changed pitch.

Something down there knew they'd arrived.
