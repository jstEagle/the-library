## 25. The Vault at the Source

The door was smaller than it should have been.

That was Dominic's first thought, standing in the rain with the storm screaming down the gorge behind her and Tuck's flashlight beam shaking against wet stone. After everything—the climb up the drowned spine of the escarpment, Bram hauling them through channels that hadn't existed yesterday, the Corricks' gunfire echoing somewhere below as they held the Syndicate's attention—after all of that, she had expected something monumental. A wall of steel. A gate.

Instead: a slab of concrete set into the hillside, barely taller than a man, with a recessed panel and a keyhole shaped like nothing she'd ever seen. The kind of door you'd find on a municipal pump station. The kind of door designed to be forgotten.

*Don't bring it down the mountain.* Her father's handwriting, in a logbook dated after his death. She'd been carrying those words for weeks now like a stone in her boot.

"Is this it?" Tuck's voice was thin under the rain. He was soaked to the skin, lips going gray, and he'd refused every offer to wait below. "This is the source?"

"This is the door to it," Sefa said. She'd gone very still, the way she did when old guilt surfaced. Her eyes moved over the concrete, the drainage channel cut beneath it, the faded stencil of a government survey mark. "I mapped half this escarpment and never knew this was here. It's not on any survey I signed."

"That was the point," Dominic said.

She reached into her jacket, into the inner pocket where she'd kept three things since the monitoring station: the compass, the last letter, and a key.

Not a key, exactly. Her father had called it that in his letters—*the master key*—but it was more like a machined artifact, dense brass and steel, with teeth on four faces and a core that clicked when you turned it in your fingers. He'd kept it in a drawer lined with felt, in a room he'd lived in for years while the world believed him dead. While his daughter believed it too.

She'd read his letters twice on the climb. Once for grief. Once for instructions.

*If you're reading this at the vault, then the storm came early and I'm sorry for that too. The key opens what the maps were built to hide. What you do after is yours. That was always the point of you, Dom—you never could leave a locked thing alone.*

"You okay?" Bram had come up behind them, breathing hard, one hand still on the rope he'd rigged down the flooded chute. Water streamed off his beard. Behind him, the river roared through the gorge, running backward again in slow, obscene pulses—the machine breathing beneath them, running its unscheduled test, filling itself for whatever came next.

"No," Dominic said honestly.

"Good," Bram said. "Means you're paying attention."

She knelt at the panel. Rain hammered her shoulders. The keyhole was a four-lobed socket, and when she fitted the key into it, the metal was warm. That unsettled her more than anything else—the warmth of it, like the door had been waiting.

"It'll need turning twice," Sefa said quietly. "Left, then right. I saw the mechanism specs once, before—" She stopped. Before the night the team vanished. Before the lights in the gorge and the sound of the river inhaling. "Before."

Dominic turned the key left. Something deep in the hillside shifted—a sound like a train changing tracks, transmitted through the rock into her knees. She turned it right.

The door swung inward without a sound. No groan of hinges, no scrape. Just a rectangle of darkness that breathed out air smelling of dust and ozone and, faintly, impossibly, of her father's coffee.

Tuck made a small noise. Sefa put a hand on his shoulder.

Dominic went in first.

* * *

The lights came on by themselves, bank by bank, following her down a corridor of clean concrete. Motion sensors. Forty years old if they were a day, and still working, because whoever built this place had built it to last longer than the government that funded it.

The corridor opened into a chamber the size of a chapel, domed, dry as bone despite the tons of water screaming past outside. And there the scale of it finally landed—not the room, but what the room *was*. Because the far wall wasn't a wall. It was glass. Forty feet of reinforced glass, and beyond it, lit by work lamps along its face, was the source itself.

The river didn't begin here. The river was *made* here.

Vast sluice gates, tall as cathedral doors, stood open in the rock face beyond the glass, and through them poured water from reservoirs carved into the heart of the escarpment—cisterns her father's generation had hollowed out in secret, fed by aquifers no map admitted existed. The water fell in silver sheets into a gathering basin, churned white, and funneled into the channel that became, ten miles downstream, the river every village on the lowlands drank from, fished from, named their children after.

And it was all controlled from this room. Levers. Wheels. A board of gauges and switches, brass and bakelite, arranged before a single operator's chair. One person, standing at that board, could open the mountain or close it. Could give the lowlands their river or take it away.

"One man," Sefa whispered. "It runs on one operator. That's why they needed the survey team silenced. Not because we found it. Because we understood what it meant—that whoever holds this room owns every drop between here and the sea."

Dominic walked to the operator's board slowly, her boots loud on the floor. There, on a hook beside the chair, hung a canvas jacket, faded, patched at the elbow. She knew that jacket. She'd bought the patch kit herself, age eleven, from the general store in Millbrook with money saved from mowing lawns.

Her father had worked here. Had stood at this board. Had turned these wheels and watched this water, year after year, while she grew up believing he'd died owing money.

He hadn't died owing anything. He'd died owning the most valuable room in the province—and had spent his life making sure nobody else learned it.

On the desk beside the board lay a folio, gray, government-stamped, tied with cord. The wax seal was broken; her father had read it many times. Dominic untied the cord with hands that wouldn't quite stay steady.

The claim documents.

All of them. The original engineering surveys. The water rights—full appropriation rights to every cistern, every sluice, every gallon the escarpment held, held in trust under a single name. Not the government's name. Not the Syndicate's.

*D. Dunmore.*

Her father's name. And beneath it, in fresh ink, an amendment filed years ago, transferring contingent title: *to heir, upon presentation of the master key.*

Her name. *Dominic Dunmore.* Written there in her father's careful hand, dated the week before he died, waiting for her the way a lighthouse waits for a ship it can't call to.

She sat down in the operator's chair because her legs stopped being interested in holding her.

* * *

They gave her a few minutes. She heard them moving quietly around the chamber—Sefa reading gauge boards, murmuring numbers; Tuck asking questions in a hushed voice and getting answers; Bram standing at the glass with his arms folded, watching the water fall, wearing the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

Then Bram came over and crouched by the chair. "Say it plain," he said. "How much?"

Dominic looked down at the folio in her lap. "Full appropriation rights. Every reservoir in the escarpment. The Syndicate bought the debt on this project years ago—they've been trying to find this room since before my father died. If I sign, if I sell, the claim clears every debt in the region. Mine. Yours. The Corricks'. Probably half the merchants in Millbrook." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "There's a line item in here for maintenance funding that's larger than my entire life has cost so far."

"And if you don't?"

"Then the rights revert to abandonment in five years, and the whole thing goes to auction anyway." She looked up at him. "Somebody will own this room, Bram. That was the design flaw. My father couldn't fix it. He could only choose who."

Bram nodded slowly, chewing on that. "And the lever?"

"The wheel," she corrected, nodding toward the board. "The sluices are running full-open right now. Unscheduled test. If it keeps raining the way it's raining, the downstream gates can't pass that volume. Tuck's village floods. Every village floods."

"So you close them."

"I close them," Dominic said, "and I demonstrate exactly what this room can do—to anyone watching. Including Ives, who is somewhere below us right now, climbing, with an enforcement detail and a smile." She pressed her palms against her eyes. "Every move I make in this chair tells the world what I'm worth. Closing the sluices proves the claim works. Opening them proves I'll use it. Doing nothing drowns the valley."

The vertigo hit her then, properly, the way she'd been dreading since the monitoring station. It wasn't fear of the Syndicate. It wasn't even grief. It was the specific, sickening lightness of holding something that outweighed everything she'd ever carried—every debt, every chased storm, every year of staying small and invisible and safe. She had spent her whole adult life being worth nothing on paper, and nothing on paper had a certain freedom to it. Nobody hunted a woman worth nothing.

Now she was worth a river.

"Dom." Tuck's voice. He'd come to stand beside the chair, dripping, solemn, twelve years old and older than that. "My village is downstream."

"I know."

"You'd close it. For us." It wasn't a question, but it wasn't entirely a statement either. He was asking her to say it out loud, the way kids do—asking her to make it real.

Dominic looked at him—at this boy who'd stowed away on her boat because the same company chasing her had bought his family's future out from under him—and felt the vertigo shift into something else. Not lighter. Steadier. Power, she realized, only tips you over if you try to stand on it alone.

"Yeah," she said. "First. Before anything else. Before Ives, before the debts, before any of it—the sluices get closed and your village stays dry. That happens whether I own this room for an hour or my whole life."

Tuck nodded once, satisfied, and went back to Sefa at the gauge board.

Bram squeezed her shoulder, hard, once, and followed him.

Dominic sat alone in her father's chair, in her father's room, with her name on a folio worth more than every debt in the region, and let herself feel the full weight of it for exactly as long as it took the rain on the glass to blur the falling water into something that looked almost like mercy.

Then she stood up, because the gauges were climbing, and because somewhere below in the dark, boots were coming up the spine of the escarpment, and because her father had left her one more thing besides the key and the claim:

A choice.

She put her hand on the main sluice wheel.

Behind her, from the corridor, came a sound that didn't belong—footsteps, unhurried, cheerful even now, even here, at the top of the world in the middle of a monsoon.

She knew that walk.

She didn't turn around.

"Well," said Ives, warm as a lamp in a cold room, "isn't this a lovely surprise. You found it after all."
