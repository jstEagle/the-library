## 26. Ives's Last Offer

The rain on the vault's upper hatch sounded like applause.

Ives stood in the corridor with water to his shins and a smile he had been maintaining for eleven days without a single crack, and he took stock of the situation the way he took stock of everything: as an inventory. One fugitive salvage pilot, cornered at the source of the Karst Escarpment with her hand on the most valuable piece of machinery in the province. One defected surveyor whose credentials were already in his breast pocket, traded for a boy who wasn't worth the paper. One river pilot and two Corrick brothers somewhere below, holding his enforcement detail's attention in flooded channels that were eating boats like they were made of sugar. And one monsoon, early, furious, and entirely off schedule—which was, in fairness, the fault of the machine under his feet running a test it was never supposed to run tonight.

None of it worried him. That was the thing people never understood about Ives, the thing that made them call him frightening when really he was only honest: none of it worried him because worry was not a line item. Worry did not appear anywhere in the ledger. The Syndicate had taught him that in his first year, when he'd wept over a family they'd dispossessed in Marrow Flats and his mentor, a soft-spoken woman named Odile with reading glasses on a chain, had taken his hand kindly and said, *Ives. Feelings are real. They're just not assets.* He had been twenty-three. He had never forgotten it, and he had never once, in nineteen years, let a feeling onto a balance sheet.

So he stood there in the flooded corridor, smiling at the back of Dominic Dunmore's head, and he did the arithmetic.

She didn't turn around. Good. People were easier to negotiate with before they turned around; the face always complicated things. He had learned that too. Faces were where feelings lived, and feelings were not assets.

"Dominic," he said, using her name now, because names opened doors that smiles alone couldn't. "You're standing on roughly forty percent of the fresh water in this region. Do you know what that's worth? I'll tell you, because it's my job to know, and knowing is a kind of kindness. It's worth more than every debt on the escarpment combined. More than every debt in three provinces. Your father built a fortune under a mountain and then hid it from everyone, including you, including me, including—" he paused, savoring it— "including the people who owned his debts. Which, incidentally, included him."

* * *

Ives had liked Edmund Dunmore. That was another thing nobody believed about him, and it happened to be true.

He'd met the man twice, years ago, across a table in the Vellum Syndicate's lowland office, back when Dunmore was still alive in the world's accounting and the reservoir project was still a rumor with excellent paperwork. The first meeting had been about restructuring. The second had been about silence. Both times, Dunmore had sat with his hands flat on the table like a man pinning down something that wanted to fly away, and both times he had listened more than he spoke, and both times Ives had come away thinking: *there is a man who understands what things cost.*

He'd been wrong, of course. Dunmore understood what things cost better than anyone Ives had ever met—he simply refused to pay any of it in the accepted currency. When the Syndicate had bought the project's debt out from under the government consortium, expecting to inherit the source claim with it, Dunmore had done something no ledger anticipated. He had died. On paper, thoroughly, expensively, with witnesses and a grave and a grieving daughter who inherited nothing but a compass and a head full of weather.

For six years the claim had sat in legal limbo, unenforceable, unownable, a fortune locked behind a dead man's signature—and Ives had spent those six years being the one person at the Syndicate who never stopped believing the door could be opened. That was why they called him the optimist. It was not affectionate. In the Syndicate, optimism was a diagnosis. But Odile had retired, and the new blood ran numbers instead of people, and so when the survey team vanished into the escarpment and the trail went cold, it was Ives who had proposed the salvage job. It was Ives who had picked Dominic Dunmore specifically—not despite her debt but because of it, because a drowning woman swims toward anything that floats, and because her father's compass would point where no map dared.

He had told himself it was kindness. He had almost believed it. That was the trick of the work, the part he'd mastered and the part that kept him sane: if you arranged the trap well enough, the person walking into it genuinely benefited, and so did you, and the only casualty was the truth about which of you had chosen.

And now here she was. At the door. With her hand on the wheel.

* * *

"Your father," Ives said, taking one careful step forward through the floodwater, "was the best client I ever had. I mean that. Most people lie to us about their assets. He lied to us about his existence." He let the smile widen a fraction. "You have his stubbornness. I recognized it the day I sat in your truck with your ledger open on my knee. You looked at me like I was weather. Most people look at me like I'm a wall."

Dominic's shoulder shifted. Not turning—just settling, the way a person settles before lifting something heavy. He catalogued it and continued.

"Here is what I've come to offer, and I want you to hear all of it, because I've watched you for eleven days now, up this mountain, through floods and portages and a rival crew and my own enforcement detail, and I have developed a genuine professional respect for you, which in my line is rarer than you'd think. So: full forgiveness. Every page of your ledger—the truck, the gear, the interest, the compounding, the little fees we don't advertise—all of it, closed, stamped, burned. And not just erased. Paid forward. A clean account, a letter of good standing, and a signing bonus large enough that you will never again have to chase a storm for a client who won't pay. You will never stand in a doorway while a man like me reads your life aloud to you. You will be the one who owns things."

He paused, because pauses were instruments, and let the rain fill the silence.

"All you have to do is sign the claim."

* * *

She turned around then, finally, and Ives got the face he'd been negotiating around, and it was worse than he'd prepared for—not because it was hard, but because it was tired. Rain-wet hair plastered to her forehead, a cut above her eyebrow from the climb, eyes that had been awake too long and had decided to stop pretending otherwise. She looked at him the way people look at a storm they've decided not to run from anymore.

"You came up the spine yourself," she said. "In this."

"I did." He gestured modestly at his soaked trousers, his ruined shoes. "I felt this conversation deserved my personal attention. My people are thorough but they lack nuance, and nuance is what you deserve."

"The boy. Tuck. You tried to take him at the station."

"I retrieved an asset," Ives said gently. "A minor with no claim on anything, whom your surveyor purchased with credentials that were never hers to sell. If it helps, I regret the method. Children shouldn't be collateral. But Dominic—" and here he let a real note enter his voice, because he had one, he kept it tuned like everything else he owned— "everything you've seen me do, I did to reach this room. Not the vault. This conversation. Because I knew, the moment I read your father's file six years ago, that it would end with the two of us, and that you would be standing exactly where you're standing, and that you would need someone to say plainly what everyone else in your life has been too cowardly or too sentimental to say."

"And what's that?"

"That keeping it costs more than selling it. That's all. That's the whole wisdom of my profession, and I'll give it to you free, which should tell you how much I want this signature." He spread his hands, palms up, an old gesture, an honest-looking one. "Look at what it's already cost. Your father spent years down here in the dark writing letters he never sent. Sefa's team is dead—every one of them, silenced by the same people who will send more men if you refuse me. The Corricks are drowning in a gorge for wages. That boy nearly lost his freedom today and his village will lose everything downstream if this machine keeps testing itself. And you—you are standing in a flood at the top of the world, exhausted, hunted, holding a lever that could kill thousands or a pen that could save one person completely." He tilted his head. "Guess which choice the river will thank you for."

* * *

The terrible thing—the thing Ives would carry out of this room either way, the thing he would think about later in whatever office they gave him or whatever road they put him on—was that he meant it. All of it. He was not performing sincerity; he had simply never learned the difference between performing it and having it, because in nineteen years no one had ever asked him to. The Syndicate did not hire liars. Liars were unreliable. The Syndicate hired believers, and Ives believed, with his whole tidy heart, in the ledger. In the beautiful, impartial, unsentimental idea that everything could be counted, and that what could be counted could be settled, and that a world where everything was settled was a world where nobody had to be afraid.

He had looked at Dominic Dunmore's debt—the truck, the gear, the interest snowballing through four bad seasons—and he had thought: *this woman is carrying a weight that can be removed. I am the man who removes weights. Therefore I am her friend.* It had never once occurred to him, not in eleven days of watching her haul boats and feed strays and sleep sitting upright against a gunwale, that the weight might be the least of what was holding her together. That some people are built around their debts the way a tree is built around a fence wire, and pull the wire and the whole shape changes.

He was about to find out.

"Sign," he said softly, and produced the folder from inside his coat, dry, because he had kept it dry the entire climb, pressed against his chest like a heartbeat, because he was a professional. "One signature. The claim transfers, the Syndicate holds the water rights, and the machine gets shut down properly—by engineers, with compensation schedules for every village downstream, funded out of the first year's revenue. Tuck's village doesn't just survive; it gets bought out at triple market. Sefa gets her credentials back and immunity. Bram gets his boat deconfiscated and a contract. Even the Corricks get paid, though between us, they'd betray you by Thursday regardless." He held out the pen. "Everyone walks away. Everyone lives. That is the offer, Dominic, and I have authority to make it, and I am making it because out of everyone in this entire rotten arrangement, you are the one person I believe deserves to walk away rich."

The pen hung in the air between them, and the rain hammered the hatch above like a crowd that couldn't decide whether to cheer or riot.

* * *

Dominic looked at the pen for a long time.

Then she looked past Ives, down the corridor, toward the dark throat of the stairwell where the floodwater ran, and said, quietly, "How many collectors did you bring?"

"Enough."

"And how many are left?"

Ives smiled, because the question deserved honesty and honesty was his best weapon. "Fewer than I brought."

"That's what I thought." She reached out—not for the pen, and he watched, with something adjacent to grief, as her hand passed it by—and gripped the sluice wheel beside her, and the iron groaned, and somewhere beneath their feet, vast and patient, the machine began to answer. "Here's my counter-offer, Ives. You get to leave. Wet, empty-handed, and owing me a favor I'll never collect. Take it. It's the last fair deal anyone in your profession is going to get on this mountain."

And Ives, the optimist, the believer in ledgers, felt—for the first time in nineteen years—a number appear on his books that he could not count.

Above them, the hatch buckled.
