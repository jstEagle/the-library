## 28. Closing the Sluices

*Sefa*

The vault smelled like her father's office—ozone and old paper and the faint mineral breath of deep stone—and Sefa hated it for that.

She stood at the control console with the shutdown manual open on its stand, pages soft as cloth from years of handling. Someone had read this document so many times the corners had gone round. She didn't have to guess who. The annotations in the margins were in a hand she'd last seen signing off on survey permits two years ago: Dominic's father, writing to himself, or maybe writing to whoever would finally stand here and mean it.

*If you are reading this because you intend to shut it down,* the margin note said, *start at the sluice master, not the pump relays. The relays will lie to you.*

"The relays will lie," Sefa said aloud.

Dominic looked up from the master key panel, rain still dripping off her braid onto the floor tiles. "Everything lies down here. Go on."

Sefa traced the sequence with her finger before touching anything. That was how you did it—you walked the whole procedure in your head first, every valve, every interlock, every point where the machine could refuse you. She'd been a surveyor for eleven years. She knew instruments the way Bram knew water, and she knew this much for certain: the people who built this place had never once imagined that someone would come down into the vault during a full-capacity test, in the teeth of a monsoon, with no claim to sign and nothing to gain, and simply turn it off.

The builders had planned for thieves. For saboteurs. For governments changing their minds. Every safeguard assumed greed. There was no lock against someone doing it for free.

"Sluice master first," she said. "Then we bleed the penstocks in order—east, center, west. If we do it wrong the pressure differential will crack the headrace tunnel and everything we're standing on becomes part of the river."

"And if we do it right?"

"Then the river goes back to being a river."

Dominic set her hand flat on the master key—a physical thing, absurdly analog, a lever of machined steel in a wall of humming consoles. Her father's key hung from her neck on a cord, and Sefa could see it moving with her pulse.

"Say it again," Dominic said. "The reason."

Sefa had expected the question. She'd asked it of herself all night while the storm hammered the mountain and the gauges climbed and Tuck slept badly against a supply crate, dreaming about water.

"Because nine people came up this river to stop this test and never came home." Her voice held. She made it hold. "Because I ran when they told me to erase everything, and running kept me alive but it didn't keep me clean. Because there are forty villages downstream and one of them is a boy's whole world. And because—" she touched the margin note, the dead man's handwriting, "—because somebody spent years down here making sure this moment was possible for us. It seems ungrateful not to finish it."

Dominic nodded once. That was enough. With Dominic, one nod carried more weight than most people's speeches.

"East penstock first," Sefa said. "On my count. And Dominic—if the interlocks fight us, if the system tries to protect itself, don't hesitate. Override everything. This machine has been protected long enough."

* * *

*Dominic*

The first override felt like breaking into her own house.

That was the only way Dominic could describe it—the resistance in the console wasn't hostile, exactly. It was proprietary. The system queried her father's key, found it valid, found it *master*, and then hesitated anyway, as if the machine itself had been taught that no one holding that key would ever use it for this.

"Authorization accepted," the console said, in a voice like a patient clerk. "Note: shutdown during active capacity test voids operational warranty and terminates all downstream service contracts."

"Service contracts," Dominic repeated.

"Fourteen municipal agreements," the console said helpfully. "Termination exposes the operator to liability exceeding—"

"Who's the operator?" Sefa cut in.

There was a pause. A real pause, Sefa thought later—the machine consulting its own ledgers and finding them embarrassing.

"Vellum Water Holdings," the console said. "A subsidiary."

Of course it was. Of course the Syndicate's name was already printed inside the mountain, waiting for them like a spider in a dry room. Her debt, Sefa's dead team, Tuck's village buyout—all of it flowing downhill from this one console, this one lever, this one company that had bought the water before anyone thought to ask whether water could be sold.

"Terminate them," Dominic said.

"Confirm termination of fourteen municipal service—"

"I confirm. All of them. Void everything." She leaned toward the pickup grille. "And log the authorization under Dunmore, E., master key holder."

The machine paused again. "Elias Dunmore is recorded as deceased."

"He got better," Dominic said. "Log it."

The console logged it. Somewhere beneath their feet, three hundred meters of rock and older rock, the east penstock began to close—not fast, nothing down here was fast, the builders had designed every movement to be dignified, deliberate, irreversible-but-slow—and the sound came up through the floor like a whale turning over.

Then the machine fought back.

It was subtle at first. The display showing penstock position froze at forty percent and began, gently, reasonably, to suggest they reconsider. A maintenance advisory appeared recommending postponement until post-storm inspection. When Sefa overrode that, the system rerouted the shutdown command through a relay that didn't exist on any schematic she'd memorized—a ghost circuit, undocumented, designed by someone who'd trusted no one, possibly including himself.

"It's lying," she said. "The relays lie. My father wrote it down and I still almost believed the screen."

"Where does the ghost circuit actually go?"

Sefa pulled the manual off its stand and flipped to the wiring addendum—the loose pages, the ones annotated in three different hands across what must have been a decade. Elias Dunmore's notes marched down the margins like a man arguing with the future. *They added this after year two. Bypass here. Trust the gauge, not the light.*

"Gauge," Sefa said. "Physical gauge, north wall. If the needle says closed, it's closed, whatever the screen claims."

The needle said thirty-eight percent and falling. Slowly. Agonizingly. Above them, through a hundred meters of limestone, the monsoon was doing its best to drown the argument, and somewhere downstream the river was rising around villages that had already evacuated because a boy's stolen radio data and a defected surveyor's broadcast had gotten there in time.

In time. Everything now depended on *in time*.

"Center penstock next," she said. "While the east one finishes."

* * *

*Sefa*

The center penstock was where the grief caught up with her.

She'd been outrunning it since the night of the lights in the gorge—two years of running, first physically, then professionally, taking jobs that kept her moving, kept her measuring other people's water so she'd never have to think about the team she'd left behind. Okafor, who'd taught her to read karst. Mireles, who sang off-key. Team lead Vashti Corren, whose final order—*erase everything, take nothing, tell no one*—had sounded like madness until tonight, when Sefa finally understood it had been a shield thrown over a secret too heavy to carry and too dangerous to drop.

They hadn't vanished. They'd been silenced for refusing to run this test. And Sefa had survived by obeying half the order—telling no one—while breaking the other half, keeping the maps, keeping the credentials, keeping the guilt folded up small in her chest where it could ache quietly for two years.

Now she stood at the center penstock control with her hand on the release, and the grief arrived all at once, the way floodwater arrives through a karst window—no warning, just the ground opening.

"Sefa." Dominic's voice, low. Not hurrying her. Just anchoring her.

"They stood right here." Sefa's hand was steady; she was grateful to it for that. "Okafor stood right here and said no, and they killed him for it, and the machine logged it as a personnel attrition event. I've seen the logs. Attrition. Like weather."

"We can't bring them back."

"No." She swallowed. "But we can make their refusal count. That's the only monument they get, Dominic. Us. Finishing it."

She threw the release.

The center penstock answered immediately—it was the newest, the one the Syndicate's engineers had rebuilt during the takeover, and it closed with an efficiency the old machinery lacked, which was somehow worse, proof of how much money had flowed into making the theft work smoothly. Sixty percent. Eighty. The needle swung, honest and mechanical, and the sound beneath the mountain changed pitch, the great inhale-exhale rhythm they'd heard at the gorge faltering, stuttering, losing its meter.

The river was learning to breathe on its own again.

"One more," Dominic said.

* * *

*Dominic*

The west penstock refused.

Not subtly, this time. The moment Dominic keyed the command, alarms woke throughout the vault—not klaxons, something more civilized than that, a soft chime and a courteous text banner: *MANUAL INTERVENTION DETECTED. SAFETY LOCKOUT ENGAGED. PLEASE CONTACT YOUR VELLUM REPRESENTATIVE.*

"Contact your Vellum representative," Dominic read aloud. "Ives is going to be so disappointed he missed this call."

"Lockout's physical," Sefa said, already moving. "See the housing? Manual wheel behind the panel. The electronic lockout is theater—they always leave the mechanical failsafe, because the one thing the builders feared more than saboteurs was being locked out of their own machine during a failure."

The panel came away with a pry bar and bad language. Behind it: a wheel the size of a wagon tire, recessed in the rock, its spokes worn bright by hands. Elias Dunmore's hands, probably. Years of them.

"It'll take both of us," Sefa said.

They put their backs into it together. The wheel resisted, then groaned, then gave in increments, each quarter-turn accompanied by a change in the vault's deep hum, the mountain's plumbing rearranging itself around their stubbornness. Dominic's shoulders burned. Rainwater and sweat stung her eyes. She thought, absurdly, of the collector's ledger—of numbers in neat columns, of debts accruing interest in the dark—and turned the wheel another quarter-turn as if she were tearing a page out.

*This is what the debt was,* she thought. *Not money. This. Fourteen villages' worth of this.*

The needle reached zero.

For a moment nothing happened. The vault hummed its dying note, the consoles blinked their courteous complaints, and Dominic had time to think *maybe it didn't work, maybe we did all this for—*

Then the river steadied.

She felt it before any instrument confirmed it—a change in the vibration coming up through the floor, the pulsing rhythm that had haunted them since Widow's Bend smoothing out into something continuous, something natural. Sefa lunged for the external gauge bank, the old analog dials wired directly to sensors in the channel, and watched the needles settle like birds coming to roost.

"Flow rate normalizing," Sefa read, and her voice cracked on the second word. "Stage height dropping. Turbidity—" she laughed, wet-eyed, disbelieving. "Turbidity's clearing. The river's settling its own sediment. It hasn't done that in two years. It hasn't been allowed to."

Dominic put her hand on the console, on the dead lever, on the warm metal of the machine that had eaten a survey team and nearly eaten a valley, and felt it go quiet under her palm. Not silent—the mountain was never silent—but quiet the way a held breath is quiet when it finally lets go.

Downstream, water was arriving in Tuck's village the way water was supposed to arrive: late, ordinary, asking nothing.

* * *

*Sefa*

They sat on the vault floor afterward, backs against the console, sharing a canteen neither of them had the appetite for, listening to the storm spend itself against the mountain.

"You know what gets me," Sefa said eventually. "The builders. The original ones, before the Syndicate. They engineered every kind of protection—locks, ghost circuits, legal tripwires, a whole architecture of distrust. But they never once imagined this." She gestured at the shutdown console, the zeroed needles, the two of them sitting in the wreckage of a billion-unit scheme. "Someone walking in with a valid key and no price. The system literally doesn't have a defense for it. We didn't beat the security. We confused it."

"Good," Dominic said. "Let it stay confused."

Above them, faint through the rock, boots on the access stairs. Then Tuck's voice, cracking with the effort of sounding casual: "The gauges stopped doing the weird thing. Bram sent me down to check you weren't dead. Also there's tea. Also I'm not going back up those stairs twice."

Sefa laughed—really laughed, the first time in two years, and it hurt her ribs and she didn't care. The boy came the rest of the way down, took one look at the open panels and the zeroed dials and the two exhausted women on the floor, and understood, the way children understand large things without needing them explained.

"It's off," he said. "It's really off? The village—"

"Is getting its river back," Sefa said. "All of them are."

Tuck sat down between them without asking, which was, Sefa reflected, exactly how he'd joined the crew in the first place, and exactly why he belonged to it.

* * *

*Dominic*

Later—much later, when Tuck had gone back up with his report and the tea, when the storm had settled into its long midnight grind—Dominic stood alone at the console and opened the furnace hatch.

The claim documents were still in her pack. The full inheritance: water rights worth more than every debt in the region, signed, sealed, legally hers through her father's key, hers to sell, hers to trade, hers to burn. Tomorrow she would decide. Tonight she just needed to look at them one more time in the furnace light, to feel the full vertigo of what she was holding.

She was reaching for the pack when the vault's comm panel chimed—an incoming transmission on the Syndicate's private band, the frequency Ives had used at the station door, patched automatically into the master console he'd never dreamed would fall out of his reach.

Dominic hesitated. Then, because she was no longer a woman who flinched from voices, she keyed it open.

Ives's voice came through, stripped of its studio polish by distance and weather, and underneath the cheer—for the first time since she'd met him in her truck with his ledger and his smile—there was something else. Something frayed.

"Dominic. The flow telemetry just went flat. Every market indicator on the source went flat at once, and my board is asking me questions I don't have answers to, and I find myself—" a pause, a breath, the sound of a man discovering the limits of optimism, "—I find myself needing to know. Did you do it? Did you actually—"

She let him talk to the silence for a while.

Then she keyed the channel one last time. "Check your ledger, Ives," she said. "One entry. Paid in full."

She closed the channel, and turned to the furnace, and opened her father's letters to choose which single page she would keep—and that was when she heard, above the storm, above the settling mountain, the unmistakable sound of the vault's outer door grinding open on rusted hinges that no one on her side of it had touched.
