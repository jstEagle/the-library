## 29. The Burning

The vault smelled of cold iron and old paper, and after the storm's roar outside it was the quietest place Dominic had ever stood in.

She had expected—she didn't know what. Gold light. A hum of power. Some cathedral feeling to match what the documents were worth. Instead it was a concrete room the size of her truck, lit by a single caged bulb, with a bank of filing cabinets along one wall and a furnace in the corner that some long-ago engineer had installed for burning classified waste. The claim documents sat in a steel case on the table, bound in government-issue green, stamped with a survey number she now knew by heart because her father had written it on everything: his letters, his maps, the margins of the logbook Sefa carried like a relic.

*Hydrographic Survey KS-114. Karst Escarpment Source. Water Rights, Full Engineering Claim.*

Worth more than every debt in the region. Worth her freedom, twice over, with enough left to buy a life she'd stopped letting herself imagine years ago—a house on high ground, a boat that didn't leak, Tuck fed and schooled, Bram's boat returned with interest, Sefa's name cleared in a way that didn't require burning anything at all.

All of it was sitting right there in a green folder, and all she had to do was carry it down the mountain instead of carrying it to the furnace.

"You don't have to decide standing up," Sefa said from the doorway. She'd been there a while. Dominic hadn't heard her come in over the rain hammering the access tunnel behind them. "I used to tell the team that. Big decisions go better sitting down."

"Did they listen?"

"No." Sefa came into the room properly, shaking water off her jacket. "Halvard used to make decisions mid-stride and then defend them for six months. That's how we ended up—" She stopped. The sentence ended where the team had ended, which was nowhere anyone could find them. "That's not why I came down."

"Why did you come down?"

"To watch." Sefa said it without flinching. "Whatever you choose, I want to have seen it. I ran once. I'm not running from this room."

Dominic looked at the folder. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. She'd spent three weeks on this river with her heart going like a gauge needle in a flash flood, and now, at the exact center of everything, her body had gone calm as a barometer before a change.

Outside, muffled through meters of rock, the monsoon kept working. Somewhere above them Bram was holding the Corricks' attention on the east channel, keeping the Syndicate's enforcement detail pointed at weather instead of at the tunnel mouth. Somewhere downstream Tuck's village was either evacuated or drowning, depending on whether Sefa's broadcast had reached them in time. Somewhere in this room, in one of these cabinets, were the letters her father had written her for eleven years and never sent.

She'd read four of them. She'd stopped there—not because she couldn't bear more, but because she'd needed to be sure of what she was about to do *before* she let him talk to her any further. Dead men are persuasive. Dead men who loved you are unbearable.

"Dominic." Sefa pulled a stool out from under the table and set it down with a small, deliberate clack. "Sit or don't. But I've done the math you're doing, and I can save you the hour. If you sign, you're free and everyone you love is still owned. That's the whole ledger. There's no line item where selling it saves the villages. Ives will promise one. He'll even believe it while he promises. But the Syndicate doesn't buy water rights to leave them alone."

"I know."

"I know you know. My father used to say knowing and believing live in different rooms of the house." Sefa leaned against the filing cabinets, arms crossed. "You just have to walk from one room to the other."

Dominic sat down. The stool was cold through her wet clothes. She opened the steel case.

The documents inside were beautiful, in the way that instruments are beautiful. Survey plates. Flow measurements taken across eleven years by a man who was supposed to be dead, each sheet initialed in that slanted, impatient hand she'd have known anywhere—in a library book, on a grocery list, on the back of a photograph. The engineering schematics for the sluices beneath the gorge. The reservoir footprint, drawn honestly, covering half the karst and every lowland crossing below it. And on top, the claim itself: title, provenance, transfer rights. A single signature away from being the most valuable piece of paper between here and the coast.

Her father had signed nothing. Eleven years in this mountain and he'd left the claim unsigned, and she understood that now the way she understood weather—not as information but as pressure, as a front she could feel arriving in her own chest. He hadn't been able to destroy it either. He'd left it exactly here, in the middle, for someone else to decide. That wasn't a gift. It might have been the cruelest thing he ever did to her, and he'd done it from beyond his own grave.

"He couldn't burn it," she said aloud.

"No," Sefa agreed. "I don't think he could."

"But he could have. He had the furnace. He had eleven years."

"And then who would have known the machine existed? Who would have known what the Syndicate bought? The claim isn't just ownership, Dominic. It's evidence. As long as it exists unsigned, it proves everything—the project, the debt structure, the buyout, all of it." Sefa pushed off the cabinets and crossed to the table. "I told you I filed the logbook with Marisol Vane before we climbed. The story's already moving. But Vane's story is words. This is paper with a government seal on it. Words get denied. Paper gets audited."

"So don't burn it. Give it to Vane."

"That's an option." Sefa said it carefully, the way you handle something with edges. "It's also an option to keep it. Sign it yourself. You'd be within your rights. Nobody in this room would think less of you."

Dominic looked up. "You'd think less of me."

Sefa held her gaze for a long moment, and then, honest to the bone the way she always was, nodded once. "Yes. And I'd still row you off this mountain tomorrow. Both things are true. That's what found family means—you get to disappoint us and stay."

Something in Dominic's chest went loose and hot. She laughed—one short, cracked sound—and put her face in her hands for a second. When she looked up, Sefa was watching her with the expression of a woman watching a gauge she trusted.

"The villages," Dominic said. "If I sell, the sluices get rebuilt. Bigger. The test runs again, scheduled this time, with lawyers. Tuck's village becomes a lake bed with a compensation office. And if I give it to Vane, it becomes evidence in a fight that takes years, and the Syndicate spends those years buying judges and delaying audits until the paper rots." She turned the folder over in her hands. "There's only one version where nobody owns this."

"Burning it," Sefa said quietly.

"Burning it. The discovery is worthless if it can be owned. So it stops being a discovery. It goes back to being a river." She stood. "Vane gets the logbook, the letters' contents, my testimony, Sefa's testimony, the gauge data. Everything except the thing that makes it property. Let them fight over a ghost. Let them audit ash."

"They'll never stop looking for it. They'll dig this mountain apart."

"Good," Dominic said. "Let them spend their money on holes in the ground instead of people."

* * *

The furnace took ten minutes to heat, and she spent them reading.

Not the claim. The letter—the fourth one, the one she'd stopped on, folded soft as cloth now from handling. She read it standing in the furnace's growing warmth, and she read it slowly, because it was the last time it would exist only for her.

*Dominic—if you're reading this, then either you found me or you found what I couldn't finish. I'm sorry for both. I watched you learn storms from the porch of a house I was paying for with lies, and you learned them better than I ever did, and I need you to know that the compass wasn't sentiment. A compass doesn't tell you where to go. It tells you where you are. That's the whole trick of it. Most people die never knowing where they are—they just know what they owe. Don't be most people. Whatever they offer you for this mountain, remember: the river was here first, and it will be here last, and it has never once asked anyone for payment.*

She folded it along its worn creases and put it inside her jacket, against her ribs, where the compass rode on its lanyard.

Then she took the claim documents—all of them, the plates and the schematics and the unsigned title, eleven years of her father's careful, damning work—and she carried them to the furnace door.

It opened on orange light and a sound like a held breath.

"You'll want to feed it slowly," Sefa said. "Paper that thick smothers the fire if you rush it."

"How many times have you burned things in furnaces, Sefa?"

"More than a surveyor should admit to." But her voice had gone rough, and when Dominic glanced over, Sefa was looking at the documents the way you look at a grave. "Those plates are Halvard's flow measurements. He took them in a kayak, in February, in water with ice in it. He was so proud of them." A breath. "Burn them well, then. They deserve a good fire."

Dominic fed the claim in first. The title page caught at the corners, curled, blackened, and lifted into flame with a whump of displaced air, and the government seal went from green to gold to nothing. She held it until the fire took it fully, then let the last unburned corner drop.

The schematics next. Then the plates, one at a time, the way Sefa said. Each one flared and folded and became light. Eleven years of February kayaks and midnight calculations and a dead man's stubborn, secret love, all of it going up a steel throat into the dark above the escarpment, and Dominic stood in the heat with tears running freely down her face and made no effort at all to wipe them away, because some fires you're supposed to feel.

The last thing in the case was a single sheet she hadn't noticed before—a transfer-of-rights form, blank, with her father's initials in the corner and a space where a signature would make someone rich. She held it over the flames and paused, one last time, the way you pause at a door.

Ives's voice was in her head, cheerful as ever: *Full forgiveness, Ms. Dunmore. Walk away free.* The Corrick brothers, splitting the difference. The version of herself who signed, who came down the mountain solvent and safe and hollow, who bought back her invisibility at the price of everyone else's.

The compass hung against her ribs, pointing, as always, at wherever she actually was.

She was here. That was the whole trick of it.

She let the form go.

It burned fast, almost eagerly, and when it was gone she closed the furnace door and stood in the sudden dimness listening to the fire work, and what she felt was not grief, exactly, and not relief. It was lighter than both. It was the feeling of setting down a pack you'd forgotten you were wearing—your shoulders rising, your breath coming easier, the world suddenly larger in every direction. She owed the Syndicate money. She would probably always owe the Syndicate money, on paper, in ledgers, in the little smiling books Ives carried. But she did not owe them this. She had never owed them this. The debt had been theirs all along—owed to the river, to the drowned valley, to the vanished team whose names Sefa was restoring sheet by sheet—and she had just paid it the only way it could be paid.

"Well," Sefa said, into the quiet. Her voice wobbled and steadied. "Halvard would have hated that. And then, after about a day, he'd have thought it was the best idea anyone ever had."

"That's the nicest eulogy I've ever heard."

"It's the only kind I give." Sefa wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, and then straightened. "The fire's hot enough to take the rest of the cabinet files if we want. Payroll records. The internal memos naming who ordered the survey erased. Evidence burns the same as claims."

Dominic considered it. Then shook her head. "Leave those. Those aren't ownership—those are accountability. Vane can subpoena the ashes of those if she has to." She looked around the vault one last time: the cabinets, the table, the caged bulb, the furnace ticking as it cooled. Her father's room, emptied now of its terrible inheritance, holding nothing but truth and heat. "We take what can't be owned and can't be sold. We leave what can convict."

They climbed back toward the tunnel, and at the threshold Dominic stopped and put her hand flat against the cold rock wall, the way you touch a headstone.

"Bye, Dad," she said. "Debt's paid. River's free."

The mountain said nothing, which was, she thought, exactly what an honest man would say.

* * *

They came up out of the access tunnel into the teeth of the storm, and the world had changed shape while they were underground.

Bram was waiting at the tunnel mouth under an overhang, soaked to the skin, grinning like a maniac, and behind him the Corrick brothers' skiff sat low in flooded water where dry stone should have been. "You took your time," Bram shouted over the rain. "Syndicate detail's pinned in the east channel—Corricks strung them out chasing decoys—but that won't hold past dawn. And the river's coming up faster than anything I've run. We need to move *now*, and we need to move heavy."

"Move heavy?" Sefa shouted back.

Bram jerked his thumb downstream, toward the black roaring gap of Widow's Bend, barely visible through the sheets of rain. "The gauge data you broadcast—the flood pulse is peaking early. Every crossing between here and the lowlands drowns by morning. There's one window, one route, and it runs straight through the worst water on this mountain." His grin widened, and there was no fear in it at all, which was somehow the most frightening thing on the entire escarpment. "So. You burned it, then?"

Dominic touched the compass at her ribs and the letter beside it, and felt the absence of the folder like a missing tooth—strange, new, and impossibly light.

"Burned it," she said. "Everything except what can't be owned."

Bram looked at her for a beat, rain streaming off his chin, and then threw back his head and laughed. "Then there's nothing left on this mountain worth stopping for," he yelled, "which means the only thing left to do is survive getting off it!"

He turned the skiff's bow toward Widow's Bend, and the river, swollen and singing, seemed to lean in to listen.
