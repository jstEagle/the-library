## 30. The Descent Begins

Bram had spent thirty years learning what water did when it got angry, and he'd never seen it like this.

The river below the monitoring station wasn't a river anymore. It was a freight train that had jumped its rails and decided to keep going anyway—brown, roaring, carrying whole trees sideways in its grip like a man shaking off dogs. The rain came down in sheets so thick he couldn't see the far bank of the gorge, couldn't see the sky, could barely see Dominic standing three feet away shouting at him through cupped hands.

He read her lips more than heard her. *Same route. Down. Now.*

His hands shook on the wheel of the *Marrow*, his confiscated boat turned rescue craft turned something else entirely, and he hated that they shook. Not fear. Fear he knew how to carry; fear was just ballast with a bad attitude. This was the other thing—the thing he'd felt once before, at the crossing where he'd lost his license and nearly lost his boat, when he'd realized the water didn't care about his skill. It only cared about whether he respected it.

He respected it now. God, he respected it.

"Corricks are holding at the upper sluice," Sefa said, appearing at his elbow with a radio pressed to her ear. Rain ran off her hood in ropes. "They've got the Syndicate detail pinned in the old haul road. Ossie says—" static chewed the transmission— "says they can give us an hour. Maybe less."

An hour. Bram looked at the flooded channel that was supposed to be their exit route and did the math he didn't want to do. Nine days up, everyone said. The monsoon had given them six, then four, then this—a storm that had come down the escarpment like it held a grudge, early and hard and personal.

One hour to run a descent that should have taken two days.

"We're not making Widow's Bend before dark," he said. "We're not making it before anything. We're making it or we're dying on this water, and I need everybody to understand which of those is more likely."

Dominic turned from the rail. Her face was gray with exhaustion, hair plastered flat, and there was a burn blister along one forearm from the vault furnace—he'd watched her feed the claim documents into it piece by piece, feeding ten years of her life into fire, and she hadn't flinched until the last page caught. But her eyes were clear. Clearer than he'd ever seen them.

"Bram." She gripped his shoulder. "You're the pilot. If you say we wait out the peak, we wait."

He wanted to say wait. Every instinct built over three decades of river work said wait—wait for the crest, wait for the surge to pass, wait for daylight, wait for anything. Waiting was what kept pilots alive.

But waiting meant the Syndicate's second enforcement team, already moving down the east ridge. Waiting meant the flood stage rising past the point where the lower channels were even navigable. Waiting meant Tuck's village downstream, warned but not yet evacuated, betting everything on data Sefa had broadcast on a borrowed frequency and a boy's faith that people would listen.

Waiting was what the Corricks were doing for them right now, pinned under gunfire at a sluice gate, paying a debt they'd promised to defer and then, apparently, decided to pay all at once.

"No," Bram heard himself say. "We go now. While they're holding the door."

* * *

Loading the boats in that rain was an act of controlled madness.

The *Marrow* took the weight: Dominic, Sefa, the gauge case with its irreplaceable data, the fuel they had left, which was almost none. The second boat—one of the survey team's rigid inflatables, patched twice since the portage—took Tuck and the gear that wouldn't drown: the letters, the compass, the logbook wrapped in oilcloth against Sefa's chest like a second heartbeat.

Tuck stood at the edge of the water with his jaw set in a way Bram recognized, because he'd worn the same expression at fourteen, telling his own father he was going to be a pilot whether the river approved or not.

"I can handle the inflatable," the boy said.

"You can handle it in calm water," Bram said. "This isn't calm water. This is the river deciding what it wants to be today, and today it wants to be a landslide."

"So I tie myself in and I steer anyway."

Bram opened his mouth to refuse him and found Dominic looking at him—not challenging, just watching, the way she watched storms. Letting him make the call. That was the thing about her nobody understood who hadn't been out here: she didn't lead by commanding. She led by trusting you in front of witnesses, so that failing her became impossible.

"Tether line," Bram said finally. "Twelve feet, clipped to the bow ring. You go in the drink, you stay attached to my boat, and you do exactly what I say, when I say it, no hero moves. Deal?"

Tuck spat into the flood, some village ritual, and grinned. "Deal."

They pushed off into brown chaos at 4:17 in the afternoon, though afternoon was a technicality. It might as well have been midnight. The first mile nearly killed them twice.

The drowned route—Sefa's phrase, from the falsified maps, the real route her dead guide had marked—ran through a chain of channels that in dry season were barely creeks. In this water they were torrents funneling between karst walls, and every junction was a decision made at speed with incomplete information. Bram steered by memory and by feel, reading the surface the way his mother had taught him to read faces: the V-shape of a submerged rock, the smooth glassy tongue of a hydraulic, the boil lines that told him where deep water ran and where the river was lying about its depth.

"Hard left!" Sefa screamed, pointing. "Channel narrows!"

He went hard left. The *Marrow* climbed a standing wave, hung at the top for one impossible moment with the propeller singing free air, and slammed down the back side hard enough to knock Dominic to her knees. She came up laughing—a wild, cracked sound, the laugh of someone who had burned everything she owned and discovered she was still standing—and Bram thought, absurdly, *she's going to be fine. Whatever happens, that woman is going to be fine.*

Behind them, Tuck's inflatable surfed the same wave. The boy stood in the stern, tethered, steering with his whole body, and he was *good*. Scary good. Village-boy good, raised on this water in its gentler moods, translating everything he knew into this new violent language faster than Bram could teach it.

Then the sound reached them.

It came up through the hull first, through the water itself—a deep subsonic groan, the mountain clearing its throat. Bram had heard the river inhale once before, at the gorge, when the sluices cycled. This was different. Bigger. The full-capacity test, still running unscheduled beneath their feet, and somewhere upstream the machine was opening everything at once.

"The lower channels," Sefa breathed. "If the test pushes a surge down the main stem—"

"How long?"

"Minutes. Maybe."

Maybe. Bram made the call he'd been dreading: he abandoned the planned route. No more careful channel-hopping. He pointed the *Marrow*'s bow straight down the throat of the main flooded gorge, where the current was fastest and the water was deepest and the only obstacles were the ones that could kill them instantly instead of slowly.

"Hold on!" he roared. "Everybody hold on and pray to whatever you've got!"

They ran the gorge.

He would remember it afterward in fragments, because that was how the mind saved things it couldn't hold whole: the walls blurring past, wet stone streaked with runoff like the escarpment was crying from every pore. Dominic bailing with a ration tin, methodical, unhurried, as if bailing were a form of prayer. Sefa with the logbook inside her jacket, one arm locked around the gauge case, her lips moving—counting, he realized later. Counting the bends ahead from memory, calling them to him half a second before he needed them, surveyor and pilot braided into one instrument.

And behind them, always behind them, the smaller boat and the boy, tethered, upright, *steering*.

The surge caught them at the third bend.

It came around the corner like a wall—six feet of moving water, maybe eight, rolling and brown and crowned with foam, pushing a headwave that lifted the *Marrow* from behind and threw her forward faster than Bram had ever taken any boat anywhere. For ninety seconds he didn't steer. Steering was meaningless. He *aimed*—kept the bow pointed down-slope, kept the hull from broaching, rode the monster's shoulders while it carried them toward the sea.

When it finally spent itself into the wider water below the gorge, they were five miles downstream and all of them were screaming, and none of them could have said with what emotion.

* * *

Dusk, or what passed for it. They pulled into a karst alcove above the flood line, a black mouth in the limestone where the water couldn't reach, and checked each other like survivors of a wreck counting heads.

All present. All breathing. Tuck's inflatable had a torn chine and half its air gone, but the boy had brought it in clean, and Bram caught himself clapping him on the shoulder harder than he meant to, and the boy beaming like he'd been handed a license.

Then the radio crackled.

Ossie Corrick's voice, stripped down to gravel by static and distance: "*Marrow*, Marrow, this is Corrick. We are disengaging. Repeat, disengaging. Bought you your hour and then some. How'd the run go?"

Bram keyed the mic. "We're through the gorge. Five miles down, all hands. Where are you?"

A pause. Then, dry as dust: "Wet."

Laughter broke out around the alcove—genuine, helpless laughter, the kind that comes out of people when terror lets go of its grip. Even Sefa laughed, and Sefa hadn't laughed since the camp.

"They're falling back to the haul road," Ossie continued. "Syndicate's got boats on the lower river, though. Two, maybe three. They'll sweep the channels at first light looking for you. And Bram—" the voice dropped, went quiet in a way radios shouldn't be able to manage— "we're done running. Whatever you need at Widow's Bend tomorrow, we'll be there. Me and Denny both. Consider us paid up."

Bram stared at the radio. The Corrick brothers had shadowed them, threatened them, traded truces built on mutual distrust and explicit promises of future betrayal. And now they were offering themselves as escort at the most dangerous crossing on the river, for nothing, for free, for—

For the same reason, he supposed, that a defected surveyor carried a dead man's logbook against her heart. For the reason a stowaway boy steered a sinking inflatable through a gorge rather than ride safe in someone else's wake. For the reason a debt-ridden storm chaser fed her inheritance into a furnace rather than sell it.

Some debts you paid in money. Some you paid in truth. And some—the only ones worth having—you paid by showing up.

"Copy, Corrick," Bram said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Widow's Bend. First light. Don't be late."

"Wouldn't dream of it." A pause, and then, almost gentle: "Tell the boy he steers better than his pilot does."

Tuck, listening over Bram's shoulder, glowed like a lamp.

* * *

Night settled over the alcove, and the storm peaked above them—they could hear it hammering the plateau, could see the lightning strobing the clouds from within like something enormous turning over in its sleep—but down here in the gorge the rain slackened to a steady drumming, and for the first time in days, nobody was moving.

Sefa broadcast the gauge data again on the borrowed frequency, updated with the surge readings, pushing it downstream toward the villages one more time. Dominic sat with her back against the limestone, the one letter she'd saved open on her knees, reading it by flashlight in small sips, the way you drank something you couldn't afford to finish. Tuck slept the boneless sleep of the young, wedged between the two adults nearest him, and neither adult moved away.

Bram took first watch at the alcove mouth, because taking first watch was what he did, and because he wanted to look at the water.

It was still high. Still angry. But the pulse had changed—the frantic surging settling into something longer, slower, the river finding a rhythm again after the machine's convulsions. Sefa's shutdown sequence was holding, then. Somewhere above them, buried in the mountain, the sluices were closing for good, and the water was remembering what it was supposed to be.

He thought about his boat. Confiscated, impounded, written off years ago in a ledger somewhere—and here she was, running a flooded gorge with a burned-out fugitive, a deserter, a boy, and a fortune's worth of ash floating somewhere upstream. The Syndicate had taken his boat on paper. They'd never actually managed to take it at all.

Footsteps behind him. Dominic lowered herself onto the wet stone beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched.

"He'd have liked you," she said, after a while. "My father. He liked people who showed up."

"I mostly show up because I'm bad at leaving."

"That's the same thing." She watched the river. "First light. Widow's Bend. You know what's down there."

"I know what's down there." Everyone did. The worst crossing on the escarpment, in the highest water anyone had recorded, with Syndicate boats sweeping the channels and two Corricks as their only cover. "I know."

"And?"

Bram looked out at the dark water running true at last, source to sea, carrying nothing that belonged to anybody.

"And I've been waiting thirty years for a run worth the risk," he said. "Get some sleep, Dominic. We cast off at gray light."

She squeezed his shoulder once and went back inside. Above the gorge, the storm thundered on, spending itself against the escarpment, and Bram sat his watch alone with the river, counting the hours until dawn, listening—for the first time in his life without dread—to the sound of water coming down the mountain.
