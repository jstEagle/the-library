## 31. Ives Undone

The message came through at 4:17 in the morning, which was the first sign.

Ives kept irregular hours by design—optimism, he had learned, required being awake when other men despaired—but the Syndicate's regional office did not transmit at 4:17. The Syndicate transmitted at business hours, in clean batches, with the tidy indifference of a machine that never needed to apologize. A transmission before dawn meant someone had been sitting on a decision all night, and had finally decided to stop sitting.

He read it twice, standing in the doorway of the monitoring station's comms room with the rain hammering the mountain behind him like applause from an audience that wanted its money back.

*Claim documentation: destroyed. Source assets: non-recoverable. Project valuation: zero. Your position and your commission are terminated effective immediately. All Syndicate property in your possession is to be surrendered to the enforcement detail on site. You will remain where you are pending audit.*

Pending audit.

Ives read it a third time, because the first two readings had been performed by a man who still believed in the architecture of his own life, and the third reading was performed by whoever was left after that man finished falling.

Behind him, in the vault antechamber, his enforcement detail was packing up the seized boats' manifests, the confiscated supplies, the survey credentials Sefa had traded away for the boy's life—all of it suddenly worthless inventory, paper attached to nothing. He could hear Corporal Venn arguing quietly with someone over the radio, and the tone of the argument told him everything the message hadn't: they'd known already. The office had told them first. They had let him sleep.

That was the part that stung, he noticed with clinical interest. Not the termination. The sequencing.

He set the handset down gently, the way you set down something you might need to throw later, and went to find a mirror.

* * *

There wasn't one, of course. Monitoring stations buried under karst did not come equipped with mirrors; the survey team had been engineers, not philosophers. He settled for the polished face of the relay housing, which gave him back a smeared, green-tinted version of himself, and he studied it the way he studied debtors—noting the tells, cataloguing the cracks.

The smile was still there. That surprised him. Forty-one years of practice will do that; the smile had outlasted two marriages, one bankruptcy that wasn't his, and a father who had taught him arithmetic using collection notices as flashcards. The smile sat on his face the way the escarpment sat on the valley: load-bearing, immovable, indifferent to weather.

But his eyes above it were doing something new. He watched them for a while. They looked, he decided, like a man reading a ledger and finding the columns don't balance—and not by a little. By a category. As if someone had entered a number where a name should be, or a name where a number should go, and now the whole document was speaking a language he didn't recognize.

Dunmore had done this. He kept coming back to that, turning it over. Not the Corricks, who were muscle with a boat. Not the pilot, who was competent but purchasable. Not even the defector, who had cost him the boy and gained him nothing. It was Dunmore—the invisible woman, the small debtor, the client-who-wouldn't-pay who had become the asset-who-wouldn't-sign—who had stood in front of a fortune large enough to erase every debt in three districts and burned it.

He had offered her freedom. He had offered it sincerely. He had run the numbers for her right there in the vault, walked her through it line by line, the way you'd walk a friend through a contract, because that was what he genuinely believed he was doing: performing an act of kindness so large it would retroactively justify everything. Forgive the debt, forgive the interest, forgive the years—sign here.

And she had looked at him with something worse than hatred. She had looked at him with *pity*, brief and precise, like a doctor confirming a diagnosis she'd suspected for some time. And then she'd fed the claim into the furnace page by page while the storm tried to take the roof off, and she hadn't even hurried.

You couldn't collect on pity. There was no column for it.

* * *

By six, the rain had found a rhythm it liked and settled into it, and Ives went outside to stand in it, because the alternative was standing inside, and inside had begun to feel like a room in a house that had already been sold.

Venn found him there. The corporal had the particular posture of a man delivering news he'd rehearsed in the hope of not having to deliver it.

"Mr. Ives."

"Corporal." He turned up the smile a notch, out of habit, and watched it fail to land. Interesting. It always landed. It landed on debtors, on judges, on his ex-wife's lawyer, on the Syndicate's own auditors. "You have orders."

"We're pulling out at first light. Road's washout risk past the bend, and the office wants the equipment below the flood line." Venn wouldn't look at him directly. "All the equipment."

"Ah," said Ives. "The boat."

"The boat."

"And my kit."

"Your kit's on the manifest, sir." Venn said *sir* the way you say it to a man whose name has just come off the door. "Along with everything else. Audit's going to want a full accounting."

"Of course it is." Ives considered the rain running off the brim of his hat. "And me?"

Venn's jaw worked. To his credit—he was a decent man, Venn, decent men made excellent instruments precisely because they hated knowing it—to his credit, he tried to find a version of the sentence that wasn't the sentence.

"You're to remain on site," he managed finally. "For the audit. Transport can't be spared. There's a supply run scheduled for—" He stopped. They both knew there wasn't. There was no supply run scheduled. There was a monsoon on the mountain and a river running backward and a regional office that had just written off an entire project, and nobody, anywhere in that beautiful balanced ledger, had entered a line item for retrieving Ives.

"I see," said Ives pleasantly. "Well. Give my regards to the office."

"Sir—"

"That's all, Corporal."

Venn hesitated long enough to make it mean something, then pressed a foil packet of rations and a signal flare into his hands—off the books, unmanifested, the first honest transaction Ives had witnessed in weeks—and went back inside.

Ives stood alone on the loading apron with two thousand tons of water falling out of the sky and did the thing he always did when the world became illegible.

He ran the numbers.

* * *

Here is what the numbers said.

Total career earnings, twenty-two years with the Vellum Syndicate: comfortable, invested, and—this was the elegant part—almost entirely encumbered. His flat in the city was leveraged against his portfolio. His portfolio was leveraged against his commission projections. His commission projections had been built, with great care and total sincerity, on the source claim, which was currently ash in a furnace beneath his feet. Every asset he owned was a promise that the next quarter would resemble the last one. He had spent two decades constructing a man who only existed forward.

The Syndicate had simply reached into the future and taken it back.

He almost admired it. That was the humiliating thing, standing there in the rain doing the arithmetic of his own ruin: he *almost admired it*. It was clean. It was exactly what he would have done. If some optimistic middle manager had brought him a project that evaporated overnight, Ives would have cut him loose by lunch and slept beautifully afterward, and he would have called it discipline, and he would have meant it.

The system ate its own. He had always known that. He had watched it happen to others with the mild professional interest of a man watching weather in another county. He had simply never believed he was edible. Optimists, he'd thought, were load-bearing. You kept the optimist the way you kept the building's foundation—unexamined, essential, assumed.

But foundations get written off too, when the building comes down.

He laughed. Just once, short, startled out of him like a hiccup, and the sound bounced off the karst and came back wrong, and he decided not to do that again.

* * *

He spent the morning doing what he knew how to do: work.

There was an audit to prepare for, absurdly, and preparing for audits was a form of prayer he still remembered. He inventoried the station. He reconciled the manifests. He found the survey team's original requisition files in the archive room and read them with genuine interest, because grief, he was discovering, was mostly just attention with nowhere to go, and the dead team's paperwork deserved some.

They had been good people. That came through even in requisitions—extra medical supplies requested without being ordered to, a note about the boy's village downstream flagged for review, a marginal comment in a careful hand: *if the test runs at capacity, the lower valley floods. Recommend we refuse.* Someone had written *refuse* and underlined it twice, and eleven days later that someone was a missing person, and the project had proceeded, and the Syndicate had bought the debt on all of it sight unseen because the numbers were beautiful.

The numbers had been beautiful. That was the whole story, wasn't it. Somewhere in a clean office, a man Ives had never met had looked at these figures and felt the specific pleasure of a balanced column, and had never once imagined the river, or the village, or the woman feeding documents into a furnace, or himself standing in the rain holding a foil packet of rations and a flare, doing sums that ended in zero.

Zero. He wrote it in the margin of the requisition file, out of some obscure honesty, and then crossed it out, because even now—even now—some clerk in his skull objected to untidy records.

* * *

In the afternoon the radio, which the departing detail had left powered down, crackled once on its own—residual charge, atmospheric noise, the mountain talking to itself—and Ives caught himself listening for her.

Dunmore. Out there somewhere in the flood with her strays, descending through water that didn't forgive, carrying nothing but a compass and one letter. He found he was thinking about the letter. She'd kept one letter out of the fire, out of hundreds, and he had watched her choose it, and he had thought at the time: *sentimental. An asset left unrealized.* Now he turned the image over and found it looked different from this side of the ledger. She had walked out of that vault owning less than anyone he'd ever met and owing less than anyone he'd ever met, and the two facts were the same fact, and there was no column for it anywhere in his training.

What do you call a woman with no debts? he wondered. The Syndicate had a term for everyone. Debtor, asset, liability, collateral. But there was no entry for free. The system had no word for it because the system had never met one.

He had, though. He'd met one, recently, in a vault, in the rain, and she had pitied him, and he was beginning—slowly, reluctantly, the way ice begins—to understand why.

* * *

Evening came down the gorge like a lid closing. The rain eased to a steady gray murmur, and in the lull Ives heard the river change.

He'd been on the mountain long enough now to know its voices, and this one he recognized from the data: the deep pulse, the inhale, the great sluices beneath the escarpment cycling down for the night. Except tonight the cycle sounded different. Slower. Rougher. Like a machine winding toward something, or failing toward it. Sefa's shutdown sequence, maybe—or the unscheduled test still grinding through its final stages, or simply the storm overloading everything. He was no engineer. He was a collector. He collected what was owed, and the mountain owed him nothing, and for the first time in his life he stood inside a silence that no amount of smiling could convert into an advantage.

He ate half the ration packet. He saved the rest, automatically, and then laughed again—that same wrong bounce off the karst—because saving half a ration packet was a debtor's instinct, a survivor's instinct, an instinct for a tomorrow that had no line item anywhere, and it was the most honest thing he'd done in years.

Then he took out the signal flare, turned it over in his hands, and did not light it.

Because here was the question the whole day had been walking him toward, and he made himself look at it straight, the way he'd once made debtors look at their ledgers: if he fired the flare, someone might come. The Corricks, maybe—they were still on the mountain, covering the woman's exit, and the Corricks owed the Syndicate money too, and might be persuadable. Or the office might send someone after all, eventually, if the audit went badly enough for them that a witness became useful. Either way, rescue meant return. Return meant the ledger. And the ledger, he understood now with perfect clarity, had already closed his account. Whatever came down that mountain to fetch him would not be coming for Ives. It would be coming for the discrepancy.

Or he could not light it. He could sit out the storm in a dead monitoring station with a dead man's letters and a furnace that still held warmth, and in the morning descend on his own feet, by his own route, owing the mountain nothing and the Syndicate less than nothing—which was to say, a grudge.

His father's voice, faint, from thirty-five years down: *Everything is owed, boy. The trick is being the one who collects.*

His father had died owing four creditors and a landlord, and the Syndicate had bought the debt at discount, and that was how Ives had gotten his first job—working off a dead man's balance in a filing room, smiling, because smiling was free and everything else was billed.

The flare sat in his hands, red and patient, a small bright promise of the old life.

Out in the dark, the river inhaled. Held it. Let it go.

Ives put the flare back in his pocket, unlit, and started looking for the station's emergency descent charts—because if Dunmore could find a way down through the flood, then so, damn it, could he, and he intended to arrive at the bottom of this mountain as the one thing the Vellum Syndicate had no procedure for:

A man they couldn't bill.
