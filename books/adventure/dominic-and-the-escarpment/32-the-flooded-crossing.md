## 32. The Flooded Crossing

The rain came sideways now, in ropes, and the river at Widow's Bend had stopped pretending to be a river.

It was a freight train with the brakes cut.

Dominic stood on the mudbank with rain hammering the back of her neck and watched the water where the bend used to be. Two days ago she'd stood on this same bank with a functioning gauge and a river that, for all its violence, still ran in one direction. Now the channel was a brown chaos forty feet wider than it should have been, choked with whole trees turning end over end, and the far bank—her far bank, the low side, the side that led down to Tuck's village—was a smear of green through the downpour.

"Tell me we're not doing this," Bram said beside her. He had to shout to be heard over the rain and the river both. His boat—the confiscated skiff he'd won back from the Corricks' impound with a crowbar and a promise neither of them intended to keep—sat behind them in the shallows, riding high and light, which was the last thing it was about to be.

"We're doing this," Dominic said.

"Then tell me why we're doing this."

She pointed at the sky, or where the sky should have been. "Because the gauge data says the sluices are cycling closed. Sefa's been watching the pulse intervals all morning. When they shut down for the maintenance window, the river drops—maybe three feet, maybe four, for maybe twenty minutes. That's our crossing. That's the only crossing."

"And if the interval's wrong?"

"Then we're swimming."

Bram looked at her the way he always did when she said something like that—flat, unimpressed, a man cataloguing the many reasons he should have stayed a smuggler—and then he spat into the flood and went to check the boat. That was Bram's whole philosophy in one gesture: the world was terrible, and you went anyway.

Sefa crouched under the tarp they'd rigged over the gear, her survey instruments wrapped in oilcloth, her hands moving over the gauge readout with the focused calm of someone who had decided that panic was a luxury. "Pulse interval's holding at eleven minutes," she called. "Closed phase is running longer each cycle. Next one starts in six minutes. Maybe seven."

Tuck was already in the boat.

He was fourteen and he sat in the bow like he'd been born there, which, given the village he came from, he more or less had. He'd tied everything down twice. He'd bailed the bilge with a coffee can until Bram threatened to tie him to a tree. He was soaked to the bone and grinning, because Tuck grinned at everything, and Dominic had learned over the past weeks that the grin was armor—the same way Ives's smile was armor, except Tuck's had a person inside it.

"Six minutes," Tuck said, holding up six fingers, rain streaming off them. "I counted too. I got five and a half."

"You got nothing," Bram said, hauling the last of the gear aboard. "You got a coffee can and opinions."

"And the gauge data," Tuck said. "Which is the same thing Sefa's got, because I helped."

Dominic climbed into the stern and took her position at the sweep oar. The boat settled under the combined weight of four people and everything they hadn't been willing to burn or bury: the instruments, the logbook, the one letter she'd kept from her father's room, wrapped in waxed cloth against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Everything else was ash or mud or memory. This was what remained. This was enough.

The plan was simple, which meant it was lying.

The sluices closed. The river dropped. They crossed at the bend, where the channel narrowed and the current—reduced—could be fought rather than obeyed. They landed on the low bank, made the flooded cutline down to the village road, and reached the evacuation point before the water did. Sefa's broadcast from the monitoring station's transmitter had gone out on the emergency band two days ago, and the villages had moved, but moved wasn't safe. Moved was only the first word in a longer sentence.

The rain thickened. The river roared. Somewhere upstream, deep under the escarpment, a machine built by men who thought water was a commodity began, on schedule, to hold its breath.

"There," Sefa said.

Dominic felt it before she saw it—a change in the boat's motion, the water beneath them losing its shove. The brown chaos didn't calm, exactly. It sank. The surface dropped like something exhaling, and the snags and deadfall that had been traveling at killing speed suddenly slowed, wallowing, and the far bank seemed to lean closer.

"Go," Dominic said. "Go, go, *go*."

Bram hit the motor and it caught on the second pull, and they surged out into the gap.

For ninety seconds it was almost easy. That was the trap of it—crossings that start easy make you forget what the river is. Dominic worked the sweep to hold their angle against the residual current, Bram feathered the throttle, and the boat planed across the dropped water in a long diagonal toward the low bank, spray coming off the bow in sheets, Tuck up front calling the snags like a race commentator—"*left, left, big one left, clear, clear—*"

Then the pulse ended early.

Later, Sefa would swear the interval held. Later, the data would show the sluices cycling back open ninety seconds ahead of pattern, some failsafe in the machine's dying logic tripping on a pressure differential no one had anticipated. But in the moment there was no data. There was only the sound—a deep, subterranean groan through the hull, through the water, through the bedrock itself, like the river clearing its throat—and then the flood coming back.

Dominic saw it before anyone. She'd spent her life reading storms, and a storm surge and a sluice surge were cousins: the same wall, the same hush, the same wrongness in the air. "Open it up," she said, calm, terrifyingly calm, her voice coming from somewhere outside her. "Bram. Full throttle. Now."

Bram opened it up.

The wall of water came around the bend maybe three hundred meters upstream. It didn't look fast. That was the horror of moving water—your eyes refused to do the math. It looked like a hill deciding to relocate. It picked up a half-submerged tree the size of a chapel and carried it like a twig.

The bow wave caught them first. The boat lifted, slammed, lifted again. Gear tore loose—Sefa threw herself on the instrument case and rode it down as the deck tilted forty degrees. Bram fought the motor, the prop cavitating in aerated water, the engine screaming a note it was never built to scream. And the current, the real current, the river with its breath back, took hold of the hull and began to swing them.

They were going to broach. Dominic knew it in her hands before her head admitted it—sweep oar bucking, the boat rotating beam-on to the surge, and a boat beam-on to that water was a leaf, was a toy, was a coffin with good ventilation.

"Tuck!" Bram's voice, cracked in half. "Line! Get on the line!"

There was a safety line rigged bow to stern, a rope to grab if the boat went over. Tuck grabbed it. Tuck was *good* at grabbing it; he'd practiced; they'd all practiced on flat water in the evenings while the storm built, Bram drilling them like a man who'd lost passengers before and intended never to again.

The surge hit them broadside.

The world went white and brown and upside down. Dominic felt the hull leave the water entirely—felt the strange weightless instant where the river decided, not her—and then the impact as they came down wrong, and the cold closed over her head, and the sweep oar was gone, and her hands found nothing.

She came up gasping inside the boat's air pocket, the overturned hull above her, and for one disoriented second she thought: *everyone roll clear, everyone roll clear,* the drill, Bram's voice in her memory—*never stay under a swamped hull in floodwater, the recirculating current will pin you and drown you against your own boat—*

She rolled clear and came up in the open flood, and the river took her.

Floodwater is not water. It is a solid that occasionally forgives. It tumbled her, found every pocket of air in her jacket and squeezed it out, drove her down against the silt-smell and the roar, and when she surfaced she got half a breath and a snapshot of the world: the boat, righted somehow, Bram hauling on the motor, Sefa clinging to the bow; the far bank impossibly far; and Tuck—

Tuck was not on the line.

Tuck was ten meters downstream, a small figure in a yellow rain shell, arms windmilling, going under, coming up, going under. The recirculating current at the bend—the same hydraulic that had flipped them—was catching debris and holding it, a slow revolving trap of water, and it had caught the boy the way it caught the trees.

Dominic did not think. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that would rearrange something permanent in her: there was no thought. There was no calculation, no ledger, no weighing of odds. Her whole life she had been a calculator—of storms, of debts, of distances between herself and danger, the mathematics of staying invisible, staying alive, staying unowned. Every survival instinct she had ever honed said: *hold the boat, hold the boat, the boat is the survival platform, you go in that water you die.*

She went in the water.

She pulled the knife from her belt, cut the tangled safety line free of her harness so nothing could snag her, and dove off the stern into the flood, and the last thing she heard was Bram roaring her name in a voice she'd never heard him use.

* * *

The river hit her like a building falling slowly.

Cold first—snowmelt cold, escarpment cold, a cold that went through skin and muscle into the marrow and made her limbs stupid. Then the tumble, the endless tumble, her body a rag in a current that weighed more than the boat. She'd trained herself in whitewater once, years ago, a client's course she'd taken for the certification and never used. *Don't fight the current. Float feet-first, on your back, feet up to take the rocks. Swim for the eddy, not the bank.* The training surfaced now like a diver's reflex, and she obeyed it, feet up, back arched, riding the chaos, getting her one glimpse of the surface per second like frames of a film: sky, rain, the yellow shell—*there*, downstream, caught in the eddy's rotation, going around and around the hydraulic like a leaf in a drain that never quite drains.

He wasn't swimming. That was the bad thing. The boy who'd grown up on this river, the boy who counted pulse intervals and called snags like a race commentator—he wasn't swimming. He was floating face-down.

She swam.

She swam the way you swim in a nightmare, all effort and no motion, the river canceling every stroke, but the eddy was rotating and rotation meant the boy would come around to her side of the circle eventually, and physics was on her side for the first time in weeks. She timed it. She waited—one rotation, two, her lungs burning, the cold chewing through her—and on the third pass he came around and she lunged and her hand closed on the back of his rain shell.

The shell tore.

Half of it stayed in her fist. The boy started to slide away and she got her other arm around his chest, under the armpits, a full-body clamp, and the river objected immediately—it hit them both like a verdict, drove them under, ground them against something that might have been a snag or a rock, and Dominic's shoulder screamed and she did not let go. She would remember that too. The shoulder screaming and her not letting go, the pain arriving like information and being dismissed like a clerk.

*Hold the boat.* Every instinct. *The boat is survival.*

Tuck's head broke the surface and he was coughing, convulsing, alive—alive, coughing up the river, and his eyes when they found hers were wide and black with a terror that no grin had ever been armor enough for.

"I got you," she said. The river tore the words away but she said them anyway, said them into his face, because he needed to hear a human voice say it. "I got you. Kick. *Kick.*"

The eddy's edge was a line you could feel, a seam in the water where the rotating trap met the main current, and past it—she'd seen it on the way in, one frame of the film—the snag jam, a raft of downed trees wedged against the bank, the one solid thing in this whole drowning world. Getting to it meant crossing the seam, and the seam meant the main current would take them, fast, downstream, toward the gorge and the next bend and the next hydraulic and the odds running out like sand.

But the seam was also the only exit.

"On three," she said. "Three."

There was no one and no two. She kicked on three and they crossed the seam and the river took them like a debt collector—sudden, total, no negotiation. The world blurred. Trees came at them. She turned her back to the jam at the last second, put herself between the boy and the wood, and they hit the snag raft with a force that drove the air out of her and would have broken her back if the water hadn't been carrying half of it.

Her hand found a branch. Her hand found a branch and her arm found a branch and her whole body, all of it, every debt-ridden invisible storm-chasing ounce of her, found that branch and *held.*

The current pulled. She held. The current pulled harder, trying to peel them off into the gorge, and Dominic Dunmore, who had spent thirty-one years letting go of everything before it could cost her—clients, homes, her father's memory, her own name in certain rooms—held.

"Tuck," she gasped. "Climb. Climb *now*."

He climbed. She felt him move over her, felt his feet find holds in the tangled wood, felt the weight come off her arm by degrees, and she held on and held on and held on, and the river pulled, and she held.

Somewhere above the roar, an engine.

Bram brought the boat in like a man threading a needle in an earthquake. She never understood how he did it—the approach angle, the throttle work, the way he used the eddy behind the snag jam as a brake—and she never asked. She only knew that suddenly there were hands, Bram's hands and Sefa's hands, dragging her over the gunwale, and then Tuck, and then the boat was backing out into the current, riding high and half-swamped, and Bram was bailing with the coffee can while steering, which should not have been possible and was.

Dominic lay in the bilge water and coughed up half the river.

* * *

The far bank, when they finally grounded on it, was the sweetest mud she had ever felt in her life.

She lay on it a long time. Rain on her face. Her shoulder a fire. Tuck a few feet away, on his back, chest heaving, staring at the sky like it owed him an apology. Sefa knelt between them, checking pulses with two fingers, saying nothing, which from Sefa was a whole speech.

Bram stood over them, dripping, the coffee can still in his hand, looking down at Dominic with an expression she'd never seen on him—something cracked open behind the flat gray eyes.

"You cut your line," he said finally. "And went in. Into *that*."

She managed a nod. Talking was expensive right now.

"That's the opposite of every rule I ever taught you."

"Sorry."

Bram looked at her a moment longer. Then he turned away, fast, and walked to the bow, and busied himself with the mooring line with tremendous intensity, and Dominic understood that the old smuggler was wiping his eyes and wished to be allowed the fiction that he wasn't.

Tuck rolled his head toward her. Water was still leaking out of him in small coughs. His grin was back, but it was a different grin—wider, wobblier, the armor set aside.

"You came in after me," he said.

"Someone had to. You float face-down."

"I was *resting*."

"You were drowning."

"I was resting *aggressively*." The cough took him then, a long one, and when it passed he was quiet, and when he spoke again the wobble was in his voice instead of the grin. "Nobody's ever come in after me. Not for me. My whole village, everyone's leaving everything, everyone's just—" He stopped. Started again. "You didn't even think about it. I saw your face. You just went."

Dominic lay on the mud and looked at the rain.

She thought about ledgers. She thought about Ives's smile, and the Syndicate's arithmetic, and her father's letters, and the whole vast machine—of water, of money, of debt—built on the idea that everything is owned and every debt gets collected and the only question is who pays. She had spent her whole life inside that arithmetic, a number in someone else's column, and she had gone into the river without one thought about the math, and she was still here, and the boy was still here, and the math had simply never mattered at all.

"I'm getting slow," she said.

Tuck laughed—a real one, cracked and wet and fourteen years old—and Sefa laughed too, and even Bram's shoulders, at the bow, shook once in a way that was absolutely not laughing, no sir.

Dominic pushed herself up on the good arm. Downriver, through the rain, the low country waited—the flooded cutline, the village road, the evacuation point, the last miles of a journey that had started with a collector's smile in her truck and ended, if the river allowed it, with all of them standing on dry ground owing nothing to anyone. The storm was still building. The worst of the monsoon was still coming. The river between here and there was still a freight train with the brakes cut.

But the boy was alive, and the crew was whole, and her shoulder hurt in a way that felt like a promise.

"Everyone in the boat," she said, and her voice came out rough and new. "We're not done yet."

She was reaching for the gunwale when Sefa's hand closed on her arm—hard, urgent—and Sefa pointed downstream, to where the cutline met the water.

A boat was coming up the flooded road. Gray hull, gray tarp, riding low with too many men.

And on its bow, upright and unhurried in the pouring rain, a figure in a good coat raised one hand in a small, cheerful wave.

Dominic knew that wave. She knew that smile, even at this distance, even through the rain.

"Ives," she said. "He's already here."
