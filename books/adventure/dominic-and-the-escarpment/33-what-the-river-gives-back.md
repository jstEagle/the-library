## 33. What the River Gives Back

The river had me.

That was the first thing I understood, down in the brown roar where the world stopped having edges: the river had me, and it was not going to give me back out of kindness. I'd spent thirty-two years learning what water does when it decides something, and every lesson came due at once. It rolled me. It pressed me against the drowned fence line under Widow's Bend—the one Tuck had warned about, the one Bram had tried to steer wide of—and it held me there like a thumb on a beetle while the boat went on without us, because of course it did, because boats don't wait and rivers don't either.

Tuck was somewhere upstream of me or downstream of me or nowhere at all, and I could not make my body answer the question. My hands were. My hands were doing exactly what they were built to do, clawing at the fence wire, finding the gap Sefa had flagged on the way up—*there's a break at the third post, remember it, everyone remember it*—and pulling me through into slower water behind the bend where the current eddied back on itself like a thought reconsidering.

I surfaced.

The sky was the color of wet slate and coming down in ropes. The escarpment stood over everything, black and streaming, and the flood that had swallowed the crossing was carrying whole trees past me at the speed of a galloping horse. Somewhere in all of it was a boy who weighed maybe eighty pounds soaking wet, which he very much was.

"TUCK!"

The river said nothing. The river never says anything. That's the thing nobody tells you about water—it has no voice of its own, only the voices you throw into it, and mine came back at me shredded.

I got an arm over the fence post and hung there, coughing, lungs full of the Karst Escarpment's entire watershed, and I did the arithmetic I'd been doing since the moment we shoved off: two in the water, one boat, four people aboard if Bram had kept them aboard, and a crossing that was less a crossing now than a dare the river had made with itself.

Then I saw him.

A flash of yellow—Sefa's rain shell, the one she'd wrapped around him at the portage because he wouldn't stop shivering—downstream, maybe forty meters, bobbing at the surface in a way that meant he was still fighting and not winning. He went under. Came up. Went under again, and this time what came up wasn't a boy, it was a hand, and then not even that.

I let go of the post.

I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the only currency I've got left that isn't counterfeit: I didn't decide. There was no weighing, no ledger, none of the cold cornered calculation that had gotten me through every bad choice since my father died. My hands simply opened, and the rest of me followed them, and the river took me the way it takes everything—completely, without negotiation.

* * *

Here is what I know about those next ninety seconds, and most of it I know secondhand, from Bram, from Sefa, from Tuck himself telling it later around a fire with his hands doing half the talking, because memory is a thing survivors pass around until everyone owns a piece:

Bram saw us go in. Saw the boat—his boat, the *Lark*, confiscated twice and stolen once and the only thing he'd ever loved that hadn't left him—swing broadside as the load shifted, and he made the call that separates pilots from passengers. He put her bow into the flood instead of across it. "You don't fight a river sideways," he told me afterward, shouting it over the storm because by then shouting was just how he talked. "You fight it going where it's already going, and you steal your exit on the way."

He drove the *Lark* downstream faster than the flood was carrying me, which should not be possible and was, because Bram knew the bend, knew the seam of slack water along the far cutbank where the current doubles back, and he put the boat into that seam like threading a needle during an earthquake.

Sefa was already rigging. Of course she was. She'd spent nine days upstream turning salvage line and survey cable into things that held, and now she paid out the longest line they had—sixty meters of braided poly, light enough to throw, strong enough to hang a truck off of—looped around the winch cleat with a quick-release she'd tied in the dark without looking, because some hands learn knots the way other people learn prayers.

She couldn't throw sixty meters. Nobody can. So she didn't. She threw twenty, let the river take the rest, and played it out the way you play a fish, letting the current carry the line across the seam until it lay across the water between the boat and the place where I'd gone under, a lifeline laid down by geography instead of muscle.

And Tuck—Tuck, who had gone under twice, whose lungs were full of the same river mine were—Tuck found the line first.

That's the part that undoes me even now. Not that they saved us. That *he* saved us, half-drowned, eight years old going on eighty, with nothing left but the stubbornness that had carried him stowing away onto my truck three weeks ago. His hand came out of the water and closed on the poly line, and Sefa felt the weight hit it—she said it was like the river itself had grabbed hold—and she screamed the only order that mattered: "BRAM. WEIGHT ON THE LINE."

Bram put the *Lark* against the current and held. Engine screaming, transom buried, the whole boat trembling like a living thing asked to do more than living things can do. And the line came tight, and the river pulled, and the boat did not move, because Bram had spent thirty years learning exactly how much a river can take before it takes you instead, and today he drew that line in moving water and dared the Karst to cross it.

I came up on the line.

I don't remember finding it. I remember the burn of it across my palm—that's how I know my hands were still working, still smarter than I am—and I remember hauling myself along it hand over hand toward the weight at the other end, and the weight was Tuck, and Tuck was not moving.

* * *

They pulled him over the gunwale first. Later, Sefa would tell me she made that call without thinking—lightest weight comes out first, get the line clear for the second pickup—but I've watched her say it, and I've watched her face when she says it, and I don't believe her. She pulled the boy first because he was a boy, and because she had run from a dying team once and had sworn, in whatever private country guilt builds inside a person, that she would never again leave someone in the water.

He wasn't breathing.

I know this the way you know things in nightmares, clearly and from outside myself: I was hauled over the side—I have no memory of climbing; I think Sefa and Bram simply *took* me the way you take cargo, necessary and ungentle—and I knelt on the flooded deck of the *Lark* with the boy's head cradled in my two wrecked hands, and he was gray, and he was still, and the rain hammered both of us like it was trying to drive him deeper into wherever he'd gone.

"No," I said. To him, to the river, to the ledger of the entire world. "No, you don't. You don't get to do this. You stowed away on *my* truck. You're *mine*. You don't—"

I tilted him. Cleared his airway the way the field medic course had taught me eleven years ago in a classroom that smelled of floor wax, the lesson filed away under *someday* alongside so much else. I breathed for him. Once. Twice. The river ran out of his mouth and nose in a gray gout across the deck, and I turned him, and I pushed, small sharp pushes, counting, and on the fourth push the whole world narrowed to the sound of my own pulse in my ears—

—and Tuck coughed.

It was not a graceful sound. It was the ugliest, wettest, most magnificent sound I have ever heard in my life, and it came out of that small body like the river reversing, like the sluices under the escarpment opening, like everything blocked and held and drowned in him deciding all at once to flow again. He vomited up half the Karst watershed, gasped, coughed again, and then did the single most Tuck thing imaginable:

He started crying, and trying to talk at the same time, so that what came out was mostly bubbles. But I caught the shape of it. I caught the shape of it because I'd been hearing the shape of it for weeks.

"—told you—" *cough* "—told you I could—"

"You could *drown*," I said, and my voice cracked clean in half, and I didn't care. "You could drown, you absolute—you *idiot*—"

"I held the line," he wheezed, indignant even at the edge of death, gripping my wrist with a hand gone white and pruned. "Did you see? I *held* it."

I looked at Sefa. Sefa looked at me. And Bram, both hands still welded to the wheel, holding us in the seam while the flood tried every second to take his boat apart, shouted back over his shoulder without turning around:

"Somebody check whether the girl can breathe, before the boy talks her to death."

* * *

I could breathe. Mostly. What I mostly did for the next several minutes was cough, bent double on the deck with Sefa's arm around me, bringing up river water in quantities that seemed structurally impossible for a body my size. Every cough hurt like a debt being collected. And somewhere in the middle of it—between one convulsion and the next, in the little clean space the pain left open—something happened in my chest that had never happened before.

I laughed.

Not a chuckle. Not the dry, armored almost-smile I'd been issuing for three decades, the one I kept in my pocket for collectors and clients and men who thought a woman alone on the flats was a resource. A real laugh. It tore out of me sideways, ugly and wet and unstoppable, tangled up in the coughing so that I couldn't tell where one ended and the other began, and it scared Sefa badly enough that she grabbed my face in both hands and checked my eyes like I'd taken a knock.

"Dominic. *Dominic.* Look at me. Are you—"

"I'm fine," I gasped, and laughed again, and God help me it felt *terrible*, it felt like ribs cracking, it felt like the best thing that had ever happened to me. "I'm fine, I'm—Sefa, he *held the line*. He's eight. He held the line and he's lecturing me about it. We nearly died and he wants *credit*."

"He wants credit," Sefa agreed, and her voice did something strange in the middle, and I realized she was laughing too, or crying, or both, the way people do when the fear leaves all at once and the body doesn't know what to do with the room it leaves behind. "He gets credit. He gets all the credit. I'm putting it in the log."

"Put it in the log," Bram called back. He'd finally eased the *Lark* out of the seam, running us down the flooded channel toward the high bank above the old ferry landing, and now that his hands were free of the emergency his voice had gone rough and low, the way it did when he was feeling something too big for his vocabulary. "Whole thing. Names, times, positions. Best piece of boat handling I ever did and half of it was a kid on a rope." He spat over the side, which was his version of a toast. "River gave 'em back. Both of 'em. You mark that down too."

The river gave them back.

I sat on the deck of the *Lark* with the rain hammering down and the escarpment sliding past, black and ancient and indifferent, and I turned that phrase over like my father's compass in my palm. Because it wasn't true, was the thing. Rivers don't give. Rivers take, and occasionally lose their grip, and everything else is what people do—Bram holding a boat against a flood with his arms shaking, Sefa laying a line across chaos with knots she tied blind, a boy refusing to let go of a rope with the last strength in his body, and me, apparently, diving back into a drowning flood for someone else's child without a single thought in my head.

Everything the river gives back, somebody pays for. That was the whole book of it. That had always been the whole book of it—my debts, my father's secret, the Syndicate's smiling ledgers, the water rights worth more than every loan in the valley. Somebody always pays. The only question the world ever actually asks you is: *who?*

For thirty-two years I'd arranged my life so the answer was always *me*. Small debts, small payments, stay invisible, stay cheap, stay alive. It had worked. It had worked right up until a boy went under a flooded fence line and my hands opened without asking my permission, and I understood, kneeling in the bilgewater of a stolen boat, that I was done arranging anything. The ledger was ash. The claim was ash. All that was left was what you refuse to sell and who you refuse to let go, and I had refused, and the river had given him back, and I was laughing like a madwoman in the rain because it was the first time in my entire life I'd spent something I couldn't afford and come out richer.

Tuck crawled over to me through the sloshing deck, still gray-lipped, still dripping, and leaned against my side like it was his assigned seat. Maybe it was now. Maybe it had been since the truck.

"You went in after me," he said. Not a question. He'd seen it, or part of it, from the water.

"You went in after the line," I said.

"Yeah, but you didn't even *think* about it." He said it with the flat wonder children reserve for adults behaving irrationally. "Bram says you didn't even think."

"Bram should mind his wheel."

"Bram," Bram said, "heard everything, and is putting it in the log."

I put my arm around the boy. He was shivering again, the good kind now, the kind that means a body has decided to keep operating, and Sefa was already stripping the survival blankets out of the dry bag, and ahead of us the high bank rose out of the flood with a stand of pines on it that would give us shelter and firewood and a night of being, improbably, alive.

We grounded the *Lark* in the shallows below the pines, and Bram killed the engine, and in the sudden quiet the storm filled the whole world—rain on water, wind in the pines, the deep animal groan of the swollen river working at its banks. Tuck sneezed. Enormously. Three times.

"That's the river leaving," Sefa said solemnly, wrapping him in foil. "All of it, eventually."

"It better hurry," Tuck said. "I drank a lot of river."

And I laughed again. Second time in one day. A personal record, and a new baseline, and I suspected—not hoped, *suspected*, the way you suspect weather—that there would be more. That this was what solvent felt like. Not the money. This. A deck full of bilgewater, a boy leaking river under a foil blanket, a pilot muttering over his engine, a surveyor checking my pupils for the third time because she'd lost a team once and wasn't losing this one.

* * *

We made camp above the flood line, and Bram got a fire going under the pine canopy with the grim competence of a man who has dried out after many rivers, and we ate heated ration tins and drank tea that tasted like pine smoke and victory. Tuck fell asleep mid-sentence, sitting up, mouth open, one fist still loosely closed around a length of the poly line he'd refused to surrender. Sefa laid him down without waking him and stood over him a moment longer than she needed to.

"He held the line," she said quietly, to me, to the fire, to the team that had died in the gorge while she ran. "Nine days I've been waiting to find out what any of this was for. That. That was what it was for."

I didn't say anything. I'd learned, somewhere on this mountain, that some things are better logged than spoken.

But later, when the others slept and I took the middle watch, I sat with my back against a pine and my father's compass open in my hand, and the needle swung and settled, swung and settled, pointing the only direction it had ever pointed. Downriver. Toward the lowlands, the crossing, the Syndicate office with its smiling ledgers, the world where I owed money and answers and, now, a story.

Let them look for the claim documents. Let them dig through the ashes of the vault and the wreckage of their own staged test and find nothing they could hold, nothing they could bill, nothing they could own. Let Ives smile his way up a dead mountain chasing paper that no longer existed.

What the river gives back, it gives back changed. I'd gone into the water at Widow's Bend as a woman paying everyone's debts, and I'd come out—as I coughed up half the Karst and laughed until my ribs screamed—as something the ledgers had no column for.

Downriver, the storm was breaking. Come morning, we'd run the last water home.

And I intended to walk into the Vellum Syndicate's local office dripping wet, smelling of pine smoke and river, owing absolutely nothing—and I intended to enjoy every second of it.
