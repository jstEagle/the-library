## 34. Coming Down the Mountain

Tuck had never seen the river from above before.

From the ridge, two days out of the gorge, it looked like something a child had drawn with a fat gray pencil — a long looping line across the flats, swollen past its banks, brown as tea left too long. It wasn't the river he knew. The river he knew was narrow and quick and cold, the one he'd swum in since he could walk, the one that ran behind his mother's drying racks and turned the mill wheel at Halvard's place. That river had been swallowed whole. What lay below them now was a different animal, wide and slow and full of everything it had torn loose on the way down.

"Still rising?" Bram asked, coming up beside him with the scope. He didn't hand it over — Tuck had learned that Bram's scope was like Bram's opinions, offered only when he felt like it — but Bram angled it so Tuck could see along the barrel anyway.

"Flood crest should've passed Widow's Bend by now," Bram said, mostly to himself. "If those sluices are shut like she says."

"They're shut." Tuck said it flat, because saying it any other way would let the fear back in. "Sefa threw the breakers herself. I watched her do it."

"I know you watched her do it." Bram lowered the scope and looked at him with that pilot's face he made when he was doing arithmetic nobody else could see. "I'm asking what the river thinks about it."

Tuck didn't have an answer for that. Nobody did. They'd come down off the escarpment through three days of rain that fell sideways, through channels Bram navigated by memory and nerve, with the Corrick brothers running interference somewhere behind them — Tuck had seen their boat once, far back, riding shotgun across a flooded cut with both brothers standing up in it like they were daring the water to try something. And all the way down, the question had sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: *did we make it in time?*

Because Sefa had broadcast the gauge data on the second night, hunched over the monitoring station's transmitter while the storm tried to peel the roof off, sending the flood projections to every relay tower on the lower river. Village evacuation order, she'd called it. Hydrological emergency, official channel, survey credentials attached. Tuck had stood next to her while she sent it and understood, even then, that if the message came too late or nobody believed it, everything he'd done since he crawled into Dominic's truck bed under a tarp — every cold night, every portage, every time he'd kept his mouth shut and his hands busy so they'd have no reason to send him home — all of it would be for nothing. His village would be a memory with the roofs still on it.

*Did we make it in time?*

Dominic appeared out of the rain below them, climbing the ridge with that long stride of hers, hood back like the weather was a personal insult she refused to acknowledge. She'd been quiet since the vault. Not bad-quiet. Tuck had spent enough of his life reading adults' silences to know the difference. This was the quiet of somebody carrying something heavy and finally knowing what it weighed.

"River's dropping at the bend," she said. "Bram checked twice. Whatever's still coming down is runoff, not release." She looked at Tuck, and something in her face shifted — softened, almost, though she'd deny it. "We'll know soon."

"You keep saying that," Tuck said.

"Because it keeps being true."

He wanted to say more. He wanted to ask her what she'd burned in that furnace, why she'd done it, whether she was sorry — but there was a rule among the four of them now, unspoken and load-bearing: you didn't ask about the mountain yet. The mountain was still too close. You asked about water depth and rope condition and who had the last of the dried fish. The rest would come later, down in the lowlands, when there was ground under your feet that wasn't trying to kill you.

They descended through the afternoon, and the storm began to die.

It didn't go all at once. It went the way storms actually went, which was nothing like the way stories told it — raggedly, grudgingly, the wind dropping in stages, the rain thinning to a drizzle and then to a fine mist that hung in the air without falling. By late day the clouds tore open in the west and light came through them in long slanted bars, gold on gray, and the whole drowned valley lit up like something newly made. Tuck stopped on the trail and just looked. Water everywhere, yes — fields gone, roads gone, the low pasture a lake — but the sky was rinsed clean and the air smelled like wet stone and green things growing, and somewhere below, faint on the wind, he heard a sound he'd known his whole life and never thought he'd be this glad to hear.

A bell. The chapel bell at Millbrook, ringing fast and repeated. The all-clear pattern.

His legs were moving before his brain caught up. He took the trail at a run, slipping in the mud, Bram shouting something after him that he didn't hear, and came around the shoulder of the hill where the village finally showed itself below —

— and it was *there*.

Millbrook stood. That was the first thing, the thing his eyes grabbed and wouldn't let go of: the village stood. Roofs on. Chapel on. The mill, God help him, the mill still upright with its wheel hanging dripping and half-submerged but *attached*, turning slow and lazy in the floodwater like it had all the time in the world. The houses nearest the river were dark and windowless and waist-deep in brown water, and the fields were a sea, and the footbridge was gone entirely — but the village stood, and people stood with it, clustered on the high ground by the chapel in clusters of forty, fifty, a hundred, more than he'd ever seen in one place, because they weren't just Millbrook anymore. They were everyone from the low farms and the fishing shacks and the riverside rows, all of them alive, all of them up on the knoll with tarps and carts and cookfires already smoking, and when the bell rang they rang it again, faster, and someone down there spotted the four figures on the ridge and started pointing and shouting, and the sound that came up the hill was not a cheer, exactly. It was bigger than a cheer. It was the sound a hundred people make when they find out they get to keep going.

Tuck's mother was in the third row.

He knew it before he could pick her out individually — knew it the way you know a voice through a wall — and then he *did* pick her out, small and soaked and gray-faced with her arm around Widow Cett's shoulders, and she was looking up the hill at him with an expression he had no name for, and Tuck found that his body simply took over. He went down that slope like a landslide. Mud took his feet out from under him twice and he got up both times without breaking stride, and then he was through the crowd edge and she had him by the arms and was shaking him and hitting him and holding him all in the same motion, and she was saying his name over and over in a voice cracked down the middle, and he was saying "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I had to—" and she said, "I know. I know you had to. Don't ever do it again," which meant she'd do it again herself, and they both knew it, and neither of them said anything else for a long time.

When he could breathe again, he turned and found the others coming down more slowly, the way adults did everything, and the crowd was parting for them with a kind of wary awe. He heard the words moving through it like current: *the survey woman, the gauge data, that's her, that's the one who sent the warning.* Sefa kept her head down until an old man — Halvard, miller's hands and miller's stubbornness — planted himself in front of her and gripped her forearm the way you grip a rope.

"The message came at dusk," Halvard said. "Two days before the water. We'd have argued. Half of us would've stayed to argue longer. But the numbers were plain as scripture, and they had the survey seal on them, and old Pem here served with the survey thirty years back and said no lie ever wore that seal." He jerked his chin at a stooped figure nearby. "So we went. Every soul, up the knoll, with the stock we could drive. And when the water came through the low rows it came through *empty*."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, "The team I ran from would have wanted that message sent. They died rather than run the test. I just finished their sentence."

Halvard shook his head. "You finished your own. Rest of it's details."

* * *

They ate that night on the knoll, all together, out of pots that belonged to nobody and everybody. The Corricks arrived at full dark, poling their battered boat up the flooded lane like they owned it, and were fed without being thanked and thanked without being fed anything extra, which seemed to suit everyone. Rafe Corrick sat down across from Dominic, looked at her for a while, and said, "Heard the claim's ash."

"Heard your employers dropped you," Dominic said.

"Same fire, probably." Rafe shrugged and bit into his bread. "No hard feelings either way. A job's a job. That one's done."

And that was all anyone said about the mountain, which Tuck understood was its own kind of language.

Later, when the fires burned low and his mother had finally been persuaded to sleep, Tuck walked the edge of the knoll and looked out at the water lying silver under a sky full of washed stars. The river was down half a foot since evening. You could see it dropping, mark by mark on the chapel steps, steady as breathing. Somewhere upstream the sluices were closed and the machine under the escarpment was silent, and the river was just a river again, running true from its source to the sea, owing nothing to anybody.

Footsteps behind him. Dominic, of course. She came and stood at the rail of the little overlook platform — built by his grandfather, Tuck remembered, back when the village believed the high water would never come — and leaned on it, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"My mother wants to feed you forever," Tuck said. "She says debt's a family thing."

Dominic huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Your mother and I are going to disagree about debt."

"She wins. Everyone knows that."

That got the real laugh, short and rusty, like a door opening after years. Tuck decided he'd count it as payment for every worry he'd carried since the crossing at Widow's Bend, since the moment he'd gone under and seen the brown water close over him and been absolutely certain, in the simple way children are certain, that he was going to die — and then a hand had closed on his collar, and a woman who owed money to dangerous men had come into a flooding river after a stowaway boy she'd never asked for, without one second's hesitation. He hadn't thanked her properly. He suspected he never would, because some thanks were too big to say out loud, and she'd have hated it anyway.

"Dominic," he said instead. "The bell rang today."

She looked at him.

"It doesn't always ring," he said. "That's what I figured out, out here. Bad news travels fast and good news has to be rung. Somebody has to climb the tower and pull the rope, every single time, or nobody finds out things can end well." He shrugged, embarrassed suddenly, fourteen years old and wise beyond his own comfort. "Anyway. Today it rang."

Dominic was quiet a moment. Then she put her hand briefly on top of his head, ruffling his hair like she'd been wanting to do it for weeks, and said, "Yeah. Today it rang."

She started back toward the fires, then paused. "Oh — almost forgot. There's a man in the village asking after us. Down by the landing, dry clothes, city boots. Says he's a journalist." Her mouth twitched. "Sefa's already gone to find him. Something about names that need restoring."

Tuck grinned. "City boots. In Millbrook?"

"City boots," Dominic confirmed solemnly, "in Millbrook. Pray for him."

She went down toward the firelight, and Tuck stayed at the rail a while longer, listening to the river drop, mark by mark, all the way home.
