## 35. The Ledger Settled

The Vellum Syndicate's local office sat on Cordwainer Street like a well-fed spider: three stories of pale stone, brass lettering, windows cleaned so often they seemed to apologize for the view. Dominic had walked past it a hundred times in six years, always on the far side of the street, always with her collar up, the way you pass a dog you know will bite if it catches your eye.

Today she crossed the street.

She'd slept four hours on Bram's boat, moored at the public landing where anyone could see it, which was the point. She'd eaten eggs at the harbor café where Tuck had charmed the cook out of an extra roll and Sefa had read the morning broadsheet twice through, checking for their names. The names were there. Sefa's journalist friend had moved fast — *VANISHED SURVEY TEAM FOUND; RESERVOIR PROJECT "NEVER AUTHORIZED," MINISTRY CLAIMS* — and beneath it, a smaller headline that made Dominic's chest go tight and warm at the same time: *DAUGHTER OF LATE SURVEYOR DUNMORE RETURNS FROM THE ESCARPMENT.*

No longer invisible. She'd spent six years being invisible, and it turned out visibility was just another kind of weather. You dressed for it or you drowned.

The bell over the office door chimed, bright and stupid.

Inside smelled of paper and lemon polish. A receptionist with a face like a closed ledger looked up, took in Dominic — river-stained jacket, salt-crusted boots, the bruise along her jaw from the flooded crossing still going yellow — and reached for something under the desk. Probably a button. Probably a very polite alarm.

"I'm here to see whoever holds my account," Dominic said. "Dominic Dunmore. Tell them I'm not leaving."

"They'll be with you shortly," the receptionist said, in the tone of someone who meant *never*, and picked up a telephone with a hand that wasn't quite steady.

Dominic sat down on a chair designed to make waiting feel like punishment and waited like she was enjoying it. Through the window she could see Bram across the street, leaning against a lamp post with his arms crossed, doing an impression of a man with nowhere to be. He was a terrible impression. He kept looking at the door. Behind him, half-hidden by a fruit cart, Tuck sat on the cart's step sharing an apple with the vendor's kid, watching the office with the flat attention of a boy who'd learned young what buildings like this one cost villages.

They came for her in eleven minutes. She counted.

Ives did not come down himself. She'd heard from the Corricks — who had, in the end, paid their deferred debt honestly, and then some — that Ives had been left on the mountain when the Syndicate's extraction boat pulled out without him, and that he'd come down the long way, alone, and hadn't been seen since. The man who met her instead was new to her: silver-haired, soft-handed, wearing the smile of someone whose job description included the word *finality*. His card said MERRICK. Senior Reconciliation.

"Miss Dunmore." He gestured toward the stairs. "Shall we?"

"Lead on."

* * *

The reconciliation room was on the top floor, all walnut and quiet light, with a table polished to the point of dishonesty. On it sat a single ledger, bound in green leather gone soft at the corners, and Dominic knew it before Merrick touched it. She'd seen its twin in her truck six years ago, opened across her passenger seat by a smiling man named Ives while rain hammered the flats outside.

"Six years ago," Merrick began, "your late father—"

"My father's debts died with him," Dominic said. "You bought paper, not people. And the paper is dead too. Sit down and let me save us both an hour."

Merrick sat. That surprised her a little. The good ones always listened first.

"You've been busy," he said.

"I have."

"The Ministry is calling the reservoir project unauthorized. Unratified. A private venture conducted under false government cover." He turned a page in the ledger without looking at it. "Which means the collateral securing your debt — the survey assets, the source claim, the water rights — never legally existed."

"Now you're getting it."

"What remains," Merrick went on, "is a personal obligation. Principal, interest, penalties. A considerable sum." He looked up, and his eyes were not unkind, which was somehow worse than Ives's cheerfulness. This man had done the arithmetic on her life and filed it. "The Syndicate is prepared to discuss terms."

"There's nothing to discuss."

"Everyone says that at the start."

"Merrick." She leaned forward. "Let me tell you what you actually hold. You hold a claim to water rights attached to a project that has been publicly disowned by the Ministry, exposed by three newspapers, and physically shut down. Its sluices are welded — ask your own engineers; they tried to reopen them last week and the Corrick brothers sold them the wrong maps. You hold debt secured against ash. I watched it burn. I fed the furnace myself."

"We could pursue other assets."

"Like what? My truck?" She almost laughed. "Take it. It has two hundred thousand miles on it and a transmission that sounds like a bag of hammers. Take the compass while you're at it — oh wait, you can't. It isn't collateral. It never was. It's the only thing my father ever gave me that wasn't a lie, and it doesn't appear anywhere in your ledger because he was careful, and I am his daughter."

Merrick folded his hands. "There are witnesses now," he said, not as a threat but as a diagnosis. "Journalists. Evacuated villagers. A defected surveyor giving sworn statements. Two salvage brothers with a grievance and a memory for numbers. And a boy from Lowmarsh whose buyout contract the Syndicate countersigned." He paused. "You understand my position. The local office has been instructed to close every account connected to the escarpment matter. Quietly. Cleanly. With no further publicity."

"And mine?"

"Yours," Merrick said, "is the loudest."

"So close it loudly."

He studied her for a long moment. Outside, rain had started again — a soft, ordinary rain, the kind that just fell, that didn't breathe or reverse or take orders from anything. She found she liked it better than any storm she'd ever chased.

"Walk me through it," Merrick said finally. "Convince me there's no version of this where the Syndicate recovers value. Because if there is one, my instructions require me to pursue it."

"Gladly." She stood, because some things you say standing. "One: the project is dead, so the collateral is dead. Two: the debt was signed against my father's estate, and the estate consisted of a compass, a truck, and a headstone you people made sure was dug before he ever got in it. Three: the original loan terms were predatory even by your standards — I've read them now, properly, with a lawyer Sefa found who owes her a favor. Clause nine voids enforcement if the securing venture proves fraudulent. Your own paper hangs you. Four—" she tapped the ledger, "—every name in that book connected to the escarpment is about to become a defendant in a class action filed Monday morning, and the Syndicate's local office can either be a plaintiff's ally or a co-respondent. Choose fast."

Silence. The rain ticked at the window.

"You've changed," Merrick said.

"No," Dominic said. "I stopped hiding. There's a difference."

He opened the ledger. He took out a pen. And in front of her, in small, precise, final strokes, he wrote across the open page — across six years of interest, across the forged signature, across the number that had lived in her chest like a stone — a single line:

*Account settled. Obligation discharged. No further claim.*

Then he blotted it, closed the book, and slid it across the table to her.

"A receipt," he said. "You've earned a strange kind of one."

Dominic looked at the ledger and found, to her surprise, that her hands were shaking. Not fear. Something older than fear, finally letting go of whatever it had been holding.

"It's not enough," she said quietly. "Tuck's village lost a season. Sefa's team is still listed as deserters in two places. My father is still a fraud in the obituaries."

"No," Merrick agreed. "It isn't enough. But it's what this room can give, and this room is finished." He rose, extended his hand; she didn't take it, and he withdrew it without offense, like a man who understood exactly what he was. "For what it's worth, Miss Dunmore — the Syndicate mispriced you from the start. We priced you as collateral. You were never collateral. You were the liability."

"That," Dominic said, picking up the ledger, "is the nicest thing your company has ever said to me."

* * *

Outside, the rain had thinned to mist. Bram pushed off the lamp post the moment she appeared, reading her face the way he read water.

"Well?"

She held up the green ledger.

Bram whistled, low and long. "Six years," he said. "And it fits in one hand."

"It always did. I just couldn't see how small it was." She tucked it under her arm. Across the street, Tuck was already running toward them, and behind him Sefa came at a walk, carrying the newspaper folded under one arm like a weapon she no longer needed to draw.

"Is it done?" Tuck asked, skidding to a stop, breathless. "Are you — do you owe them anything?"

Dominic crouched so they were eye to eye. "Nothing," she said. "Not a copper. Not a favor. Not a look over my shoulder. I owe them nothing, and starting today, everything I owe goes somewhere else." She tapped his chest once, lightly. "Starting there. And there—" Sefa, "—and there." Bram.

Bram grunted, embarrassed, and turned to study the harbor as if it required urgent navigation. Sefa smiled the small, careful smile she was learning to wear.

"The lawyer says the class action files Monday," Sefa said. "And my journalist wants the logbook pages back by Thursday. After that, the team's names go in print. All of them. Even mine."

"Especially yours," Dominic said.

They stood a moment in the mist, four people on a wet street, nobody chasing anybody. Then Tuck tugged her sleeve.

"So what happens now?"

Dominic looked down the hill toward the harbor, where Bram's boat rode at the public landing, and beyond it toward the flats, where the sky was doing something interesting — building, stacking, the first honest thunderhead she'd seen since the escarpment, rolling up out of the west with no Syndicate in it, no debt, no trap. Just weather. Just work.

"Now," she said, straightening up, "somebody owes me money."

Bram turned around slowly. "Run that by me again?"

"A client." She was already walking, and the others fell in beside her because that was simply what happened now. "Came by the café this morning while you were arguing with the harbormaster. Grain broker, needs a storm tracked off the flats before it takes out his harvest convoys. Cash on delivery. No strings." She grinned, and it felt strange and good in her face, like a muscle remembered. "Turns out chasing storms pays better when nobody owns the chaser."

Tuck whooped. Sefa laughed. Bram muttered something about boats needing refitting and money being the root of all refitting, but he was smiling as he said it.

Above the rooftops, the thunderhead climbed, and Dominic Dunmore — solvent, visible, accompanied, free — turned toward it and didn't run.
