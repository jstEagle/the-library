## 36. The Survey Team's Names

The journalist's office was above a print shop in Lowmarsh, and it smelled like ink and old rain. Sefa stood on the stairs for a long time before she knocked.

She had the logbook in a waterproof bag against her chest, the way she'd carried it down the mountain, through the flooded crossing, past the Syndicate's empty office with its windows dark and its sign already half unscrewed from the wall. She had carried it through three days of aftermath, and every one of those days she'd told herself: tomorrow. Tomorrow I'll hand it over.

Tomorrow was a coward's calendar. Her father used to say that. She was thinking about her father a lot lately, which was new, because for eleven months she had trained herself not to.

She knocked.

The woman who opened the door was fifty-something, wire-haired, with ink stains on her fingers and the unimpressed expression of someone who had been lied to professionally for thirty years. "If you're here about the escarpment story," she said, "you're the ninth one this week, and the other eight were selling something."

"I'm not selling anything."

"Everyone says that first." But she stepped back and let Sefa in.

Her name was Odile Vess. Sefa knew the name the way everyone in the river country knew it — the reporter who'd been pushed off two papers and one government post for refusing to soften things. There was a framed retraction on her wall, the only award she displayed. *We regret the error,* it said, in someone else's apologetic font. Underneath, in Odile's handwriting on a card: *I don't.*

Sefa sat across the desk from her and put the waterproof bag down between them like something that might go off.

"What is it?" Odile asked.

"Names," Sefa said. "Eleven of them."

* * *

It took four hours to tell it properly, and Sefa told all of it — not the cleaned version, not the version where she was brave. The version where she ran.

"The night they vanished, we were at Camp Three, upriver of the gorge. Halven Ostrander was team lead. He'd been strange for a week — taking calls on a scrambled line, burning drafts of his own reports. Then the sluices opened below us without a scheduled test, and the whole gorge made this sound—" she stopped, found it again, "—like the river inhaling. Lights came up out of the rock. Not ours. And Ostrander came into the mess tent white as paper and said we were to erase everything. Instruments, logs, samples. He said the project didn't exist and neither did we."

"And you believed him?"

"I believed he was scared enough to mean it. That's worse." Sefa turned the bag over in her hands. "I didn't erase anything. I took my field copy of the logbook and I ran. Downriver, in the dark, no boat. I left them there. Eleven people, and I left them because I was afraid, and then I spent eleven months telling myself they'd walked out some other way, that being gone wasn't the same as being dead, that if I ever went back I'd find the camp empty and everyone fine."

"Were they?"

"We went back in the spring." Dominic's face when they'd found the camp — tents intact, meals half-eaten, instruments still running, and not one footprint leaving. Sefa had stood in the doorway of the mess tent and understood, finally and completely, what she had run from. "No. They weren't."

Odile wrote none of this down. Sefa noticed after an hour — the notebook stayed closed on the desk, and Odile just listened, which was its own kind of instrument.

"You're not recording," Sefa said.

"I'm deciding whether you're credible. Recording comes after." Odile leaned forward. "Here's my problem. Nine people have come to me about the escarpment. Two wanted money to confirm a story. Three wanted me to kill a story. One was a child who mostly wanted to talk about a boat. And the last two were lawyers whose firm doesn't exist. So you'll forgive me if I want to know why you're different."

"Because I'm the one who should have come to you eleven months ago, and everything that happened since is partly my fault."

"That's a start." Odile finally opened the notebook. "Show me the book."

* * *

The logbook opened to Halven Ostrander's neat surveyor's hand, and then, threaded through it in a different script — smaller, angled, certain — the annotations of a dead man who hadn't been dead. Dominic had gone quiet the first time she saw that handwriting. Sefa had watched her trace the letters with one finger, not touching them, and say nothing for a full minute.

Now Odile read the way she listened: completely. Page by page, cross-referencing dates, asking short questions with long consequences. Who signed the funding authorizations. Which ministry seal appeared on the requisitions. What the gauge data showed. When the buyout offers started hitting villages downstream — Tuck's village among them, Tuck's name offered up by Sefa like a piece of evidence, because it was.

"Here," Odile said at last, tapping a page. "This is your affidavit. Right here in his hand. 'Funding for the Karst source survey is routed through Vellum-held paper. If anything happens to this team, the debt structure is the trail.' He knew."

"He knew the Syndicate owned the project's debt. He knew what that meant. The survey wasn't mapping water. It was appraising it." Sefa heard her own voice go flat and hard. "They weren't going to publish findings. They were going to price them."

"And when the team balked—"

"They stopped existing."

Odile sat back. Outside, the print shop clattered through its afternoon run, the building shivering faintly with it, like a pulse.

"Eleven names," Odile said. "Give them to me in order. Full names, roles, next of kin if you know them."

Sefa gave them. It was the hardest hour of the hardest month of her life, harder than the gorge, harder than the crossing, because saying each name aloud made it a fact instead of a wound. Halven Ostrander, team lead; his wife ran a seed exchange in Territ. Mirei Sol, hydrologist; two daughters. Ansel Corbet, instruments. Petra Voss, medic. The Okonkwo twins, Dara and Emeka, geologists, who finished each other's surveys the way they finished each other's sentences. Jun Harada, cook, who fed them all and asked nothing. Liss Ferrow, junior surveyor, nineteen years old, who'd taken the job to pay for her mother's care. And Callum Reeve, logistics, who'd once smuggled extra rations into Sefa's pack because he'd decided she wasn't eating enough.

"Next of kin for Ferrow?" Odile asked.

"An aunt in Millbrook. I have the address. I wrote to her once and never sent it." Sefa pulled the letter from her coat — creased, resealed twice, coward's mail. "You can deliver it. Or I can. I think I should. I think that's part of this."

Odile looked at the letter, then at her. "Yes," she said. "It is."

* * *

The story ran nine days later under a headline Sefa would remember for the rest of her life, mostly because it refused to be dramatic:

**ELEVEN NAMES: THE KARST SURVEY TEAM THAT THE RECORDS FORGOT**

Not *murdered*. Not *silenced*. Not any word Odile couldn't prove. Just *forgot* — and then, over fourteen columns, the forgetting undone. The funding trail through Vellum paper. The falsified maps, decoded, with the reservoir's true footprint laid over the polite blank spaces of the official charts. The gauge data showing engineered pulses. The testimony of a defected surveyor, named in full: **Sefa Marun, 31, of Territ**, who had run, and was saying so in print, in her own words, with her own guilt attached, because Odile had insisted that too.

"You cut the parts where I look decent," Sefa said, reading the draft.

"Nobody trusts a clean witness. They trust a scarred one." Odile had tapped the paragraph where Sefa admitted leaving. "That stays. That's the load-bearing sentence."

The response came in waves. First denial — a ministry statement calling the report "unverified and irresponsible," issued so fast it could only have been pre-written. Then the ministry statement began to unravel, because Odile had spent those same nine days doing what she did: putting documents in front of clerks who'd been waiting years for permission to talk. A procurement officer confirmed the Vellum routing. A cartographer admitted the forbidden zones were drawn by order, not by survey. A retired engineer, coaxed out of silence by the names themselves, confirmed the sluice tests and added, devastatingly, a schedule.

Then the wave that Sefa hadn't braced for: the families.

Mirei Sol's daughters arrived at the print shop on the fourth day, and Sefa met them on the stairs she'd once frozen on, and the older one — sixteen, furious, exactly the age Liss Ferrow had been — said, "You knew my mother," and Sefa said, "Yes," and told her everything. How Mirei had argued with Ostrander about sample protocols and won. How she'd taught Sefa to read seepage patterns in the dark. How she'd laughed at Bram's cooking and lied about liking it.

"You ran," the girl said. Not cruelly. Just checking.

"Yes."

"Did they suffer?"

"No," Sefa said, and it was true, as far as anyone could know, and she held the girl's eyes while she said it because a lie delivered honestly was still a lie but the truth delivered honestly was a gift. "Whatever happened, it was fast. The camp said fast."

It was the first time she'd said *the camp said* out loud to a family member, and it cost her something, and she let it be taken.

By the second week, the retraction economy of the capital was in full motion. Two officials resigned. A third was arrested at an airport. The Vellum Syndicate's local charter was suspended pending investigation — suspended, Sefa noted, not revoked, because institutions amputate limbs before they confess to disease. The debt ledgers tied to the dead project were frozen, which meant, among other things, that a storm chaser named Dominic Dunmore had walked out of the Lowmarsh office owing nothing, and had celebrated, according to Bram, by buying an unreasonable amount of boat paint.

And on the fifteenth day, the government did the thing governments do when a story gets too heavy to bury: they announced an inquiry.

"A commission," Odile said, reading the announcement with the enthusiasm of a doctor reading a terminal chart. "Three years, sealed sessions, a chair who owes his career to the ministry. It'll produce four hundred pages and change nothing."

"Then why publish at all?" Sefa asked.

Odile looked at her over the top of the announcement. "Because the commission isn't the point. The names are the point. Eleven people are on the public record now as having existed, having worked, having objected. You can't un-name them. Every future lie about the escarpment has to climb over their headstones. That's restitution. Not justice — justice is bigger than us. Restitution is what you can actually carry."

Sefa thought about that for a long time. About carrying.

* * *

She went to Millbrook herself, with the unsent letter and a jar of river honey Bram had insisted was appropriate, though for whom remained unclear.

Liss Ferrow's aunt was small and quick-eyed and made tea before she asked why Sefa had come, which Sefa recognized as a technique for making people confess voluntarily. It worked.

"I was on the team with your niece," Sefa said. "I ran the night they disappeared. I've been running since. This is her field logbook — the original goes to the inquiry, but Odile Vess printed copies for the families. This page has her entries. She was good. She was better than good. On the last day she flagged a data anomaly nobody else caught, and if we'd lived, that flag would have shut the project down six months early."

The aunt read the page for a long time. Her hands shook slightly, and she steadied the book on the table like a woman steadying a boat.

"She wrote to me," the aunt said finally. "From the mountain. Last letter came in the spring, a year ago. She said the work mattered. She said, 'Auntie, we're mapping something they don't want mapped, and I finally understand why.'" She closed the book gently. "I thought she was being dramatic. Nineteen-year-olds are dramatic."

"She wasn't."

"No," the aunt agreed. "I'm learning that." She looked up. "You ran. And now you're walking into every house they left behind. Why?"

Sefa had an answer ready — the polished one, about truth and restitution and the public record. She discarded it.

"Because running is a debt," she said. "And I've seen what happens to people who carry debts they can't pay. I'd rather pay this one."

The aunt nodded slowly, and poured her more tea, and for an hour they talked about Liss — her terrible singing, her fear of eels, her habit of naming instruments after ex-boyfriends so she'd enjoy decommissioning them. Grief, Sefa learned, was mostly inventory. You counted what was left until the counting became bearable.

At the door, the aunt pressed the honey jar back into her hands. "Give this to whoever needs sweetening," she said. "And, Miss Marun — the girl who ran came back with the names. In this family, that counts."

Sefa made it to the lane before she cried, and only a little, and she decided that also counted.

* * *

The last copy she delivered by hand, upriver, to a grave on a hillside above Territ where a stone read **HALVEN OSTRANDER, SURVEYOR** — a marker his wife had commissioned after the story broke, replacing the blank municipal plaque that had stood there for a year, because even the dead had been redacted.

His widow met her at the gate. She was planting bulbs, autumn ones, the kind that wait underground all winter for their moment.

"You're the one who brought them back," the widow said.

"I'm the one who left," Sefa said. "Dominic Dunmore brought them back. And Odile Vess. And a boy named Tuck who's the reason the downstream villages got warned. I just carried a book."

"Mm." The widow wiped her hands and studied her with the frank assessment of a woman who had married a man who measured rivers for a living. "Halven used to say the river keeps honest accounts. Everything taken upstream shows up downstream eventually. You're downstream, Miss Marun. What showed up."

"The truth. Late. Damaged. But it showed up."

"Then set it down." The widow gestured at the fresh-turned earth. "He'd have hated the inquiry and loved the headline. He cared about names. He said a surveyor's real product wasn't maps — it was the record. Anyone can draw a line. The record tells you why the line is where it is." She smiled, briefly, at some private archive. "Go home, Miss Marun. Stop carrying it. You've paid."

Sefa stood at the grave a while after the widow went inside. She didn't leave the logbook — the inquiry needed it — but she left a copy, wrapped in oilcloth, weighted with a stone, the way you'd cache supplies for someone you expected to return.

Then she walked down the hill toward the river, where a refitted boat sat at the crossing with fresh paint and an unreasonable amount of it, and Bram was arguing with Tuck about rigging, and Dominic was watching the water with a compass in her hand, and Sefa felt the strangest thing, unfamiliar in her chest, light as ballast released.

Belonging, maybe. Or the beginning of it.

She was halfway down the path when Dominic looked up and called to her, and the tone of her voice — sharp, bright, urgent in a way that had nothing to do with danger — carried across the water:

"Sefa! You're going to want to see what the river just brought in!"
