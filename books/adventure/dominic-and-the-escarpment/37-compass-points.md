## 37. Compass Points

The cemetery sat on a rise above the lowlands, where the ground was too poor for farming and good enough for the dead. Dominic had been there twice in her life—once at nine, when they'd lowered an empty box into the earth because there had been nothing else to lower, and once at nineteen, when she'd come drunk and furious and thrown her father's compass into the grass, then dug it back out of the mud before she'd even reached the gate.

She hadn't been back since. Eleven years of not coming back. She knew every reason she'd told herself, and she knew now that none of them were reasons. They were just weather. You don't drive toward the storm that killed you.

The truck took the switchbacks slowly, the way Bram would have taken them, checking each curve before committing. She'd left the others at the crossing below—Bram refitting the boat's stern with new planking he insisted on planing himself, Sefa drafting honest maps by lamplight, Tuck asleep in the cab of Bram's second truck with a survey manual open on his chest like it could teach him something through osmosis. She'd told them she needed a day alone, and none of them had asked why, which was its own kind of gift.

The grass up here had gone to seed. The headstones leaned at angles that suggested nobody paid much attention anymore, which suited her. Her father's stone stood near the back fence, granite gone soft-edged with lichen, the inscription worn to a whisper: *Aldous Dunmore, 1951–2014. He charted what he loved.*

She stood over it for a long time without speaking.

*He charted what he loved.* Whoever had chosen those words—and she suspected it had been his colleagues at the survey office, the ones who'd signed off on the fiction of his death without ever knowing it was a fiction—had gotten closer to the truth than they knew. He had charted what he loved. He'd charted rivers and aquifers and the hidden plumbing of the escarpment, and he'd loved them so completely that when the people who owned his debts tried to turn that love into a ledger entry, he'd chosen to die rather than let them.

Except he hadn't died. That was the thing she still couldn't make sit flat in her chest. He'd faked it. He'd let her stand at a graveside at nine years old and grieve a man who was alive, forty kilometers north, writing letters he never sent.

She crouched down and pulled the weeds from around the base of the stone. It was stupid work—the weeds would be back in a week—but her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

"I'm not here to forgive you," she said finally. Her voice came out rougher than she expected, unused to open air. "I want to be clear about that up front. I've had three weeks to think about it and I'm still not there."

The wind moved through the seed heads. Somewhere downslope, a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"But I understand you now. That's different. Understanding is cheaper than forgiveness, and I can afford it."

She sat down in the grass, cross-legged, the way she used to sit across from him at the kitchen table while he marked up charts. She'd forgotten that—the way he'd hold his pen loose, ready to abandon it for a better idea mid-stroke. She'd spent eleven years remembering only the leaving. The leaving had gotten bigger than the man.

From inside her jacket she took out the letter. One letter, kept from the stack of unsent correspondence she'd found in his quarters beneath the escarpment—she'd burned the rest in the vault furnace along with the claim documents, because grief that could be archived was still property, and she was done owning things that owned her back. But this one she'd kept. It was dated six years ago, written in his cramped surveyor's hand on monitoring-station paper, and it was the only one addressed to her by name instead of *my daughter*, which somehow made it more his.

She didn't read it aloud. She knew it by heart now; she'd read it every night since the descent, until the folds had gone soft as cloth. But she held it in both hands, facing the stone, the way you'd show someone a photograph.

"You wrote that you hoped I'd inherited your stubbornness and not your debts." A breath. "You got it half right. The Syndicate bought my debt off a haulage loan I took when I was twenty-three. Same paper trail that led back to the project. Ives told me that like it was a kindness—that it meant we were connected." She shook her head. "It did mean that. Just not the way he thought."

The grass tickled her wrists. Above the lowlands, the sky was doing something she recognized: building cumulus over the far ridge, tall and clean, no anvil yet, nothing organized. Weather with nowhere to be. She watched it the way other people watched television.

"The river's free," she said. "I don't know if Sefa's broadcast made it to you—obviously it didn't—but the sluices are closed. Permanently. We welded the master valve after the shutdown sequence, which Bram called redundant and Tuck called awesome. The water runs true from the source to the sea. Tuck's village stands. Half of it evacuated during the flood warning and came back to find their houses exactly as they'd left them, which Tuck says is the first time anything like that has ever happened to anyone from Karst Hollow." She smiled despite herself. "He tells that story like he personally negotiated with the river. Maybe he did. I've stopped being sure where the boy ends and the weather begins."

A cloud shadow crossed the cemetery, cool on her shoulders, and moved on.

"I burned the claim," she went on. "Forty million in water rights, give or take, and I put it in a furnace because it wasn't mine. It was never mine. Dad—it was never *ours*. That's what took me so long to understand. You figured it out years before I did. You lived in a hole in a mountain for eight years rather than sign your name to something that would have made you rich and everyone downstream poor." Her throat tightened. "I used to think that was cowardice. Disappearing. Letting me bury an empty box. Now I think it was the bravest boring thing anyone's ever done, and I hate that I understand it, and I understand it completely."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, impatient with herself. Crying at a grave was allowed—she'd earned it, probably, somewhere in the arithmetic of the last month—but she'd never liked doing things she was allowed to do.

"Here's what I came to tell you, though. The actual business." She unfolded the letter, smoothed it on her knee. "You wrote: *If you're reading this, then either the project failed or you found your way to me, and I've stopped being able to imagine which would be worse.* Well. Neither. The project failed and I found my way past you, and it turns out both of those were survivable. You wrote that debt is just a story powerful people tell about other people's labor. I believe you now. I walked into the Vellum Syndicate's local office two weeks ago owing them everything, and I walked out owing them nothing, and the difference wasn't money. The difference was witnesses. The difference was a boy who wouldn't stay behind and a pilot who wouldn't run ahead and a surveyor who turned herself in to save him. You can't collect on people who stop being afraid, Dad. Nobody ever writes that in the ledger, but it's true."

The wind picked up, tugging at the letter in her hands. She folded it away, careful, back inside her jacket where it had worn a soft rectangle against her ribs.

"I'm done running from you," she said. "That's the last item. I've been chasing storms for eleven years because storms go where they're pointed and they don't leave. You left. So I chased the things that couldn't. And then I climbed a mountain and found out you'd been at the top of the worst one the whole time, keeping it from swallowing the valley." She laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it and some underneath. "You absolute bastard. You made me a storm chaser by dying wrong."

She stayed a while longer after that, saying less. She told him about the boat, and the name they'd painted on the hull—*Compass Points*, Bram's suggestion, voted in over his own objection that naming boats was bad luck. She told him about Sefa's maps, the honest ones, being copied and carried to every village on the lowland circuit. She told him about the client call she'd taken that morning—an open-flats chase, no strings, real pay—and how she'd said yes before the fear could get a word in.

When the light began to go long and gold across the headstones, she stood. Her knees cracked. She put her hand flat on the granite, on the worn words, and left it there until the stone warmed under her palm.

"He charted what he loved," she said. "So will I. Different charts. Same love. That's the inheritance, and nobody can put a lien on it."

She walked back down through the seeding grass without looking back, which was not the same as not looking forward. At the gate she paused, compass in hand, and checked it out of old habit. The needle swung, wobbled, settled—pointing not north but northwest, toward the escarpment, toward the source, toward the buried room where a dead man had spent eight years writing letters to a daughter who was alive.

She didn't correct it. Some instruments know their heading better than their owners do.

At the bottom of the hill, the truck was waiting, and so was Tuck—awake now, sitting on the tailgate with the survey manual, watching her come down the track with an expression she was learning to read as *worried affection*. Behind him, the evening sky over the flats had begun to organize itself, towers going up in earnest, the first distant flicker of something with intent.

"Dominic," Tuck said, pointing past her shoulder. "You're going to want to see this."

She turned. On the horizon, under a bruising green light, the storm was already talking to itself.

* * *

She drove back to the crossing with the window down and the smell of rain coming in ahead of schedule, and Tuck narrating the barometer's fall like a sports commentator. By the time she reached the boatyard, Bram was already standing in the shallows looking at the sky with the particular squint of a man doing math he doesn't like.

"Landfall's tomorrow night," he said. "Maybe the night after. That's not the problem."

"What's the problem?"

Bram jerked his thumb upstream. "Sefa's gauge relay picked up something an hour ago. Widow's Bend. The river's pulsing again—small, but it's there. Something's turning the sluices over."

Dominic felt the ground shift under a day that had been going so well. "We welded that valve."

"We welded it," Bram agreed. "Question is whether somebody upstream had a spare key."

Behind them, from the boat, Sefa called out—voice tight, holding a radio handset like it had bitten her: "Dominic. You need to hear this. It's the Syndicate frequency. Someone's asking for Ives."

The needle of her father's compass, clipped to her collar, trembled once against the wind coming down from the mountain—and steadied, pointing home.
