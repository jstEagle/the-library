## 38. A Crew of Her Own

Bram had owned three boats in his life, and he'd loved all of them the way you love things that can kill you.

The first went to a bank. The second went to the Syndicate, confiscated off a mooring line he still thought about at night—the specific knot they'd cut, the cheap way they'd done it, like his boat wasn't worth the courtesy of untying. The third was the one currently sitting on the shingle below Widow's Bend with its hull stove in along the port side and half the Karst Escarpment's history soaked into its bilge.

He stood over it in the morning light with his hands on his hips and made the sound pilots make when a boat is dying: a long exhale through the teeth.

"It's not dying," Sefa said behind him. She had her survey case open on a flat rock, instruments laid out in rows like surgery. "It's being refit."

"Those are different words for the same funeral."

"You're being dramatic."

"I'm being *accurate.* Look at it." He crouched and put his palm flat against the hull, the way you'd check a fever. "Cracked strake here, here, and—yep—here. Rudder post's scored. Engine mounts sheared clean off during the crossing. And something downriver of the intake I don't even want to name until I've had coffee."

Dominic came down the bank carrying two mugs and handed them around without asking who wanted what. That was new. Six weeks ago she'd have stood at the top of the slope and waited to be invited into her own conversation. Now she just arrived, pressed hot metal into your hands, and looked at the boat with an expression Bram recognized from mirrors: grief held at arm's length by practicality.

"Tuck says we can fix it," she said.

"Tuck is eleven."

"Tuck is eleven and has spent more hours in engine rooms than either of us." She sipped. "He says the crack isn't structural. He says if we pull the planking back and sister it with the oak from the Corricks' abandoned skiff, it'll hold longer than the original did."

Bram drank his coffee and said nothing, because the boy was right, and because saying so out loud felt like admitting how much the kid had gotten under his skin.

* * *

The Corricks' skiff was exactly where they'd left it after the descent—wedged in the willows two bends downstream, listing, abandoned the morning Hal Corrick shook Bram's hand at the flooded crossing and said, "We're square," which from a Corrick was practically a sonnet. Bram had rowed out expecting salvage. What he found instead was Tuck already inside it, up to his shoulders in the bow compartment, passing tools out to nobody.

"There's a whole toolbox under the thwart," the boy said, voice echoing. "Corrick kept good tools. Cheap man, good tools. Never figured that out."

"Give me the adjustable and the flat pry bar."

Tuck passed them down hand over hand, then wriggled out backward, hair full of spider silk, grinning like the boat had told him a joke. "She's got good bones. Like ours."

"She's got *dead* bones. We're grave-robbing."

"We're *recycling,*" Tuck said, with the moral certainty of someone who'd recently learned the word. "Sefa says nothing on this river gets wasted. It just changes hands until somebody uses it right."

Bram pried the first plank free and thought about that. Nothing on this river gets wasted. The gauge at Widow's Bend, salvaged twice now—once by a vanished survey team, once by four people in a stolen week—was bolted to its piling again, broadcasting honest numbers to every village terminal within range. The falsified maps were corrected maps now, redrawn in Sefa's careful hand, copies distributed to anyone who asked and several who hadn't. Even Ives, presumably, was somewhere being recycled by the Syndicate's ledger logic into whatever the system does with optimists who come home empty-handed.

The river took everything and gave it back changed. Maybe boats worked the same way.

* * *

They worked nine days.

Bram ran the refit because it was his boat and his hands knew the work, but a crew formed around him the way weather forms—without permission, without a plan, just pressure and moisture and time. Dominic became his second without either of them discussing it; she had a genius for sequencing, for looking at forty tasks and knowing which six had to happen before the other thirty-four meant anything. Sefa handled everything that measured, calibrated, or recorded, and recaulked seams with a precision that made Bram suspicious she'd built boats in a former life and simply never mentioned it. And Tuck—

Tuck was everywhere. In the engine bay, in the rigging, up the mast cradle Bram had explicitly forbidden, holding fasteners in his mouth and opinions in both fists. He slept on the boat. He ate on the boat. When Bram caught him one midnight running the bilge pump on a dry hull just to hear it work, he didn't have the heart to yell, so he sat down next to him instead and taught him the compression ratios.

"You're going to make me train you properly," Bram said.

"I'm going to make you *want* to."

"That's worse."

But it wasn't. That was the thing Bram kept circling and couldn't land. For twenty years he'd piloted other people's cargo on other people's schedules, and every deckhand who'd ever signed onto his crew had left within a season, because there was nothing to stay for—a wage, a bunk, a river that didn't care. This was different. This was four people sanding varnish in the rain because the map had to be finished, arguing about paint colors because the *name* mattered, and none of them, not once, talking about wages.

* * *

The name argument lasted two days and nearly ended the found family experiment.

Dominic wanted something plain. *Survey Vessel Dunmore.* Sefa wanted something accurate. *Hydrographic Unit Three,* resurrected from the dead team's registry as a kind of restitution. Tuck wanted *The Inhaled Gorge,* which Bram vetoed on the grounds that no insurance assessor alive would write a policy on a boat named after a place where the river runs backward.

"It's not about insurance," Tuck protested.

"Everything is about insurance. That's the first law of rivers."

They settled it the way crews settle things—by exhaustion. On the eighth evening, painting the transom in the last of the light, Dominic knelt down with the brush and wrote it small and clean:

**COMPASS POINTS**

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Sefa laughed—not her careful field laugh, the real one—and Tuck whooped loud enough to scatter herons off the far bank, and Bram looked at the letters drying in the sunset and felt something in his chest go loose that had been tight since the day the Syndicate cut his mooring line.

Her father's compass. Her choice of heading. All of them aboard.

"Yeah," he said. "That'll do."

* * *

The first contract came before the paint was fully cured.

A woman from the village at Miller's Reach walked eleven kilometers up the towpath because the government hydrology office had stopped answering letters—had, in fact, boarded up its windows since the scandal broke—and her irrigation season was starting blind. She'd heard about the crew that shut down the sluices. She'd heard the names read out in the market square from the journalist's dispatch. She had forty credits, a hand-drawn sketch of her channel network, and the expression of someone prepared to argue.

"Forty credits won't cover fuel," Bram said carefully.

"I know what it covers. It covers honesty. Everything else I've been quoted covers lies."

Dominic took the contract. Took it at forty credits, and then quietly put thirty-five of it back into the woman's pocket at the fuel dock, which Bram pretended not to see and Sefa absolutely saw and said nothing about either, because that was the culture they were building—one where the books balanced and the conscience did too, and nobody audited each other's kindnesses.

They mapped Miller's Reach in a day and a half. Every channel, every silted culvert, every illegal diversion some upstream landowner had installed during the chaos years. Sefa documented the diversion with photographs and coordinates and filed it with the same journalist, and by the time the crew tied up that night, the landowner had already received a visit from the regional water board, and the woman from Miller's Reach had water in her fields for the first time in three seasons.

She stood on the bank watching them load gear, and she said, "I thought you'd be bigger. The story makes you sound bigger."

"We're four people and a boat," Bram said.

"The river doesn't need big. It needs *true.*"

He turned that over all the way back downriver. Four people and a boat. Twenty years of flying other men's flags, and it turned out the flag that fit was one he'd helped sew himself, badly, in a boatyard, with a stowaway holding the corners.

* * *

On the tenth evening, moored below the escarpment with the whole cliff face lit gold and the river running true from source to sea, Bram sat on the cabin roof with a chart across his knees and realized he was happy in a way he had no practice at.

Sefa climbed up beside him with the season's schedule scrawled on butcher paper. "Fourteen contracts requested. We can take maybe eight."

"Take the villages first."

"That's what I ordered them. Low pay, high priority." She tapped the list. "There's one at the bottom, though. Government request. Regional water authority wants a full escarpment source-to-mouth survey. Official commission. Signed seal."

Bram looked at her. "The same government that erased a survey team?"

"The same one that's currently very interested in looking reformed." She shrugged. "Could be genuine. Could be a leash. Hard to tell from the paper."

Below them, Dominic was teaching Tuck celestial fixes on the foredeck, the boy's laughter carrying across the water, and Bram weighed the offer against the gold light and the sound of it.

"Take the meeting," he said finally. "But we walk in as a crew, and we walk out as one, whatever they say. Nobody signs anything alone. Not anymore."

Sefa rolled the butcher paper and smiled. "That's the first rule worth putting in a charter."

"What charter?"

"The one we're apparently writing." She tapped his knee and nodded past him, downriver, where a storm front was stacking up over the flats—big, organized, beautiful, the kind of sky Dominic could read like scripture. "Because your pilot's instinct says that weather is coming our way, and your new client list says somebody's going to want us chasing it."

Bram followed her gaze to the storm, then to the woman on the foredeck pointing out stars to a boy who'd never needed to be found because he'd never been lost—just waiting for the right boat.

"Then we'd better finish the charter," he said.
