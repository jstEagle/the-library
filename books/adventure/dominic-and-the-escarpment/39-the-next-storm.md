## 39. The Next Storm

The letter came on a Tuesday, which was how Dominic knew it wasn't from the Syndicate. The Vellum Syndicate did not believe in Tuesdays, or in anything that arrived without an invoice attached.

She found it wedged under the wiper of the truck, parked outside Bram's boathouse where she'd been sleeping more nights than not, on paper so heavy it felt like money. Her name typed out clean: *Dominic Dunmore, Field Meteorology & Chase Services.* No title after it. No *Esquire.* No *or assigns.* Just her name, spelled right, which already put this client ahead of most.

Inside: a single sheet, a retainer check that made her sit down on the tailgate, and a map of the open flats east of the escarpment with a hand-drawn circle around nothing in particular. The note beneath it read:

*Supercell complex expected to initiate over the flats within the week. We want data from inside the circulation, not outside it. You set the terms. You pick the crew. No exclusivity clauses, no salvage riders, no forgiveness jobs. If you say no, we'll hire someone worse and regret it.*

It was signed *M. Okonkwo, Atmospheric Research Cooperative,* and the signature looked like it had been written by someone who signed things fast and meant them slowly.

Dominic read it three times. Then she checked the check again, because the first two readings had to be wrong about the number.

*No strings.* She'd heard those words before. Ives had said them, once, in a truck cab that smelled like ledger paper and cologne, right before he explained what the strings were woven into. *Optimism, Ms. Dunmore,* he'd said, smiling like a man showing you his teeth were also his keys. *Every contract is just optimism with terms.*

But this one had no terms. She read it a fourth time looking for the trap—the rider buried in clause nine, the lien hidden in the footer, the little sentence that turned a job into a leash. There was nothing. There was a phone number, a weather service subscription code, and a line at the bottom that said: *Payment on completion. Not before, not contingent, not reversible.*

She laughed. It came out rusty, like an engine turning over after a season in the shed.

Bram found her there twenty minutes later, still on the tailgate, the check in one hand and her father's compass in the other, holding them up side by side as if weighing which one pointed true.

"That's either good news," Bram said, "or you've finally cracked."

"Client."

"We have a client?"

"*I* have a client." She tapped the letterhead. "Cooperative. Research outfit. They want someone to drive at a storm on purpose and come back with numbers."

Bram leaned against the truck and crossed his arms, and she watched him do the arithmetic every river pilot does when handed something that looks free. "What's the catch?"

"I've been looking for forty minutes. There isn't one."

"There's always one."

"Not this time." She said it and then heard herself say it, and realized it was the first time in years—maybe the first time since her father died—that she'd stated something about money with total confidence and no asterisk. The debt was gone. Not forgiven, not deferred, not restructured into a kinder shape of the same cage. Gone. The Syndicate's local office had spent three weeks discovering that their claim on her was collateralized against a project that no longer legally existed, held by a company whose directors were currently being interviewed under oath by people with subpoena power, thanks to a journalist named Ferro and a logbook Sefa had carried down a mountain through a flood. The ledger had been settled. She'd walked out of that office owing nothing, and the clerk behind the desk had looked at her the way clerks look at weather they can't bill.

So now here was a stranger offering her money to do the thing she was actually good at, on her own terms, and her hands didn't know what to do with that. They kept checking for the string.

"You're going to take it," Bram said.

"I'm going to take it."

"Good. Because Tuck already told everyone at the market we're famous, and Sefa's been redrawing the boat's registration all morning like a woman expecting a knock on the door."

* * *

They took the job in the boathouse that evening, all four of them around the table where the charts lived, and it went less like a signing and more like a mutiny conducted politely.

Sefa read the contract twice, front and back, with the flat attention she gave to survey documents, and pronounced it "clean, which frightens me more than a dirty one." Then she added a rider anyway—a data-sharing clause, cooperative-owned findings published openly, no proprietary black-boxing—and the Cooperative accepted it by return wire without comment, which told Dominic everything about who these people were.

Tuck, fifteen now and taller than any of them liked to acknowledge, appointed himself logistics officer by acclamation nobody voted on. He requisitioned, in order: fuel, rope, a second radio antenna, and a case of the orange candies he claimed were "mission-critical." Nobody argued. Mission-critical candy had gotten them through Widow's Bend; nobody was going to die on that hill.

Bram negotiated the one thing he cared about, which was that the chase vehicle would carry a boat. "Flats flood," he said. "You know this. You've been flooded off flats. I've pulled you off flats. We are bringing the boat."

"We're chasing a tornado, not a river."

"The river is wherever the rain decides. Bring the boat."

They brought the boat.

And Dominic sat at the head of the table—which was not the head of the table, it was just the chair closest to the coffee, but somehow it had become the head of the table—and watched her crew argue about antenna placement and tire pressure and whether Tuck was allowed to drive on paved roads (he was not; the vote was three-to-one with Tuck abstaining on grounds of principle), and felt something settle in her chest that she didn't have a word for yet.

She'd spent six years being invisible. It had been a policy, then a survival strategy, then a personality. Stay small, stay unlisted, stay off ledgers. A woman nobody could find owed nothing and lost nothing. She'd worn invisibility like her father's old rain jacket—too big, smell of someone else, but it kept the worst of it off.

Now she was visible. Listed. Contracted under her own name with a crew attached and a reputation that preceded her into rooms, and the strange part—the part she kept turning over like a stone—was that visibility hadn't cost her anything. It had cost her everything, once, up on the escarpment: the claim burned, the inheritance refused, half the river swallowed and given back. But what it bought was this. A table. People around it. Work worth doing, priced honestly.

The compass sat on the table between the candy and the charts. Her father's compass, brass-cased, needle drunk near iron, the one thing she'd kept from the vault besides a single unsent letter that lived now in the breast pocket of her chase jacket, over the heart, where letters belong.

Tuck noticed her looking at it. "Which way's it pointing?"

"Nowhere," she said. "We're indoors. It's sulking."

"It always points somewhere."

"It points where I decide," she said, and heard her father's voice underneath her own, and didn't flinch from it this time. That was new too. Grief had stopped being a locked room and become a house she could walk through with the lights on.

* * *

The storm announced itself two days early, the way honest storms do.

Dominic stood on the flats at dawn with the wind already combing the grass flat and the sky doing that thing she'd spent her whole life learning to read—the green-black bruise building over the western horizon, the air pressure dropping so fast her ears popped, the birds going quiet in that specific offended way birds have when the atmosphere breaks its promises. Behind her, the truck idled with the boat trailer hitched, antennas up, Tuck running diagnostics and narrating them to no one at volume.

Sefa stood beside her with the anemometer. "Initiation window moved up. Okonkwo's models say ninety minutes."

"Okonkwo's models and my gut agree. That's rare enough to be frightening."

"What does your gut say?"

Dominic breathed in. The flats smelled like rain and cut grass and diesel from the road, and underneath it all, faintly, like a signature, like handwriting—the escarpment, thirty miles west, standing blue-gray against the storm light. Her father was in that stone somewhere. So was Ives, presumably, though the last report she'd heard had him defending himself before a Syndicate tribunal with a smile that reportedly "no longer reached the eyes," which was the single most satisfying sentence anyone had ever relayed to her.

"My gut says," Dominic said, "that this is the best day of my life, and I've had worse ones recently enough to appreciate the ranking."

Sefa almost smiled. Sefa rationed smiles like water in a drought, so when one came it meant something. "Then let's go earn our retainer."

They rolled out at 0700 with the storm wall building purple-black ahead of them, and it was—God, it was *good.* It was the old feeling, the chase feeling, the reason she'd ever done this work before debt got its hooks in it: the truck humming, the wind rising, the whole crew moving as one animal with four opinions. Bram calling road conditions from the passenger seat like a man reading rapids. Tuck on radios, relaying to the Cooperative, his voice steady and professional except when it wasn't. Sefa logging data with the calm of someone who had once erased nothing and would never erase anything again.

And Dominic driving straight at the ugliest sky in the world, completely solvent, completely visible, completely *free.*

At 0940 the cell dropped its funnel two miles north of their position, a gray rope reaching down out of a rotating green-black base, and Dominic pulled them into position—not too close, never too close, she'd learned that lesson in blood and floodwater—and let the instruments drink.

"Data's beautiful," Sefa reported. "Clean circulation, clean pressure trace. Okonkwo's going to lose their minds."

"Tell them the crew of the *Brightwater* sends regards."

"We don't call it the Brightwater."

"We're calling it the Brightwater. I decided at breakfast."

The tornado crossed the road a half-mile ahead, took a fence line, missed a farmhouse by a hundred yards, and dissolved back into the mesocyclone like a thread returning to a spool, and the whole crew exhaled at once, and Tuck whooped loud enough to distort the radio, and Bram said, "See? Flats. Easy. Didn't even need the boat."

The rain hit ten minutes later, horizontal and warm, drumming the roof like applause.

* * *

They camped that night on high ground above the flats, storm receding east in flickers of internal lightning, the sky clearing from the west into that washed-forever blue that only comes after. The Cooperative's payment confirmation had come through at dusk—full amount, on completion, exactly as promised, no deductions, no riders—and Dominic had stared at the notification for a long time before showing anyone.

"So that's it," Tuck said, poking the fire. "We're legit."

"We're legit."

"Sefa's legit?"

"Sefa's legit. Her pardon came through Tuesday."

"Bram's legit?"

"Bram squints at it, but yes."

"And you're—" Tuck looked up, firelight on his face, and asked it the way kids ask things, which is to say without any armor at all: "Are you legit? Like, actually? All the way through?"

Dominic thought about it seriously, because he deserved a serious answer. She thought about ledgers and leashes, about a collector's smile, about a logbook in her dead father's hand dated after his death. About burning paper in a furnace and swallowing half a river and walking out of an office owing nothing. About a compass that pointed wherever she decided.

"Yeah," she said. "All the way through."

Tuck nodded, satisfied, and threw another stick on the fire, and the conversation moved on to whether the boat needed a name if the truck had one (it did; the truck was *Ledger-Settled*, over Bram's objections, and the boat was pending).

Later, when the others slept, Dominic walked out past the firelight onto the flats alone. The grass whispered. The eastern horizon still muttered with the dying storm, but the west was clear, and far off, barely visible in the starlight, the escarpment stood against the sky like a spine.

She took out the compass. Opened the case. The needle swung, wobbled, and settled—pointing west, toward the high ground, toward the source, toward everything that had happened and everything it had made her.

She didn't close the case. She just held it, letting the needle rest where it wanted, and understood that it wasn't pointing at the mountain anymore. It was pointing at whatever came next. The next storm, the next river, the next bad contract offered by a good-seeming stranger, the next kid stowing away in a boat, the next impossible choice between what pays and what's right. There would be more. There were always more. But she wasn't braced for them anymore. She was *facing* them.

Her father had left her a debt she never owed and an inheritance she refused to sell, and between those two facts she had built a life. Not the life she'd planned. Better. Wetter. More crowded.

"Point anywhere you want," she told the compass quietly. "I'll follow."

The needle trembled, steadied, and held.

Behind her, at the fire, Tuck's voice drifted up through the dark, arguing with Bram about boat names, and Sefa's low laugh answered, and the wind came across the flats carrying the smell of rain that had already fallen and rain that was still coming, and Dominic Dunmore—visible, solvent, accompanied, free—turned toward the sound of her crew and started walking.
