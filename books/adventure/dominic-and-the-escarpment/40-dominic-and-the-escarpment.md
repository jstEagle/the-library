## 40. Dominic and the Escarpment

The high ground above Widow's Bend was called Lookout Ledge by the people who lived in its shadow, though nobody Dominic had met could say what anyone was supposed to be looking out for. She'd decided, on her third morning up there, that it was probably weather. It was always weather. The escarpment made its own sky the way other places made their own noise—constantly, and without apology.

She sat on a shelf of pale limestone with her boots hanging over a drop that would have killed her twice over, and she watched the river.

It ran true. That was the phrase Sefa used, and it had stuck. *Running true* meant the water moved the way water was supposed to move—downhill, steady, unbothered by anything except gravity and the shape of the land. No pulses. No reversals. No long inhale from the gorge where the sluices had once opened and closed like the mountain breathing through gills. Just a river, doing river things, all the way down to the sea.

Three weeks since the vault. Three weeks since she'd burned the claim documents in the monitoring station's furnace while Ives stood in the doorway with his smile finally coming apart at the seams like wet paper. Three weeks since the machine beneath the escarpment had gone quiet for good, and the monsoon had broken over them like a held breath finally released.

Dominic had spent most of those three weeks learning how to sit still.

It was harder than storm chasing. Harder than the ascent. There was a particular kind of restlessness that lived in a person who had spent years making herself small—small debts, small jobs, small footprint, invisible to the Vellum Syndicate and every outfit like it—and then discovered, somewhere on a drowned mountain, that she had never actually been small at all. The stillness kept snagging on something. Like a boat dragging an anchor it didn't know it had dropped.

So she came up here. Every morning, more or less. She brought coffee in a dented thermos and her father's compass, and she sat on the limestone shelf and watched the water run true, and slowly, day by day, the anchor came up.

* * *

"You're going to fall off that thing one of these days."

Bram's voice carried up the slope before he did. He climbed the last stretch with the easy, rolling gait of a man who had spent forty years on moving surfaces and considered solid ground mildly suspicious. He dropped onto the ledge beside her, produced his own cup from somewhere inside his coat, and held it out for coffee without asking.

"Boats capsize," Dominic said, pouring. "Ledges don't."

"Ledges have edges. Same thing." He settled back on his palms and squinted out at the valley. Below them, the river braided silver through the green, past the rebuilt crossing at Widow's Bend, past Tuck's village with its new roofs and its old name, past the terraced fields that had been underwater in some futures and weren't in this one. "Sefa's got the gauge data cleaned up. Full season's record. She's calling it the first honest hydrological survey this escarpment's ever had."

"Is she putting her name on it?"

"Sefa Brightwater-Okafor." Bram said it deadpan, but his eyes were laughing. "Well. Not the Brightwater part. But yes. Her name, front page, no erasures. Journalist filed it Tuesday. Apparently there's a parliamentary inquiry now." He sipped. "Names are coming back, Dom. All of them. Even the ones that were buried under your father's."

Dominic turned the compass over in her hands. Her father's initials were worn nearly smooth on the brass casing—she'd traced them so many times as a child that she'd erased him a little, which felt, these days, like the truest metaphor for what the world had done to Elias Dunmore. Faked death. Buried quarters. Unsent letters in a drawer, addressed to a daughter who'd grown up thinking she was the only thing he'd ever abandoned.

"I went to the grave again yesterday," she said.

"I know. You told me you were going."

"I told Tuck. Tuck tells everyone."

"Tuck is a communications network with a missing tooth," Bram agreed. "What did you tell him? The grave. Not Tuck."

"That it was paid. The debt. All of it." She watched the light move on the water. "I told him about the letter I kept. The one where he wrote that he hoped I'd never have to learn what things cost—the real cost, not the ledger cost. And I told him I'd learned anyway, and that it was okay, because I'd also learned what things were worth. Different lists entirely."

Bram nodded slowly, the way he did when he was letting a silence do work instead of filling it. Out on the river, a barge moved downstream, riding low with survey equipment—*their* equipment, the refitted boat's first contract, mapping the channels below the bend for the villages that drank from them. Honest maps. Politely lying maps retired forever.

"The Syndicate office closed," Bram said after a while. "You hear?"

"I heard."

"Packed up overnight. Left the furniture. Ives's desk chair is sitting in the town square now. Kids take turns spinning in it." He glanced sideways at her. "They're calling it the Optimist's Chair."

Despite herself—despite everything Ives had taken from them, the boats seized, the supplies dumped, Tuck nearly walked off the mountain in chains—Dominic laughed. It came easier than it used to. Everything did.

"He believed it, you know," she said. "Right up until the end. That he was the kind one. That forgiveness was a product he was generous enough to sell."

"They always do. The worst ones smile best." Bram drained his cup. "That's why what you did matters, Dom. You didn't beat him with a bigger ledger. You took the ledger away."

* * *

Tuck found them an hour later, which meant they heard him twenty minutes earlier, crashing up the switchback trail with the total disregard for stealth of a boy who had recently learned that being seen was survivable. He arrived at the ledge breathing hard, hair full of burrs, holding something behind his back with the elaborate casualness of a terrible liar.

"There you are," he said. "I've been looking everywhere."

"We're on top of a cliff," Dominic said. "There's nowhere else."

"Exactly. Everywhere else is down." He plopped between them, accepted a swig of coffee, grimaced at it, drank more anyway. Then he thrust the thing from behind his back at her: a folded square of paper, soft with handling. "Village gave me this to give you. Well. Mama Osei made it. Everyone signed it. Even the Corricks, when they came through last week."

Dominic unfolded it. It was a map—not a survey map, nothing official, but a hand-drawn thing in ink and colored pencil, the river rendered in blue that had bled slightly into the green of the banks. The escarpment rose along its spine in careful crosshatching. At the source, where the vault had been, someone had drawn a small sun. And along the bottom, in a dozen different hands, adult and child, careful and scrawled: names. Dozens of names. Villages, families, the crew of the refitted boat, two Corrick brothers who had signed with an X and then, embarrassed, printed their names underneath.

At the very end, in Tuck's uneven handwriting: *Dominic Dunmore — who came down the mountain.*

She read it twice. Something in her chest did a complicated thing that she decided not to examine on a cliff edge.

"It's a debt record," she said quietly.

Tuck frowned. "It's a thank-you."

"It's both." She folded it carefully and put it inside her jacket, over the pocket where the compass rode. "The only ledgers worth keeping are the ones like this. What people owe each other because they showed up. That's the whole list."

Tuck considered this with the seriousness he gave anything that sounded like it might be a rule. Then: "Sefa says the next survey starts Thursday. Upstream tributaries. She says you're navigating."

"I'm navigating," Dominic confirmed.

"And Bram says—" Tuck grinned, gap-toothed and shameless, "—that if you fall off this ledge, he gets your share of the contract."

"Bram," Dominic called down the slope, "is a vulture."

From below, unhurried: "A solvent vulture."

* * *

She stayed up there until late afternoon, alone again, when the others had gone down to the boat. The light came long and gold across the valley, and the escarpment showed itself the way it rarely did in the season of storms: completely. Every fold of limestone, every green throat of ravine, the gorge where the river had once inhaled, the hidden spine where her father had walked for years under a false name to keep water free. In clear light it looked like what it was—not a fortress, not a prize, just land. Old, patient, indifferent to every claim ever staked on it.

Her father had tried to give it away. The Syndicate had tried to buy it. The government had tried to erase it. And it had simply remained, running its river toward the sea, waiting for someone to understand that it had never belonged to any of them.

Inheritance. She'd spent her whole life thinking the word meant what you were left. A compass. A debt. A name with a stain on it. But sitting here, with the burned claim's ash long since scattered and the paper map full of names warm against her ribs, she understood it differently. Inheritance wasn't what you were left. It was what you refused to sell. Her father had refused, and died for it, and the refusal had passed to her like blood—like water—finding its level.

The river caught the low sun and ran gold from the source to the sea, one unbroken thread, owned by no one, owed to everyone.

Dominic Dunmore stood up on the ledge, visible in the open light, owing nothing to anyone, and watched it go.

Then she checked her father's compass out of habit, laughed at herself, put it away, and started down the mountain—to the boat, to the crew, to Thursday, to whatever weather was coming next that she would finally get to meet head-on, on her own terms, with her own people, in full view of the whole bright world.

The river ran true. So did she.
