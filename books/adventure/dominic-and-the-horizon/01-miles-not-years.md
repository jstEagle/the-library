## 1. Miles, Not Years

The tide went out at four in the morning, and by six Rosalind Sterling had the riverbed to herself.

She stood ankle-deep in grey silt where the Meghna's distributary had peeled back from the wreck like a bandage coming off a wound, and she did the thing she always did at the end of a dig: she counted. Not the artifacts — those were logged, bagged, tagged, photographed twice. She counted the distances.

Eleven thousand two hundred and forty kilometers from the last dig. Four hundred from the nearest hospital that could handle a decompression case, which mattered on a salvage job even when the water was only chest-deep. Ninety meters from the village well, because the elders had asked her to keep the generators that far off, and you honored that or you packed up and went home. Sixty-two centimeters between the hull of the *Padma Star* and the mud it had been sinking into for thirty-one years, which was the number that had brought her here in the first place, and the only one anyone would remember once the reports were filed.

That was how she measured a life now. In miles, not years. Years required staying still long enough for them to accumulate, and Rosalind had stopped doing that somewhere over the Pacific a decade ago, somewhere around the time she'd learned that grief was lighter if you never set it down.

"Boss." Priya's voice came crackling through the handheld, tinny with static and morning. "Tide's turning. You've got maybe ninety minutes before this whole bed goes back under."

"I know."

"You've been standing there staring at that bulkhead for twenty minutes. I can see you from the barge. You look like a woman arguing with a shipwreck."

"I'm not arguing." Rosalind crouched, pressed her palm flat against the corroded steel. It was warm already, the sun climbing fast over the flat green country to the east, and beneath the warmth she could feel the faintest vibration — the river's pulse, the water coming back. "I'm saying goodbye."

A pause on the line. "You want me to pretend I didn't hear that?"

"Pretend you didn't hear that."

"Copy." Static. Then, softer: "Chai's on. Okonkwo made real food. Dex is trying to steal the good tarp again."

"On my way."

She took one more minute anyway. The *Padma Star* was nothing — a river freighter, no treasure, no history anyone outside three districts would care about — but it had gone down with eleven souls aboard in '93, and nine of them had come home because of what her crew had pulled out of the mud this week. That was the work, when it was good. Not gold. Names. Bones given back to the families who'd spent thirty years setting an extra place at the table out of habit.

Her father would have called it small work. Her father had spent his career chasing things that glittered, and it had killed him in every way except the literal one — though Rosalind suspected, lately, from the way the letters from his nurse had changed tone, that the literal one was coming for him too.

She shut that thought in the box where she kept it and waded back toward the barge.

* * *

The camp came down in ninety minutes because her crews came down in ninety minutes, always. Priya ran the rigging and the engines and half the budget on a laptop held together with tape and spite. Okonkwo broke down the medical station with the same unhurried precision he used for everything, folding gauze like he was origami-ing a swan. Dex — sixteen, all elbows and stolen bandwidth, officially their runner and unofficially their entire communications department — was up on the barge roof coiling cable and narrating his own life like a sports commentator, which nobody had ever asked him to stop doing.

"—and Sterling takes the ladder like she's got somewhere better to be, which, rude, because we both know she doesn't—"

"Dex."

"—nothing, boss. Great dig. Ten out of ten. Very muddy."

They ate on the deck as the barge pulled downstream, rice and river fish and Okonkwo's suspiciously good pepper sauce, the green country sliding past on both banks, children waving from the shallows the way they always did, the way they waved at every boat, because every boat might be somebody's father coming home. Rosalind ate with her boots off and her back against the rail and let herself feel, for exactly the length of one meal, the thing she refused to call contentment.

This was the freedom. This was what she'd built. No office, no name on the letterhead — every contract signed *R. Sterling Consulting*, no first name, no lineage, no connection to Edmund Sterling, whose face had been on magazine covers while hers was still learning its own reflection. No museum boardrooms. No legacy. Just the work, and the water, and these three improbable people who had followed her across four continents without ever asking why she never unpacked.

"So," Priya said, scraping her bowl. "Next contract."

"There's a ferry in Lake Volta. Ghana side. Sank in '06, two hundred souls, government finally approved recovery."

"Ghana." Priya said it the way other people said *root canal*. "It's monsoon-adjacent, isn't it."

"It's fine."

"It's always fine. The last one was fine. The one before that was fine. Boss, I love you, but your definition of fine has a body count of hypothermia cases."

"One hypothermia case. And Okonkwo fixed him."

"I fixed him," Okonkwo said mildly, not looking up from his tea, "because someone has to counterbalance your definition of fine."

Dex swung down from the roof, landing on the deck with the gracelessness of youth. "There's also the thing," he said, and something in his voice made them all look up. "Email came in this morning. I flagged it low priority because — I don't know. It felt weird."

"Weird how?"

"Weird like, it's addressed to *Sterling*." He handed her the tablet. "Not R. Sterling Consulting. Just — Sterling."

Rosalind looked at the screen. The sender was a name she didn't recognize, but the subject line read: *Re: E.S. — final survey, Karakoram.* And below it, a single line: *He's asking for you. Time is shorter than you think.*

She closed the tablet and handed it back.

"Delete it."

"Boss—"

"Delete it, Dex."

He deleted it. He was good about things like that; it was part of why she kept him, though she'd have denied it under oath. But she saw him glance at Priya, and she saw Priya glance at Okonkwo, and she knew they'd talk about it later in the galley after she turned in, the way they talked about everything, the way a family talks about the thing everyone sees and no one names.

The river carried them on. Somewhere behind them, upstream past a thousand bends and a border and half a mountain range, an old man was dying in a house she hadn't entered in ten years, and he was asking for her, and the miles between them were the only currency she had left to spend.

She spent the afternoon checking mooring lines instead.

* * *

They put in at Chandpur that evening, a port town strung along the confluence like beads on wet string, all rust and neon and the smell of diesel and frying onions. Rosalind liked ports. Ports were honest — everything in them was either arriving or leaving, nothing pretended to belong. She paid the crew off for the night, told them to eat something that hadn't been dehydrated, and walked alone to the dockside bar she'd been told about by a boatman, the kind of place with plastic chairs and a fan that moved air around without cooling it and a bottle of warm beer for less than pocket change.

She was halfway through the beer when the phone rang.

Unknown number. International code she knew by heart, because she'd known it since childhood: the country code of the house she grew up in, the house with the survey maps papering the hallway and the smell of pipe smoke and old paper.

She let it ring.

It rang out. Thirty seconds of silence. Then it rang again, and this time she looked at it properly, and she thought: nurses don't call twice unless it's bad. Nurses call once to be polite and twice because the man in the bed has been asking, or because the doctors have said words like *weeks* instead of *months*, or because someone has to hear it and write it down and make it real.

She picked up on the third ring of the second call.

"Sterling."

"Miss Sterling?" A woman's voice, careful, accented, tired in the way caregivers get tired — not of the work but of the weight of it. "My name is Anneliese. I'm your father's nurse. I've written to you four times."

"I don't read most of my mail."

"Yes. He knows that too." A pause. Down the line, faintly, Rosalind could hear the sound of a house at night — a clock, wind against glass, the particular silence of high altitude. "I'm calling because he's asked me to stop writing. He says if you wanted to be found, you wouldn't need letters."

"That sounds like him."

"He also says—" Anneliese stopped. Started again. "Miss Sterling, I've nursed dying men for twenty years. Most of them want the same things. Their pain managed, their affairs ordered, and one person summoned who doesn't want to come. I'm not calling to guilt you. I'm calling because he asked me to tell you one thing, and then he asked me never to contact you again, and I keep my word to dying men."

Rosalind turned the beer bottle slowly on the table, watching the condensation ring spread on the wood. "What thing?"

"The final survey. The Karakoram one. He wants you to know it wasn't lost. He hid it. And he says—" the nurse's voice dropped, went careful, like someone walking on ice, "—he says what's in it is worth more than his name, and that some people will do anything to make sure it stays buried, and that if anyone ever comes to you offering to go find it, you should say no. Whatever they offer. However much they pay."

The fan overhead ticked through its rotation. At the next table, two fishermen argued cheerfully about engine parts. The world went on being ordinary, and Rosalind sat very still inside it.

"Why are you telling me this?" she said. "If he asked you never to contact me — why break it?"

"Because yesterday a man called the house. He said he was a colleague. He asked questions about the survey that colleagues don't ask." A breath. "And today your brother's name came up in his questions. So I broke my word. Forgive me."

The bottle stopped turning.

"Eli's dead," Rosalind said. "Ten years. What possible question—"

"I don't know, Miss Sterling. I only know the man's voice made your father try to get out of bed, and he hasn't stood since March." The nurse's voice gentled. "I'm sorry. I've said what I was asked to say and more than I should have. Goodbye."

The line went dead.

Rosalind sat in the plastic chair for a long time, the warm beer going warmer, the fan ticking, the port lights smearing themselves across the black river outside. *Say no,* the old man had said, from his bed, through his nurse, across all those miles. *Whatever they offer.*

Which meant, of course, that someone would offer.

She finished the beer, paid in cash, and walked back along the dock with her collar up against the river damp, telling herself the tightness in her chest was just the humidity, just the end-of-dig fatigue, just anything other than what it was. Behind her the port clanged and hissed. Ahead of her the barge rode at anchor, one light burning in the galley where her crew sat up late talking about her, the way families do.

She was almost aboard when she heard footsteps on the planking behind her — unhurried, deliberate, the walk of a man who had never once in his life needed to hurry because the world always waited for him.

She knew the walk before she turned around. She'd known it since university, since the field school in Corsica, since a decade of crossing paths at conferences and auctions and the sites of other people's tragedies.

"Hello, Rosalind," Marcus Vane said, stepping into the galley light with a document folder under his arm and a smile that had opened a hundred doors and closed just as many. "Lovely river. Terrible timing. We need to talk about your father."
