## 2. The Rival's Offer

The bar at the end of Fishery Ghat Road had no name, which was how Rosalind liked her bars. A tin roof sweating condensation, a ceiling fan turning with the conviction of something that had given up years ago, and a proprietor who asked no questions because questions cost more than they earned. She'd been coming here for six days since the dig closed out — long enough to establish a pattern, which was careless of her, and she knew it, and she came anyway.

She sat at the same table every night. Corner seat, back to the wall, sightline to the door and the window that looked out on the moored salvage barge. Old habits. You could take the girl out of the survey camps, but you couldn't take the survey out of the girl: she still thought in sightlines, escape routes, load-bearing walls.

The beer was warm and cheap and tasted faintly of rust, and she was halfway through the second bottle when Marcus Vane walked in.

She knew before she saw him. The room changed — not dramatically, nothing so kind as drama. It just tightened. The two fishermen at the far table stopped talking mid-sentence, the way rooms do around money, and the proprietor's posture shifted from indifference to attention. Then Vane filled the doorway, ducking under the lintel like the building owed him an apology for its height.

Ten years since she'd seen him up close. He'd gone silver at the temples, which suited him — he'd probably paid someone to make it look accidental. Same tailored jacket, wrong for the climate, worn anyway because Marcus Vane had never once dressed for the room instead of the version of himself he was selling. His eyes found her in the corner in under three seconds, which told her he'd already checked the layout from outside.

"Sterling." He said it like a handshake.

"Vane." She said it like a door closing.

He crossed the room anyway, pulled out the chair across from her, and waited — a courtesy he performed rather than felt — until she shrugged, which he took as invitation. It always amazed her how much a man could build an entire career out of misreading shrugs.

"You're hard to find," he said.

"No, I'm easy to find. I'm hard to approach."

"Fair." He signaled the proprietor with two fingers, ordered whatever she was having, and settled in with the ease of a man who believed time worked for him. "Bangladesh. I'll admit, I had you pegged for Peru this season."

"I keep my schedule where I keep my heart. Not in any port."

"That's a terrible line."

"It's mine, though."

His drink arrived. He didn't touch it. That was another thing about Vane — he never drank when he was working, and Marcus Vane was always working. She'd learned that watching him charm ministry officials in Kathmandu a decade ago, back when they were both younger and the word *rival* hadn't yet curdled into something with teeth.

"How's the knee?" he asked.

"Rains are coming. It knows."

"And the crew? Still collecting strays?"

"They're not strays. They're people who prefer me to everyone else available. There's a difference."

"There really isn't." He smiled, and she hated that part of her noted it was still a good smile, the kind that opened doors in four languages. "I need to talk to you about a job."

"No."

"You don't know what—"

"I know what a job is, Marcus. It's a thing you offer me when you want something you can't take. No."

He let the silence sit. Outside, a launch went downriver trailing lights, and the water slapped against the pilings in that patient way water has, like it's keeping count of something.

"The Sterling Final Survey," he said quietly.

And there it was. Her father's last expedition. Eleven years gone now, vanished into the Karakoram along with four men, a full instrument set, and — though the world's press never quite managed this detail — her brother Eli, who wasn't supposed to be on it, who'd stowed away because he was nineteen and immortal and wanted one season in the high country with his father before university took him.

One season. The glacier kept it.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said, and her voice came out level, which cost her something behind the sternum.

"Of course you don't." He reached into his jacket — slow, deliberate, showing her his hands the way you do with people who've handled weapons — and set a folder on the table between them. Heavy stock. A wax seal, because of course there was a wax seal; subtlety had never been Vane's love language. "Then you won't mind if I talk about it alone."

She should have walked out. She knew that even then, some cold auditor in the back of her head filing the moment under *errors*. But Eli's name had been said in a dockside bar in Bangladesh by the last person alive who'd been on that final expedition's planning team, and her body stayed in the chair the way bodies stay at gravesides.

* * *

Vane talked for twenty minutes. He was good at it — always had been. The story unspooled clean: a land surveyor in Skardu, clearing a dead client's effects, had found a partial copy of Edmund Sterling's final field notes. Coordinates. Instrument logs. A route marked through the Panmah region that no official record acknowledged. Enough to reconstruct where the expedition had been when it disappeared, and possibly enough to recover the full survey cache — if the ice had kept it.

"The family hired search parties for years," Vane said. "Official ones. They found wreckage of the base camp, some equipment, nothing else. Everyone assumed crevasse. Everyone assumed the mountain ate it."

"Everyone assumed correctly."

"Except your father planned that route himself, Rosalind. And Edmund Sterling did not get eaten by mountains. Mountains got audited by him." He leaned forward. "Someone made that expedition disappear. And someone has spent eleven years making sure nobody went looking properly. Every permit application blocked. Every salvage claim contested. Every academic who expressed interest suddenly funded elsewhere. You know what that pattern looks like."

"I know what paranoia looks like. I've got a decade of field experience."

"Do you?" He opened the folder, turned it, slid a single photocopied page across the table. Her father's handwriting — cramped, slanting left, the handwriting she'd learned her letters underneath, tracing the shapes of his field notes at the kitchen table while he pretended not to watch. *Day 41. Cache secured. Do not log contents. Tell no one until Karachi.* And beneath it, in different ink, pressed hard enough to emboss: a name she didn't recognize and a string of numbers.

Her hands were steady on the table. She made them be steady.

"Forgery," she said.

"Maybe. I've had it analyzed twice. Both labs say the hand is genuine." He paused, letting that land. "You'd know better than any lab."

She looked at the page and felt ten years old and nineteen at once — the kitchen table, the glacier, both. Her father had come home from that expedition exactly once, hollowed out, before the stroke took most of his speech and all of his expeditions. He had never said Eli's name in her presence again. Neither had she. Between them they'd built a silence so complete it had its own weather system, and she'd moved to the other side of the world to live in it.

"What do you actually want, Marcus?"

"A crew. Your crew. You lead, I fund, we go in at the end of monsoon, when the passes open. We recover the survey. We find out what happened to your brother." He said it simply, without flourish, which was how she knew it was the prepared version. "And you get your name back."

"My name isn't lost."

"It's buried. There's a difference, and you of all people—" He caught himself, almost apologized, didn't. "Every contract you've signed for ten years has had your father's name scrubbed off it. Don't look surprised; I've read your filings. You've built a whole career on being a Sterling in everything but signature. This job puts the name back on the map. Both names."

The fan turned. The river counted. She became aware that her bottle was empty and couldn't remember drinking it.

"Why you?" she asked. "Why now? You've got funding, crews, ministries that return your calls. Why do you need me?"

Something flickered across his face — fast, professional, but there. The look of a man whose script had skipped a line.

"Because the route is yours," he said. "Eli mapped half of it before—" He stopped. Started again, smoother. "Because the porters' villages on the approach will work for a Sterling and no one else. Because I need someone the mountain hasn't already beaten once. Take your pick. All true."

"All rehearsed."

"Everything true is rehearsed. That's what makes it true."

She picked up the folder. It was heavier than paper should be — inside, she could feel the density of maps, schedules, a letter of credit. She stood, walked the three steps to the proprietor's counter, where a kerosene lamp burned in a glass case against the mosquitoes, and held the corner of the folder to the flame.

It caught slowly. Wax seal first, bubbling, then the heavy stock curling brown and black. The proprietor watched with the expression of a man who had seen many things burn in his establishment and rated this one unremarkable. She held it until the fire reached her fingers, then dropped the last burning corner into the brass spittoon by the door and watched it finish.

When she turned around, Vane hadn't moved. He was watching her with something she couldn't immediately classify, and then she could: respect. Genuine, appalled, calculating respect. He was revising his model of her, and she understood with sick clarity that the revision made her more valuable, not less.

"Burn the copies too," she said. "All of them. The labs', the surveyor's, whatever's in your safe. That page doesn't exist."

"It exists," Vane said softly. "That's the problem. It exists, and other people know it exists, and those people are not sentimental about paper." He rose, buttoned his jacket, laid something on the table — a second folder, identical weight, identical seal. "You burned my only original. Fortunately I'm a careful man."

"I won't read it."

"You will. Maybe not tonight. Maybe not this month. But the monsoon ends in nine weeks, and when it does, someone is going up that glacier, Rosalind. If it's not you, it's people I can't fully control, and I'm telling you plainly — I have tried to control them, and it costs more than I expected." He put money on the table, far too much, and nodded to the proprietor. "Your father sent me a letter in March. Before the second stroke. One sentence. Want to hear it?"

She said nothing, which he took as yes, because he always did.

"He wrote: *Send Rosalind. She'll know why.*"

The room went very quiet, or she did — one of the two, hard to tell the difference anymore. Vane let it hang for exactly as long as a professional lets things hang, then touched two fingers to his temple, a little salute, and walked out into the heat and the dark and the sound of the river.

She sat back down. The second folder sat where he'd left it, smug as a loaded gun.

She didn't touch it. She finished her warm beer instead, and then, because the alternative was sitting with *she'll know why* echoing in the chamber where her brother used to be, she waved the proprietor over and ordered the local rum, the kind that came in unlabeled bottles and tasted like decisions.

* * *

She was three fingers into the rum when she finally opened the folder, because she was going to, and they both knew it, and pretending otherwise would just waste good rum.

Maps first. Current satellite imagery of the Panmah, overlaid with the old expedition grid in red — her father's grid, the numbering system she'd grown up napping under. Then the logistics: permits already drafted, a letter of credit from a firm called Meridian Holdings that had no internet presence older than four years, a crew manifest with her own people's names already on it. Priya Chandra, engineer. Okonkwo, medic. A runner listed only as *Dex, 17, references pending.*

He'd built the whole expedition around her crew. Around her. Months ago, maybe a year. The realization arrived cold: this wasn't an offer. It was a fait accompli wearing an offer's clothes, and she'd been cast in it before she ever heard her name in that doorway.

At the bottom of the folder, beneath everything, a single photograph. Glossy, recent, taken from a distance with a long lens: a hospital room in Edinburgh. An old man in a bed by the window, thin as a folded chart, holding a pen over a legal pad with a hand that shook. Through the window behind him, grey Scottish light on grey Scottish stone.

On the back, in Vane's neat block capitals: *HE'S RUNNING OUT OF TIME TO TELL YOU WHY.*

Rosalind sat in the unnamed bar with the photograph face-down on the table and the river counting outside, and she understood that she had already lost the argument she'd won by burning the first folder. The question was only whether she went up that mountain as herself or as Vane's instrument — and whether those had ever been, in the whole history of ambitious men and stubborn women, entirely different things.

Nine weeks. The monsoon would end. Someone was going.

She drained the rum, pocketed the photograph, left the folder lying open on the table like a wound she was choosing not to dress, and walked out into the Bangladesh night to start making the phone calls she'd sworn off making — beginning, God help her, with a teenage runner in Chattogram who owed her a favor and owned a boat.

The mountain, she told herself, was just geography.

She almost believed it.
