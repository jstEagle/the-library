## 3. Crew of Strays

Dex answered on the fourth ring, which meant he was awake, and said nothing for a full three seconds, which meant he was deciding how much trouble to pretend not to be in.

"Boss," he finally said. "Weird timing."

"Weird how?"

"I was literally just thinking about you. Like, two minutes ago. I was looking at the tide tables and I thought, I wonder if Sterling's eating anything with a vegetable in it."

"Dex."

"That's a no, then."

"It's four in the morning where you are."

"It's four in the morning everywhere, boss. That's the whole point of four in the morning." A pause, and the background noise behind him shifted — the creak of a hull, water slapping pilings, the particular acoustic of someone standing on a deck at night. "You're calling me at four in the morning about a job."

She leaned on the railing outside her guesthouse room, watching a fishing boat's lights crawl up the channel. "I'm calling to ask if your boat is still seaworthy."

"My boat is beautiful. My boat is a poem." Another pause, longer. "How far?"

"Karakoram."

The silence that came back wasn't teenage anymore. It was the silence of someone doing arithmetic — distance, altitude, season, and underneath it all, the one name nobody on her crew ever said out loud because they'd all been there the year after, when she'd come back from the funeral and started working eighteen-hour days and pretending it was fine.

"The route through the high passes," Dex said carefully.

"Maybe. Depends on the ice."

"Boss."

"You don't have to come."

"I know I don't have to come." She could hear him moving now, pacing his deck the way he paced everything, restless as water. "That's not — okay, look. You know I'm in. You called anyway because you wanted to hear yourself say it out loud first, and that's fine, I get it, but let's skip to the part where you tell me what's actually wrong with this job, because there's something wrong with this job or you wouldn't have burned through Priya and Okonkwo already before getting to me."

She hadn't called them yet. But she wasn't going to tell him that.

"Vane's funding it," she said.

Dex whistled low. "Oh, so it's a *bad* something-wrong. Great. Love that for us."

"He wants my father's last survey. Recovered intact, delivered to him personally, no copies."

"And you said—"

"I said yes."

Another whistle, even lower. "Huh."

"What?"

"Nothing. It's just — you told me once, like two years ago, drunk off half a beer in Kochi, that if Marcus Vane ever offered you a job, the correct response was to check his pockets for your wallet." He let that sit. "So either the money's incredible or it's not about the money."

"It's never about the money, Dex."

"Yeah," he said quietly. "I know. Okay. I can be in Chattogram in two days. Three if the engine part comes in."

"What engine part?"

"The engine part I definitely mentioned and you definitely forgot. Bring gaskets. And boss?" The lights on the fishing boat blinked out, one by one, going dark against the water. "Whatever this actually is — we're coming anyway. You know that, right? You didn't need to call at four in the morning to ask."

She hung up before he could hear whatever her face was doing.

* * *

Priya took eleven hours to answer, which was fast for Priya, who treated communication the way she treated machinery — with suspicion until proven necessary.

Her video feed resolved into a workshop somewhere in Leh, all copper shavings and good light, Priya herself framed from the chin down because she'd never once in six years pointed a camera at her own face.

"Let me guess," she said, before hello. "Something's broken."

"A boat. Possibly a plan."

"Mm." The sound of a wrench being set down with deliberate care. "Which one first?"

"The boat's Dex's problem. The plan is mine." Rosalind sat down on the edge of the bed and gave her the shape of it — Vane, the Karakoram, the survey, the window between monsoon and winter. She left out Eli, left out the glacier, left out the way her hands had shaken burning the contract. Priya would find those things on her own; she always did.

When she finished, Priya was quiet long enough that Rosalind checked whether the call had dropped.

"You understand what this is," Priya said finally.

"Do I?"

"You're the best salvage lead working the margins, and Vane knows it, and he also knows you'd rather eat glass than work for him. So either he's desperate, or he doesn't actually care whether you say yes, because the job happens either way and your name on the manifest is worth more than your competence." A beat. "Or both. Usually both."

"You've been reading contracts again."

"I've been breathing air again. Same thing." Something clanked; she was picking the wrench back up, which meant she'd already decided. "I'll meet you in Skardu. Don't fly commercial, Vane will have eyes on the manifests. I'll come overland with the drilling rig disassembled in crates. Nobody questions a woman shipping machine parts into the mountains."

"Priya."

"Don't thank me. Thank me by not dying. Also — Rosalind." The wrench stopped. "If this goes where I think it goes, near the ice, near that survey — you tell us. Whatever you're carrying. You don't get to do the thing where you eat it alone and we all pretend we didn't watch you choke. That's the price of my rig."

It was the closest Priya had come in six years to saying *I love you*, and they both knew it, and neither of them acknowledged it, which was its own kind of grammar.

"Deal," Rosalind said.

* * *

Okonkwo she found in person, because Okonkwo didn't do phones — claimed they flattened the human voice into something untrustworthy, like a photograph of a meal. He was running a clinic out of a converted shipping container in Gilgit, stitching up porters and trekkers and the occasional government official who'd had too much confidence and not enough rope, and when she walked in he looked up from a splint, took in her face in one professional sweep, and said to his patient:

"You're done. Come back Thursday. Walk slower than you think you need to."

Then, to Rosalind: "Sit. Before you fall down. When did you last sleep more than five hours?"

"I sleep great."

"Liar. Sit."

She sat. He finished cleaning his instruments with the unhurried precision of a man who believed most emergencies were failures of preparation, laid them out in their case, closed it, and only then turned around and gave her his full attention — which was a lot of attention, Okonkwo being built along the lines of a load-bearing wall, with a voice that came up from somewhere below his boots.

"Vane," he said.

"How did you—"

"Because you have the face of a woman who has agreed to something she hates. There are only three men alive who put that face on you, and one of them is dead, one of them is dying, and the other one wears cologne you can smell through a satellite phone." He folded his arms. "Tell me I'm wrong."

"You're not wrong."

He exhaled through his nose, slow, and pulled a second stool over, and for a while neither of them spoke. Outside, the bazaar noise of Gilgit went on — vendors, engines, a muezzin warming up toward evening prayer. Inside the container it smelled of antiseptic and diesel and the dried chilies he kept strung above the door because, he said, a clinic should smell like somewhere people live.

"Eli's route," he said at last. Not a question.

"Maybe. Possibly lower. Depends on the calving schedule."

"Rosalind."

"It depends on the calving schedule, Okonkwo."

He studied her the way he studied wounds — thoroughly, without flinching, with the patience of a man who understood that the worst damage was usually under the intact skin. Ten years ago he'd been the medic on the dig where her brother died. He'd been the one who worked on Eli for forty minutes in the cold. He'd been the one who told her, afterward, kneeling in the scree, holding her shoulders while she made sounds she didn't remember making. If anyone on earth had earned the right to refuse this job, it was him.

"When do we leave?" he said.

She blinked. "You're not going to fight me on it?"

"I fought you on it ten years ago. I lost, and you went anyway, and you came back smaller and I decided I would rather be next to you next time than home wondering." He stood, joints cracking like green wood, and began pulling supplies off shelves with the brisk economy of a man whose yes had been given before the question. "Broad-spectrum antibiotics. Altitude meds. Sutures. Blood-clotting agents, the good ones, which means I need to make some calls I won't enjoy." He glanced back at her. "And Rosalind — if we go up past the ice line, I want a real conversation about turnback times. Written down. Signed. Not the thing you did last time, where the conversation happened and then got quietly ignored."

"This time is different."

"They always say that." But he said it gently, and put a hand briefly on her head as he passed, the way you'd steady something valuable on a shelf. "Eat something. You look like a rumor."

* * *

They converged on Chattogram nine days later, in stages, the way her crews always did — strays answering a whistle only they could hear.

Dex met her at the dock with his boat, the *Maliha*, scrubbed within an inch of her life and smelling of fresh paint over old rust, and behind him, to Rosalind's complete lack of surprise, stood a girl of maybe sixteen with a duffel bag and an expression of ferocious hope.

"No," Rosalind said.

"She's my cousin," Dex said.

"She's a child."

"I'm sixteen," the girl said, "and I can run a river better than he can, and he talks in his sleep, and I know all his passwords, and you need a fourth set of hands because nobody runs a boat like this with three." She stuck out her chin. "Nadia. I fixed his bilge pump in March while he was asleep. Ask him."

"He fixed my bilge pump," Dex admitted, "while I was asleep. Which is technically theft of labor but honestly the best work anyone's done on this boat since I owned it."

Rosalind looked at the girl — at the rope-callused hands, the salt-stained sleeves, the absolute refusal to blink — and felt the familiar vertigo of seeing her own younger self wearing someone else's face. That was the trap of a crew like hers. You collected strays, and then the strays grew roots in you, and then every job became a thing you could lose.

"Rules," she said. "You follow orders the first time. On the water, my word is weather — you don't argue with it, you react to it. You carry your own pack. And if I say a place isn't safe, we don't go, and you don't make the big sad eyes at me, because I invented the big sad eyes and I'm immune."

Nadia's grin split her face open. "Deal."

"God help me," Rosalind muttered, and shook her hand.

* * *

By evening they were all aboard, the *Maliha* riding low with gear, and Rosalind stood at the stern watching Priya and Okonkwo argue cheerfully about stove placement while Dex taught Nadia the current patterns of the Karnaphuli with a stick and a puddle of spilled tea, and she let herself have the moment — the whole warm, ridiculous, improbable moment — of these five people in one place, laughing, loading, alive.

This was the part she was good at. This was the part she'd built out of the wreckage of everything else: a family assembled from leftovers, held together by competence and loyalty and the unspoken agreement that nobody asked anybody about their bad years. Priya with her crates of disassembled secrets. Okonkwo with his signed turnback times and his chili strings. Dex, who at nineteen had stowed away on a Bangladesh dig and turned out to be the best river pilot she'd ever seen, and Nadia, who was going to be better.

*Just another job,* she told herself, watching the sun go down orange over the container cranes. *Up the rivers, into the hills, recover some papers, hand them to a man I despise, come home.*

But her hands, wrapped around the stern rail, remembered the weight of the folder. *Eli Sterling, final survey, 2014.* Remembered the photograph of her father young and grinning in front of a glacier that had killed one son and was now, apparently, the destination.

"You're doing the face again," Dex said, appearing beside her with two cups of tea, handing her one without asking.

"What face?"

"The one where you're smiling at us but you're actually somewhere cold." He sipped his tea. "It's a good crew, boss. Maybe the best one. Whatever's waiting up there — we've walked into worse with less."

"Have we?"

"No," he said cheerfully. "But it sounded supportive."

Below them, Nadia laughed at something Priya said, and Okonkwo's laugh rolled out under it like a bass note, and for one full minute Rosalind stood in the middle of the warmth and let it hold her.

Then she went below, alone, to the chart table, and unlocked the drawer where she'd kept them for ten years without opening them: her brother's maps. His handwriting in the margins. His route, inked in confident blue lines, straight up the valley she'd sworn never to enter again.

She spread them flat under the lamp, and the blue lines waited, patient as scars.

Tomorrow they'd sail upstream, and every mile would take her closer to the ice.

The first map, the oldest one, had a note in the corner she'd never noticed before — small, hurried, pressed hard enough into the paper to read with her fingertips.

*If anything happens to me, ask Dad what he traded.*

She sat very still, the lamp humming, the crew laughing above her, and read it again.
