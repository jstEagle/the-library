## 4. The Sworn-Off Route

The maps came out of the bottom of the duffel, which is where I kept everything I didn't want to look at and couldn't bring myself to throw away.

I'd moved them through eleven countries in ten years — rolled in waxed canvas, wrapped in a spare shirt, buried under rope and ration packs like a body under floorboards. Eli's maps. Hand-drawn, mostly, because my brother never trusted a printed contour he hadn't walked himself. His lettering was cramped and slanted left, the numbers small and exact, and every map had a margin note in pencil that only made sense if you knew him: *water here after noon*, *goat track lies*, *Priya was right about the bridge*.

He'd been twenty-six when he drew the last one.

I unrolled them across the floor of the Gilgit guesthouse at one in the morning, because that was the hour when the decision had to be made and I'd spent three days pretending it didn't. The contract copy Vane had left sat folded in my jacket pocket like a splinter working toward the heart. Priya was asleep two doors down. Okonkwo had gone to bed with the disciplined cheerfulness of a man who already knew what I was going to do and wanted no part of watching me do it. Dex was seventeen and slept like the dead, which is the privilege of the young.

And me. I sat cross-legged on cold tile under a bare bulb and spread out the route that killed my brother.

* * *

You have to understand what the Karakoram does to a survey line. It isn't a mountain range so much as an argument the planet is having with itself, still unresolved, plates shoving and buckling, peaks growing even as they shed themselves into valleys. The glaciers don't sit still. They flow — meters a day, groaning, calving, swallowing and excreting rock until a route you mapped in June is a lie by September. Eli used to say mapping the Karakoram was like photographing a river and calling it a portrait.

His final route sheet ran from Skardu up the Braldu, over the Biafo–Hispar glacial traverse — sixty kilometers of ice, the longest such corridor outside the poles — and then north past a glacier he'd marked only as K-7 in his notes, because naming it felt too much like claiming it. That was where our father's last survey terminated. That was where the documents were cached. And that was where, ten years ago this coming August, the ice decided it was done holding.

I traced the line with one finger and did not let myself stop at the place I knew was coming, the way you don't let yourself look down when you're crossing a ledge. But the finger stopped anyway. Fingers are honest.

There was a small x in blue ink, and beside it, in Eli's hand: *bad serac zone — go early, go fast.*

We had not gone early.

* * *

Ten years ago. You want the flashback? Fine. Here it is, the way it always comes back — not in order, but in weight.

It comes back first as sound: the crack, deep and wet, like the mountain clearing its throat, and then the long white roar of four hundred tons of ice letting go of the wall above us. It comes back as cold — not the clean cold of altitude but the specific cold of spray off a collapsing serac, hitting your face like thrown gravel made of winter. It comes back as my father's voice, shouting coordinates into the wind while the world came apart, because the man could not stop measuring even while it killed his son.

We were three days behind schedule. Weather window closing. My father wanted one more reading from the upper station — one more set of numbers for a survey I would later learn was never really about topography at all — and Eli said fine, we'll go at first light, before the sun warms the ice. First light. We were roped and moving by six. By nine the sun had been on the face for two hours, and the serac field above the route was ticking and settling like a house in a fire, and I remember saying — I have spent ten years not remembering whether I said it, and remembering that I said it — "Dad, this is wrong."

He said, "Twenty minutes."

Eli laughed. He laughed the way he did everything, easily, like the world was fundamentally on his side. He turned around to say something to me — I will never know what, because his mouth opened and the mountain answered instead — and the serac went, and the world went white, and when the white cleared there was a hole in the glacier where my brother had been standing, and my father was staring at it with his instruments still in his hands.

They never recovered him. Glaciers keep what they take. Somewhere under thirty meters of moving ice there is a young man with a laugh like summer, drifting downhill a few centimeters a day, and some part of me has spent a decade calculating where he might emerge, as if the ice might someday give him back and give me back the sentence he never finished.

The inquiry called it bad luck. A freak release, an unstable pocket, nothing anyone could have predicted. My father signed the report with a steady hand. I signed it with mine. Then I took my name off every contract that followed, and I stopped going home, and I told myself that distance was freedom, and I almost believed it.

What I didn't know then — what Teodor's existence on that flooded river had begun to whisper, what the sealed crates in the hold were starting to shout — was that the accident had a shape. That luck doesn't leave margin notes. That my father had been carrying something on that ice worth more than readings, and that men who pay for expeditions the way Vane's backers pay do not accept *lost in a tragic accident* as an ending. They send people to check.

But that night in Gilgit I didn't know any of that yet. That night all I had was ink and memory.

* * *

I sat with the maps until the bulb buzzed and my legs went numb and the call to prayer came faintly up from the valley, threading through the generator hum. I should have burned them years ago. I should have burned them the way I'd burned Vane's contract — a clean act, a door closed. Instead I'd carried them like a pilgrim carries a relic of a saint who never performed a miracle, just died.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't surface all at once. It surfaces like a body in water. It goes down and stays down and you build your whole life on the assumption of its absence, and then one day you're doing something ordinary — unrolling a map, tying a knot, hearing someone laugh in another room with the same pitch — and it comes up beneath you without warning, arms out, mouth open, and for a moment you're standing on solid tile and drowning at the same time.

Dex found me like that. Of course he did. Seventeen-year-olds move quietly and notice everything; it's their one advantage over adults.

He stood in the doorway in his hoodie, hair flattened on one side, and looked at the maps, and looked at me, and didn't ask. He crossed the room, sat down against the wall next to the maps like it was the most natural thing in the world, and said, "Your brother drew these?"

"Yeah."

"He was good."

"He was better than good." My voice came out rough. I let it. "He was the best of us. Which, given the competition, wasn't a high bar, but still."

Dex studied the route line, the little x, the penciled warning. He was smart enough to read a route sheet and smart enough to read me, and I watched him decide, deliberately, to be kind about it. "Is this where we're going?"

"That's the route."

"And that—" he tapped near the x, careful not to touch it— "is where?"

"Where we're not stopping."

He nodded slowly. Then: "Okonkwo says you've been deciding for three days." A pause. "He says it like it's a medical condition."

A laugh got out of me before I could stop it, short and cracked, and it broke something loose — not the grief, the grief stays, the grief is load-bearing now — but something adjacent to it. I looked down at Eli's cramped left-slanting letters. *Go early, go fast.* Ten years of refusing this route, and Vane had known exactly which key to press. Not money. Not glory. Just the simple arithmetic: if I don't go, someone worse will, and they'll walk over my brother's grave with a shovel.

"Here's what happens," I told Dex, because he was awake and someone should hear it said out loud. "We go upstream tomorrow. River routes to Skardu, then the Braldu. We take the job, we find the cache, and we get out before anyone decides we know too much. Nobody stops at the x. Nobody gets sentimental about ice."

"You okay?" he asked. Seventeen-year-olds have no tact and infinite mercy, sometimes in the same sentence.

"No," I said. "But I'm going anyway. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole job."

He considered that with the seriousness he gave everything, then reached over and straightened one corner of the map that had curled. "Then I'm glad it's your name on the contract," he said, and went back to bed.

* * *

I rolled the maps last. Slowly, along Eli's creases, the way you fold a flag.

One thing I hadn't noticed in ten years of not looking: a second notation, faint, in different graphite, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper. Below the x, below *go early, go fast*, someone had written — my father, it had to be my father, the capitals were his — a single line:

*THERE IS MORE ON THIS ICE THAN WATER.*

I sat very still for a long time.

Then I put the maps in the duffel, on top this time, where I could reach them, and went to knock on Priya's door, because if we were doing this, we were doing it right, and I needed her eyes on boat manifests before dawn.

Upstream at first light. Toward the mountains, toward the cache, toward the x.

Toward whatever my father had left on the ice besides my brother.
