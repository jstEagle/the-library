## 5. Upstream

The river out of Chittagong ran the color of strong tea, and by noon it had taught us our first lesson: nothing about this trip was going to be clean.

I stood at the bow of the *Mekhala*, our chartered cargo boat, watching the banks unspool — rice paddies drowning in their own green, tin roofs catching light like scattered coins, kids waving from a ferry crossing with the easy joy of people who didn't know what we were carrying. Behind me, the deck was stacked with crates and cases and the particular chaos of four people who'd worked together long enough to stop apologizing for their mess.

"Rosalind." Priya's voice came from somewhere under the tarp. "You want to explain why we have forty kilos of drilling compound?"

"We don't have forty kilos of drilling compound."

"We have forty kilos of drilling compound." She emerged, pushing her goggles up into her hair, holding an inventory clipboard like a weapon. "Manifest says survey equipment. Survey equipment does not include shaped charges. I counted."

Vane's logistics team had loaded the boat in Karachi. I hadn't checked it. That was on me.

"Maybe he anticipated ice," I said.

"Ice you core, you don't blast. Not on a scientific survey." She let that sit between us the way she let everything sit — patient, precise, refusing to be rushed off her own conclusion. "Unless it's not a scientific survey."

"It's a salvage job."

"Salvage jobs I've done with you for six years. This one has a client who won't give us his funding source, manifests that lie, and a route nobody's run since your brother—" She stopped herself. Even Priya, who could strip an engine blindfolded in the dark, knew which wires not to touch.

"Eli's route is the only viable one," I said. "That's all it is."

"That's never all anything is with you."

She went back under the tarp. I stayed at the bow and watched the water carry us north, and told myself the cold feeling in my stomach was just the wind coming down off the hills.

* * *

We made the confluence at dusk, where the braided channels gathered themselves into something deep enough for the big boat, and Okonkwo cooked.

This was the ritual that held us together, more than any contract: wherever we were — a dig site in the Atacama, a rusting freighter off Mombasa, now this deck crawling with river moths — Okonkwo cooked at the end of the day, and we ate together, and for forty minutes we were not employees or orphans or people running from things. We were a table.

He'd claimed the stern as his kitchen, a two-burner rig wedged between fuel drums, and tonight it was rice and river fish and a peanut sauce he refused to write down because, he said, recipes were how food stopped being love. Dex sat cross-legged on a crate, seventeen years old and all elbows, eating like the meal might be confiscated.

"Dex," I said. "Satellite check."

"Already did." He fished out the battered handset he treated better than his own teeth. "Weather window's closing. Monsoon's early this year — like, rude-early. Every pass above three thousand meters goes white inside ten days, maybe less."

"Ten days puts us at the glacier mouth?"

"If the river cooperates." He turned the screen toward me. The satellite image showed the Karakoram wrapped in cloud like a fist closing. "If it doesn't cooperate, we're stuck in the gorges until September."

Priya looked up from her plate. "And Vane knew the schedule when he booked this."

"He's a gambler," I said.

"He's a man who read the same forecasts we're reading and booked anyway." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light. "There's a difference."

Okonkwo ladled more sauce onto my plate without asking, which was his way of voting in whatever argument was happening. "Eat," he said. "The mountains will still be suspicious tomorrow."

Dex laughed. Priya almost did. And I ate, and the fish was good, and the river talked to itself in the dark, and underneath all of it ran the thought I couldn't drown: Vane had planned this route, this season, this boat, down to the last crate. Men like Marcus Vane didn't gamble. They arranged.

* * *

I found the second thing wrong at midnight, and it wasn't mine to find.

I'd gone forward to check the anchor line — old habit, bad sleep, the river slapping the hull in a rhythm just off from a heartbeat — and found Priya already there, crouched over the forward hold with a flashlight clamped in her teeth, a pry bar in her hand.

"You're up," she said around the light.

"You're breaking into our own cargo."

"I'm auditing our own cargo. There's a distinction, and it matters legally." She worked the pry bar under the lid of the last crate, the one stenciled GEOLAB SYSTEMS — KARACHI in fresh paint over older, clumsier lettering. Fresh paint over old stencils. Somebody had relabeled these.

The lid came up with a groan. Inside: foam packing, cut with professional care, cradling six metal canisters the size of thermoses, each one banded with a serial plate and a hazard diamond I recognized from a bad year in the Svalbard archives.

"Cesium sources," Priya said quietly. "Radiographic. Shielded, but not shielded well enough to be legal to move like this. No import papers, no transport manifest, no radiation officer assigned." She sat back on her heels. "Rosalind, this isn't a survey kit. This is somebody's dirty little portable lab, and we're the mule."

I looked at the canisters and felt the trip tilt beneath me, the way a boat tilts when ballast shifts. "Could be legitimate industrial gauges. Some surveys use—"

"For what? Ice density? You use sonar and cores. Nobody ships cesium upriver in mislabeled crates unless they don't want anyone asking questions." She snapped the flashlight off, plunging us into the dark and the moth-thick air. "And here's the part I keep chewing on. These were loaded in Karachi. Vane's people. But the stencil underneath — I got a look before the paint. It said Peshawar."

Peshawar. The other end of the Karakoram. Someone had moved this equipment across the country to load it onto a boat going back the way it came, wearing a disguise.

"Maybe it's not connected to us," I said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"Everything on this boat is connected to us. We're standing on it." She set the lid back down gently, aligning it so no one would know it had been opened. "Question is what we do. Report it, dump it, or play dumb and find out who it belongs to."

Playing dumb was the salvage crew's oldest skill. We'd spent a decade making ourselves small and deniable — no famous names on contracts, no paper trails, no questions at borders. It had kept us alive. It had also, I understood suddenly, made us exactly the kind of people men like Vane liked to hire: good at their work, hungry for redemption, and trained never to look too hard at the money.

"We play smart," I said. "We document everything. Serial numbers, photos, dates. If this goes sideways, we're witnesses, not accomplices."

"And if it goes sideways before we can witness anything?"

"Then we're fast."

She huffed — half laugh, half disgust. "Six years, and you still think speed is a plan."

"It's the only plan that's ever loved me back."

She left. I stayed crouched in the hold a while longer, listening to the river, doing arithmetic I didn't like. Forty kilos of explosive. Six radiation sources. A client who'd timed the expedition against the monsoon. A route chosen not because it was best but because it was Eli's — because I would follow it anywhere, even now, even after ten years, and Vane knew that. Vane had always known that.

I'd taken this job because refusing meant someone worse would go. Standing in that hold, I began to wonder if that was true, or if someone worse had already gone — and we were the respectable face riding along behind them.

* * *

Morning brought fog, and the fog brought the gorge.

The river narrowed into walls of gray stone hung with vines, water rising brown and muscular around us, and the crew fell into the wordless choreography of tight water — Dex on depth soundings, Priya on the engine, Okonkwo aft keeping the weight balanced, me calling the channel from the bow. For a few hours there was no room in my head for anything but the next bend, the next rock, the next hundred meters of river trying to kill us politely.

It was almost restful. Danger always had been, for me. That was its own confession, and I tried not to hear it.

We cleared the worst narrows by late afternoon and tied up at a ledge village called Rakchham — six houses, a shrine, a family that sold diesel and eggs and asked no questions, which was why boats like ours stopped there. While Okonkwo negotiated eggs through a mixture of Urdu, gesture, and charm, I climbed to the shrine to get signal.

One bar. Enough for text.

There was a message waiting. Not from Vane — Vane communicated through contracts and intermediaries, never directly, another thing I should have weighed harder. From a number I didn't know:

*Ms. Sterling. Your father asks after you. He hasn't much time, and less patience. — R., the nurse.*

I stared at it until the screen dimmed. Ten days upstream from anywhere, holding a boat full of lies, and the past had found the one bar of signal in the whole gorge to reach me.

I didn't answer. I put the phone away and stood looking down at the river, at my boat, at my strange little family loading eggs and laughing about something, and I made the deal I always made: later. Later I'd call. Later I'd decide about the crates. Later I'd ask Vane the questions whose answers I was afraid of.

Later was the country I'd lived in for ten years. It had excellent views and no address.

Below me, Dex shouted something up — a boat, he said, a small boat coming up the gorge against the current, which nobody did, which made no sense, engine sputtering, one man aboard, listing hard to starboard and taking water.

"Looks like he's been out there a while," Dex called. "What do we do?"

I looked at the stranger's boat fighting the current, and then at our holds full of secrets, and every instinct I owned said leave it alone, keep moving, don't pick up strays on a job that's already bleeding.

But Okonkwo was already untying the throw line, because that was who we were when I wasn't looking.

"Get him aboard," I said.
