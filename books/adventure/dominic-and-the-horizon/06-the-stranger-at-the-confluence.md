## 6. The Stranger at the Confluence

The confluence came up on them the way bad news does — all at once, and too late to turn around.

Rosalind saw it first from the wheelhouse: two rivers meeting in a brown argument, the smaller one carrying silt down from the high country in ropes and ribbons, the bigger one swollen fat with monsoon runoff. Where they collided, the water stood up in ridges and whorls, a standing wave field that looked like something breathing under a blanket. And snagged in the worst of it, wedged against a half-drowned boulder garden, was a boat.

Not a good boat. A long, low river skiff with a canvas canopy gone gray with mildew, listing hard to port, its stern quarter stove in as if something had taken a bite out of it. A man crouched on the bow like a heron, bailing with what looked like a cooking pot.

"Dex," Rosalind said. "Tell me you see it."

"I see it." Dex was already on the rail, binoculars up, sixteen years old and wired on instant coffee since dawn. "One guy. Alive, I think. He's — okay, he's waving. No, wait. He's *not* waving. He's pointing."

"Pointing where?"

"Downstream of him." Dex lowered the glasses slowly. "There's a kid's jacket caught on the rocks. Just the jacket."

Rosalind took the wheel over from Priya without asking. "Okonkwo. Lines and throw bags. Priya, I need you on the engine, ready to kill it on my mark. We're going to thread the gap on the left of the boulder field and come alongside."

"That gap is maybe two meters wide," Priya said.

"Then don't hit the edges."

"Captain," Okonkwo said mildly, from the deck, "I would like to note for the record that I am a medical doctor and this is not medicine."

"Noted. Go get wet anyway."

* * *

It took forty minutes and cost them a fender, a length of railing, and most of Rosalind's composure. The stranger's skiff was pinned by the current; they couldn't pull it free, so they pulled the man instead. He came across the gap between boats on a line Okonkwo threw, hitting their deck in a controlled sprawl that told her, before anything else did, that he'd done this before. That he knew how to fall.

He was somewhere past fifty, lean in the way of men who eat when there's food and work regardless. Gray stubble, sun-cracked lips, a canvas jacket that had been good twenty years ago. His hands were wrecked — rope burns, split knuckles, the nails of a man who digs. He stood up out of his landing crouch, took stock of the four of them with one slow sweep, and said, in accented but precise English:

"Thank you. There was a boy. Eleven, maybe. From the village upstream. He went in an hour before I did. I couldn't reach him."

"Show me where," Rosalind said.

They found the body two kilometers down, hung up in a willow that the flood had bent nearly flat. Okonkwo brought him ashore and did what could be done, which was nothing, and then more than nothing — because he stayed with the boy until a fishing family came down the bank, and he spoke to them through the stranger, who translated into a dialect Rosalind didn't recognize, low and fluent and unhurried, and the family's shoulders came down from around their ears by the end of it. Whatever he said, it was the right thing. It usually takes a lifetime to learn how to say the right thing to a mother.

When they got back aboard, the stranger was sitting on their deck crate with his ruined hands wrapped in a towel Okonkwo had given him, waiting to be dealt with. Not anxious. Waiting the way cargo waits.

"You'll want to put me off at the next town," he said.

"The next town is underwater," Rosalind said. "You'll ride to Skardu junction with us. Then we'll see."

"My name is Teodor."

"I didn't ask yet."

"No," he agreed. "But you were going to."

Dex snorted from the rail. Priya, who hadn't stopped watching the man since he'd come aboard, said nothing at all, which from Priya was a paragraph.

* * *

They gave him the corner of the main cabin behind the chart table, which was the closest thing their boat had to a guest room, and Rosalind spent the evening finding reasons to walk past it.

She told herself it was security. The man had appeared out of a flooded gorge in the exact stretch of river Vane's itinerary had flagged as a resupply point, alone, with a damaged boat and no visible supplies except a dry bag that never seemed to leave his shoulder. In her experience, coincidence was just logistics you hadn't audited yet.

So she audited.

His skiff, before they let it go to the river, had been registered out of Gilgit — she'd checked the faded registration plate herself. Solo travel, upstream, in monsoon season, toward the Karakoram. Nobody goes up there in July unless they're paid to or running from something, and Teodor moved like neither. He moved like a man on schedule.

At dinner — rice, lentils, the last of the good tea — he ate quietly and answered questions with the economy of someone rationing words.

"What were you doing up here?" Rosalind asked.

"Surveying."

"For whom?"

"A project that no longer exists."

"That's not an answer."

"It's a true one." He looked at her then, directly, and his eyes were pale gray and entirely unbothered. "Your employer will have told you not to discuss the expedition with strangers. I extend you the same courtesy."

"He's not my employer. And you speak Shina." She'd heard him with the fisher family. "And Balti — I heard you switch when the grandmother came down. And your Urdu has a Hunza accent, if I'm guessing."

"You guess well."

"Nobody speaks four mountain languages for fun."

"No," Teodor said. "Usually for a salary." He refolded his towel over his hands. "Ask your question properly and I may answer it."

Priya set down her spoon. Dex leaned forward. Okonkwo, at the end of the bench, went very still in the way of a man pretending to be interested in his food.

Rosalind held the stranger's gaze. "What do you know about Edmund Sterling?"

The name landed on the table like a dropped tool.

Teodor didn't flinch, didn't glance away, didn't perform surprise. What he did was worse. He nodded slightly, as if a hypothesis had been confirmed.

"I worked for him," he said. "Two years. Field assistant on the last three surveys. I can read his handwriting better than he could — he wrote left-handed after the stroke in '09 and never trusted it, so he abbreviated everything, and half his abbreviations exist nowhere else on earth." He paused. "You're his daughter. You have his chin and none of his patience."

The cabin got very quiet. Dex looked between them like a spectator at a tennis match played with knives.

"You say that like you know me," Rosalind said.

"I know *of* you. Your father kept a photograph of you in his survey kit. You, age maybe nineteen, on a dock somewhere, laughing at whoever held the camera." Teodor's voice didn't change at all. "He looked at it more than he looked at the maps. I used to think that was sentiment. Later I understood it was penance."

"My father doesn't do penance."

"No," Teodor agreed. "He does restitution. It looks similar from a distance. Up close it's colder." He rose, carefully, favoring his burned hands. "Thank you for the meal. Thank you for pulling me out. If you put me off at Skardu junction, I'll ask no more of you."

He went aft to his corner. The three of them sat looking at each other over the cold dregs of the tea.

"Well," said Okonkwo.

"He knows too much," Priya said flatly. "Or he's lying about all of it. Either way—"

"Either way he's the first person on this river who's said my father's name out loud," Rosalind said, "and he did it without flinching. That's either a trap or a door."

"And you can't tell which?"

"No," Rosalind said. "That's what makes it interesting."

* * *

She found him an hour later, awake, sitting in the open stern doorway watching the rain come down on the black water. She sat down across from him without being invited, which she noted, and he accepted, which she also noted.

"Prove it," she said.

"Prove what?"

"That you worked for him. Anyone can claim it. Show me something only his assistant would know."

Teodor considered her for a long moment. Rain drummed on the canopy overhead. Somewhere forward, Dex was supposed to be asleep and was very obviously not.

"He keeps a ledger," Teodor said finally. "Not a financial one. A debt ledger. Every favor anyone ever did him, written down, dated, with a column for whether it's been repaid. He started it the year your brother died." He watched her face as he said it, and she hated him a little for watching, and hated herself more for not looking away. "There are only three entries marked repaid in full. One of them says *R.S., 2014, she carried me off the ice and never billed me for it.*"

The river ran. The rain fell. Rosalind discovered that her hands had closed into fists in her lap without consulting her.

"Eli carried him," she said. Her voice came out level, which cost her. "Off the glacier. And then went back for the instruments."

"Yes."

"You weren't there."

"No. I joined the year after. But he talked about it. Once. For about four minutes, on a ridge above Shimshal, and then never again." Teodor turned to look at her fully. "I'm not here to sell you comfort, Ms. Sterling. I'm here because your father sent a final survey out of the Karakoram eleven months ago and it never arrived, and I'm the only living person who knows both the route and his shorthand well enough to reconstruct what was in it. I've been trying to reach it since spring. The river disagreed."

"Vane has the survey contract."

"I know."

"Vane hired *my crew.*"

"I know that too." Something moved behind the gray eyes — not quite sympathy. Recognition, maybe. "Which tells me either Marcus Vane has developed a sudden interest in cartography, or he wants that survey found by someone expendable and credible. You're both. I'm sorry. I imagine you'd already worked it out."

She had. Mostly. Hearing it in a stranger's flat, kind voice made it solid in a way her own suspicions never managed.

"Why tell me?" she said. "You could sit on this. Let us walk you right into whatever Vane's building."

"Because you pulled a drowning man out of a flood and then went looking for a dead child's body so his mother wouldn't have to." Teodor shrugged, small, almost embarrassed. "I've spent two years deciding who to trust with this. You've been deciding for ten minutes. I liked your odds."

"That's not trust. That's a bet."

"In my experience they're the same animal. Only the stakes differ." He pulled the towel tighter around his hands. "Get some sleep, Ms. Sterling. Tomorrow the river turns north, and after that it stops being a river."

Rosalind stood, and paused at the doorway, rain misting cold against her neck.

"The photograph," she said. "On the dock. Was it Karachi?"

Teodor looked up at her, and for the first time since the confluence, something in his face shifted — warmed, barely, like ice going to water at the exact freezing point.

"Gwadar," he said. "He always said Gwadar. He said it was the only place he ever saw you happy."

She went forward through the dark boat, past Dex's feigned sleep, past Priya's genuinely sleepless lamp, and stood a while at the bow window watching the water run north.

In the morning, when she came out with the tea, Teodor's dry bag was open on the chart table while he searched it — and Priya was standing over a folder of papers that had spilled from it, pages dense with cramped left-handed notation, abbreviations existing nowhere else on earth.

Half of them, Rosalind realized with a cold drop in her stomach, she recognized.

They were Eli's.
