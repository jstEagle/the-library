## 7. Monsoon Lock-In

The rain began an hour after they passed the last village with a name, and it did not stop.

Rosalind had worked monsoons before. You learned to read them the way sailors read weather — the green bruise on the horizon, the way the birds went quiet, the smell of wet stone rising off the gorge walls before the first drops hit. You learned that a monsoon was not weather at all but a condition, a season with a temperament, and that the only correct response was to get out from under it.

They had not gotten out from under it.

"We should have been through the gorge by now," Priya said, standing under the wheelhouse awning with her tablet wrapped in plastic and her expression wrapped in something harder. She had to raise her voice over the drumming on the canvas. "The window closed three days ago. The passes are shut."

"The window didn't close three days ago." Rosalind kept her eyes on the water. "It closed this morning. The forecast said we had a week."

"The forecast said we had a week." Priya let that sit. "Whose forecast?"

Rosalind didn't answer, because the answer was the same answer to everything on this trip: the itinerary Vane's office had sent, the river permits, the porter contracts, the fuel caches marked on maps she hadn't drawn. Every document on this expedition had passed through Vane's hands before it passed through hers. She had told herself it was efficiency. Vane was meticulous; Vane had always been meticulous; meticulous was not the same as honest but it was adjacent, and adjacent was enough when you were tired and the money cleared.

The gorge walls rose on either side of them, black basalt streaked with waterfalls that hadn't existed a week ago. The river had come up four meters in two days. It ran the color of strong tea, carrying whole trees sideways, and their boat — a shallow-draft salvage barge she'd run the Mekong in, the Chindwin, the rivers of her whole scattered decade — sat low in the water in a way she did not like.

She went below.

* * *

The hold smelled of diesel and wet canvas and, underneath it, the sweet organic rot of things soaking when they shouldn't be. She found the water before she found the source: six centimeters sloshing across the deck plates, rising around the spare fuel drums, cold enough that it had to be coming through the hull rather than over the gunwales.

"Okonkwo," she called up the ladder. "Bring the light."

He came down with the good lamp and held it where she pointed, and together they traced the leak to a seam aft of the bilge pump, a weeping line of rust-bright water where the plate had flexed. Not a catastrophic failure. Not yet. But the pump was rated for seepage, not for a river trying to climb into the boat, and the river was winning by inches.

"When did this start?" she asked.

"Couldn't say. I checked the bilge yesterday evening — dry."

"Yesterday evening the river was two meters lower and we weren't slamming through standing waves." She crouched, pressed her palm flat against the hull plate. The metal was cold and trembling with the engine's labor. "We've been working her too hard. She wasn't built for this water."

"Nothing's built for this water," Okonkwo said mildly. He was a large, unhurried man who had pulled bullets out of people in three countries and splinters out of Dex with the same expression of professional patience. "The question is whether we stop and wait it out, or keep going and fix her while she runs."

"Stopping means tying off to the gorge wall, and there's nowhere to tie off that isn't underwater or about to be."

"Then we keep going and fix her while she runs."

Rosalind looked at the water level. Six centimeters. She thought about the pump's capacity, the fuel they were burning fighting the current, the four hundred kilometers of gorge between them and the high valley where the survey cache waited. She thought about the word *window* and how Vane had used it — *the glacier opens the route for a few days each year, Rosalind, and this is the year, I've done the arithmetic* — and how she had checked his arithmetic and found it sound, because Vane's arithmetic was always sound, that had never been the problem.

The problem, she was beginning to understand, was what he'd been counting.

* * *

They ran the river all day, or the river ran them; it was getting hard to tell the difference. Dex took the wheel for the middle stretch because the kid steered like he'd been born on brown water, all instinct and no fear, and Rosalind sat in the wheelhouse with the charts spread out and did the thing she'd been avoiding since the rain started.

She did the arithmetic herself.

The monsoon had come early — that was the official line, the radio chatter, the village headman shrugging at the sky. Early rains happened. But she pulled the weather logs Priya had been keeping since Karachi and laid them against the dates in Vane's itinerary, and the itinerary had been finalized in March, and the March forecast — the one Vane's own meteorologist had supplied — showed the front arriving on schedule. Mid-July. A week of margin.

The front had arrived eleven days early.

Nobody's forecast was that wrong. Not Vane's meteorologist's, not the regional models, not the village elders who had read these skies their whole lives. Eleven days was not an error. Eleven days was a decision.

She sat with the charts and the logs and the rain hammering the wheelhouse roof like a fist, and she made herself say it plainly, in her own head, in her own voice: *He sent us in late. He moved the departure twice — once for permits, once for a crew delay that never got explained. He burned our margin and called it bad luck. He put us in this gorge with the water rising and the passes closing, and he did it on purpose.*

But purpose toward what? A cleanup operation needed the evidence gone, not the people carrying it stranded on a leaking boat in a flooded gorge. Unless stranding was the point. Unless the plan was for the river to do what rivers do to expeditions that go missing in monsoon season — swallow them, scatter them, turn them into a paragraph in someone else's memoir. *The crew was lost in the floods of '19. A tragedy. She should never have taken them upriver in that season.*

And who would they blame? The woman who led them. The woman who'd read the same forecasts, signed the same itinerary, taken the same money.

"Oh, Marcus," she said quietly, to no one, to the rain. "You absolute bastard."

The wheelhouse curtain moved. Dex stood there with two chipped enamel mugs of tea, hair plastered flat, sixteen years old and watching her with the quick read-the-room intelligence that had made him worth stealing away from the Karachi docks. "You've got your murder face on," he said.

"I don't have a murder face."

"You've got three faces. Money face, tired face, and the one you're wearing, which is the one before the murder face." He set a mug by her elbow. "Whose murder?"

"Nobody's. Drink your tea."

He drank his tea, perched on the chart table with his sneakers dripping, and after a while he said, "The boat's leaking. I heard Okonkwo and Priya talking. Are we going to sink?"

"No."

"You're sure?"

"I've been on boats that were sinking, Dex. This isn't one of them. This is a boat that's annoyed with me, which I deserve, because I pushed her too hard." She turned the mug in her hands. "But I'm going to ask you to do something, and I need you to do it without making it a thing."

"Okay."

"Keep your eyes open. The crates in the forward hold — the sealed ones, the ones we were told not to inventory. I want to know if anyone opens them. I want to know if anything on this boat moves that we didn't put there."

Dex's face did something careful. "You think someone's on the boat?"

"I think this expedition has more hands on it than the ones I hired. I don't know whose they are yet. That's all. Eyes open, mouth shut."

"Eyes open, mouth shut," he repeated, and slid out into the rain, and Rosalind looked at the charts again and saw them differently now — not a route but a trap with the exits marked in someone else's handwriting.

* * *

They tied off at dusk, or what passed for dusk, the sky so low and gray that day and evening had merged into one long bruised hour. There was a shelf of rock just above the new waterline where an old landslide had left a shelf of shattered stone, and Priya ran lines to it with the competence of a woman who had moored boats in worse, and for the first time in thirty hours the engine went silent, and the silence was almost worse.

The gorge at night, in monsoon, was a sound you felt in your ribs. Water falling somewhere above them in the dark, everywhere at once. The river grinding past at the hull. Rock settling. The boat's timbers and plates ticking as they cooled, as the water pressed at them, as the whole structure quietly negotiated with the fact of its own submersion.

Rosalind did the pump rounds every hour. On the third round the bilge was at nine centimeters, and the pump was running, and the math was no longer comfortable. She stood in the hold with the lamp and did the math anyway, because she was who she was: at this rate, the pump losing ground, they had perhaps thirty hours before the engines took water. Thirty hours of river, or thirty hours of sitting still while the river rose around them. Neither was a plan. Both were better than panicking, and she filed the numbers away and climbed the ladder with her face arranged into the no-murder face.

Teodor was on deck.

He sat under the awning with his back against the wheelhouse, a thermos cup in his hands, watching the black water. He'd been with them eleven days now — pulled off a half-sunk skiff at the confluence, quiet as furniture ever since, fixing things before anyone asked, speaking the gorge dialects with the porters like a man reading his own mail. Rosalind still didn't know what to do with him. He asked for nothing. He noticed everything. And twice now, when she'd mentioned her father's survey work, he'd said something small and precise that no stranger should have known — the way Elias Sterling numbered his field pages, the coffee stain on the 1974 Karakoram folio that everyone assumed was a map symbol.

"Your bilge," he said, without looking up. "Nine centimeters?"

Her spine went cold. "You've been counting."

"I count everything. It's how I stay alive." He gestured at the deck beside him. She sat, because the alternative was standing in the rain pretending she hadn't heard, and because — she was honest enough to admit it — she wanted to know what he knew, and this was the first hour since the confluence that he'd seemed to be offering.

"The pump's losing," she said. "Thirty hours, give or take, if we sit still. Less if we run."

"If we run, the flexing widens the seam. I looked at it this afternoon." He turned the cup in his hands. "You're wondering about the dates."

She kept her voice level. "What dates?"

"The departure. The two delays. The forecast that was wrong by eleven days." He looked at her then, and in the low light his face was unreadable in the way of people who had practiced unreadability for a long time. "You've already done the arithmetic. You did it this afternoon in the wheelhouse. I could see it through the curtain."

"You watch me a lot, Teodor."

"I watch everyone. It's how I stay alive," he said again, and there was something under the flatness of it this time, something that sounded almost like an apology. "You want to know if Vane did this on purpose."

"Say what you came here to say."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere upstream a slope let go — a deep, wet, geological sigh, stone and water moving together — and the river's note changed pitch, and they both listened to it the way sailors listen, cataloguing, assessing, filing.

"Your father," Teodor said, "kept a weather log. Forty years of field seasons. He used to say that weather was the only honest collaborator — it never flattered you, it never took credit, it just arrived." He set the cup down. "The front that hit this gorge came eleven days early. But it didn't come early everywhere. It came early *here*. There's a satellite pass record — commercial imagery, the kind Vane's office buys by the bundle. If you pull it, you'll see the front sitting over this drainage for six days before it broke. Stationary. Like it was waiting for us."

Rosalind stared at him. "Weather doesn't wait."

"No. But forecasts can be *made* to wait. There are ways to shape a regional model if you control the inputs, and Vane's office controls the inputs for this corridor — they bought the meteorological cooperative in Gilgit two years ago. It's in the filings. Nobody reads the filings." He said it the way he said everything, quietly, as if reporting the position of a sandbar. "I'm not telling you the monsoon was manufactured. I'm telling you the monsoon was *expected*, and the itinerary was built to put this boat in this gorge when it arrived, and the crew manifest was built so that the only person who'd notice would be too busy bailing to say so."

The cold in her spine had spread, settled, become a kind of clarity. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because the water's at nine centimeters," Teodor said, "and in thirty hours it'll be at the engines, and after that it won't matter what any of us know. I've been waiting for the right hour to tell you, and I think this is it. I think we're out of hours."

"Tell me what?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Rain ran off the awning in a beaded curtain between them and the dark. When he spoke again, his voice was lower, stripped of its careful neutrality, and it was the voice of a much younger man, a tired one.

"I was your father's last assistant, Rosalind. The final survey — the one Vane sent us to find — I've seen it. I helped pack it. And it isn't a survey. It's a record. Your father documented something being taken out of that valley that should never have left it, and he documented who paid for it to be taken, and the people who paid have spent ten years making sure that record stays lost." He looked at her, and the rain ran down his face and he didn't wipe it away. "Vane isn't trying to recover the survey. He's trying to be the last man standing near it when the river closes over this boat. And you — you were never hired to lead this expedition. You were hired to be the name on the manifest when it goes down."

The bilge pump, below them, chugged and faltered and chugged again, keeping its losing time.

Rosalind sat very still in the rain, in the gorge, in the trap, and found — to her own surprise — that the fear in her chest had a familiar shape. It was the shape of ten years ago, a glacier, a radio call that never finished. She had sworn off this route, this work, this whole category of trusting men with sound arithmetic. And she had come anyway. And the water was rising.

"Start from the beginning," she said. "And talk fast."
