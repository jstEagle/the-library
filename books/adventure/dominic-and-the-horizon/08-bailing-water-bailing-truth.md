## 8. Bailing Water, Bailing Truth

The bilge pump died at four in the morning.

Rosalind knew it not by sound but by silence — the small mechanical heartbeat that had kept the *Kestrel's* stern dry for six days simply stopped, and in its place came water: a soft, insistent slosh against the hull plates, like something patient knocking.

She was up before Priya's alarm could go off. Bare feet on wet decking. The hold was ankle-deep and rising, black water carrying the smell of river silt and diesel, and somewhere above them the rain came down with the steady violence of a thing that intended to keep going until the mountains themselves gave up.

"Hand pump," she said to the dark, and Dex groaned from his bunk, and Okonkwo was already moving, because Okonkwo always heard the bad news first.

They pumped in shifts through the grey hours. Rosalind took the longest stretch, as she always did, working the handle in a rhythm she didn't have to think about so her mind could go where it wanted, which was nowhere good. Six days locked in this flooded gorge. The passes closed behind them by landslides they'd heard rather than seen, the river risen twenty feet up cliffs that had no business being underwater, the whole world reduced to rain and rock and the narrow brown throat of water they were tethered to, bow and stern lines run to trees that might not hold if the surge came again.

Vane had timed this. She was sure of it now. He'd had the weather data, the same seasonal models any competent expedition planner used, and he'd pushed them upstream anyway, into the window when retreat became impossible. You don't trap people by accident. Not twice.

By dawn the water was down to her shins and holding. Priya jury-rigged the pump back to life with wire and profanity, and Okonkwo made tea that tasted of smoke and condensed milk, and the boat settled into the grim routine of the besieged.

Teodor appeared on deck last, as he always did, moving quietly the way big men sometimes learn to move — like an apology for taking up space. He took a bail bucket without being asked and worked the stern corner where the leak wept through a seam, methodical, unhurried, saying nothing.

Rosalind watched him over the rim of her cup.

Six days of this. Six days of a man who spoke three dialects of the Karakoram fluently, who read river currents like a native, who had appeared out of the flood at the confluence with a damaged boat and no cargo worth mentioning and no story that held together under pressure. Six days of him knowing things he shouldn't know. The name of the village elder two bends downstream. The fact that her father preferred blue ink. That one he'd let slip on day three, correcting a heading on her chart copy — *your father wrote it the other way round* — and then gone very still, as if he'd stepped off a ledge and was waiting to find out how far the fall was.

She hadn't pushed. Not yet. But the water was rising in more ways than one, and there is only so long you can share a leaking hull with a stranger before either trust or suspicion has to win.

"Teodor." She set the cup down. "Sit."

He looked at her. Rain drummed the canopy overhead. Behind them, Dex slept in a nest of rope coils, and Priya was asleep sitting up against the engine housing, and Okonkwo had drifted below with the medic's gift of sleeping anywhere.

He sat.

"I'm going to ask you questions," Rosalind said, "and I'd like you to consider, carefully, whether lying to me on a sinking boat in a gorge I can't leave is really the position you want to be in."

"That's fair," he said.

"Who are you?"

"You know my name."

"I know what you told me. Teodor Vasik, hydrographic surveyor, contracted through a firm in Islamabad. That's a costume. Costumes fit too well or not well enough, and yours fits like it was tailored. So. Who are you?"

He turned the bail bucket over in his hands, studying it as though it might offer counsel. When he looked up, something in his face had changed — not softer exactly. Unburdened. Like a man setting down weight he'd carried so long his spine had grown around it.

"I was your father's assistant," he said. "For eleven years. Until four years ago, when he stopped leaving the house."

The rain filled the silence.

Rosalind did not move. Some part of her brain — the part that had kept her alive through cave-ins and border crossings and the particular hell of a decade spent around men who wanted things from her — went cold and procedural, cataloguing exits, weapons, the distance to the rail. Another part, older and less disciplined, simply hurt.

"You're lying," she said.

"I'm not."

"My father hasn't had an assistant in fifteen years. He hasn't had a *survey* in—"

"He has. You just weren't on the list of people told." Teodor set the bucket down between them, deliberately, like laying a card face-up. "After Eli died, Aldous Sterling ended his public career. What he didn't end was the work. He ran one more survey. Private. Unfunded, unaffiliated, unrecorded. Two seasons in the high Karakoram, 2014 and 2015. I was his hands and his eyes, because by then his own were going."

"You're describing a man who couldn't climb to his own mailbox."

"People change fast when their son dies on a glacier." Teodor said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "He wasn't climbing. He was documenting. There's a difference, and it mattered to him enormously, near the end."

"Near the end of what?"

Teodor glanced at the sleeping shapes, the rain, the grey light coming down the gorge. Then back at her.

"Of the truth," he said.

* * *

She made him wait while she woke Priya — not because she needed the engineer, but because she refused to hear whatever came next alone, and because Priya awake meant Priya armed with a wrench, and some animal part of Rosalind wanted that within reach.

Priya listened to the first sentence, said "I bloody knew it," and then sat down and shut up, which was the most diplomatic thing Rosalind had ever seen her do.

"The survey your father ran," Teodor began, "wasn't geological. Not only. In the second season, working a lateral moraine above the Chogo Lungma ice, he found evidence of an excavation. Recent. Professional. Someone had cut into the ice cave system under the serac field and taken something out — and done it badly enough to leave traces. Tool marks. Packing materials. A broken crate stave with a shipping mark."

"What kind of something?"

"A bronze. Gandharan school, maybe older — the region's full of them, most of it looted over the centuries, most of it gone. But this piece hadn't been gone. It had been *hidden*, deposited in the ice centuries ago, possibly deliberately, possibly just lost, and it survived because nobody knew it was there." His voice stayed level, but his hands had started moving, sketching shapes in the air, and Rosalind recognized the gesture with a jolt she couldn't afford: her father talked with his hands exactly like that. "Aldous documented everything. Photographs, stratigraphy, the works. And then he traced the shipping mark, and the shipping mark led to a broker, and the broker led to a consortium — collectors, mostly, respectable men with foundations and board seats — and one of those men is the money behind Marcus Vane."

The rain hammered. Somewhere upriver, ice shifted in the gorge wall with a sound like a door closing.

"Your father tried to go through channels," Teodor continued. "Ministry of Culture, antiquities boards, the usual. Every channel leaked. Every report he filed came back to the consortium faster than it reached the officials. By 2016 he understood that the survey itself had become dangerous — not the artifact, the *documentation*. Proof of the theft, proof of the network, provenance chains that would unravel a dozen reputations. So he sealed it. Split the archive. Half went into storage in Skardu under a false name. Half went into the ice, back into the cave system, where he'd found the traces in the first place." A pause. "And then he wrote to Vane."

Rosalind felt the floor tilt, or imagined she did. "Wrote to him *what*?"

"A warning. That the survey existed. That copies would surface if anything happened to him. It was insurance — clumsy, desperate, the move of a sick man with no allies left. And it backfired, because Vane didn't need to kill him. Your father was already dying, already buried in that house with his nurse and his morphine and his ghosts. Killing him proves nothing. The documents were the problem. So Vane spent years finding them. Found the Skardu half last year — it's gone, bought or burned, we don't know which. The other half is still in the ice. And this expedition—" Teodor spread his hands, taking in the leaking hull, the flooded gorge, the whole doomed geometry of it, "—isn't a recovery mission. It's a cleanup. Vane isn't hiring you to find the survey, Rosalind. He's hiring you to be standing next to it when it disappears, so that when the story gets told, it's the Sterling daughter who lost it. Or worse. Who sold it."

"Why?" Her voice came out flat. "Why me? Why dress it up at all?"

"Because a cleanup needs a fall guy with credibility, and there is no one on earth whose failure will be believed more completely than yours. The daughter who already lost her brother on that route. The daughter who swore off her father's work. Everyone in the trade knows the story. Vane wrote half of it himself, over ten years, at every conference table and every bar in this business." Teodor's voice dropped. "You're not the crew, Rosalind. You're the alibi."

* * *

Afterward she stood at the rail for a long time, watching the brown water slide past the hull, and let the cold do its work.

It wasn't shock, exactly. Shock was for surprises, and nothing Teodor had said surprised her; that was the worst of it. Every piece fit a shape she'd been refusing to trace for days — the sealed crates Priya had flagged outside Gilgit, the advance team that had gone ahead without radio protocol, Vane's smile at the dockside bar in Chittagong, warm as a lamp over a trap. She had known. Some part of her had signed the contract knowing, because refusing meant someone worse would go, and because a thin, shameful thread in her had wanted the route anyway — wanted the glacier, wanted the last place Eli had stood, even if wanting it was a wound she kept reopening.

But knowing a thing and hearing it spoken aloud in the rain by a man wearing her father's gestures were different animals.

"Priya," she said quietly. "If I asked you to turn this boat around right now — run the lines, take the gorge at flood stage, odds maybe one in three — would you?"

Priya didn't pretend to misunderstand. She looked at the water, at the cliffs running with runoff, at the taut bow line humming faintly in the current. "I'd ask what we're turning around *for*. Running means Vane wins by default. Staying means we drown slow. Those are the options you're giving me?"

"Those are the options the river's giving us."

"Then I'd say," Priya said slowly, "that a third option usually shows up about now, and it's usually worse than both."

Rosalind almost smiled. Ten years of digs and disasters, and Priya's fatalism remained the most reliable instrument on any expedition.

She went aft. Teodor was still bailing, of course — steady, uncomplaining, working the seam like penance. She picked up the second bucket and worked beside him, and for a while the only sounds were water and rain and the creak of the tethered boat.

"If my father sent you," she said finally, "why not tell me at the confluence? Why the hydrographer act? Why let me haul you aboard half-trusting you?"

"Because your father didn't send me." Teodor set the bucket down. "He doesn't know I'm here. He doesn't know I've been looking for the archive for two years, or that I tracked Vane's funding, or that I got myself hired onto this crew through a firm that thinks I'm exactly what my papers say. He's past sending anyone anywhere." He met her eyes. "I'm here because I helped him seal that cache, and because I watched what the truth cost him, and because someone has to carry it out of the ice who isn't doing it for money. I lied to you because you had every reason to put me off this boat, and I need to be on it. That's the whole of it. I'm sorry."

"You understand that changes nothing," Rosalind said. "You understand that a man who lies to me for six days and then tells me a beautiful story is not, by that act, trustworthy. You understand that."

"Yes."

"And that if you're Vane's man — planted, patient, playing the long game — this speech is exactly the speech I'd want to hear."

"Yes," he said again, and didn't flinch, and she hated how much that steadied her.

Below, Dex's voice floated up through the hatch, arguing sleepily with Okonkwo about ration counts, and the ordinary sound of it went through her like a blade. Three people on this boat who trusted her. Three people who'd followed her into a flooded gorge on a contract she'd burned once already and signed anyway. If Teodor was telling the truth, she'd sailed her family into a slaughterhouse. If he was lying, she was about to hand the knife to a stranger because he bailed water nicely and missed her father.

Both possibilities required the same decision, which was probably how you knew it was the right one.

"We don't turn around," she said. "We can't, and we wouldn't. We go on to the high route, same as planned, same as Vane expects. But we stop reporting position. Radio silence except scheduled check-ins with pre-recorded chatter — Priya can fake our coordinates, make us look slower than we are. Vane's people think we're two days behind where we'll actually be." She looked at Teodor. "And you're going to show me, on paper, everything. The cave location. The sealing method. The shipping marks. All of it. If one detail contradicts my father's handwriting style, one, I'll know."

Something moved through his face — relief, maybe, or the exhaustion of a man allowed at last to stop performing. "You remember his handwriting that well?"

"I grew up reading it," Rosalind said. "Field notes were the only letters he ever wrote me."

She left him at the stern and went below to wake Dex, because the boy had sharp eyes and better luck than anyone deserved, and because if there was a hidden cache on this boat — Vane's crates held more than supplies, she'd bet the *Kestrel* herself on it — she wanted it found before the river rose again.

Dex sat up rubbing his hair into worse chaos. "We there yet?"

"No," Rosalind said. "But I need you to do some digging."

His grin was instant and slightly feral. "Finally."

Above them, the rain kept falling, and far up the drowned gorge, beyond the second bend where the cliffs narrowed to a slot, something boomed — deep, tectonic, wrong. Priya's head came up. Teodor's too.

"That's not thunder," Priya said.

No, Rosalind thought. Thunder came from above.

That sound had come from the mountain itself — from the glacier, calving early, shifting in the warming rain. The high route was opening ahead of schedule.

So, very likely, was the trap.
