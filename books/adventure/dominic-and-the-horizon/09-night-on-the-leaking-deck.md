## 9. Night on the Leaking Deck

The storm came down the gorge like something with a grudge.

Rosalind felt it before she heard it — a pressure change in her ears, a hush in the water that meant the river was holding its breath. Then the walls of the gorge lit white, and the thunder arrived not as sound but as impact, slamming off the rock faces and doubling back on itself until the whole world was one continuous detonation.

"Deck! Everybody on deck!" She was already moving, barefoot, the boat's cabin too small to die in.

Priya had the engine hatch open before Rosalind finished shouting. Okonkwo came up with Dex by the collar, the kid protesting that he'd been up anyway, which was true, which was worse. The rain hit like gravel flung sideways. Above them the cliffs vanished into cloud; below them the river had gone from brown to the color of wet iron, running fast enough to carry trees.

And there were trees. Whole trunks came down the current, root-balls first, spinning slow and terrible, each one capable of opening their hull like a tin can.

"Priya — can you hold us mid-channel?"

"I can hold us *near* mid-channel." Priya's hands moved over the throttle without looking at it. "Mid-channel is where the debris is. I'm threading."

"Thread faster."

Teodor appeared beside her in the rain, saying nothing, handing her a coil of rope as if he'd read her mind. He'd been aboard four days now and still hadn't explained himself beyond the version he'd given them at the confluence — surveyor, ex-assistant, stranded — and every hour she didn't throw him off the boat made her angrier at herself. But his hands knew boats. When the bilge alarm started shrieking an hour ago, he'd been below with the pump before anyone else registered the sound.

That was the other thing. The pump was running because they were taking water.

"How bad?" she shouted over the storm.

He leaned close so she could hear him. His voice, when it came, was calm in a way that should have been reassuring and instead felt like being told the exact dimensions of a grave. "Seam above the keel, port side. We're gaining maybe ten centimeters an hour. In this chop, more."

"And if we stop moving?"

"We sink slower."

*Wonderful.* She tied herself to the rail and went to work.

* * *

They fought the boat for six hours.

It wasn't strategy after the first hour; it was rhythm. Bail, check the seam, bail, wedge the emergency patch kit's rubber sheet against the split wood while Priya held the bow into the waves, bail again. The river threw them sideways and they threw themselves back. Lightning turned the gorge into a series of photographs: black water, white foam, Dex's face streaked with rain and terror he was trying very hard to disguise as boredom.

At some point Okonkwo took Dex below and dosed him with something mild, because the kid stopped arguing and started working, small and quick, passing tools. At some point Priya's engine coughed, swallowed water, and roared back to life, and everyone pretended not to have heard the cough.

At some point Rosalind realized Teodor had been bailing beside her for three hours without a single wasted motion, and that she'd stopped watching him like a suspect and started watching him like a crewman, and that this frightened her more than the storm did.

Around two in the morning, the storm broke apart against the mountains the way storms do — not ending but scattering, the thunder retreating up-valley, the rain thinning to a steady hiss. The river calmed from violence to menace. They found a side eddy behind a rock spur and tied off, engine ticking, everyone breathing like they'd run the length of the gorge.

The leak was still there. It would keep being there until daylight let someone see what they were patching.

"Sleep in shifts," Rosalind said. "Priya, you first. Okonkwo, take Dex down. I'll take the deck."

"You've been up longest," Okonkwo said.

"I'll take the deck," she said again, in the voice that ended discussions, and Okonkwo, who had worked for her three years and knew exactly how far that voice could be pushed, went below without another word.

She expected Teodor to go too. He didn't. He sat down against the rail across from her, pulled a tarp over both their knees without asking, and looked out at the iron-colored water as though it were scenery.

"You don't have to stay," she said.

"No," he agreed. "I don't."

She let it go. There wasn't enough energy left to fight someone who kept handing her rope.

* * *

The boat groaned against its lines. Water seeped. Somewhere upstream, the storm continued its private argument with the mountains, flashes flickering along the cliff tops like signal fires.

Rosalind found herself talking before she decided to.

"My brother hated boats."

Teodor didn't turn his head. "Did he."

"Hated them. Said they were just holes you paid to fill with water." She almost smiled. "He was a climber. Mountains were honest to him. A mountain either holds you or it doesn't, and it never pretends otherwise. Boats lie. They float right up until the moment they don't."

"What happened to him?"

The question landed softly, which is why it cut. Most people asked it wrong — with pity, or worse, with curiosity dressed as sympathy. Teodor asked it the way you'd ask *what happened to your engine*, a mechanical question about a machine that had stopped working.

"He died." Ten years, and she could say it now in a flat voice, the way you'd report weather. "Ten years ago. Karakoram. A glacier called the Sersang. There was an accident during my father's last survey. Icefall. He was under it."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. You didn't drop it on him." She heard her own voice and was surprised by what was in it — not grief exactly, something older and drier. "Everyone says sorry. My father said sorry for ten years, mostly by not speaking to me. Sorry is free. Nobody ever tells me what actually happened up there."

Teodor was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. The bilge pump cycled. Somewhere below, Dex talked in his sleep.

"The reports said icefall," Teodor said finally. "A serac release. Wrong place, wrong minute."

"That's what the reports said."

"But you don't believe them."

"I believe Eli Sterling didn't stand under unstable ice. He taught me glacier travel before I could drive. He read terrain the way Priya reads engines. If there was a serac hanging over that route, he'd have routed around it, or waited, or turned back." Her hands had closed into fists inside the tarp without her permission. "So either the reports are wrong, or my brother made a mistake no one who knew him believes he could make. Those are the only options, and I've lived with both of them for a decade, and I hate them equally."

The river slid past, patient, carrying its debris southward.

"There are more than two options," Teodor said.

She turned to look at him. In the dark his face was mostly shadow and the pale line of an old scar over one eyebrow she'd never asked about. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning sometimes the people writing the reports need them to say something specific." He said it evenly, without drama, the way he said everything, and somehow that made it land harder than if he'd shouted. "Meaning sometimes an accident is a word people use when the truth would cost money."

Her pulse picked up. "You've read the reports."

"I've read everything your father wrote between 2009 and 2014." He paused. "I told you I was his last assistant. That part was true. What I didn't tell you is what he was working on when he stopped."

The storm-light flickered on the cliff tops. Rosalind became aware of her own heartbeat, of the water seeping beneath her boots, of the fact that she was tied to a leaking boat in a flooded gorge with a man who had known her father and had chosen, day after day, not to tell her the one thing she'd wanted to know for ten years.

"You're going to tell me now," she said, "or you're going to swim."

"Yes," Teodor said. "But not tonight. Tonight the boat needs both of us, and what I have to say needs all of you listening." He met her eyes. "Tomorrow. When there's daylight. I promise you'll hate me less in daylight."

"That's a low bar."

"It's the only bar I'm confident I can clear."

Despite everything — the exhaustion, the suspicion coiling in her chest, the leak whispering under the deck plates — she almost laughed. Almost.

* * *

Around four, the seam got worse.

She heard it before the pump did — a change in the sound of the water under the floor, a hollowness where there shouldn't be hollowness. By the time she had the flashlight on, the bilge was showing a hand's depth of water where twenty minutes earlier there'd been inches.

"Wake Priya," she said, and Teodor was already moving.

They spent the next ninety minutes doing surgery by flashlight. The seam had opened further — the storm's pounding had worked the caulking loose along half a meter of planking, and the river was coming in with the calm persistence of something that intended to win eventually. Priya drove wooden wedges and hammered oakum into the gap while Rosalind held the light and Teodor held Priya's ankles, both of them braced against the roll. Okonkwo ran the manual pump in shifts with Dex, who had come up unbidden and refused, this time, to be sent below.

"Talk to me," Priya gritted, hammering. "Somebody talk. I think better with noise."

"Eli used to sing when he was scared," Rosalind heard herself say. The words came out before she could stop them, and once they were out she couldn't find a reason to take them back. "Badly. On purpose. He said fear was just adrenaline looking for a job, and singing gave it one."

"Sing, then," Dex said, pumping.

"I don't sing."

"I do," Teodor said, and began — low, rough, entirely tuneless — something that might have been a mountain ballad in a language Rosalind half recognized from her father's field recordings. It was terrible. It was perfect. Priya laughed and hammered straighter. Dex pumped in time. The water came in and they pushed it out, and somewhere in the middle of the third verse Rosalind realized her hands had stopped shaking.

They won the way you win against rivers: slowly, locally, temporarily. By dawn the inflow was down to a seep the pump could handle, the wedges held, and the sky over the eastern ridge had gone the color of old pewter with a thin gold seam underneath.

Priya collapsed onto the deck planks and slept where she fell. Okonkwo herded Dex below. The river ran on, high and brown and indifferent, and the gorge walls stood dripping in the early light like the pillars of some ruined cathedral.

Rosalind sat down next to Teodor. Neither of them spoke for a while. The tarp was still over their knees. She noticed she hadn't moved it.

"You said tomorrow," she said.

"It's tomorrow."

She looked at him. Rain-wet, exhausted, forty-something with gray coming in at his temples and hands scarred from ropes that weren't hers. A stranger who bailed water like he'd done it his whole life and sang like a man who'd learned grief in more than one language.

"Start with my brother," she said. "Start with what really happened on the Sersang."

Teodor looked out at the water for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was quiet enough that she had to lean toward him to catch it.

"Your father didn't lose the survey to the storm," he said. "He hid it. And the last thing he wrote before the icefall — the last entry in his own hand — was a warning that Marcus Vane's backers were coming to make sure it stayed lost."

The gold seam on the horizon widened. Downriver, around the next bend where the gorge narrowed, Rosalind saw something she'd been too tired to notice before: a second boat, beached and listing against the rocks, flying no flag at all.

Nobody was moving on its deck.
