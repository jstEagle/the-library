## 10. What Vane Paid For

The gorge narrowed for two days until the river was less a river than a held breath — brown water compressed between walls of black schist, the boat threading through with inches to spare on either side. Priya ran the engine at half-throttle and sweated through her shirt doing it. Okonkwo stood at the bow with a pole, pushing off rock shelves that appeared and vanished under the silt-laden surface like the backs of sleeping animals.

Rosalind sat amidships with the survey coordinates spread across her knees and did not look up when Dex said, "There's something on the left bank."

She'd been hearing it in his voice since dawn — not fear exactly, but the particular flatness he got when he'd seen something his brain hadn't finished categorizing. Dex was sixteen and had grown up in salvage camps; there wasn't much left that unsettled him. That was what unsettled her.

"Something," she repeated.

"Smoke. Old smoke. And — I don't know. Crows."

Crows meant carrion. Carrion in the Karakoram foothills usually meant a dead goat or a dead mule. She folded the coordinates and stood.

"Take us in," she said.

* * *

The camp sat on a gravel shelf forty meters above the waterline, tucked against an overhang that would keep off rain and, from the river, was nearly invisible. Rosalind saw why Vane had chosen it before she saw anything else: sightlines down the gorge for a kilometer, one approach route, water within carrying distance. It was a professional camp. Whoever had built it had built camps like it before.

The fire pit was cold and had been cold for days — she judged four, maybe five, from the state of the ash and the rain-softened edges of the trench. Two tents, expedition grade, the kind Vane's money bought. One had collapsed. The other still stood, its door flap open like a mouth mid-sentence.

Okonkwo crouched at the edge of the site and put out a hand to stop Dex from going further. "Boots," he said quietly. "Everyone watch where you walk."

"Why?" Dex asked.

"Because someone died here," Okonkwo said, "and whoever made them die might have left things behind that we don't want to step on."

Priya had gone very still by the boats. Teodor stood beside her, and Rosalind noticed — filed away without wanting to — that he'd positioned himself where he could see both the camp and the river. Not casually. Deliberately.

She walked in alone.

The first man was behind the collapsed tent. He'd been dead long enough that she made herself look at the ground instead of him, at first — the scuffed earth, the drag marks, the brass casing glinting near the overhang wall. Nine-millimeter. Then she made herself look at him anyway, because he deserved that much and because she needed her eyes working.

Two shots, close range. One in the chest, one — she looked — no. She looked at the ground again. The crows rose off the rocks above and wheeled once and settled, patient as accountants.

The second man was in the standing tent. He'd gotten farther, maybe, or been brought back here. His kit was scattered around him in a way that told her someone had searched it thoroughly and recently: papers fanned out, a satellite phone opened and gutted, battery removed, SIM tray empty. The tent's aluminum poles had been bent back deliberately. Nothing about the scene was casual. This was housekeeping.

"Roz." Priya's voice from the edge of the camp, carefully level. "You need to see this."

There was a third body. She hadn't seen it from the approach because it had been moved — dragged to the lip of the shelf and rolled into a crevice between boulders, covered with a tarp weighted by stones. A bad job of hiding, or a hurried one, or — and this thought arrived cold and complete — not hiding at all. Marking. A message for whoever came next.

This man's hands were bound with zip ties. There were burns on his forearms that she refused to describe even to herself. He'd been questioned. He'd answered, or hadn't, and then the answers had stopped mattering.

Dex had come up despite being told not to, because he was sixteen and that was what sixteen did, and Rosalind heard his breath change behind her. She turned and put herself between him and the crevice, too late, but she did it anyway.

"Go sit with Priya," she said.

"I'm fine—"

"Dex." She kept her voice low and even, the voice she used on clients and customs officials. "Go sit with Priya. That's not a request."

He went. She watched him go all the way to the boats, and only then let her shoulders drop half an inch.

* * *

They searched the camp the way she'd taught them to search wreck sites: methodically, silently, each person assigned a quadrant, everything photographed before it was touched. Okonkwo handled the bodies — covering them properly, taking what identification he could find, saying whatever quiet words he said over people he'd never met. Priya pulled the gutted satellite phone apart further and found nothing, which was itself information. Whoever had done this had wanted the team unreachable and untraceable.

Teodor found the manifest.

It was wedged under a stone at the back of the overhang, a waterproof pouch, the kind surveyors used. Inside: a supply list in Vane's cramped handwriting, a route map marked with dates, and a radio schedule — check-ins every twelve hours with a relay station in Skardu, coded callsigns. The last logged check-in was six days ago. There had been no check-in since.

"They missed their window," Teodor said. He held the paper without reading aloud, but Rosalind read over his arm because it was her name on the contract and these were, whatever else they were, her problem now. "Someone will have come looking. Or someone sent them and already knew."

"You knew them?" she asked.

He didn't answer immediately. Rain ticked against the tarp overhead. Down at the boats, Dex was pretending to organize rope coils and watching them with the whole of his attention.

"I recognized one," Teodor said finally. "The one by the tents. Anselm Reyes. He worked logistics on your father's Tibet survey, eleven years ago. Before my time with him, but his signature was on requisitions I processed later. He went private after. High-end extraction work." He paused. "Vane's kind of contractor."

"You're telling me Vane hired men who used to work for my father."

"I'm telling you Vane hired men who knew how to move quietly through this exact terrain, in this exact season, ahead of this exact expedition." He folded the manifest closed. "That's not a coincidence, Rosalind. That's a plan."

* * *

She made them push off an hour before dark, upstream another two kilometers to a side channel Okonkwo remembered from the charts, deep enough to hide the boat under overhanging birch. They ate cold rations without a fire. Nobody suggested one.

Then she sat on the deck with the manifest in her lap and made herself do the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the gravel shelf.

Timeline. Vane's advance team had come through here five, six days ago — moving fast, light, ahead of the main expedition. They'd been killed here, at this camp, sometime after. Which meant the killers had followed them. Which meant the killers knew the route, the schedule, the callsigns. Which meant the killers were either better informed than Vane's own advance team, or —

Or they were Vane's. The second wave. The cleaners of the cleaners.

And the timing. She'd known it since the passes closed, since the monsoon locked them into the gorge with a leaking hull and no exit, but she'd dressed it up as bad luck, as weather, as the mountain being the mountain. Now she laid it out flat and looked at it. Vane had hired her in June. The permits, the boats, the porters' contracts — all arranged with a speed that should have embarrassed her. The departure date had put them on the river precisely late enough to be trapped. Precisely late enough that whatever happened up here would happen with no witnesses coming in and no testimony getting out until spring.

She was not the leader of an expedition. She was the alibi. Sterling's daughter, famous enough to lend legitimacy, expendable enough to bury — and if the crew survived, they'd carry home a story about weather and tragedy and a rival salvager who'd tried and failed to find an old man's lost survey. Grief as cover story. Her grief, specifically. Eli's glacier, chosen so that if she broke down, if she turned back, it would look human rather than suspicious.

"He timed us," she said aloud. The others looked up. "The whole trip. The monsoon isn't a risk he miscalculated. It's the design. We're sealed in here with whatever he's cleaning up, and when the water drops, either we find nothing and go home, or we don't come home at all and the record says the river took us."

Priya set down her cup. "The crates," she said. "I told you about the crates in Gilgit. Sealed, armed guards, manifests that didn't match weights. I thought smuggling. Weapons, maybe."

"It's not weapons," Teodor said.

Everyone looked at him. He met their eyes one at a time, unhurried, a man who had decided something on the gravel shelf and was now done pretending otherwise.

"No," Rosalind said. "Tell me what it is. All of it. Tonight, on this deck, because I am done learning about my own expedition from dead men's camps."

* * *

What he told her took twenty minutes and rearranged the last ten years of her life.

Her father's final survey — the one that had ended his career, his reputation, his marriage to the field, the one Rosalind had sworn off and Vane had dangled in front of her like bait — had documented more than ice cores and strata. In a high cave above the calving face of the Siachen shoulder glacier, the old man's team had recorded the removal of an artifact. Not discovered. Removed. Taken decades earlier by a colonial-era expedition whose records had been burned, whose porters had been paid to forget, whose finds had passed through three private collections and vanished. Her father had traced it — provenance, photographs, the lot — and written it all into the survey appendix, because that was who he'd been underneath the ambition: a man who believed the record was sacred.

The artifact's current owners had spent thirty years making sure the record stayed buried. When word reached them that a dying surveyor's last work described it in detail, they'd hired a fixer. The fixer was Marcus Vane.

"And the accident," Rosalind said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Eli. On the glacier."

Teodor was quiet for a long moment. "Your father sent Eli's team to retrieve the appendix materials. To bring them out safely. Someone else was sent to make sure they never arrived."

The river said nothing. The birch leaves dripped. Somewhere far downstream, thunder stacked itself against the gorge walls.

She thought about burning Vane's contract in the dockside bar, the way the edges curled, how satisfied she'd felt — how certain she'd been that refusing him was the strong move. He'd left a copy anyway. Of course he had. He'd never needed her yes. He'd needed her name, her face, her famous grief, attached to an expedition designed to fail. And if she'd said no, someone worse would have led it — someone with no history here, no reason to hesitate, no crew worth keeping alive.

He'd counted on her pride. He'd counted on her guilt. Both had worked.

"We turn back," Priya said. "Run the gorge at night, take our chances—"

"On a leaking hull, in flood stage, past a gorge we barely cleared at noon?" Okonkwo shook his head slowly. "We'd be trading a maybe for a certainly."

"So we stay and die politely?"

"We stay," Rosalind said, "and we finish it."

They all looked at her. She realized her hands had stopped shaking somewhere in the last hour, and that frightened her more than the shaking had.

"Vane thinks he sent a cover story up this river," she said. "He sent witnesses. Four of them, plus whatever Teodor is. The advance team is dead because they knew too much and had nowhere to be. We're different. We have a boat, we have the coordinates, and as of tonight we have the truth — most of it. What we don't have is time. When the water drops, his second wave comes up this gorge looking for loose ends, and we are the loosest ends on this river."

"So we run," Dex said. He'd crept forward during the talking, the way he always did, and nobody had the heart to send him aft anymore.

"No," Rosalind said. "We climb. The high route opens when the glacier calves — days, not weeks, if the pressure readings are right. The cave is above the calving face. The evidence is in the cave. We get there first, we secure it, and we come down the far side into Baltistan where there are police, journalists, and a British consulate that owes me a favor." She looked around the circle of faces, lit by one small shielded lamp. "Vane wants this buried with everyone who carries it. So we stop carrying it alone. We make it public before he can reach us."

"And if the calving doesn't open when you think?" Priya asked.

"Then we'll be standing on a glacier arguing about geology," Rosalind said. "Which is still better odds than the gorge."

Nobody laughed. But nobody argued, and in her crew that was ratification.

* * *

Later, when the others had turned in, she stood at the rail watching the black water slide past the hull. Teodor came to stand beside her, close enough that their sleeves touched, and neither of them moved away.

"The man in the crevice," she said. "Reyes. He worked for my father."

"Yes."

"And Vane had him killed."

"Vane, or the people Vane works for. Eventually it stops mattering whose hand is on the knife."

She nodded. Below them, the bilge pump cycled on, ran its eight seconds, clicked off. Eight seconds. It had been six when they left Gilgit.

"How much else haven't you told me?" she asked.

He considered the question honestly, which she was beginning to understand was his default setting. "Enough to fill another night," he said. "But not about the survey. About your father. About Eli." He turned to look at her, and in the shielded lamplight his face was older than she'd first taken it for, and kinder. "When we're off this river. You'll want to be sitting down."

"That's what everyone says before they ruin your life."

"Sometimes," Teodor said, "it's what people say before they hand it back."

Downstream, faint beneath the rain, the thunder kept counting. Six days since the last radio check-in. Five days, maybe fewer, until the water dropped. She stood at the rail and did the arithmetic again, and this time the numbers didn't frighten her. They aimed her.

At dawn they would break camp and start climbing.
