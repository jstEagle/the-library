## 11. Dex's Discovery

They buried the advance team at dawn.

Rosalind had wanted to leave them where they lay — the mountain would keep them better than any grave — but Okonkwo insisted, and she couldn't argue with the logic of a medic who'd spent twenty years treating bodies as things that deserved dignity whether or not anyone was left to witness it. So they dug into the frozen scree with ice axes and their hands, and they laid four men down under cairns of grey stone, and Priya said a few words in Tamil that Rosalind didn't understand but felt anyway, low and rhythmic, like water over rock.

Teodor stood apart, as he always did when there was nothing to fix or carry. He watched her instead of the graves. She felt his attention the way you feel weather coming — pressure behind the ears, a change in the air.

"You knew," she said to him quietly, while Dex was out of earshot hauling stones. "When you told us about the artifact. You knew Vane's people were already up here."

"I suspected."

"Suspected." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Four men, Teodor. Somebody shot them in the back and left them in a crevasse like garbage. And we're walking the same route with the same names on the same manifest."

"Yes," he said simply. "That's why I'm here."

She didn't have an answer for that. She turned back to the cairns and put another stone on top, and her hands were steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have.

* * *

They moved by mid-morning, higher into the gorge, because staying near the graves felt like standing in an open doorway in winter. The valley narrowed around them, walls of black schist streaked with snowmelt, the river below running white and furious with monsoon runoff even now that the rains were slackening. The trail — if it could be called that — clung to the eastern wall in a series of switchbacks cut by generations of porters whose grandchildren still walked it, though not this late in the season, and not after what they'd found.

Dex went first. He always went first on the technical stretches; he was seventeen and weighed nothing and moved across broken ground like gravity was a rumor he'd declined to believe. He carried the rope coil over one shoulder and hummed under his breath, some half-remembered pop song from Karachi, and the sound of it kept the rest of them moving when the air got thin and the drop beside the trail got long.

Rosalind climbed behind him and thought about Eli.

Not deliberately. Grief didn't work that way anymore; ten years had worn a channel in her, and her thoughts ran down it without her permission, the way the river ran down its gorge. Eli at fourteen, teaching himself knots off a diagram in their father's study. Eli at nineteen, standing on the bow of a survey boat in Greenland, arms out, shouting into the wind that he could see the curve of the earth. Eli at twenty-six, waving from the trailhead the last morning she ever saw him alive, saying *catch up, Roz, you're slow*, grinning, gone.

The report had called it a serac collapse. Unpredictable. A hundred tons of ice letting go without warning on a glacier in the Karakoram, burying two members of Edmund Sterling's final survey team. Her father had signed the report. Her father had come home from that mountain old, and she had come home from that mountain alone, and neither of them had ever spoken of it again, and the silence between them had calcified into something harder than the grief itself.

She'd sworn off this route. Sworn off the whole range. And then Vane had shown up with a contract and her father's name on it, and she'd told herself it was about protecting the crew, about keeping worse people out, about justice — all true, all partial. But underneath, in the place she didn't look, she'd known she was climbing toward the glacier. Toward the last place her brother had been.

"Roz!" Dex's voice dropped down the switchbacks, bright with the particular excitement that made her stomach tighten. "You gotta see this!"

Everything was *you gotta see this* with Dex — a marmot, a weird rock, a prayer flag bleached to ghost-colors. But something in the pitch of it now, something underneath the excitement, made her move faster than the altitude liked.

He was fifty meters above her, off the trail, wedged into a shallow overhang where the cliff face folded back on itself. He'd tied himself off — good, he was learning — and he was crouched in the shadow of the fold with something in his hands.

"I saw the cairn," he said. "Little one. Not a grave — wrong shape, too small, and no stones stacked on top, just laid against the crack. Like a marker." He held it out. "Like somebody wanted it found, but only by somebody looking."

It was a flat slab of schist, hand-sized, and scratched into its face with something sharp — a piton tip, maybe — was a single character. Not Tibetan, not Urdu. A letter of the Latin alphabet, gouged deep:

**E**

The world tilted.

Rosalind heard herself say, very calmly, "Where exactly," and heard Dex answer, and understood none of it, because her body had gone somewhere else entirely — back ten years, back to a tent in base camp, back to a man from the consulate holding her elbow too hard while he told her about ice and time and probability. Her vision narrowed to the letter on the stone. Four strokes. She'd taught him to write it. She used to trace it on his back when he was small, spelling words in the dark to make him laugh.

Eli.

"He marked caches like this," she heard herself say. "On surveys. It's how Dad's teams flagged supply drops. E for—" Her voice failed.

"For Eli," Dex said softly. He was watching her with those quick dark eyes that missed nothing, and she realized he'd already worked it out, had probably worked it out the moment he saw the letter, and had been waiting, giving her the extra seconds she needed. Seventeen years old and he already knew how to be gentle with her. That was going to break her heart later. Right now there wasn't room.

"Okonkwo," she called down the slope, and her voice came out level, professional, the voice of a woman running a dig. "Bring lights. Priya, rope anchor up here. We've got a cache."

* * *

The crack in the cliff went back three meters and opened into a hollow the size of a coffin, dry, sheltered, invisible from the trail unless you knew to look. Someone had lined it with a groundsheet gone brittle with age. Someone had stacked supplies against the walls: tins rusted at the seams, a spirit stove, a coil of rope still wrapped in wax paper, medical kit with ampoules that had frozen and shattered years ago. And at the back, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with cord, a metal document case, military green, stenciled with a survey number Rosalind recognized the way you recognize your own name shouted across a room.

Her father's field number. Final expedition.

And beneath it, in the same careful stencil: **E.S. — PERSONAL. DO NOT OPEN WITHOUT WITNESS.**

Eli Sterling. Personal.

Her hands wouldn't work. That was the thing nobody told you about the worst moments — they weren't cinematic. They were clumsy. She fumbled the cord twice, three times, and finally Priya reached past her without a word and cut it with her knife, and Teodor held the light steady, and the oilcloth fell open.

A journal. Cloth-bound, swollen with damp and refreezing, the cover warped. And tucked inside its front cover, a photograph.

Rosalind took it out with both hands, the way you handle a bird with a broken wing.

It was the team. Final survey, base camp, eleven people grinning into a summer sun that never quite warmed the Karakoram. Her father front and center, beard frosted, eyes fierce. And there — second row, arm slung around a laughing Balti porter named Yusuf, mid-joke — Eli. Twenty-six forever. Alive in silver gelatin.

Behind the photograph, folded into quarters, a page torn from the journal proper. Rosalind unfolded it with numb fingers and read her brother's handwriting, the big looping scrawl she'd teased him about for twenty years, and the world came apart along its seams.

*Roz — if you're reading this, then either I'm dead or Dad finally grew a conscience, and honestly either way I'm surprised.*

*Listen. The survey found something at the ice cave. Not geology. An object — old, older than anything has any right to be up here, and Dad thinks it was taken. Looted, decades ago, maybe longer, brought up the mountain and hidden in the ice by men who meant to come back. There are papers in the cache that prove who took it and who paid. Dad wants to publish. He says it'll end him professionally and he doesn't care. He says it's the only honest thing left to do.*

*But Vane's backers know. Vane knows. I heard him on the sat phone with someone in Islamabad and the word he kept using was "containment." Roz, containment is what you do to a spill. To a fire. To people.*

*I'm copying everything tonight. If anything happens to me, it's not the ice. Whatever they tell you, whatever the report says — it's not the ice.*

*Tell Dad I said yes. Tell him I'm proud to be his son on this one, finally, for the right reasons.*

*Catch up, slowpoke. You always were.*

*— E*

The page shook in her hands.

Around her, the others had gone still. She was distantly aware of Priya reading over her shoulder, of Okonkwo's breath catching at *containment*, of Teodor lowering the light so it fell full on the page, his face carved out of shadow and flame. Distantly aware, the way you're aware of weather through a wall.

Because here was the vertigo, the true vertigo, the kind that doesn't come from heights: every load-bearing fact of her life had just been pulled out from under her, and she was falling through the space where they'd been.

The accident hadn't been an accident. Her brother hadn't died of bad luck on bad ice. He had died because he'd learned what Vane's people were, because he'd copied documents, because he'd been a loose end on a mountain where loose ends got tidied. And her father — her father had signed the false report. Had written *serac collapse* in his own hand and sent it to the consulate and let his daughter live ten years inside a lie, let her carry the weight of a random universe, a meaningless death, when the truth was that men had killed Eli and gotten away with it and her father had *let them*.

Or — the other possibility, the one that made her knees go — her father hadn't signed it freely. Had been made to. Had spent ten years dying by inches in a wheelchair in Cheltenham, sending money to porters' villages, hiring strangers to go back up the mountain, unable to say the truth aloud because saying it would get the next person killed.

Both possibilities were unbearable. Both were probably true.

"Rosalind." Teodor's voice, quiet. "Sit down before you fall down."

"I'm fine."

"You're not. Sit."

She sat, because her legs made the decision for her. The scree was cold through her trousers. Somewhere above them a raven called, once, twice, the same two-note phrase ravens had been calling over this valley since before there were people to hear it.

Priya read the page again, lips moving. Then she looked up, and her face had the expression Rosalind had seen on her only twice before — in a flooded mine in Bolivia, and on a boat off Socotra when the engines died in pirate water. The expression of a woman recalculating every assumption at once.

"The advance team," Priya said. "The ones in the crevasse. They weren't Vane's cleanup crew." She tapped the journal. "They were the *first* cleanup crew. Ten years ago. These are the replacements."

"And we're the cover," Rosalind said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. Flat, reasonable, dead calm. "He hires me — my name, my reputation, my father's daughter — so that if anyone asks, this is a legitimate salvage expedition led by Edmund Sterling's kid recovering his lost survey. And everyone who actually knows what's up here ends up in a crevasse, and I sign a clean report at the bottom, and the artifact stays erased, and the Sterlings stay respectable." She laughed again, that broken sound. "He didn't hire me despite who I am. He hired me *because* of it. My name is the alibi."

Dex had picked up the photograph. He was looking at Eli's face, then at hers, doing the arithmetic of family resemblance — the same jaw, the same stubborn set of the brow — and when he spoke his voice had lost all its teenager brightness.

"So the accident. Your brother. It was—"

"It was murder, Dex." She said it out loud for the first time in ten years, and the word went through her like a piton through ice, cracking her clean down the middle. "It was murder and I spent a decade grieving the wrong thing. I grieved the mountain. I grieved chance. I built my whole life on top of a lie and called the building freedom."

Okonkwo crouched in front of her, big hands resting on his knees, and looked at her with the patience he used on the dying. "What do you need, Rosalind?"

What did she need. Rage, mostly. It was rising in her now, filling the space where the vertigo had been, and it was the purest thing she'd felt in years — cleaner than grief, hotter than fear. Ten years of distance, of measured miles, of never unpacking, of keeping her heart in no port — and it had all been built on a foundation of lies laid by men who filed human beings under *containment*. Every mile she'd run had been a mile they'd chased her. And she'd never even known she was running.

She needed the rest of the journal. She needed the documents in the case. She needed the ice cave. She needed Vane.

She stood up.

"Pack everything," she said. "The case, the journal, the photo, the supplies — the tins might still be good and we may need them. Dex, mark this location on both maps and memorize it. If we get separated, this cache gets found. Understood?"

They moved. They were good — her strays, her misfits, her crew of people nobody else would hire — and within minutes the hollow was empty and the marker stone was back in place and they were roped up and climbing again, higher, toward the glacier, toward the calving route that would open soon and close behind them.

Teodor fell into step beside her. For a long stretch neither of them spoke, boots crunching, breath smoking, the river roaring far below.

"You knew about the journal," she said at last. It wasn't a question.

"I knew your father hid copies. I didn't know where. I hoped—" He stopped. Started again. "I hoped his son had been as thorough as his daughter."

"Eli was thorough."

"Then he's still helping you." Teodor glanced sideways at her. "Ten years dead and one step ahead of the men who killed him. That's a good brother to have."

Rosalind looked up the valley. The glacier waited at its head, a river of dirty ice hanging between two black peaks, and somewhere inside it was the cave, and the survey, and the truth, and whatever remained of the moment her brother had decided — knowing what it might cost — to be his father's son for the right reasons.

"Dex," she called forward. "How fast can you climb?"

He looked back over his shoulder, and the grin he gave her was Eli's grin, young and reckless and lit from inside.

"Faster than anybody chasing us," he said. "Why? What's behind us?"

Rosalind turned. Far below, tiny as insects on the switchbacks they'd abandoned an hour ago, four figures were moving up the trail.

"Not yet," she said. "But there will be."
