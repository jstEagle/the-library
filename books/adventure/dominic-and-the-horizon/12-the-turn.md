## 12. The Turn

The journal sat on the chart table between us like a body we were all pretending not to look at.

Rain hammered the deck above. The boat leaked in three places now — Priya had stuffed the worst of them with epoxy-soaked cloth and a prayer, and the bilge pump ran every eleven minutes, a sound I'd stopped hearing hours ago, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. We were moored in a flooded side channel off the gorge, hemmed by black water and blacker rock, and somewhere behind us, downhill along the drowned river, four men from Vane's advance team lay where someone had left them.

Dex hadn't spoken since he'd handed me the journal. He sat in the corner of the cabin with his knees pulled up, seventeen years old and suddenly looking every day of it, watching me the way people watch a fuse burn.

I opened the journal again. Eli's handwriting. Rounder than mine, always had been, the letters of a boy who wrote fast because he thought faster. The pages were warped with old water damage, ink bloomed into gray flowers, but legible. Legible enough.

*Day 9. Father won't say what's under the ice, only that it can't stay there and it can't leave either. He's been taking readings at night when he thinks I'm asleep. There's a second set of notes he keeps separate from the survey proper. I found them. I wish I hadn't.*

I turned the page. My hands were steady. That surprised me. Somewhere underneath the steadiness I could feel the thing that wasn't steady, enormous, patient, waiting for me to stop being useful so it could have me.

*Vane's men came up the valley today. Father said they were porters. They weren't porters.*

*Day 11. I moved the cache. If he asks me why, I'll tell him the truth: because I don't trust the man paying for this expedition, and neither should my father, but my father is past listening. The cave below the serac. Third chamber. I've marked it on nothing — it's in my head and nowhere else.*

*Day 12. They know I know.*

That was the last entry. After that, blank pages, and then the journal simply ended, because Eli had ended, ten years ago, on a glacier two hundred kilometers north of where I was sitting, in an accident the reports called a crevasse fall, an accident I had built my entire adult life on top of the way you build on a grave — carefully, and never looking down.

"Rosalind." Okonkwo's voice, low. He'd been standing at the porthole for a long time, watching the rain come off the gorge walls in sheets. "You need to eat something."

"I need to think."

"You can do both. People do both all the time." He crossed the cabin, put a tin cup in front of me — tea, over-sweetened, the way he made it for shock victims — and stayed standing beside me, not touching, just present, the way he'd been present through every bad night of six years of digs. "Whatever's in that book, it's been in the world for ten years. It'll keep another hour."

"It kept because nobody was looking." I closed the journal. "Vane's men weren't looking. Vane wasn't looking. Everyone who might have looked is dead or bought or —" I almost said *grieving*, and swallowed it. "It kept because I didn't want to find it. That's not the same as safe."

Priya looked up from the engine housing she'd half disassembled on the bench. Grease to her elbows, hair tied back with a zip tie because she'd lost her last elastic somewhere around the confluence. "Say the word and I'll have the radio apart in five minutes. We call the consulate in Skardu, we call the police, we call anyone with a gun that isn't pointed at us—"

"No."

The word came out flat and final, and everyone heard it, including me.

"No?" Dex said.

"No radio." I stood up. My legs ached from two days of bailing water and pretending I wasn't afraid, and I let myself feel the ache, because it was real and most of what I'd felt lately wasn't. "Think it through. Who do we call? The nearest authorities are in Skardu, forty hours downstream if the roads aren't washed out, which they are. Vane has money in this country the way rivers have water. The moment we transmit, we're a location. Every radio in these valleys can be triangulated, and the men who killed his advance team knew exactly where to find that team, which means they have better intelligence than any consulate desk officer reading our coordinates off a Mayday."

"So we do nothing," Priya said.

"We do the thing they don't expect." I turned to the chart table, to the maps spread across it — Eli's old maps, the ones I'd carried for a decade without ever tracing the route north past the moraine line, and Teodor's copies of my father's final survey, annotated in a hand I was beginning to know as well as my brother's. "We go up."

* * *

Teodor had been quiet since the cache. Quieter than his baseline, which was already a kind of silence you could store things in. Now he rose from the bench where he'd been sitting with his back to the bulkhead, arms loose, watching me with those gray eyes that gave away nothing except, occasionally, everything.

"The high route," he said. Not a question.

"The calving window." I put my finger on the map, on the glacier I had spent ten years drawing routes around the way you walk around a hole in the floor. "The monsoon ends within days — pressure's already shifting, Okonkwo's knees called it before the barometer did. When the meltwater drops, the terminal face destabilizes and calves, and for roughly seventy-two hours the icefall below the south col reconfigures. Right now it's a wall. After the calving, it's a staircase. My father's survey used that window. So did—" I paused. "So did Eli."

"And Vane's remaining men," Teodor said, "will be watching the same window. It's the only way up. They know it. You know it."

"Then we get there first."

"They're armed. We're not."

"We're not yet." I looked at Priya. "The crates."

Her mouth twitched — not quite a smile, more the expression of a woman whose suspicions had finally been invited to dinner. "I was wondering when we'd get to the crates."

* * *

They were lashed under the forward tarp, six of them, sealed, stenciled with a shipping company's name that Priya had traced, on the third night of the monsoon lock-in, to a holding company that traced to a foundation that traced, through three more shells, to the same backers funding Vane's expedition. She cracked the first one open with a pry bar while the rest of us watched the rain.

Rope. Pitons. Two hundred meters of static line, modern, military surplus. A collapsible aluminum ladder. Four headlamps still in factory packaging. And beneath the technical gear, wrapped in oilcloth: two shotguns, disassembled, and a box of shells.

Nobody spoke for a moment. The bilge pump kicked on, ran its eleven minutes, shut off.

"He armed us," Dex said slowly. "He armed us and didn't tell us."

"He provisioned his cleanup crew," I corrected. "We were supposed to be cover — civilians, salvage permits, nothing worth reporting. But he packed for contingencies. In case the mountain got difficult. In case anyone got difficult." I picked up one of the shotgun barrels, checked the bore, set it down again. My hands were still steady. "In case I got difficult."

Okonkwo had gone very still in the way he went still before delivering a diagnosis. "Rosalind. If we take those weapons, we stop being witnesses. Whatever happens on that glacier afterward—"

"I know what we become." I looked at him. "But understand something, all of you. Those men killed four people. They will kill us the moment we stop being useful, and they will burn the cave, and the journal, and everything Eli died protecting, and then they will fly home and Vane will send flowers to somebody's funeral and give a speech about scientific integrity. That is the plan. That has been the plan since before we signed." I picked up the oilcloth again. "I'm done being part of someone else's plan."

* * *

We worked through the night, and it was the best I'd felt in ten years, which should have frightened me and didn't.

Priya rebuilt the outboard's fuel line with parts cannibalized from the water pump, because the last thirty kilometers of the approach were shallow braided channels the boat could barely float and we'd need every revolution. Okonkwo repacked the medical kit for altitude — the dexamethasone, the antibiotics, the sutures he hoped not to use and carried anyway, always. Dex took inventory with the fierce competence of a kid who'd grown up running packages through ports where mistakes cost fingers, and when he finished he sat down across from me and slid the manifest over and said, quietly, "I want to come all the way up. Not just to base. All the way."

"Dex—"

"He was my age." His voice cracked and he hated it, I could see him hating it. "Eli. In the journal. He was seventeen. Nobody asked him if he wanted to be brave, right? It just happened. So I'm asking. Let me choose it."

I looked at him for a long time. Rain on the deck. Pump cycling. Somewhere out in the dark, the river carrying our smell downstream toward men with rifles.

"Rope team three," I said. "You stay tied to Okonkwo and you do exactly what he says even if I order otherwise. Deal?"

Something lit up in his face that I hadn't seen since before the advance team, since before the monsoon, maybe since I'd met him — and I understood, with a lurch, that I'd just done the thing my father never did, the thing that had gotten Eli killed: I'd let a kid onto a mountain because he asked. I held onto that understanding. I didn't let it change my answer. Some lessons you learn by refusing to repeat them and some you learn by repeating them with your eyes open, and I couldn't tell yet which this was.

Teodor appeared beside me at the chart table while the others argued about load weights. He didn't touch the maps. He touched the journal instead, one finger, resting on the cover, light as a man testing whether a stove is hot.

"There's something I haven't told you," he said.

"I know."

"My father's handwriting—" he stopped, corrected himself. "Your father's. I told you I knew it from the archives. That was true. It wasn't the whole truth. The last eighteen months of his work — I transcribed most of it. He was losing his eyesight faster than he admitted. He needed hands that could write what his eyes could barely read." He looked at me then, and for once there was no careful blankness; there was something rawer, and younger. "I've read the final survey, Rosalind. All of it. Including the pages that were never filed. I know what's in the third chamber of the cave below the serac. And I know why your father buried it instead of reporting it — and it isn't what you think. It isn't shame."

The pump cycled. Eleven minutes. Ten.

"Then tell me what it is," I said.

"Not tonight." He straightened, and the rawness folded itself away, banked like a fire. "Tonight you sleep, because tomorrow we pole thirty kilometers of floodwater and then climb. But I'll tell you this much now, because you deserve it before you decide anything else: your father didn't hide the artifact to protect himself. He hid it to protect the valley. What's under that ice doesn't belong in a museum or a vault or a private collection, and it doesn't belong to Vane's backers, and your father figured that out too late to stop the looting and just in time to make sure it could never be sold." His voice dropped. "Eli figured it out first. That's why he moved the cache. That's why they—"

He stopped. The sentence finished itself in the space between us, in the rain, in the dark water sliding past the hull toward the sea and everything we'd ever run from.

"That's why they killed him," I said. Out loud. For the first time in ten years, out loud, not *the accident*, not *what happened on the glacier*, but the word itself, plain as a piton driven home. "They killed him."

"Yes."

I nodded once. Then I rolled up the charts, squared the edges against the table the way my father taught me, and felt something settle in my chest that I recognized distantly, like a language I'd spoken as a child: not peace, nothing like peace. Purpose. Cold, specific, directional.

* * *

At dawn the rain thinned to a drizzle and the barometer began its slow climb, and Okonkwo's knees were officially vindicated. The gorge water had dropped half a meter overnight. Through a tear in the cloud to the north, for maybe ninety seconds, we saw them — the Karakoram, white and impossible, floating on their own weather like a country that had never agreed to belong to the world.

Teodor stood at the bow beside me, poling us into the main channel. Behind us, the flooded lowlands. Ahead, the braids, the moraine, the glacier, the window that would open in three days and close forever.

"Vane's men will have spotters on the lower ridge by tomorrow," he said.

"Then we'll be invisible until we're not, and fast when we're visible." I checked the compass, set our heading, and allowed myself one breath of the cold clean air coming down off the ice. "How long to the calving face if we push?"

"With this crew?" He glanced back — Priya coaxing the rebuilt engine to life, Okonkwo lashing loads, Dex already rigged and ready, watching the mountains like they owed him something. "Two days. Maybe less."

"Good." I took the pole from him and set it against the current, and the boat swung north, into the throat of the gorge, toward the place where my brother had died and my father had lied and I had spent ten years measuring my life in distances instead of decisions. "Because we're going to beat them there. And this time, when the ice moves — we're the ones standing on it."

Ahead, far up the valley, so faint it might have been thunder, the glacier spoke.
