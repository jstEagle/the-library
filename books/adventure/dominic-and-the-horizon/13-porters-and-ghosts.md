## 13. Porters and Ghosts

The village appeared out of the rain like something remembered rather than seen.

Rosalind smelled it first — woodsmoke and juniper, animal dung burning in iron stoves, the particular sour-sweetness of barley fermenting in a covered pot. Then the dogs, three of them, announcing them from the terraces above the river. Then the shapes: flat-roofed stone houses stacked against the mountainside like masonry that had grown there, their walls the same gray-brown as the scree, so that for a full minute she stared at what she thought was rockface and realized were windows.

"Askari," Teodor said quietly, beside her. "The porter village."

They came down off the moraine in the last gray light, six of them now instead of five, Dex half-carrying Okonkwo, whose fever had broken sometime in the night but who walked like a man made of wet rope. Priya had the survey tube strapped across her back along with her own pack, refusing help twice. Rosalind carried nothing but the pistol she'd taken from Vane's dead advance team and the weight of everything Dex had found in the cache.

Eli's journal sat against her ribs inside her jacket. She had not opened it since the ice cave. She did not know if she could open it again.

The first villager to reach them was an old woman with a face like weathered walnut and eyes that went straight past Rosalind to Teodor — and stopped. She said something rapid in Balti, and Teodor answered, and whatever he said made her grab his sleeve and turn and shout up the hill in a voice that scattered the dogs.

"They know him," Priya said.

"They knew my father," Teodor corrected. "I'm told I have his hands."

* * *

The house they were given was the largest one, two rooms and a low ceiling black with decades of smoke, and by the time Rosalind got Okonkwo horizontal on a pile of quilts the room was full of people bringing things — butter tea in a dented thermos, tsampa pressed into cakes, a bowl of dried apricots that Dex fell on like a starving dog. An old man with a white beard trimmed short examined Okonkwo's ankle, which Priya had splinted, nodded with the gravity of a physician, and produced a poultice that smelled like it could strip paint.

"He says hot stone, then this, then rest," Teodor translated. "He set fractures on the Biafo glacier for thirty years. He's offended by the splint but willing to forgive it."

"Tell him the splint is mine," Priya said, "and I don't apologize either."

The old man laughed without needing the translation.

Rosalind stood by the door with her tea going cold and watched it happen — the crew folding into the warmth of the room, Dex already teaching a boy of maybe ten to shuffle cards, Priya arguing geometry with the old bonesetter through Teodor's patient relay — and felt the strange vertigo of being welcomed somewhere she had done nothing to earn. It happened sometimes, in the margins of the world. Strangers fed you because feeding strangers was what the place was for. But this was different. This had a current under it, and she couldn't read where it flowed from.

The old woman from the trailhead — Aya Dolkar, they called her, though whether that was a name or a title Rosalind couldn't tell — sat down across from her on a low stool and looked at her for a long time. Then at Teodor. Then back.

She spoke. Teodor listened, and Rosalind watched something move across his face that she'd never seen there before — not the careful blankness, not the watchfulness. Something older and more tired.

"She's asking," he said, "if you are Edmund Sterling's daughter."

The name landed in the smoky room like a stone dropped down a well. Dex looked up from the cards. Even Okonkwo lifted his head off the quilts.

"How does she know that name?"

Teodor didn't answer right away. He turned his teacup in his hands, once, twice.

"Because Edmund Sterling paid for this roof," he said. "And the school below it. And the bridge over the Braldu that washed out in '19. And the medicine chest in the bonesetter's house, restocked every year for eleven years, through a man in Skardu who never asked questions." He looked up. "Because every autumn, for as long as most of these people can remember, money has come here from England with no letter attached. And because when I arrived last spring and showed them my father's handwriting, Aya Dolkar decided I was family."

Rosalind set down the tea. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd have expected shaking.

"My father has never been to Pakistan," she said. "He told me once he'd never been east of Istanbul."

"He hasn't." Teodor's voice was gentle, which was worse. "That was rather the point."

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down and the villagers had withdrawn to their own houses and Okonkwo slept under the bonesetter's poultice, Rosalind climbed the ladder to the roof.

The rain had stopped. The clouds had torn open along the western ridgeline, and the peaks above the valley stood up into a sky rinsed clean and cold, catching the last light like blades. Somewhere below, a goat complained. Somewhere above, ice shifted in a register too deep to hear, felt only in the teeth.

Teodor came up a few minutes later, moving carefully on the wet timber, and sat down at the other end of the roof without asking if the space was taken. That was one of the things she'd decided she trusted about him: he never filled silence just to own it.

"Eleven years," she said.

"Give or take."

"The accident was ten years ago."

"I know."

"So the payments started after." She worked it forward, the way she'd work a tide table. "After Eli died. After the survey was buried. He started sending money to the porters' village on the route he swore he'd never look at again."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Teodor pulled his collar up against the cold. When he finally spoke, he spoke to the mountains, not to her.

"You should understand what your father was, before the end. Not the famous man — the working one. I was his assistant for six years. I catalogued his samples, typed his letters, drove him to airports. He was brilliant and vain and careless with people, and he treated me well, which I've learned is not the same as treating people well generally." A pause. "But the Karakoram changed him. The final survey — the real one, the one Vane wants erased — he came back from it wrong. Quiet. He'd sit with the maps for hours and not touch them. And then one day he told me to set up a payment schedule to a village called Askari, and he said—" Teodor stopped. Started again. "He said, *I left weight there, Teodor. Weight doesn't burn.*"

Rosalind's throat tightened. "Eli's body was never recovered."

"No."

"And my father never said his name. Not once. Not at the funeral, not after. Ten years, and he acted like Eli had been a stranger who died in someone else's war." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she used on crews when the weather turned. "I decided he was a coward. I built my whole life on that decision. Every contract with the name struck out, every port I didn't stay in — all of it downstream of the fact that my father couldn't say my brother's name out loud."

Teodor was quiet for a while. Below them, light moved behind the schoolhouse window — someone grading papers late, or a child reading by lamplight.

"Did you ever ask him why?" he said.

"I haven't spoken to him in nine years."

"Then you decided he was a coward without ever checking the evidence." He said it without accusation, the way he'd note that a rope was frayed. "I'm not telling you he wasn't. Maybe he was. But I watched a proud man spend eleven years paying a debt he couldn't speak of, to people he couldn't visit, for a son he couldn't mourn publicly — because mourning publicly would mean explaining how Eli died, and explaining would mean naming the artifact, and naming the artifact would bring Vane's backers down on everyone Eli had loved. Including you." He turned to look at her then, and his eyes in the dark were steady and sad. "Silence isn't always cowardice, Rosalind. Sometimes it's a load-bearing wall. You pull it out and see what the house does."

"That's a generous reading."

"I'm a generous man. Ask anyone I've paid."

It startled a laugh out of her — short, cracked, real. First laugh since the ice cave. She pressed her sleeve against her eyes quickly, but he'd already looked away, giving her that much privacy, and she was grateful.

"Aya Dolkar wants to talk to you tomorrow," he said. "There's something she keeps for the Sterling family. She wouldn't say what. She said—" he translated slowly, tasting the words, "*the daughter should carry what the father sent.*"

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know. But she's been waiting eleven years to give it to someone, and she's outlived two husbands doing it, so I'd wear good boots."

* * *

Morning came hard and blue, the monsoon visibly dying on the horizon, the air scrubbed thin and bright. Aya Dolkar led Rosalind up a path behind the village to a small stone hut with a door of woven willow, and inside, on a shelf above the smoke line, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord gone brittle with age, was a wooden box.

She set it in Rosalind's hands and spoke at length, and Teodor, who had followed them up and stood filling the doorway, translated in a low voice.

"She says your father sent this the second year. A man carried it over the pass on foot, in winter, which nearly killed him, and Aya Dolkar promised to keep it until a Sterling came for it. She says she hoped it would be the brother." A pause. "She's sorry it isn't."

Rosalind untied the cord. Inside the box, under a layer of wool, lay a brass compass — Eli's compass, the one their father had given him at sixteen, engraved on the back with words she'd memorized at eight years old and hadn't let herself think of in a decade. Beneath the compass, folded into a square gone soft with handling, was a letter.

Her father's handwriting. Unsent. Addressed simply: *To whoever finds my son.*

She read it standing in the smoke-dark hut while the old woman watched her with walnut-face patience, and by the third paragraph her vision blurred, and by the fifth she was sitting on the floor without remembering sitting down, and Aya Dolkar put a hand on her head the way you'd steady a child, and Rosalind Sterling, who had measured her life in miles precisely so she'd never have to measure it in this, wept for her father and her brother in a porter's hut four thousand meters up a mountain neither of them had ever properly left.

The letter didn't ask forgiveness. That was the thing that broke her open. It explained — the artifact, the theft, the men who'd wanted it buried, the choice he'd made to keep Eli's route secret even from his own children, believing distance was protection. It ended: *I am not a good man. I am trying, too late, to be an honest one. If you are reading this, I failed at both.*

* * *

She came down the path an hour later with red eyes and the box under her arm, and found the crew packed and waiting, and found Priya looking east with binoculars and an expression Rosalind had learned, over two hundred miles of gorge, to dread.

"How bad?" she asked.

"Bad's relative." Priya handed her the glasses. "See the col, north ridge, where the cloud's breaking?"

Rosalind found it. The notch in the skyline, sharp as a cut. And on the notch, small with distance but unmistakable, movement. Figures. Four of them, maybe five, roped together, moving with the unhurried competence of men who knew exactly where they were going.

Behind them. Two days behind, maybe less, if the weather held the way it was breaking.

"They're not following our trail," Priya said. "They're taking the direct line. They know where we're going."

Rosalind lowered the glasses. In her pocket, Eli's compass pressed its engraved back against her palm, and she thought about load-bearing walls, and houses, and what her father had tried to build out of silence and bank drafts, and how Vane's men were coming to finish pulling it all down.

"Dex," she said. "How fast can this village move us uphill?"

The kid grinned, and it wasn't entirely a nice grin. "Aya Dolkar's grandson says there's a goat track over the shoulder of the peak that cuts a day off the glacier approach. Says his grandfather ran salt on it." He paused. "Says it's not really a track."

"Nothing worth carrying ever is." Rosalind shouldered her pack and looked up at the white shoulder of the mountain, where the route to the ice cave opened like a held breath. "Wake Okonkwo. We leave within the hour — and somebody find out if the goats mind company."
