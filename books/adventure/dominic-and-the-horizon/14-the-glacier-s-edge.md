## 14. The Glacier's Edge

The rain stopped on the ninth morning the way a fight stops — not with resolution but with both sides too exhausted to continue.

Rosalind woke to silence so complete it felt like a sound in itself. For nine days the gorge had roared around them, water hammering the hull of the *Mariana Q* where they'd wedged her against a shelf of rock above the drowned village landing, and now there was nothing but drip and settle and the distant, mineral groan of the mountains doing what mountains do.

She climbed out onto the deck. The sky was rinsed clean, high and blue at the top, still bruised purple along the ridgelines where the last clouds were being pulled apart. Everything below was brown water and wreckage — a footbridge gone, terraces sheared, the river running fat and yellow with the mountains' leavings. But up. Up was clear.

"Route's open," Priya said from the wheelhouse doorway, a mug of tea steaming in her hand. She'd slept maybe three hours; Rosalind could see it in the way she leaned against the frame like the frame was holding her up. "Teodor thinks four days. Maybe five if we're lucky."

"We're not lucky."

"No," Priya agreed. "But we're fast."

* * *

They left the boat with old Bahadur, the village headman's brother, who had spent the storm nights ferrying stranded families past them in a dugout and who now looked at the *Mariana Q*'s leaking seams with the expression of a man being asked to babysit a dying animal. He promised nothing except that no one would touch her, which in these valleys was worth more than a contract.

Dex wanted to stay with the boat. Rosalind saw it happen — the kid calculating, weighing the boat against the mountain, choosing the thing that couldn't follow them uphill. She put a hand on Dex's shoulder before the argument could form.

"You're coming."

"The boat—"

"Is wood and diesel. It floats or it doesn't. You're the only one of us who can read a slope." That wasn't entirely true — Teodor could read a slope like scripture — but Dex needed to hear it said, and besides, it was true enough. Ten days ago Dex had found Eli's journal hidden in a false bottom of the survey crate, and since then the kid had carried it everywhere, wrapped in oilcloth against the chest, like a second heartbeat. Rosalind hadn't asked for it back. She didn't know what she'd do with it yet, and until she knew, it was safer in Dex's hands than in hers.

Okonkwo redistributed the medical kit for altitude — blister packs, the syringe of dexamethasone he'd been hoarding, salt tablets. Priya stripped the expedition down to rope, hardware, stove, tent, and the survey documents themselves, which rode in a waterproof tube strapped across Rosalind's back where she'd feel it if anyone tried to take it off her.

Teodor stood at the rail looking north, toward the white wall at the end of the valley, and did not pack anything because he'd never unpacked much to begin with. His bag was a rectangle of straps and buckles that seemed to have been designed by someone who hated comfort. He caught her watching him.

"What?" he said.

"Nothing. You look like a man going home."

Something moved across his face — not quite a flinch, not quite a smile. "I worked up here two winters," he said. "Before your father hired me. The ice gets into you. Like sand in a watch."

"And after?"

"After, you can't keep time anymore." He shouldered his bag. "We should move while the trail's still draining."

* * *

The first day they climbed out of the flood zone through country that looked like it had been skinned. Landslides had come down everywhere the monsoon had found a weakness, which was everywhere, and twice they crossed slopes that were still moving — slow, viscous rivers of gravel that groaned under their boots. Teodor led. He read the mountain the way Okonkwo read a pulse: hand out, head tilted, waiting for the tell.

At midday they passed the first cairn of Eli's route — a stack of stones Rosalind recognized from a map drawn ten years ago in her brother's cramped, slanted hand. Someone had rebuilt it since. Fresh stones, pale where the lichen hadn't reached.

"Porters maintain this line," Teodor said, when he saw her stop. "Your father paid for it. Every year, even after—" He stopped himself, but Rosalind heard the rest: even after the accident, even after he stopped speaking to you, even after everyone decided the Sterling name was finished.

She touched the top stone. It was warm from the sun. She walked on.

By late afternoon the valley narrowed and the air changed — thinner, colder, smelling of stone dust and something almost sweet, the smell of old ice. Behind them, far below, the river ran the wrong color. Rosalind looked back once and saw, on the trail they'd abandoned at the confluence, a line of men moving fast and low, no pack animals, no tents. Six of them. Maybe seven.

"Hunters," Priya said quietly. She'd seen them too. Of course she had; Priya saw everything mechanical and most things human.

"They'll make the moraine camp by dark," Teodor said. "We're four hours ahead. Tomorrow, three."

"Then tomorrow we climb in the dark," Rosalind said.

Nobody argued. That was the thing about her crew — her strange, assembled family of strays — that they argued about everything except the moments that mattered, when they simply moved.

* * *

They made the moraine camp as full dark came down, a chaos of boulders dumped by the glacier centuries ago, and Rosalind allowed one fire, small, shielded in a rock hollow, long enough to melt snow and get hot food into everyone. Okonkwo checked Dex's fingers — early frostnip on two of them, caught in time — and dosed him with paracetamol and a lecture. Dex took both without complaint, which frightened Rosalind more than any argument would have. The kid had gone quiet since the journal. Not scared-quiet. Loaded-quiet.

She sat down next to Dex on the windward side of the boulder, where the cold kept people honest.

"Give me a page," she said.

Dex looked at her. In the firelight the kid's face was all angles and chapped skin, sixteen going on forty. "You sure?"

"I'm sure I've been avoiding it for ten years and I'm done."

Dex unwrapped the oilcloth and handed over the journal without letting go of it entirely, the way you hand someone a knife handle-first. Rosalind opened it to a page near the end, dated nine days before the accident.

*V. came up again today. Says the client wants the eastern chamber logged as sterile — nothing of interest, close the record. But the chamber isn't sterile. There's a piece in there older than anything I've ever touched, and it didn't come up the mountain by itself. Somebody brought it in and meant to take it out and got stopped. R. would know what to do. R. always knows. I keep writing to her in my head and not sending it because she'd come, and if she comes she'll be in the middle of whatever V. is actually doing.*

Rosalind read it three times. The words didn't change. Her brother had known. Had named her, in ink, nine days before the ice took him — had thought of her, and protected her from a distance, the way she'd spent ten years protecting his memory from every angle but the true one.

"He knew," she said aloud. Not a question.

"He knew," Dex said. "And he stayed anyway. Because leaving meant the artifact went out unrecorded." Dex's voice cracked on the last word and steadied. "I think that's why he stayed."

Rosalind closed the journal and gave it back, and found that her eyes were wet and that the cold was freezing them at the corners, and that none of it mattered, because tomorrow they had a glacier to cross and men behind them, and grief, like everything else on this mountain, would have to be carried, not set down.

* * *

They climbed through the pre-dawn black by headlamp, roped in pairs on the steeper ground, and came up over the lateral moraine just as the light hit the ice field, and Rosalind forgot, for one full breath, how to breathe.

The glacier filled the valley ahead of them like a river that had been told to hold still and had obeyed for ten thousand years, barely. It was gray-white and fissured, seamed with blue shadow, and along its whole snout — the terminus, where it ended in a cliff of dirty ice sixty meters tall — it was moving. You couldn't see it move. You could hear it. A deep, irregular percussion, like artillery fought to a draw somewhere under the world. Every few minutes a section of the face let go and fell, and the sound rolled up the valley and came back changed.

"There," Teodor said. He pointed to a notch high on the glacier's western flank, where the ice pressed against the valley wall. "The high route. Your father's survey crosses there — the ice bridge over the crevasse field. In monsoon, the meltwater undermines it and it drops. When the melt stops—" he shrugged, "—it refreezes. For a few days it will hold a man's weight. Then not."

"How many days?"

"Three. Four if the cold comes early." He glanced at the sky, reading clouds the way other men read newspapers. "It won't come early."

Behind them, down-valley, the percussion of calving continued, patient as a clock. Ahead, the notch. Somewhere between the two, six or seven armed men closing at a rate they could calculate to the hour.

Priya did the arithmetic out loud, because Priya always did the arithmetic. "They gain on us on flat ground. We gain on them on steep. The ice bridge is steep. If we hit it tonight, we're across before they're on the ice at all."

"And if the bridge is already gone?" Okonkwo asked.

"Then we've walked four days to die at a very scenic dead end," Priya said, "and I'd like that noted in the log as your fault for asking."

Okonkwo smiled the tired smile of a man who has heard worse bedside prognoses. Rosalind checked the tube on her back, checked Dex's ropes, checked the horizon, and gave the order to move onto the ice.

* * *

The glacier was a country unto itself. They crossed it all day, weaving through seracs the size of houses, following Teodor's memory and the faint ghost of old flagging tape — red plastic strips gone pink and brittle, tied to stakes by survey teams years ago, some of them, Rosalind realized with a lurch, by her father's own hands. Twice the ice spoke under their feet, a cracking boom that traveled up through their boots and teeth, and twice they froze and waited and the mountain decided, this time, to keep its arguments to itself.

In the afternoon they found the crevasse field, and with it the bridge.

It was still there. A spine of ice arched over a gap wide enough to swallow the entire crew and everything they loved, glazed with fresh freeze, blue-black and glassy at the edges. It looked impossible. It looked, Rosalind thought, exactly like the kind of thing her brother would have crossed without hesitation and written about later in handwriting that slanted like it was leaning into wind.

Teodor tested it first — alone, weight shifting incrementally, listening to the ice with his whole body. Then he anchored a fixed line and they crossed one at a time, boots ringing on the frozen surface, the gap breathing cold up at them from thirty meters down.

Dex crossed last, and halfway across, stopped.

"Dex." Rosalind's voice came out flat and hard. "Move."

"I hear something." The kid's head was cocked. "Under the ice. Like—"

The boom came then, real and enormous, from down-valley — the glacier's snout letting go of a piece the size of a cathedral — and the vibration ran the length of the ice field and up through the bridge, and for one suspended second Rosalind watched the arch flex, visibly flex, a shudder passing through it like pain through a body.

Dex ran the rest of the way. Rosalind hauled the kid off the ice by the pack straps, and they lay on solid rock at the far side, chests heaving, while the echo of the calving rolled away up the valley and came back smaller each time, like something falling asleep.

"It held," Dex said finally.

"It held today," Rosalind said. She looked back the way they'd come. Far below, at the edge of the crevasse field, tiny as insects, the line of men had reached the glacier's edge. They'd stopped at the boundary where the ice began, and even at this distance she could see them doing what Teodor was doing — reading the surface, timing the booms, waiting for the ice to give them a window.

Or waiting for her team to be committed to the crossing, with no room left to run.

"They'll cross at first light," Teodor said beside her. He'd come up silent as ever. "Maybe sooner. The bridge won't take that many crossings in quick succession. It's refrozen, not healed. There's a difference."

"Then we don't sleep long," Rosalind said.

Above them, another hour's climb, the notch waited — the high route, the pass, the descent on the far side toward the ice cave where her father's final survey lay cached in the dark, where Eli had died, where the truth had been frozen in place for ten years like a fly in amber. Three hours' hard climbing. Maybe four.

Below them, the hunters. Above them, the pass. Between them and safety, a bridge that would hold for days only, and a glacier that was keeping its own count.

Rosalind shouldered the tube with the survey documents and started upward.

Behind her, down in the blue dusk of the ice field, the glacier calved again — and this time, the sound it made was different. Longer. Closer to the bridge.
