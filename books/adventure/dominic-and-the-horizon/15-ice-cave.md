## 15. Ice Cave

The cave mouth was a black seam in the glacier's shoulder, no wider than a coffin stood on end, and Rosalind found it the way her father had taught her to find everything: by looking for what didn't belong.

Ice moved. That was the first law of the Karakoram — ice moved, and anything that stayed still inside it was either dead or hidden. The seam above the cave had melted and refrozen in a pattern too clean, too vertical, a drip-line like a curtain drawn over something. Ten years ago she'd have walked past it. Ten years ago she hadn't yet learned that her family's whole history was made of things hidden behind ordinary surfaces.

"Airflow," Teodor said quietly beside her. He held up a strip of survey tape and let it hang. It drifted inward, faint but constant. "There's a second opening somewhere. The cave breathes."

Priya crouched at the seam with the headlamp, testing the edges with an ice pick. "It'll take me twenty minutes to widen this enough for a person. Thirty if we want it wide enough to get out fast." She didn't say *if we need to get out fast*. Everyone heard it anyway.

Behind them, down the moraine, the wind carried the sound of the glacier working — that deep internal groaning, like a ship's hull under strain. Somewhere south of them, Vane's remaining men were moving. Dex had spotted their cook fire at dusk, two ridgelines back, close enough that Okonkwo had insisted they bank their own and eat cold.

"Twenty minutes," Rosalind said. "Then I go in first."

"You don't have to—" Okonkwo started.

"I do." She said it without drama, which was the only way she could say it at all. "It's my father's cache. And whatever's in there—" Her voice caught on the edge of something. She swallowed it. "Whatever's in there belongs to my brother too."

Priya worked in silence after that, chips of blue ice flying, and Rosalind stood with her hands jammed in her pockets and watched the tape flutter and thought about how she had spent ten years measuring her life in miles because miles couldn't lie to her. A mile was a mile whether you crossed it alone or not. Grief wasn't like that. Grief changed its distance every time you looked at it.

The seam opened with a crack like a rifle shot, and cold air poured out, ancient and mineral, smelling of stone and deep time.

* * *

Inside, the cave was a cathedral that had never been meant for worship.

Teodor's headlamp swept across walls of layered ice, blue and green and white, compressed snow from centuries of winters, and the light went through it rather than off it, so the whole chamber seemed lit from within, like standing inside a lantern. Their boots crunched on old scree. Their breath hung in clouds that rose toward a ceiling lost in darkness.

"There." Dex pointed, and his voice came out hushed, the way people talk in churches and hospitals.

Fifty meters in, where the cave narrowed, someone had built a wall. Not ice — stone. Dry-stacked rocks mortared with frozen meltwater, deliberate, careful, the work of a man who knew he might not come back and wanted whatever he left behind to survive him. A man who built things to last. Rosalind knew those hands. She'd watched them mend nets, repair engines, fold maps along their creases until the creases wore through.

"Eli built this," she said.

Nobody argued. Nobody had to. Teodor stepped up beside the wall and ran his gloved fingers over the stones, and when he spoke his voice was rough. "Your father described his technique once. He said Eli stacked rocks the way other people write letters."

Rosalind took the pick from Priya's belt and began pulling stones.

Her gloves were useless; she stripped them off and worked bare-handed until her fingers burned, and the cold bit into the cracks of her knuckles, and she didn't care, because underneath the wall was a canvas rucksack, army-green, mildewed at the seams, with a red thread tied around one strap. Eli had tied red thread on everything. He said it was so he could find his gear in the dark. She had told him once that was the stupidest system she'd ever heard, and he'd grinned and said, *Works though, doesn't it.*

She had the bag out and in her arms before she realized she was crying.

She didn't make a sound. That was the discipline of it, the terrible discipline — ten years of practice at crying silently, in bunks and bathrooms and the dark behind wheelhouses, so that no one would ask her questions she couldn't answer. But the cave was small and full of people who loved her, and Dex put his hand on her shoulder blade, awkward and fierce, and left it there.

* * *

They cleared a space against the ice wall and Okonkwo lit the camp stove, more for light than warmth, and set out tea nobody drank. The rucksack held three bundles wrapped in oilcloth: the survey folio, sealed in waxed canvas, dry as the day it was packed; a tin box of instruments and film canisters; and a notebook. Cheap, spiral-bound, water-warped at the corners. Eli's handwriting on the cover in grease pencil: *R. — if you're reading this, I'm sorry and also you're welcome.*

A laugh tore out of her, ugly and sudden, and turned into something else halfway through.

"You don't have to read it aloud," Priya said gently.

"Yes, I do." Rosalind wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "If I read it alone I'll make it into whatever I need it to be. You all heard Vane's version. You all heard my father's silence. Somebody should hear the truth in a human voice, out loud, where it counts." She looked around the circle of faces — Priya steady, Okonkwo solemn, Dex pale and unblinking, Teodor with his jaw tight as cable. "All right?"

No one said no.

She opened the notebook. The pages were soft as cloth. Eli's writing slanted hard to the right, always hurrying, always going somewhere.

*"Day 6. Dad's hands are bad again. He won't say it but he can't hold a pencil past noon, so I'm doing the survey entries and he checks them. He keeps saying this is the last one, the one that sets us up, and I keep not asking him why a survey needs armed porters."*

Rosalind stopped. Looked up. "Armed porters."

Teodor nodded slowly. "Vane's backers provided security. Your father protested it in writing. I filed the letter myself."

She read on.

*"Day 9. Found out what the 'client' actually wants. It's not the route. It's not the minerals. There's a site up-ice — a shrine, maybe older, maybe looted decades ago and stashed — and the client wants it documented just enough to own the story and then erased. Dad figured it out two days ago and hasn't slept since. He says we document everything. Everything, and multiple copies, and we get it out through channels they don't control. I've never seen him scared before. Now I have."*

The stove hissed. Somewhere far above, the glacier ticked and settled, a house creaking in the night.

*"Day 11. The porters' headman came to Dad privately. Told him men were paid to make sure nothing leaves this valley except what the client approves. Dad sent the first copy down with the headman's nephew tonight, wrapped in a prayer flag bundle. If you ever meet a family in the valley with money they can't explain, Rosie — that's us. That's Dad trying to be decent with the only currency he has left."*

Rosalind's throat closed. Chapter thirteen. The village. The school roof, the new bridge cables, the clinic supplies that had arrived for years, unexplained. Her father, dying in a hospital bed a world away, had been paying a debt in installments for a decade, and she had read it as abandonment.

*"Day 13. They know about the copies. One of the porters didn't come back from the lower camp. Dad wants to go down. I told him no — they'd take him apart looking for the folio and he'd give it up in an hour, everyone knows he would, he folds like wet paper when it's people instead of principles. So I'm taking the cache up to the ice cave tomorrow and I'm going to make it look like it never existed. Then we walk out clean and blow the whole thing open from London. That's the plan. I'm writing it down so it stays a plan and doesn't turn into a hero thing. Eli. Stop making it a hero thing."*

Rosalind lowered the page. Her hands were shaking.

"He went back," she said. "He got the cache here, and then—"

"Read," Teodor said softly. "There's more."

*"Day 14. Cave secured. Wall built. On my way down I saw them waiting at the moraine — four men, not porters, wrong boots, wrong guns. They weren't following Dad. They were following me. They let me build the wall. They wanted to know where it was, and now they do, and the only reason I'm writing this instead of running is that if I run they go down and take Dad apart to find out what I hid. So. Hero thing. Sorry, Rosie.*

*If someone finds this: the folio is real, the artifact is stolen, the client's name is in Dad's sealed letter inside the tin. Don't trust anyone who offers you money for it. Trust the ones who offer you nothing.*

*I'm not scared. Okay, I'm scared. But I keep thinking about the horizon, how you always said it's not a wall, it's an invitation. I'm going to walk out there and be very boring and very cooperative and very much alive, and in six months we'll laugh about this in some dockside bar while you pretend you're not glad to see me.*

*Tell Dad he was right about the copies.*

*Tell Rosie she was right about everything else.*

*— E."*

The last page was dated the day before the accident. The official report — the report Rosalind had read a hundred times, the report that had built the wall between her and her father — said Eli Sterling died in a crevasse fall during an unauthorized solo excursion. Negligence. Poor judgment. *The son's instability*, one evaluator had written, and her father had never contested a word of it, and she had spent ten years believing her brother had been careless, and her father complicit in covering his shame.

He hadn't been careless. He'd been a decoy. He'd walked down that moraine carrying the location of the truth in his head, offering himself up so that a sick old man could walk away, and the men in the wrong boots had made sure the decoy never talked — and her father, broken-handed and terrified, had signed whatever he was told to sign, and buried his son twice: once in the ice, and once in a lie.

Rosalind closed the notebook.

For a long moment nobody moved. The stove hissed. The ice glowed.

Then she stood up, and the fury that came off her was so pure and so quiet that Dex later said it was the scariest thing he'd ever seen — scarier than the storm, scarier than the gunfire on the river.

"They didn't kill him for the folio," she said. "They killed him because he wouldn't give them the location. And then they let my father live with the story that it was his fault. That was the payment. That was the mercy." She picked up the tin box and held it like something with a pulse. "And Marcus Vane has been on their payroll ever since, tidying up, and he hired me — he hired *me* — to carry the eraser."

"Rosie." Okonkwo's voice, low. "Breathe."

"I am breathing." She was. Fast and shallow, but she was. "Here's what happens now. We photograph every page. Three copies, three carriers, separated routes. The original goes out with me. And when Vane's men come up that moraine—"

She stopped. Because Teodor had gone rigid, his head tilted toward the cave mouth, and a moment later she heard it too: voices. Distant, distorted by ice and echo, but unmistakable. More than one. Moving closer.

Dex killed the stove. In the sudden dark the cave glowed blue around them, beautiful and cold as the inside of a lung.

"How long?" Rosalind whispered.

Priya was already at the entrance, flattened against the wall, peering through the widened seam. "Two minutes. Maybe less. They brought lights."

Rosalind slid Eli's notebook inside her jacket, against her heart, where it should have been for ten years.

"Then they're early," she said, "and we're not done."
