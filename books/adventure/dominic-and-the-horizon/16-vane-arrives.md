## 16. Vane Arrives

The wind changed first.

Rosalind had spent enough years in high country to know that mountains spoke before they moved — a shift in the pitch of the wind through the seracs, a pressure drop you felt in your fillings, the light going thin and metallic like foil. She was folding Eli's journal back into its oilcloth when she heard it: not the glacier, but boots on scree. Many boots. Deliberate.

"Dex." Her voice came out low and even, and she was proud of that. "Company."

Dex was up from the cache in an instant, all elbows and alarm, pressing his eye to the gap in the ice wall they'd used as a door. "Three," he whispered. "No — four. And someone's hanging back at the moraine with a radio."

"Vane?"

"I can't see his face. But he walks like he owns the ground."

He did walk like that. He always had. Rosalind remembered Marcus Vane crossing a dig site in Uzbekistan ten years ago, younger then, hungrier-looking, moving through other people's excavations like a man doing them a favor by standing on them. The hunger had refined itself since. What came around the shoulder of blue ice now, picking his way over the calving debris in a charcoal down jacket that probably cost more than Priya's entire toolkit, was hunger with a tailor.

"Rosalind." He stopped six meters out, hands visible, empty. A courtesy. A performance of one, anyway. Behind him, two men she didn't recognize flanked wide — one drifting toward the boat-side of the cave mouth, one toward the high rocks where Okonkwo had cached the medical kit. Flanking without appearing to flank. Vane had learned that trick from her, or she'd learned it from him; she could no longer remember which, and it didn't matter now.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look like your father." He smiled, and it almost reached his eyes, which was the most dangerous thing about him. "I mean that as a compliment. I always have."

Teodor stepped out of the shadow of the ice arch beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched. Vane's gaze flicked to him and something moved behind it — recognition, calculation, a small cold satisfaction, like a man confirming a receipt.

"And there he is," Vane said softly. "Teodor Lindqvist. I wondered which side of the mountain you'd wash up on."

"The wrong one," Teodor said. "For you."

"We'll see."

* * *

The wind gusted through the cave mouth, carrying the smell of the ice — that ancient, mineral breath, cold stone and deep time. Inside the cache behind them lay the survey, her father's final work, and Eli's journal, and the truth about what had been taken from the Hunza valley in 1979 and who had paid for it to disappear. Outside stood four men and a man-shaped problem named Marcus Vane.

"Before we do anything regrettable," Vane said, settling onto a boulder as if it were a club chair, "let me tell you what happened to my advance team."

Rosalind went still. "You know."

"I found them yesterday. Or what was left. Two days ahead of me the whole way, and they still managed to die stupidly." His voice didn't change register, didn't soften, and that was how she knew how angry he actually was. "Avalanche, officially. Except avalanches don't take a man's radio and leave his satellite phone. Someone wanted them silent and unfindable. My backers are thorough people, Rosalind. You've met their work. You just haven't met them."

"And you brought us here anyway."

"I brought you here because you're the best field leader on three continents and because I genuinely believed — believe — that this ends better for everyone if you're standing inside the tent rather than outside it in the weather." He spread his hands. "But yes. Also because dead porters raise questions and missing rivals don't."

Priya made a sound behind him — not quite a laugh. Vane didn't turn. That took discipline, or contempt, and with Vane it was usually both.

"So let's talk about mercy," he said.

"There's a word I haven't heard from you since Karachi."

"Karachi was business. This is mercy." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the performance dropped and she saw the actual man underneath — tired, fifty-three, a man who had spent thirty years climbing toward a summit that kept receding and had finally decided to buy the mountain instead. "Here is what I'm offering. Not to them. To you."

"Don't," Teodor said quietly.

"Let her hear it." Vane's eyes never left hers. "The documents come to me. All of them — survey, journal, photographs, whatever Dex found in that cache that made your face look like that. In exchange: the expedition report goes out under Sterling and Vane. Joint credit. Your father's name rehabilitated, your brother's death ruled what it actually was — no more 'exposure,' no more 'pilot error,' no more ten years of people looking at you sideways at conferences wondering if the Sterlings are cursed or careless. The artifact goes back to the valley through proper channels, quiet, dignified, done. And the men who killed those porters—" he tipped his head toward the ice, "—and very possibly your brother, get handled by people whose job is handling things. You walk away clean. Everyone walks away clean. That's the offer."

The wind filled the silence.

It was a good offer. That was the obscene thing about it. Rosalind stood there with Eli's journal burning a hole through the oilcloth against her ribs and admitted to herself, in the privacy of her own skull, that every single thing Vane had just described was a thing she wanted. Her father's name restored. Eli's death given its true shape instead of the shape the inquiry had pressed it into, flat and blameless and unbearable. Ten years of running — of measuring her life in kilometers because years were too heavy — ended, retroactively justified.

And all it would cost was handing the only leverage that made any of it real to the man whose money had put killers on this glacier.

"You've rehearsed that," she said.

"Every night since Gilgit."

"I believe you." She crouched down slowly, putting herself below his eye line, which she knew he'd hate, and picked up a shard of ice, turning it in her fingers. "Here's what I keep snagging on, Marcus. If the artifact goes back 'through proper channels' — whose channels? Yours? The same channels that funded a cleanup team? If my father's name gets rehabilitated, who writes the rehabilitation? Because I've read enough salvaged archives to know that provenance is everything. A story is worth exactly as much as whoever controls the telling."

"You'd control it. Jointly."

"With you holding the pen."

"With anyone, someone holds the pen." He stood, restless now, and walked to the edge of the cave mouth, looking out at the glacier — the great white river of it, cracked and groaning, holding its breath before the monsoon's end set it loose. "You think I'm the villain of this. I've watched you think it for ten years and I've let you, because it was cheaper than the truth. The truth is that I was in the room in 2014 when the decision got made to bury the survey. I argued against it. I lost. And then I stayed, and took the money, and told myself I'd fix it later, and later became a decade, because that's what later does." He turned back to her. "I am offering you the chance to fix it now, cleanly, without anyone else dying on this ice. Including you. Including that boy."

"Dex stays out of this."

"He's been in it since he signed on. So has Priya. So has your doctor. The only question left is whether they're in it alive."

Behind her, she heard Dex shift his weight, and Okonkwo's breathing change — the medic had come up silently from the lower cache and was standing at the ice wall with his arms crossed, watching Vane the way he watched a wound he hadn't decided whether to open.

"And if I say no?" Rosalind asked.

Vane sighed, and it was almost gentle. "Then I take the documents off your body, or off the bodies of whoever's carrying them, and my backers send the next team, and the next, until this glacier gives up what it's holding. You can't win this by being right, Rosalind. Right isn't a force multiplier. I'm offering you the only version of this story where everybody keeps breathing."

* * *

She looked at Teodor.

He was watching her with an expression she was beginning to be able to read — the stillness of a man who had already decided something and was waiting to see if she'd arrive at the same place on her own. On the boat, in the flooded gorge, he'd told her the truth in pieces, letting her assemble it, refusing to hand her conclusions. He was doing it now. *Your call,* that stillness said. *I'll back whichever one you make.*

That was its own kind of temptation. She noticed that too. Vane offered erasure; Teodor offered witness. It was astonishing how much heavier the second one was.

"My brother wrote forty pages in that journal," she said. "Did you know that? Forty pages. He was twenty-six and he wrote like a man building a case, because he was. He documented the crate transfers at Skardu. He photographed the manifest. He hid copies in three locations because he didn't trust—" her voice caught, and she let it catch, let Vane hear it, "—because he didn't trust the adults. He was right not to."

"Rosalind—"

"He died eleven days after writing the last page. Exposure, the inquiry said. Alone, the inquiry said. And you're standing here telling me that if I give you the paper version of his last act, you'll make sure the world learns what happened to him." She stood up. "You understand why I don't believe you. You're asking me to trade the evidence for the verdict. Nobody's ever gotten the verdict first. Not once, not anywhere, not in any dig I've ever worked. You trade the verdict for the evidence, and then the evidence disappears, and the verdict becomes whatever the man holding the pen says it was."

"That's cynical."

"That's stratigraphy," she said. "Everything buried tells you about the layer above it."

Something flickered across Vane's face — respect, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. He glanced at his men. The one by the high rocks had stopped pretending to scout and was simply waiting, and the waiting had a texture to it that Rosalind recognized from bad contracts and worse endings.

"Last chance for the merciful version," Vane said.

"No."

"No conditions? No counteroffer?"

"One condition." She held his eyes. "You turn around, you walk down to the moraine, you get on that radio, and you tell your backers the survey was destroyed in the calving. You tell them it's gone. Then you go home, Marcus, and you live with it, the way my father lived with it, the way I lived with it. That's the mercy. I'm offering it to you."

The silence stretched. Somewhere deep in the glacier, something boomed — a long, tectonic groan, the ice adjusting its grip, and every face at the cave mouth turned toward the sound and then back again.

"You always did mistake stubbornness for principle," Vane said quietly.

"And you always mistook purchase for possession." She lifted the oilcloth bundle slightly, so he could see it, so he could see that she would rather feed it to the crevasse than hand it to him. "This doesn't go to you. It goes to the valley. It goes to the press. It goes wherever it has to go to make sure that the next crew you hire to climb up here and erase something finds out that the last one talked."

Vane looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something she hadn't expected: he nodded, once, slow, like a man closing a ledger.

"All right," he said. "The hard version, then."

He raised two fingers.

And the man by the high rocks brought his rifle up off his shoulder with the smooth economy of someone who had done it many times, and Rosalind was already moving — shoving Dex toward the ice wall, shouting Okonkwo's name — when the glacier answered first.

The boom wasn't a groan this time. It was a crack like the sky splitting along a seam, and the whole face of the serac field above the moraine shuddered, shed a skin of powder snow, and began, with the unhurried majesty of ten thousand tons of compressed time, to fall.

"MOVE!" Vane screamed — at his own men, at hers, at her — and for one suspended second the cave mouth was nothing but motion and white roaring air, and Rosalind understood, with the strange clarity that arrives only at the exact moment everything goes wrong, that the mountain had just entered the negotiation.

And it had not come to bargain.
