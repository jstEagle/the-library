## 17. Calving

The ice cave mouth faced east, which meant the dawn came in like an accusation.

Rosalind stood in it, the survey tube strapped across her back, and watched the glacier below the moraine begin to move. Not move the way ice moves — a centimeter a day, a patience measured in centuries — but move the way a thing moves when it has decided. The seracs above the calving face had been groaning all night, a sound like a ship's hull working in heavy seas, and now, in the first gray light, she could see the fractures running through them like veins in a wrist.

Behind her, the cave was quiet. Eli's last pages were folded against her chest, inside her jacket, over her heart, because she hadn't known where else to put them. The rest of the survey — the photographs, the field notes, the provenance ledger that named the artifact and named the men who'd taken it — was in the tube. Vane wanted the tube. Vane's backers needed it gone. And Vane had four armed men and the only other route off the ice.

"Talk to me, Priya," she said into the radio.

Static. Then, thin and breathless: "Scree slope's gone. They took the lower traverse while we were inside. Ros — they're cutting the angle. Ten minutes, maybe less."

"Okonkwo?"

"Dex is with me in the rocks above the cave mouth. He's scared but he's quiet. Teodor's coming out to you."

She heard boots on ice behind her and didn't turn. She knew his tread now. Ten days on a leaking boat and a week on the glacier will teach you a man's footsteps better than his face.

"They'll want us alive at first," Teodor said, coming to stand beside her. His breath plumed in the cold. "You're the cover story. The Sterling name makes the recovery legitimate. They need you to sign something, photograph something, be photographed. After that—"

"After that we're a cleanup line item." She flexed her hands. They were steady. She noted that with a kind of distant surprise, the way you notice weather. "How much ammunition do they have?"

"Enough. And they don't need much. They need you to come down off that moraine, Rosalind. That's all this is. A negotiation with a cliff for a witness."

She almost smiled. Almost. "You've been spending time with Dex."

"He's teaching me card tricks. I'm teaching him how to read a glacier. We're both terrible students."

Below them, four figures in white-and-gray technical gear came up the ice in a staggered line, moving well, moving fast, roped and disciplined. Professionals. Vane hadn't hired muscle; he'd hired a small army's leftovers. And at the rear, unmistakable even at distance by the way he climbed — hands first, always hands first, like a man pulling the world toward him — Marcus Vane.

The seracs groaned again. Longer this time. A note that went through the soles of her boots.

"Rosalind." Teodor's voice had dropped into the register he used for truths. "I've done the math three times. When that face lets go, the debris run will take the lower traverse. All of it. Everything below the blue ice line."

"I know."

"The traverse is where they are."

"I know that too."

He was quiet for a moment. "And you're standing here doing the math a fourth time."

"Doing the math is not the same as wanting the answer."

"No," he said. "But it's the same as being able to live with it."

She looked at him. Frost in the stubble along his jaw, eyes red-rimmed from a night spent keeping watch, and looking at her with no judgment in him at all — just the terrible patience of a man who had spent years watching her father do exactly this: stand at the edge of a decision and weigh human lives against the truth.

"The evidence doesn't save anyone if we're dead," she said. "And they don't stop being murderers if the mountain does it for them."

"No. They don't."

"Then say the rest of it, Teodor. Say what my father would say."

He exhaled. "Your father would say that the survey is a record, not a weapon. And that the moment you use the mountain to kill, you've written yourself into the ledger with the rest of them."

The seracs cracked. Somewhere above, a chunk the size of a truck went, and the report rolled down the valley like thunder with a grudge. The climbers below stopped, all four, and looked up. Even at that distance she could see the calculation in their posture — the same calculation she was making. Everyone on this ice was doing the same arithmetic this morning. That was the joke of it. That was the whole rotten joke.

Vane's voice came up the slope through a megaphone, absurd and tinny, a man shouting across a cathedral.

"Rosalind! We can do this the easy way!" A pause, wind tearing at the sound. "You walk down with the tube, you sign the recovery affidavit, and you walk away rich. Your father's name gets restored. Everything Eli died for becomes a footnote in a success story! Or you stand up there, and I take it, and nobody writes anything!"

She lifted the radio. "You want to negotiate, Marcus, you come up yourself. Send your dogs home."

A laugh, distorted by the megaphone and the wind. "I'm coming up. But I'm coming up with an escort."

He started climbing.

* * *

He was slower than his men. That was the thing about Vane that ten years of rivalry had taught her and ten years of distance had never let her forget: he was not a coward, whatever else he was. He climbed with the same hands-first hunger he brought to everything, and he came up the moraine alone, unroped, while his four professionals held position on the traverse below — positioned, she understood with cold clarity, exactly where the debris run would land.

He reached the top breathing hard, and for a moment they just looked at each other. Ten years. The last time they'd stood this close, they'd been arguing about a dig in Oman, and Eli had been alive, and she had thought Vane's ambition was a flaw rather than a load-bearing wall.

"You look terrible," he said.

"You look rich."

He smiled, and it was the old smile, the one that had gotten him free drinks on three continents and her forgiveness exactly once. "The affidavit is in my jacket. One signature. That's all. You have no idea what I'm offering you, Rosalind. Your father's name, clean. Your brother's death given meaning. A career—"

"Eli's death given meaning." She said it slowly, tasting it. "You've rehearsed that line."

"I've rehearsed the whole speech."

"I can tell. The middle part's weak."

His smile flickered. Below them, the seracs groaned — that long, hull-working groan — and both of them glanced up at the face, and both of them looked back down at his men on the traverse, and she watched him do the arithmetic. She watched him arrive at the same answer she'd been refusing all morning.

"Move your men," she said quietly.

"The ice isn't going to calve on my schedule."

"It doesn't calve on anyone's schedule. That's the whole point of it. Move your men, Marcus."

"They're professionals. They know the risk. They read the terrain better than—"

"They're standing on a runout zone because you told them to hold a position, and you're standing up here because you decided your life was worth more than theirs, and the difference between you and me, Marcus, is that I've been doing that math for ten years and I know exactly what it costs."

The glacier answered before he could.

It didn't start with the seracs. It started with a sound under their feet — a deep, subterranean crack, like the mountain clearing its throat — and then the whole calving face, the entire wall of it, a hundred meters of blue ice that had been holding itself together since before there were maps, let go.

There was no decision in it. That was the thing no one told you. All her life she'd imagined avalanches and collapses as moments of drama, of choice, of heroism against nature. But it was simply a wall becoming a wave. Tons of ice sheared off the face and fell, and the sound was not thunder, it was the sound of the world's largest door slamming, and then the debris hit the glacier floor and became a moving thing — a gray-white flood of ice and rock and pulverized everything, flowing down and across and over the lower traverse.

Over his men.

Vane turned. She saw it hit him — the full weight of it, the arithmetic completing itself in his face, the four white figures on the white slope vanishing into the white flood. She saw his mouth open. No sound came out that she could hear over the roar.

And then he was moving. Down the moraine. Toward them. Toward the flood. Toward his men, hands first, the way he did everything, as if he could pull them out of it.

She was moving before she decided to.

Later, she would try to reconstruct the decision and fail. There was no decision. There was a man running toward a debris flow, and there was her body doing the only thing it knew how to do, which was the thing Eli had done for her once, on a different mountain, ten years ago — going after someone the mountain had already decided about. She caught Vane at the base of the moraine where the debris fan had already begun to spread its leading edge, a slow gray tongue pushing boulders ahead of it like a plow, and she got a fist in his collar and hauled, and he fought her, actually fought her, screaming a name she couldn't hear, and she screamed back — "They're gone, they're gone, MOVE" — and drove her shoulder into him and turned them both and ran.

The edge of the flow chased them up the moraine. She felt it — felt the ground shake under each surge, felt the cold breath of displaced air — and then Teodor was there, and Okonkwo, and Dex, four sets of hands pulling them onto the high rocks as the debris tongue licked the base of the moraine, reached, and stopped. Spread. Settled. Became, over the course of thirty seconds, a landscape.

Silence, after. The terrible, ringing silence of a valley that had just been rearranged. Somewhere under that gray field were four men with families and paychecks and a professional's understanding of risk, and somewhere under it too was the answer to every question Vane had ever asked.

Vane was on his knees at the edge of the rocks. Not praying. Just kneeling, the way a building kneels when its foundation goes. His hands were in the debris mud. He was looking at the new glacier, the one that hadn't existed five minutes ago.

"I told them to hold," he said. His voice was very small. "I told them to hold the position."

"Yes," Rosalind said. "You did."

"I told them—"

"You did." She crouched in front of him. She should have felt triumph. She had imagined this moment for ten years in a hundred shapes — Vane ruined, Vane exposed, Vane on his knees — and now that it had arrived it felt like nothing she'd imagined. It felt like a body in water. It felt like grief with the serial numbers filed off. "Marcus. Look at me."

He looked at her. And she saw that behind the shock there was something else, something that had been there all along, under the ambition and the charm and the rehearsed speeches: a man who had spent his whole life believing that the world was a ledger and that he was owed a favorable balance, discovering that the ledger had been kept in ice, and ice does not negotiate.

"The tube," he whispered. "The survey. If it comes out, everything — the backers, they'll — my name—"

"Your name is the least of what's buried here today."

"They'll come for it. The backers. They have people in the valley. They have—"

"Then they can explain to the authorities why their advance team is dead in a ravine and their cleanup crew just got erased by a glacier." She stood. Her legs were shaking now, now that it was over, the way they always waited until it was over. "The evidence goes down the mountain. All of it. To the press, to the police, to your backers' competitors if that's what it takes. And your confession goes with it."

He looked up at her. "Or you leave me here."

The offer hung in the thin air. Leave him. Walk away. Let the mountain finish the arithmetic — one more body under the ice, one less witness, and the world simplified by exactly one man. No one would ever know. The glacier kept better secrets than any grave.

Teodor had come up beside her. He said nothing. Okonkwo said nothing. Even Dex, seventeen years old and shaking, said nothing. The choice was entirely, perfectly, horribly hers.

She thought about Eli. She thought about the journal in her jacket, and the last pages, and the sentence she'd read by headlamp at three in the morning: *If I don't come down, tell Ros the mountain didn't take me. Men did. Don't let her become them.*

She held out her hand.

Vane stared at it.

"Get up," Rosalind said. "You're going to confess. Every name, every payment, every order. You're going to do it in a valley station, in front of a police officer, on the record. And then you're going to live with it for a very long time, because dying is easy, Marcus, and my brother doesn't get easy. Neither do you."

The wind came down the glacier and pushed at all of them. Below, the debris field steamed faintly in the new sun, settling, becoming geology. Above, the seracs that remained stood in the blue morning light like the walls of a city that had finally, after ten thousand years, opened its gates.

Vane took her hand.

His was freezing and shaking and lighter than she expected, the hand of a man who had never carried anything heavier than a pen. She hauled him to his feet, and he swayed, and Teodor steadied his other arm, and just like that the four of them — the rival, the stranger, the doctor, the kid — were a roped party on the worst climb of their lives.

"The tube," Vane said, dazed, looking at the strap across her shoulder. "You're really going to—"

"It goes down with us. All of it." She turned her face toward the valley, toward the river, toward the dying father and the unfinished conversation waiting at the bottom of the world. "Eli didn't die so I could bury the truth. He died so I could carry it."

She took the first step down. Behind her, the glacier calved again — smaller, tired, an afterthought — and the sound rolled down the valley ahead of them like a warning, or an escort, and Rosalind Sterling walked into it carrying everything.
