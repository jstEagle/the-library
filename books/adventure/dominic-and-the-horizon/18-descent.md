## 18. Descent

The mountain let them go the way a fist opens — slowly, finger by finger, leaving marks.

Rosalind came down out of the ice on legs that belonged to someone else. Her left arm was strapped to her chest where Vane's man had put a bullet groove through the meat of her shoulder before the glacier finished the argument for all of them. Priya had dressed it with field sutures and half their remaining antibiotics and a look that said *you were lucky* in three languages. The cold had kept the wound honest; now that they were descending into green air, it throbbed like a second pulse.

Behind her, the line of survivors moved at the pace of the slowest body.

That was Teodor's rule, announced once at the cave mouth while the ice dust was still settling: *nobody gets left to be found.* He'd said it quietly, the way he said everything, and Rosalind had understood it wasn't sentiment. It was evidence management. Dead men left on mountains became stories, and stories could be bent. Men carried down alive told the truth whether they wanted to or not.

So they carried everything. The survey cache wrapped in Okonkwo's spare tarp and distributed across four packs so no single fall could take it. Eli's journal against Rosalind's chest, inside her jacket, zipped in. And Marcus Vane, who had stopped being an expedition financier somewhere above the seracs and become cargo.

He walked. That was the strange mercy of it. His ankle was broken — Priya had splinted it with an ice axe shaft and climbing tape — but he refused the litter Dex lashed together from poles and rope, because being carried by these people was a humiliation he couldn't afford even now. So he hopped and leaned and swore through his teeth, one boot dragging, and Rosalind walked behind him for two days watching a proud man learn what gravity thought of him.

The first night down, camped in the boulder field below the moraine, he tried to talk.

"Sterling." His voice had lost its boardroom polish somewhere around the calving. It was just a voice now, scraped raw by wind and by whatever had happened behind his eyes when the ice took his men. "You should know I wasn't going to—"

"Don't." She didn't look up from checking the cache bindings. "Whatever sentence you're building, save it. You'll want it later, and you'll want it to be true."

He was quiet a long time. Then: "Your brother's journal. Did he write about me?"

That surprised her enough to make her look. Vane sat against a boulder with his leg propped, staring at the fire like it owed him something. In the firelight she could see how much the mountain had taken off him in ten days — the tan, the certainty, the expensive scaffolding of a man who'd never once doubted the architecture of his own life.

"No," she said. "Eli wrote about the artifact. About your backers. About the survey your money bought and then tried to unbuy." She tied off the last knot. "He didn't think you were important enough to name."

Something moved across Vane's face that might have been pain, or relief, or both wearing the same coat. He turned back to the fire and said nothing more that night.

* * *

They descended through climate zones like falling through years. Ice gave way to scree, scree to scrub pine, pine to terraced barley and the smell of woodsmoke and dung fires, and every thousand feet the world got louder — water, birds, insects, voices — until Rosalind realized she'd been living in silence for weeks and hadn't noticed it as silence until it ended.

Her shoulder got worse before it got better. On the third day fever visited her in waves, and she remembered the descent in pieces: Teodor's hand steady between her shoulder blades on the exposed traverses. Okonkwo forcing water and white tablets into her at intervals, counting aloud so she'd stay anchored. Dex chattering endlessly about nothing — village dogs, the price of apricots, whether yaks could be insulted — because Dex had figured out that talking kept her walking.

"You're doing the thing," Dex said on the fourth morning, when Rosalind paused at a switchback and stared too long at the valley floor below.

"What thing?"

"The measuring thing. Miles instead of people." Dex shrugged, embarrassed by their own perceptiveness. "Teo does it too. You both count distance like it's going to add up to something."

Rosalind looked at the kid — sixteen, wire-thin, who'd found a dead man's cache and a dead man's truth and hadn't flinched from either — and felt something in her chest shift like ballast settling.

"Maybe it does," she said. "Maybe it adds up to this."

Dex considered that with the seriousness teenagers reserve for anything an adult says sincerely. Then: "Nah. This is people. Distance is just how we got here."

They walked on.

* * *

The village of Hushe received them the way mountain villages receive the wrecked: without ceremony and without judgment. A woman named Zubaida — whose brother had ported for Sterling surveys for twenty years, whose household had been one of the quiet recipients of the money Rosalind's father had been sending for a decade — fed them all before asking a single question. Rice, dal, flatbread, tea thick enough to stand a spoon in. Rosalind ate sitting on the floor of a stranger's kitchen with her arm in a sling and Eli's journal heavy against her ribs, and cried without noticing she was crying, and Zubaida simply refilled her plate and said nothing, which was the correct answer.

It was there, in Hushe, that the satellite phone finally caught signal.

Priya brought it to Rosalind on the roof where she'd gone to be alone with the evening light on the peaks they'd come down from. "Valley station at Skardu has a police post. Army detachment. Customs office." Priya held out the phone. "And a magistrate who owes me a favor from a job in Gilgit that I will never describe to you."

Rosalind took the phone. It was warm from Priya's pocket and heavier than any object she'd ever held.

"He'll confess?" she asked.

"He'll confess," Priya said. "Because if he doesn't, we have the journal, the survey, photographs of the advance team, the crate manifests from the boat, and a magistrate who owes me. Because his backers will disown him the moment this surfaces, and he knows it. And because—" she hesitated, choosing honesty over comfort, "—because some part of him wants to. I watched him listen to you read Eli's pages in that cave. Men don't recover from that, Rosalind. They either bury it deeper or they put it down."

Rosalind dialed.

Vane answered on the second ring, in the room Okonkwo had given him, and she could hear in his breathing that he'd been waiting for the call. Waiting, maybe, since the calving. Since the moment the mountain had made its position on the matter unmistakably clear.

"Skardu," she said. "Tomorrow. There's a jeep coming up for us. You ride down in it, you walk into the post, and you tell the magistrate everything. Names. Accounts. The buyers. All of it."

"And if I run?"

"Then you spend the rest of your life knowing that a nineteen-year-old died protecting a truth you sold, and that the only person who ever offered you a way to put it down was me, and you threw it away." She paused. "Also Priya has a magistrate."

A sound came down the line that she realized, after a moment, was a laugh — dry, cracked, genuine. "You're not what your father advertised."

"Nobody is. That's rather the point of the whole expedition."

Silence. Then Vane said, quietly, in the voice of a man setting down something he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it had weight: "I'll be ready at dawn."

* * *

The jeep ride down took nine hours on roads that were less roads than arguments between rock and river. They rode in silence mostly, seven people and one broken man in a vehicle built for five, packs wedged between knees, the Karakoram peeling away behind them ridge by ridge like bandages coming off a wound.

At a checkpoint outside Skardu, soldiers waved them through after a glance at papers Priya produced, and Rosalind watched Vane watch the soldiers, calculating and then visibly abandoning the calculation. There was nowhere left to calculate toward. She saw the exact moment he understood that — a small collapse around the eyes, quickly mastered — and she looked away to give him the privacy of it.

They reached the station at dusk. The police post was a low building with a flagpole and a courtyard where a constable was washing a motorcycle with enormous care. The magistrate — a compact, gray-templed woman named Farhat whom Priya greeted like an old sparring partner — met them under the single working floodlight.

Vane got out of the jeep last. He stood in the courtyard on his splinted ankle, holding his crutch like a man who'd forgotten what hands were for, and looked at the building he was about to enter.

"It won't bring him back," he said. Not to Rosalind, exactly. To the courtyard. To the mountains darkening above the town. To Eli, wherever Eli was, if Eli was anywhere.

"No," Rosalind said. "Nothing brings anyone back. It brings the truth back. Those are different cargoes."

He nodded once, and walked — hopped, dragged, grimly — through the door.

What followed was six hours of testimony that Rosalind heard only in fragments, called in and out to corroborate. She learned later, from Farhat herself, how it went: how Vane started strong and procedural, giving names and shell companies with the fluency of long rehearsal, and how somewhere past midnight he stopped performing and simply talked — about the backers, about the buyer in the Gulf who'd commissioned the erasure, about the advance team he'd sent ahead with instructions he'd pretended not to understand, about the accident ten years ago that his money had helped convert from crime to tragedy.

"He said," Farhat reported, pouring tea in her office at three in the morning, "that your father knew. That Sterling suspected for years and chose the mountain's version because the truth would have cost him everything." She set the cup down. "I am telling you this because you will hear it eventually, and better from me, tired and plain, than from a transcript."

Rosalind held the tea and didn't drink it. Her father. Who had spent a decade sending money to porter villages. Who had gone silent and dying in a house she hadn't entered in ten years. Who had known — or half-known, or refused to know — and chosen the lie that let him keep his name.

She should have felt rage. What she felt instead was exhaustion so complete it had passed through grief and come out the other side as something almost like calm. Later, she thought. There would be time for the rest of it later. There was a nurse's number in her jacket pocket, and a phone, and a dawn coming.

"Is he done?" she asked.

Farhat glanced toward the interrogation room, where through frosted glass a shape sat motionless at a table. "He is writing. He insists on writing the account himself, in full, tonight, while his nerve holds." A pause. "He asked that you have it. Whatever comes of the case. He asked that it go to you first."

"Why me?"

"Because," Farhat said, with the mild precision of a woman who had listened to confessions for thirty years, "you are the one who carried him down."

* * *

Dawn found Rosalind on the station roof, the valley below turning gold and smoky, the peaks behind her already losing themselves in haze. Her shoulder burned dully under the fresh dressing Okonkwo had applied at first light. Below in the courtyard, the crew was loading the jeep — not to leave, exactly. Just to move. That was the old grammar. But Dex was arguing cheerfully with Priya about where to eat breakfast, and Okonkwo was teaching the motorcycle-washing constable a card game, and Teodor stood at the gate looking up at her with a question in his face he was too patient to ask out loud.

In her hand: the envelope Farhat had given her. Vane's written confession, forty pages, signed and witnessed. And beneath it, tucked where he must have slipped it while no one watched, a second sheet in his own handwriting, unsigned:

*Sterling — the account numbers for the escrow your father opened in '09 are on page 31. He funded the original recovery honestly. His mistake was trusting me to finish it. I've instructed my counsel to release the funds to you. Consider it not restitution. Consider it the survey finally paid for.*

Rosalind read it twice, folded it small, and put it away with Eli's journal.

Below, Teodor climbed the stairs and stood beside her, not touching, close enough that his sleeve brushed hers. Together they watched the light come down the valley, touching the fields, the rooftops, the road south.

"There's a number," she said, "I've been carrying for ten years. Nurse's number. My father's house." She turned it over in her fingers like a stone she'd finally decided to throw. "I keep thinking about what Dex said. That distance doesn't add up to anything. People do."

Teodor said nothing. He was very good at saying nothing. But he took the phone from her hand, held it out to her again, screen lit, dial pad open, and waited — the way he'd waited through storms, through revelations, through the whole long descent — for her to decide.

Rosalind looked at the number. At the man beside her. At the road going down into the world.

Then she pressed call, and lifted the phone to her ear, and listened to it ring.
