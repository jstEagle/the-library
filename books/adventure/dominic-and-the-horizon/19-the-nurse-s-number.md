## 19. The Nurse's Number

The line rang four times. Rosalind counted them the way she counted everything now — calibrations, intervals, the distance between one thing and the next.

On the fifth ring, a woman answered. Not her father. A voice like warm water, careful and professional: "Sterling residence."

Rosalind's mouth went dry. Ten years of not calling this number, and she'd imagined every version of it except the ordinary one — that a stranger would pick up, that she would have to say her name out loud to someone who had no reason to know it.

"This is—" She stopped. Started again. "I'm his daughter."

A pause on the other end. Then something shifted in the voice, the professionalism softening into something almost startled. "Rosalind?"

"Yes."

"He's talked about you." The nurse said it simply, without judgment, which was somehow worse than if she'd said it with any. "He's having a good morning. He's in the garden room. I'll take it out to him — do you want me to tell him who's calling first, or just hand him the phone?"

The question was a kindness and a trap at once. If she said *tell him*, she gave him time to arrange his face. If she said *just hand it to him*, she got whatever came before the arrangement — surprise, maybe, or nothing at all.

"Just hand it to him," Rosalind said.

* * *

She heard the nurse cross a room. Heard a door open onto what must have been open air, because there was birdsong suddenly, thin and domestic, the sound of a world where nothing had ever calved or collapsed or burned. Heard the nurse say something low and indistinct, and then a longer silence, and then a different breathing on the line. Older. Slower. With a rattle in it that she recognized from other endings, other beds, the sound of lungs doing their work at half capacity.

"Hello?"

His voice. Thinner than she remembered. But his voice.

"Dad." The word came out of her like something dislodged. "It's Roz."

Another silence. Long enough that she pulled the phone away to check the call hadn't dropped. When she put it back, he was still there, still breathing, and when he finally spoke it was barely above the birdsong.

"I know. I knew before she said it. You breathe like your mother when you're deciding whether to speak."

"That's a strange thing to know."

"I've had ten years to learn strange things." A pause. A wet breath. "I didn't think you'd call."

"I didn't think I would either."

"Teodor," he said, and it wasn't a question.

Rosalind looked across the truck bed at Teodor, who was very carefully studying the valley below them as though it required his full attention. "Yes."

"He wrote to me. After the glacier. He wouldn't tell me how it ended — he said you should be the one to do that. Stubborn man. Worse than me." Her father coughed, a long, folding cough, and when it passed he was quieter. "So tell me. How did it end?"

And here was the thing Rosalind had not planned for: she had rehearsed this call for a decade. In hotel rooms, on boats, in the blue hour before dawn watches, she had composed speeches — accusations, indictments, the whole architecture of everything he owed her and Eli. She had built them load-bearing, ready to deliver.

Now, asked a simple question, none of them would come. What came instead was the truth, plain and unarranged.

"We found the cave. We found Eli's journal. Vane's men are in custody — Vane too. He confessed at the valley station. He cried, Dad. Did you know he could cry? Because I didn't."

Her father made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been grief; with him they had always shared a throat. "I knew Marcus Vane for thirty years. I taught him everything he used against you." Another breath, harder won. "I'm sorry. That's — I want to say it properly, while I still have the words. I'm sorry. For all of it. For sending you both up there. For letting the money matter more than the mountain. For the years after, when I let you carry it alone because looking at you was like looking at what I'd done."

Rosalind stood very still in the truck bed, the wind coming up the valley cold off the ice fields behind them, and felt the speech she'd rehearsed dissolve in her chest like salt.

"You sent money to the village," she said. "Khorogtse. Fourteen years."

"The porters' families. Yes." No defensiveness in it. "It wasn't amends. Amends is a word people use to make themselves feel finished. It was just — what I could do. It didn't bring anyone back."

"No," she said. "It didn't."

They were quiet together then, and the quiet was not comfortable, but it was honest, and she found she could stand inside it. Below them the river ran brown with monsoon runoff, carrying the mountains down to the sea grain by grain. Somewhere in that water was the glacier that had taken her brother. Somewhere downstream, eventually, all of it arrived somewhere.

"How bad is it?" she asked. "The — how much time?"

"They stopped giving me numbers. I told them to. Numbers are for surveys." A pause. "Months. Maybe fewer. The doctors use the word *comfortable* a lot, which I've learned means *we're done trying*."

"Do you want me to—"

"Come home," he said. "Yes. But not for me, Roz. I need you to hear this part clearly, because I don't have the strength to repeat it and you don't have the patience to listen twice." She heard him shift, heard the effort of it, a man rearranging himself around his own failing body. "Don't come back to make peace with me. Come back because the survey needs publishing and your name belongs on it — both names, yours and Eli's, and mine last if at all. Come back because you spent ten years measuring your life in miles and I'd like, before I go, to see you measure it in something else. But don't come back to forgive me. That's not a debt you owe. I'm not asking for absolution. I'm telling you where the work is."

Rosalind pressed her hand flat against the cold metal rail of the truck bed. Forgiveness without absolution — she hadn't known until this moment that those were two different things, that you could set down the weight without declaring the weight had never been real.

"Eli's journal says the accident wasn't an accident," she said. "You knew. Didn't you. Some part of you knew, all these years."

Silence. The longest yet. When he spoke, his voice had gone small in a way she had never heard it, not even at her mother's funeral.

"I suspected. There were things in the final survey that didn't add up — readings that had been altered, pages missing. And Vane was so eager to close the file. So eager to have everyone stop looking." A breath. "But knowing and proving are different countries, and I had lost the will to travel. I told myself the mountain took him, because the mountain taking him was a grief I could survive, and a man taking him was not. That was cowardice. I'm not going to dress it up at the end. It was cowardice, and you paid for it."

"Yes," Rosalind said. "I did."

"And you're allowed to hate me for it."

"I know." She watched Teodor pretend not to watch her. "I don't, though. That's the strange thing. I ran out of hate somewhere around the third flood. All that's left is — this. Talking. It's not enough and it's also more than I expected."

"That sounds about right," her father said. "That sounds like most true things. Not enough, and more than you expected."

* * *

They talked for forty minutes, and afterward Rosalind could not have reconstructed most of it. That was the nature of it — small, imperfect, real. He told her the garden room had a view of a fig tree that the neighbor kept threatening to cut down, and that he had developed strong opinions about the fig tree. He asked about Priya's knee and Okonkwo's hands and Dex, and when Rosalind described Dex finding the cache, her father laughed — actually laughed, a cracked and rusty sound — and said, "Of course it was the teenager. It's always the teenager. Adults look where they expect things to be."

He asked about the artifact, and she told him it was going back — the ministry had been notified, the repatriation paperwork already moving through channels faster than paperwork ever moved, because scandal accelerated everything. He was quiet about that for a while.

"I spent my whole career taking things out of the ground," he said finally. "Telling myself it was rescue. Preservation. Half of it was true. The other half was appetite." A pause. "Putting one thing back. I'd trade the whole career for it, if trading were possible."

"It's not," Rosalind said. "But the return is happening anyway. You don't get to trade. You just get to have done one right thing at the end."

"One right thing at the end," he repeated, tasting it. "There are worse epitaphs. Your mother would have laughed at it, which means it's a good one."

When the nurse came back on — he was tiring, she said gently, the color leaving his voice the way color leaves a sky — Rosalind said the thing she had not planned to say, the thing that arrived on its own schedule the way weather does.

"Dad. I'm glad I called."

"So am I." And then, quieter, the words coming slowly, each one carried a long way: "You turned out better than me. Not despite everything. I think — I think sometimes because of it. You learned early what I learned too late. That the horizon doesn't love you back. Only people do."

The line went quiet. She held it a moment longer, listening to his breathing, the rattle and the persistence of it, and then the nurse's voice returned, warm water again: "He's asleep. He was smiling when he went."

"Was he," Rosalind said, and it wasn't quite a question, and the nurse didn't quite answer, and the call ended the way most true things end — not with resolution but with a dial tone and a hand lowering a phone.

* * *

She sat down in the truck bed. Not gracefully — her knees simply decided the conversation was over and ended their employment. Teodor handed her his canteen without being asked, and she drank, and the water tasted like snowmelt and plastic, and it was the best water she'd ever had.

"Well?" he said.

"He wants us to publish. Both names. His last." She turned the canteen in her hands. "He said don't come home to forgive him. Come home because there's work."

Teodor nodded slowly. "That sounds like him."

"You knew him well?"

"Better than I admitted, worse than I claimed." He looked out at the valley. "He was not an easy man. But he was never bored, and he was never cruel to people who couldn't fight back. Those two things kept me working for him longer than I should have stayed."

"He said you wrote to him. After the glacier."

"I did." Teodor's jaw worked. "I told him you were alive. I didn't tell him anything else, because it wasn't mine to tell. Your story. Your brother's story. I just — I thought a man dying should know his daughter made it off the ice."

Rosalind looked at him for a long moment. Ten days ago he'd been a stranger at a confluence with a damaged boat and a suspicious fluency in dialects. Now he was the man who had written letters to her dying father on her behalf, who had guarded her grief like cargo, who had held out a lit phone screen and waited.

"Thank you," she said.

He shrugged, embarrassed, and she let him have the shrug, because some thanks are better delivered by not pressing.

Below them, the road switchbacked down toward the valley station, and beyond it the plains, and beyond those, eventually, a hospital room with a fig tree outside the window. Priya climbed into the cab and started the engine, and Okonkwo called something about lunch in Gilgit, and Dex appeared at the tailgate holding her battered notebook, eyes bright with the particular hunger of a seventeen-year-old who has just realized the story is bigger than the expedition.

"Roz. The journal pages — I scanned everything last night, all of Eli's notes plus the survey plates. Cross-referenced the dates." Dex flipped the notebook around. "Your dad's final survey and Eli's journal overlap for eleven days. Eleven days where they were both documenting the same site, from opposite ends. Your dad cataloguing the looting. Eli recording who ordered it." She tapped the page. "If we publish these side by side — not just the artifact return, the *whole* record — it's not a salvage report anymore. It's testimony."

Rosalind took the notebook. Read the overlapping dates. Felt the shape of the next thing assemble itself, the way routes assemble in the mind before the body ever walks them.

Her father had said: *the survey needs publishing.* Dex had just shown her what publishing really meant. Not a paper in a journal. A reckoning.

"Get me everything," Rosalind said. "All of it. We'll build the full record on the drive down — your scans, the survey plates, the ministry correspondence, Vane's confession. By the time we reach the valley station, I want it ready to file."

Dex grinned and dove back into the truck. Teodor raised an eyebrow at her.

"Not resting?" he said.

"There'll be time for resting." Rosalind climbed into the cab beside Priya, the notebook open on her knees, the road unwinding ahead of them down out of the mountains. "Right now there's a manuscript to finish, a name to clear, and a man with a fig tree waiting to read it."

Priya put the truck in gear. "Gilgit by dark?"

"Gilgit by dark," Rosalind said. "And then home."
