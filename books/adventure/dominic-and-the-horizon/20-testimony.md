## 20. Testimony

The museum in Islamabad had a receiving room that smelled of lemon polish and old paper, and Rosalind stood in it for forty minutes while a man in white gloves lifted the bronze vessel out of its crate with the tenderness of a midwife.

It was smaller than she'd imagined. Ten years of nightmares had inflated it — Eli's hands around something enormous, something heavy enough to drag a man off a glacier. But this was a palm-sized thing, cast in some alloy that had gone green-black at the seams, its surface worked with figures that might have been dancers or might have been falling. The curator said it was Gandharan, maybe older, and that its provenance was a hole in the record going back to 1971.

"That hole," Rosalind said, "is about to be filled."

The paperwork took three days. She signed forms in triplicate, spelled her name for clerks who kept glancing at her face like they were checking it against something, and answered the same question eleven times from eleven different officials: *And you found this where?*

"An ice cave above the Biafo Glacier," she said each time. "Documented in my father's final survey."

Each time, someone's eyebrows moved. Each time, she let them move. There was no more reason to protect the survey, or the man who'd written it, or herself. The whole point was to stop protecting any of them.

Teodor sat through the interviews beside her, translating when the dialects got technical, saying nothing when they didn't. He had a way of being present without taking up space, which she'd first read as evasion and now understood as discipline. On the second day, between sessions, he handed her tea in a glass tumbler and said, "You know your father will want to argue with every line you publish."

"He can argue from his bed," she said. "He's earned the right to nothing else."

"You don't mean that."

She looked into the tea. "No," she admitted. "I don't."

* * *

The journal went to a forensic document examiner first, because Vane's lawyers would come for it and everything attached to it needed to be bulletproof. Rosalind watched through a window as the examiner photographed Eli's pages under raking light, isolating ink chemistry, dating the paper stock. His handwriting was their father's handwriting, nearly identical — the same forward-leaning urgency, the same cramped margins — and seeing it enlarged on a screen did something to her chest that she hadn't prepared for.

Eli had been twenty-six. He'd written the last entry four days before the glacier took him, and the entry wasn't about fear. It was about inventory.

*If I go down, the cave is marked on the north fork, third serac, blue paint. Dad doesn't know I copied the manifest. Someone has to hold the original outside the family.*

Someone has to hold the original outside the family. He'd been twenty-six and already thinking in contingencies, already managing their father's secrets the way Rosalind managed contracts — keeping the famous name off things, keeping the dangerous truths distributed so no single loss could bury them all.

Except the glacier had taken him anyway, and the secret had stayed buried with him for ten years, and their father had spent those ten years letting the world believe his son died in an ordinary accident rather than admit what the survey actually recorded.

The examiner looked up from the screen. "The ink matches the dated entries," she said. "This is authentic."

Rosalind nodded once and walked out before her face could do anything she'd have to account for later.

* * *

They published in two places at once: a peer-reviewed archaeology journal, because the artifact deserved rigor, and a long-form investigative outlet, because the theft deserved witnesses. Priya built the timeline graphics. Okonkwo reviewed the medical sections — the advance team's deaths, the altitude, the cold. Dex, who had found the cache and Eli's journal and who had spent the descent asking questions no one else dared ask, wrote a sidebar about what it was like to be seventeen and holding a dead man's notebook, reading it by headlamp in a tent while grown adults pretended not to cry.

The piece ran under two bylines: R. Sterling and E. Sterling, posthumous contribution.

Her father's editor called it unprecedented. Rosalind called it overdue.

The morning it published, she was in Lahore, in a hospital corridor that smelled of antiseptic and jasmine from a vase someone had placed too close to the oxygen equipment. A nurse — the nurse, the one whose calls she'd refused for a decade, a woman named Farah with gray threading through her braid and a voice that never once made Rosalind feel guilty, which was its own kind of miracle — led her down the hall to room 412.

Alistair Sterling was propped at forty degrees, thinner than the photographs, his hands spotted and restless on the blanket. He'd insisted on being given the newspaper himself. He'd insisted on glasses. He'd insisted, Farah reported, on being left alone with it for exactly as long as it took to read the first page, and then he'd pressed the call button and asked for his daughter.

Rosalind stood in the doorway.

He looked up. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence had ten years inside it, all the unanswered calls and unmailed letters and the one conversation they'd never had, standing in the room like a third visitor.

"You spelled the cave coordinates wrong," he said finally. "Figure six. It's off by four hundred meters."

"I know," Rosalind said. "We corrected it in the online edition."

"Good." He turned the page. His hands shook. He kept reading.

She pulled a chair to the bedside and sat, and for a long time she simply watched him read his own rehabilitation — the survey vindicated, the theft documented, the cover-up named, his name cleared of the negligence that had shadowed it since the accident. Somewhere in the middle of page three, his eyes stopped moving, and he lowered the paper, and he said, very quietly:

"Eli held the original outside the family."

"Yes."

"And you brought it back in."

"Yes."

His chin dipped toward his chest. She thought he was falling asleep. Then she saw his shoulders, and understood he was crying, silently, the way men of his generation cried — as if observed even alone, as if grief were a citation he couldn't afford.

Rosalind reached over and put her hand flat on the blanket near his, not touching, just adjacent. He didn't reach back. He didn't need to. The distance between their hands was the exact width of everything unsaid, and for once, leaving it there felt honest instead of cowardly.

"The porters' village," he said, when he could speak. "The money. You found the ledger."

"We found the school roof. And the bridge. And the clinic generator." She paused. "You could have just told us you were sorry."

"Telling you would have been for me." He looked at the ceiling. "The village was for them."

It wasn't an apology. She recognized that. It was an explanation wearing an apology's coat, and she let him have it, because she'd learned something on a leaking boat in a flooded gorge: you cannot bail out a hull by arguing with the water. You just keep bailing, and you keep the people aboard alive, and some nights that's the whole of what love manages.

"Dad," she said. "I'm not here to absolve you. I want you to know that up front."

"Thank God," he said. "Absolution is boring. Reconciliation takes actual work."

Despite everything — despite the decade, despite Eli, despite the hospital smell — she laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out.

* * *

Vane's trial opened in a courtroom in Gilgit six weeks later, because the theft charges attached to Pakistani jurisdiction and the conspiracy charges followed the evidence. Rosalind testified for two days. Teodor testified for three; his knowledge of the survey, the backers, and the chain of custody was the spine of the prosecution's case, and he gave it steadily, in his quiet way, naming names in a voice that never rose.

Vane's lawyers tried to impeach her — the burned contract, the decade of anonymous salvage work, the implication that a woman who hid her own name couldn't be trusted to tell the truth about anyone else's. Her attorney let the question hang, then asked Rosalind to state her full name for the record.

"Rosalind Eliane Sterling," she said. "Daughter of Alistair. Sister of Eli. Salvor of record on the Biafo recovery."

The stenographer typed it. Something in the room shifted, the way air shifts before weather.

The verdict came on a Thursday. Guilty on nine counts. The judge, an elderly woman with a reputation for terseness, sentenced Vane to fourteen years and, in her closing remarks, said something Rosalind wrote down afterward in her notebook because she wanted to keep it:

*"The law can return an object. Only people can return a truth. This court notes with approval that both were accomplished here — one by process, one by courage."*

Justice outside the law, completed inside it. The whole arc, closed.

Vane, escorted past the press, looked older than the man who'd slid a contract across a dockside bar. He caught Rosalind's eye as they passed. She expected hatred, or contempt, or the smooth charm he'd used as armor for thirty years.

What she saw was exhaustion. He nodded at her — a small, defeated nod, the nod of a man acknowledging a debt he could never repay — and was led away.

She felt nothing she expected to feel. No triumph. No satisfaction. Just a strange, hollow lightness, like setting down a pack after a long carry, when your shoulders keep remembering the weight for hours afterward.

* * *

That evening she went back to the hospital. Her father wanted to hear the verdict read aloud, verbatim, and she obliged, sitting on the edge of his bed with the transcript while Farah pretended to chart medications within earshot.

"Fourteen years," Alistair said. "For a bronze cup and two good men dead." He shook his head slowly. "The arithmetic of greed never balances."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "But the testimony does."

He studied her for a moment — really studied her, the way he used to study maps, looking for the feature everyone else missed. "You've changed," he said. "You measure differently now."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"Ten years ago you measured yourself in miles. Distance from me, distance from ports, distance from anything that could cost you." He tapped his temple. "Your brother did the opposite. Measured himself in how many people he could carry. I thought you were the survivor and he was the dreamer." His voice frayed. "Turns out he was the navigator and you were the ship."

Rosalind looked out the window. Lahore was going gold with evening, minarets catching the last light, kites — actual kites, children flying them from rooftops — riding the thermals above the traffic noise.

"Dex wants to run the next expedition," she said, changing the subject because her throat had closed. "Priya's designing a boat that can't leak. Okonkwo says we're medically unfit as a group and he's staying anyway."

"And Teodor?"

She didn't answer fast enough, and her father smiled — the first real smile she'd seen from him, crooked and familiar and Eli's smile exactly.

"Ah," he said. "Him."

"Don't 'ah' me."

"I'm dying, Rosalind. Let me have this." He settled deeper into the pillows. "Bring him next time. I have questions about the survey that only my last assistant can answer, and I intend to be insufferable about them while I still have the breath."

Farah appeared at the door with the evening medication tray and the particular expression of a woman who has managed a difficult genius for years and has opinions she keeps in a locked drawer. As Rosalind gathered her jacket, Farah touched her arm.

"He sleeps better since you started coming," she said quietly. "Ten years I watched him pretend he didn't notice the silence. Now he notices the sound." She paused. "Whatever you two are doing — keep doing it. Imperfectly. That's the only way it works."

* * *

Rosalind walked out of the hospital into the warm dark and found Teodor waiting on the low wall across the street, as she'd known she would, eating something fried from a cart and watching the kite-fighters with the focused attention he gave everything.

"Well?" he said.

"Guilty. Fourteen years."

He nodded slowly, processing. Then: "How is he?"

"He wants to interrogate you about field notes. He's planning to be insufferable."

"Then he's recovering." Teodor crumpled the food wrapper and didn't throw it on the ground, because he was Teodor. "There's something else. I heard from the museum today. The Gandharan piece — they've traced the original find site. A shrine complex in Swat, looted in '71. They're mounting a formal repatriation expedition. Back to the valley. Documentation, community consultation, the full protocol."

Rosalind went still. "When?"

"Spring." He glanced at her sideways, careful, the way he offered everything — as if it could be declined without damage. "They'll need a salvor of record. Someone with high-altitude experience and a habit of finishing what other people abandon."

Above them, a child's kite cut another kite's string, and the loser spun away over the rooftops while the winner climbed, climbing, into the darkening blue.

Rosalind watched it rise and felt the old pull — the horizon, always the horizon — but it felt different now. Not an exit. An opening.

"Spring," she said. "Ask Dex if he's finished his exams."
