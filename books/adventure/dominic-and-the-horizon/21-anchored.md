## 21. Anchored

The town was called Meridian, which Rosalind found almost too on-the-nose to be believed — a river town in the folds of the Karakoram's southern foothills, all slate roofs and prayer flags and a single street that ran downhill toward the water like it had somewhere better to be. They'd ended up here by accident, the way most true things happened. The valley station where Vane made his confession had no beds for four wounded salvagers, and the road down was washed out, and so they'd stopped. And then, somehow, kept stopping.

Six weeks now. She counted them the way she used to count miles, and the number sat differently in her chest.

The house they'd rented stood at the top of the street, three rooms and a leaky roof Priya kept threatening to fix and never did because, she said, the drip over the kitchen table sounded like home. Okonkwo had claimed the room with the best light for his medical texts and his interminable crossword puzzles. Dex had a mattress on the floor of what was technically a pantry and defended it with the ferocity of someone who'd slept in worse places and knew exactly how good this one was.

And Rosalind had a room with a window that faced east, toward the mountains she'd come down from, and a floor that was hers, and a bag that had not been unpacked since Bangladesh.

She looked at it every morning. It sat by the door, packed, zipped, ready — ten years of habit stitched into canvas and buckles. Ready for the flood, the fire, the betrayal, the call at 3 a.m. Ready to be gone before anyone could make leaving hurt.

This morning, she unzipped it.

* * *

The debate had started at dinner, as debates did, because Dex had found a contract.

"Listen to this," Dex said, waving a printout — actual paper, because the internet in Meridian came and went like weather. "'Recovery of colonial-era survey equipment, upper Indus tributaries. Six weeks. Hazard pay.'" A pause, significant. "Hazard pay."

"You're fifteen," Priya said. "You can't legally sign anything."

"I can carry things. I'm an excellent carrier."

"You're an excellent thief," Okonkwo said without looking up from his crossword. "There's a difference, and it's spelled c-o-n-s-e-q-u-e-n-c-e-s."

Rosalind let them argue. She'd learned that about herself somewhere between the ice cave and the descent — that she didn't need to control every conversation anymore, that the crew functioned without her hand on the tiller every second. It had been disorienting at first, like walking after a long illness. Now it felt like being part of something instead of being the thing itself.

"The client's name is on the letterhead," Priya said, scanning it. "Some foundation. Could be clean."

"Could be Vane-adjacent," Dex countered. "Everything's Vane-adjacent if you squint."

"Vane is in custody," Rosalind said. "Testifying. Cooperating fully, according to the lawyers who keep calling me."

"And his backers?" Priya asked quietly. "The ones who paid for the cleanup operation? You think they just... stopped?"

Nobody answered that. The question hung there the way those questions did, honest and unwelcome, and Rosalind thought: six weeks ago I would have answered it by packing my bag. That was the old arithmetic. Bad question equals bad place equals leave tonight.

She picked up the contract and read it properly. Then she set it down in the middle of the table, face up, where everyone could see it.

"We don't decide tonight," she said. "We decide together. All four of us. That's new. Let's enjoy how slow it makes us."

Dex grinned. Okonkwo filled in seventeen across. Priya poured more tea.

It was, Rosalind reflected, the most radical thing she'd done in a decade.

* * *

Her father called on a Tuesday.

Not the nurse. Not a lawyer. Him — voice thin and papery down the satellite line, but lucid, present, asking about her in a way he hadn't managed in twenty years. He wanted to know if she was eating. He wanted to know if the porters' village had received the last of the fund transfers. He wanted to know, hesitantly, whether she might read him some of Eli's journal again sometime, the pages about the marmots, because Eli had written funny about marmots and their father had forgotten that Eli was ever funny.

Rosalind had read him those pages twice now. Both times she'd cried afterward, privately, facing the window, watching the mountains do their patient work of standing still.

"He asks about you," Teodor said that evening. They were on the flat roof, the whole town spread below them, the river running silver in the last light. "Your father. Every call ends with it. *Is Isandro there. Is she eating.*"

"I'm eating," Rosalind said. "I told him about the apricots."

"You told him about the apricots." Teodor smiled at the horizon. "He repeated it back to the nurse like scripture. *She has apricots off her own tree.*"

"They're not my tree."

"They are now. You've been here six weeks. In porter law, that's longer than most marriages."

Teodor had stayed too. She hadn't asked him to. After the valley station, after the confession, after the lawyers, there had been a moment at the crossroads — literal, an actual fork in the road with a shrine at it — where he'd looked at her and said, "I could take the south route. There's a dig in Ladakh I could join." And she had said, "Or you could stay," and he had said, "Yes," and neither of them had dressed it up further than that. Ten years of running had taught her that the important sentences were always short.

Now he sat beside her on the roof with his knees drawn up and his damaged boat — repaired, finally, with Priya's help — tied up at the ghat below like a promise he intended to keep.

"What are you going to do with the survey?" he asked. "The original. Your father's field copy."

"It goes to the archive in Islamabad. Certified copies to the families of the advance team." She turned her father's compass over in her hands — the one from the cache, dented, its needle still true. "Eli's journal stays with me. The rest belongs to everyone it was stolen from."

"That's the right answer."

"I know. That's why it took me so long to find it."

* * *

Unpacking took two days, which embarrassed her.

Two days for one bag. She'd start, pull out the waterproof case with the documents she no longer needed to guard, and then stand in the middle of the room holding a spare shirt like it might detonate. The bag wasn't luggage anymore; it was armor. Zipped shut, it meant nothing could get in. Unpacked, it meant she lived here. It meant the next knock on the door might be Dex wanting dinner or Priya needing a second opinion on the roof, and not a rival with a contract or a stranger with her family's handwriting memorized.

On the second evening she emptied the last pocket: a roll of tape, a dead headlamp, a photograph she'd forgotten she carried — Eli at fourteen, gap-toothed, holding up a fish he absolutely had not caught himself. She put the photograph on the windowsill, facing out, so the mountains could see him too.

Then she folded the empty bag and put it under the bed.

Not thrown away. Not yet. She wasn't a woman who lied to herself about what she was. But under the bed was different from by the door, and she knew the difference was the whole point.

That night she slept without dreaming of water.

* * *

The contract decision came a week later, decided the way she'd promised: together, slowly, around a table with food on it.

They turned it down.

"Not because it's dirty," Rosalind explained to Dex, who took it hardest. "Because we don't know yet. And 'we don't know' used to mean 'yes, fast, before anyone asks questions.' We're allowed to ask questions now."

"But hazard pay," Dex mourned.

"There will be other contracts. Better ones. Ones where we know who's paying and why, and we walk in as ourselves instead of as cover for somebody's sins." She tapped the table. "That's the whole lesson, kid. Everything we did wrong started with not asking."

Priya, meanwhile, had been doing something in the shed. Rosalind had noticed the lights burning late, the smell of solder, the muttering. When Priya finally unveiled it — a rebuilt sonar rig, salvaged from the wrecked expedition boat, mounted on a frame sized for Teodor's repaired vessel — she presented it like a woman laying down a winning hand.

"For the spring," Priya said. "When the water drops. There are reaches of the Indus gorge nobody's ever surveyed properly. Not for treasure. For the record. Charts, depths, hazards — the kind of data that keeps other boats from ending up like ours did." A shrug, elaborately casual. "If anyone were interested."

Rosalind looked at the rig, and at Teodor, and at the crew around the table, and understood that the next contract had already been drafted — not by a foundation, not by a rival, but by the people in this room, for reasons that would survive scrutiny.

"I'm interested," she said.

Okonkwo raised his teacup. "To the record."

"To the record," they echoed.

Dex sulked briefly about hazard pay, then asked if the survey would require an excellent carrier. It would. Everyone knew it would.

* * *

On the last morning of the monsoon's tail — a bright, rinsed morning, the air scrubbed down to its bones — Rosalind walked down the street to the ghat where Teodor was caulking seams. She brought apricots. She sat on the warm stone and ate them and watched him work and said nothing for a long time, because silence with him had become a language she spoke fluently now.

"My father asked something strange yesterday," she said eventually. "He asked if I was happy. Not safe, not successful. Happy."

"What did you tell him?"

"I said I didn't know yet." She tossed a pit into the river. "But I know where I'd look for the answer. That's new. I used to think the answer was always somewhere else — over the next pass, downstream, past the horizon. Ten years of proving it, one dig at a time." She looked up at the mountains, and they looked back, indifferent and enormous and, for the first time in her life, not calling to her. "Turns out the answer was a crew. A leaky roof. Apricots."

Teodor set down his caulk iron. "The bag's under the bed," he said. Not a question. He'd seen the room; he noticed everything.

"The bag's under the bed."

"And if the horizon calls?"

"Then I'll go," she said. "With them. Toward something, not away from it. There's a difference, and I spent ten years learning it the hard way so I could teach it to a teenager who thinks hazard pay is a personality."

He laughed — quiet, real, the laugh she'd crossed a glacier to earn without knowing it. Below them the river ran high and bright with snowmelt, heading for the plains, and for once Rosalind Sterling watched water leave and felt no urge to follow it.

Behind them, up the street, Dex was shouting something about lunch, and Priya was shouting back, and Okonkwo's crossword pen scratched on through the open window, and the whole imperfect orchestra of a life played on without her conducting it.

Rosalind stood, brushed stone dust from her knees, and held out a hand to pull him up.

"Come on," she said. "We're expected."

And for the first time in ten years, she knew exactly where.
