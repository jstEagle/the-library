## 22. Horizon, Not Escape

The survey began, as all good surveys do, with a list.

Rosalind sat on the floor of a rented room in Skardu with the shutters open and the morning coming in sideways, gold and unhurried, and made a list in a notebook that had once belonged to her father. It was not a long list. Rope, two hundred meters. A theodolite she'd bought at auction from the estate of a dead Swiss engineer who had loved it more than his wife, if the engraving on its case was any indication — *Für die Berge, immer* — for the mountains, always. Batteries. Tea. The small brass compass Eli had carried, which now lived in her breast pocket the way it had lived in his.

And people. That was the part that would have been missing from every list she'd written for ten years. She looked at the words she'd put down — *Teo, Priya (remote), Okonkwo's blessing, Dex's terrible handwriting* — and felt something in her chest unclench by degrees, like a fist remembering it could also be a hand.

Ten years of lists, and every one of them had been an inventory of escape. What to carry, what to leave, what to abandon when the money ran out or the dig went bad or the name Sterling started to draw attention like blood draws sharks. Her father had taught her surveying as a way of seeing: you don't look at a landscape, you interrogate it. You ask it where it's been. For a decade she had used that discipline to ask nothing of herself at all. Miles were easier than years. Distances were measurable. You could stand at the end of one and say, *there, I have gone somewhere*, without ever having arrived at anything.

She closed the notebook. Outside, the town was waking — the call to prayer braided with a shopkeeper dragging up his shutter, a boy arguing cheerfully with a donkey, the Indus muttering to itself below the bluff like an old man reviewing his grievances. Skardu smelled of dust and diesel and apricot smoke, and Rosalind stood in the window and breathed it in and did not want to leave it, which was new, which was the whole point.

She was going anyway. That was the other half of it, the half that had taken her longest to learn.

* * *

Teodor found her at the bazaar an hour later, because Teodor always found her, the way water finds the low ground — patiently, and without making a fuss about it. He was carrying a crate of dried apricots and a rolled topographic sheet under one arm, and he had the particular set of his shoulders he got when he'd been talking to officials, which meant someone had asked him questions about their permits and he had answered in four languages until they gave up.

"Deputy commissioner wants to know why a salvage crew is doing a glaciological survey," he said, handing her an apricot.

"Because we're not a salvage crew anymore."

"He doesn't believe that."

"Neither did I, six months ago." She bit into the apricot. It was fierce with sugar, sun-concentrated, better than anything she'd eaten in years of expense-account restaurants. "What did you tell him?"

"That we're mapping meltwater channels above the Biafo for the university consortium, that our funding is public record, and that if he has further concerns he's welcome to hike up and inspect us personally." Teodor's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling — a small tectonic shift at one corner. "He declined."

"Everyone declines." She studied him in the flat white light off the snow peaks. Six months since the descent. Six months since Vane's confession, transcribed in a valley-station office while Rosalind held a pressure bandage on Dex's arm and watched a man dismantle his own legend sentence by sentence, not out of remorse — she wasn't naive enough for that — but because the glacier had taken the choice away from him and left only the paperwork. Six months since her father's voice on a satellite phone, thin and lucid and terrified of saying the wrong thing, and her own voice answering, equally clumsy, both of them fumbling toward each other across ten years and an ocean like people meeting in fog. They were still fumbling. But they were meeting.

"You're staring," Teodor said.

"I'm surveying."

"Mm." He unrolled the topo sheet on a fruit stall's counter, weighting the corners with apricots, and the vendor pretended outrage and then leaned in to look anyway, because everyone leaned in; mountains did that to people. "The upper route's changed since your father's last pass. The '98 imagery shows the ice bridge where the glacier meets the ridge. If it's still there, we cut three days. If it's not, we cut nothing and lose a week going around."

"And if it's there and it fails under us?"

"Then we've made a very fast descent."

She laughed — actually laughed, out loud, in a market, and caught the vendor grinning at her — and realized she'd been doing more of that lately, laughing, sleeping through nights, eating meals that weren't fuel. Grief hadn't left. She understood now that it wouldn't, that Eli had become a permanent resident of her rather than a wound she was waiting to heal. But he'd stopped being the only resident. There was room in her for other things. There was, astonishingly, room in her for this man beside her, who knew her father's handwriting intimately and her brother's grave site intimately and her own silences better than she did, and who had never once tried to fill them with noise.

"When do we go up?" he asked.

"Tomorrow. Weather window's clean for nine days." She tapped the map, the high white country where the ice cave had given up its dead and its truth. "I want to re-survey the calving face. Properly. Documented, published, with my name on it and yours."

"Our names," Teodor said quietly. "Both Sterlings'. You're still set on that?"

"My father signed the original survey. Eli died holding it. I'm not putting either of them in a footnote." She looked at him. "You taught me that, actually. Or you demonstrated it. Same thing with you."

"I demonstrate very little else."

"You make excellent tea."

* * *

They spent the evening at Priya's old workshop, which Dex had colonized in her absence and converted into what he called Mission Control and everyone else called the Room Where Dex Lives. He was seventeen now, taller, his voice finally landed in its adult register, and he greeted them without looking up from a laptop surrounded by antenna parts and empty chai cups, which was his way of being delighted.

"Satellite passes are good through the ninth," he said. "After that, monsoon residue screws the link for a day or two. I've got your route cached, your check-in schedule cached, and a surprise cached."

"A surprise," Rosalind said.

"Okonkwo recorded a message. It's forty minutes long. I listened to the first five and then I decided life was too short."

"He's worried about altitude sickness protocols."

"He's worried about everything. It's his whole personality." Dex finally looked up, and his grin faded into something more careful, the way it did when he was about to be sincere and resented it. "You're really doing it. Going back up there."

"We're going up there."

"To the place where—" He stopped himself, which was growth; a year ago he'd have plowed straight through. "Yeah. Okay. You're really doing it."

Rosalind crouched so they were eye level. "Dex. Look at me. We are not going back to anything. That's the trick of it. I spent ten years thinking every trip out was a flight from something — from the accident, from Dad, from myself. And the mountains kept giving me back the same lesson and I kept failing the exam." She tapped the map on the wall, the penciled line climbing out of the valley toward the ice. "This time we're walking toward something. Toward the data. Toward the truth with our names on it. It sounds like a poster in a dentist's office when I say it out loud—"

"It really does," Dex said.

"—but it's the difference between running and arriving. I only just learned it. You're seventeen. Learn it early and save yourself a decade."

Dex considered this with the gravity he reserved for engine repairs and video games. Then he reached under the desk and produced a small parcel, badly wrapped, and shoved it at her. "For the summit camp. Don't open it till you're above five thousand meters. It's tradition now. I'm establishing traditions. Someone has to."

She weighed it in her hand. Something dense, something that rattled. "If it's a flare gun wrapped in a sock—"

"It's not a flare gun."

"That's exactly what someone wrapping a flare gun would say."

"Go," Dex said, grinning again. "Before I get sentimental. It's disgusting when I get sentimental."

* * *

That night she called her father.

It had become a ritual over the six months, these Sunday calls, awkward and precious, conducted across the time zones and the oxygen masks of his hospital ward. The nurse — Marta, whose number Rosalind had refused a hundred times before finally pressing call — usually answered first and handed the phone over with the brisk tenderness of a woman who had heard enough family history to write several novels.

"Rosalind." His voice was thinner each week. The doctors talked in months now, gently, in the passive voice, as though the dying were weather. "You're leaving tomorrow."

"Early. Before the wind comes up."

"The Indus valley wind. It'll be a tailwind if you time it right. Your grandfather taught me that. Nobody believes it until they've walked it." A pause, the wet sound of his breathing. "I've been reading the draft. The paper. Yours and Eli's name together on the title page."

"How does it read?"

"Like the truth." Another pause, longer. "I spent my whole career teaching students that a survey is a moral act. That you record what is there, not what you wish were there. And then I let them bury what was there because I was afraid, and afraid men hire people like Vane, and afraid men break things they can't fix." She heard him swallow. "Your brother understood the moral act better than I ever did. At nineteen. At nineteen, Rosalind."

"I know, Dad."

"I keep the paper on the table by the bed. Marta reads me the abstract when my eyes get tired. I like hearing your names. I like—" His voice frayed. He steadied it the way he'd once steadied a theodolite, with deliberate care. "Come see me when you come down. Whatever you find up there, however tired you are. Come down through the valley station and fly east and sit in this ridiculous chair next to me for one afternoon."

"One afternoon," she said. "Maybe two."

"Two," he agreed, and she could hear the smile in it, ruined and real. "Tell Teodor the ice bridge at the confluence failed in '03. Check the moraine line before you trust it."

She smiled at the ceiling of her rented room. "He already knows."

"Of course he does. That boy was always the best of my assistants. Tell him—" A coughing fit took the rest of the sentence. When he came back his voice was smaller. "Tell him I said thank you. He'll know for what."

She told him she would. She told him she loved him, which was still new in her mouth, still slightly too large, like boots not yet broken in, but hers. He said it back. Then Marta came on and told Rosalind, in the tone of a co-conspirator, that he'd slept well and eaten everything and argued with the physiotherapist, all signs of health in a man like him, and Rosalind lay in the dark afterward with the phone on her chest and felt the strangest thing, which was peace, ordinary and undramatic, sitting in her like ballast.

* * *

They left at first light.

The trail climbed out of the valley through apricot orchards and poplar shade, past the village where the porters had sheltered them, where Hafiz the elder met them at the same stone wall and pressed boiled eggs and salt tea on them and refused payment with a wave that ended all discussion. Children ran alongside for the first kilometer, shouting questions at Teodor in Balti, and he answered them gravely, one by one, as though each question deserved a full expedition report, until the children collapsed in giggles and turned back.

Above the tree line the world opened. The Karakoram assembled itself around them peak by peak — granite spires the color of storm clouds, icefalls hanging like frozen avalanches of light, the air thinning and sharpening until every breath felt minted fresh. Rosalind walked with the compass in her pocket and her father's corrections in her ears and Eli's journal digitized on the drive in her pack, all of them along, all of them hers.

On the third day they reached the confluence, and the ice bridge was gone, exactly as predicted, and they spent half a day hauling loads around the moraine, cursing and sweating and laughing, and on the fourth day the high route opened ahead of them the way it had opened once before, briefly, dangerously, generously — the glacier's seasonal shift pulling back from the ridge like a curtain.

They stood at its edge in the blue morning.

"There it is," Teodor said.

"There it is," Rosalind agreed.

A year ago — a lifetime ago — a sight like this would have hit her like a door opening out of a burning building. *Run. Go. Put miles between you and everything.* Now she stood at the threshold of the high route and felt something entirely different, something she had no old word for and had to build a new one out of parts: it felt like being invited. Like the horizon was not a wall she was fleeing toward but a hand extended, saying *come and see*, saying *there is more, and you may bring your people*.

She thought of the parcel in her pack, still unopened, waiting for altitude. She thought of Dex's terrible handwriting on the check-in schedule, of Okonkwo's forty-minute message she'd promised herself she'd listen to tonight, of Priya's voice on the last satellite call — *bring me back core samples, and photographs, and don't die, in that order*. She thought of her father in his ridiculous chair, keeping their paper by his bed like a lamp.

"Last chance to turn around," Teodor said, and there was no weight in it, because they both knew turning around was no longer a thing either of them did out of fear. Turning around was just navigation now.

"Not a chance," Rosalind said.

She stepped onto the ice, and the mountain took her weight, and held.

Behind them the valley fell away in terraces of green and gold; ahead the glacier ran white and endless toward the col, toward the survey lines waiting to be drawn, toward the work. Somewhere below, faint as memory, she could almost hear Dex's voice crackling through the static of her imagination: *check-in due at eighteen hundred, don't be late, establish traditions.*

Rosalind checked her watch, set her bearing, and walked toward the horizon — not away from anything, not ever again — with the whole bright distance opening in front of her like a page, and her name already written on it.
