## 1. Six Tides

The contract came in on a Tuesday, folded inside a fish-market envelope like it was ashamed of itself.

Felix Yarrow read it twice at the counter of the Kestrel's Wheel, a tea stall in Port Anselm that smelled of diesel and cardamom and the slow rot of the seawall. He read it a third time standing in the rain under the awning while the harbor lights smeared across the black water, and by the fourth reading he had stopped looking for the catch and started looking for the exit.

The numbers were too good. That was the first thing. Salvage work ran on percentages—ten, fifteen if the client was desperate or stupid—and this one offered thirty of gross, cash against delivery, no survey fee, no equipment bond, no questions about how he'd source a decompression mix on short notice. The second thing was the coordinates. They sat forty nautical miles off the Verren Shelf, in water that every chart Felix had ever stolen, bought, or memorized called empty. Not deep-empty. *Nothing*-empty. A hundred and ten meters of blue with no wreck marked, no obstruction noted, no reason for anyone to have ever dropped a sonar puck there.

Except someone had. Someone had found something down there, spent money finding it, and then decided they didn't want their name anywhere near the recovery.

The third thing was the paper itself. Good stock. Official letterhead that wasn't official—the crest had been printed slightly wrong, the way forgeries are always printed slightly wrong by people who've only seen the real thing through glass. But the watermark was right. Felix held it up to the stall's bare bulb and saw the thread pattern of government issue, the kind used by ministries that didn't appear on ministry lists.

He'd been running salvage out of Port Anselm for three years, which was two years longer than he'd stayed anywhere since he was twenty-six. Three years was a record. Records made him itch.

*Never stop moving.* His father had said it as a joke about sharks, back when his father still joked. Felix had turned it into scripture somewhere around the night he'd left Marrow Bay with a duffel bag and a name that wasn't his anymore. Eleven years, four names, two coastlines. He knew the mechanics of departure the way other men knew prayer: what to pack first, which debts could wait a tide and which couldn't, how to read a harbormaster's face for the question *should I know you?* He was good at leaving. It was the only thing he'd ever been good at without trying.

And this contract, sitting damp in his hand, was the kind of thing that made men stop moving. Permanently.

He took it anyway.

* * *

The crew assembled on the *Magpie's Debt*—his boat, sixty feet of converted trawler with a dive gantry welded on crooked and an engine that ran on stubbornness and filtered bilge water. Priya arrived first, as always, already checking the gas manifolds with the grim competence of a woman who trusted equipment more than people. Then Ravi, who did engines and complained about them in equal measure. Then Tomas, last and breathless, smelling faintly of his wife's cooking and apology.

"Six tides," Felix told them, standing at the rail with the chart unrolled on the winch drum. "Monsoon makes landfall inside the week. We work fast, we work clean, we're back before the sky falls."

"Where?" Priya asked.

He put his finger on the chart, forty miles out, and felt the lie assemble itself in his mouth like a man assembling a weapon. Because the truth was complicated. The truth involved navy markings—he'd seen them, stamped into the salvage brief where a clerk had gotten lazy, a hull designation half-sanded but legible to anyone who'd spent time around military tonnage. *Vessel of record: struck from registry.* Which meant either the navy had lost a ship and was embarrassed, or the navy had sunk one and was careful, and both of those possibilities emptied a dive deck faster than a fire drill.

So he lied. Cleanly, easily, with the fluency of long practice.

"Merchantman," he said. "Wartime loss, cargo of machine parts and bullion plate. Client's a private syndicate, insurance recovery. They want it quiet because quiet is cheap."

Priya looked at him for a moment too long. She had a face built for noticing things—flat brown eyes that took measurements—and Felix watched her take one now, filing his answer somewhere behind her ribs.

"Bullion plate," she repeated. "At thirty percent."

"Syndicate money," Felix said. "Don't look a gift crane in the mouth."

Ravi whistled low. Tomas grinned, and Felix hated himself a little for how easy it was—that grin, the way hope moved through a man's body like current through wire. Tomas had three kids ashore and a roof that leaked. Thirty percent of a bullion run would re-shingle his whole life.

"When do we sail?"

"Morning tide."

* * *

That night Felix sat alone in the wheelhouse with a bottle he didn't open and the contract spread under the dome light, and he did the arithmetic of unease.

Too good. Too quiet. Too far off any chart worth trusting. And underneath all of it, humming like a bad bearing: *why me?* Port Anselm had a dozen salvage skippers, some with better boats, some with cleaner papers. But Felix had a reputation—not the kind men bragged about, the other kind. The kind that said *this one doesn't talk to officials. This one has reasons to stay away from harbors with customs posts. This one can be hired precisely because he cannot be questioned.*

Someone had chosen him the way you choose a tool. For its silence.

He thought about walking it back. Morning was hours away; he could tear the contract, tell the crew the client folded, take a legitimate job scraping barnacles off ferry hulls until the monsoon passed and then move on—Marrow Bay was eleven years cold now, probably. Probably.

But the number kept working on him. Thirty percent. Enough to buy the *Magpie's Debt* outright, clear, no liens, no borrowed engine parts. Enough to stop owing anyone anything—which was, he admitted to the dark wheelhouse, a different thing than stopping moving, though at three in the morning the two ideas wore each other's clothes.

And there was something else, something he didn't examine closely because examining things was how you found reasons not to do them. The wreck was uncharted. Uncharted meant unknown, and unknown meant *first*. In eleven years of running, Felix had never once been first at anything. He'd been early, he'd been gone, he'd been a rumor in six port towns. But never the man standing over a thing no one else had touched. Some starved, patient part of him wanted that the way a drowning man wants air, and he recognized the wanting, and he distrusted it, and he knew he'd follow it anyway.

Outside, the harbor breathed. Rain came in off the water in long gray sheets, and beyond the breakwater the sea had already changed its voice—deeper, more insistent, the monsoon sending its advance scouts ahead of the main body. Six tides. The weather bureau on the radio said seven, but the bureau was ashore and the sea was not, and Felix trusted the sea.

He slept three hours in the bunk behind the wheelhouse, and dreamed of water closing over a hatch cover, and woke with his heart going like a pump line.

* * *

They sailed on the morning tide under a sky the color of wet slate.

By noon the coast was a smudge. By mid-afternoon it was a memory, and the *Magpie's Debt* rolled into the long Pacific swell with her decks streaming and her crew quieter than Felix liked. Quiet crews thought. Thinking crews asked questions.

Tomas broke first, naturally. Tomas broke silence the way other men broke wind—frequently, cheerfully, without consequence. "Bullion plate," he said, mopping rust off the gantry cable. "You believe our luck, skipper? Three years of scrap runs and now this."

"Luck," Priya said, from the helm, in a voice that made the word sound like a diagnosis.

"You don't believe it?"

"I believe the money's real." She adjusted course two degrees, eyes on the compass. "I'm wondering why nobody else found it first. Forty miles, Felix. There are fishing boats out here. There are survey vessels. A wreck big enough to carry bullion doesn't hide for eighty years unless it's hiding on purpose."

"It hid because the charts are wrong," Felix said. "Charts are wrong all the time."

"Sure." She let it sit there between them, unargued, like a tool left on a deck plate for someone to trip over later.

Felix went forward and checked the anchor windlass he wouldn't use and the dive tanks he'd already checked, and understood, with the clarity that comes from knowing yourself too well, that Priya was right and that he had known she was right since Tuesday. The job was dirty. The question—the one that would decide whether he died rich, died poor, or simply died—was *how* dirty, and whether dirt was the kind you washed off or the kind that got into the grain of you.

Sonar contact came at 16:40, forty-one miles out, exactly where the contract said it would be. The return bloomed on the screen like a flower opening: a hull shape, large, intact, lying on its beam ends in a hundred and ten meters of cold green water. Bigger than a merchantman. Felix stood over the display with the rain hammering the wheelhouse roof and felt the itch turn into something with teeth.

"That's no freighter," Ravi said quietly, leaning in beside him. Ravi knew hulls the way priests knew scripture. "Look at the sheer. Look at the bow section. Skipper—that's a warship profile."

"Old warship," Felix said. "Lots of those went down."

"Not here." Ravi straightened up, wiping his hands on a rag that was filthier than his hands. "Not in water this shallow for a combatant. Somebody scuttled her, or somebody lost her and never reported it, and either way—" He shrugged, and left the sentence unfinished, the way careful men do.

Below them, forty fathoms down, the thing waited with its secret sealed in steel.

Felix looked at the sonar bloom and then at the horizon, where the clouds had begun stacking themselves into the tall, bruised towers of the monsoon's vanguard, and he counted tides the way a gambler counts chips. Six. Maybe five, if the sea decided to lie the way everyone on this job was lying.

"Rig for diving," he said. "First pair goes down at slack water."

Priya met his eyes across the wheelhouse, and said nothing, and wrote nothing in the log, and Felix understood that she was giving him one last chance to be honest before the water made honesty expensive.

He took the helm instead, and turned the *Magpie's Debt* into the swell, toward the shape on the bottom that was already, he could feel it, turning toward him.
