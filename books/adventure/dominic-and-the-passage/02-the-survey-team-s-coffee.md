## 2. The Survey Team's Coffee

Priya noticed the smell before she saw anything: woodsmoke and instant coffee, carried flat across the water like a rumor.

They were two hundred meters off the reef line, the *Marrow* idling on a glass-calm sea that would not last past the week. Monsoon light lay over everything, bruise-colored and heavy, and ahead on the low spit of coral and sand that the charts called nothing at all, there was smoke. A thin gray thread of it, rising straight up in the windless air.

"Nobody said anything about a camp," Tomas said. He had the binoculars up, elbows braced on the rail. "Nobody said anything about anybody."

"Nobody said anything about a wreck either," said Devi from the wheel, "and here we are."

Priya took the binoculars from Tomas without asking. Through the lenses the camp resolved in pieces: two dome tents, khaki, guy-lines taut and professional. A folding table. A tarp strung between a stunted palm and a tripod mast with an antenna on it. A generator, silent. And movement—or the suggestion of it. A figure in a field jacket, back turned, standing at the table as if stirring something. As if it had been stirring something for a long time.

"Flag?" Felix asked. He'd come up from the engine room with grease to his elbows and that look he got when the numbers on a job stopped adding up, which on Felix Yarrow was a permanent condition dressed as calm.

"Can't see one," Priya said. "No flag. No markings on the tents."

"Government, then," Devi said, and spat over the rail in the way of someone who'd had dealings with governments and considered the two of them finished. "They don't put their names on things they don't want found."

Felix was quiet a moment. Priya watched him do the arithmetic she'd already done and arrive, she was almost sure, at the wrong answer. That was the thing about Felix: he was brilliant at reading water, weather, hull stress, the exact moment a man was lying to him—and hopeless at the one calculation that mattered, which was how much trouble he was worth.

"We dive the wreck today," he said. "Camp's not ours. Not our problem."

"It's somebody's problem," Priya said.

"It's somebody's coffee," Felix said. "We're divers, Priya. We dive."

* * *

The wreck lay in thirty-one meters, on its port side, half-buried in a fan of white sand that the current kept sweeping clean. It was bigger than the contract said. The contract had said coastal freighter, two hundred tons, wartime loss. What Priya swam down toward was closer to six hundred, hull lines wrong for a freighter, wrong for anything she could name, the steel below the waterline uncorroded in a way that made the hair on her arms rise inside her suit. Thirty, forty years in tropical water should have eaten it. The plating looked like it had been in the sea since Tuesday.

Felix had lied to them about the markings. She'd known it within a day of taking the contract—she'd seen the photographs he'd tried to keep folded small, and the navy pennant number had been burned into her memory by a childhood spent in a port town where those numbers were painted on boats that made your father disappear. She hadn't said anything. Not yet. She was waiting to see whether he'd lie to them about something that mattered, and she was honest enough with herself to admit she wasn't sure where that line was drawn, with Felix, because he lied about everything and nothing all at once, the way a man does who has spent so long being someone else that the someone else has grown roots.

They worked the forward section first. Felix cut the padlock off a hatch—it was padlocked, which was its own quiet obscenity, a wreck locked like a shed—and they ran their lines into the dark.

It was Priya who found the survey gear.

It was stowed in what had been the crew mess, a compartment that should have been silted to the overhead after decades underwater and instead was clean, dry-sealed, lit by her own lamp and nothing else. Three hard cases, government gray, stacked with military neatness. A fourth case open. Inside: a magnetometer, a side-scan sonar head, sample vials labeled in a hand too small to read, and a logbook, waterproof paper, the last entry dated eleven days ago.

*Site confirmed. Structure inconsistent with registry. Recommend suspension of survey pending review. We are moving to the island camp to await pickup.*

No pickup date. No signature. The ink hadn't even feathered.

She brought the logbook up because that was procedure—her procedure, the one she'd learned from a proper salvage school before she'd learned that proper salvage schools were where people like her went to be taught how little they'd ever own. Felix took it from her on the deck, read it once, and his face did something she'd never seen it do. It stopped. Not went blank—stopped, like an engine catching and choosing not to turn over.

"Eleven days," she said. "Felix. The camp's eleven days abandoned and the smoke is still—"

"I know."

"Somebody has to report it. There's a protocol. Distress, missing persons, there's a whole—"

"No."

The word came out flat and final, and around them the deck went quiet in the way a room goes quiet when a marriage argues in public. Devi looked up from the winch. Tomas set down the tank he was carrying.

"Felix," Priya said, keeping her voice level, because she'd learned that from him too, "there are people missing. Possibly dead. Possibly eleven days dead on a beach we can see from here, and you're saying no."

"I'm saying we finish the survey of the hold, we document the wreck, and we get out ahead of the monsoon, and we do not radio anyone, because the first question any official will ask is *who are you and what is your license number and why are you diving a wreck that does not appear on any chart I have*, and I will tell you right now I do not have answers that end this conversation well." He handed the logbook back. His hands were steady. His voice was almost steady. "You want to be a hero, be a hero after we're paid."

"That's not what this is."

"No," he said. "It's not. But it's what we're doing."

He walked aft to check the compressor, which did not need checking, and Priya stood with the logbook in her hands and understood, with the particular clarity of someone watching a man walk toward a cliff he cannot see, that Felix wasn't protecting the job. He was protecting himself. And that whatever was in the water below them, he already suspected it had his name on it somewhere.

* * *

She went to the island that evening. She told herself it was to check the tide gauge they'd left on the reef flat, which was even true.

The camp was worse up close than it had been through binoculars, because up close it was comfortable. That was the thing she couldn't shake, walking between the tents in the last copper light. It was a *nice* camp. The tents were pitched by someone competent. The tarp was strung to shed rain. There was a clothesline with two shirts and a pair of socks on it, dry, waiting to be taken in. A folding chair sat angled toward the water the way a person angles a chair when they intend to sit in it and watch the sunset, and beside it, on the sand, a pair of boots, placed neatly side by side, laces tucked in.

Nobody walked out of a camp like that barefoot.

The fire pit held the remains of the day's cooking—rice, she thought, and something from a tin—and beside the fire, on a flat rock, sat three enamel cups. She touched the nearest one before she could stop herself, the way you touch a stove you already know is hot.

It was warm.

Not sun-warm. The rock under it was cool; the evening air was cool; the sun had been down behind the storm front for an hour. The cup was warm the way a cup is warm when someone set it down four minutes ago to go do something and meant to come back.

Priya stood in the middle of the camp with her heart going hard and made herself be a professional about it, because being a professional was the only prayer she had. She walked the perimeter. The sand held their tracks—hers, going in, crisp in the damp—and it held the survey team's tracks, dozens of them, a camp's worth of comings and goings pressed into the coral sand. The tracks all pointed *in*. Around the tents, around the fire, around the waterline where someone had washed a pot: movement in every direction, and not one line of prints leading away from the camp toward the reef, the jungle, the boat landing, anywhere. Eleven days of a camp that had been lived in, and then everyone had simply stopped, mid-meal, mid-stir, mid-sunset, and the sand had closed behind them like water.

The radio in the comms tent was charged. She checked—she couldn't not check—and the battery read full, and the set was tuned, she saw with a sensation like cold water in her ears, to the maritime distress frequency. As if someone had been sitting here, fully provisioned, fully powered, one button-press from rescue, waiting to be rescued, and had never pressed it.

She got back to the boat after dark. Felix was on deck, alone, mending a net he never used, which was what he did with his hands when his mind was running.

"The cups were warm," she said.

His hands kept moving. "Tidal heat. Rocks hold—"

"The rocks were cold, Felix. The cups were warm. Eleven days, Felix." She crouched so she could see his face. "There's a man's boots sitting by a chair pointed at a sunset he didn't get to finish. There are no tracks out. The radio's charged and nobody called. And you're going to sit there and tell me the sea did it."

For a long moment he said nothing. The generator thumped below them. Out past the reef, the monsoon front stood on the horizon like a wall being built by hand, lightning stitching inside it, patient.

"Eleven days ago," Felix said finally, not looking at her, "I signed a contract for this wreck from a man who paid half up front in cash and didn't blink at the number. I've been telling myself it's salvage. I've been telling myself a lot of things." He tied off the net with a hard, unnecessary knot. "Get some sleep, Priya. Tomorrow I'm opening the hold."

"And the camp? The people? Do we report them tomorrow?"

Felix looked out at the storm light on the horizon, and when he answered, his voice was so quiet she had to lean in to catch it.

"The people in that camp are not missing," he said. "Missing means someone's looking. Nobody's looking. Which means whoever made them disappear already knows exactly where they are." He stood, folded the net over his arm. "And tomorrow we find out if they know about us."
