## 3. What the Wreck Carries

The wreck sat in forty meters of green-black water, and Felix Yarrow went down to her alone.

He told himself it was because the hold survey needed a steady hand, someone who could read a hull the way Priya read weather. He told himself it was because Tomas's shoulder was still bad and the kid, Dev, burned air like a kerosene lamp. He told himself a lot of things on the way down, following his line through water that thickened from jade to ink, and none of them were the truth.

The truth was that he didn't want witnesses for what he was about to look at.

* * *

She was a coastal freighter once—hundred and ten meters, riveted plates gone soft with rust, her superstructure collapsed into herself like a fist closing. The navy charts called the coordinates empty water. The contract papers called her *unidentified derelict, cargo unknown*. But Felix had run salvage long enough to know that ships didn't get anonymous. Ships got *made* anonymous. Somebody scrubbed her name off every registry from Colombo to Singapore, and somebody paid good money to make sure nobody asked what she'd been carrying when she went down.

Somebody with a budget.

He swam her port side, gloved fingers trailing the plating, feeling the way the metal had buckled inward along one long seam—not an explosion, not a grounding. Pressure. Something that had pushed from inside as she settled. His light found the hull numbers he'd photographed from the tender two days ago: stenciled, half-eaten by corrosion, but there. A pennant prefix. Naval.

He'd lied to his crew about that. Told them the markings were merchant marine, some defunct shipping line, nothing worth walking away over. Priya had looked at him a beat too long before nodding, and he'd spent the night after listening to the generator tick and telling himself the lie was kindness. It wasn't. It was fear—the same fear that had kept him moving for eleven years, port to port, name to name. Never stop moving. Never let anyone build a file on you. And here he was, anchored over a ship that existed only in the negative space of official records, working a contract signed by a lawyer whose office address turned out to be a mail drop.

Too good to be clean. He'd known it from the first wire transfer.

* * *

The main hatch to the hold was aft of the collapsed bridge, and it stopped him cold.

Not jammed. Not welded. Sealed—from the inside. The dogs were thrown, all twelve of them, and the wheel was locked down with a bar that had been bolted across it, bolted by hands working in the dark, from within. Felix hung in the water before it, breathing slow through his regulator, and felt the hair rise on his forearms under the wetsuit.

Hatches got sealed from outside to keep water out. Hatches got sealed from inside when the people below them didn't want something coming in—or didn't intend to come out.

He thought about the survey camp up on the atoll. Cups still warm. Boots lined by the tents. Radios charged, logbooks open, a pot of rice half-eaten—and not one footprint leading away, not one trail through the sand, as if eleven trained government divers and technicians had simply been lifted off the face of the earth between one minute and the next. Priya had wanted to radio it in. Felix had said no. *Officials ask questions,* he'd told her. *Questions need answers.*

Now, looking at that barred wheel, he understood that the questions had already been asked. Long ago. By whoever sealed this hatch.

He worked the bar free—it took three attempts, his knife prying at corroded bolts while his air gauge bled down past two-thirds—and spun the wheel. The dogs released one by one, each with a sound like a knuckle cracking, transmitted straight through the water into his teeth. The hatch swung inward.

Blackness. Absolute. His light went in and died against it, swallowed whole, and for a moment the beam just laid itself flat against nothing, like a hand pressed against glass.

Then the dust moved. Silt, disturbed by the pressure change, bloomed out of the opening and wrapped around him, and inside that cloud his light caught shapes. Crates. Racks. Rows of them, stacked with a precision that had nothing to do with cargo and everything to do with filing.

Felix went in.

* * *

The first crates were ordinary enough—machine parts, canned stores, the mundane ballast of a ship that wanted to look innocent. It was the second tier that stopped him. Document cases, steel, hundreds of them, each stenciled with a classification band and a number sequence too long to be anything but deliberate. He pried one open with his knife tip. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job for decades, were paper files. Photographs. Typed pages, the ink still legible, the language formal and bureaucratic and wrong in a way he couldn't yet name.

He took one photograph out and held it in his light.

A man. Standing against a wall, unsmiling, holding a placard with a number on it. The kind of photograph taken of prisoners. Taken of people being processed. Filed.

Felix put it back with hands that weren't quite steady and told himself it was history. Old war surplus, old atrocities, the kind of thing that surfaced out of wrecks every few years and made the news for a week. He was a salvager. He didn't do history.

Then he found the manifest.

It was in a tube lashed to the rack frame, waterproofed, and whoever had prepared it had expected it to survive. He unrolled it against a crate top and pinned it flat with his knee, and his light moved down the columns, and the world went very quiet around him, quieter than forty meters of ocean had any right to be.

The manifest listed cargo. That was normal. What wasn't normal was the categories. Not *crates*. Not *tonnage*. Columns headed with words like TRANSFER and RECORDS and SUBJECTS, and beside SUBJECTS, numbers. Three hundred and eleven. Human beings, listed the way you'd list sacks of rice—weight, allocation, destination—with a column for disposition left blank on every single line. Blank. As if nobody had ever come back to write down what happened to them.

Three hundred and eleven people who had been erased so thoroughly that even the record of their erasure had been sunk to the bottom of the sea.

Felix's breath rasped in his own ears. He thought of the hatch, barred from within. He thought of the hull plates buckled inward. He thought of a ship going down with her own crew sealing themselves in below decks, or sealing something else in, or being sealed in, and his mind shied off that thought the way a horse shies off a snake in the trail.

And then his light caught the last page. The appendix. Names—not the subjects', those were numbers—but the names of the men who had run the transfers. Officers, administrators, paymasters. A chain of signatures, page after page, authorizing, receiving, signing off.

His eye slid down the signatures and snagged.

*Kavanagh.*

Not Edmund Kavanagh, the novelist whose books sat in airport shops. No. This was older, colder: **T. R. Kavanagh**, Paymaster, Third Transfer Group. And beneath the signature, in the same faded ink, a home address that Felix knew better than he knew his own heartbeat, because he'd grown up in it. Because it was his grandfather's house. Because his grandfather had never talked about his naval years, had drunk himself quiet every night of his life, and had died leaving a locked sea chest that Felix's father burned unopened on a beach one winter, saying only, *some things are better at the bottom of the sea.*

Felix had built his whole adult life on that sentence. He'd changed his name twice, buried his family name so deep that sometimes he woke up not remembering which version of himself was the real one. He ran from debts, from warrants, from the slow accumulation of paper that follows a man who won't stay still—and underneath all of it, underneath everything, he'd been running from a name. From the idea that blood carried weight. That a file somewhere might have his grandfather's signature in it, and his own name waiting on the next blank line.

He'd told himself he was paranoid. Men who live off the grid tell themselves that. It keeps the nights warm.

But here it was. Forty meters down, in a ship that officially did not exist, in a manifest that should have rotted unread: his family name, attached to three hundred and eleven numbered human beings, filed and sunk and forgotten by everyone except whoever had sent a survey team to this exact spot—and then made that team disappear the same way the cargo had disappeared. Cleanly. Without tracks. Without paperwork.

Because that was the point. That was what this ship *was*. She wasn't a wreck. She was a method. A way of making things vanish—people, records, evidence—in one hull, at the bottom of the sea, where no one would ever be required to answer for them. The navy's silence wasn't embarrassment. Embarrassment leaks. Embarrassment gets leaked by rivals and journalists and bitter retirees with axes to grind. This silence was seamless. This silence was *maintenance*.

Protection. Someone was still protecting this. Someone had sent a survey team, seen what they saw, and erased them the way the ship erased everything else. And now a salvage boat with six souls aboard sat anchored directly above it, and the man who'd signed the contract was a mail drop, and the monsoon was four tides out, and Felix Yarrow—who had spent eleven years making sure no list anywhere contained his name—had just discovered his name had been on a list since before he was born.

His air gauge read low. He barely registered it.

He rolled the manifest closed, worked it into his dive bag alongside the photograph and one of the steel document cases, small enough to carry, heavy enough to matter. His movements were calm, mechanical, the muscle memory of a man who has recovered things from bad places before. Only his breathing betrayed him, fast and shallow, fogging the inside of his mask.

At the hatch, he paused. Looked back once at the racks receding into the dark, row after row, the ship's belly full of vanished people and vanished proof. Somewhere in that black, he knew, were more names. Maybe other families' names. Maybe, if he dug deep enough, a number that matched a photograph of a young man against a wall—

No. He turned. He kicked for the surface, up the line, through water that lightened from black to ink to green, and broke into daylight and wind and the slap of swell against the tender's hull.

Priya hauled him aboard, talking—something about the depth, his time, his air—but Felix stood dripping on the deck, staring south toward the horizon where the weather was building, and said nothing.

Because far off, at the edge of sight, riding the line between sea and sky, there were boats. More than one. Running lights burning in broad daylight, which meant they wanted to be seen.

Which meant they wanted *him* to see them.

Felix's hand closed around the strap of the dive bag, around the manifest, around his grandfather's name, and held on.
