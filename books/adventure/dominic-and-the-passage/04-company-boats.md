## 4. Company Boats

Tomas Reyes had learned to read boats the way other men read weather. His father ran a ferry out of Puerto Almendro for thirty years, and before Tomas could swim he could tell a fishing boat from a freighter by the sound of her engines alone. So when he came up on deck at first light with two mugs of coffee and saw the three vessels sitting a half-mile off the wreck site, he knew before anyone said a word that they were wrong.

Not pirate-wrong. Not navy-wrong either. Something in between, something that didn't have a name in any harbor he'd worked.

They'd arrived in the night. That was the first wrong thing. The *Magpie's Rest* had been alone on the water for six days, and the sea between here and the nearest port was empty as a dead man's pocket. Boats didn't appear out of nothing. Boats came from somewhere, and these had come from nowhere, which meant they'd been waiting somewhere close, dark-running, engines cold, letting Felix's crew settle in like flies on a trap.

The second wrong thing was the flags. All three vessels flew the same pennant — a plain white field with a single black bar running diagonal, corner to corner. Tomas turned it over in his head while the coffee went cold in his hands. He'd worked the Andaman run, the Malacca strait, the Gulf of Thailand, two seasons out of Darwin. He knew every flag that meant salvage, every flag that meant survey, every flag that meant *keep clear, government business*. This one matched nothing. It wasn't in any registry. It wasn't trying to be.

That was the third wrong thing, and it was the one that sat in his stomach like a swallowed hook. Flags that lie are common. Flags that don't bother to lie are rare, and rare things on the water usually mean money, and money usually means someone who can afford to lose it.

"Company boats," Priya said at his shoulder. She'd come up quiet, the way she did, and her binoculars were already up. "Three of them. Two dive tenders and something bigger sitting back — see her? Low freeboard, no crane markings. She's not salvage. She's a mother ship."

"For who?"

"For whoever pays them." Priya lowered the binoculars. Her face, which could go flat as slate when she wanted, was flat now. "Tomas. Go get Felix."

Felix was already on deck. He came up the companionway the way he came up everywhere — without sound, without hurry, a man who'd spent so long moving that he'd made stillness into a skill. Tomas had worked for him two years now and still couldn't say he knew him. Felix Yarrow paid fair, dived harder than men half his age, and gave away nothing. No stories. No port he called home. When the crew drank ashore, Felix drank one beer and left. When they asked where he'd learned to read a wreck's bones the way he did, he smiled the smile that ended conversations.

Tomas had stopped asking because he had a wife and a daughter in Puerto Almendro and a mortgage on a house that leaked, and a man with a family learns quickly which questions are worth the answers.

Felix took the binoculars without a word and stood at the rail for a long time.

"Registry?" he said.

"None," Priya said. "That pennant's a blank. And Felix — they're not running dive gear. I can see the decks. They're running winch rigs. Heavy ones."

Felix lowered the binoculars slowly. Tomas watched his face do something it almost never did: it hesitated.

"Get the tender ready," Felix said. "I'm going over."

"You're what?" Tomas heard his own voice crack up an octave and hated it.

"They flagged us at dawn with a light. Twice. That's an invitation." Felix handed the binoculars back. "You don't leave an invitation sitting. You answer it, or you spend the rest of the contract wondering what they decided about you while you hid."

"And if it's not an invitation?"

"Then I'll know that too."

* * *

They watched from the rail as the tender crossed the half-mile of green water. Tomas stood with Priya and the cook, Anselm, who had come up from the galley still holding a spoon, and the three of them said nothing, which was its own kind of conversation.

The lead vessel was maybe ninety feet, steel-hulled, painted a gray that wasn't navy gray and wasn't commercial gray — a color that existed to be forgotten the moment you looked away. Men moved on her deck with the economy of professionals. No shouting. No waving. When Felix's tender came alongside, a ladder went down, and Felix went up it, and the whole transaction had the smoothness of something rehearsed.

"How long do we give him?" Anselm asked.

"However long it takes," Priya said.

It took forty minutes. Tomas counted every one of them against the rhythm of the swell, the way his father had taught him to count — one wave, two wave, three — because counting kept the fear from filling up the spaces. When Felix came back down the ladder and the tender turned for home, the gray ships did not follow. They simply stayed where they were, patient as reefs.

Felix climbed aboard and stood dripping at the rail, and for a moment he didn't look at any of them. That was the thing that frightened Tomas most, in the end. Not the boats. Not the flag. It was Felix standing there with the answer in his mouth and taking an extra second before he let it out.

"Buyout," Felix said finally. "Full contract value, paid today, plus a bonus. We walk away from the wreck, we sign a paper saying we never dived her, and we go home."

"That's good money," Anselm said. It came out half-question.

"It's very good money."

"And the paper?" Priya's voice was careful. "What happens to the paper?"

Felix looked at her. "They keep it. They keep everything."

Tomas felt the hook in his stomach twist. "Felix. What did the man offering it look like?"

"Clean. Polite. Suit that's never been wet." Felix's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "He knew the contract number. He knew the fee we're being paid, to the digit. He knew—" Felix stopped.

"He knew what?" Priya said.

"He knew my name."

"You're Felix Yarrow," Anselm said. "It's on the contract."

"No," Felix said quietly. "He knew my name."

Nobody asked what that meant. Tomas wasn't sure he wanted to know, and from the look on Priya's face, she'd already guessed. There was a silence on the deck of the *Magpie's Rest* that had weight to it, the kind of silence that comes before a hull gives way.

"So we take the money," Tomas said.

Everyone turned. He hadn't meant to say it out loud, but it was out, and his father's blood in him said *stand behind it*.

"Tomas," Priya started.

"No, listen to me. Listen." He set his cold coffee down on the rail because his hands wanted something to do and he didn't trust them. "I have a family. You all know this. Marisol, and Lucia is six, and we have a house with a roof that comes apart every wet season, and I signed onto this job because Felix said it was clean salvage and clean salvage pays off that roof twice over." He was aware his voice was rising and couldn't stop it. "Now there are three ships out there that came from nowhere, flying a flag from nowhere, and a man in a dry suit who knows things he shouldn't, and we are diving a wreck the navy says doesn't exist, on a coast where a government team walked off the face of the earth and left their boots behind. And you're telling me the offer on the table is full pay plus a bonus to just — stop? To go home?"

"Yes," Felix said.

"Then we take it! That's not a threat, Felix, that's a door opening. Men like that don't offer doors. They offer windows, and you go through them or they put you through them." He heard it coming out of him, all of it, two years of not-asking questions finally finding their voice. "Who are you? Who did he think you were? What name did he know?"

Felix met his eyes, and there was something in them Tomas had never seen there before — something tired, and old, and almost sorry.

"The name I had before this one," Felix said. "That's all you need to know right now. I'm sorry it touches you. I didn't think it would. I was wrong."

"And the money?"

"We don't take it."

"Felix—"

"We don't take it, Tomas." And there it was — not the smile that ended conversations but something underneath it, something that had been holding the whole man upright for years. "Because I've taken money like that before. Once. A long time ago. And the paper they keep isn't a receipt. It's a leash, and it doesn't come off, and one day you're six years down the road and a man in a dry suit shows up at your daughter's school with a contract number and your name, to the digit, and asks you for one more favor." He paused. "I've been running from a favor for eleven years. I'm not putting one around Lucia's neck."

Tomas stood there with his argument dying in his throat, because it wasn't an argument anymore, it was just fear with the skin stripped off, and fear was the one thing he'd promised Marisol he'd stop bringing home from the water.

"So what do we do?" Anselm asked, into the silence. "We refuse. And then what?"

Felix looked out at the three gray ships, patient on the swell, and Tomas watched him do the arithmetic that men like Felix did — the kind that ended in someone's name being subtracted.

"Then they stop offering," Felix said. "Priya, I want a diver on the wreck within the hour. We're not done. We're ahead of them, and we stay ahead of them."

"And if we can't?" Priya asked.

Felix didn't answer. He went below, and the hatch closed soft behind him, soft as everything he did.

Tomas stayed at the rail. The gray ships sat in the morning light with their blank flag, and he thought about Lucia's roof, and the paper they keep, and the name Felix had before this one, and he understood — with a cold, settling clarity, like a stone finding the bottom — that the *Magpie's Rest* was no longer a salvage boat on a contract.

She was in the water with something that hunted, and the only question left was which of them it would take first.

He was still standing there when Priya touched his arm and pointed astern, toward the open sea, where a fourth vessel had come over the horizon — bigger than the others, moving fast, and flying no flag at all.
