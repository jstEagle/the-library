## 6. Three Tides

Priya woke to the sound of the sea doing something wrong.

She'd grown up on this coast; she knew the water's vocabulary the way she knew her mother's voice. The slap of chop against the hull, the long sigh of swell, the creak and knock of a boat at anchor—these were words. What she heard now was silence between them, a gap where the rhythm should have been, and when she rolled out of her bunk in the dark and put her bare feet on the deck plates, she understood before she looked why.

The *Marrow* was drifting.

Not swinging on her anchor—drifting, slow and lazy, stern to the wind, the wreck site sliding away beneath them like something being taken back.

"Lines are cut," Tomas said from the bow. He had a flashlight in his teeth and a length of frayed polypropylene in his fist, and his face above it was gray. "Both of them. Clean cuts. Look at this."

She took the rope end. The cut was surgical, one pass, no sawing, no fraying where a stressed line might part under load. Someone had come alongside in the dark, quiet as oil, and let them go.

Out past the reef line, three sets of running lights hung in the black like a constellation that hadn't been there yesterday. The rival fleet. They hadn't even bothered to hide it. Why would they? By dawn the *Marrow* would be miles downwind, out of position, burning fuel she couldn't spare to claw back against a building sea—and by then the monsoon's leading edge would be a bruise on the southern horizon, three tides away, maybe less.

"They want us gone," Priya said. "They could have boarded us. Taken the boat."

"They wanted us to leave." Tomas spat over the rail. "Taking the boat makes noise. This way we just... aren't there anymore."

The word *disappeared* sat between them, unspoken, wearing the faces of six government surveyors who had left their coffee cooling on a camp table.

Priya went aft to wake Felix.

* * *

He was already awake. She knew it from the quality of the dark in his cabin—the lamp off but the smell of him alert, coiled, the way a trap smells different from an empty room. When she knocked he said "Come" before her knuckles landed the second time.

She told him about the lines. He listened without moving, sitting on the edge of his bunk with his hands loose between his knees, and when she finished he nodded once, as if she'd confirmed a sum he'd already run.

"How much fuel?" he asked.

"Enough to reposition. Not enough to run for it, if that's what you're thinking."

"I'm not thinking that."

"What are you thinking?"

For a long moment he didn't answer, and Priya realized she was looking at a man doing arithmetic he didn't intend to share. It was the same look he'd worn since the day they'd found the survey camp—the cups still warm, the radios charged, the boots lined up neat as churchgoers outside tents no one had slept in. Since then Felix had gone quiet in a new way. Not his usual quiet, which was the quiet of a man keeping his mouth shut around things better left unsaid. This was the quiet of a man listening to something inside himself.

"We hold station," he said finally. "Drift back onto the site with the tide, reset anchors at first light, and work while the weather window holds."

"The fleet is right there."

"Yes."

"Felix. They cut our lines. Next time it won't be rope."

"I know what it'll be next time." His voice stayed level, but something moved behind it, deep down, like a shape passing under a boat. "Three tides, Priya. That's what we've got. I need you to keep them working until I'm ready."

"Ready for what?"

He looked at her then, and for the first time since she'd signed onto the *Marrow*, Felix Yarrow seemed to actually see her—not as crew, not as hands and lungs, but as a person standing in front of him asking a fair question. Whatever he decided in that moment, he kept it. "Ready," he said, "to stop running."

Then he told her the watch rotations, the dive schedule, the fuel rationing, all of it crisp and practical, and the moment closed over like water.

* * *

It was Dev who left.

She should have seen it coming—maybe she had, and filed it under things you don't say on a small boat. Dev was the youngest of them, twenty-three, quick in the water, quicker with a joke, and he'd been the first to laugh when Felix lied about the navy markings on the wreck's hull plates. But laughter is cheap currency, and it spends fast when the lights on the horizon don't match any registry and a man half-mad in a sea cave has started saying your names like he's reading them off a list.

Dev came to her at slack tide, while the others were eating, and stood at the rail beside her turning his cap in his hands.

"Priya. I need to say something and I need you to let me say it."

"Say it."

"There's a skiff coming at dusk. From the company boats." He wouldn't look at the horizon. He looked at his cap instead, at his own knuckles white around it. "They offered me a ride ashore. And—" He swallowed. "I gave them the charts. The survey overlays. The site plan."

The sea made its ordinary sounds. Somewhere below them, forty meters down, a sealed hold waited with its cargo of people who had never officially existed, and here on the surface a boy was confessing to treason over salted fish and rice.

"You gave them—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, because the others were ten feet away and some fractures you don't let the whole hull hear. "How long ago?"

"Two nights. Before they cut the lines." His eyes were wet now, furious at themselves. "I thought—I thought if I gave them something, they'd leave us alone. All of us. My sister's got a baby coming, Priya, and I'm out here playing diver in a graveyard for people who got erased, and nobody's going to write our names anywhere either. Nobody's going to remember us if it goes wrong."

"That's not your call to make for the rest of us."

"No." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, hard, like punishing it. "No, it isn't. That's why I'm telling you. Take my share out of the kitty. Report me if you have to. But I'm going ashore on that skiff, and I'm sorry, and I'm not sorry, both, I can't—" His voice cracked and he hated that too; she could see him hating it.

She should have felt rage. She searched for it and found something worse: understanding. Because wasn't that the whole story of this coast? Everyone ran. Everyone sold what they could carry. Felix had built a whole life on it—a false name, a clean slate renewed every season, a rule he wore like armor: *never stop moving.* Dev was just doing it younger and worse.

"Go," she said.

He blinked. "What?"

"Go. Before Felix finds out, because he will find out, and I don't know what he'll do, and I'm not sure I want to know." She took the cap out of his hands and pushed it back against his chest. "But hear me, Dev. Whatever they promised you—they're not taking you ashore to thank you. You're carrying their charts and their goodwill in the same bag, and men like that empty bags."

He went pale. She watched the thought land, watched him try to set it down, watched him fail.

"The skiff comes at dusk," she said. "Be on it or don't. Either way, I never had this conversation."

* * *

Dusk came up copper and heavy, the air thick enough to chew. The skiff appeared exactly on schedule, a low shadow detaching from the nearest company boat, and Dev stood at the rail with his duffel while the rest of the crew pretended not to watch, which is to say everyone watched.

Felix came out of the wheelhouse last.

Priya's stomach dropped. She'd meant to keep it from him—meant it honestly—but Felix Yarrow had survived warrants and debts and whatever name he'd been born under by reading a deck the way other men read faces, and the deck that evening was shouting. He walked past the others without a word, stopped at the rail two arm's lengths from Dev, and looked at him for a long, terrible moment.

"The charts," Felix said.

Dev's throat worked. "Yes."

"Did you tell them about the cave? About the man?"

"No. Nothing like that. Just the site plan, the overlays, I swear it."

Felix nodded slowly. Then he did something Priya would turn over in her mind for weeks afterward: he reached into his jacket, took out a folded paper—the master chart, the one with the cave marked, the one thing aboard worth killing for—and held it out.

"Then give them this too," he said. "Tell them I keep everything important in the chart table locker. Tell them I sleep hard."

"Felix—" Priya started.

"He's leaving anyway." Felix's eyes never left Dev's face, and there was no anger in them, which was somehow more frightening than anger. "Let him leave carrying something true. It's the only kind of lie I respect."

Dev took the chart with shaking hands. At the rail's ladder he paused, looked back once—at Felix, at Priya, at the boat that had been his whole world for eleven months—and said, "Three tides," like a warning, like a prayer, and went down into the skiff.

They watched the shadow cross the copper water. They watched it reach the company boats. None of the running lights changed.

* * *

That night the crew gathered in the wheelhouse without anyone calling it. Rain had started—fat, warm, sporadic drops, the monsoon's scouts—and the windows ran with it so the lights on the horizon smeared into long wounds of color.

Tomas spoke first. His family was ashore; everyone knew it; he carried their photograph laminated behind his watch face like a saint's medal. "We could still run," he said. "South, ahead of the weather. Write the season off. Live."

"And do what next season?" Ravi, the engineer, arms crossed. "Work honest wrecks? Half these boats owe money to men who make those three look like monks."

"It's not about the money," Tomas said. "It's about being alive to spend it."

"It's about whether he's worth it." Ravi jerked his chin at the cabin door, behind which Felix had shut himself hours ago. No light under the door. No sound. "He's known since the markings. Maybe before. He lied to get us out here, and now there's a man in a cave who knows his real name, and a fleet that wants what's in that hold, and we're sitting on top of all of it like it's ours."

Priya looked around the wheelhouse—at Tomas's laminated saints, at Ravi's crossed arms, at young Meera who hadn't spoken since the cave, who kept washing her hands though there was nothing on them.

"He didn't lie about the danger," Priya said. "He lied about the flag on the hull. Everything else he's told us straight, even when straight cost him. Even tonight." She told them about the chart, about Felix handing the truth to a deserter to carry to their enemies. "You want to know if he's worth staying for? Ask yourself what he's planning that needs the enemy to think they already know everything."

Silence, except the rain thickening on the roof.

Tomas looked down at his watch, at the faces behind the glass. When he raised his head, his jaw was set the way it got before bad dives. "Three tides," he said. "I can hold a boat together for three tides."

At midnight, Felix emerged. He moved through the wheelhouse like a man in a dream he'd finally agreed to, checked the barometer, checked the drift, and stood a moment at the wet glass looking down at the water that covered the wreck, the hold, the sealed door, the records of every person the sea had been paid to forget.

"At the bottom of the tide tomorrow," he said quietly, to no one and to all of them, "I'm going down alone."

Priya opened her mouth to argue and found she already knew it was useless. Some doors only open from the inside.

Above the southern horizon, the clouds had begun to organize themselves into something with intent. Three tides. The rain counted them down, drop by drop, like a clock learning to hurry.
