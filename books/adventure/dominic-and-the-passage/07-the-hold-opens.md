## 7. The Hold Opens

Felix went down alone because alone was the only way he could be sure of what he'd find.

He told Priya it was a decompression risk, that the hold was too tight for two sets of tanks and two sets of nerves. That was true, but it wasn't the reason. The reason was that whatever waited inside that hull had already swallowed eleven people whole, and Felix Yarrow had spent thirty-one years making sure that if the sea ever took him, it would only get one.

The wreck sat in forty meters of green-black water, a freighter called the *Meridian Star* on no chart, in no registry, owned by no company that had ever filed a loss report. Navy gray under a decade of rust, but navy gray all the same—the paint job Priya had found, the markings Felix had lied about to his own crew. He'd told them she was a coastal trader gone down with machinery aboard. Machinery. He almost laughed into his regulator now, thinking of it, as he dropped through the water column hand over hand down the shot line, the monsoon swell rolling above him like a slow fist.

Three tides left. Maybe less. The barometer had been falling since dawn, and the horizon to the southeast had gone the color of a bruise going bad.

At twenty meters the light thinned. At thirty-five the hull rose out of the murk, listing hard to port, her superstructure furred with coral, her hatches standing open where the survey team had cut them—and one hatch that stood closed. Sealed. Bolted from the inside.

Felix had thought about that for two days. A hatch bolted from the inside meant someone had gone in and chosen not to come out. Or someone had gone in and been prevented from coming out by something other than steel. Either way, the answer was behind that door, and the door had been waiting for him the way all doors wait—patiently, without judgment, indifferent to whether what came through them was ready.

He hung at the hatchway, running his light along the wheel. The bolts were drawn back. Someone had opened it recently—the survey team, before they vanished. They'd gone in. Whatever happened after had happened in the dark down here, forty meters under a sea that officially did not contain this ship.

Felix pulled himself through.

* * *

Inside, the hold was a cathedral built by men who hated cathedrals. His light swept across tiered racking welded floor to ceiling, row after row, and every rack held crates. Not cargo crates—he'd stripped enough wrecks to know cargo when he saw it, and this wasn't it. These were document cases, archive boxes, steel filing cabinets chained shut with government seals still bright on the hasps. Ten years underwater and the seals hadn't bleached. They'd been treated. Everything down here had been treated, preserved, packed against time itself.

A ship built to keep records safe from the sea. Or to keep the sea from ever finding the records.

His light found the first body at the base of the ladder.

The man sat with his back against the racking, legs out, one arm across his chest as if he'd simply lain down mid-sentence. His dive gear was intact. Tank still more than half full—Felix checked the gauge himself, because he didn't believe it until he saw it. Regulator dry. No flooding, no trauma, no sign of struggle. The man's face was calm in a way faces underwater are never calm, and beside his right hand lay a slate, the kind divers write on, the grease pencil letters still legible through the film of silt:

WE DIDN'T DROWN. IT WAS ALREADY DECIDED WHO WOULD COME BACK UP.

Eleven names followed. Six were crossed out.

Felix hung there in the black water reading it twice, three times, his own breath hammering in his ears loud enough to feel like another presence in the hold. He knew what drowning looked like. He'd pulled bodies off reefs from Zanzibar to the Sulu Sea, and drowned men wore their deaths on them—bloat, panic frozen into the features, hands torn from clawing at things. This man wore nothing. He looked like he'd fallen asleep in a deck chair.

And the note said *decided*. Not an accident. Not the sea. A decision, made by someone, somewhere, about who would surface and who would stay.

Felix's hands were steady. He noticed that the way you notice strange weather—detached, from a distance. Thirty-one years of running had taught his body to go cold and efficient exactly when his mind wanted to scream. He took photographs. He had a camera in a housing on his harness for salvage documentation, and he shot the body, the slate, the seals, the racking, working the frame edges carefully, methodically, a man doing inventory in a tomb.

Then he moved deeper into the hold, because the tomb had a heart and he intended to see it.

* * *

It was at the forward bulkhead, behind a false wall of racking, that he found the office.

That was the only word for it. A desk bolted to the deck. A lamp wired into the ship's residual power—a trickle, barely enough, but enough. And stacked on the desk, squared and neat as a bank teller's drawer, files. Paper files, wax-sealed, stenciled with a designation Felix recognized from a life he'd buried: the same classification block that had appeared on the manifest he'd photographed three days ago, the manifest that listed not cargo but people. Names. Dates of embarkation. And in the final column, a single word repeated down the page like a litany:

*Resolved.*

He opened the top file with numb fingers, careful of the paper, careful of everything.

The *Meridian Star* had never been a freighter. She'd been an erasure. A vessel chartered—no, *built*, the specifications were right there in the engineering reports—to take people who had become inconvenient and turn them into paperwork. Witnesses, defectors, accountants who knew too much, scientists whose research embarrassed ministries. They came aboard at night from launches with no lights. They were logged in. Somewhere past the hundred-fathom line, they were logged out again as *resolved*, and the records of who they'd been went into these sealed cases, and the cases went into this hold, and the hold was scuttled in deep water on a reef no chart admitted to, and everyone who needed to forget could forget, because the proof slept under pressure where no salvage company would ever spend the money to look.

Except one had. Except he had.

Felix turned a page and his breath stopped so completely that for a moment he forgot to breathe at all.

There was a section near the back—not thick, maybe thirty pages—of people who hadn't boarded the *Meridian Star* at all. People whose files were kept here anyway, in reserve, pre-built, waiting. Erasures in preparation. And the fourth file in the stack bore a name he had not spoken aloud in nine years.

**KAVANAGH, E. — FELIX.**

Not Yarrow. Kavanagh. The name from before, the name he'd drowned in a harbor in Colombo along with a boat he'd loved and a debt he couldn't pay and a man he'd hurt badly enough that running had seemed cleaner than facing it. He'd built Felix Yarrow out of salt water and silence, and he'd believed—right up until this moment, with the lamp's weak glow trembling on the page—that he'd built him well enough.

But there it was. Photograph, younger, thinner. Known aliases, listed in order, and *Yarrow* sat third on the list. Current location: unknown. Status column blank, except for a date stamped in red six weeks ago—the week before the survey team arrived. Before any of them had ever heard of this wreck.

They hadn't come here for the ship. They'd come here for him. The survey team, the vanishing, the rival fleet flying flags off no registry—all of it had been moving around him like water around a stone, and he'd been too busy running to feel the current.

The wreck remembered everyone who touched it. The madman in the cave had screamed that, and Felix had pitied him. Now he understood the man hadn't been mad. He'd been *read*. They all had. The wreck was a ledger, and touching it entered your name.

Felix sat in the flooded dark of a dead ship's secret office and felt something in his chest that wasn't fear. Fear he knew; fear was an old roommate. This was quieter and worse. It was clarity. Cold, complete, arriving all at once like pressure at depth.

Every instinct he owned was screaming the same instruction it had screamed for thirty-one years: *take nothing, leave nothing, surface clean, run.* Take the air in your tanks and go, burn the boat if you have to, put four hundred miles between yourself and this hull before anyone knows you were here. You know how to disappear. It's the one thing you've ever been good at.

But running had a shape now, and he could finally see it clearly. Running was just erasure performed from the inside. He'd spent his whole life doing to himself what this ship had done to its passengers—logging himself out of existence, resolving his own name, sealing his own history in a dark place and scuttling it. He was the *Meridian Star*. He always had been. And the men who built this hold had never once had to chase him, because a man who erases himself does their work for free.

His old name was already in the file. His new name would be soon. There was no version of the next six months in which Felix Yarrow stayed invisible—only versions where he chose what the visibility cost and what it bought.

So he made the choice there, forty meters down, in the house of erased men.

He took the files.

Not all of them—he couldn't lift the weight and he didn't need to. He took the manifest. He took the survey team's fate in photographs, the slate, the calm dead man with half a tank of air. He took the reserve section with his own name in it, because that page proved the machine was still running, still hungry, still stamping *resolved* on the living. He packed them into a waterproof case he'd brought for exactly this purpose without letting himself think about why, cinched it to his harness, and gave the office one last sweep of his light.

Behind the desk, taped beneath the drawer, he found one last thing: a radio log, the survey team's, salvaged from their camp and brought down here by whoever among them had figured out the truth first. The final entry was dated the night they vanished. Six words in a hurried hand:

*IT KNOWS WE READ IT. GOING DOWN TO STAY.*

They hadn't disappeared. They'd hidden—in the one place they'd calculated their hunters would never flood with light again. Some of them, anyway. The ones who were decided.

Felix kicked for the hatch, the case dragging at his harness like a second spine, and did not look back at the man sitting calmly against the racking. Above him the water grew lighter, greener, and then the swell hit him, the long monsoon rollers shoving him sideways on the ascent line, and he broke the surface into wind-driven rain with the sky the color of wet iron.

Priya hauled him over the gunwale herself, took one look at the case, one look at his face, and asked nothing.

Tomas was at the wheel, and beyond the bow, far off across the graying water, three sets of masts had appeared where yesterday there were none. The company boats. Closing.

Felix stood in the rain holding ten years of other people's disappearances against his chest, and understood that the moment he showed it to anyone, Felix Yarrow would stop existing—and that for the first time in his life, he didn't mind.

"Get us to the lee of the reef," he said. "We've got one tide left, and I need to decide who to give this to."
