## 8. Monsoon Landfall

The sky went the color of wet slate an hour before the wind arrived, and Tomas knew, the way a man knows his wife's moods from the sound of her footsteps, that they were going to die out here.

He'd grown up on this coast. He had weather in his bones the way other men had religion, and everything in him was screaming that Felix Yarrow should have run for the sheltered water north of the point two tides ago. Instead they were limping south with a cracked exhaust manifold, one working bilge pump, and three hundred meters of steel wreck dragging at their hearts like an anchor they couldn't cut.

"We can still make the harbor," Priya said. She stood at the wheel while Felix crouched over the engine housing, hands black to the wrist, shouting measurements over the rising hiss of spray.

"Harbor's closed," Felix said. "Harbor's been closed since yesterday. Port authority sealed it—storm protocol." He didn't look up. "You want to know who called that in? I'll give you one guess."

Tomas didn't need a guess. The company boats had been sitting off the headland all morning, four hulls flying flags that matched no registry he'd ever seen, gray and patient as sharks off a bleeding whale. They hadn't approached. They didn't have to. They'd simply parked themselves between *Magpie* and every safe anchorage on forty miles of coast, and let the monsoon do their arithmetic for them.

"They're waiting for us to break," Priya said.

"They're waiting for us to sink," Felix said. "Breaking is just faster."

Tomas hauled the last of the dive gear below and lashed it down, and his hands were shaking, and he hated himself for it. He thought of Marta. He thought of the girls, of the photograph taped inside his locker, of the promise he'd made her—that salvage money would fix the roof before the rains came, that this season would be the last season, that he'd be home by Christmas. Every year he made that promise. Every year the sea took a little more of it.

*Take the buyout,* he'd told Felix that night on deck, when the company man in the pressed shirt had offered them numbers with too many zeroes. *Take it. It's not cowardice, it's arithmetic.* And Felix had looked at him—not angry, not contemptuous, just tired in some place deeper than his body—and said, *They're not buying the cargo, Tomas. They're buying witnesses.*

Tomas had gone to his bunk and stared at the overhead and understood, finally, what kind of man he'd signed on with. Not a brave one. A cornered one. There was a difference, and the difference was that a brave man chooses the fight and a cornered man has it chosen for him, and both of them bleed the same.

The first squall line hit them like a door slamming.

* * *

Rain came sideways, hard enough to sting, and the wind went from a shove to a fist in the space of ten minutes. The sea stood up in gray walls with white manes, and *Magpie* began to talk to herself—a groaning in the hull, a shudder through the deck plates, the sound of a boat doing math she couldn't win.

Felix took the wheel himself. "Tomas—pump. Priya—navigate by feel, charts are useless in this, you know these waters better than any plotter."

"I know them in daylight," she shouted back.

"So do they," Felix said. "That's the whole game now."

Because that was the thing Tomas realized, crouched over the bilge pump with seawater sloshing around his knees: the storm wasn't killing them. The storm was *herding* them. The company boats had fallen in behind, running the same seas, keeping station a half-mile back where the rain blurred them into ghosts. They weren't attacking. They were driving *Magpie* the way dogs drive a wounded boar—toward the reef field east of the wreck site, the one every chart marked with a skull and the words *do not anchor*. The reef they had come to strip.

Drive them onto it, and the sea would do the work no court would ever hear about. Wreck found on a known hazard, lost with all hands, tragic, case closed. No bodies needed surfacing. No questions asked. The company would even send flowers, probably, to somebody's widow, if anybody could prove there'd been a crew at all.

Tomas pumped until his arms burned and watched the depth sounder tick down—forty fathoms, thirty-five, thirty—and felt something go cold and clear in him, colder than the water, clearer than fear.

Marta's face. The girls' faces. And then, unbidden, Felix's voice from that first night: *Nobody on this boat is here because they ran out of choices. You're all here because you chose me, and I've never once asked why.*

He had asked why, though. In his own way. Every day, silently, all season long.

"Tomas!" Felix's voice through the hatch. "How's she riding?"

"She's riding like a coffin with an engine!" Tomas bellowed back, and heard Felix laugh—one short, wild bark of a laugh, the laugh of a man standing on the gallows making jokes about the rope—and something in Tomas broke loose and laughed too, helplessly, absurdly, pumping bilgewater in a hurricane and grinning like an idiot.

And in that moment, without ceremony, without deciding it, Tomas chose. Not the money. Not the shore. Not the arithmetic. He chose the madman at the wheel who'd refused to leave a half-mad stranger in a sea cave, who'd opened a hold full of erased people because someone had to carry the truth or drown with it, who'd looked at four armed hulls and a monsoon and said *we go through*.

*We go through.* As if there were a through. As if the sea had a far side today.

Tomas kept pumping.

* * *

The reef announced itself as a change in the water's color—a sudden pale green under the gray, the seabed leaping up to meet them—and then as a sound, a long grinding roar where waves broke over coral that had been sharpening itself for ten thousand years.

"Hard to port!" Priya screamed. "There's a channel—I can see it—there's a channel!"

"There's no channel," Felix said. "That's what they want us thinking."

"It's REAL—" But even as she said it, Tomas saw her see it: the gap in the breaking water too straight, too convenient, a lane of calm leading directly between two coral teeth. Dredged, maybe. Or maybe just luck. Or maybe baited, the way you bait a trap with the only path through.

Felix didn't take it. Felix did the one thing Tomas would never have imagined, the thing that separated him from every skipper Tomas had ever served under: he turned *Magpie* toward the reef itself, toward the worst of the breaking water, and ran the boat straight at the wall of white.

"If we're going on the rocks," he said quietly, almost gently, into the wind, "we'll pick our own."

What happened next, Tomas would spend the rest of his life failing to describe properly. There was a wave—it must have been thirty feet, a moving hill of ocean, the kind of sea old fishermen named after dead wives—and it picked *Magpie* up the way a hand picks up a coin, and instead of slamming her down it carried her, surfing her across the reef flat in a shriek of torn foam, keel clearing coral by what the instruments later swore was four feet and what Tomas's eyes swore was less than the width of a lie.

Behind them, through the rain-lashed aft port, Tomas saw the lead company boat follow.

They took the channel.

Of course they took the channel. It was right there. It was safe. It was exactly where a sensible hunter would put a sensible prey, and the sea, which keeps its own ledgers, which remembers every hull that ever came to strip its dead, rose up inside that narrow dredged lane like something waking from a long sleep and folded the company boat in half against the coral with a sound Tomas felt in his teeth.

The second hull tried to turn away and the third collided with it in the whiteout, and then the reef—the reef they had come to strip, the reef whose wreck they'd paid men to vanish over—took them the way it took everything, patiently, thoroughly, without malice and without mercy. Running lights sank. A flare went up and was strangled by the rain. By the time *Magpie* cleared the far side of the shoal and staggered into the deep green water beyond, there was nothing behind them but foam and debris and one orange life ring turning slowly in the chaos like an eye refusing to close.

No one spoke. The engine coughed, caught, coughed again. The bilge pump thumped its idiot heartbeat. Rain hammered the deck like applause from a crowd that wanted more.

Priya was crying and laughing at once, both hands locked on the wheel. "The channel," she kept saying. "I saw the channel, I *told* him—"

"You did," Felix said. His knuckles were white on the spokes, his face gray with exhaustion, and he was looking back the way they'd come, at the place where three hulls full of armed men were learning what the wreck's protectors had learned long ago. "And they believed you. That's what saved us. They believed there was such a thing as a safe way across."

Tomas sat down on the engine housing, right there in the flooding bilge, and shook. Not from cold. From the sheer animal relief of being alive, and from the knowledge arriving in him all at once, whole, undeniable:

He was staying. Whatever waited ashore—warrants, questions, the company's longer reach, whatever Felix's real name dragged behind it—he was staying. Marta would understand, or she wouldn't, but his daughters would grow up hearing about the night their father rode a wave across a cursed reef rather than take blood money, and that story was worth more than any roof.

"Felix," he said.

Felix turned.

"The manifold's done. We make harbor on one cylinder and prayer." Tomas wiped salt from his face. "But we make harbor."

Felix looked at him for a long moment, and something passed between them that had no words in either of their languages—gratitude, maybe, or recognition, two men seeing each other clearly for the first time in a season of shadows.

Then Felix's eyes went past Tomas's shoulder, to the horizon astern, and hardened.

Because there, riding out the tail of the storm where the others had died, a fourth shape had appeared. Smaller. Slower. Unmarked, unpainted, running dark with no lights at all—and it wasn't following them.

It was heading for the wreck.
