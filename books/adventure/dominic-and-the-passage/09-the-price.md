## 9. The Price

The harbor at Kalapett was the color of old pewter when Felix came ashore, and he stood in the surf for a long moment with the records bagged against his chest, letting the water pull at his ankles as if it wanted them back.

He had not slept. Nobody had slept. The *Mariana Q* limped into the outer anchorage under a jury-rigged foresail, her port rail scarred where the company boat's hull had ground against her in the white water, and Tomas had brought her in on instinct and fury while Felix sat below decks with his arms wrapped around a waterproof case like a man holding a wound closed. Now the storm had passed inland, leaving the sky scrubbed and enormous, gulls working the fish market, the whole town going about its morning as if the sea hadn't spent the night trying to kill everyone Felix loved.

The case held eleven kilos of paper, film, and magnetic tape. It held the names of forty-one people who had been loaded onto a navy transport twenty-two years ago and never logged again. It held the manifest in his own handwriting—*F. Kavanagh, deckhand, age 19*—on a crew list that officially did not exist. It held photographs of the hold, sealed from the inside, and of the survey team's camp, and of the sea cave where a man named Oren Voss had hidden for nine days, raving about a wreck that remembered.

It held enough to end careers, maybe a government. And it held, folded into the manifest's back pages, everything anyone would ever need to find Felix Yarrow—the name he'd worn for fifteen years, the name that had no warrants attached because it belonged to no one at all.

Priya met him at the seawall. She looked like she'd aged a decade in three days, salt-cracked lips, eyes red-rimmed from wind and something else. Behind her, up the hill, the others were already working the boat—Tomas hammering at the sprung planking, Ravi coiling lines with the mechanical steadiness of a man keeping his hands busy so his mind wouldn't start asking questions.

"You haven't decided," Priya said.

"I've decided six times."

"Which one stuck?"

Felix looked out at the harbor. The rival fleet's remaining vessel sat low and dead against the reef line, visible only as a black ribcage at low tide, its crew pulled off by coast guard launch at dawn—men who claimed, all of them, to have no employer, no registry, no names worth writing down. The sea had done what Felix couldn't: it had stopped them. But stopping things wasn't the same as ending them. Somewhere ashore there were people who had paid for those boats, and they would want to know what happened to their investment, and eventually they would trace it back to a salvage diver with a borrowed name and a case full of paper.

"The broker in Colombo pays cash," Felix said. "No names. I've used him before. We'd be rich by Thursday."

"And then?"

"Then we're gone. New harbor, new flag, new name on the transom. I know how to do it. I've done it my whole life." He heard how it sounded even before Priya's face changed—not disgusted, exactly. Tired. Tired on his behalf.

"Forty-one people," she said quietly.

"Forty-one people are dead, Priya. Paper doesn't raise them."

"Paper keeps the next forty-one alive."

* * *

They argued through the morning, or rather the crew argued around him while he sat on the seawall with the case between his feet like an anchor he couldn't raise. Tomas wanted the money—he said it plainly, without shame, because Tomas had a wife and two daughters in a rented room above a chandlery and had never once pretended otherwise. "You can't eat justice," he said. "I've tried. It tastes like nothing and it doesn't keep."

Ravi didn't argue at all. He just kept looking at Felix with an expression Felix recognized because he'd seen it in mirrors for years: the look of a man watching someone prepare to run and knowing, from experience, exactly how the road feels afterward. Empty. Fast. Free the way drowning is free.

It was Priya who ended it, near noon, by putting a newspaper on the seawall beside the case. A coastal weekly, ink still smearing, the kind of paper that got read by fishermen and forgotten by everyone else. On page four, a column by a woman named Devika Sen, about missing survey teams and naval silence, written before any of this happened—written on rumor and stubbornness alone.

"She's been chasing this story for two years," Priya said. "She lost her job over it. She works out of a room above a print shop and she publishes whatever she can get past the censors. If you sell to the broker, this dies quiet and the men who built that wreck go build another one. If you give it to her—" she tapped the page, "—you stop being nobody. You understand that? Not famous. *Known.* Your real name, your real record, your warrants, all of it, in print, forever. There is no coming back from that. No new harbor far enough."

Felix looked at the newspaper for a long time.

He thought about the rule. The one rule, older than the *Mariana Q*, older than any of them: *never stop moving*. He had built it the hard way, at nineteen, standing on a dock in Karadi watching men in navy coats take away everyone he'd sailed with while he slipped into the crowd with a forged seaman's card and a name he'd invented from a gravestone. Fifteen years of harbors. Fifteen years of leaving before dawn, of friendships kept shallow on purpose, of crews hired and left behind the moment the horizon started feeling like home. He had told himself it was survival. Some nights, in the honest dark of a bunk, he had known it was cowardice wearing survival's clothes.

And now here was the arithmetic laid out on a seawall in the sun. Eleven kilos of other people's erased lives, weighed against one man's invented one.

"Where does she work?" he asked.

* * *

Devika Sen's office was a room above a print shop that smelled of hot lead and turpentine, and she received him the way she must have received every source in two years: without surprise, without warmth, with a kettle already on. She was small and gray-streaked and her hands shook slightly until she picked up a pen, at which point they went perfectly still.

"You're the diver," she said. "From the wreck the navy says isn't there."

"There's a lot I'm not," Felix said. "That's sort of why I'm here."

He set the case on her desk and opened it, and watched her face as she saw the manifest, the photographs, the crew lists, the cargo declarations stamped and countersigned by men whose signatures meant the state itself had signed for forty-one disappearances. Her stillness deepened. She read the way Oren Voss had talked—fast, obsessive, unable to stop—and when she reached the page with *F. Kavanagh, deckhand, age 19*, she looked up.

"This is you."

"That's me."

"You understand what happens if I publish this. Every name in this file gets printed. Including yours. Your warrants are active in three jurisdictions. There are people—worse than police—who will read this over breakfast."

"I know."

"They'll come for you."

"They're already coming. The boats on the reef settled that question." He sat down across from her, and found, to his own surprise, that his hands were steady. "I've spent fifteen years making sure nobody could find me. Do you know what that costs? Everything. Every friend, every harbor, every version of myself I might have become if I'd stayed anywhere long enough to grow roots. I ran because running was easier than being caught. And then I found a hull full of people who got caught anyway, by their own government, and erased so cleanly that even the sea forgot them." He turned the manifest toward her. "I can sell this and stay a ghost. Or I can give it to you and be a man again—a wanted one, but a man. I'm tired of being a ghost, Ms. Sen. Ghosts don't get to fix anything."

She studied him for a long moment. Then she took out a fresh notebook, wrote the date at the top of the first page, and said, "Start at the beginning. Your beginning. All of it."

* * *

He talked until the light went amber and then gray. He gave her Karadi, the dock, the men in navy coats. He gave her the forged papers, the borrowed name, the fifteen years of harbors in order. He gave her the contract, the false markings, the warm coffee cups, the sealed hold, Oren Voss in the cave saying *the wreck remembers everyone who touches it*—and Felix telling her, flatly, that the wreck hadn't needed to remember him, because he'd never managed to forget himself.

When he finished, she closed the notebook and said, "I'll need three days to verify what I can. Then it runs. Front page, every paper that will carry it—I have friends at two nationals who owe me." She paused. "You should be gone by then."

"No," Felix said.

"It's not a suggestion. It's advice. People who appear in my stories have a way of—"

"I know what they have a way of." He stood, and his knees ached, and he realized he was smiling. "That's the whole point. For fifteen years I left every place before anything could arrive after me. Warrants, debts, ghosts—I outran all of it, and none of it ever caught me, and none of it ever *ended* either. It just followed, forever, at a respectful distance. So this time I'm going to stand still and let it catch up. Whatever arrives—police, worse than police, my own stupid history—I'll be here when it does. Standing still is the first honest thing I've done since I was nineteen."

Devika Sen looked at him the way Priya had looked at him on the seawall, and said, very quietly, "You're certain."

"I've never been certain of anything in my life. That's the point too."

* * *

He walked back to the harbor through streets full of evening cooking-smoke, and the case stayed behind him on her desk, lighter by eleven kilos and heavier by everything else. At the seawall, the crew was waiting—they'd clearly drawn straws or simply refused to disperse—and Priya read his face before he said a word.

"You gave it to her."

"Gave it to her."

Tomas swore softly, once, and then, unexpectedly, put his hand on Felix's shoulder, brief and hard, like setting a bone. "My girls are going to read about you in the paper," he said. "Better they read you were right than that you were rich and gone."

Down the quay, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the customs shed and started toward them at an unhurried walk—navy coat, civilian hat, a face Felix had last seen across a crowded dock twenty-two years ago, older now but unmistakable, carrying a folder with Felix's true name already written on the cover.

Felix didn't move.
