## 10. Anchored

The harbor at Kalapettai smelled of diesel and drying fish and, this early in the morning, of rain that hadn't arrived yet. Priya stood on the deck of the *Marianne V* with a cup of tea going cold in her hands, watching Felix sand the same patch of rail he'd been sanding for three days.

He wasn't doing it badly. That was the strange part. A man who wanted to be somewhere else worked fast and sloppy, left the job half-finished so he'd have an excuse to leave it. Felix sanded like a man building something meant to last. Slow strokes. Even pressure. The kind of patience she'd never once seen from him in two years of running charters out of ports that changed names every season.

Three weeks since the monsoon. Three weeks since Tomas had lashed himself to the wheel and driven them through white water with the hunters' boat coming apart on the reef behind them. Three weeks since they'd come ashore carrying waterproof cases full of other people's erased lives, and Felix had walked—not run, *walked*—to the office of a journalist named Devi Raghunathan and put everything on her desk.

His name was public now. Not Felix Yarrow. The other one. The name from before, the one that had warrants folded around it like old laundry. It was in the papers, on the news feeds, spoken in the harbor bars in the tone men used for lottery numbers and dead celebrities. Some people said he was a hero. More said he was a fool. A few—the ones who mattered, the ones with lawyers and quiet phone lines—said nothing at all, which was worse.

Priya drank her cold tea and watched him work.

"You missed a spot," she said.

"Did not."

"There's a whole centimeter near the cleat you've been avoiding like it owes you money."

Felix looked. There was. He grunted and moved to it, and Priya felt something loosen in her chest that she didn't have a clean name for. Two years she'd crewed for him, and in all that time she'd catalogued his habits the way you catalogue weather—when he'd pay, when he'd lie, when the itch would take him and they'd be gone by dawn with the mooring fees unpaid and the cook's wages short. She had learned to sleep light and pack ready. Everyone had. It was how you loved a man like Felix Yarrow: you kept your bag by the door.

Nobody kept their bag by the door anymore.

* * *

Tomas came up the gangway at seven with bread and the newspaper under his arm, and Priya saw him make the decision not to open the newspaper until he'd put the bread down. It was a small kindness, performed carefully, the way you handle a live wire.

"Page six," he said finally, setting it on the hatch cover between them. "There's a photograph."

Felix didn't stop sanding. "Of what?"

"The wreck. They've released the survey footage. Or some of it." Tomas smoothed the paper flat. "And there's a hearing scheduled. Port authority, next month. They want you to testify."

"I know. Devi told me."

"And?"

"And I'll be there." Felix blew dust off the rail. "Standing still, remember? It's my new thing. Very relaxing."

Tomas laughed, but it came out wrong, caught on something. He looked at Priya, then back at Felix, and Priya understood that the two of them had been having conversations without her—or rather, that Tomas had been having conversations with himself about her, deciding what she could bear to hear.

"Just say it," she said.

Tomas rubbed the back of his neck. "The company boats are back. Two of them, anchored past the breakwater. Flying proper flags this time, registered, legal as church. They're not diving. They're just sitting there."

"Waiting for what?" Felix asked, though Priya could see he knew.

"For the hearing to go one way or the other. For you to be arrested, maybe. Or cleared. Or—" Tomas shrugged. "Or for you to get tired. Men get tired, Felix. Even men who sand rails like they're being paid by the hour."

Felix set down the sanding block. For a long moment he looked past them, past the breakwater, to where the two ships sat on the horizon line like punctuation marks at the end of a sentence nobody wanted to finish reading.

"When I ran," he said quietly, "I always knew what I was running from. Debts. Warrants. My own face. Simple arithmetic—you leave before the sum comes due." He picked up the block again. "This is different. Now I'm waiting for things to arrive. Summons. Lawyers. Maybe worse. And I find I don't mind it. Isn't that the strangest thing you ever heard?"

"No," Priya said. "It's the first sane thing you've ever said."

* * *

They worked through the morning. The *Marianne V* needed everything—new planking where the hunters' wake had opened her seams, a rebuilt winch, the anchor windlass stripped and greased. Priya re-caulked while Tomas fed her cord, and above them Felix swung between the mast and the deck rigging like a man who'd finally stopped being afraid of heights, or of falling, or of whatever it was he'd actually been afraid of all those years.

At noon the boy came. Fifteen, maybe sixteen, barefoot, holding a package wrapped in oilcloth like it might bite.

"You're the crew of the diver," he said. "The one in the papers."

"We're nobody's crew in the papers," Tomas started, but Priya waved him off.

"Who sent you?"

"A woman. Journalist. She said—" the boy recited it carefully, obviously memorized— "*he'll know what it means: the manifest, page nine.*"

Felix came down the rigging fast after that.

Inside the oilcloth was a photocopy. Page nine of the manifest they'd pulled from the hold—the manifest of cargo that was never cargo, the list of people the navy had erased from one hull and one history. Someone had annotated it. In the margin beside a single line item, in red ink, someone had written a date, a port, and a name.

The name was Felix's. His real one. His original one, older than the warrants, older than the debts—the name from the very bottom of his buried life, the one even Devi Raghunathan's stories hadn't dug up.

Beside it, in the same red ink: *ALIVE. ACCOUNTED FOR.*

"He's alive," Felix said. His voice did something Priya had never heard it do—cracked, just slightly, like ice over moving water. "One of them. From the hold. One of the people they shipped out made it off."

"That's good," Tomas said. "That's—Felix, that's good, isn't it?"

"It's a witness." Felix stared at the page. "A witness who knows my name. Who's been alive all this time, somewhere, while I was—" He stopped. Started again. "Devi wants me to meet him. Before the hearing. She says if he testifies too, the whole thing comes apart. The company, the navy, all of it."

"And if he doesn't want to be found?" Priya asked.

Felix looked at her, and she saw the old arithmetic start up behind his eyes—the flight math, the exit routes, the tide tables of a man who'd spent twenty years calculating how fast he could disappear. She watched him do it. And then she watched him stop.

"He waited thirty years in the dark because of what happened in that hull," Felix said. "I can wait a week in the light."

* * *

Evening came down over Kalapettai the way it always did, gold going to copper going to violet, the fishing boats coming home in loose squadrons, their lights stuttering on one by one across the water. Priya sat on the foredeck with her legs dangling over the bow, and one by one the others joined her—Tomas with his radio playing low, the cook with a bottle he pretended was for sharing, and last of all Felix, who lowered himself onto the deck beside her with the careful movements of a man whose body had opinions about ladders now.

Out past the breakwater, the two company ships burned their riding lights. Watching. Waiting. Let them wait.

"They'll offer you money again," Priya said. "After the hearing. Whatever happens. They always do."

"Probably."

"And?"

Felix was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he nodded at the harbor—at the boats, the lights, the smell of frying fish drifting off the quay, the whole loud ordinary machinery of a place where people stayed.

"When I was a kid," he said, "we lived above a chandlery in a port like this one. My father repaired nets. Every morning I'd watch the deepwater ships leave and think, *that's me someday, that's the only honest thing, to keep moving, because anything that stays gets found.*" He turned the photocopy over in his hands, folded and unfolded it. "Took me forty years to notice those ships always came back. Same harbors. Same faces. You don't get found by staying, Priya. You get found by leaving. Staying is the only place anybody ever actually finds you."

She looked at him. Sanding dust still in his hair. Hands ruined from rope and salt and honest work. A man with two names, both of them public now, both of them his.

"Tomas says the windlass will be done Thursday," she said. "And there's a charter inquiry from a marine survey outfit—legitimate, checked twice. Six days, coastal run. Good pay."

Felix smiled, and it reached all the way up, no shadow behind it, no exit already calculated and held in reserve.

"Six days," he said. "I'll be here."

Below them the harbor settled into night, and the *Marianne V* rode her moorings—anchored, not tied down, anchored—and Priya found she wasn't keeping her bag by the door anymore either. Somewhere out beyond the lights, a witness with a thirty-year-old secret was deciding whether to come in from the dark, and somewhere ashore, men with quiet phones were making their own calculations. But that was tomorrow's weather.

Tonight the tide came in, gentle and certain, lifting the hull the way it lifted every hull that stayed to meet it.
