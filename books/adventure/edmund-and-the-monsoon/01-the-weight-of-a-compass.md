## 1. The Weight of a Compass

The compass had been his father's, and August Ravensworth had been trying to sell it for three weeks.

Not because he wanted to. Because the pawnbroker on the corner of the market road kept offering him insultingly small sums for it, and August kept saying no, and the two of them had settled into a kind of ritual — the old man holding it up to the light, squinting at the brass casing, naming a figure that wouldn't cover a week of rooms, and August pocketing it with as much dignity as a man can manage when his entire net worth fits in one jacket pocket and is worth less than the jacket.

"Brass is brass," the pawnbroker said this morning, which was his way of saying good morning.

"It's a surveyor's compass," August said. "It ran the first full traverse of the Kessarin headwaters. My father held it when the river was still a rumor."

"Brass," the pawnbroker said, "is brass," and August walked out with the compass still in his pocket and eleven rupees to his name, which was enough for one more night at the Blue Heron, if the Blue Heron's definition of *night* remained as generous as it had been lately.

The Blue Heron was not a good bar. It was a bar that had given up on being anything else, which August found honest. The ceiling fans turned with the weary patience of animals that had learned not to hope. A ceiling tile sagged over table four like a man dozing off mid-conversation. The beer came in bottles whose labels had faded to a color best described as *regret*, and it was cold, and it was eleven rupees a bottle if you bought two, and August intended to buy two, because tomorrow he would have nothing, and a man should mark the occasion.

He was three sips into the first bottle when the collector arrived.

* * *

Corvo's collector was named Dhanu, and he was not a large man, which August had learned early was its own kind of warning. Large men needed to convince you. Dhanu simply arrived, the way weather arrives, and waited for you to adjust.

He took the stool beside August without being invited, which was also his way. He ordered nothing. The barman, who had seen him come in, had already begun polishing glasses with the focused intensity of a man hoping to become invisible.

"Ravensworth." Dhanu said the name the way you'd read it off a shipping manifest. "You're a difficult man to find."

"No, I'm not. I'm here every night. That's the opposite of difficult to find."

"Corvo prefers his debtors to move around a little. It shows initiative." Dhanu folded his hands on the bar. "The number is forty-two thousand. You know the number."

"I know the number. I've memorized the number. I've composed small songs about the number."

"Corvo doesn't like songs."

"Corvo doesn't like anything. That's what makes him consistent." August turned the bottle slowly on the wet ring it had made. "Dhanu, I'm going to be honest with you, because you're the only person in this town who talks to me and I've grown attached. I have eleven rupees. Tomorrow I have a compass worth maybe two hundred, and a man who keeps offering me forty for it out of what I can only assume is spite. There is no version of the arithmetic where this ends well for Corvo."

"Corvo knows your arithmetic." Dhanu's voice didn't change at all. That was the unsettling thing about him — nothing about him ever changed. Not his voice, not his posture, not the mild attention in his eyes, which were the color of dishwater and about as warm. "He also knows what you used to be. He knows about the Kessarin traverse. He knows you ran the Sengma gorge at flood stage when the survey company said it couldn't be done, and he knows why the survey company blacklisted you afterward, and he thinks — and I'm quoting him, Ravensworth, because he made me memorize it — he thinks that a man who ruined himself doing the right thing is a man who can be ruined into doing the wrong one."

August looked at his beer.

"That's a very Corvo thing to say," he said finally.

"He also said to tell you he's a patient man, but patience is a loan too. It accrues." Dhanu stood, unhurried, and put a folded slip of paper on the bar between them. Not money. An address. "There's a woman at the River Rest hotel. Room nine. She's been asking after river men, and the only name anyone would give her is yours, because everyone else in this town is either afraid of you or ashamed of you, and she struck Corvo's people as someone who'd prefer the first."

"And why would Corvo send me work?"

"Corvo doesn't send you work. Corvo sends you information. What you do with it is your own affair — until it isn't." Dhanu paused at the door, and for the first time all evening something in his face moved, some faint suggestion that beneath the weather there was a man, and the man was tired. "Ravensworth. Take the job. Corvo's patience is long, but it isn't infinite, and I've watched him wait out men who were better at surviving than you'll ever be."

Then he was gone, and the bar's noise came back in around the space he'd occupied like water filling a hole.

August sat for a long time. Then he finished the first bottle, ordered the second, and unfolded the slip of paper. The River Rest. Room nine. He knew the hotel — it was the one with the generator that ran from six to midnight and the lobby fan that had been broken since the last government, whichever government that had been.

He should have thrown the paper away. He thought about throwing the paper away with real commitment, the way you think about quitting smoking or learning the violin — sincerely, and while doing nothing of the kind.

Instead he drank his beer, and when he'd finished it he walked out into the wet heat of the border town evening, past the pawnbroker's shuttered window, and turned left toward the River Rest, because eleven rupees was not a future, and a rope is a rope even when you can feel the loop at the end of it brushing against your neck.

* * *

Room nine's occupant had chosen the hotel's only table in the courtyard, under the one working light, and she was waiting for him before he'd made it across the lobby, which meant someone had told her he was coming, which meant Dhanu had called ahead, which meant August had been managed from the moment the door closed behind the collector. He noted this. He filed it. He was too tired and too broke to do anything with it.

She was maybe fifty, silver at her temples, dressed in the kind of expedition khaki that costs more than a month of the Blue Heron. She had a folder squared precisely on the table in front of her and a cup of tea she plainly wasn't drinking, and when she looked up at him her eyes did the thing he'd spent his career dreading in clients: they took his measure and found the measurement disappointing.

"August Ravensworth," she said. "The Kessarin traverse. The Sengma gorge."

"Both correct. You've done your reading."

"I've done my reading." She gestured at the chair across from her. He sat. "My name is Ansel. I represent a consortium of mining interests. I'm going to be direct with you, because I'm told you respond badly to being handled."

"Who told you that?"

"Everyone."

"Everyone's right." He leaned back. The chair complained. "So. Directly."

Ansel opened the folder. Photographs, fanned out with a curator's care. A river valley, green and violent with rain. A survey camp — tents, a generator, the tidy geometry of a well-funded operation. Faces. Eight of them, arranged in a grid like a school yearbook. "The Halcyon Expedition. Nine people. They went upriver eleven weeks ago to survey a tributary valley. Six weeks ago, their satellite check-ins stopped. Five weeks ago, the charter company that dropped them went looking and found the second camp abandoned. Since then, nothing. No bodies, no radio, no ransom demand. The consortium has interests in the region it would prefer not to have disturbed by a search party full of journalists and government men. We want someone quiet. Someone who knows these rivers. Someone expendable."

"That last part was very honest."

"I find honesty economizes on time." She slid a paper across. A number. It had more digits than anything August had seen in two years, and one of them, near the front, was the digit he needed most: the one that began *forty*.

Forty-two thousand. To the rupee. He stared at it and felt something in his chest he refused to name, because naming it would have meant admitting how badly he wanted it.

"That's a coincidence of a number," he said.

"It's not a coincidence. Your debt is a matter of record in this town. The consortium believes a man works harder when the money has a destination." She folded her hands, mirroring Dhanu without knowing it, or perhaps knowing it exactly. "Full payment on recovery of the expedition or evidence of its fate. Half up front, wired tonight. Do we have terms?"

August looked at the photographs. Eight faces. He looked at the number. He thought about the pawnbroker, and the Blue Heron, and Dhanu's dishwater eyes, and the small songs he'd composed about a figure he could now make disappear with a signature.

He thought: *this is a rope.*

He did not think: *this is also a noose.* That thought was eleven weeks and one flooded valley away, and no one had ever accused August Ravensworth of seeing the river before he was in it.

"Half tonight," he said.

"Half tonight."

"Then we have terms." He picked up the pen she offered. At the door of the courtyard, pausing with the folder under his arm, he found himself asking the question he'd been circling all evening. "The Halcyon Expedition. Nine people. Why does a mining consortium care enough about nine missing surveyors to pay a blacklisted guide a small fortune?"

Ansel was already walking away, her voice drifting back over her shoulder, pleasant and final as a closing door.

"Because we sent them, Mr. Ravensworth. And because what they found wasn't in the brochure."
