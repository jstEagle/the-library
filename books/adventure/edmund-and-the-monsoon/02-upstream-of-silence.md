## 2. Upstream of Silence

The boat was called the *Charitable Intent*, which August took as either a joke or a threat, and after three days in Mercer's company he had narrowed it down to both.

She was a converted patrol craft, forty feet of gunmetal stubbornness with a shallow draft and an engine that grumbled like a debt collector. Mercer had brought six crew, two crates of equipment August wasn't allowed to open, and a wardrobe that made no sense whatsoever for monsoon country—linen shirts pressed to knife-edge creases, a different silk scarf for each morning, leather shoes that he polished nightly on the deck while the river tried its best to murder them.

"You're staring," Mercer said on the fourth morning, without looking up from his coffee.

"I'm wondering how long those shoes will last."

"Longer than your optimism, I suspect." He smiled. It was a good smile. That was the trouble with it—it had clearly been fitted by professionals, like everything else about him. "We make the confluence by noon. There's a village there. We'll ask our questions."

August leaned against the rail and watched the banks slide past. The river had thickened overnight, gone the color of strong tea, carrying whole trees downstream at a walking pace. The monsoon was still weeks out, officially, but the water already knew something the calendars didn't.

"Questions," August said. "You keep using that word like it means questions."

"What would you prefer I use?"

"Nothing. I'd prefer you didn't answer." He scratched his jaw. Three days without a razor; the stubble felt like armor. "I've run rivers for mining outfits before, Mercer. The ones who talk about asking questions are the ones who bring shovels."

Mercer sipped his coffee and let the silence sit between them like a third passenger. Then: "Mr. Ravensworth. Do you know what I admire about you?"

"Your shoes?"

"Your honesty about your own corruption. Most men in your position pretend they're taking this job out of curiosity. You know it's the money. It's refreshing. It also makes you predictable, which is useful." He set the cup down with a small ceramic click. "The Halcyon Expedition went silent eleven weeks ago. Nine people. Satellite phones, emergency beacons, the works. Either they're dead—and we grieve—or they've gone quiet on purpose, and then we have a different kind of problem."

"And if they've gone quiet on purpose?"

"Then someone will need to explain to me why." Mercer's smile returned, warmer now, which somehow made it worse. "You find people, Mr. Ravensworth. That's what the file says. Find them, and we'll all go home rich or bereaved, whichever applies."

* * *

The village at the confluence had a name that appeared on no map August owned, which was normal, and no dogs, which was not.

He noticed it before anything else—the absence. River villages ran on dogs the way cities ran on traffic lights. They announced strangers, chased pigs, stole fish, slept in the middle of every path. This village had none. It had chickens, it had children who watched the boat from behind a granary with the stillness of herons, it had an old man mending a net who never once looked up. But no dogs.

"They were paid to move them," said the headman's daughter, when August finally got her talking, trading on a shared language of gestures and three words of river dialect he hadn't used in six years. She was maybe nineteen, sharp-eyed, unimpressed by Mercer's linen. "Before the last boat came through."

"The last boat," August said carefully. "When was that?"

Her eyes flicked toward Mercer, standing too far away to hear but close enough to see, and she did the thing that people do when they are afraid and polite at the same time: she answered a different question entirely. "The surveyors were kind," she said. "They bought fish. They showed photographs of their families." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "That was before."

"Before what?"

But she was already folding her hands, already becoming a stranger again, and the old man with the net spoke for the first time—not to August, but to the air, in dialect, one sentence. August caught only the shape of it, but the daughter translated with her face rather than her mouth: *We sold our memory. Don't ask us to buy it back.*

Back on the boat, Mercer was pleasant about it. "Frightened villagers. Common enough up here."

"They weren't frightened of the surveyors."

"No?"

"They fed them. Photographed them. And then somebody came afterward and paid the village to forget, and *that's* who frightens them." August watched Mercer's face do nothing at all, which was itself an answer. "You knew the expedition passed through here."

"I knew they filed a resupply request from this district. It's in the record." He turned his scarf-end over in his fingers, considering it. "You have a suspicious mind, Mr. Ravensworth. It must have served you well, once."

"It ruined me, actually. That's why I'm cheap."

* * *

Two days further upriver they found the second village burned.

Not burned—abandoned, and then burned, which was a different thing and a worse one. The houses stood black against the green, roofs collapsed inward, but the burning had been tidy, almost administrative. No scatter of belongings. No animals dead in the doorways. The cooking pots were stacked. The boats were gone.

"Resettlement," Mercer said, standing in the ash with his hands clasped behind him like a man touring a garden. "The government does it sometimes. Flood zones, mineral claims."

"There's no flood zone here. There's no claim registered here—I checked the registries in town, that's half of what Ansel paid me for." August crouched and sifted ash through his fingers. Underneath, the ground was hard-packed where floors had been swept daily for generations. "Somebody told these people to leave, and they left so fast they stacked their pots first, because that's what you do when you expect to come back. And then somebody burned the village so they couldn't."

"Or so nobody could follow them into it." Mercer crouched beside him, immaculate, and picked up a scrap of charred wood. "You're building a story, Mr. Ravensworth. Stories are comforting. Facts are less so. Here is a fact: nine people are missing, and you are being paid to find them, and every hour you spend narrating is an hour they might be bleeding."

August looked at him for a long moment. "Do you ever get tired? Of talking like a brochure?"

"Constantly." And for one unguarded half-second, something moved behind Mercer's eyes—something tired and young and human—before the polish slid back over it like water closing over a stone. "Come along. Daylight's spending itself."

* * *

They found the second camp on the sixth day, in a bend of the river where the current slowed and the jungle leaned in close enough to touch the water.

August saw it before the boat did—a strip of silvered tarpaulin through the trees—and held up a hand. Mercer, to his credit, actually slowed. They put in downstream and walked in, August first, because that was what they were paying him for, and because walking second meant trusting the man behind him, and he wasn't ready to trust anyone whose shoes matched his scarf.

The camp had been a proper one: four tents, a cook shelter, a gear line, a surveyor's bench built from split bamboo. Eleven weeks of weather had softened it, greened it, started the slow work of taking it back. But the weather hadn't done this.

Every tent had been opened. Not torn—opened, deliberately, the zippers run back, the contents turned out and gone through with the patience of a customs inspection. Notebooks, instruments, clothing, food: all of it lay in the rain-mud, sorted into piles. Someone had spent hours here. Someone had been looking for something specific.

And they hadn't taken anything.

That was the detail that walked up August's spine and settled between his shoulders like a hand. He'd been robbed before, professionally speaking—he'd been robbed by Corvo's people twice, and once by his own deckhand, and he knew the grammar of looting. Looters take. Looters take the boots and the batteries and the tinned meat and the whiskey, and they leave the rest in contempt. These piles were complete. Boots still laced. A rifle racked against the bench, bolt intact, mud rusting the action. A satellite phone lying face-up in the muck, its battery compartment open and empty—but the phone itself left behind, because a phone without a battery is a paperweight, and paperweights don't tell stories.

"Ransacked," Mercer said behind him, "but not looted."

"You read my notes."

"I read everything." Mercer moved through the camp the way he moved through everything, touching nothing, missing nothing. He stopped at the cook shelter and lifted the edge of a tarp with one finger. Beneath it: a surveyor's transit, wrapped in oilcloth, oiled against the damp. Protected. Left. "Whoever did this wanted information, not equipment. And they wanted whoever comes next to know they'd been thorough."

"Or they wanted us to think that."

"Oh, I do enjoy you." Mercer said it lightly, but his eyes had gone flat and busy, cataloguing. "Six tents' worth of gear. The expedition fielded nine."

August counted again, slower. Four sleeping tents, a supply tent, the cook shelter. Six. "Maybe they split up."

"Maybe." Mercer turned a slow circle, taking in the treeline, the river, the ridge above. "Or maybe three of them left in a hurry, and whoever searched this place knows exactly which three, and exactly why, and exactly where they went." He smiled at August, and this time the smile reached all the way to his eyes, and August wished devoutly that it hadn't. "Find them, Mr. Ravensworth. All nine. Dead or alive, found or fled. The consortium pays for completeness."

That night August sat on the deck and couldn't sleep, and finally gave up trying. He sat with his back against the rail and watched the river run black under a sky bruising purple with coming rain, and he made himself say it plainly, the way he used to say hard things to clients before the drinking made it easier to lie.

This isn't a rescue.

A rescue doesn't burn villages. A rescue doesn't pay people to forget. A rescue doesn't leave a rifle leaning against a bench like a signature. Somewhere upstream, nine people had found something, and the finding had gone wrong in a way that required silence, and the consortium hadn't sent him to bring anyone home. They'd sent him because a blacklisted guide with debts and no family and no reputation left to lose was the cheapest possible instrument for whatever came after finding. He was not the search party.

He was the mop.

He thought about turning the boat around. He actually stood up, one hand on the rail, and looked back downriver at the dark, and calculated—fuel, current, the six crew, the two crates he wasn't allowed to open. Then he thought about Corvo's collector, and the number, and what happened to men who owed that number and ran.

He sat back down.

Ahead of them, far up the valley, lightning flickered inside a cloud with no sound attached, like a signal lamp sending something in a code he couldn't yet read. The rain began a few minutes later, soft and steady, the monsoon's first courtesy call.

In the morning, washed clean and cold, they found the map fragment pinned under a stone at the edge of camp—torn from a larger sheet, water-stained, marked in a geologist's careful hand with a route leading away from the river, up a side gorge, toward a village that appeared on none of their charts.

Someone had wanted the next party to find it.

August just couldn't make himself believe it was a friend.
