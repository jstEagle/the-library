## 3. What the River Kept

The map fragment had been Mercer's idea of generosity.

"Found it in the second camp," he'd said, holding it out between two fingers like something he'd fished from a drain. "Torn. Useless on its own. But you're the river man—perhaps you can make sense of what's left."

August had turned it over twice under the tarp of the camp that night, rain drumming the nylon above him, Mercer sleeping—or pretending to sleep—three meters away with his boots on. Boots on. August had guided enough men through enough bad country to know what boots-on meant. It meant a man expected to run, or expected to chase.

The fragment was a quarter of the Halcyon Expedition's survey map, the upper-left corner of the river valley, and someone had torn it with intent. Not weathered, not rotted—torn, along a ruler-straight line that had removed exactly the portion showing the middle reaches of the valley. Where a village would be, if villages existed there. Where a geologist with a sense of humor might write *beneath the village that must not be surveyed* in a notebook, if geologists wrote such things.

What remained was enough for August. Twenty years of reading water had taught him to read paper the same way: by what the current left behind. The contour lines on the surviving quarter showed a gorge narrowing to a throat, a side-canyon feeding in from the east, and—penciled in a hurried hand, in a code that wasn't code at all but a surveyor's shorthand for *look here*—a small circle with the letters *H-4* and an arrow pointing downstream.

H-4. The fourth hydrological station, if the Halcyon team had followed standard practice. Or the fourth something else.

"You're smiling," Mercer had said from the dark, without opening his eyes.

"Am I."

"Men smile when they think they've figured something out. It's rarely anything worth smiling about."

August had put the map fragment in his shirt pocket, against his chest, where a man keeps the things he isn't ready to admit he cares about, and had said, "Get some sleep. We move at first light."

He had not slept. He had listened to Mercer breathe in slow, even, theatrical intervals, and he had thought about the empty villages downstream, paid to forget, and the ransacked camp that had been searched but not looted, and he had arrived at the conclusion he'd been circling for three days the way a man circles a dead animal in the water, not wanting to confirm what it is.

They weren't looking for survivors. They were looking for paper.

* * *

First light came gray and sullen, the monsoon clouds sitting on the valley walls like a lid on a pot. Mercer stayed with the boat and two of the hired porters, who had stopped making eye contact with anyone on the second day and now moved through the landscape like men apologizing to it.

"You'll take the east bank," Mercer said, handing August a machete and a radio that probably worked. "The station should be a kilometer up the side-canyon. Call in when you find it."

"And if I find something else?"

"Then you'll call in about that too." Mercer smiled his polished smile, the one that fit his face the way a fitted sheet fits a bed—smooth, complete, hiding everything underneath. "You're a guide, Ravensworth. Guide me to the thing I'm paying for."

*The thing I'm paying for.* Not *the people I'm paying to find.* August had noted the grammar the way he noted a change in water color—something shifting underneath, something about to drop.

The side-canyon swallowed him within twenty minutes. The jungle here was old and wet and indifferent, a cathedral of green where the light came down in coins and everything dripped. Leeches found his boots within the first hundred meters. He let them. A man who'd been drinking away his last money in a border-town bar six weeks ago didn't have the right to complain about leeches.

He found the first marker at the half-kilometer point: a strip of survey tape, pink once, now the color of old skin, tied to a sapling. Then another. The Halcyon team had been methodical. Methodical men don't vanish. Methodical men get *made* to vanish.

The station, when he found it, was a clearing with a concrete benchmark and a rusted instrument tripod, and it told him nothing except that the expedition had been here, and gone. But the arrow on the map fragment hadn't pointed at the station. It had pointed downstream, and downstream meant the river, and the river meant the shallows below the gorge throat where, in August's experience, rivers keep the things they take.

He descended through the trees toward the sound of water, and the sound grew, and the air changed, and then the canyon opened and there was the river—high, brown, muscular with early monsoon, hauling debris past him like it had somewhere to be.

He walked the shallows along the eastern bank, scanning the eddies and the snag-piles, calling himself ten kinds of fool. Looking for a body. Looking for a body *on purpose*, which was a different thing entirely from stumbling across one, and he wasn't sure which kind of man that made him.

The river kept him waiting an hour. Rivers are patient; it's the one thing they have over people.

Then, snagged in the roots of a fallen fig tree at the tail of an eddy, he saw the yellow.

A rain jacket. Expedition-yellow, Halcyon's color, filled with river and time. August waded out, the current shoving at his knees like a drunk trying to start a fight, and got a hand on the jacket and pulled, and the river gave it up with the reluctance of a creditor.

Dr. Edmund Hale. August knew the name from the briefing file—lead geologist, fifty-one, twenty years in the field, a reputation for being *difficult*, which in the consortium's language meant honest. The river had kept him three weeks and had been gentle about it, all things considered. The face was past being a face. But the arms—August noted this with the clinical part of his brain that had seen too much—the arms were crossed over his chest, and tucked beneath them, wrapped in three layers of plastic and bound with survey tape, was a notebook.

A man doesn't wrap his notebook in plastic and hold it to his chest as he drowns unless the notebook matters more than the drowning.

August dragged Hale to the bank with the current fighting him the whole way, and knelt there in the shallows, breathing hard, water in his boots, and said, because it seemed like the thing to say, "Sorry, mate. Somebody should have."

He opened the notebook on his knee.

The early pages were pure geology—strike, dip, assay numbers, the tidy handwriting of a man who loved his work. Then, a third of the way in, the handwriting changed. Got bigger. Got *angrier*.

*The body is not in the gorge. The body is under the valley floor. It runs from the eastern scarp to the river—a strike extension of the Halcyon trend, but richer. Orders of magnitude richer. And it sits directly beneath the village.*

August turned pages with fingers that had gone cold, though the day was warm.

*The consortium's model was wrong. Deliberately wrong? I don't know anymore. What I know: if this data reaches the company, the village is finished. Relocation is the polite word. There are two hundred people living on top of the richest ore body I have ever seen, and I have been asked, in writing, to confirm the survey so the claim can be filed.*

*I have been asked what I will tell my colleagues who disappeared last week.*

*Chen went into the treeline yesterday and did not come back. I am going to give the data to the village. They can decide. It is their ground. It was always their ground.*

*If you are reading this, the river kept me. Keep it from them.*

August sat in the shallows with a dead man's diary in his hands and felt the last comfortable lie of his life go out of him like air from a punctured boat.

*Keep it from them.* Them. Mercer. The consortium. The polished man with the polished smile and the radio that probably worked, three kilometers downstream, waiting for August to call in and report the thing he'd been paid to find.

And Corvo. August's stomach turned over slowly, the way it did in big water. Corvo, who'd been too easy about the debt lately. Corvo, who'd said *go north, there's work, it'll square us.* Corvo hadn't squared anything. Corvo had *sold* him. Sold his passage, his name, his blacklisted river knowledge, to men who needed a guide nobody would miss.

He was never meant to come back. He'd been hired the way you hire a mine detector.

August laughed—once, short, no humor in it, the sound bouncing off the canyon walls and coming back smaller. Twenty years of reading water and he'd missed the biggest rip current of his life until it had him by the ankles.

He was still kneeling there, notebook in hand, deciding—not deciding, *knowing*, the way you know a line through a rapid before you run it—when the jungle went quiet.

Not silent. Quiet. The particular, deliberate quiet of a place where the birds have all agreed to shut up at once.

August didn't move. He let his eyes do the work, sliding across the treeline above the bank, reading it like water. Twenty meters upstream, a shadow that wasn't a shadow shifted its weight. Thirty meters beyond it, a branch that hadn't moved kept not moving in the wrong way. Two of them. Maybe three. Rifles, almost certainly—Mercer hadn't struck him as a man who traveled with machetes alone.

The radio in his jacket pocket weighed four hundred grams and felt like an anchor. They'd hear the call. They'd trace the signal. And if he didn't call, they'd come looking, and the looking would end here, at the eddy, next to what was left of Dr. Edmund Hale.

*Keep it from them.*

August slipped the notebook inside his shirt, against his chest, where a man keeps the things he's decided to care about, and did the only thing a river man does when the water turns against him.

He went into the current.

It took him like a hand closing, brown and cold and furious, and he let it, tucking, rolling, surfacing twice for air, letting the river that had kept Edmund Hale carry him downstream past the watchers in the treeline, past the mouth of the side-canyon, past the point where a man with a radio was waiting for a call that would never come.

A shot cracked through the rain. Then another, slapping the water a meter to his left, close enough to feel.

August dove, and the jungle rose up ahead on the southern bank—a wall of green, dripping, indifferent, and for the first time in twenty years, the only home he had.

He hauled himself out of the water at the roots of a fig tree, gasping, bleeding a little from a rock strike on his forearm, and looked back once at the river that had tried to keep him too.

Somewhere upstream, men were coming. Somewhere upriver, a village sat on top of the richest ground in the country, not knowing what it was worth, which was the only thing keeping it alive.

And somewhere in his shirt, against his heart, a dead man's handwriting told him what he was now: not a guide, not a debtor, not a disgraced man buying his way back.

A courier. The last one.

August turned south, into the trees, and started to run.
