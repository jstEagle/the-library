## 4. Ilsa

Ilsa Vane had learned to measure her days in small dishonesties.

She told the villagers she was a botanist. She told herself she wasn't afraid. She told the mountain, every morning when she climbed to the ridge and scanned the river for boats, that she would know what to do if one came.

The first two lies were almost true. The third was wearing thin as old rope.

Three months since the Halcyon Expedition had ended—not with a crash or a flood but with an argument around a cook fire that turned into something worse. Three months since she'd watched men she'd respected decide that a vein of ore running beneath a living village was a problem of logistics rather than conscience. Three months since she'd run into the jungle at night with nothing but a field kit and the clothes she stood in, and been found at dawn by a woman named Sari who looked at her mud-caked face and said, simply, "You'll want rice."

That was the whole of her welcome. You'll want rice. It remained the most generous sentence anyone had ever said to her.

Now she lived in the house behind Sari's, mended nets badly, catalogued plants nobody needed catalogued, and tried very hard not to think about the notebook hidden in a waterproof sleeve beneath the floorboards—pages that could buy this valley's death if they ever reached the wrong hands.

The morning the stranger came up the river trail, she was teaching Sari's granddaughter, Mei, to tell edible ferns from the ones that made your tongue go numb.

"That one," Ilsa said, pointing. "And that one looks like it, but it's a liar."

"Like you?" Mei asked, seven years old and merciless.

"Exactly like me," Ilsa said. "Which is why you should always check twice."

The stranger appeared where the trail bent out of the fern brake—a big man, rain-soaked, moving with the deliberate care of someone who knew the ground could kill him. He carried no rifle. He did carry the look of a man carrying something heavier than gear, and Ilsa's stomach dropped through the soles of her boots, because she'd seen that look before. On Mercer's people. On the survey crew that had come sniffing in June.

*Consortium,* she thought. *They sent someone at last.*

She put herself between him and Mei without deciding to. The man stopped ten paces off and raised both hands, palms out, which was either surrender or theater.

"I'm looking for shelter before dark," he said. His accent was lowland, his voice roughened by river travel. "Storm's coming. I can pay."

"We don't want money."

"Good," he said. "I don't have any."

Despite everything—the fear, the three months of watching the water—Ilsa felt something in her chest twitch toward laughter and hated it. She kept her voice flat. "The village doesn't take visitors."

"The village," he said, glancing past her at Mei, who was peering around Ilsa's leg with open curiosity, "has a child standing in the rain, and a storm coming down the valley like a freight train. I'll sleep under a rock if I have to. But I'd rather not die stupid."

"You might anyway," Ilsa said. "Depends what you're carrying besides that pack."

He met her eyes then, and whatever he saw in hers made him go still. "You think I'm one of them."

"I think you're either one of them or you're food for them. From here it's hard to tell the difference."

Something moved across his face—not guilt exactly. Recognition. "Fair," he said quietly. "I'm August Ravensworth. I was hired to find the Halcyon Expedition." He paused. "I found what was left of it instead. A dead man in the shallows. His notebook was gone from where it should've been, and I've been wondering ever since who took it."

Ilsa's hand drifted, entirely on its own, toward the pocket where the map fragment lived. He noticed. Of course he noticed.

"So," he said. "You're the survivor."

"I'm a botanist," she said.

"Right," he said, in the tone of a man agreeing with a liar because he was one himself. "And I'm a tourist."

* * *

Sari overruled them both, because Sari overruled everyone. August got the dry corner of the longhouse and a bowl of rice, and Ilsa got assigned to watch him, which suited her fine; she intended to watch him right up until she understood him, and then decide whether he needed to be fed or buried.

He ate like a man who'd forgotten food existed. He thanked the cook twice. When Mei crept close to examine his compass, he didn't shoo her—he opened it flat on his palm and let her watch the needle tremble north.

"It's broken," Mei announced.

"It's confused," August said. "There's iron ore in that hillside. Enough of it to pull the needle sideways." He caught himself, and Ilsa watched him catch himself—the flicker of a man realizing he'd just said *ore* out loud in a village that had every reason to fear the word. "Geology," he added lamely. "Rocks. Boring."

"Fascinating," Ilsa said sweetly, from across the fire. "Tell us more about rocks."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she held the look without blinking, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with hostility. Two people checking each other's locks, finding them rusted open.

Later, while the storm built its pressure against the mountainside and the longhouse shutters rattled, he sat beside her near the door, uninvited, and spoke low enough that only she could hear.

"How many of them are left? Your colleagues."

"None." She kept her eyes on the rain. "They argued. About whether to report what they found. Some wanted to sell it. One wanted to bury it. The argument—" She stopped. Started again. "The argument didn't end well, and then the jungle finished it. I ran. They sheltered me." She nodded at the sleeping forms along the walls. "People who owed me nothing."

"Why hide here instead of walking out?"

"Because everything I know is worth killing for, and the only safe place for it is nowhere." She turned to him. "Your turn. Who hired you?"

"A mining consortium. Through a fixer named Mercer. Polite man. Terrible cologne." August's mouth twisted. "They told me it was a rescue mission. By camp two, I figured out it was a cleanup. By the body in the shallows, I understood I wasn't coming back. Guides who see too much don't get paid, Ilsa. They get retired."

"And yet here you are, telling me all this."

"Here I am," he agreed. "Telling you all this."

"Why?"

He was quiet a moment. Rain drummed the roof like impatient fingers.

"Because I've spent four years being exactly what people paid me to be," he said finally, "and I'm tired in a way sleep doesn't touch. And because when I saw that man dead in the water, I thought: there but for a different set of debts, go I." He glanced at her. "You know how that is. I think you do."

She did. God help her, she did. She looked at this stranger with his ruined hands and his careful honesty arriving four days too late to be convenient, and she thought: *we are the same kind of tired.* Two liars sitting by a fire, each holding a truth that could burn the world down, each pretending to be smaller than they were.

"Don't trust me," she said.

"Wasn't going to."

"Good. Because I don't trust you."

"Also good." He almost smiled. It was a ruin of a smile, but it was trying. "We understand each other perfectly."

* * *

The raid came at the hour when night is thickest, and it came without warning because Mercer's scouts were professionals.

Ilsa woke to the dogs—not barking but falling silent, which was worse—and then to a whistle from the watch post, cut short. She was moving before her mind caught up, grabbing Mei from her mat because the girl slept nearest the door, pressing her down behind the grain bins with a hand over her mouth and a whisper: *not a sound, not a breath.*

Boots in the compound. Flashlight beams slicing through shutter slats. Voices, low and clipped, speaking the trade-tongue of the lowland crews. Searching the houses one by one.

Through the slats Ilsa saw them reach the headman's house, saw the beam swing across the wall where the family portraits hung, and heard the scout say a word she knew: *the woman scientist.* They had a description. They had orders. This was never a search—it was a retrieval.

A child cried out. Not Mei—one of the younger ones, stumbling half-asleep from the far room into the compound, straight into the flashlight beams.

Everything happened fast after that. A scout turned, rifle swinging down off his shoulder—not to aim, Ilsa realized later, just to free his hands—but the motion read wrong in the dark, and the child froze screaming, and the second scout raised his weapon to fire a warning shot that would not have stayed a warning—

August came out of the dark like weather.

She never quite reconstructed how he crossed the compound that fast, only that he did, that he put his body between the muzzle and the child, that the shot went off anyway and took him along the ribs—a hot, ugly graze that spun him half around—and that he stayed on his feet, grabbed the boy by the collar, and shoved him bodily through the doorway into the longhouse before the scouts could decide what they'd just started.

Then the village woke up. Dogs, horns, forty people pouring out with machetes and fire-hardened poles and the pure furious weight of numbers, and the scouts—professionals, yes, but three against a valley—melted back into the treeline the way they'd come.

Afterward, Ilsa knelt beside August in the lamplight, tearing her spare shirt into strips while Sari brought the bitter green paste that closed wounds. The graze ran along his lowest rib, shallow, bleeding freely but not deep. He hissed when she pressed the cloth to it.

"Hold still."

"Wasn't planning on dancing."

"You idiot." Her hands were steady; her voice wasn't, quite. "You walked into gunfire for a child you've known one day."

"He was closer to the bullet than I was." He winced. "Seemed like a paperwork problem I could solve."

"That's not how you talk about it."

"No," he admitted. "It isn't."

She tied off the bandage and sat back on her heels and looked at him—at the sweat on his gray face, at the boy he'd saved already asleep again against his mother, at the whole trembling, defiant, unbroken village around them—and the last of her suspicion went out of her like a lamp in wind. Not because he'd taken a bullet. Plenty of men take bullets for show. But because he hadn't hesitated, and because now, adrenaline draining, he looked less like a hired guide than like a man setting down something he'd carried so long he'd forgotten its shape.

"They'll come back," she said. "More of them. That was a probe."

"I know." He breathed carefully around the wound. "And the storm's still coming. When it breaks, the river takes the lower camps and every easy road out of this valley—for them and for us."

"Then we're trapped together."

"For a few days." He looked at her, and there it was again, that recognition, that terrible mutual honesty. "Ilsa. Whatever you're hiding under those floorboards—it's why they came, and it's why they'll keep coming. Sooner or later you're going to have to tell me what I'm bleeding for."

Outside, thunder rolled down the gorge like a door slamming somewhere deep in the mountain, and the first fat drops of the monsoon began to fall.
