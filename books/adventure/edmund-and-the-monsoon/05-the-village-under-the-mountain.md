## 5. The Village Under the Mountain

**August**

August woke to the smell of woodsmoke and frying plantains, which was a considerable improvement over the smells he'd been waking to for the past decade.

He lay on a woven mat in the headman's house, his ribs aching where Mercer's scout had clipped him two nights ago, and listened to the village come alive around him. A rooster with delusions of grandeur announced dawn from three feet away. Somewhere a woman laughed at something a child had said. A dog scratched itself against the wall of the house with the thoroughness of a creature who had all morning and no appointments.

It was, he thought, the kind of place a man could disappear into. The kind of place that didn't ask what you'd done before you arrived, only whether you could carry water and keep your opinions about other people's cooking to yourself.

Ilsa Vane sat across the fire pit, mending a net with the focused hostility of someone who considered knots a personal enemy. She hadn't spoken more than forty words to him since the raid. He counted it as progress that she'd stopped checking whether he was armed every time he stood up.

"Your shoulder's weeping again," she said, without looking up.

"It's expressing itself."

"It's infected." She set down the net, crossed the room, and inspected the graze with the bedside manner of a field surgeon and the warmth of one. She cleaned it, packed it with a paste that smelled like it could strip paint, and tied it off. "You flinched."

"I'm brave, not stupid."

"You're both, actually. I've watched you." But something in her voice had shifted a degree — not toward trust, exactly. Toward the possibility of it.

The headman, a broad-shouldered man named Toma whose gray hair was braided with river clay, appeared in the doorway and beckoned them both outside. His face had the particular stillness August had learned to read on mountains: the look of a man about to show you how deep the crevasse goes.

* * *

**Ilsa**

Ilsa followed Toma up the switchback trail behind the village, August limping along beside her, and hated how easily she'd fallen into walking next to him. Two days ago she'd have put a machete between them. Then she'd watched him throw himself over a child during the scouts' night raid, taking a blade's edge across the shoulder without a sound, and now here they were, climbing together like colleagues.

Toma stopped at a shelf of rock above the terraces and turned to face the valley.

"Show him," he said.

So Ilsa did what she'd sworn she would never do. She took the geologist's notebook out of its oilcloth wrapping — the notebook they'd pulled from poor Devi's chest in the shallows, waterproofed against his heart like a confession — and opened it to the survey sketch.

"Look at the ore body," she said. "Look at where it sits."

August studied the cross-section. She watched his eyes move from the vein — thick, rich, obscene in its purity — down through the sediment layers to the surface line. To the little marks Devi had drawn there himself. Houses. The shrine. The burial ground under the fig trees.

"It runs under everything," August said quietly.

"Under the homes. Under the graves. Under the well." She closed the notebook. "Devi mapped it because that was his job, and then he wrote 'must not be surveyed' because that was his conscience. The consortium's maps don't have houses on them, August. They have a footprint. Excavation means moving the village or burying it. And given what happened to the rest of us—"

"They'd choose burying." He said it flatly, without surprise, and she realized he'd already known. Not the specifics. The shape of it. Men like Mercer didn't climb into valleys for ore they intended to share.

"The others argued about it," she said. The words came out of her like splinters coming out of skin, one at a time. "For six days. Halvorsen wanted to report it — said the consortium would relocate people humanely, that refusing to report was theft from science. Priya called him an idiot. Devi wouldn't take a side, so he took the notebook and started mapping the village instead, drawing the graves onto the survey like he could make the ore ashamed of itself." Her voice caught. "They were still arguing when Mercer's men came upriver. They died arguing. I ran, and the villagers hid me, and I've been hiding ever since, telling myself I was guarding the data. Guarding it. Like it was mine."

August said nothing for a long moment. Below them, smoke rose from cookfires. A girl was chasing the self-important rooster in circles, both of them equally convinced of their own dignity.

"You drew the graves too?" he asked finally.

"I finished Devi's map. Every house. Every name I knew."

"Then it's not just data anymore. It's testimony." He handed the notebook back with a care that surprised her. "Testimony needs a witness who survives."

She wanted to hate him for saying something so decent. It made the next part harder.

"Toma wants to show you something else," she said. "Something I found in Mercer's abandoned camp. You should sit down for it, but there's nowhere to sit, so brace against the rock."

* * *

**August**

What Toma showed him was a ledger page, water-stained but legible, salvaged from the ransacked second camp by a villager who gathered paper the way other children gathered stones. August read it twice, because the first time his eyes refused to cooperate.

Financing schedule. Disbursement authorizations. And a signature block he knew better than his own heartbeat: **C. Corvo**, written in that elegant, patient hand, each letter placed like a man setting a table.

"He's funding this," August said. "Mercer's whole operation. Corvo is the money."

"Yes," Ilsa said.

Which meant the job offer in that border-town bar hadn't been luck. Which meant the consortium fixer hadn't found a disgraced guide by chance — Corvo had handed him over, pointed him upstream like a dog sent to flush game. His debt hadn't merely followed him into the valley. His debt had *bought his ticket*. Every mile he'd traveled, he'd traveled on Corvo's credit, doing Corvo's reconnaissance for free, marking the trail for men who meant to erase this place off the map and everyone on it.

"I'm not a guide," he said aloud. "I'm a receipt. They sent me up here so they could collect me later, along with the ore."

"That's why I didn't trust you," Ilsa said. "And why I do now. You look like a man who just found out the rope he grabbed is attached to something."

August braced against the rock and laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, though somewhere underneath, absurdly, there was relief. At least the shape of his ruin was finally visible. A man can live with almost any trap if he can see the bars.

Below, the village went about its morning. Toma's granddaughter was teaching the dog to swim in the shallows, an education the dog resisted on principle. An old man repaired a roof ridge, singing badly. The graves under the fig trees kept their silence, holding generations of people who had never once been asked whether they'd like to become a mine.

"These people took me in," August said. "Fed me. Stitched me up. They know I came with Mercer's stamp on me, and they fed me anyway."

"They did the same for me," Ilsa said. "That's the thing about this valley. It doesn't ask where you're from. It asks what you'll do now."

And there it was — the thing he'd been circling since the bar, since the compass, since the first drink he'd bought with money he owed to a smuggler. Belonging, offered freely, in the exact place where keeping it required betraying everyone who'd ever signed his name to anything. Corvo owned his debt. Mercer owned his contract. The consortium owned his blacklisted reputation, such as it was. Every piece of paper with his name on it pointed back downstream.

But nobody owned this. Nobody owned the taste of plantain smoke or the sound of a child losing an argument with a rooster. That belonged to whoever was willing to stand on this shelf of rock and say *no*.

"I need to tell you what I am," August said. "All of it. What I owe, who I owe it to, what they promised me if I hand you over."

Ilsa's jaw tightened. "You're going to tell me they offered you a clean slate."

"A clean passport and a zero balance. Deliver you and the cores, walk away whole." He met her eyes. "I told them I'd think about it. That was yesterday. Before the ledger page. Before—" he gestured at the valley, helpless, "—before breakfast."

"And now?"

"Now I'm thinking about it very differently."

Downstream, faint as a headache, came the sound August had been dreading since dawn: an engine. Not the village's lone pump boat — something bigger, deeper, running against the current with purpose.

Ilsa heard it too. Her face went still.

"That'll be the storm behind the engine," she said. "Monsoon's breaking early this year. And I'd bet the ledger page your friend Corvo doesn't wait for good weather."

The rooster, sensing nothing, crowed triumphantly at the sky.

August picked up the notebook and held it against his chest, the way a dead man once had, and started down the trail toward the river.
