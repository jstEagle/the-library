## 6. Terms

Mercer came to him at dawn, carrying two enamel mugs of coffee like a man delivering a peace treaty.

"Thought you might want this," Mercer said, offering one. "It's terrible. But it's hot, and out here that's most of what coffee has to offer."

August took the mug because refusing coffee in the tropics was a sin against the species. It was, as promised, terrible — scorched, over-sweet, tasting faintly of the tin it had been boiled in. He drank it anyway.

They stood at the edge of the consortium's forward camp, a tidy arrangement of green canvas and satellite equipment that Mercer's scouts had thrown up on the river terrace two days earlier, close enough to the village that August could see the smoke of morning fires rising through the mist. The villagers had gone quiet since the camp appeared. Quiet the way a house goes quiet when a stranger's boots are on the porch.

"I'll come to the point," Mercer said. He was a man who always came to the point, but he liked to announce it first, the way some men announce they're about to be honest, which is how you know they haven't been. "The consortium is prepared to make you whole."

"Whole," August repeated.

"Your debts. All of them. Corvo's ledger, the interest, the — let's call them penalties. Wiped clean. In addition, you'd have new papers. A clean passport, a clean name if you want one, a clean everything. There's a mining town in the north where a man with your river experience could make a very respectable living. No one would ever ask about the Ravensworth name or what happened at the Kessel Gorge."

August looked at the river. It was running higher than yesterday, browner, carrying branches and whole islands of torn vegetation. Somewhere upstream, the monsoon was gathering itself like a man cracking his knuckles.

"And what do I have to do to be whole?"

"Deliver Dr. Vane. And the survey cores she's sitting on." Mercer sipped his own terrible coffee with the composure of a man who had never once in his life drunk anything he didn't want to. "That's it. That's the entire transaction. She walks out, the cores walk out, you walk out a free man. Nobody even has to be hurt. The consortium would prefer it that way, actually. Corpses generate paperwork."

"You've thought about the paperwork."

"I think about everything, Ravensworth. It's why I'm standing here drinking bad coffee and why you're standing there drinking bad coffee, and the difference between us is that mine is a choice."

August had to give him that. It was almost funny, in the way that everything Mercer said was almost funny, the way a knife is almost a letter opener.

"I don't know where the cores are," August said.

"You know where she is. That's ninety percent of the problem, and the other ten percent is a woman who trusts you, which — forgive me — is the part I find genuinely remarkable. You've known her two weeks."

"Long enough to know she'd rather burn the cores than hand them over."

"Then she'll burn them and we'll all go home poorer, and the valley will be surveyed next year by people less patient than me." Mercer turned his mug slowly in his hands. "August. May I call you August? The offer expires when the river does. Once the monsoon lands properly, this valley closes for six months. Whatever's decided here gets decided in the next few days. I'd rather you decided it with us."

He set the empty mug on a crate, touched two fingers to his hat brim — the gesture of a gentleman in a country with no gentlemen — and walked back toward the green tents.

August stood a long time with his coffee going cold, doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. The number at the bottom of Corvo's ledger had followed him for three years. It had woken him at night. It had a weight, that number, like a stone sewn into the lining of every jacket he owned. And here was a man offering to cut it out with a clean knife and hand him a new life on top, and all it would cost was one exhausted woman and eleven villagers' homes.

*One exhausted woman,* said the hungry voice, the one that had been whispering since the Kessel Gorge ruined him. *She's a stranger. The village isn't even yours. You could be free by Friday.*

He poured the coffee out into the mud, which seemed fitting, and went to find something useful to do with his hands.

* * *

The something useful turned out to be helping the villagers reinforce the shrine roof, which was less a shrine than a wide eave where the valley's oldest woman, Ama Suri, held court and dispensed tea and opinions in equal, scalding measure.

"You walk like a man carrying a sack of wet rice," she told him, watching him lash down a bamboo pole. "In the shoulders. What is it you carry?"

"A debt," August said, before his mouth could remember to be careful.

"Ah." She sipped her tea. "Money?"

"Money, mostly."

"Mostly." She looked at him the way she looked at weather — as though he were a system she had seen many times before and found predictable and slightly disappointing. "The tall one with the soft voice came to the village yesterday. Offered the headman money for the ground under the graves."

August's hands went still on the lashing. "What did the headman say?"

"He said the ground was not his to sell. He said it belonged to the ones in it." Ama Suri shrugged. "The tall one smiled. I do not like his smile. It is a smile that has been folded and carried in a pocket."

August tied off the lashing, tested it with his whole weight, and tried to think of anything to say that wasn't a warning, because a warning would confirm what the villagers already suspected — that he had come up that river with the folded smile's people, and might still belong to them.

"You carry more than rice," Ama Suri said, watching him. "But rice goes bad. Remember that. Some things you carry only get heavier."

* * *

The boat came upriver at dusk, running its lamps low, and August knew it before he saw the hull. He knew it the way a dog knows its owner's footsteps — not by sight but by the shape of the sound, the particular lazy thump of an engine maintained by men who were paid whether it ran or not.

Corvo stood at the bow in a dry, immaculate linen suit, which was its own kind of obscenity this far into the wet season. He was a broad, comfortable man with the face of someone's fond uncle, and that face had ruined more lives than the river had.

"August Ravensworth," Corvo said warmly, as August came down the bank. "You look terrible. Monsoon country agrees with you like fish agrees with a bicycle."

"Corvo."

"Mercer tells me you've been very helpful. Finding camps. Finding bodies." Corvo stepped off onto the mud and looked around at the darkening valley with the satisfaction of a man inspecting a property. "And finding the doctor, though you haven't mentioned that part. You're a poor liar, August. You always were. It's one of your few honest features."

"I don't know what you want me to say."

"Nothing. I want you to listen." Corvo strolled along the bank, hands clasped behind his back, a man taking the air. "Mercer made you an offer. Debt forgiven. New papers. Very generous. The consortium can afford generosity; it's a rounding error to them. But here's what Mercer doesn't understand about debt, August, because he's never owed anyone anything in his life." He turned, and the fond uncle was gone, just for a moment, the way lightning is gone. "Debt doesn't expire. It *transfers*. If you sell the doctor to Mercer, you don't walk away free. You walk away owing *me* for the sale. My cut. My interest. Three years of it, compounded. You'd be starting your clean new life thirty thousand deeper than you are tonight."

The river boomed somewhere upstream. The first real rain of the season hit the far ridge in a grey curtain and began walking toward them across the water.

"You financed this," August said. "Mercer's whole operation. That's why I was hired. You bought my way up this river."

"I invested in you, August. There's a difference. Investments I can lose. Debts I cannot." Corvo smiled the folded smile, and August understood that Ama Suri had seen it too, from across a valley, because some smiles carry. "So think about your options carefully. Option one: you bring me the cores and the doctor, and we settle everything between us, man to man, and you walk away owing nothing to anyone, because I'll have what I want and Mercer can go back to his paperwork. Option two: Mercer gets them, and you owe me forever. Option three—"

"There's always an option three."

"Option three, you run, and the monsoon runs you down, and I collect from whatever they find of you. I'm a patient man, but the river isn't, and I own the river's patience, if you follow me."

August followed him. He followed him all the way down into the cold place where three years of nights lived.

"I need time," August said.

Corvo's eyebrows rose, genuinely amused. "Time. You know what time is, August? Time is the interest rate. But fine. The storm's coming in tonight — you can hear it. Nothing moves in a monsoon's first night, not even me. So you have until the rain stops." He put a hand on August's shoulder, heavy and almost kind. "You were the best guide I ever ruined. Think about that. Think about which of us did the ruining."

He walked back to his boat, and the boat backed off the mud and slid away downriver with its lamps low, and the rain arrived.

It came all at once, the way monsoons do — not a beginning but a verdict. The sky opened and the valley disappeared inside the sound of it, and August stood in the downpour until he was soaked to the skin, doing the arithmetic again, and this time the number at the bottom of the ledger was not money.

It was a woman who trusted him. Eleven homes on a vein of ore. An old woman who had told him rice goes bad.

The old hunger for a clean slate was still there, whispering, *one exhausted stranger, free by Friday* — but the whisper sounded thinner tonight, watered down, like coffee stretched too far.

Behind him, up the slope, a lantern moved in the village. Someone was awake. Someone was watching the river the way he was.

August turned toward the light, and the rain came down like the sky settling accounts.
