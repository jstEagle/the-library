## 7. Rising Water

The rain came on the third night, and it did not fall so much as arrive, like a relative who intends to stay the season.

Ilsa Vane stood under the headman's eaves and watched the valley disappear. One hour there had been terraces, footpaths, the brown-green mosaic of millet and taro. The next there was only water moving through everything, patient and total, the river climbing its own banks with the calm of something that had done this ten thousand times before and would do it ten thousand more.

"Your colleagues picked a bad year to be greedy," said Ama Deki beside her, folding her arms into her shawl.

"They picked a bad decade to be anything," Ilsa said.

The old woman made a sound that might have been laughter or might have been a cough. With Ama Deki it was often both.

Ilsa had been in the valley forty-one days. She knew this because she still kept the expedition's field calendar, marking off days in pencil out of a habit so deep it had survived the deaths of everyone she'd ever marked them with. Forty-one days of learning which roof leaked, which grandmother disapproved of her boots, which child—Tashi, eight years old, missing two teeth and fearless about it—followed her everywhere like a small determined shadow she hadn't asked for and could no longer imagine living without.

Forty-one days since she'd watched Halvorsen's face change at the assay results, and understood that the men she'd crossed an ocean with were already gone, replaced by something wearing their faces.

She didn't think about that. She thought about drainage ditches and seed stores and whether the granary stilts were high enough. Grief was like the ore body: best left where it lay, unexcavated.

But the rain made thinking impossible to avoid. Water found every buried thing.

* * *

By morning the lower camps were gone.

Not destroyed—*absorbed*. The surveyors' tents they'd stripped for canvas, the coring platform, the equipment cache August had spent two days hauling uphill: all of it underwater, shapes beneath brown glass. The river had risen four meters overnight and showed no interest in stopping.

"The gorge exits," August said. He'd spread the expedition's topographic sheets on the headman's floor, weighting the corners with stones and a bowl of dried chilies that Ama Deki had contributed without being asked and then defended fiercely when Tashi reached for one. "Both of them. The south trail's already cut—we saw it go yesterday afternoon, whole section just slid into the water like it was never there. The north route runs through the gorge system."

"Through," Ilsa repeated.

"Through." He tapped the map. "Three kilometers of slot canyon. In dry season it's a scramble over boulders. Right now it's a tunnel with a river in it."

"And you want to take eleven villagers through a tunnel with a river in it."

"I want to take eleven villagers through a tunnel with a river in it *before Mercer's boats reach the head of the valley*, yes." He looked up, and Ilsa saw the thing she'd been seeing more of lately—the guide surfacing through the drunkard, the way bedrock surfaces through topsoil after a washout. "There are eddies. Side chambers. Places to hold if the water spikes. I ran worse channels than this for tourists who paid for the privilege."

"You ran them sober?"

A pause. "Mostly."

Ama Deki snorted from her corner.

Ilsa studied the map instead of him, because studying him had become dangerous to her composure in ways she refused to examine. The gorge line wound down the page like a stitch closing a wound. Eleven villagers. Most had never left the valley; two were children; one was Ama Deki, who had announced she was coming and dared anyone present to object.

And the raft gear. That was the miracle nobody had prayed for—the expedition's equipment cache, hauled uphill by a man who hadn't known why he was doing it, sat dry under the meeting-house floor: four frames, six pontoons, paddles, throw lines, three hundred feet of rope. Halvorsen had bought top-of-the-line gear for a survey crew. It would carry refugees instead. Ilsa found she liked the symmetry more than she could say.

"We'll need two days to rig the rafts and scout the first chamber," August said. "After that we go, rain or no rain."

"It'll be no rain," Ama Deki said darkly. "It'll be all rain."

* * *

They rigged the frames in the meeting house while the storm leaned on the roof. Ilsa had expected chaos—eleven people, most of whom had never seen a cataraft, working by lamplight—but the villagers built things the way they did everything else, unhurried and exactly, as though the rafts had always existed and were merely being remembered. Old Pemba, who claimed ninety years and looked every one of them plus several he was saving for later, turned out to have lashed bamboo frames across the river for thirty years and corrected August's rigging twice.

"Your knots are city knots," Pemba informed him.

"My knots held on Class V water on three continents."

"City water." Pemba re-lashed the joint. It did look better.

Tashi appointed himself Ilsa's assistant, which meant handing her tools slightly too late and narrating everything. "This is a pontoon. This is also a pontoon. There are many pontoons." She let him inflate one entirely by hand pump, mostly because his fierce concentration while doing it was the only thing all week that had made her chest feel light.

August worked beside her in silence for an hour before saying, quietly, "You could run. Alone. Tonight, upstream on foot before the boats close the north end. You know it. I know you've thought about it."

She tightened a strap. "Eleven people can't run on foot."

"You could."

"I could have run forty days ago. I stayed." She pulled the strap until it sang. "You don't get to make my cowardice or my courage convenient for you, Ravensworth. Whichever this is, it's mine."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "Fair."

That was the thing about him she couldn't reconcile with the consortium's file—the file she'd read by firelight the night she decided not to kill him. *Disgraced. Indebted. Unreliable.* And here he was re-lashing pontoons in a flooded valley with nothing in it for him but trouble, humming some off-key song about a lighthouse while Pemba criticized his knots. Two liars, she'd thought once, recognizing each other's exhaustion. But somewhere in the last week the lying had quietly stopped, and neither of them had announced it.

* * *

Mercer's men came at dusk on the second day, and they did not come for the rafts.

Ilsa heard the boats before she saw them—engines throttling against the current, wrong-sounding in a valley that belonged to oars and rain. By the time she reached the headman's house, half the village was already pressed flat behind the terrace walls, and August had an arm locked across Tashi's chest, holding the boy down, his other hand white-knuckled around a paddle he had clearly grabbed for no tactical reason whatsoever.

Four men came up from the landing beach. They didn't search the houses. They didn't touch the granaries. They walked straight to the headman's door, waited with terrible politeness, and when Dorje emerged—small, gray-robed, eighty if he was a day—they took his arm the way you escort a guest, and walked him down to the boats.

One of them nailed a paper to the doorpost. Then they were gone, engines fading upriver into the rain.

Nobody moved for a long moment. Then Ama Deki walked out into the open, crossed the terrace, tore the paper off the nail, and brought it back as though retrieving laundry.

Ilsa read it over her shoulder. Typed. Laminated, even, in this weather. *The man Dorje is held to ensure cooperation. Deliver the foreign woman and the survey materials within two days. No harm will come to anyone. The company is reasonable.*

"'Reasonable,'" Ilsa said.

"They spelled it right, at least," August said. But his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man doing arithmetic he hated. Two days. Deliver Ilsa and the cores—or whatever remained of them—or the old man paid. Everyone in the room knew what *paid* meant, even the children, especially the children, who had learned long ago what men with laminated papers were for.

Dorje had raised Ilsa's tea for her every morning for forty days. He had never once asked what she was hiding, only whether she was sleeping, and when she lied and said yes, he had nodded as though lies between friends were simply another kind of courtesy.

"He knew," Ama Deki said suddenly. "All winter, he knew someone would come for the mountain someday. He used to say—" she stopped, gathered herself, started again in her cracked voice. "He used to say the mountain keeps its own accounts. That whoever came to collect would leave owing more than they carried."

Ilsa looked at the rain hammering the terraces, the drowned lower camps, the river eating the world with such patience. She had spent forty-one days grieving a landscape, mourning camps and maps and colleagues as though grief were a thing that happened to her. Standing in the lamplight with a laminated threat crumpled in her fist, she understood at last that she had been counting the price wrong.

The price was never the data. Never the ore, the valley, the drowned trails, even her dead. The price was an old man's arm in a stranger's grip, a boy's heartbeat under August's forearm, eleven lives stacked like ballast in a raft that wasn't built yet.

Other people. It had always been going to be other people.

"Two days," she said aloud. "We go tomorrow. First light, before their deadline means anything, before they're positioned. All twelve of us." She looked around the room—at August, at Pemba, at Tashi glaring at the doorpost with the fury only the toothless can properly manage. "And then we're going to show Mr. Mercer exactly how reasonable this valley is."

August met her eyes across the lamplight. Something passed between them that needed no translation: fear, agreed upon, and converted.

"Tashi," he said. "Get me the pontoons. We're working tonight."

* * *

They launched in the last black hour before dawn, rain falling straight down like a verdict, four rafts sliding off the terrace shelf into water that had swallowed the landing beach whole. Ilsa took the bow of the lead raft with a paddle in her hands and Ama Deki behind her, refusing all assistance on principle.

Below them, somewhere in the dark, the gorge waited with its river in it.

Above them, on the ridge trail, a lantern swung—Mercer's pickets, awake early, looking for a delivery that was never coming.

And downstream, faint under the rain, Ilsa heard the first white water begin to speak.
