## 8. The Gorge Run

The rain came down like a verdict.

August stood at the mouth of the gorge at first light, if you could call it light—the sky was the color of wet slate, and the river beneath it was no longer a river but a brown, brawling argument. Eleven villagers stood behind him in their rain capes, clutching the expedition's raft gear like it might bite them. In a way, it would.

"That's the way through?" asked Tam, the headman's nephew, a boy of maybe nineteen with a fisherman's calm that August had come to rely on more than he'd admit. He pointed his chin at the churning water. "That looks less like a river and more like a grudge."

"It's a channel," August said. "Technically."

"Technically," Ilsa repeated beside him, flat as a stone. She'd tied her hair back with survey twine, of all things, and there was mud on her cheekbone she either didn't know about or had stopped caring about around the same time he had. "You told me last night you'd run this gorge once before."

"I did run it once before." He didn't mention the rest. He didn't have to; she knew the rest. The rest was why he was blacklisted from every licensed outfit on three rivers, why his name showed up in guide association newsletters under *Cautionary Notes*. The rest was a client who hadn't listened, a channel August had warned against, and a man who'd gone into the water and not come out. Six years ago. It had a name—the Kessel Run of his particular damnation—and he had sworn never to look at it again, let alone run it.

And now here he was, about to lead eleven people and one stubborn scientist into it, because behind them Mercer's boats were already moving up the flooded valley, and because the only other exit was a trail the flood had eaten whole.

"The water's higher than when I ran it," he said. "That's bad. But high water also fills the undercut ledges, which means the rock walls stop being knives and start being cushions. Mostly." He looked at the eleven faces. Old Sombai, who'd insisted on coming because her granddaughter was one of the eleven. The weaver Dara, who had quietly packed her loom weights because she said a person should arrive somewhere with something of her own hands. Tam. The child, Anou, whom August had taken a graze for two nights ago and who now regarded him with the solemn loyalty of someone who had decided a debt was owed and would be repaid in obedience.

He hated all of them a little for trusting him. He loved them for it too, and the love was worse.

"Rules," he said, raising his voice over the rain. "One: nobody is a hero. If you go in the water, float feet-first, point downstream, and don't grab the rope unless I say. Two: if your raft capsizes, hold the raft. A raft floats even upside down. You do not. Three—" he hesitated, then said it anyway— "if I tell you to jump, jump. Don't think. Thinking is how people drown."

Ilsa glanced at him. "That's also how people end up in jail," she murmured.

"One thing at a time," he said.

* * *

The first hour went almost well, which August knew better than to trust.

They put in above the gorge mouth in four rafts—the expedition's gear, bless its dead owners, had been military surplus and absurdly overbuilt. August took the lead boat with Tam and Anou. Ilsa took the sweep position in the second with old Sombai, who turned out to paddle like she'd been born doing it and scolded the younger villagers in a tone that carried clear across the roar. The river took them fast, faster than any commercial run, and the walls closed in, grey stone streaked black with forty centuries of monsoon stain, ferns hanging off the ledges like drowned men's hair.

August read the water the way he used to read it, before, back when reading water had been a joy instead of an indictment. Tongues of current marking the safe lines. Holes to avoid, boils to skirt. His body remembered everything his reputation had tried to bury, and there was a moment—running the first big drop, the bow lifting, spray hitting like thrown gravel, Anou whooping once before remembering to be solemn—that August felt something he hadn't felt since before the disgrace. Not happiness. Something older than that. Competence. The plain animal pleasure of being exactly the right person in exactly the wrong place.

Then the second drop tried to kill them, and the pleasure was over.

The channel split around a midstream fang of rock, and August chose left because his memory said left, and his memory was six years out of date and the flood had moved a house-sized slab of bank into the left channel sometime since. He saw it late—a pale shoulder of new stone just under the surface—and hauled the oar over, and they missed it by a handspan, and the raft spun, and the third raft behind them did not miss it.

He heard it happen more than saw it: the crack of the tube against rock, Dara's shout, the world tilting. When he got the lead raft turned in the eddy below, the third boat was upside down and three of its passengers were clinging to the hull, and one was not.

Sombai's granddaughter—Mali, nineteen, quick-eyed, always laughing—was gone.

August's mind did what trained minds do: stripped the panic out and gave him numbers. Current speed, distance to the next rapid, the one chance—a gravel bar on river right below the bend, backed by a strainer of fallen bamboo that would kill a swimmer but could be reached from the bar. "Tam, take my oar. Ilsa, keep them together!" And he went over the side.

The water hit him like a mob. Cold in a way the warm country had no business producing, shoving him, rolling him, filling his ears with a sound like the inside of a bell. He swam the ferry angle hard, arms burning, and caught Mali's arm just above the elbow as she went by—she'd done the right thing, feet-first, floating, terrified and disciplined—and for thirty seconds they were just two rag dolls in the fist of the river while he kicked for the eddy line behind the gravel bar.

They made it. Barely. He dragged her onto the shingle and she vomited half the river and then clung to him, shaking, saying something in her own language over and over that he later learned was *don't tell my grandmother*, which broke his heart so cleanly he almost laughed.

Above them, Ilsa had gotten the flipped raft righted and the others ferried down. When they all regrouped on the bar, Sombai took Mali's face in both hands and said nothing at all, which was somehow louder than anything.

"We lost the fourth raft," Ilsa reported. Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't. "And we're one boat short, and Mercer's engines—I heard them. Upstream. Maybe twenty minutes back."

August looked at the map in his head. There was the sane route: continue down the main channel, runnable, visible, and wide open to pursuit. And there was the other thing. Two miles down, the gorge pinched into a slot the maps called the Throat, where the river dropped through a gap barely wider than a raft, and after the Throat the channel vanished into a limestone maze that flooded only at high water—which meant it was flooded now, which meant it was navigable now, by exactly one kind of fool.

A sane guide would take the main channel and pray.

"You're making the face," Ilsa said.

"What face?"

"The one you made before you suggested something terrible."

"The main channel's a shooting gallery," he said. "Mercer has motors and we have paddles. But the Throat feeds the old bypass channels through the karst. My old outfit surveyed them years ago—they were never commercially viable, too shallow, too flaky, one wrong turn and you're in a sump. Nobody runs them. Nobody sane." He looked at the eleven of them, soaked and shivering and alive. "But the flood's raised the level enough to float them, and Mercer's boats can't follow—his drafts are too deep, and his pilots won't know the turns. It's a maze in there. It's my maze. I'm the only person who ever bothered to memorize it, and I only memorized it because I was young and showing off."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we get very intimately acquainted with a limestone sump."

Ilsa held his eyes for a long moment. Rain ran off her chin. "Six years ago," she said quietly, "you told a client not to run a channel, and he ran it anyway, and it ruined you. This time everyone will do what you say. Does that frighten you?"

It did. It frightened him down to the marrow, and he said so, because lying to her had stopped working somewhere around chapter five. "Terrified," he said. "But I'd rather be terrified holding the oar than safe holding nothing."

She nodded once, and started redistributing passengers.

* * *

The Throat was worse than he remembered and better than he feared, which averaged out to survivable.

They ran it one boat at a time, lining the rafts through the approach on ropes because the entry wave was a standing green monster taller than a man. Then the drop itself—two seconds of freefall chaos, the walls flashing past close enough to touch, Anou screaming with what August sincerely hoped was joy—and then the river changed character entirely, spreading out into the flooded karst, a drowned forest of limestone towers with current threading between them like thread through needles.

Here the race became quiet. No more rapids—just navigation. August stood in the bow of the lead raft with a broken paddle shaft for a pointer, calling turns from memory: *left at the twin pillars, right where the vine hangs, straight past the shrine rock—someone hung prayer flags on it once; they were underwater now, streaming like green hair.* Behind him the other rafts followed his wake precisely, because a foot off the line meant a submerged pinnacle that would open a tube like a tin can.

Twice they heard engines echo somewhere far off to the east, distorted by the karst, hunting the main channel. Once, horribly, closer—a scout boat actually probed the maze entrance behind them, and August heard the pilot's voice bounce off the walls, swearing, and then the engine reversed and faded. Mercer's men were good, but they were good with charts, and there were no charts for this. There was only a disgraced guide's rotten, unforgivable memory, and for the first time in six years August was grateful for it—grateful for the obsession that had made him learn these channels, grateful even for the ruin, because the ruin was the reason he'd been here to memorize them.

His disgrace, transmuted. Not redeemed—transmuted. Lead into something that floated.

Near dusk the karst opened, the current gentled, and they slid out onto a broad, brown, ordinary stretch of river miles below the gorge, mist rising off the water, rain finally thinning to a drizzle. Eleven villagers. One scientist. One disgraced guide. All present. All breathing.

Nobody cheered. They were too tired. But old Sombai pressed a handful of roasted rice into August's palm, closing his fingers over it like a benediction, and Anou fell asleep against his knee in the bottom of the raft, and Ilsa sat beside him with her shoulder touching his, watching the mist.

"They'll figure out where we exited by morning," she said.

"I know."

"And Corvo's waiting at the river mouth."

"I know that too." August chewed the rice. It was the best thing he'd ever tasted, which said more about the day than the rice. "Which means tomorrow we stop running and start negotiating. And Ilsa—when we get there, whatever happens, you take the notebook and the villagers and you don't look back. Promise me."

She turned and looked at him for a long time, the way she'd looked at the ore core samples, like she was reading something written in him.

"No," she said.

Before he could answer, Tam, in the stern, sat up suddenly and pointed downstream, where the mist was parting around a shape anchored mid-channel: a long black boat, riding lights doused, and standing in its bow with a lantern held high, patient as a toll collector, a broad silhouette August knew better than his own reflection.

Corvo had not waited at the river mouth.

Corvo had come to meet them.
