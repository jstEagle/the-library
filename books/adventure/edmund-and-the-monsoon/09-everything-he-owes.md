## 9. Everything He Owes

The river mouth was a gray argument between the flood and the sea, and August Ravensworth stood in the middle of it with his hands where everyone could see them.

He had walked out of the treeline an hour before dawn, alone, mud to the eyebrows, and planted himself on the shingle bar like a man planting a flag on a country that had already been sold. Behind him, upriver, eleven villagers and one stubborn scientist were supposed to be moving east through the mangroves toward the fishing coast. Ahead of him, moored in the brown churn, sat Corvo's boat — the *Magpie*, all black paint and bad intentions — with Mercer's two launches tied alongside like well-behaved dogs.

Corvo came down the gangplank personally, which August took as either a compliment or a death sentence. With Corvo it was usually both.

"Ravensworth." Corvo was a small man who dressed like a large one, cream linen pressed sharp enough to shave with. He stopped three paces away and looked August over the way a butcher looks at a carcass he's already decided is tough. "You look terrible."

"I've been having a wonderful time, actually."

"You owe me forty thousand."

"The number does come up in conversation a lot."

Mercer stood behind him on the deck, dry under an umbrella held by one of his scouts, watching with the pleasant expression of a man doing arithmetic. The survey cores were stacked on the *Magpie*'s aft deck under a tarp — August could see the crate corners through the canvas. Mercer's men had pulled them from the gorge cache two days ago while everyone was busy not drowning. That was the whole game now. Cores for passage.

"Here are my terms," Mercer said, descending without waiting for the gangplank to be lowered, which told August everything about how much Mercer valued other people's procedures. "You confirm the location of Dr. Vane and the villagers. My men collect them quietly. You come with us. Your debt is extinguished. There's a passport with your name on it in Singapore by Thursday. Clean as a whistle."

"A whistle that's been through a war," August said.

"A clean whistle," Mercer said, with the patience of a man explaining something to a child.

August looked at the water. It was high — monsoon-high, the kind of high that ate sandbars whole and turned the channel markers into suggestions. He'd run this mouth twice before the disgrace, once in weather like this, and both times he'd prayed to a god he didn't believe in with a fluency that would have embarrassed a bishop.

"They're gone," he said. "East bank, mangrove cut, before first light. Rafts broken up, gear scattered. You'll never find them in that maze unless I walk you in."

"And you will," Corvo said. It wasn't a question.

"I will," August agreed. "On one condition. They walk to the coast road unmolested. No boats following, no men ahead of them. They get to the road, they get transport, they're somebody else's problem forever. Then I'm yours. Debt, testimony, whatever you want."

Corvo smiled. It was the smile of a man who had heard better lies from worse liars. "Done."

*Too easy,* said the part of August's brain that had kept him alive in mountains for twenty years. He ignored it. There wasn't time to be smart. There was only time to be heavy enough to hold everyone's attention while Ilsa got clear.

She was fifty meters upriver in the mangroves, he knew, because she hadn't left when she was supposed to. Of course she hadn't. He'd handed her the geologist's notebook at the last bend — pressed it into her hands with both of his, the way you hand someone something breakable — and said, "Burn it. Every page. Then keep walking."

She'd looked at him with those gray eyes that catalogued things, the way she probably catalogued rock strata, and said, "You're going to trade yourself."

"I'm going to negotiate aggressively."

"You're a terrible liar for a man whose entire career was telling people the mountain was safe."

"That's the thing about mountains," August had said. "They don't know you're lying."

Now he stood on the shingle and lied with his whole body: shoulders loose, eyes steady, the performance of a man who had made peace. Mercer's scouts fanned out along the treeline. Corvo went back aboard. And August climbed the *Magpie*'s gangplank into the smell of diesel and wet rope, and sat where they told him to sit, and watched the mangroves for a long time until nothing moved there, and let himself believe, briefly, that it had worked.

It was almost noon when the cores came up from the hold and Mercer opened the crate, and August saw Mercer's face do the thing faces do when the arithmetic stops working.

"These are river stones," Mercer said quietly.

"Are they?" August said. "Huh."

Mercer turned very slowly. "Where are the real ones?"

"Gone where the notebook went," August said. "Which is to say, somewhere fire has jurisdiction."

He had swapped them at the cache, two nights ago, in the dark, in the rain, with hands that shook so badly he'd dropped the same stone three times. It had felt less like cunning and more like a child hiding vegetables under a napkin. But children get away with it more often than anyone expects.

Corvo came out of the cabin. He did not shout. That was the worst part. He just looked at August for a long moment, and then at Mercer, and then at the river, and August understood with perfect clarity that the terms had changed. Debts don't expire, Corvo had told him once. They transfer. And a dead debtor transfers his debt to whoever finds his body useful.

"Overboard," Corvo said to his men, mildly, the way you'd ask for another drink.

What happened next happened fast, and August's honest account of it, later, would always be slightly shamefaced, because it was not brave and it was not clever. Two men took his arms. He put his heel against the gunwale and made them work for it, mostly out of principle. And then the river reached up and grabbed the boat.

Not gently. Not dramatically, either — no wall of water, no cinematic wave. Just the flood pulse coming down from the gorge country on schedule, the way it had every afternoon for nine days, lifting the *Magpie* off her mooring like a hand sliding under a card. The mooring line snapped with a sound like a rifle. The deck went to fifteen degrees. The crate of river stones slid across the planking and took out the legs of the man holding August's left arm, and August went down with him, and the world became noise.

Through the noise: engines. Two of them. Mercer's launches, cutting across the churn toward the drifting *Magpie* — and behind them, absurdly, impossibly, a third engine, a long-tail boat running the flooded channel from the east with its propeller shaft sticking up out of the water like a periscope, driven by a woman who handled it the way Ilsa Vane handled everything, which was competently and with visible disapproval.

And standing in the bow of the long-tail, poling, soaked to the ribs, grinning like this was the best day of his life: old Somchai, the village headman's brother, seventy-one years old and built like a teak root.

*Ilsa,* August thought, with a feeling that was equal parts fury and love. *You were supposed to burn the notebook and walk away.*

She had burned the notebook. He'd find the ash later, confirmed by the smell of it on her jacket. She just hadn't walked away. She'd gone back through the mangroves, found Somchai and four of the younger villagers who had refused to leave without him — refused, over her objections, in a debate she lost in three languages — and they had read the river the way their family had read it for six generations, and known exactly what the flood pulse would do to a moored boat at midday, and arrived on time.

The *Magpie* swung beam-on to the current. Mercer's lead launch tried to come alongside to transfer men and misjudged the seam, and the flood took the gap between the two hulls and widened it and then closed it, hard, with a crunch that folded the launch's bow like paper. Men went into the water. Mercer went into the water.

August saw him surface once, twenty feet downstream, reaching for a floating fuel can with the same tidy competence he brought to everything, and then the river folded over him the way a page turns, and did not open again.

There was no triumph in it. August wanted there to be. He searched himself for it the way you search a pocket. All he found was the cold arithmetic of it — a man who had come up this river with polished manners and an umbrella, and gone down in it with neither — and the strange, unearned fact of his own continued breathing.

Corvo's remaining men got the *Magpie* re-moored, barely, and then Corvo stood on his tilting deck and performed the fastest recalculation of his career. His muscle was scattered in the current. His buyer's samples were river stones. His enforcer was fish food. And upstream, somewhere in the drowned green, was a valley full of people who knew his face.

"Ravensworth," Corvo called across the water, and for one strange moment his voice was almost conversational. "You know what the difference between us is?"

"About forty thousand," August called back.

"Debt survives," Corvo said. "Men don't. Remember that." And the *Magpie* backed water, turned her black stern to the whole ruined scene, and ran for the coast with what remained of her dignity.

The long-tail bumped against the shingle. Ilsa jumped down into knee-deep water and waded to him, and for a moment they just stood there, two people dripping at each other.

"You were supposed to leave," August said.

"You were supposed to have a plan that didn't end with you in the river," she said. "We're even."

Somchai poled the boat in behind her, still grinning, and said something in his own language that made the young villagers laugh, and when August asked Ilsa what it was, she hesitated and then translated: "He says the fat spider has run home to his web, and the web is wet."

"He's not wrong," August said. He looked at the water where Mercer had gone down, and at the mangroves where the notebook's ashes lived now, and at the eleven — no, sixteen, counting the ones who'd doubled back — muddy, exhausted, impossible people who had decided he was worth rowing back for.

His debt was supposed to die today. Instead it had transferred, the way debts do. Not to Corvo. To them.

Ilsa put her hand on his shoulder, briefly, the way you'd steady a piece of equipment. "Can you get us home?" she asked. "In this?"

August looked at the flooded channel, at the standing waves, at the sky still stacked with rain. Twenty years ago he'd have said no. Ten years ago he'd have said yes and meant it. Today he said:

"I know a channel no sane guide would attempt."

Somchai laughed. "Then we go," he said, in English, deliberately, so there'd be no translating. "All of us."

* * *

They ran the channel at dusk, and it was terrible, and it worked. But that night, camped on high ground above the valley with the rain finally thinning to a whisper, August woke to lantern light coming up the trail from the coast road — a government patrol, rifles slung, led by a tired-looking officer holding a piece of paper.

"August Ravensworth?" the officer said.

August sat up slowly. "That depends," he said, "entirely on what's written on the paper."
