## 10. After the Rain

The rain had followed him, in a way. Not the monsoon itself—that had spent its fury on the mountains weeks ago—but a thin regional drizzle that came down on the jail's tin roof like someone patiently tapping a spoon against a pot, reminding August Ravensworth that the world was still there, still going about its business, and he was not part of it.

He didn't mind. That was the strange thing. He kept waiting to mind.

The cell was small but dry, which after the gorge felt like a luxury hotel. There was a cot with a blanket that smelled of mildew and government soap, a window barred at the height of his shoulder through which he could see a slice of courtyard and, beyond it, the grey-green shoulder of hills he'd once been paid handsomely to guide people across. He could name every ridge in that view. He'd named half of them himself, back when naming things had been his job—when he'd been the man with the compass and the answers, before the bankruptcy, before Corvo, before the river.

Now he was the man in cell four, and mostly what he did was sleep, eat the rice they brought him, and answer questions from an inspector named Dhawan who came twice a week with a folder and a thermos of tea he never offered to share.

"You understand," Dhawan said on one of those visits, settling onto the stool outside the bars as though it were his own kitchen, "that officially, none of this happened."

"None of what?" August said.

"That's the spirit."

It had taken three conversations for August to understand what kind of arrangement he'd walked into. He'd surrendered himself at the river mouth expecting handcuffs, charges, maybe a trial where Mercer's employers sent lawyers like weather fronts. Instead there had been a quiet room, a man from the provincial survey office who never gave his name, and a long conversation in which August described—in careful, boring detail—a mining claim that no longer existed, based on survey data that no longer existed, conducted by an expedition whose records were now silt somewhere at the bottom of a flooded gorge.

"And the ore body itself," the nameless man had said. "You're certain you couldn't find it again?"

August had looked him in the eye and lied with the fluency of a man who'd spent two years lying to debt collectors. "That valley ate better mapmakers than me."

Which was true, in its way. It had eaten the Halcyon Expedition whole.

The nameless man had nodded slowly, made a note, and closed his folder. And that had been that. Somewhere in an office, a claim file was being misfiled or shredded or simply allowed to rot; somewhere else, a consortium's lawyers were discovering that their asset had evaporated into rumor. The valley would stand. No road would come. No dredge would chew up the graves beneath the mountain to get at the metal sleeping under them.

In exchange, August got a cell, a number, and the permanent, unglamorous knowledge that he had done one clean thing in a dirty decade.

* * *

Ilsa Vane came on a Tuesday, because visiting day was Tuesday and she was, above all things, a woman who respected procedure when procedure suited her.

He heard her before he saw her—the particular clipped rhythm of her boots on the corridor concrete, a sound he'd last heard crossing wet stone above white water while eleven villagers and one disgraced guide held their breath behind her. The guard buzzed the door. She stepped into the visiting room carrying a paper bag and an expression that managed to be both annoyed and relieved, which August had learned was simply her face.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You should see the other guy."

"I did. He drowned." She sat down across the scarred table and set the bag between them. "From Tam. She insisted. She said you'd be eating jail food and wasting away."

Tam. The girl whose arm he'd pulled behind him when Mercer's scout fired into the village night. Eleven years old and already organizing supply runs like a quartermaster. August opened the bag: dried fish, mangoes wrapped in leaves, and a fistful of sweets so aggressively colored they probably violated several treaties.

"She knows I'm fine," he said.

"She knows nothing of the sort. She thinks prison is a place where strong men go to fade dramatically." Ilsa leaned back, arms crossed. "I told her you were too stubborn to fade. She said that's what worries her."

He laughed—actually laughed, the first time in weeks—and heard how rusty it came out, like a gate hinge in the rain.

"So," he said. "The valley."

"The valley." Her voice changed, went quieter, the way it did when she talked about things that mattered. "They've replanted the lower terraces. The headman sends his regards and also a complaint, which I'm instructed to deliver verbatim: your raft knots were an insult to rope everywhere, and next time you will learn proper knots before touching their lines again."

"Next time," August repeated. "There's a next time?"

"There's always a next time. That's rather the point of surviving." She studied him for a moment, and he recognized the look—she was checking him the way she checked equipment, for damage that hadn't declared itself yet. "The location is unrecorded. Officially the region is 'insufficiently surveyed.' Your testimony helped with that. The word of a disgraced guide who claims he can't retrace his own route carries surprising weight with bureaucrats. Apparently incompetence is the one credential they trust."

"It's the only one I have left."

"No," Ilsa said, and her voice sharpened. "It isn't. Stop rehearsing your eulogy. You ran a channel no sane guide would attempt and brought ten people through alive. One lost—not to your judgment, to the water, to chance, to the same chance that spares fools and drowns saints. I've written the report. It says exactly that. Whether anyone ever reads it is out of our hands, but it exists, and it's true, and you don't get to pretend otherwise just because self-pity fits more comfortably than the truth."

August sat with that. Outside the barred window, the drizzle kept up its patient tapping.

"My reputation's finished," he said finally. "No consortium, no expedition company, no outfit on this side of the border will touch me. I turned myself in. I destroyed—well. I was adjacent to the destruction of the most valuable mineral discovery in a generation. Word travels."

"Yes," Ilsa agreed pleasantly. "Word travels. Do you know what word has traveled to me? Three letters. Two from universities, one from a conservation society, all asking whether I know of any guides familiar with the upper rivers who might be available for work of a, quote, sensitive and unofficial nature." She smiled thinly. "Apparently there are people who want maps drawn by someone honest enough to burn them."

"That's not a reputation. That's a novelty act."

"It's a start. Most reputations are." She pushed the bag an inch toward him. "Eat the mangoes before they turn. Tam will ask."

* * *

She stayed most of the hour, and they talked the way survivors talk—about small things first, then, carefully, the larger ones. About Dawa, the villager the river took in the gorge, whose family had held a ceremony at the confluence and spoken his name into the current so the water would remember him kindly. About Corvo, who had fled downstream empty-handed and was now, according to the gossip that reached even jails, a man whose creditors had begun to smell blood in the water. Debts transfer, Corvo had told him once. August found he took a certain savage pleasure in watching the principle applied upward.

"And your debt," Ilsa said. "Officially."

"Officially, my creditor's operation no longer exists, and no one has come to collect. Unofficially—" he shrugged— "I imagine somewhere there's a ledger with my name in it, waiting for someone ambitious to find it. But ledgers need men to enforce them, and Mercer's men are feeding the fish."

"So you're free."

"I'm in a cell."

"You know what I mean."

He did know. And that was the thing he kept turning over at night, on the mildew-smelling cot, listening to the rain: he was, for the first time in years, actually free. No collector at his elbow. No fixer with a contract. No rope that was also a noose. And freedom, it turned out, was lighter than he'd expected and much less comfortable—like a pack with everything stripped out of it, leaving only the frame.

He'd spent two years believing that if he could just clear the debt, just get back to zero, he'd be a man again. The zero had arrived, and it turned out zero wasn't home. Home, whatever it was, seemed to be something you chose rather than something you paid off. A valley that wouldn't be on any map. A girl who worried about his fading. A scientist who insulted his knots and defended his honor in the same breath.

"Visiting hours," the guard called, unnecessarily, since everyone in the room owned a watch.

Ilsa stood, collected her composure like a jacket, and paused with one hand on the table.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the expedition died arguing over whether to sell a valley. You nearly died choosing not to. Whatever the record says about you—and it will say very little, and most of it wrong—I know which of us I'd follow upriver."

Then she was gone, boots ticking down the corridor, and August sat alone with the mangoes and the colored sweets and the strange, unfamiliar weight of being somebody's answer rather than somebody's problem.

* * *

That evening the drizzle stopped, and the clouds over the hills tore open along one seam, letting down a bar of late gold light that crossed the courtyard and climbed the wall of his cell and laid itself across the cot like something delivered.

August lay in it and thought about channels. The one he'd run in the gorge—the one that ruined him, years ago, the shortcut he'd trusted against all sense, the error that cost a client his leg and August everything else—everyone had told him afterward that a good guide stays on the mapped water. Safe. Known. Dead.

But he'd run that channel again this season, eyes open, with people depending on him, and it had held. Same water. Different man. He supposed that was the whole of what he'd learned, and it had taken two bankruptcies, a smuggler, a flood, and a jail cell to teach it: the route doesn't make the guide. The guide makes the route.

Somewhere north, past the hills he could see through the bars, a valley was drying out under a clearing sky. Terraces greened. Rope was being retaught. A vein of metal slept on under homes and graves, unmolested, unrecorded, worth nothing to anyone because no one knew—and that, he decided, was the most valuable thing he'd ever protected.

A key rattled in the corridor lock. The guard put his head in, wearing the expression of a man delivering news he didn't fully approve of.

"Ravensworth. Inspector says there's a visitor. Says she's not on the list, but—" he glanced at his clipboard— "she's got a letter from the provincial office, and honestly, nobody upstairs wants to argue with the provincial office. Also she says she'll wait."

"She?"

The guard consulted the clipboard again, frowning. "Name of Montrose. Says she's a novelist. Says—" his frown deepened— "says she's writing a book about a river, and she's been told you're the only guide who can tell her which parts are true."

August sat up slowly. The gold light had slid off the cot and was climbing the wall, and beyond the window the hills stood washed and clear, every ridge sharp as a blade, the whole drowned world rinsed and shining and full of roads he hadn't walked yet.

"Well," he said, swinging his feet to the floor. "Show her in."
