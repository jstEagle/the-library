## 1. The Tide Keeps Books

The tide went out so far that morning it looked like the sea had filed for bankruptcy and was liquidating its assets.

I was on the flats by six, before the gulls got organized, reading the shore the way my father taught me to read a section profile: top layer recent, middle layer promising, bottom layer either treasure or trouble depending on how much you liked trouble. The town of Greywater Landing—population declining, optimism pre-deceased—had put out word that a shell midden had surfaced on the north spit after the winter storms, and the historical society would pay cash for catalogued finds. Cash being the operative word. I'd been in town eleven days and I already knew the historical society's treasury was a coffee can in Dora Ellingboe's kitchen, but a job is a job, and I've eaten worse than what a coffee can buys.

My name that week was Sam Okafor. It's a good name. It belongs to nobody I know personally, which is the main qualification.

"Sam!" Dora called from the seawall, where she stood wrapped in a coat that had survived more winters than most of the town's marriages. "You'll want to see the water line. It's wrong."

Water lines are always wrong somewhere; that's their nature. But I climbed up anyway, because when an old woman who's lived her whole life against a sea says the water is wrong, you look. The tide tables pinned outside the chandlery said minus 1.4 feet at 6:40—a record for the station, they kept saying, record since they started keeping records, which is the kind of phrase that makes an archaeologist's spine prickle like a change in soil compaction under your boots. The sea had pulled back past the kelp line, past the boulder field, past anything the oldest fisherman could remember seeing naked, and it kept going, revealing a seabed that hadn't seen air in living memory: ribbed sand, dead eelgrass lying flat as combed hair, and pools of stranded water holding confused fish like evidence bags.

"Huh," I said, which is what I say instead of the things I'm actually thinking.

"You're the digger," Dora said. "What do you make of it?"

What I made of it was money. Exposed seabed means exposed stratigraphy, and exposed stratigraphy near a fishing town that's been here two hundred years—and had people on it for four thousand before that—means artifacts sitting in the open like unattended wallets. I made of it also something colder, which was the knowledge that whatever the tide gave back today it would take back tonight, and everything not recovered would go under again, possibly for another century. The shore keeps books, my father used to say. Terrible bookkeeper. Never balances. Just keeps adding entries and drowning the ledger.

"I'll work the north spit first," I told her. "Then the point, if the light holds."

Dora squinted at me with the particular suspicion of small-town elders toward anyone young and competent who arrived without a car, without luggage beyond a duffel, and without answers to the question *so where are you from exactly*. "You're not staying long, are you."

It wasn't a question. Small towns always know. "I go where the work is."

"Mm." She pulled her coat tighter. "Everyone says that. Nobody means it. Either they mean *I'm leaving* or they mean *I never left and I hate it*." She looked out at the bared seabed, and something moved behind her face like a fish under ice. "That water's never been that far out. Not in my life. My mother used to say the tide keeps accounts, and someday it comes to collect."

"Cheerful town," I said.

"We're a dying town," she corrected. "We get to be cheerful about whatever we want."

* * *

The midden was better than advertised. That's the thing about me—I'm good at this. Not good the way my father was good, which was good at talking about it, good at grants and lectures and the particular performance of expertise where you hold a potsherd up to the light and let everyone watch you be wise. I'm good the way a pickpocket is good: quiet, fast, and only interested in what's actually there.

By nine I had three test pits opened on the spit and a grid staked with driftwood. The midden ran deeper than the storm cut suggested—layers of crushed shell, charcoal lenses, fish bone so dense it packed like plywood. Clam, cockle, some salmon vertebrae, a scatter of fire-cracked rock. A kitchen midden, generations deep. Somebody's family had eaten here for a very long time, and the way the layers lay told me they'd eaten well, then less well, then not at all—the upper layers thinning out around the time the cannery closed, the story of the whole coast written in garbage, which is the only honest archive anybody ever keeps.

I was cataloguing a bone awl—nice one, polished from use, somebody's hands had worn it smooth over years—when the shadow fell across my pit.

"You're the one doing Dora's dig."

He was maybe sixty, built like the pilings under the old cannery dock, wearing rubber boots and the expression of a man who has audited many strangers. Behind him, two younger men in matching rain gear watched me with the flat attention of people deciding whether I was weather or threat.

"Doing some contract work for the historical society, yes."

"University sent someone too." He handed down a folded flyer, damp at the crease. "Coastal Survey Project. They want locals to help map the intertidal sites while the tide's out. Pay's decent. Room and board at the field station."

I read the flyer because refusing without reading is amateur hour. Coastal Survey Project, Pacific Rim Archaeology Program, Dr. E. Halloran, principal investigator. *Documenting endangered cultural resources during anomalous low-tide event.* They even had a logo. Universities love a logo.

"They paying per diem?" I asked, which bought me a moment to think.

"Per diem, mileage, equipment."

Here's the problem with university surveys, and I say this with love, having grown up inside one like a barnacle on a research vessel: universities come with names attached. Names on permits, names on rosters, names on sign-in sheets that live in databases forever. I have spent fifteen years making sure my name appears nowhere that outlives me. A per diem is a fine thing, but a paper trail is a debt that compounds.

"Appreciate it," I said, folding the flyer into my pocket—refusing outright looks suspicious; pocketing it looks noncommittal, which is safer—"but I've got a commitment through the week."

"The midden?" He glanced at my grid, my neat little squares, my labeled bags. His eyebrows did something complicated. "That's decent work. Where'd you train?"

"Family business," I said, and went back to my trowel, which is the universal archaeologist signal for *this conversation has ended*, though civilians rarely respect it.

They stood there a moment longer. The older one—Halvorsen, I'd learn later, third-generation gillnetter, the kind of man whose distrust is actually discernment—looked at my hands. People underestimate hands. Mine have dirt worked into the knuckle creases that won't ever come out, calluses in the pattern of a trowel grip, a scar across the left palm from a profile collapse in a place I won't name. Halvorsen looked at those hands and then at my face, and whatever math he did, he didn't share the answer.

"Tide comes back at four," he said finally. "Faster than you'd think. Water doesn't warn you twice."

"No," I agreed. "It just collects."

* * *

I worked the point in the afternoon, and that's where the day stopped being a job.

The point was different geology—basalt, wave-cut, a shelf of it stepping down into the revealed seabed—and at the base of the lowest step, in a seam that should have been underwater until the end of the world, the retreating tide had scoured out a hollow. And in the hollow, half-buried in sand that had no business being there, was stone. Worked stone. Not local basalt—something finer-grained, carried, placed.

I should tell you what I felt, and what I felt was nothing, professionally speaking, because feeling things on a site is how you break things. But underneath the professional nothing there was the other thing, the thing I carry everywhere like a stone in my boot: the recognition that every find is a message from someone who couldn't know you'd be the one reading it. My father used to call it correspondence with the dead. He said it beautifully, in lecture halls, to applause. I've never found it beautiful. I've found it heavy. Every artifact is a letter, and you can't write back, and mostly the letters say *we were here and now we aren't,* which is not news anyone wants delivered daily.

I knelt and cleared the sand with a brush I keep for exactly this caliber of moment.

The stone was maybe eight inches long, river-smoothed, and carved. Not decorated—carved with intent, with a mark cut deep enough to survive centuries of burial. I turned it in the flat grey light and my hands, which do not shake, shook.

Because I knew the mark.

Not from any textbook. Not from any site report. From a kitchen table in a house we lost, from a man drawing it absently in spilled flour while he talked about a glacier I wasn't allowed to ask about, saying *this is how I sign my work, kiddo, remember it, it might matter someday.* A spiral broken by a straight line passing through it—a passage through a turning world, he'd called it, though he never said passage to where.

My father's mark. On a stone, buried in a sealed seam beneath a headland in a town I'd chosen off a map precisely because nothing had ever happened here.

There are moments when the ground itself lies to you—when something is intrusive, out of context, placed rather than deposited—and every instinct I own was screaming *intrusive, placed, deliberate.* Someone put this stone here. Someone who signed his work the way Edmund Nakamura signs his work.

Except my father had been dead to me for twelve years and missing from all public record for six, and the last postcard he ever sent me was from a coastal town four hundred miles north, postmarked the summer three climbers walked into a sea cave and didn't walk out.

I sat back on my heels. The gulls were yelling about something. Far out, the water waited at the edge of the world it had abandoned, patient as a creditor.

"Okay," I said aloud, to the stone, to the seabed, to nobody. "Okay. What did you do?"

The stone didn't answer. Stones never do. That's the whole problem with them—they keep everything, and they tell you nothing unless you already know how to listen, and what I knew how to listen for was my father's voice, and my father's voice was saying, in memory, in flour-dust, in the tone he saved for the few true things he ever told me:

*The tide keeps books, kiddo.*

I bagged the stone. Labeled it. Didn't photograph it—some part of me, quick and animal, already knew this was not an entry for Dora's coffee-can ledger. And when I looked up from the bag, the light had gone strange and low, and the water was coming back.

Halvorsen had warned me. Water doesn't warn you twice. It came across the flats faster than walking pace, silent, seamless, erasing my grid, my pits, the midden, the whole morning's careful accounting, and I stood on the basalt shelf with my father's signature in my pack and watched the sea close its ledger over everything, and I understood that I had about eighteen hours to decide whether I was leaving this town the way I always leave towns—

—or whether, for the first time in fifteen years, something had followed me instead.
