## 2. What the Water Returns

The tide went out the way a liar leaves a room—slowly at first, then all at once, and with something important missing.

I was on the flats before dawn because that's when the money is. Shell middens don't keep banker's hours; they keep tide's hours, and the tide had been advertising a record low for three days like a carnival barker with a grudge against the sea. The harbormaster had posted numbers on the fuel office window in grease pencil, updating them each morning with the grim satisfaction of a man watching his own town become an anomaly. *Minus 2.1 meters. Minus 2.4.* Old-timers stood at the seawall with their hands in their pockets saying they'd never seen the bottom of the channel, and younger people said it too, which was its own kind of lie, since none of them had been alive long enough to have seen anything.

I'd been digging middens for the Kestrel Point historical society—well, for Mrs. Aldous, who *was* the Kestrel Point historical society, a one-woman institution operating out of a kitchen that smelled permanently of diesel and dill—for eleven days. She paid by the bag and asked no questions, which made her my favorite employer of the season. The university survey crew had offered me real money and a real title two days after I arrived, and I'd turned them down with the practiced smile of someone who has learned that titles come with paper trails. You can't vanish from a payroll. You can vanish from a beach.

So: dawn, minus 2.4 meters, and me walking out across seabed that hadn't seen air since before anyone currently breathing had been born.

The flats were doing something strange to the light. Exposed kelp lay in ropes and coils, black and gleaming, like the hair of drowned things. Starfish the size of dinner plates clung to rocks they would not be able to hold onto by noon. A seal watched me from a pool with the flat moral judgment only seals can manage. Everything smelled of iodine and rot and cold iron—the smell of the ocean being caught without its clothes on.

And beneath the headland, where the chart said there was nothing but cliff base and surge channel, there was a hole.

* * *

I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the one inheritance my father left me that I've managed not to pawn: I should have walked away.

Here's what I knew at that moment. One: the charts showed no cave. Two: the locals had a legend about a cave under the headland that "took payment," which I'd heard twice in the tavern and once from a kid selling bait, always delivered in the tone people use for things they believe but wish they didn't. Three: my father had worked this coast in his twenties, before I existed, back when he was still the kind of archaeologist whose name opened doors instead of closing them. Four: I had spent my entire adult life developing an instinct for places where my father had been, the way some people develop an allergy. Hives of the soul.

The cave mouth was maybe four meters high, half-collapsed on its eastern side, framed in rock so dark it looked cut rather than eroded. The tide had pulled back past it like a curtain, and inside there was shadow going back further than my headlamp wanted to reach.

I stood there for a while doing the arithmetic every digger does. Tide tables said we had perhaps five hours before the water started its return, less if the record behaved like records do and overcorrected. The floor of the approach was gravel and old shell—*old* shell, the bleached kind, not last week's dinners—and I could see, even from thirty meters out, that the deposit lines on the walls ran higher than any tide in the tables. This cave had been sealed a long time. Whatever the water was returning today, it wasn't returning for the first time.

Curiosity, I told myself. Purely professional.

Dread wears curiosity's coat remarkably well. It fits in the shoulders. It smiles. It walks you right up to the entrance and says *just a quick look, just to rule it out,* and you go along with it because you've never once in your life ruled anything out, that's the whole disease of you.

I went in.

* * *

The passage narrowed and then, unexpectedly, widened—a chamber tall enough that my lamp beam lost its ceiling. The air inside was wrong in a way I took a moment to name: it was dry. Sea caves are wet things, dripping, breathing salt. This one breathed dust. Somewhere behind the walls, water moved—I could hear it, a thin persistent trickle, running *away* from the entrance, deeper into the headland, which was backwards, which was impossible, which I noted and filed under *later* because the alternative was turning around and I apparently wasn't going to turn around.

The floor was midden. Real midden, deep and layered, centuries of it—charcoal lenses, fire-cracked stone, bone sorted into the gray paste that time makes of meals. Someone had lived here once, or sheltered here, or died here, and the deposits said *long ago*, pre-contact maybe, older than anything Mrs. Aldous was paying me forty cents a bag to pull off the town beach. My hands itched. That's the truth of what I am: show me a stratigraphy and my hands itch to read it like a ledger, every layer an entry, every entry a debt somebody left behind.

But it was the objects that stopped me.

The lantern came first, because lanterns announce themselves. Rusted nearly to lace, sitting upright on a flat stone as if someone had set it down meaning to come back. The glass was fogged white with corrosion. I crouched beside it and did not touch it, because you don't touch things until you've looked at everything around them, and everything around it included a boot.

A woman's boot. Leather, gone stiff and dark, laces still tied—which meant it hadn't been pulled off, which meant the foot had either left it voluntarily through some means I didn't want to imagine or hadn't left it at all. It sat at an angle, toe down, heel up, the way a boot falls when the leg wearing it has been removed by water or time or both. There was no other bone in sight. The sea keeps books, my father used to say, but it's a sloppy accountant; it loses pages constantly and balances the rest by drowning them.

I knelt there longer than I meant to. The trickling water ran its backwards errand somewhere in the dark. My breath sounded loud and stupid.

Then I saw the stone.

It was wedged in a crack in the chamber wall at chest height, deliberately, visibly—not dropped, *placed*. A slab of gray basalt the size of a hymnal, and carved into its face, deep enough to survive decades of damp, was a mark I knew better than my own signature, because for years it *had been* my signature, or close enough: the spiral-with-a-break, the broken spiral, the mark my father cut into every site he ever recorded, every tool handle, every field journal cover, the margin of every letter he ever sent me before the letters stopped.

He called it his apology mark. He never explained what it was an apology *for*. When I was nine I thought it was a snail. When I was nineteen I thought it was vanity. Now I mostly think it's a wound he kept reopening on purpose.

My father had been in this cave.

Not his expedition generically. Not the coast broadly. Him, specifically, kneeling where I was kneeling, cutting that mark into stone with the short chisel he carried in his breast pocket and used for nothing else. And then he had left—walked out with the tide, presumably, leaving a lantern and a woman's boot and a message addressed to whoever the water eventually let in.

The dread stopped wearing its coat at that point. It stood there plainly, in the beam of my headlamp, and it looked a great deal like my father's handwriting.

* * *

I did what I always do when grief ambushes me mid-task: I got organized.

Photographs—I still shoot film for site documentation, digital files can be subpoenaed, film can be burned. Scale references from my kit. Sketch map of the chamber, the crack, the stone's orientation, the boot's position relative to the lantern, the lantern relative to the entrance line. Notes on the midden layers visible in the wall cut. Twenty minutes of work that steadied my hands because work is the only prayer I know.

The stone itself I photographed in place from six angles. Then I sat back on my heels and had the argument.

Leave it. Obviously leave it. It's evidence of something—of *what*, exactly? A missing person, possibly, though the boot could be fifty years old, could be older. A marker, certainly. A message, plausibly, and messages addressed to strangers are rarely friendly. The tide would come back and re-seal the door and whatever this was would go back to being legend, and legends are the safest place for things to live. Legends don't get people ransacked. Legends don't get people followed home.

But.

But my father had put it there, and my father had spent thirty years making sure nothing he touched stayed touchable. He'd erased himself so thoroughly that when the scandal broke I couldn't find a single colleague who'd admit to sharing a tent with him. He was a man who cleaned up after himself with religious thoroughness. If he had *left* something—if he had carved a mark into stone and wedged it into a crack where the sea itself would guard it—then it wasn't forgotten. It was *deposited*. Deliberately. For recovery.

By whom?

Well. By whoever knew the mark. By whoever had spent their childhood tracing it in the dirt of a dozen campsites while he explained, patiently, that it meant *I'm sorry* and refusing, always, to say to whom.

The water was already thinking about coming back. I could feel it in the changed note of the trickle behind the walls, in the cold pushing up through the gravel. Out beyond the entrance the light on the flats had gone from gold to pewter.

I took the stone.

I'm not going to dress it up. I wrapped it in my shirt, put it in my pack, and walked out of that chamber past the boot I chose not to look at again, and I told myself it was documentation, preservation, responsible custody of cultural material—all the phrases we use for taking things. The truth is simpler and uglier: it was addressed to me, or to the ghost of who I used to be to him, and I have never once in my life left mail unopened, no matter how much it costs.

The seal in the pool outside was gone. Of course it was. Even the animals know when to stop watching.

* * *

I climbed the headland path as the water began its return, and from the top I watched the cave mouth take its first drink—a tongue of green sliding in over the gravel, patient as rent. In an hour the door would be shut. In a day the tide tables would climb back toward ordinary and the whole coast would pretend the morning had been a rumor.

In my pack, against my spine, the stone rode like a second heartbeat.

Back in my rented room above the bait shop, I set it on the desk under the lamp and finally looked at it properly, and that's when I saw what the rush of the cave had hidden: the mark wasn't alone. Beneath the broken spiral, smaller, shallower, cut by a different hand—a narrower hand, a careful hand—were letters. Weathered almost to suggestion, but I read soil for a living; I can read worse than weathering.

Three letters. *E.D.M.*

And below those, in the same small careful script, a date: *1987.*

I sat very still. Outside, the gulls were working the returning tide, screaming about it the way gulls do, and somewhere down the street Mrs. Aldous's kitchen light came on, and the world continued being a place where things could be dated.

1987. The year, if the tavern talk was to be believed, that three climbers went into these hills and did not come out. The year my father led his last expedition before the fall, before the disgrace, before the silence that raised me as surely as he didn't.

The cave takes payment, the locals said. Three climbers. One boot. One lantern. One stone with my father's apology cut into it, dedicated to someone named Edmund—or E.D.M., initials, a grave marker for a man the glacier or the sea or the family had swallowed whole.

I poured the last of the coffee and drank it cold, standing up, the way you do when you already know you won't be sleeping.

Because here was the thing I couldn't stop circling, the entry in the ledger that wouldn't balance: my father had *left* that stone. Men who bury secrets don't carve directions to them into basalt. Which meant the stone wasn't the secret.

It was the receipt.

And somewhere in this town, tonight, someone else had done the same arithmetic I had—because as I turned down the lamp, I heard, faint on the stairs, footsteps stopping outside my door. Not passing. Stopping.

Listening.
