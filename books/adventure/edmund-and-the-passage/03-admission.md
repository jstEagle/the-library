## 3. Admission

The woman who ran the bait shop was named Odell, and she had the kind of face that suggested she'd already heard every lie a person could tell and found most of them derivative.

"You went in there," she said. It wasn't a question. She set down the coffee pot she'd been using as a conversational prop and looked at me the way you look at a dog that's gotten into something.

"I looked in," I said. "There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed. "One of them gets you killed slower." She poured herself a cup without offering me one, which told me everything about how this conversation was going to go. "You're the one digging the middens for Halvorsen. The Nakamura kid."

"Felix."

"Sure." She said it like she'd filed the name under fiction and moved on. "Your father came through here too. Nineteen eighty-seven. Led a whole crew of fancy people up the headland with their bright jackets and their instruments that cost more than my boat."

I kept my hands around my own cup and my face doing nothing. This is a skill. You learn it young when your last name is a headline in certain circles. "I wouldn't know. I was—" I did the math out loud anyway, because refusing to do it would have been louder. "Not born yet."

"No," Odell said. "But he talked about you. The whole expedition did, some nights, at this very counter. 'My kid,' he'd say. 'My kid reads soil like other people read books.'" She sipped her coffee and watched me over the rim. "Funny thing to brag about."

"It's a living."

"So was his." She set the cup down with a click that ended something. "Three of them went into that cave in June of '87. Voss, Lindqvist, and the financier's man—Edmund Hale. None of them came out. Your father came down off that headland two days later with half his gear abandoned and a story about weather that nobody believed then and I don't believe now." She shrugged, a small economical motion, like folding a flag. "So when I say the cave takes payment, I'm not being colorful. I'm being arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," I repeated.

"Three in. Zero out. That's the kind of sum this town doesn't forget." She turned back to her register, conversation over, and I stood there holding coffee I hadn't paid for because she hadn't asked me to. At the door she spoke again without looking up. "The tide comes back tomorrow night. Whatever's under the headland goes back under the water where it's been keeping its books for forty years. My advice? Let it balance itself."

* * *

I didn't let it balance itself. That's not really in my nature, and by then it wasn't really in my power either, because I'd spent the afternoon turning the carved stone over in my hands until I'd memorized it the way you memorize a face you're going to have to identify later.

The mark cut into it wasn't decorative. It was a surveyor's glyph—old style, pre-digital, the kind you chisel into bedrock or paint on a claim stake so the next person knows: *someone was here, someone measured this, someone means to come back.* Except my father's version had a habit. He added a second stroke to the base line, a little tail, like a signature flourish. He said it was so his marks couldn't be forged. I used to think that was vanity. Standing in Odell's shop with the stone in my pocket, I started thinking it might have been fear wearing vanity's coat.

Because here's what I knew, sitting in that bait shop while Odell performed arithmetic at me: the stone was fresh-cut. Not 1987-fresh. Recent. Within years, maybe months. Which meant either my father had been back—and he hadn't been within five hundred miles of this coast since I was fourteen—or someone else was cutting his mark, and there are only a few reasons a person does that, and none of them are sentimental.

I walked back along the harbor road in the long gold light, past boats that had stopped being boats and become furniture, past the cannery with its windows boarded in a pattern that looked almost deliberate, almost like writing. A town that keeps its dead industries standing isn't sentimental. It's bookkeeping. It's saying: *we remember what we were owed.*

A kid was sitting on the cannery steps, maybe ten, mending a net with the bored competence of someone who'd been doing it since before they could name what they were doing. They watched me pass.

"You're the one who went in the cave," the kid said.

"Word travels fast."

"Odell told everybody." The kid didn't look up from the net. "She also told everybody you'd be dead by Friday, but she gave it fifty-fifty odds, so."

"Generous."

"My grandpa says the cave takes payment. He says it took his brother." Now the kid looked up, and their eyes were old in a way that had nothing to do with age. "He says it doesn't take money."

"What does it take?"

The kid went back to the net. "Whatever you can't afford to lose."

* * *

Halvorsen paid me that evening in cash and unease. He counted out the bills twice, which I understood—he was checking whether *I* was counting them—and then held the envelope an extra beat before letting go.

"The university people called the house," he said. "Looking for you. Said you'd turned down a contract."

"They found me anyway. Impressive paperwork."

"That's not the part that bothered me." Halvorsen was a big man gone soft at the edges, the way driftwood goes soft, but his eyes were still knot-hard. "The part that bothered me is they knew you'd turned it down before you'd told anyone. And the part that bothered me more than that—" he leaned in, lowering his voice as if the harbor itself might repeat him, "—is that a man came by yesterday asking after you. Polite fellow. Good coat. Asked if you were local, asked where you were staying, asked what you'd been digging." He straightened. "I told him you were a drifter and not worth the asking. Which was a lie, but it was a cheap one, and cheap lies are the only charity I've got."

"What did he look like?"

"Like a man who's never once been cold." Halvorsen handed over the envelope. "Get off the beach, Nakamura. Whatever the tide showed you, it showed somebody else first."

* * *

My room was above the chandlery, third door off a landing that smelled of rope tar and decades. It cost eleven dollars a night and came with a bed, a chair, a window that didn't quite close, and a landlady who communicated exclusively through notes taped to surfaces. I climbed the stairs with Halvorsen's warning folded into my jacket next to the carved stone, running the day's ledger in my head the way I do before sleep—the way my father taught me, actually, though he'd have hated knowing that. *Read the entries,* he used to say over camp dinners, tapping the ground with two fingers. *Everything leaves a record. Your job is just to be literate.*

Today's entries:

One. Odell knew my father's name before I gave it, and knew things about 1987 that weren't in any public record I've ever found. Small towns keep archives in their mouths.

Two. The stone was recently cut. Someone is signing my father's work, or forging it, or continuing it.

Three. A polite man in a good coat was asking after me a full day before the tide dropped far enough to show the cave. Which meant he hadn't come about the cave. He'd come about me. Or about my father. Or about whatever connects us across thirty-seven years and one disappearing act.

Four. The university knew I'd refused them before refusal was public. Somebody was reading my mail, literal or otherwise.

Add it up and you get a number I didn't like. I sat on the bed with the stone in my palm and did what I always do when the ledger turns ugly: I made a list of exits. Bus south at 6:40. Fishing charter if I traded labor. The coastal trail, ugly but unwatched. Three exits, all viable, all available before morning.

I should have taken one. Any of them. I want to be honest about that, because everything that came after—the glacier, the family, the flood, all of it—started here, in this room, on this night, with me looking at three open doors and choosing instead to sit still and examine a rock.

But here's the thing about running that nobody tells you until you've done it long enough: eventually you notice that you're not running *from* anything anymore. You're just running *in place*, and the places change, and the doors stay open, and you keep not going through them, because some part of you has figured out that the thing chasing you isn't behind you. It's ahead. It's waiting at every exit, patient as tide, and it has your name on a list.

Or maybe that's just what I told myself at midnight, holding a stone with my father's handwriting on it, in a town where three people had walked into a hole in the world and not walked out.

* * *

I woke at 2:14 in the morning because the window that didn't quite close was suddenly closed, and I hadn't closed it.

That's the detail that saved my life, if you want to call it that. Not courage, not skill—just a bad latch and a habit of cataloguing. I lay still in the dark and listened. Below me, through the floorboards, the chandlery ticked and settled. Outside, the harbor breathed against the pilings. And inside the room—

Inside the room, someone was breathing. Carefully. The breathing of a person who has trained themselves to breathe quietly and knows they're failing.

I did the math in the dark. One intruder, positioned near the desk, between me and my pack. My boots were by the door, which meant the door was a luxury. The window was shut, which meant they'd come in through it and sealed it behind them—which meant they planned to be a while, and didn't plan to leave in a hurry, and considered me asleep rather than armed.

I am not armed. I've never been armed. But I grew up on sites where the difference between a collapse and a close call was whether you'd braced the shoring before dinner, and the lesson underneath that lesson is: *know where the weight is before anything moves.*

The weight in that room was the chair. Old oak, heavy as sin, left by some previous tenant with better furniture priorities than mine.

I don't remember deciding. I remember the space between heartbeats, and then I remember swinging the chair in a flat arc toward the sound of the breathing, and the sound becoming a grunt, and the grunt becoming motion—a body going past me, low, professional, toward the window. Glass cracked. Cold air poured in like water finding a level. And then the room was empty except for me, the ruined chair, and the mess.

They'd gone through everything. Not ransacked—*audited*. Every item lifted, examined, replaced. My clothes refolded wrong by half an inch. My pack's buckles rebuckled in a different order. My father's photograph—the only one I carry, him squinting into sun at some dig site, hand raised against the camera—turned face-down. People who search a room tell you things. This searcher had told me they were thorough, unhurried, and looking for something specific enough to leave everything else untouched.

I checked the obvious hiding spots first. Cash: untouched. Passport: there. The journal fragment I keep sewn into my jacket lining: present, though the stitching had been redone with slightly different thread, which I found less reassuring than you'd think.

Then I checked my pocket, where the carved stone had been when I fell asleep.

Gone.

I stood in the middle of the wrecked room with cold air moving through the broken glass and felt something I couldn't immediately name. It wasn't fear, exactly. Fear would come later, on the ice, in the water, in plenty of forms. What I felt that night was closer to recognition. Like receiving a bill you'd known was coming—itemized, dated, addressed in a hand you know better than your own.

Because here's what the theft told me, and it told me clearly: whoever sent that polite man in the good coat didn't want me. If they'd wanted me, I'd have woken up somewhere else, or not woken up. They wanted the stone. They wanted my father's mark, cut fresh into rock from beneath a headland that hadn't shown its cave mouth in living memory. They wanted proof of something—or the absence of proof—and they'd waited out a record tide and searched a drifter's eleven-dollar room to get it.

Which meant the stone mattered. Which meant the mark mattered. Which meant my father—whoever he'd become, wherever he'd gone, however thoroughly he'd erased himself from my life—had left something under that headland worth breaking windows for.

I gathered what was mine, including the chair leg I'd broken off and kept, because a club costs nothing and weighs little. Downstairs, the landlady's note on the door said, in her only registered tone: *GLASS IS EXTRA.*

Outside, the town slept under a sky going grey at the edges. The tide had turned; I could feel it in the way the harbor water moved, that subtle reversal, the sea beginning the slow work of taking back what it had lent. By tomorrow night the cave mouth would be gone. Whatever was inside it—lantern, boot, my father's signature in stone—would go back under the books.

Except the stone wouldn't. The stone was in someone's pocket, moving through the dark toward whoever had sent it, and I was standing on a landing with a broken chair leg and a decision.

Three exits. Bus, boat, trail. All still open. All still viable.

Instead I went down to the harbor road and stood at the rail above the black water, and I looked north along the coast to where the headland crouched over the retreating sea, and I asked myself the question I'd been avoiding since Odell said *your father came through here too.*

What did you find, Dad? What was worth three lives, worth your name, worth thirty-seven years of silence between us?

And underneath it, quieter, the question that actually moved my feet:

Who else is still paying for it?

Behind me, up the street, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the cannery and started walking my way—not hurrying, not hiding, walking with the unhurried confidence of someone who knows exactly where I am and thinks I don't know where they are. Tall. Good coat. The gait of a man who's never once been cold.

I tightened my grip on the chair leg and did not run.

Not yet.
