## 4. The Nakamura Name

The bus to Harrow Point left at six in the morning, which meant I left the boarding house at five, which meant I didn't sleep, which was fine. Sleep and I had been on a trial separation for years and neither of us had filed the papers.

I packed the way I always packed: fast, by feel, everything that mattered already living in one duffel. The trick to leaving a place is to never fully arrive in it. I'd stayed nine days in Salt Hollow, which by my standards was practically a lease with a mortgage. I'd dug three middens, eaten too much chowder, and let a woman named Petra at the fish cannery tell me her life story over two lunches. All of it now closed like a ledger at the end of a fiscal year.

Except the cave. The cave didn't close.

I'd spent the night turning over what I'd found down there the way you turn over a stone you know has something under it and don't want to look at anyway. A woman's boot, salt-stiff, laces still knotted. A lantern rusted to the color of dried blood. And the stone — a flat piece of slate the size of a paperback, with a mark cut into it that I would have known anywhere, because I'd spent my childhood watching my father cut it into things. Tent stakes. Tree trunks. The doorframe of a rented house in Kamloops, with a pocketknife, at two in the morning, when he thought I was asleep.

A spiral with a break in it. A spiral that doesn't close.

He called it his signature. He called it a lot of things, my father, most of them after the third beer. *It means the work is never finished, Felix. It means you leave the line open.* What it actually meant, I'd come to believe, was *I was here and then I was gone, and you can't follow me.*

And now it was in a cave under a headland in a town whose name I'd had to look up, sitting next to a boot that belonged to a woman who'd been dead longer than I'd been alive, and someone had ransacked my room the night before and taken the stone and left everything else, including the cash in my boot, which told me everything I needed to know about who'd done it. Thieves take money. People with a purpose take the thing that matters.

So: Harrow Point bus, six a.m., duffel on my shoulder, and the Nakamura name doing what it always did, which was arriving somewhere and ruining it.

* * *

The bus stop was a shelter with a schedule from the previous decade taped to the glass, three miles down the coast road because Salt Hollow's actual center of town was too proud for public transit. I walked it in the dark with my collar up, and the sea on my left did its breathing thing, in and out, patient as a creditor.

I noticed her at the shelter before she noticed me noticing her, which is to say I noticed her the way you notice a rock in a riverbed that's the wrong shape. She was sitting on the bench with her hands folded, a woman of maybe sixty, gray hair cut short and practical, wearing a rain shell that had been repaired with actual stitching rather than tape. Nobody stitches a rain shell unless they intend to keep it for a decade. Nobody waits at a bus shelter at five-thirty in the morning unless they intend to intercept someone.

I considered the options. There was one road and it ran through her, or rather she sat on it, but the coast path went over the headland and I'd walked worse in worse weather. I could add four miles and a climb and be free of whatever this was.

But here's the thing about running: it costs. Every detour, every alias, every town you leave at five in the morning — it all goes on a tab, and the tab compounds. I'd been paying interest my whole life. And I was tired, in the bone way, the way that isn't fixed by sleep, and some traitor part of me wanted to know what a woman who stitches her own rain shell wanted badly enough to sit in the dark for an hour.

So I stopped, ten feet off, and said, "If this is about the room, the landlady's already got my deposit."

"Petra said you'd run." She didn't get up. Her voice was flat and northern, the vowels worn smooth like beach glass. "Petra also said you'd walk, not take the bus. Said you had the look of someone who doesn't like being in a metal box with windows."

"Petra talks too much."

"Petra's the only reason I found you at all, so I'll forgive her." She stood, unhurried, and I got a better look at her face. It was a face that had been handsome and was now something more severe — weathered, deliberate, with eyes that did the same thing mine did, cataloguing. She was reading me the way I read a shoreline. I knew the technique because I'd invented my own version of it, and I did not enjoy being on the receiving end.

"You're the Nakamura kid," she said.

"I'm nobody's kid."

"You're Edmund Nakamura's kid. Felix." She said my name like she was checking it against a list. Maybe she was. "You have his hands. He had that same way of standing, weight on the balls of the feet, like he might bolt."

"I'm going to bolt right now, actually."

"No, you're not." She said it without heat, the way you'd say *the tide's coming in.* "Because if you were going to run, you'd have run last night, after the break-in, and you didn't. You ran this morning, after you'd had time to think about what the break-in meant. Which tells me you're not running from the people who took the stone. You're running toward a decision. And decisions can be interrupted."

I hate being read. I want to be clear about that. I've built a whole life on being illegible — names changed at borders, work histories invented, a smile I keep in reserve for people who ask too many questions. And this woman had taken me apart in ninety seconds with the gentle efficiency of someone filleting a fish.

"Who are you?"

"Maren Voss."

The name landed somewhere under my ribs. Voss. The cave legend had come with names attached — the town told its story the way towns do, in fragments, and one of the fragments was a family that had never accepted the sea's verdict. Three climbers went into the cave in 1987 and the water kept them, and one family had spent thirty years refusing to sign the receipt.

"You're one of the vanished," I said. "One of the families."

"My father was Henrik Voss. He went into that cave on the fourteenth of June, 1987, with two other people, and he did not come out." She said it the way you recite a phone number you've dialed so many times your thumb knows it. "And your father led the expedition that sent them in."

* * *

Here's what I knew about my father's year of 1987, which is to say here's what I knew, which is to say almost nothing. I was four. What I had was residue: a photograph of him on a glacier, younger than I am now, squinting into a sun that no longer exists; a box of core samples in my mother's garage that she kept threatening to burn and never did; and the fact — the plain, ugly, documented fact — that 1987 was the year everything curdled. The year the funding dried up. The year the university stopped returning his calls. The year my mother started sleeping with her back to him and then, eventually, stopped sleeping in the house at all.

He never talked about Salt Hollow. He never talked about any of it, that's the thing about my father — he was a man who'd had a hole punched in him and had spent the rest of his life walking around with his hand over it, talking loudly about anything else. Glacial till. Sediment cores. The never-closing spiral.

"I don't know anything about 1987," I told Maren. "That's not a deflection. It's an inventory."

"I believe you." She reached into her shell jacket and brought out a plastic sleeve, the kind archivists use, and inside it was a folded map, old, soft at the creases, hand-annotated in pencil gone silver with age. "I've spent thirty years learning who to believe. You'd be surprised how short the list is. You're on it provisionally, and only because your father's mark was in that cave, and because someone wanted it badly enough to break into a room above a chowder house to get it."

"You know about that."

"I know everything that happens in Salt Hollow. It's a small place and I'm a patient woman. Those two facts together are worth more than money." She held out the map sleeve. "Do you know what this is?"

I didn't take it. Taking it would be a decision, and I was trying to keep my decisions in escrow. "Should I?"

"It's the route your father drew. Up the cave stream, inland, to the source under the Vinter Glacier. He drew it in the spring of '87 and gave a copy to my father, because my father was the one who could climb it." Her voice did something small and terrible on the word *climb* — a hairline crack in a load-bearing wall. "Three people went up that route. The glacier closed. The water kept them. And the family — my family — decided that someone had to be responsible, and your father was the someone, and he was gone before the ice finished closing."

"He ran," I said. It wasn't a question. I knew the shape of it. I'd been running the same route my whole life; I could recognize the trailhead anywhere.

"He ran," Maren agreed. "And I have spent three decades trying to decide whether that makes him a coward or a survivor, and I've come to the conclusion that it might make him both, and that the same might be true of his child, and that I don't have the luxury of sorting you two into neat piles anymore. Because the stone was in that cave, Felix. His mark, in the cave, near the boot, near the lantern. Which means either he went back — after, when everyone thought he'd washed his hands of it — or he left it there before, as a message. And either way, the people who took it from your room last night know what it means, and they're not the kind of people who take things for sentimental reasons."

"The family," I said. "Your family."

"My family," she said, and for the first time something moved behind her eyes, something old and tired and angry. "What's left of it. What it became. I don't call it my family anymore, but names are stubborn. So is blood."

* * *

We sat on the bench in the shelter while the sky went from charcoal to dirty silver, and she told me the parts she was willing to tell, and I listened the way I dig — carefully, in layers, noting everything and trusting nothing until it's been cross-sectioned.

The Voss family had been shipowners, then timber, then just money — old money with a grudge. When the climbers vanished, the law had done what the law does with a cave and a tide and three missing people: it wrote a verdict of accident and went home. The Voss family did not accept the verdict. They convened their own. And somewhere in the thirty years since, the family's grief had hardened into something with structure and rules and enforcers — a private justice, they called it, and they said it without irony, which was the most frightening part.

"And they think my father took something," I said. "From the cave. From the source."

"They think he took the reason three people died and buried it. Whatever your father found up there — and he found something, Felix, the money that funded that expedition came from my family and my family does not spend money without expecting returns — whatever it was, it's been under that glacier for thirty-seven years, and my family has been trying to dig it out almost as long. And now the tide's dropped farther than anyone alive has seen, and the cave's open, and the route will be open for a matter of weeks before calving season shuts it for another decade." She folded her hands again. "The window is open. Everyone who's waited thirty years is moving at once."

"Including you."

"Including me." She looked at me straight on. "I'm not going to lie to you, because you'd catch it and then we'd both have wasted a morning. I need you. Not the map — I have the map. You. Because you read ground the way your father did, and I've watched you work the middens, and you are better at it than he ever was, and I say that as someone who hated him for thirty years. The child out-dug the father. The whole coast says it."

"The whole coast says a lot of things."

"The whole coast said my father was dead within a week of him going missing. The whole coast was right." She let that sit. "I need someone who can dig, and climb, and lie when it's useful, and I need them to not be one of mine, because I don't trust one of mine as far as I could throw the glacier. And you need—" She paused, and I braced, because people love to tell you what you need and they're always wrong. "You need to know what your father did. You've been running from it your whole life and you didn't even know what *it* was. That's the worst kind of running. You're fleeing a room you've never entered."

The bus came at 6:04, headlights swinging up the coast road, and I stood up, and Maren Voss stood up with me, and we faced each other in the shelter with the duffel between us like a border crossing.

"If I get on that bus," I said, "you'll be at the next stop. And the one after. You've got thirty years of patience."

"I do."

"And the family will find me either way. They already have my smell."

"They do."

"And if I go with you, I'm not confessing anything. I'm not my father's proxy. Whatever he owes, I didn't sign for it."

"Debts don't transfer," Maren said. "But they attach. To names, to hands, to children who inherit the same fingers. I'm not asking you to pay. I'm asking you to come read the ledger with me. You're the only other person alive who can."

The bus doors hissed open. The driver looked at me with the flat patience of a man paid by the hour.

I want to tell you I made the decision for noble reasons. I want to tell you it was about truth, or justice, or the dead woman's boot, or the spiral that doesn't close. But the honest version is smaller and colder: I stood in the doorway of that bus and I thought about spending another decade under another name in another town, digging other people's garbage for other people's money, running from a room I'd never entered — and I thought, *I am so tired.* And then I thought about my father's mark cut into slate in the dark under the headland, the line left open, the work unfinished, and I thought: *he left it open for a reason. Maybe the reason was me.*

I stepped back. The doors closed. The bus went on to Harrow Point without me, and I watched it go with the specific grief of a person watching an exit seal itself, and then I turned around.

"Conditions," I said to Maren Voss.

"Name them."

"I dig where I decide to dig. I leave when I decide to leave. And if it turns out my father killed those people, I'm the one who decides what happens next. Not your family. Not your justice. Me."

She considered it. I could see her considering it, actually — the arithmetic moving behind her eyes, thirty years of want against one stranger's terms. Then she put out her hand, and her grip was a digger's grip, and I hated how much I respected it.

"Then you'd better see the rest of the map," she said. "Because there's a river on it that runs the wrong way, and until you understand that river, you don't understand anything your father was involved in."

She unfolded the map on the bench, right there in the shelter, in the gray morning light, and held it flat with both hands, and I leaned over it, and that was the moment I stopped running — though I didn't know it yet, and would have denied it under oath.

The river ran uphill.
