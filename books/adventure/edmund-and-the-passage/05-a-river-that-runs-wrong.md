## 5. A River That Runs Wrong

I told Felix Nakamura to get in the truck, and they got in the truck, which was the first thing about them that made sense to me. Everyone else I'd ever asked to do something had asked why first. Felix just looked at me for a long second with those ledger-keeper's eyes—counting me, weighing me, deciding what I'd cost—and then climbed in and shut the door like we'd been doing this for years.

We drove north out of town with the sun going orange behind us and the headland shrinking in the mirror until it looked like a closed fist.

"You want to tell me where we're going," Felix said. It wasn't a question. It was an invoice.

"Somewhere I can show you something without an audience."

"Meaning someone's watching."

"Someone's always watching in that town. Half of them are related to me." I took the turnoff past the cannery ruins, the one that turned to gravel and then to two ruts and then to a suggestion. "The other half are waiting for the tide to come back so they can stop talking about it."

"And you're not waiting?"

"I already know what the cave is."

That got me a look. Not surprise—Felix didn't seem built for surprise—but interest, the way a locksmith looks at a lock. "You've been inside."

"No." I let that sit while the truck bucked over a washout. "Nobody's been inside. That's the whole point. The cave isn't the thing. The cave is the door, and the door only opens when the water lets it, and the water—" I laughed, because thirty years of explaining this to people who nodded politely and then went back to their lives had taught me to laugh at the exact moment before the ridiculous part. "The water comes out of a glacier sixty miles inland. Uphill. Through solid rock. And once every decade or so, the ice opens a route along it, and for a few weeks you can walk from the sea to the source."

Felix was quiet long enough that I checked whether they were still awake.

"A river that runs wrong," they said finally. "That's the legend?"

"That's the *fact*. The legend is everything people stapled onto it afterward. Drowned brides. A troll's drainpipe. Admission charged by something under the hill." I shrugged. "Towns need stories the way boats need ballast. But the hydrology doesn't care what anyone believes. My father surveyed it. He put instruments on that stream for eleven years. He traced it up the gorge with dye and a barometer and a stubbornness I inherited like a disease. It goes into the ground below the town and comes out of the tongue of the Vinter Glacier, and there is no honest explanation for how."

"There's always an explanation."

"Then my father never found it. And he was better at finding things than anyone I've known." I heard my own voice do something on that last word, and I watched Felix hear it too. They were good at listening the way they were good at everything else—quietly, completely, like digging.

* * *

My father's name was Anders Voss. You won't find it in any archive worth trusting, because the family bought the archives' silence the same year he disappeared, but he was the best surveyor on this coast, and in 1987 he led five people into the gorge below the Vinter Glacier and none of them came back.

Five. People say three, because three families still live here to count themselves. The other two were hired men from down-coast whose relatives stopped asking around 1994. Grief keeps books too, and some ledgers just close.

I was nine years old when the expedition left. I remember my father kneeling in the yard to tie his boot, which he did badly, always, and looking up at me and saying, "Keep the map dry, little fish." He called me little fish because I'd nearly drowned at six and come up laughing, which worried him more than crying would have. The map was a joke between us—he'd drawn me a treasure chart of the garden, X marking the spot where he'd buried a tin of hard candy—and I kept it in my room for years after, through every move, every new house, every year the family said *Anders is dead, Maren, let Anders be dead.*

He wasn't dead. I know that now the way you know a bone is broken: by the wrongness of the weight on the other end.

Because eight months after the search was called off, a letter came. No return address, postmarked from a town four hundred miles south. In my father's hand. One page. I have read it maybe ten thousand times and I could recite it drunk, concussed, or dead, and part of me thinks I will.

Most of it was nothing. Weather. A line about his boots. An apology to my mother that managed to apologize without saying for what, which was my father all over. And then the last paragraph, the one that turned my life into the narrow straight trench it has been ever since:

*If you're reading this, the route held longer than we planned and shorter than we hoped. Don't come looking. The man who can finish this work is Edmund Nakamura, and if he ever sends his child this way, trust the child—the hands don't lie in that family. Burn this after you read it. Forgive me for the boots. —A.*

Edmund Nakamura.

I was twenty-two before I understood that the letter was a message in a bottle thrown not to my mother but forward, through time, to whoever Edmund Nakamura's child turned out to be. My father knew. Knew what would happen, or suspected, and couldn't say it plainly because plain words get people killed in my family, and instead he wrote a sentence about hands.

So I spent thirty years learning everything about Edmund Nakamura. The brilliant digger. The disgrace. The expedition financier's protégé who walked away clean while five men fed a glacier. The man who changed towns like shirts and never once surfaced anywhere a Voss could reach him. And somewhere in all that reading, I learned he had a kid. A kid who grew up on digs, who could apparently read soil like scripture, who vanished off the record entirely sometime in their twenties—which, given what I know about my own family's methods, struck me as less a lifestyle than a survival strategy.

And then the tide went out farther than it had gone in living memory, and the sea showed everyone a door, and three days later a stranger with Nakamura hands was prying shell out of a midden on the flats like the shore owed them money.

You don't believe in fate. Fine. Neither do I. I believe in patience and arithmetic. Thirty years of arithmetic pointed at that stranger on the flats, and I am not a woman who ignores a sum that comes out even.

* * *

I stopped the truck where the two ruts gave up entirely, at the mouth of the old survey trail, and got out the map.

Not the garden treasure—that burned with our third house, another story, another fire I don't believe was accidental. This was my father's real work: a base map of the gorge, hand-drafted, annotated in pencil over eleven summers, showing the stream's impossible course from the salt flats to the glacier's tongue. Every instrument station. Every dye test. Every date the ice had opened, reconstructed from tree scars and flood debris and one photograph from 1931 that shows a gap in the glacier wall you could drive a truck through.

Felix stood beside me in the cold coming off the water and read it the way I'd watched them read the beach that morning. Top to bottom. Left to right. Looking for errors.

"This is a survey grade map," they said. "This is better than survey grade. Who drew this?"

"My father."

"When?"

"Last time was the summer before he went in. 1986." I tapped the glacier's edge, where the penciled gaps clustered. "Here's the pattern. The tongue of the Vinter calves back against the valley wall, and when it does, it opens a corridor along the bedrock channel the river cut before the ice ever came. The river keeps the channel clear from underneath—it's why the stream runs warm-ish at the source, why it never freezes solid. The ice opens. The river runs daylight for the first time in a decade. And for a few weeks—my father estimated forty days, give or take, depending on the melt season—you can walk up the channel from the snout to the source."

"And then?"

"And then the glacier advances again, or the melt season ends and the corridor chokes with rubble, and the door shuts for another ten years." I looked at them. "The last opening was 1987. Do your own math."

Felix did their own math. I watched them do it—the flicker behind the eyes, the same look they'd had counting tides on the flats. "Nineteen years since the last window," they said slowly. "But the cycles aren't uniform."

"Eleven years, fourteen, nineteen, nine. My father thought it tracked with the Pacific decadal oscillation plus local accumulation, but the sample size is garbage and he knew it. What he was sure of was this." I touched the map again, lower down, where the gorge narrowed to a slot. "The window opens early and closes fast. You get maybe two weeks of safe travel at the start before the melt makes the icefall suicidal. We're inside that window right now. We may be the only two people alive who know it."

"Your family doesn't know?"

"My family knows the legend. My family burned my father's surveys in—" I stopped. Recalculated. There was no version of this conversation that didn't eventually arrive at the whole truth, and I'd decided on the flats that Felix Nakamura was going to get the whole truth, because you don't ask a person to walk into a glacier on partial information. Not if you're asking them to walk in for *your* reasons. "My family destroyed my father's work and called it closure. What survived is this copy, because I stole it when I was sixteen, and a duplicate of the field notes I rebuilt from memory over twenty years, which is either impressive or pathetic depending on the day."

Felix looked at me for a long moment. Then: "Why show me? You don't know me. You intercepted me leaving town, which, for the record, is behavior that would make most people run faster."

"I know your hands."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I've got that matters." I folded the map along its old creases and held it out. "Your father was supposed to finish my father's work. That's what the letter says. The man who can finish this work. Which means whatever's at the source—whatever five people died for in 1987—your father knew about it, and walked away, and let my family bury it. And I have spent half my life being angry about that." I heard the anger in my voice and didn't sand it down. Felix deserved to hear it. People who sand things down are usually hiding the grain. "But the letter also says trust the child. So here I am, trusting the child, and hating myself for it, and doing it anyway. Take the map."

Felix didn't take it.

They looked at it the way you look at a contract—with the specific attention of someone who has signed bad ones before. "What's at the source?" they asked. "Not the legend. What did they go to find?"

"I don't know. Nobody knows. My father's last letter doesn't say. The family says treasure, which means it isn't treasure, because my family lies the way rivers run—downhill and toward the sea." I exhaled. "Whatever it was, it was worth killing five people over, and worth thirty years of making sure nobody went back. That's what I know. That's everything I know. And every year the window opens and closes and I stand on the headland and watch it happen, and I do nothing, because I can't do it alone and I don't trust anyone alive except—" I gestured at them, at the whole improbable fact of them, "—apparently you."

"The hands don't lie," Felix quoted softly. Something moved across their face—not softness exactly. Recognition. The look of someone hearing a dead parent's voice out of a stranger's mouth, which is a cruelty and a gift in the same envelope.

They took the map.

They unfolded it right there in the wind, holding the corners down, and studied it with an intensity that should have been flattering and mostly just made me nervous, because I could see them finding things in it I'd never found. The way they tilted it against the light. The way their thumb stopped on a penciled notation near the snout—a depth measurement, corrected twice—and stayed there.

"He dated his corrections," Felix said. "Same pen pressure on the original and the fix. He redrew rather than erased. That's—" They stopped. Swallowed. When they spoke again their voice had gone flat in the particular way voices go flat when they're carrying too much. "I know this hand. I grew up watching it."

"I know," I said.

"You don't know. You know *of* him. I set his stakes when I was seven years old. I mixed his plaster. I—" Felix folded the map with sudden, careful precision, like closing a wound. "He never talked about 1987. Not once. Twenty years of camps and fires and long drives and he never said the word Vinter. I used to think it was shame."

"And now?"

"Now I think it was a debt." They looked up at me, and the wind pushed their hair across their face and they didn't bother with it. "You said the window's open now. Two weeks of safe travel."

"Less, if the melt runs hot."

"Then we shouldn't be standing in a truck bed having feelings about cartography." They tucked the map inside their jacket, against the body, where a digger carries the things that matter. "Gear. Food. Rope. Where's the nearest place to buy any of that that isn't crawling with your relatives?"

"Harstad's Landing. Six hours north. It's small enough that nobody watches and mean enough that nobody asks."

"Good." Felix climbed back into the truck, and just before pulling the door shut, paused. "Maren. Your father wrote that letter eight months after the search ended. From four hundred miles away. Which means he got out."

"Yes."

"Which means somebody else didn't want him to talk about it more than my father wanted to come home." They said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "Think about what kind of secret buys that much silence."

I had thought about it. For thirty years, in the dark, in the space between one breath and the next, I had thought about almost nothing else.

We drove north with the headlights on and the map riding warm against Felix's chest, and I watched the road unspool and tried not to count the hours until Harstad's Landing, because counting was all I'd done for thirty years and it had gotten me exactly one stranger and one window and no closer to my father than a bootlace in a yard.

It was full dark when we saw the lights behind us.

Not police—police announce themselves. These hung back a quarter mile, patient, keeping pace, the way my cousin Alder drives when he wants you to know he's there and wants you to wonder why he hasn't passed yet.

Felix saw them too. Watched the mirror for a long moment, then reached over and turned off the dash lights without being asked.

"Well," they said. "Tell me that's not family."

I didn't tell them that.
