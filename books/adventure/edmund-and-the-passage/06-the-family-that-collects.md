## 6. The Family That Collects

We made it forty miles before the family caught up.

I want that on the record, because Maren would tell it differently—she'd say we *let* ourselves be found, that she had a plan, that the plan was sound. But I was there, and I know what happened: we stopped at a diner outside Kestrel Bend because I was hungry and she was tired of my driving, and while we were arguing about pie versus function, a man in a gray wool coat sat down at our table like he'd been invited.

He hadn't been. Nobody invites a man who moves like that. He moved the way water moves through a crack—patiently, and with total confidence about where he was going.

"Felix Nakamura," he said. Not a question. He pronounced it correctly, which unsettled me more than anything else he did that day. Most people get it wrong, and I've spent years letting them.

"And you're the bill," I said.

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more like the memory of one, kept in a drawer somewhere. "Alder Voss," he said, as if that explained everything. To be fair, from the way Maren went still beside me, it did.

"You know him," I said to her.

"I know *of* him." She hadn't touched her coffee. "We've never been introduced."

"We should fix that." Alder pulled out a chair—the fourth leg of our table, which until that moment had held nothing but my duffel—and sat down with the unhurried grace of a man who owned the chair, the table, the diner, and possibly the concept of sitting. "Cousin Maren. Thirty years, and this is how we meet. In a diner with laminated menus."

"The family doesn't send Christmas cards."

"The family sends me." He said it without pride or complaint, the way you'd say *the family sends invoices*. Then he turned to me, and I got my first real look at his eyes, which were pale gray and entirely without hurry. "You have something of ours."

"I don't," I said. "I had something of my father's. Different thing."

"Is it?" He folded his hands on the table. They were big hands, clean, the nails trimmed short—a working man's hands on an accountant's schedule. "The stone your father carved. The mark he cut into it. That mark belongs to an expedition that lost three people. Everything connected to that expedition belongs to us now. That's not theft, Mr. Nakamura. That's estate law. We're simply the estate."

"That's not how estate law works."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "It's how ours does."

* * *

Here's what I knew about Alder Voss before that morning: nothing. Here's what I knew after ten minutes: everything I needed to.

Some men threaten you. Alder didn't threaten. He *contextualized*. He told me about the weather in the mountains, about how the Vinter Glacier was having an early season, about how roads close and don't reopen. He mentioned, apropos of apparently nothing, that the last man who'd taken something from the family had driven a logging truck for eleven years afterward without a single accident, and then retired, and then died peacefully, and wasn't that a mercy, because accidents happen to people who make mistakes.

"You're describing a hostage negotiation," I said.

"I'm making conversation." He signaled the waitress—actually raised two fingers, and she came, and he ordered three coffees without asking anyone—and when she'd gone he leaned back and looked at Maren. "Your mother's asking after you. She's worried. You left no address."

"My mother hasn't asked after me since 1994."

"She's asking now. Grief loosens people." He turned back to me. "You understand why we can't let this go. Three climbers went into that cave in June of 1987. Your father led them out—or led most of them out—and then he disappeared with whatever they found. For thirty years this family has kept a ledger. Names, dates, debts. Your father's page is open. You're holding his pen."

"I'm holding a rock."

"A rock with a name cut into it." His voice stayed level, courteous, almost warm. That was the worst part. There was no mask slipping, no flash of the animal underneath, because there was no underneath. The courtesy went all the way down. "Edmund. You've seen it. You know what I'm talking about."

I did know. I'd spent two nights knowing it, turning the carved stone over in my hands in a motel room while Maren slept badly in the other bed, reading the letters again and again. *E-D-M-U-N-D*, cut deep and clean by a hand I recognized, because I'd watched that hand carve my initials into a doorframe when I was six. My father's mark. On a stranger's name. In a cave where a woman's boot had sat rusting under thirty years of tide.

"He took something," Alder said. "From the source. Something worth three lives, or he believed it was. And then he buried it so deep that even we couldn't find it, and he raised you—" a small pause, surgical, "—at arm's length, moving every two years, teaching you to dig but never to stay. Does any of that sound like an innocent man to you?"

It sounded like my childhood, is what it sounded like. It sounded like every town we'd left before I learned the names of streets, every school I attended for six weeks, every time my father woke at night and checked the locks twice and then stood at the window looking east toward mountains we never visited. I had thought, growing up, that we were running from debt collectors, from the law, from some ordinary shame. I had built whole theories. None of them had included a glacier.

"You want a confession," I said.

"I want the truth. Confessions are just truth with paperwork." He accepted his coffee from the waitress with a nod that somehow made her feel appreciated, which annoyed me on her behalf. "Your father took something from that mountain. We believe you can help us find it. Help us, and the ledger closes. Your name comes off our books. You stop being a line item and go back to being weather."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you stay interesting." He smiled then, fully, and it was a good smile, well-made, and it chilled me to the marrow because it was the smile of a man describing a filing system. "Interesting people require attention. Attention requires travel. Travel requires—" he gestured gently at the diner, the road, the whole gray morning, "—resources. We have resources, Mr. Nakamura. We've had thirty years to accumulate them."

* * *

Maren finally spoke. "You'll get nothing from them through me. I'm not part of your collection."

"You're the only part that ever mattered." For one moment—just one—the courtesy thinned, and I saw something underneath after all. Not menace. Older than menace. Grief, worn smooth as river stone from thirty years of handling. "My uncle Hal went into that cave with your father, Maren. My aunt Petra. Tom Brandt. I was nine years old. I watched my grandmother set three places at every holiday table for a decade because she refused to believe the ice had them. The law called it misadventure. The law closed its file in fourteen months." He picked up his coffee, drank, set it down with a soft click. "So we opened ours. And ours doesn't close until every entry is paid."

"Paid by whom?" Maren's voice cracked on it, just slightly. "By their children? By strangers? Hal is dead, Alder. Petra is dead. Whoever owes you is dead too, and you're shaking down ghosts' kids because the alternative is admitting nobody's coming back."

"The alternative," Alder said quietly, "is forgetting. And this family doesn't forget. It's the only thing we do well."

He rose, buttoned his coat, and laid something on the table between us. A photograph, old, curling at the corners. An expedition group shot, 1987 by the date stamp: five people in matching parkas squinting into sun. Third from the left, younger than I'd ever seen him, thinner, already going gray at the temples—my father. And beside him, laughing, a broad-shouldered man with Maren's exact jaw.

"Hal," Maren whispered.

"They were friends," Alder said. "Did you know that? Your father and my uncle were friends. Which means either your father betrayed him, or something on that mountain killed them all and your father ran. Either way, someone in this photograph knows. And everyone in this photograph is dead except him." He tapped my father's face once, lightly, almost tenderly. "And now there's you."

He walked out. No threats issued, no deadline given, no phone number left. Just the photograph and the check—he paid for all three coffees, including mine, which felt less like generosity than like establishing that even my caffeine was now on his account.

* * *

We sat in silence for a full minute after the bell above the door stopped swinging.

"He followed us from the coast," Maren said finally. "Forty miles. Maybe further. He knew which diner."

"He's known where we are since we left town. Probably since the motel." I turned the photograph over. On the back, in pencil, faded: five names, and next to my father's, a single word in a different hand—*owes*. "That ransacked room. They took the stone."

"They took the stone," Maren agreed, "which means he already has half of what he wants, which means this meeting wasn't about the stone at all."

"It was about me." I set the photo down. "He wanted to look at me. Size me up. Decide whether I'm my father's heir or just his leftover."

"And?"

"And I think I passed, which is the problem." I looked out the window. The parking lot was empty except for our car and, across the road, a gray sedan I was nearly certain hadn't been there when we arrived. "Maren. How many of him are there?"

She didn't answer right away, and the length of her silence was the answer. "The family isn't large anymore," she said at last. "But it doesn't need to be. It has cousins in every county line, men who owe it favors, men it keeps photographs of. Alder travels alone because alone is enough."

"Great. Terrific. One polite apocalypse." I drained my coffee, which had gone cold, which felt appropriate. "Here's what I keep snagging on. He said *the source*. He said my father took something *from the source*. He knows what's up that glacier better than we do. So why does he need us at all?"

Maren stared at the photograph. I watched her arrive at it a half-second before I did—watched it land in her face like cold water.

"Because he can't find it," she said slowly. "Thirty years, and the family's resources, and every cousin in every county—and he can't find it. Because the route only opens when the ice opens, and the ice doesn't open for money." Her eyes came up to mine. "It opens for people who can read ground. People who can find a way up a mountain that doesn't officially exist. Felix—he doesn't want your confession. He wants your hands."

Outside, the gray sedan's engine started—unhurried, confident, patient as water finding a crack.

And behind the diner, past the dumpster and the weeds, I noticed for the first time a drainage ditch running along the property line, carrying snowmelt from the hills—which was normal, except that the water in it was running the wrong way. Uphill. Steady and unmistakable, toward the mountains, as if the land itself had decided whose side it was on.

Maren saw it too. We looked at each other, and neither of us said it out loud, because saying it would make it a plan, and plans get people followed.

But I put the car in gear anyway, and instead of taking the highway north, I turned down the gravel track that ran beside the ditch—uphill, against every rule water has ever obeyed—toward the place the river came from.
