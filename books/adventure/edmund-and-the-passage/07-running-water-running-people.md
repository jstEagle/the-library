## 7. Running Water, Running People

Alder Voss knocked the way a landslide arrives—politely, and with total certainty that you were already buried.

We'd been in the back booth of the Anchor Line for forty minutes when he came through the door. I knew him before Maren did, or maybe she'd known him all her life and had simply gotten very good at not looking at him. Big man, weathered like driftwood, wearing a canvas jacket two sizes too respectable for the way his eyes moved around the room. Counting exits. Counting us.

"Back way," Maren said without moving her lips much. "Through the kitchen."

"Is there a back way?"

"There's always a back way. I've spent thirty years making sure of it."

She stood first, casual as a woman going to pay, and I followed her through the swinging door into a kitchen that smelled of fryer grease and old decisions. The cook didn't look up. Either Maren had paid him to be blind or thirty years of practice had made blindness a town-wide skill.

The alley behind the Anchor Line was narrow, cobbled, and ran uphill—which struck me as wrong even then, though I couldn't have said why yet. Towns are built downhill. Water runs down them. That's the whole arrangement, the oldest contract humans ever signed: you build where gravity does your hauling. This alley disagreed with the contract.

Maren went up it anyway, fast, and I went after her.

"He'll check the harbor road first," she said. "Then the bus stop. Then the church, because Alder believes people hide in churches. He's wrong about that more often than you'd think."

"You know him well."

"I grew up with him." She said it the way you'd say *I grew up with a dog that bites*. "He was kind once. Before the family gave him a job instead of a soul."

We came out above the fish sheds, on a shelf of ground where the town's oldest graves leaned seaward like passengers on a listing ship. Below us the harbor sat empty under a sky the color of dishwater. The tide was coming back in. Somewhere beneath the headland, the cave I'd found two nights ago was swallowing itself again, taking its rusted lantern and its woman's boot and its carved stone with my father's mark on it—the stone somebody had already stolen from my room, which meant somebody else wanted my father's secrets even more than I did, and had better funding.

And below us, faintly, I heard water.

Not the sea. Sea has a voice like a crowd. This was singular, insistent, a thread of sound running under everything else, and it was coming from uphill.

"Maren," I said. "Where's the stream?"

"What stream?"

"The one from the cave. It comes out of the headland, runs across the shingle—" I pointed down toward the beach, where I'd watched it for an hour that morning while pretending to dig midden shells for a man who paid by the bucket. Except the channel down there was dry. Bone-dry, cracked mud, a week's worth of sun baked into it. "It should be right there."

"It dries out," Maren said. "Hasn't run since the tide dropped. Everyone says the cave drank it."

"Caves don't drink." I was already climbing, following the sound, over a wall and through gorse that fought back. "Water doesn't just stop. It goes somewhere. It's still going somewhere—it's going *up*."

She caught up with me at the old mill leat, a stone channel cut into the hillside two centuries ago and abandoned since, and we both stopped, because the leat was running. Full. Clear water moving fast along a channel that every map in the county marked as dry, flowing steadily inland, away from the sea, gaining elevation with every yard.

"That's impossible," Maren said.

"I know." My heart was doing something complicated. Half of it was fear—the good kind, the kind that keeps you alive—and half was the other thing, the thing that had put me in caves and middens and trouble my whole life. "Isn't it wonderful?"

"You're a strange person, Felix Nakamura."

"My father used to say the same thing. Usually right before he abandoned me somewhere."

I knelt at the leat and put my hand in the water. Cold enough to hurt. Snowmelt cold, glacier cold, a temperature that had no business existing at sea level in late summer. And the current—here's what made my skin crawl, here's what I'd carry up the whole mountain later like a stone in my boot—the current was steady. No pulse of rain, no surge of tide. Just a constant, patient push, like something enormous breathing very slowly far upstream.

"It's coming from the glacier," I said. "Whatever route it takes, it starts up there. On the Vinter."

"Nobody's walked that route in decades. The ice opens for a few weeks, maybe, some years. My father tried in '87." Her jaw did something tight. "Three climbers went in. Zero came out. And now Alder wants the map they died carrying, and he thinks you're the key to it because of whose name you wear."

"I didn't ask for the name."

"Neither did I ask for mine. We're both carrying things we didn't pack." She looked up the hill, at the impossible water. "So. Do we follow it?"

* * *

Here is what I will say about running from Alder Voss: he makes it easy, in one sense, because he moves like a man who has never doubted anything, and doubt is the fastest thing on a mountain. He would go to the harbor road first, exactly as Maren predicted. He would waste an hour being certain.

We spent that hour becoming uncertain together, which is how trust gets built between people who don't believe in it.

We went up through the town's spine—a street so steep the houses on it had stairs instead of gardens—and past the last of the rooftops into bracken and sheep-cropped turf. The leat climbed with us. Sometimes it dove under a wall and came out the other side; sometimes it vanished into the ground entirely and we tracked it by sound, by the wet green line of grass that followed it like a rumor follows a liar.

An hour in, we crossed the coast road. The actual, official, map-printed coast road, the one that runs along the shore below the town. We were a hundred feet above it and half a mile inland, and the stream was there too, running under the road through a culvert, still going up.

"Felix." Maren had gone very still. "How long have we been walking?"

"Since the Anchor Line. Ninety minutes, maybe."

"And we're inland. Uphill. Away from the sea." She turned a slow circle. "So why can I smell salt?"

She was right. The wind had shifted and it carried the sea with it—but underneath the salt was another smell, older, mineral, the smell of a deep cellar or a fresh-cut quarry. Stone and cold and time. The land was exhaling something it had been holding since the ice age.

We followed the culvert to where it emerged from a seam in the hillside, a black slot in the rock no wider than a coffin, and the water came out of it fast and flat and utterly silent in a way moving water should never be silent. No babble. No argument with the stones. It flowed like it was keeping a secret.

I've dug on five continents. I've pulled Roman coins out of English mud and mummy beads out of Egyptian sand, and I thought I understood what ground was: ground is honest. Ground is the one witness that never lies, because it doesn't know how. Every layer tells you exactly what happened to it, if you speak the language.

That seam was lying. Or rather, the land around it was lying—wearing a costume of ordinary hillside over whatever was underneath, the way Alder wore a respectable jacket over whatever he was.

"The maps are wrong," Maren said. "All of them. My father's letter said the river runs from the glacier to the sea, but it never said—" She stopped. Started again. "It never said the town was sitting on top of it."

"Or the town knows," I said. "And decided not to."

Think about it. A fishing town, three hundred years old, built on a coast with one safe harbor. Somebody, sometime, dug a well and hit a stream that ran the wrong way. Somebody noticed the mill leat filling from the uphill end. Somebody heard water under the church floor during a drought. And the town did what towns do with things that don't fit the ledger: they wrote it down sideways, in legend. *The cave takes payment.* *Don't follow the water.* A warning dressed as a ghost story, handed down until nobody remembered it was survey data.

"They knew," Maren said quietly. "My grandmother used to say the hill was hollow and the hollow wasn't empty. I thought it was a story to keep children out of the mines."

"Most warnings start as field notes."

We stood there a long moment, listening to the impossible water. Then, behind and below us, an engine. Not many roads led up here, and only one vehicle would be on this one at this hour, moving with that particular unhurried confidence.

Alder.

* * *

We ran.

Not bravely—I want to be honest about this. There was nothing cinematic in it. We crashed through bracken with our lungs full of knives, and Maren knew this country the way I know soil, calling turns before I saw them: *left, wall, drop, duck*. Twice she took routes that looked like suicide and turned out to be sheep tracks, and twice I trusted her with my ankles and my life in the same step, which is either the beginning of partnership or the end of good judgment, and possibly those are the same thing.

The stream stayed with us. That was the strange mercy of it. However the water threaded through the hill, it kept surfacing beside our route—seeping from a bank, chuckling under a slab, welling up in a bog that steamed cold in the morning air—and every emergence told us the same thing: *this way, this way, deeper in, higher up.*

"It's a guide," I panted, at one point, bent double against a boulder.

"It's a coincidence."

"Nothing about this water is a coincidence. It's been running this route since before there were people to walk it. If it leads to the glacier, it leads to the source, and if it leads to the source—" I straightened up. My legs were shaking and my mind was suddenly, terribly clear. "Maren. Your father didn't find the route. He *followed the river*. Same as us. The whole expedition was a tracing operation."

Her face did something I hadn't seen it do before—grief and hope fighting for the same territory. "His last letter said the water was the door. I thought he meant the cave."

"He meant all of it. The cave is just the front entrance. The river is the passage, top to bottom, sea to ice." I looked up the valley, where the hills folded higher and greyer, and somewhere beyond them, invisible, the Vinter Glacier sat in its hanging country like a held breath. "And Alder knows it too. That's why he wants the map. But he doesn't need the map. He needs the river, and the river is right here, and it's been here the whole time, waiting for someone stubborn enough to walk upstream."

Behind us, faint but gaining, an engine changed gears.

We ran again.

* * *

By midday we'd left the roads behind entirely, and the land had stopped pretending to be ordinary.

The valley narrowed into a gorge whose walls sweated cold air. The stream ran beside us now openly, a proper river, knee-deep and glass-clear, and it did something in the narrows that I will describe as accurately as I can and let you decide whether to believe me: it stepped *up*. A series of low falls, each one a foot, maybe eighteen inches, water climbing stone shelves like a stairway, smooth and unhurried, no spray, no turbulence. Physics filed a complaint and was ignored.

Maren stood watching it with her arms wrapped around herself. "Tell me you're seeing this."

"I'm seeing it."

"Tell me it makes sense."

"Not even slightly." I crouched and touched the water again. Still snowmelt cold. Still patient. "But I've read enough of my father's notes to know he saw this too. He drew it. There was a sketch in his office years ago—water going upstairs, he called it. I thought it was a joke. He never joked."

"Then he knew. In '87. He knew the river ran wrong, and he went up it anyway, and he took my father with him." Maren's voice had gone flat, which I was learning was worse than loud. "And only the river came back."

The gorge climbed all afternoon. Our legs turned to ledger entries in red ink. We ate cold food standing up, drank from the river because thirst outranks caution, and kept one ear cocked down-valley for engines, though the terrain had grown too steep for any vehicle Alder owned. He'd come on foot eventually. Men like Alder don't quit; they convert obstacles into schedules.

Near dusk, the gorge opened into a bowl of moorland, and there—for the second time that day, and worse than the first time—we found the river running uphill.

It emerged from a spring at the bowl's high edge. Not seeped. *Emerged*, with force, a full-grown river born from a hole in the hillside at the top of its own valley, pouring downhill toward the sea we'd left that morning. Which meant the water we'd followed all day, the water in the leat and the culvert and the stepping falls, had somehow traveled from this spring *down* to the town, and also traveled *up* from the town to this spring, and both journeys were true, and the landscape was asking us to hold two contradictory facts in our heads at once and smile about it.

"It's a loop," I said slowly. "Or it looks like one. Downhill one way, uphill the other, depending on which mouth you're standing at." I laughed, and it came out ragged. "The land isn't broken, Maren. It's *layered*. There's a route underneath that doesn't obey the surface rules—some plumbing through the rock, maybe through the ice itself, and the water takes whichever path suits it. The surface is the lie. The underground is the truth."

"Like the family," Maren said. "Respectable on top. Running the wrong direction underneath."

"Everything honest is like that, if you dig deep enough."

We made camp in the lee of a boulder, cold-sited, no fire, sharing a blanket because neither of us had packed for a war. The stars came out hard and close. Below us, miles back, the town showed a scatter of lights, small and warm and built on top of a secret. Somewhere between us and those lights, a big man in a respectable jacket was walking uphill in the dark, certain of everything.

Maren spoke into the dark. "My father's letter said: *the water remembers the way even when men forget it.* I thought it was poetry."

"It's directions."

She almost smiled. In the starlight it looked like a crack in old ice. "Felix. When we find what's up there—if we find it—whatever my father did, whatever yours did, whatever the family's owed... do you actually think we get to walk away with it?"

"No," I said. "But I think we get to *know*. And knowing is the only inheritance I've ever wanted that didn't come with conditions."

She was quiet a long time. Then: "Sleep. Tomorrow we climb."

I lay awake longer than she did, listening to the river. All night it ran past our camp, steady and cold and patient, going wherever it had decided to go, obeying no law I'd been taught, and I thought: *my father listened to this same water, thirty-eight years ago, and it told him something that cost him his name, his career, and me.*

Tomorrow we'd climb toward the glacier, following a river that lied about direction, chased by a man who believed in verdicts, carrying a debt neither of us had signed for.

The water, at least, knew the way.

Whether we'd survive learning it—that was tomorrow's admission fee.
