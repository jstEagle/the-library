## 8. The Digger's Hands

I have spent thirty years learning to distrust competence. It's the currency of liars—everyone who ever lied to my family was good at something, and they led with it like a lantern. So when Felix Nakamura knelt at the mouth of that drainage tunnel under the old cannery road and put two fingers to the soil the way a doctor takes a pulse, I stood back and waited for the trick.

There wasn't one. That was the problem.

"Clay's holding water," Felix said. "Which means the stream we're following isn't just passing through here. It's being fed from somewhere above us." A pause. "Above us being a relative term, given what this river's been doing all morning."

The river had been running uphill. I want to write that sentence again because even now, a day later, my hand wants to soften it, to make it reasonable. The cave stream—the one that vanishes beneath the headland at low tide, the one my father wrote about in a letter that read more like a confession than a postcard—comes out of the ground again four kilometers inland and forty meters higher than where it goes in. Water does not do that. Water has rules. And yet there it was on our second morning of walking, seeping out of a seam in a hillside like the land had simply forgotten which direction down was.

Felix hadn't flinched. I had. I'll admit that here, in the privacy of my own skull, where no one can bill me for it.

* * *

We'd been moving since before dawn, putting distance between ourselves and Alder Voss, who had found us in a diner with the unhurried confidence of a man who has never once been asked to leave anywhere. My cousin. I keep having to re-learn the word every time I say it. When we were children he held my hand crossing the ice on the mill pond, and now he carries a family ledger and a length of climbing rope and calls what he does justice, and some part of me—the part that was eight years old—is still waiting for him to let go of the rope and explain himself.

Felix didn't want to run inland. Felix wanted to run anywhere, the way water wants downhill, instantly and without sentiment. But the stream was the only thread connecting the cave to whatever my father had been chasing up on the Vinter Glacier, and I said so, and Felix looked at me for a long moment with an expression I've decided to call *audit*.

"You're asking me to follow a river that runs wrong," Felix said.

"I'm asking you to follow it until it stops lying."

And Felix—who by every account I'd collected over three decades of digging through other people's grief was a person who could not stay in one room longer than a night—had shrugged and picked up the pack.

So that's how we came to be crouched in the root cellar of a collapsed homestead forty meters above the sea, watching water seep uphill out of the earth, while somewhere behind us a man with my surname walked our trail with a map he'd tried to take off my father's corpse thirty years ago.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Felix Nakamura before yesterday, gathered the way I gather everything—slowly, obsessively, from people who mostly wanted me to stop asking:

That Edmund Nakamura's child had been raised on digs, sleeping in truck beds and site tents from age six. That the child could read a soil profile at twelve the way other children read comics. That when Edmund Nakamura's career burned—after 1987, after the expedition, after three climbers went into a cave and only his reputation came out—the child kept digging anyway, under other people's names, on other people's crews, always the best hands on any site and always gone before the season report was filed. That there were rumors. Rumors that the child had once braced a collapsing trench in the Atacama with a jack handle and their own back for eleven minutes while two colleagues crawled out. Rumors that the child out-dug the father, and hated hearing it, and hated more that it was true.

Rumors are cheap. I've bought a lot of them over the years and most turned out to be counterfeit. So I watched.

I watched Felix walk the slope above the seep without appearing to look at it, eyes down, reading the ground the way I read a room—which is to say, constantly, involuntarily, for exits and for what people were hiding. Every ten paces Felix would stop, press a palm flat to the earth, and move on. I counted. Eleven times in a quarter kilometer.

"What are you doing?" I finally asked.

"Listening."

"To dirt."

"To dirt," Felix agreed, not offended, which somehow made it worse. "You spend enough years getting paid by the cubic meter, you learn the ground talks. Compaction, moisture, layering—it's all speech. This hillside is saying something very strange." Felix crouched, scraped away the moss with a thumb, and showed me a cross-section the size of a saucer: dark organic mat, then a band of gray clay, then—impossibly—a lens of clean glacier silt, the kind you find under a hundred meters of ice. "This is till. It doesn't belong here. It belongs up on the Vinter, under the tongue." Felix looked up at me, and for the first time since I'd known them—and I'd known them exactly two days—I saw something that wasn't wariness or audit or the flat professional calm of a person perpetually ready to leave. It was interest. Real, hungry, unguarded interest. "Maren. The glacier comes to us. Or its insides do. There's a conduit under this whole valley and your father's river runs through it."

I felt that in my sternum. Thirty years I'd chased the legend from the outside—interviews, archives, the family's sealed records, my mother's silence, my father's last letter with its single maddening line: *the source remembers what we did.* I'd treated the route as a place. Felix was telling me it was an organ. The mountain had a throat, and the cave at low tide was its mouth, and somewhere upstream of everything was the thing it had swallowed.

"That's why the tide matters," I said slowly. "That's why the cave 'takes payment.' It's not a cave. It's—"

"A drain. With a tide gate." Felix stood, brushing off knees. "Your drowned climbers didn't get lost in a sea cave. They got pulled into plumbing."

I sat down on a fallen beam and didn't say anything for a while, because if I opened my mouth I was going to say my father's name, and I have a rule about saying his name in front of strangers, which is that I don't, because the sound of it does things to my face I don't authorize.

Felix pretended to study the seep. It was a kindness, and I resented it, and I was grateful for it, in roughly equal measure.

* * *

The collapse happened at four in the afternoon, and I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only inheritance my father left me that I actually want.

We'd followed the seep upstream—or what passed for upstream, the water's logic being purely decorative—to where the hillside pinched between two rock ribs, and there the till layer surfaced in a cut bank maybe three meters high, riddled with old roots. The stream ran along its base in a channel the width of a coffin. Felix wanted to see the cut face. I wanted to camp; it was getting toward the gray hour when the light goes soft and dishonest. Felix won, because Felix had already started walking, and I've learned that arguing with Nakamura mid-stride is like arguing with weather.

"Watch the toe," Felix said, meaning the base of the bank. "It's undercut. See how the water's chewed it hollow? Whole face is standing on nothing but habit."

I saw it. I saw it and I stepped closer anyway, because directly below the cut face, half-buried in the stream gravel, was a shape I recognized. Metal. Green-painted, corroded, stenciled. An expedition crate. 1987-issue, the same stencil pattern as the crates in the photographs from the basecamp, the ones I'd memorized so thoroughly I could draw them blind.

"Felix." My voice did something embarrassing. "Look."

Felix looked. And then Felix looked up—at the cut face, at the roots, at the gray clay sagging in a way clay should not sag—and grabbed my arm and said, very calmly, the way people talk when they are already moving, "Back. Now. Don't run, run is how the ground wins."

I ran. Of course I ran. The ground won anyway, partially.

The face let go with a sound I will hear for the rest of my life—a deep wet exhalation, like the hillside had been holding its breath since 1987 and chose that moment to give up. Two tons of clay and root and glacier silt came down in a slab, and I was clear of it, mostly, except my left leg wasn't, pinned to the knee under the edge of the slab where it had slumped into the stream channel, and the water that ran uphill was suddenly running over me, cold enough to make my thoughts go white.

And Felix—Felix, who had been three steps ahead, who was *clear*, who by every law of self-preservation I've ever observed should have used those three steps to become a rumor in the opposite direction—came back.

Not heroically. That's the detail I keep returning to, the one that undid me. No dive, no shout. Felix came back the way a person returns to a job site: assessing, already working. Dropped to knees in the floodwater beside me. Put both palms flat on the slab.

"It's resting on you, not crushing," Felix said. "Good news. Bad news is it's resting on you in a stream bed, and this water's still rising from somewhere." A glance upstream, quick, professional. "It pulses. Tide-linked, probably, even this far in. We have maybe twenty minutes before this channel fills to your chin."

"My leg—"

"Is attached and complaining, which is what legs should do." Felix was already moving along the slab's edge, reading it. "I need a lever. Something long and something that won't snap." Eyes sweeping the bank debris. "Root ball, far side—that birch trunk. Help me or don't, but don't get under anything."

I helped. I dragged myself around on my elbows through freezing water with my leg pinned like a bad joke, and together we wrestled the dead birch free, and Felix wedged it under the slab's lip with a precision that made my chest hurt—found the fulcrum point by touch, tested it twice, shifted it two centimeters, tested again.

"When I lift, you pull straight out. Not sideways. Straight, along your leg, like pulling a boot off." Felix looked at me then, and what was in that face wasn't bravery. It was concentration, total and unglamorous, the face of someone doing arithmetic with tons and minutes. "Maren. Listen. If it drops again, I'm going to shove you clear and it's going to take my hands. That's the deal. Say yes to the deal."

"Why would you—" 

"The deal, Maren."

"Yes. Fine. Yes."

"On three. One—"

Felix lifted on two. I learned later this is deliberate—diggers count past the number so the puller moves early, before the load fully rises, because the load never stays risen long. My father taught crews that trick. I know because I found it in his field notes. I did not tell Felix this. Some inheritances you keep in your pocket like stones.

The slab rose maybe fifteen centimeters. It screamed along my leg. I pulled, straight, along the bone, the way I was told, and the world went red at the edges, and then I was out, on the gravel bar, and the birch was bending, and Felix rode the trunk down as the slab dropped—rode it, controlled it, turned two tons of falling earth into a slow settling thump instead of a kill—and then there was silence except the pulse of the impossible water, and Felix sitting in the shallows flexing both hands, one finger at a time, checking each like a pianist.

Neither of us spoke for a full minute.

Then Felix said, "Your boot's ruined," and started laughing—not much, just a breath or two, exhausted and involuntary—and I found I was laughing too, or making a sound adjacent to laughing, sprawled on a gravel bar with a crushed-booted leg and a dead birch for a lever, four kilometers inside a geological lie, hunted by my own cousin.

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of the cut bank, above the high-water line, which Felix established by reading the moss stains and gravel sorting rather than by guessing. While I taped my ankle—bruised deep, not broken; Felix checked it with hands that were gentler than the voice directing them—Felix built a fire small enough to hide in a hat and cooked rice with dried fish in a dented pot, and I watched, and the watching turned into something I hadn't planned.

Because here is the thing. All those rumors, all those crew stories I'd collected like evidence—and I had thought of them as evidence, evidence of what Edmund Nakamura's blood was worth, whether the child carried anything of the man who'd led my father into that cave. I'd wanted Felix to be either worthless or guilty, because worthless meant useless and guilt meant leverage, and leverage was the only language my family had ever taught me to speak fluently.

What Felix actually was, kneeling in firelight checking a tarp seam twice because a wet night kills as surely as a fall, was better than the father. Not in talent—in *care*. Edmund Nakamura, by every account, dug brilliantly and abandoned completely: sites, sponsors, wives, children. Felix dug the same way and stayed for the aftermath. Braced the trench. Counted past three. Came back down the slope.

"The Atacama," I said, before I could stop myself. "Eleven minutes. Under the trench wall. Is it true?"

Felix's hands went still on the pot lid. For a moment I thought I'd crossed some line and the shutters would come down and tomorrow I'd wake to an empty bedroll and a debt unpaid. Then Felix said, quietly, "Nine minutes. People round up." A pause. "You've been asking about me."

"Since before I met you. Since the stone turned up in the cave with your father's mark on it. You're the only living thread to 1987, Felix. Of course I've been asking."

"And what did you conclude?" Not hostile. Curious, almost clinical, the way Felix had listened to the dirt. Like my answer was another soil layer to be read.

I looked at the fire. I am not good at this part. My family does confession like surgery—rarely, under anesthesia, with everyone pretending it isn't happening. But the slab had nearly taken my leg that afternoon, and Alder was a day behind us, and the water ran uphill, and it seemed suddenly, absurdly important that one true thing be said aloud in that valley.

"I concluded I needed the map you're carrying," I said. "That's what I told myself. Your father's mark on that stone is the closest anyone's come to the source in thirty years, and you're his blood, and blood was the only key the family ever respected." I made myself look up. "But tonight I watched you read a hillside, and brace a collapse, and come back down a slope you'd already cleared, and I concluded the rumors were true. The child out-dug the father. And I need you, Felix. Not the map. Not the name. You. The hands. Because whatever's up under that glacier, the route to it is going to be exactly like that cut bank—standing on nothing but habit—and I can't read ground. I read paper. Paper's all I've had for thirty years."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, the impossible water pulsed against the silence, patient as a creditor.

Felix was quiet a long time. Long enough that I started composing retractions, filing them for morning.

"Your father's letter," Felix said at last. "You said it named mine. What did it say? Exactly."

"*The source remembers what we did,*" I quoted. "*And the boy must never dig it up.*"

Something moved across Felix's face—there and gone, fast, like a fish under ice. "The boy," Felix repeated. "He called me the boy. In a letter he wrote to a stranger." A breath that was almost a laugh. "He never called me anything. To my face it was always *pass me the trowel*, or nothing at all." Felix fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "All right, Maren. Here's my terms, since we're doing honesty at altitude. I'm not doing this for your father's memory or my father's sins. I'm doing it because a river runs uphill and I need to know why more than I need to sleep, and because your cousin put his hands on my pack and I don't forgive that. And because—" the stick paused midair— "because nine minutes under a trench wall teaches you that the ground always collects eventually, and I'd rather meet this particular bill standing up. Those are my terms."

"And mine," I said, "is that when we find what the source remembers, we remember it *together*. Whatever it is. Whoever it damns."

Felix looked at me across the small hidden fire, and nodded once, and that nod weighed more than the slab had.

We slept in shifts. On my watch, near dawn, I heard Felix talking in their sleep—just a word or two, worn smooth by repetition, the way a coin wears in a pocket. I leaned closer, and the word was *Edmund*, and it took me a shameful minute to realize Felix wasn't calling for their father.

They were answering for him.

When the light came up gray and thin, Felix shook my shoulder and pointed downstream, back the way we'd come, toward the coast and the town and everything we were fleeing. On the mud of the stream bank, pressed neat and deliberate into the silt where no print had been the evening before, was a single boot heel. Fresh. Facing uphill.

Alder wasn't following our trail anymore. He was ahead of us, walking toward the source, and he'd stopped just long enough to let us know.
