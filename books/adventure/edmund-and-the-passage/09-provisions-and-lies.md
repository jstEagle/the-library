## 9. Provisions and Lies

The town was called Hessel, which is the kind of name a place gets when the people who named it have all left and nobody wants to spend money on a new sign. One main street, two bars that shared a clientele, a general store whose owner had clearly decided sometime in the nineties that expansion was for optimists. A feed store, a hardware store that was also a bait shop, and a church with a steeple leaning like it was eavesdropping on the bar next door.

We came in on foot along the river road, because Maren had opinions about vehicles.

"Anything with a registration is a leash," she said. "You want to know how Alder finds people? He doesn't chase them. He waits at the end of their paperwork."

"So we walk into a supply town carrying everything we own on our backs like a couple of nineteenth-century prospectors."

"Nineteenth-century prospectors died of dysentery. We'll die of something more modern."

It was hard to argue with her planning and harder to argue with her tone, so I didn't. We'd been walking since before dawn, following the wrong-running stream as far as it stayed visible, until it ducked under a limestone shelf and vanished toward wherever it went to practice being impossible. After that it was just country: alder scrub, old logging cuts healing over with fir, the glacier a white rumor north of everything, visible whenever the trees allowed.

My shoulders had opinions too. They were filing them in triplicate.

* * *

The general store smelled of rope, diesel, and penny candy that had outlived several owners. The man behind the counter was maybe sixty, built like a rain barrel, with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair and the particular stillness of someone who watches a lot of people come through and files most of them under *none of my business*.

Maren did the talking. She was better at it than me—not smoother, but more honest-looking. I've been told I have a face that makes people check their wallets. She had a face that made people want to tell her their troubles, which in my experience is far more dangerous.

"We need rope," she said. "Static line, sixty meters if you have it. Carabiners. Two headlamps, spare batteries. Stove fuel. And whatever food keeps without refrigeration that isn't a war crime against the palate."

The man looked at us the way you look at weather coming over a ridge. "Glacier?" he said.

"You could say that."

"Hm." He started pulling gear without further questions, which I appreciated right up until he paused over the rope and said, conversationally, "You'll want to be careful up there. Route's not open long. And there's been talk."

"There's always talk," Maren said.

"Not always this kind." He set the rope down and looked at us properly now, over the counter, with the mild eyes of a man who has decided our business has become his business whether we liked it or not. "Fella came through yesterday. Well-dressed for these parts. Polite. Bought coffee he didn't drink and showed around a photograph."

I felt it before I understood it—that cold drop in the stomach, the body knowing before the brain does. Somewhere behind me a screen door banged and I flinched hard enough that the rope slid an inch off the counter.

"Photograph of what?" Maren asked. Her voice was level. Mine wouldn't have been.

"Two people." The storekeeper's gaze moved from her to me and back, doing arithmetic. "A woman about your age. And a younger one—dark-haired, thin. Said they'd taken things from his family. Valuable things. Said if anyone saw them, his family would handle it fair, no need to trouble the sheriff, who—" and here the man's mouth did something dry, "—as everyone knows, hasn't solved anything in this county since the Ford administration."

The bell above the door jingled and I nearly went through the back wall. It was only a kid, maybe ten, coming in for bait worms, who took one look at the atmosphere and developed a sudden fascination with the candy rack.

"How much for the gear," Maren said.

* * *

We paid cash. Of course we paid cash; we had almost nothing else. The storekeeper counted it twice, gave us a canvas pack at a discount he didn't explain, and then, as we were loading up, said quietly, without looking at either of us:

"Back room's got a cot. Ten dollars. Nobody asks about the back room."

Maren glanced at him. "Why?"

"Because my brother disappeared in '87," he said, "and whoever finds out what happened to him can rob my store blind for all I care." Then he turned to the register and began squaring the bills, and that was the whole conversation.

* * *

The back room was four feet wider than the cot and smelled of mouse and motor oil. Maren sat on the floor with her back against the wall and the map across her knees, and I stood at the window with the shade pulled to a two-inch gap, watching the street, which is a thing I do. Watching streets. It's not paranoia if the story they're telling about you is true in every detail except the ones that matter.

"They're calling us thieves," I said.

"Alder's calling us thieves."

"He bought coffee he didn't drink." I kept my eye at the gap. "That's the part that gets me. The theater of it. He wants people to remember him as patient. Reasonable. The reasonable man with the photograph."

"It's smart," Maren said, and there was something bitter in how she said it, the bitterness of a person conceding a point to family. "No warrant. No police report. No paper trail anyone could audit later. Just a story, told storefront to storefront, faster than any document could travel. By the time anyone official got involved—if they ever did—the whole county would already know what happened: two thieves got what was coming to them. Everyone would nod. Everyone would help."

"Make the law irrelevant," I said. "Don't beat the police. Just make them unnecessary."

She looked up at me. "You sound like you've seen this before."

"I grew up with a father who taught me that the most dangerous thing in any dig site isn't the trench wall. It's the story people tell about why you're there." I let the shade snap shut. "Alder's not hunting us, Maren. He's *editing* us. We're a draft, and he's cutting the parts where we're people."

* * *

Here's what I knew about being hunted by narrative, and I knew it young.

When I was eleven, my father lost a university post over something he never explained, and after that we moved the way weather moves—through towns, through other people's excavations, through names that weren't quite ours. And everywhere we went, sooner or later, someone would look at my father a half-second too long, and I learned to read that half-second the way other kids learn to read traffic. It wasn't fear exactly. It was the moment a stranger's existing story about you gets confirmed by your face, and you can see the confirmation land.

My father's answer was motion. Keep ahead of the story. Never let it catch up, never let it settle, never give it a fixed address. I inherited the reflex without inheriting the reason. For twenty years I've been running from a sentence I couldn't even hear.

Now I could hear it. *Thieves. Wanted by the family.* Four words, and suddenly every stranger on this street was either a witness or a deputy, and none of them had been sworn in, and none of them could be appealed to, because there was nothing to appeal *to*. You can't surrender to a rumor. You can't hire a lawyer against a photograph.

And the truly elegant part—the part I had to admire even while my hands shook—was that Alder hadn't lied, not exactly. We *had* taken something. The stone. My father's mark, carved in rock, lifted out of a cave that local legend says charges admission. If Alder believed the stone was valuable—and he clearly did—then his story was true the way a knife is true. All it needed was a hand behind it.

* * *

Night came down on Hessel like a lid. Through my two-inch gap I watched the street empty, watched the bar across the way fill, watched one man in a good coat walk the length of the main street twice, unhurried, looking at nothing, which is how professionals look at everything.

"That's not Alder," Maren murmured beside me. She'd come to the window without my noticing, which should have frightened me more than it did. "Too heavy in the shoulders. That's one of the cousins. Torben, probably. Alder uses him for the walking-around work."

"You know his walkers by their shoulders?"

"I know my family." She said it flatly, without self-pity, the way you'd say *I know my weather*. "Torben won't act alone. He's a spotter. His job is to confirm we're here and then go find Alder, which means we have tonight, maybe tomorrow morning, before this town becomes a closed circuit."

"Then why are we still in it?"

"Because of what the storekeeper said." She turned from the window, and in the dark her face was mostly voice. "'His family will handle it fair.' That's the phrase. That's the creed. Fair handling. Trials on kitchen tables. Sentences served in silence." Her jaw worked. "My father wrote about it in his last letter. He was afraid of it. Not of dying—of being *handled*. Of becoming a closed file in somebody's ledger instead of a question somebody answers."

"And going to the sheriff changes that how? Half this county thinks the law's a punchline, and the other half owes the Voss family money or favors or both."

"It doesn't change it. But it puts a mark on the record. Even a useless record is a record. If we vanish and there's no complaint filed, we were never here. If there's a complaint, then someday—maybe years from now—someone reads it and starts asking why nobody investigated." She shrugged. "It's not much. It's a thread left hanging out of the seam."

I thought about my father again, all those towns, all those clean exits with nothing left behind. No threads. No seams. Nothing for anyone to pull. And how completely alone that had left both of us.

"Okay," I said. "But we file it and leave tonight. Torben's already walked the street twice. Third pass is when he stops pretending."

* * *

We bought the rest of what we needed in the last hour of light, splitting up the list between three different shops so no single purchase told the whole story. Rope and fuel at the general store, done. Food from the little market run by a woman who talked the entire time about her son's hockey season and required only occasional nods. Boots—I'd been walking in boots that had given notice weeks ago—from the hardware-bait establishment, where the proprietor sold me wool socks and unsolicited advice about the gorge trail north, which he delivered while gutting nothing, since it was the wrong season for fish and the right season for gossip.

"Going up toward the Vinter?" he asked, ringing the boots.

"Just passing through."

"Mm." He handed me change. "Funny. Fella yesterday asked the same route. Same answer, too." He smiled with genuine warmth, which somehow made it worse. "Small county. Everybody passes through the same places. Like water."

I thanked him and got out before my face could testify against me.

By full dark we had everything: rope, hardware, fuel, food packed in wax and foil, the map in its oilskin case riding against Maren's ribs where she'd carried it for thirty years and would carry it into the ground if it came to that. The cot in the back room cost ten dollars and we used it for forty minutes, lying down fully clothed in the dark, listening to the building tick and settle, neither of us sleeping, both of us pretending to so the other could.

At eleven we went out the back, over a fence, through a church parking lot where the leaning steeple finally lost its argument with gravity's patience, and onto the river road heading north.

Behind us, Hessel glowed its few sodium lights. Somewhere in it, Torben was confirming, and somewhere beyond him Alder was receiving, and somewhere in front of us the gorge waited with its wrecked camp and its thirty-year-old questions.

"Felix," Maren said, after a mile of dark.

"Yeah."

"The photograph. What did he tell them we stole?"

I'd been turning it over the whole walk. "He didn't say. That's the trick. Leave it vague and everyone fills in the blank with whatever scares them most. Cash. Jewelry. Something from their own house." I kicked a stone off the road shoulder and listened to it clatter down into the dark. "But here's what I keep snagging on. Alder knows what we actually have. A rock with a carving and a map older than both of us. Neither one's worth this operation. So either he thinks we know where something *is*—"

"—or he thinks we know what something *means*," Maren finished.

Ahead of us the road bent north, and above the black line of the ridges the glacier held the last of the light like a coal under ash. Somewhere up there was a basecamp with justice-marks cut into its posts, a journal with pages torn out, and a river running the wrong direction toward a source that opened for a few weeks a decade and charged admission in the only currency it respected.

We walked. Behind us, faint and getting fainter, I could almost hear the story traveling storefront to storefront, gaining weight with every telling, hunting us faster than any man could.

And the worst part—the part I didn't say out loud—was that by morning, some version of it would be true. Because I had taken the stone. And I intended to keep taking it, all the way to the ice.
