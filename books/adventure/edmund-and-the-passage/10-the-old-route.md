## 10. The Old Route

My father's letter described the trailhead the way he described everything—precisely, and as if precision were a form of apology. *Follow the gorge road past the last fence post. Where the alders give up, the river narrows. The glacier's tongue comes down to meet it like a dog that has learned to wait.* I had read that sentence so many times over thirty years that I could hear his voice in it, and for most of those years I had hated him a little for writing so beautifully about a place where he stopped existing.

Now Felix and I stood at the last fence post, and the alders were giving up exactly on schedule.

"You weren't kidding," Felix said. "He wrote you directions."

"He wrote me a will with scenery in it." I shouldered my pack. "There's a difference. Directions assume you're coming back."

Felix didn't answer that. They had a way of not answering things—a professional silence, like a doctor declining to comment on your mole. Three days out of town, three days of Alder Voss somewhere behind us like a bill we hadn't opened yet, and Felix still moved like someone who expected every campsite to be temporary. They read ground the way I'd seen no one else read it, crouching at the trailhead to press two fingers into the dirt, then standing with an expression I was beginning to recognize as their version of a weather report.

"Hard frost two nights ago," they said. "Thaw since. Nobody's come through here in a week."

"Nobody but us."

"Nobody but us." They said it the way other people say *here we go*, which is to say without enthusiasm and with total commitment.

The gorge took us in by degrees. First the walls were gentle, grassed over, dotted with the kind of boulders that look decorative until you try to walk past them. Then the grass thinned, the walls steepened, and the river—the cave's river, the one that ran wrong, uphill and impossible—came down beside us with a sound like continuous applause from an audience that never quite commits. The water was cold enough to hurt if you touched it. I knew because I touched it, because apparently at forty-six I still needed to verify things with my hands.

"Still running the wrong direction," Felix observed behind me.

"It always does here. My father called it the river's honesty. It only lies downstream, where people are watching."

"That's not honesty. That's just good tradecraft."

I almost laughed. That was the trouble with Felix Nakamura—they kept being funny at exactly the moments when laughing felt like a betrayal of the mission, and the mission was mostly grief with a route plan attached.

We walked. The gorge narrowed. And then, around a bend where the walls pinched close enough that the river filled half the floor, the glacier came into view for the first time.

I want to tell you I was prepared. I had spent three decades looking at photographs of this ice, reading survey reports, interviewing old men who'd flown over it in 1988 and pronounced it "a mess." None of it helped. The tongue of the Vinter Glacier came down between the gorge walls like something poured rather than placed, grey-blue and veined with rock flour, its snout maybe four hundred meters from the river, and the air coming off it had a smell I remembered from childhood summers at the coast—cold stone, old water, the mineral breath of things that do not hurry. It looked less like ice than like a held breath. Thirty years long.

"There," Felix said, and pointed.

At first I thought they meant the ice itself. Then I saw what they saw: a shelf of gravel above the flood line, flat enough for tents, sheltered by a shoulder of moraine—and on it, small with distance, the geometry of human order. Squares. Lines. A ridgepole catching the light.

The basecamp.

* * *

We climbed to it in the long gold light of late afternoon, and neither of us talked, which for two people who had been talking constantly for three days was its own kind of statement. The last fifty meters I climbed with my heart going like I was twenty and chasing a bus, except the thing I was chasing was 1987.

It stood. That was the first shock—that it stood at all. Tents collapsed into their own skeletons, yes, nylon rotted to lace, aluminum poles bent into the shapes of arguments, but the camp's bones were all there: the kitchen tarp strung between two boulders, the supply crates stacked in a wall against the wind, the tent platforms leveled with flat stones in a way that said *my father's hands* louder than any signature. Thirty-eight years of freeze and thaw had passed through this place like a slow landlord, and it had still kept the rooms.

And it had been tagged.

I saw the first justice-mark before I understood what I was seeing—a symbol cut into the door of the supply crate, deep, deliberate, re-cut when it weathered shallow. A circle broken by a vertical stroke, with three short lines beneath it like tally marks. I knew the mark. Every Voss knew the mark. Children in our family learned it the way other children learn the alphabet: this is how we write *judged*. This is how we write *accounted for*. My grandfather's generation cut them into barn doors and boat hulls after the war, into the lintels of men who had cheated widows, and the family called it justice because the law was far away and slow, and because calling it justice meant you didn't have to call it what else it was.

There were eleven of the marks in the camp. I counted. Eleven, on crates and tent poles and one, horribly, on the flat stone that served as the kitchen table—cut there, I realized, at a height and angle that suggested someone had sat down to do it properly. Someone had spent time here. Someone had held a chisel in this dead man's kitchen and worked carefully, because sloppy work would have been disrespectful, and the family was nothing if not respectful in its violence.

"Felix." My voice came out strange. "Come look at this."

They were already looking. They stood at the edge of the camp with that stillness they did, hands loose at their sides, eyes moving in the methodical sweep I'd started to think of as inventory. When I showed them the mark on the table, they crouched, touched the grooves, rubbed the dust between finger and thumb.

"Re-cut at least twice," they said. "Different tools, probably different years. Someone maintains these." They looked up at me. "Your family comes back."

"My family *keeps books*," I said. "That's the whole religion. Everything owed gets written down, and everything written down gets collected eventually." I heard my own voice go flat and hard and recognized Alder in it, which was the worst part. Blood is a dialect; you can't help speaking it.

Felix straightened slowly and turned a full circle, taking in the camp. "This wasn't searched," they said. "Not really. Look—crates stacked, not scattered. Kitchen gear put down, not thrown. If a rescue team had torn through here looking for survivors, or a recovery team looking for bodies, there'd be chaos. This is..." They hesitated, hunting for the word.

"Preserved," I said.

"Worse than preserved. Curated." Felix crossed to the nearest crate and lifted the lid—it came away with a sigh of rotten wood—and inside, wrapped in wax paper gone amber with age, were tins. Food. Rations, dated by the corrosion alone. "Somebody sealed this camp. Or somebody decided leaving it was the punishment." They glanced at me. "Which is it? You know these people."

I didn't answer right away, because the honest answer was *both*, and both was worse. The family had come up here—I was sure of it now, sure in my bones—in the weeks after the disappearance. Had found the camp empty, the men gone, the expedition dissolved into ice and rumor. And instead of stripping it, they had marked it. Tagged it like a debt outstanding. Every year or decade, someone had walked the gorge and re-cut the symbols, keeping the account current, telling the mountain: *we remember what we're owed.*

What we were owed, supposedly, was the truth of what Edmund Hale's expedition had taken from the source. What we were actually owed—I understood this only now, standing in the tagged ruin—was silence. The marks weren't a promise to find answers. They were a warning to everyone else to stop looking.

"They left it as a message," I said finally. "To anyone who came looking. *The family has claimed this. The account is open.*" I touched the tally lines under the circle. Three strokes. Three climbers. My father, Edmund Hale, and Tomas Brandt. The family had counted them like coins.

Felix watched me with an expression I couldn't parse, and then said, quietly, "You okay?"

"No," I said. "But I've been not-okay about this camp since I was sixteen, so at least it's familiar."

* * *

We made our own camp sixty meters off, downwind, because Felix refused—with a firmness I'd learned not to argue with—to sleep inside the wreck. "You don't camp in a crime scene," they said. "You document it and you get clear." So we ate our terrible dinner of instant noodles and hard cheese within sight of my father's kitchen, and the light went bronze, then violet, then gone, and the glacier made its small noises in the dark: cracks and settlements, the sounds of a house shifting on its foundations.

Felix slept. I didn't, or barely. Around what I suppose was midnight I gave up, crawled out of my bag, and walked back to the camp with my headlamp off, letting my eyes adjust, because I wanted to see it the way my father might have seen it on his last night—not evidence, not a scene, just home.

Moonlight did the work. The gorge held a bowl of pale light, and the wrecked tents cast long shadows, and the river ran silver and wrong below, and the glacier's snout glowed faintly blue at its seams like something lit from inside. I stood in the middle of the camp, in the exact center where the fire ring was—stones still blackened, still in place—and I said, out loud, to nobody, "Dad. I'm here. It took me thirty years, but I'm here."

Nothing answered, obviously. Mountains don't do closure. But the wind came down off the ice right then, cold and clean, moving through the ruined camp with a sound like pages turning, and I let myself pretend, for about four seconds, that it was a reply.

Then my boot caught on something.

I looked down. At the edge of the fire ring, half sunk in gravel, was a flat stone unlike the others—lighter, finer-grained, and carved. I knelt. My hands were shaking and I told them to stop and they ignored me, which was fair. The carving was worn but legible: a name, and beneath it a date, and beneath the date a single line in my father's cramped, beautiful hand, cut deep enough to survive decades of weather.

*E. HALE — 14 AUG 1987 — WE WENT UP TO SETTLE IT.*

Below that, smaller, nearly erased:

*IF WE DON'T COME DOWN, THE FAULT IS THE PLAN, NOT THE MEN.*

I stayed kneeling there for a long time. Somewhere behind me, Felix's breathing changed—they were awake, had probably been awake the whole time, listening to me talk to ghosts—but they didn't come over, and I was grateful, because I needed one minute to be a daughter before I could be a digger again.

When I finally rose and turned, Felix was sitting up in their bag, watching me across the dark.

"What does it say?" they asked.

"It says he knew." My voice was steadier than I deserved. "It says he knew exactly what they were walking into, and he wrote it in stone because paper burns and ice keeps." I looked up at the glacier, blue-black against the stars, its tongue pointing down the gorge like a compass needle aimed at everything we'd lost. "Tomorrow we follow it up. Into the tongue, along the old route, whatever's left of it."

"And if the route's gone?" Felix asked. "Ice moves. Thirty-eight years is a lot of moving."

"Then we find where it went." I walked back toward our camp, past the tagged crates, past the marked table, past the stone where my father had signed his own forecast. "Because Alder's a day behind us, the calving season opens soon, and I did not spend my whole life on this to stop at the front door."

Felix was quiet a moment. Then, in the dark, wry as ever: "You know, most people inherit money."

"I inherited a crime scene," I said, and lay down, and listened to the ice keep its accounts until morning.
