## 11. Camp of the Vanished

The gorge narrowed the way grief does—not all at once, but by degrees, each bend taking something from you that you didn't notice was gone until you reached for it.

Maren walked ahead with her father's map held open against her chest like a hymnal. She'd been quiet since the icefall came into view an hour back, and I'd learned by now that Maren Voss's silences weren't empty. They were load-bearing. You didn't knock on them.

"There," she said, and her voice did something strange—flattened out, went professional, the way people sound when they're holding themselves together with procedure.

I saw it a moment later. The gorge kinked around a shoulder of gray rock, and in the crook of that elbow sat the basecamp: three collapsed tents gone the color of old bone, their nylon bleached and furred with lichen, a scatter of aluminum poles like the ribs of something that had died mid-stride. A stone windbreak. A cache barrel with its lid sprung. And everywhere, everywhere, the marks.

I'd seen them before, of course. In chapter one of my life, so to speak—the year my father's name stopped being spoken at dinner. The Voss family justice-marks: a vertical stroke crossed by two horizontals, cut deep, meant to last. They were on the cache barrel, on the rock face above the tents, on a wooden supply crate that had somehow survived thirty-eight years of freeze-thaw without surrendering. Someone had come up here after it happened and tagged the site the way a coroner tags a body. *Case closed. Debt recorded.*

Except nobody had ever collected. That was the thing about the Voss family's justice—it kept books too, and its ledger had been open since 1987.

"Nobody's touched it," Maren said. She'd stopped walking. Her hands had gone still on the map. "Thirty-eight years and nobody's touched it."

"People don't picnic in graveyards."

"People don't climb four days into a glacier gorge to tag a campsite either." She started forward again, faster now, and I understood: she wasn't approaching a crime scene. She was approaching her father's last known address.

* * *

We spent the first hour doing what diggers do, which is to say we did nothing quickly.

I cleared debris from the largest tent platform while Maren worked the cache barrel, and I'll be honest about what I was feeling, because Felix Nakamura is many things but not a liar: I was working. That's all. My hands knew the motions—lift, sort, set aside, note—and my hands have always been smarter than my heart about sequencing. You can't feel anything if you're cataloguing. Grief requires idle time, and I intended to provide none.

The tent fabric came away in sheets. Underneath: a sleeping bag fused into a brick of mold, a boot sole, a tin cup grown green as pondwater. A primus stove with its fuel line cracked. And a canvas rucksack, slumped against the windbreak like its owner had just stepped away to check the river.

I know better than to anthropomorphize objects. I've robbed enough dead men's camps to have a professional detachment about it. But that rucksack sat there with such terrible patience that I had to look away from it for a second and pretend to examine the stove.

"Felix." Maren's voice from the barrel. "There's food here. Sealed tins. And—" a pause, the sound of metal scraping metal "—a lockbox. Empty. Pried open."

"Someone got here first?"

"No." She came around the windbreak, and she was holding a small rectangle of tin, bent double. "The box was opened from inside the family. Look at the mark." The justice-stroke, stamped into the lid before it was bent. "They took whatever he was carrying and they *recorded* it. This isn't looting, Felix. This is evidence handling. They've been running a case file for thirty-eight years."

"Then why leave everything else?"

"Because the rest of it is trash to them." She looked out at the camp—the bone-colored tents, the patient rucksack. "Or because leaving it was part of the sentence. A monument nobody's allowed to visit."

I turned back to the rucksack, because turning away from things is a skill I never mastered, and opened it.

Clothes, rotted soft. A coil of rope, still good under the mildew—I checked, because rope is rope and sentiment doesn't hold body weight. A waterproof pouch, flat and heavy.

And inside the pouch, wrapped in a wool sock that had once been army-green: a field journal.

My father's field journal.

I knew it before I saw the name. I knew it from the weight—from the specific density of a journal he'd packed himself, because my father packed books the way other men pack ammunition, tight and deliberate. I knew it from the corner wear, the exact pattern of softening that comes from a book carried in a left hip pocket for months. I had grown up watching that wear develop. I had, at age nine, drawn a picture of our house inside a journal just like this one, and gotten scolded for wasting pages, and the scolding had been the warmest thing he ever gave me.

"Felix?" Maren said.

"It's his," I said. My voice came out fine. Professional. Load-bearing. "It's his journal."

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about Edmund Nakamura, disgraced archaeologist, better talker than digger, author of my entire inheritance of damage: he wrote everything down. Not diaries—journals. Field journals, with grid references and stratigraphy notes and weather observations, because a journal without data was, in his words, gossip. He believed that if you recorded the world precisely enough, it couldn't lie to you. It was the only faith he had and he practiced it devoutly, right up until the day it failed him, or he failed it, depending which of us you ask, and we have never asked.

So I sat down on a stone that thirty-eight years ago some man sat on before walking into a river or a crevasse or a story, and I opened my father's journal, and the water had gotten to it.

Not all of it. The pouch had done its work; the middle third was legible, the early pages stiff but readable, and the last section—the most recent entries, the ones from the source itself—was a pulp of gray paper where ink had bloomed into clouds. And torn out. Pages torn out at the binding, ragged, maybe twenty of them, gone.

"Water-damaged," I said, because Maren was watching. "And torn. Someone removed pages."

"Him? Or them?"

"If it were them, they'd have taken the whole book." I turned a page carefully, using a splinter of tent pole as a spatula. "This was him. He tore them out himself. He wanted the book to survive even if the pages didn't."

The earliest entries were routine. Route notes. Food weights. Complaints about a teammate named Sorrel who snored, and about the financier—a man called Herron, whose name appeared only in the flat tone my father reserved for people he owed money to. *Herron insists we push the schedule. Herron does not understand that ice keeps its own calendar.* I almost smiled. Thirty-eight years dead and my father was still complaining about funding.

Then the route deepened, and the handwriting changed. Tighter. Faster. The margins filled with sketches—the gorge, the ice tongue, a profile of the glacier with a dotted line running through it like a vein.

*Day 19. The stream is wrong. I've said it before and I'll say it again: it runs uphill. Sorrel thinks it's a survey error. It is not a survey error. The water emerges from the glacier tongue 200 meters above the cave mouth below. There is a passage. There has to be a passage, and it runs to a source under the ice, and no one has mapped it because no one believed the water.*

*I believe the water.*

I read that line twice. *I believe the water.* It sounded like him. It sounded exactly like the man who taught me to read soil—who told me, crouched over a test pit in some rented backyard, that the ground always tells the truth eventually, your job is just to not interrupt.

*Day 23. Found the entrance. The ice opens onto a chamber—blue light, like being inside a bottle of glass. The spring rises from beneath. And there are remains. Old ones. Older than the expedition, older than the town. Someone was here before us, and they brought documents, and the cold kept them.*

I stopped. Looked up. The gorge stood around us, gray and indifferent, and somewhere above it the glacier held whatever chamber he was describing, holding it still, holding it cold.

"Maren," I said. "What did your father's letter say? Exactly."

She recited it without hesitation—she's had thirty-eight years to memorize it, and I suspect she recites it nightly like a woman saying grace backwards. *"Tell Nakamura's boy the source is real and the debt is real and they are the same debt."*

"The source is real," I repeated. "He found it. Day twenty-three." I turned pages. "And then—"

*Day 24. Herron knows. I don't know how—he was never inside—but he knows what's down there and he wants it sealed. Not studied. Sealed. He used the word 'handled.' I have been in this business long enough to know what gets handled.*

*The documents identify a killer. That's all I will write. If I write more, this page becomes a weapon pointed at someone, and I refuse to hand anyone a loaded page.*

My mouth had gone dry. Somewhere behind me Maren had stopped sorting the cache barrel; I could hear her not moving.

*Day 25. Argument with Herron. He offered money. Then he offered threats. Then he offered nothing, which was worse. Sorrel has gone quiet. I think Sorrel is afraid of the wrong person.*

*Day 26. We descend tomorrow. All of us. Herron says the chamber must be photographed and then collapsed. I say photographs can be stolen. He smiled when I said that. I did not like his smile.*

The next entry was dated two days later and the handwriting had come apart entirely—slanted, hurried, pressed so hard the pen had nearly gone through the paper.

*Day 28. Sorrel is dead. They say the river took him. The river did not take him. I know what a drowned man looks like and I know what a handled man looks like and Sorrel was handled. Herron's people were on the mountain. I saw a jacket I recognized.*

*I am going back down alone tonight. If I can reach the chamber I can take the documents out. If I take them out, Herron cannot seal the truth, only run from it.*

*If anything happens, it was my idea, not the boy's.*

There it was.

Fourteen words, and the whole world went quiet.

I want to tell you I read it with dignity. I want to tell you I set the journal down gently and processed my emotions in a healthy sequential manner. What actually happened is that I read the line, and then I read it again, and then my hands started shaking so badly I had to close the book to keep from tearing it, and I sat on that dead man's stone in a dead man's camp and made a sound I hadn't made since I was eleven years old—some small, involuntary animal noise, grief coming up through the floorboards of a room I thought I'd finished renovating decades ago.

*Not the boy's.*

I was nine years old in 1987. Nine. I was in a rented house in a town I don't remember, learning long division, waiting for a father who came home thinner each expedition and left thicker with secrets. And he was up here, on this ice, writing my name into the last clean page of his last field journal—not as a claim, not as a legacy, but as a *shield*. He was standing between me and Herron the only way a man with no power can stand: by signing the crime himself. By making sure that whatever came looking would find one guilty Nakamura instead of two.

He disappeared three weeks later. The world called it cowardice. The family called it theft. I called it abandonment, for twenty-nine years, from every town I could reach by morning.

And the whole time, the fool had been trying to keep me off the invoice.

"Felix." Maren was crouched in front of me. I don't know how long she'd been there. Her face had done the thing it does—gone still and careful, a woman handling something fragile that happens to be a person. "What does it say?"

"He knew," I said. My voice was wrecked. I hated that. I let it be wrecked anyway, because some bills you pay in front of witnesses. "He knew what Herron was. He watched them kill your—watch them kill Sorrel. And he went back down alone to take the evidence, and then he ran, and he ran for thirty-eight years, and he—" I stopped. Started over. "The last line. It's about me."

She waited. Maren is very good at waiting; she's had practice.

"*If anything happens, it was my idea, not the boy's.*"

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had rearranged itself behind them—some piece of the case file she'd been building for three decades clicking into a new position.

"That's why the family hunts you," she said slowly. "Not for treasure. For *witness*. Alder thinks you inherited guilt. But you didn't inherit guilt, Felix. You inherited the one thing Herron's heirs can't afford—you're the son of the man who saw it. As long as you exist, unconfessed, unmanaged, the file stays open."

"And the torn pages?"

"Gone with him. Wherever he went." She looked at the ruined final section, the pulp and the bloom. "Unless—"

"Unless what?"

She hesitated, and I watched her decide whether to say it, and I braced myself, because in my experience the sentences people hesitate before are the ones with hooks in them.

"Your father was a record-keeper," she said. "A man who believed precise records couldn't lie. Men like that don't destroy information, Felix. They *relocate* it. Twenty pages, torn cleanly, carried out." She nodded east, up-gorge, toward the white wall of the Vinter Glacier where it hung over the valley like a held breath. "If he took them anywhere, he took them high. Toward the source. Toward the one place on earth colder than his marriage."

I looked at the journal in my lap—my father's tidy, doomed, faithful handwriting, the data of a man who tried to make the world honest by writing it down correctly. Somewhere under that ice was either twenty pages of truth or twenty pages of ash, and either way, the only person alive who might know was a man who'd spent my whole childhood being a closed door.

The glacier ticked and settled somewhere above us, a sound like a giant shifting its weight.

"We camp here tonight," Maren said, already pulling the rope from my pack, already practical, because that was how she loved things—by giving them tasks. "First crossing at dawn."

I wrapped the journal in the wool sock, put it in the waterproof pouch, and put the pouch against my chest, where it sat like a second heartbeat, slightly out of rhythm with mine.

That night I dreamed of a rented kitchen, and long division, and a door opening, and woke before I could see who came through it—with frost on my sleeping bag and the taste of glacier air in my throat and, for the first time since I was eleven years old, somewhere to go that wasn't just *away*.
