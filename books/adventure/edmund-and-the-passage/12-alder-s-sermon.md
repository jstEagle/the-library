## 12. Alder's Sermon

I have made a study of fires. It is not a skill anyone thanks you for, but it is a skill. A fire tells you what a person is before they say a word. Felix Nakamura built ours small and disciplined, tucked behind a boulder the way a miser tucks away coin—heat without light, warmth without witness. Maren Voss sat with her boots off and her knife out, whittling nothing in particular, which is what frightened people do instead of admitting they are frightened.

I watched them for two hours before I let them see me.

That is not cruelty. That is courtesy. You do not interrupt people mid-argument; you wait until the argument has done its work, until the silence after it has settled into something they can both live inside. Then you step into the firelight with your hands visible and your voice level, and you say the thing that has been chasing them since the tide went out.

"Evening," I said.

Felix came up off the ground like a man electrocuted, and Maren did not move at all, which told me more about her than the whittling had. The still ones are the dangerous ones. The flinchers just need managing.

"Alder," she said. Not a question. She'd known I was coming. Maybe she'd known since the cave, since the stone went missing from Nakamura's room, since her cousin's name started appearing in her mouth like a stone she kept turning over.

"You've been hard to follow," I said, "and then suddenly very easy. You stopped being clever about your camps about six miles back."

"We were tired," Felix said.

"I know." I crouched at the edge of the light, close enough to warm my hands without inviting myself further in. "Tired is fine. Tired is honest. I'd rather deal with tired people than clever ones. Clever people lie to themselves first, and by the time the lie reaches me it's been through so many hands I can't tell what it was originally."

Maren snorted. "You rehearse these?"

"Every word," I said. "It's called respect."

* * *

Let me tell you how this works, because I find that people who understand the machinery suffer less. Not less pain—I can't help you there—but less confusion, and confusion is the crueler of the two.

The Voss family has been collecting debts on this coast for four generations. Not money. Money is easy; money goes to lawyers like everyone else's. We collect answers. In 1987 three climbers walked up the Vinter Glacier behind an expedition led by Edmund Voss—my uncle, though the family prefers "the lost one," as if he were a coat left on a train—and they did not come down. The police looked for a season. The police found weather, and terrain, and grief, and closed the file. The file is still closed. You could go to the county office tomorrow and ask, and some clerk with coffee breath would pull a folder thin as a hymnal and shrug at you.

The law failed the dead. That is the whole of it. That is the seed everything else grew from. When the law fails, someone must judge, or the dead stay wronged forever, and a family that lets its dead stay wronged is not a family; it is a waiting room.

So we judged. My grandfather judged first, then my father, and now me. We are not many. We are not loud. We keep a ledger—the real kind, ink and paper—and every entry in it is a person who knows something about 1987 and has decided, for their own reasons, to keep it dark. Some entries we closed with conversation. A few required more. I will not insult you by pretending otherwise. I have done things in service of this ledger that I do not discuss at fires, and I have slept afterward, which is either proof of my conviction or proof of something wrong with me, and I have never fully settled the question.

Felix Nakamura is an entry. Has been since birth, really, though the family didn't know it until the tide went out and the sea gave back a carved stone with a mark on it that our records say belonged to a man who vanished up a glacier carrying our uncle's secrets.

"You took the stone," Felix said. It wasn't a question either. This family breeds people who don't ask questions; we just haven't gotten around to breeding the answers out of them.

"I did."

"Why not take the map too? Or the journals? You had the room for an hour at least."

"Because I wanted you to come after me," I said. "And here you are."

Maren's knife stopped moving. "You stole it to bait us."

"I stole it to *start* us. There's a difference, and the difference matters." I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "If I'd taken everything, you'd have run harder and smarter, and I'd be writing letters to your next false names. Instead you're sitting across from me, warming yourselves on wood I can see, and nobody's bleeding. That's the best outcome available. I want you to notice that."

* * *

Here is what I offered, and I offer it exactly once, so attend.

To Felix: walk away. Tonight, tomorrow, whenever. Take the coast road south, take any name you like—we have a list of them, incidentally, eleven years of them, and it makes for melancholy reading—and we will strike you from the ledger untried. Your father's sins are not yours unless you make them yours. The family has no interest in punishing children for parents. We are judges, not inheritors of grudge. Grudge is what people do when they lack the patience for justice.

"What does untried mean?" Felix asked. "For you people. What does a trial look like?"

"It looks like a room," I said. "Family, elders, the accused. Questions put plainly and answered under oath—not legal oath, a better one. An oath to the dead. And then a verdict, and the verdict is carried out, whatever it is. Most verdicts are sentences of truth. Confession, recorded, delivered where it needs to go. Some are exile. One, in my grandfather's time, was neither, and we do not speak of it, which I recognize is a thing guilty organizations always say, and I have no defense except that it was forty years ago and the men who did it are all in the ground."

"And if I refuse to answer questions?"

"Then refusal is your testimony. Silence is an answer. It's usually the answer *I'm looking for*, honestly. Innocent people talk. Guilty people calculate, and calculation has a smell."

Maren laughed—one short, ugly note. "And me? Do I get to walk away too?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because you're not accused of anything yet," I said. "You're summoned. There's a difference, and the difference matters." I said it again deliberately. Repetition is how you make a thing feel inevitable. "You are a Voss, Maren. Blood doesn't get struck from ledgers. You've spent thirty years digging at 1987 with a shovel in each hand, and the family has watched you do it, and let you, because we thought you were grieving. Grief is permitted. But now you've allied yourself with the child of the man who may have killed your father, and you're walking toward the source with his map in your pack, and that is no longer grief. That is a choice. Choices come to trial."

"He didn't kill anyone," Maren said.

"Prove it."

"That's what I'm trying to *do*—"

"With what?" I leaned forward. I'll admit I leaned forward. There is a moment in every sermon when the preacher stops explaining and starts pressing, and this was mine. "With a water-damaged journal missing its pages? With a legend about a river that runs uphill? With a boy who changes names like socks? You're going to carry that into a court? Which court, Maren? The one that closed the file? The law had thirty years and quit. The only institution on this earth still willing to look at 1987 is the one you're sitting across from, calling merciless."

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, water moved in the dark—that wrong river, running its impossible errand toward the sea. I heard it and felt, briefly, the way I imagine a priest feels when the bell rings during his sermon: as though the building itself were agreeing with him.

* * *

I want to be fair to them, because fairness is the whole point of me, whatever Maren thinks. So let me lay out the case against their plan, plainly, the way I'd want it laid out against mine.

They intend to reach the source before calving season opens the glacier's throat and swallows the route for another decade. They believe something is preserved up there—documents, remains, an archive in ice. They believe it will exonerate Edmund Nakamura and condemn whoever deserves condemning, and they believe the finding of it will be enough. Truth discovered is truth established. That is the theory.

Here is the flaw. Suppose they're right. Suppose the ice opens and gives them a sealed chamber with papers in it, signed and dated, naming a murderer. What then? Who arrests him? The murderer, if he lived, would be ninety years old. His heirs hold land, ships, seats on two boards, and the affection of a town their family bankrolled through two bad seasons. The evidence would sit in Maren's pack like a coal—hot, precious, and burning a hole through everything it touched. The family would demand it. The heirs would deny it. The law would shrug its hymnal-thin shrug. And somewhere in the middle of all that, the coal would burn someone, probably Maren, possibly the boy, and the dead would be exactly as wronged as before, only now with witnesses.

Evidence does not convict. Institutions convict. And every institution within a hundred miles of this glacier has already voted.

"The family convicts," Maren said flatly. "That's your pitch. Trust the private court because the public one failed."

"My pitch is worse than that," I said, and I saw Felix's eyes narrow, and I respected the narrowing. Smart. "My pitch is: the family convicted already. Thirty-five years ago. The verdict is written. The ledger says the expedition was betrayed from inside, that Edmund Nakamura took something from the source that was not his to take, and that three men died of it. All that remains is sentencing, and sentencing requires the offender, and the offender's child just walked out of a cave holding his mark. I am not asking you to trust the family's judgment, Nakamura. I'm informing you that judgment has occurred, and offering you the only two exits that exist from under it: acquittal or sentence. Walk away, and there's no case. Come to trial, answer true, and maybe—*maybe*—you give me a reason to amend the verdict. Amend. Not overturn. I don't have the authority to overturn, and neither does anyone alive."

"And if the truth up there contradicts the ledger?" Felix asked quietly. "If your grandfather's verdict was wrong?"

I should have had an answer ready. I'd rehearsed this fire for sixty miles. But that question went through my rehearsal like a spear through canvas, because the honest answer is: then the ledger is wrong, and the ledger is the family, and the family is me, and I do not know what a man is supposed to do when the thing he has spent his life enforcing turns out to have been a mistake wearing a uniform.

So I said what I actually believe, which is more dangerous but cleaner: "Then the ledger gets amended. By me. However much it costs."

"You'd do that?" Maren said. "Against your own elders?"

"I amended it once before," I said. "Small entry. A fisherman who lied to us about seeing lights on the glacier in '87. We'd marked him evasive. He was just ashamed—he'd seen the lights and gone back to bed. I struck the entry myself and took the scolding for it. Small amendment. But I learned the weight of the pen, and whose hand it takes." I looked at her. "Your father's pen is in that ledger too, Maren. Entry forty-one. *Edmund Voss, deceased, cause unknown.* We never closed it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Your father isn't a grievance to this family. He's an open case. The only one. I have read his page more times than I've read my own name."

That landed. I watched it land. Her jaw did the thing jaws do when a person is swallowing something too large.

* * *

We sat a while. The fire got low. Felix fed it again—small sticks, disciplined, heat without light—and I thought, not for the first time, that the father had built this child the way he built camps: to leave no trace and survive anything.

"Say I take the walk-away," Felix said finally. "What happens to her?"

"She comes home. To trial."

"Your trial. Your judges. Your verdict." Felix turned to me, and the firelight caught the face properly for the first time—hollowed, watchful, older than the years in it. "You're offering me freedom priced in someone else's blood. That's not mercy. That's arithmetic."

"Yes," I said.

No point dressing it. Arithmetic is what justice becomes when the law won't carry it. Somebody counts. Somebody pays. The only question ever is who does the counting, and I would rather it be a man who loses sleep over the sums than a clerk with coffee breath who closed the file.

"And if we both refuse?" Maren said. "If we keep walking toward the glacier right now?"

"Then I walk with you," I said.

Silence. Real silence, the kind with weight.

"You're joking," she said.

"I don't joke about the ledger." I stood, brushed off my knees, and let them see the whole of me standing in their firelight—big, gray, unhurried, a man built like the door of a courthouse. "Think about it. If you reach the source and find what you think is there, you'll need someone to carry the verdict home. Evidence without an enforcer is a story. Stories die. I am the family's enforcer, and I am telling you, under this sky, that if the ice proves the ledger wrong, I will carry the correction myself, into the family hall, and read it aloud, and take whatever falls on me for it. That is worth more to you than my absence. You know it is."

"And if the ice proves the ledger *right*?" Felix asked. "About my father?"

"Then I arrest you at the source," I said, "gently, and we walk down together, and you get the trial you'd have gotten anyway—except you'll have seen it first. Whatever's up there. You'll know what you're answering for." I picked up my pack. "That's the sermon. Walk, trial, or company. Choose by morning. I'll be at the far edge of camp. I don't sleep near people I might have to hurt, and I'd rather not hurt either of you, which is a stranger sentence than anything else I've said tonight, and truer."

I walked to the edge of the light and stopped, because there was one more thing, and sermons end badly when the preacher leaves the hard verse out.

"Nakamura. The journal pages that were torn out. Your father tore them, yes?"

A pause long enough to have weather in it. "Maybe."

"When you find what he was hiding from," I said, "remember that he hid it from *everyone*. Not from us. From everyone. Men who are only hiding from enemies choose better camps." I nodded at the small, disciplined fire. "He taught you that. Ask yourself why a man who feared the whole world would send his child a carved stone with his mark on it, knowing exactly who would come."

I left them with that. Behind me the fire crackled its small, honest heat, and the wrong river ran on in the dark, uphill toward the ice, carrying whatever it carries, patient as a bill.

By morning, I knew, they would not have chosen the walk-away. People like these never do—it's the curse of being built by someone brave. So I sat with my back against a boulder and did not sleep, and listened to the two of them whisper past midnight, and at some hour before dawn I heard Maren laugh once, low and unwilling, at something the Nakamura boy said, and I thought: *there it is.*

There is a fourth option I hadn't put in the sermon. It was forming itself out there in the dark between them, and by the shape of the silence, they were already walking toward it—and it led higher than any of us had planned, and colder, and it would cost the ledger something it had never paid.

I checked the rope coils on my pack and waited for the light.
