## 13. The First Crossing

The river in the gorge did not look like much from above.

That was the first lie it told us. From the rim, a hundred feet up, it was a silver thread stitched through grey stone, something you could cover with a boot print if you were careless with scale. Maren stood at the edge of the old crossing point—the one her father's letter described, *where the rock pinches and the water forgets to be deep*—and I watched her do the arithmetic that climbers do, the quiet lip-moving count of anchors and angles and distances.

"It doesn't forget anything," I said. "Water remembers everything. That's its whole job."

"You're cheerful this morning."

"I'm terrified. It presents similarly."

We'd been walking two days since the wrecked basecamp, following the gorge as it narrowed, the walls leaning in like conspirators. The trail her father described existed mostly as suggestions: a cairn here, a rusted piton there, a ledge worn smooth by boots that had been dead longer than I'd been alive. And now the river, which had been our companion—chattering alongside us, sometimes ahead, sometimes vanishing under stone only to reappear uphill where it had no business being—had decided to become an obstacle instead.

The crossing was a traverse. Not a bridge; nobody had built bridges out here, or if they had, the river had eaten them. A rope anchored high on the north wall, sagging down in a catenary curve so that at its lowest point you'd be hanging directly over the water, boots skimming the surface, hauling yourself hand over hand across forty feet of nothing while the current reached up for your ankles.

Maren rigged it because she was better at rigging than I was, and she knew it, and she didn't pretend otherwise, which I was learning to like about her. She set the anchor behind a flake of granite the size of a dining table, tested it with her whole weight plus a bounce, then tested it again because she was the kind of person who trusted nothing once.

"Your father crossed here," she said, threading the rope through a carabiner older than both of us combined. "1987. He wrote about it."

"He wrote about everything," I said. "It's how he avoided saying anything."

She almost smiled. "You do that too."

"I know. It's genetic. Like bad knees."

The plan was simple, which should have warned me. Maren went first—her choice, her rope, her argument that if the anchor failed she'd rather find out alone—and made it across in a style I could only describe as competent fury, arms burning, boots skating on wet rock at the far side until she found purchase and hauled herself up onto the shelf. She clipped the rope taut for my crossing and shouted something the river ate whole, so I read her lips: *your turn*.

I want to record, for accuracy, that I was not afraid of the crossing itself. Heights don't bother me. Water doesn't bother me. The specific combination—water below, height above, and me suspended between them like a bead on an abacus—apparently bothers me considerably, but I didn't know that yet, and ignorance is a kind of courage.

Halfway across, the rope dipped low and the river rose up to meet me.

Not literally. Rivers don't rise. But spray off the rapids below had lacquered the rock face on the far side, and when I swung my boots toward the landing shelf, they went out from under me with the enthusiasm of a thing that had been waiting all morning to do exactly that. One moment I was a person mid-traverse. The next I was a pendulum, swinging out over the current, hands locked on the rope, the whole gorge rotating around me with a slow, indifferent grandeur.

Here is what I learned about myself in the next four seconds: I do not scream. I calculate. Some buried ancestor of mine—a fisherman, probably, someone who worked water for a living before the Nakamuras got respectable and then disgraceful—took over my body and started doing math. Rope angle. Swing arc. Distance to the wall. Depth of the pool beneath me, which the white water suggested was deep and the sound suggested was angry.

And then I saw the map case.

Maren wore it crosswise, a waxed canvas tube slung at her hip, and when she leaned out over the edge to reach for me—stupid, brave, exactly what I'd have done—the case swung free of her body and hung out over the water like bait.

Her father's map. The route up the Vinter Glacier, marked in his hand, the only copy in existence, the document that had gotten three people killed in 1987 and was working on adding us to the list. Without it we were two idiots with a grudge against a mountain. With it we were two idiots with directions.

I reached for it.

I want to be honest about the sequence, because honesty is going to matter in a minute. My hand came off the rope—one hand, then, God help me, nearly both—and went for the map case. Not for Maren. Not for the rock. For the leather strap and the rolled paper inside it, thirty years old, worth more than either of our lives by any measure we'd been using.

My fingers closed on the strap at the exact moment Maren grabbed my wrist, and the physics of the situation—two bodies, one anchor, a river conducting its business below—resolved itself the way physics does: abruptly, and in favor of whoever weighed less. She hauled. I flailed. The rope burned a line across my palm that I'd feel for a week, and then I was over the edge, on the shelf, on my back, breathing the way people breathe after the body has filed a formal complaint.

The map case was still in my fist. I noticed that before I noticed anything else. Before the pain in my hand, before the cold soaking through my jacket, before Maren's face appearing above mine wearing an expression I couldn't parse—relief and fury fighting for the same real estate.

"You let go of the rope," she said.

"I know."

"To grab the map."

"I know."

We looked at each other. The river went on below us, making its arguments to the stone.

* * *

We didn't talk about it for three hours.

This was not strategy. It was not the silent treatment or some delicate dance of wounded feelings. It was that we were moving, climbing out of the gorge on the far side, following the thread of trail upward through scree and stunted pine, and the terrain demanded all our attention and most of our breath. But I could feel the conversation waiting for us the way you feel weather coming—not in the sky, in the joints.

Maren walked ahead. She always walked ahead; I think she believed that whoever led couldn't be ambushed, which was wrong but understandable. Every hour or so she'd glance back at me, quick, checking I was still there, and every time I caught her doing it she'd look away fast, like I'd caught her at something.

At the top of the climb, where the gorge opened into a bench of grey gravel and the glacier finally showed itself—distant, white, patient—we stopped to eat. Cold oatmeal, hard bread, water that tasted faintly of stone. Maren sat on a boulder with her back to the view, which told me something about how she looked at views, and unwrapped the bread with the mechanical precision of someone who had decided not to throw it at me.

"Say it," she said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you're composing. You've been composing since the crossing. You get this look—you're doing it right now—like you're editing yourself down to the version least likely to cause damage." She tore the bread in half and held half out without looking at me. "I've spent thirty years around people who edit themselves. Just say the sentence."

I took the bread. I ate some of it. I looked at the glacier, because it was easier than looking at her, and because it was genuinely spectacular and I wasn't going to waste it on a difficult conversation.

"I reached for the map first," I said.

"I know. I saw."

"Not for you. If it had been you or the map—" I stopped. Started again, because the sentence deserved to be finished properly even if finishing it cost something. "If it had been you falling and the map safe, I would have gone for you. I believe that. But that's not what happened. What happened was the map fell and I went for the map, and you happened to be attached to it, and I've been trying for three hours to find a version of that story where I come out better, and there isn't one."

Maren chewed. Swallowed. Looked at the glacier herself, which I understood now was her version of what looking away meant for other people.

"My father wrote about a crossing like this," she said. "In his last letter. He said the hardest part wasn't the water. It was finding out what your hands do when you're not telling them what to do." She brushed crumbs from her knee with unnecessary care. "He wrote it about someone else. About a man whose hands chose the expedition's records over another climber, in a collapse, in '87. He never named him. But he kept writing about it, circling it, year after year, like a tongue returning to a broken tooth."

"And you think the man was my father."

"I think the man was someone who loved documents more than he could afford to." She turned and looked at me straight on, and her eyes were dry and very blue and very tired. "So no. I'm not surprised by you, Felix. I'm relieved. I've been wondering whether you were going to lie to me, and now I know you won't, and that's worth more than a clean rescue."

"That's a low bar."

"It's the only bar anyone clears consistently." She stood, shouldered her pack, and offered me a hand up—which, given that my palm was a raw stripe of rope burn, was either kindness or irony, and with Maren it was usually both. "For what it's worth, I'd have gone for the map too."

"No, you wouldn't."

"Yes, I would. It's my father's handwriting. You'd have gone for yours." She pulled me up. "We're not good people, either of us. We're just honest ones, starting today, apparently, because a river embarrassed us into it."

* * *

We made camp early, in a hollow below the bench where the wind couldn't strip the heat out of us, and I did something I hadn't done in years: I showed someone else my hands.

Not metaphorically. Although, fine, also metaphorically. But literally—I sat with the stove hissing between us and unwrapped the improvised bandage I'd wound around my palm at the bottom of the gorge, and Maren took one look at it and said, "That's wrong," and redid it properly, with actual technique, her fingers quick and impersonal and strangely gentle.

"Where'd you learn field medicine?" I asked.

"Same place you learned to read soil. Nowhere good." She tied off the wrap and held my hand a moment longer than strictly necessary, studying the calluses. "Digger's hands," she said. "The rumors were true."

"What rumors?"

"About Edmund Nakamura's kid. That the child out-dug the father. People who worked with him used to say it like a joke, but it wasn't a joke. It was a warning." She released my hand and busied herself with the stove. "They said he was jealous of you."

"He was tired of me," I said. "Jealousy requires thinking about someone. He mostly wanted me elsewhere." I heard how it came out—flat, rehearsed, the story worn smooth by handling—and Maren heard it too, and had the grace not to say so.

Instead she said, "My father wanted me to be anywhere but the mountains. After '87 he forbade them. So naturally I learned every route on the Vinter by heart before I was sixteen." A pause. "We're both children of doors that got closed on us."

"Is that why you've spent thirty years pushing them back open?"

"Is that why you've spent thirty years running through every open one you could find?"

We looked at each other over the stove, and something settled between us—not warmth exactly, not yet, but the end of pretending we were strangers. Two people who had been reading each other's files for days finally admitting they'd read them.

The light went long and gold and then grey. The glacier caught the last of it and held it, glowing faintly after the sky had given up, like a coal under ash. Somewhere above us, ice shifted—a sound like a door closing in an empty house—and we both looked up at the same time.

"Calving season," Maren said quietly. "Weeks away. Maybe less. The warm front's early this year."

"Then we move faster tomorrow."

"We move smarter tomorrow. Faster is how people die on ice." She banked the stove and lay back against her pack. "Felix."

"Yeah."

"The map case. When you grabbed it. What were you thinking? Honestly. Not the edited version."

I thought about lying, briefly, out of habit. Then I thought about the river, and the four seconds of arithmetic, and what my hands had chosen without consulting me.

"I was thinking," I said, "that the map was the only thing left of your father that tells the truth. And that I've spent my whole life losing things before I decided whether they mattered. And that I wasn't going to lose yours." I pulled my sleeping bag up. "That's the unedited version. It's not heroic. It's just true."

Maren was quiet long enough that I thought she'd fallen asleep. Then, in the dark:

"My father's last letter said, *if the boy comes asking, tell him the source keeps what it's given.* I never knew what he meant. I thought it was grief talking." A breath. "Tonight I think he meant the cave. The admission. Everything the glacier takes, it keeps. And maybe everything it takes from us, too."

"Comforting."

"I didn't say comforting. I said true." Her voice was already blurring toward sleep. "Get some rest. Tomorrow we see what the ice remembers."

I lay awake a while after that, listening to the river below the bench, still running its wrong direction, still keeping its books. My palm throbbed in time with my pulse. Above us, the glacier ticked and groaned in the dark like something enormous turning over in its sleep, dreaming, maybe, of the things it held—

—and in the morning, at the edge of the ice, we would learn that it dreamed of bodies.
