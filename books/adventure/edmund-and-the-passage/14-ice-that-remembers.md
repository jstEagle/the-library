## 14. Ice That Remembers

I have spent thirty years learning the difference between a shape and a body.

A shape is what you see from a distance: a dark smudge in scree that turns out to be lichen, a curled root on a riverbank, a boulder wearing last winter's snow like a shroud it hasn't earned. You learn to look at these things and say *not him* quickly, before hope can get its boots on. Hope is slow to lace up if you don't let it start.

The thing in the ice was not a shape.

We had come down off the moraine at first light, Felix ahead of me because Felix always walks like the ground owes them money and they've come to collect. The glacier's tongue had retreated another few meters since the maps were drawn—Maren Voss owns four sets of maps of this valley, spanning forty years, and I have measured its retreat the way other people measure their children—and where the ice had pulled back it left a skirt of grey debris: rock flour, splintered wood, and things the glacier had been keeping.

Felix stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into their pack.

"Don't," they said.

"Don't what?"

"Whatever you're about to do. Don't do it fast."

I looked past them. The ice face stood maybe eight feet tall at its margin, dirty blue, veined with sediment like a marble cake somebody had given up on. And set into it, half-emerged, tilted at an angle no living person chooses, was a man.

I want to tell you I did something dignified. I want to report that I approached with scientific calm, that thirty years of hunting taught me composure. What actually happened is that my knees made a decision without consulting me, and I was on them in the gravel, close enough to see frost still clinging to his eyebrows, and every rational thought I owned went out through some back door of my skull and left the lights off.

He was mummified rather than frozen—the glacier had dried him as much as preserved him, skin tanned to leather, features collapsed but present. A man perhaps fifty when he died. His hands were bare. His boots were old leather, cracked along the flex points. And on his chest, pinned where a name tag goes, was a rectangle of laminated card that the cold had kept better than it kept him.

**VOSS, E. — EXPEDITION 1987 — PERMIT 114-C.**

My father's initials. My father's surname. My father's expedition.

"That's not possible," I said, out loud, to nobody, because grief has never once waited for a conversational partner.

Felix crouched beside me. They didn't touch me. I noticed that later and understood it: they'd already learned that touching me at the wrong moment was how you lost a hand. "Maren. Look at the jacket."

I looked at the jacket. It was red wool, army surplus, the kind sold by the crate to expeditions because it cost nothing and shed drizzle adequately. There was a repair on the left elbow—a diamond-shaped patch, machine-stitched, slightly wrong shade of red.

I knew that patch. I had *made* that patch. I was eleven years old and my mother had put the needle in my hand and said, *your father tears everything he owns, learn to mend and you'll never need him,* which was her way of saying she loved us both. I had stitched crooked. Thirty years later the crooked stitches were still there, holding, the one reliable thing any Voss ever did.

"It's his spare," I heard myself say. My voice sounded like it was coming up a long stairwell. "He had two. He wore the green one climbing and kept the red for camp. Mother hated the red. Said he looked like a mailbox."

"Maren."

"He labeled everything. Everything. He used to write our names on our sandwiches." I was laughing, I realized. Somewhere under the crying I was laughing, and neither one would give ground. "Permit 114-C. He'd have fought the permit office over that number. He liked it. He said it counted down."

"Maren." Felix's voice, low and even, the voice they probably used on spooked animals and collapsing trench walls. "That's not your father."

And there it was. The sentence I needed and could not bear, arriving together, as sentences do.

Because it wasn't him. I knew it wasn't him the way you know a house isn't yours even when the porch light works. My father had been taller. This man's hands were wrong—broad, square-knuckled, a laborer's hands, not a surveyor's. And my father, whatever else he'd been, whatever he'd done or failed to do in the summer of 1987, had come home. Had walked back into our kitchen eight months after the expedition ended, thinner, silent, missing nothing visible, and had sat at our table for two more years before the family's questions got loud enough that he answered them by leaving. Whatever the glacier took in '87, it hadn't taken him. Not all the way.

But the jacket was his spare. The tag bore his name. And the man wearing both had died on his expedition, in his route, within reach of the source we were walking toward.

"Somebody wore his clothes," I said.

"Somebody borrowed them," Felix said carefully. "Or traded. Or—" They stopped. Felix is many things, most of them useful, and one of those things is someone who knows when a list should remain unfinished.

"Or he gave them away," I said. "To keep someone warm. That's the kind of stupid generous thing he did. That's exactly—" My throat closed on it.

We knelt there in the gravel for a while, the two of us and the dead man, while the wind came down off the ice and did its best to pretend none of this was happening. I got my breathing under control the way you do: badly, then less badly.

"We can't leave him," I said.

"No," Felix agreed. "And we can't take him."

"I know." The arithmetic was ugly and simple. We had rope, a shelter tent, three days of food, and a window of open route measured in days, not weeks. A body is eighty pounds of awkward that doesn't care about your schedule. Carrying him out meant abandoning the source, and the source was the only place answers had ever lived. Leaving him felt like the family's justice in miniature: the dead, filed where convenient.

"We mark him," Felix said. "Properly. GPS, photos, description. When we come down—if we come down—we bring people. Real ones. With stretchers and paperwork and somebody whose job title includes the word *recovery*."

"You sound like you've done this."

"I've been adjacent to it." Something crossed their face, quick as a fish under ice. "Digs turn things up. Not always potsherds."

So we did what could be done. Felix photographed everything with the methodical patience I'd watched them apply to shell middens and soil layers—every angle of the body, the tag, the jacket, the boots, the position of the hands. One detail Felix caught that I'd missed: the man's right fist was closed, and protruding between the fingers, stiffened in place by thirty years of freeze-dry, was a scrap of fabric. Green wool. Torn.

Green. The color of the jacket my father actually wore.

"He was holding onto it," I said. "When he died. He was holding a piece of my father's sleeve."

Felix said nothing. Which from Felix is a paragraph.

*

We made camp an hour further on, in the lee of a lateral moraine, because neither of us could stay near the ice face and neither of us suggested moving farther than an hour. Some distances are honest. An hour is honest.

I couldn't eat. Felix ate for both of us without comment, which I have learned is their love language: logistics performed tenderly.

"My mother kept his spare jacket after he left," I said, eventually, to the fire. "Hung it in the hall closet for years. Like a coat waiting for weather. She never wore it. Never threw it out either. When she died I found it folded in a box with his maps." I turned my mug in my hands. "I used to think she was keeping it in case he came back. Then I thought she was keeping it as evidence. Now I think she just didn't know what else you do with a thing that belonged to a question."

Felix fed a stick to the fire. "You thought he might be up here. All these years. That's why the map mattered."

"I thought he might be anywhere. That's worse. At least the glacier keeps its appointments." I looked up toward the icefall, blue-black in the dusk, seracs stacked like a bad idea. "Thirty years I've imagined finding him. Every version. Alive in some valley town with a new name and a good reason. Frozen on the route with his notebook full of answers. I rehearsed it so many times I thought I was ready for any ending."

"And now?"

"Now I find a stranger wearing his coat, holding his sleeve, and I don't know which ending this is. It's not closure. It's—" I searched for it. "It's a door opening onto another door. The dead are closer than the legend said. Legend says they vanished. They didn't vanish. They're *here*. They've been here the whole time, ten meters of ice away, and the ice has been reading them to itself like bedtime stories."

Felix was quiet a moment. Then, in the tone they use for facts they'd rather not be facts: "The tag said Voss, E. Your father is Erik Voss."

"Yes."

"The rival journal—the one Alder's family wants buried. The financier who was murdered. Maren, what was his name?"

I had told them once, weeks ago, in a diner, in the flat voice I use for the parts of the story I've told too many times. I told them again now and watched my own mouth say it like it belonged to someone else.

"Edmund," I said. "Edmund Hale."

Felix nodded slowly, and I saw them file it somewhere deep, next to the carved stone and their father's mark and every other piece of this ledger they carry without showing me the balance.

"Then whoever's in that ice," they said, "died close enough to Edmund Hale to matter. And close enough to your father to wear his coat home."

*

I slept maybe two hours. In my dreams the glacier spoke, but not in words—in the way ice speaks, that deep structural groan like a cathedral clearing its throat, and in the dream I understood it perfectly and woke having lost the translation.

Before dawn I got up and dug the spare journal out of my pack—not my father's; mine, the one I've kept since I was nineteen, part diary and part case file, thirty years of interviews, letters, dead ends, and grudges written in increasingly furious handwriting. I read my father's last letter again by headlamp, the one that reached us eight months after the expedition, the one that made my mother go white and fold it into her recipe box like contraband.

*I have to stay on the mountain a while longer. There is something at the source that changes what we owe each other. If I don't come back, don't let the family go looking. Tell them the mountain collected its admission and let that be the end of it.*

Admission. The cave takes payment, the town says. The glacier collects, my father wrote. Everyone in this story talks like the land is running a till, and everyone keeps paying, and nobody will show me the receipt.

Except now there was a receipt. Lying in the ice with my name on it.

Felix stirred across the fire. They sleep like a sentry—all ears, one eye available. "You should rest."

"So should you."

"Different budgets." They sat up, pulled their jacket on, and looked east, where the sky was doing its gray-to-pink negotiation. "Today we make the gorge narrows. Tomorrow, the icefall. The window's still open."

"The window," I repeated. "Three men went into that window in 1987 and one of them came out wearing a different life. Another one just came out of the ice wearing my father's coat. And we're walking in anyway."

"Yes," Felix said simply. Because Felix does not dress decisions in costume. It's one of the few things about them I'd call honest without qualification.

I closed my own journal. But before I packed it, I tore a page—blank, clean—and wrote on it the dead man's tag information, the jacket, the patch, the green wool in his fist. Date, coordinates, conditions. Then, underneath, because thirty years of hunting teaches you that records are the only prayers that get answered:

*He was found. Whoever you are: you were found.*

We broke camp as the sun cleared the ridge and lit the glacier up like something freshly forged. Ahead, the route narrowed into the gorge where the river ran wrong, and beyond that, the icefall, and beyond that, the hanging valley, and beyond that, whatever my father owed and Edmund Hale paid.

Behind us, the ice settled with a sound like a book closing.

I didn't look back. But I felt it—the whole frozen massif at my shoulders, patient as a creditor, holding its dead and its documents in the same blue vault, waiting to see whether we'd come to read them or to burn them.

One day behind us, I knew, came Alder Voss, carrying his family's verdicts. Ahead of us, sealed in the source, waited the reason for every one of them.

And in between, in my pocket, rode a scrap of tracing paper on which I'd sketched the dead man's clenched fist—because I'd remembered something in the night, something that had sat up in my chest at 3 a.m. and refused to lie back down.

In the photographs, in the gap between the frozen fingers, visible only at the right angle, in the raking dawn light Felix had caught by accident—

There was writing. Tiny, cramped, ink-on-paper, folded small enough to hide in a hand.

A note. He'd died holding a note.

And I would have bet my father's maps, my mother's recipe box, and every year I have left that I knew whose handwriting it was in.
