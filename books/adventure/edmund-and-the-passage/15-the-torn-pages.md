## 15. The Torn Pages

My father had exactly three habits that I know of, and I inherited two of them.

The first: he never wrote anything important on the first page of anything. First pages get torn out, soaked, traded, burned. Important things went deep—page nine of a fourteen-page letter, the back half of a notebook, the inside cover of a book he'd never read. I used to think it was paranoia. Then I grew up and got paranoid myself, and understood it was just bookkeeping.

The second: he numbered his pages in pencil, lightly, in the top right corner, and he always numbered them *before* he wrote on them. Which means if pages went missing, the gaps had numbers. A man who numbers blank pages in advance is a man planning for his own erasure.

The third habit—the one I did *not* inherit, or maybe the one I inherited hardest—he folded paper the same way every time. Thirds, then in half, fold-side out, so the creases took the abuse and the writing stayed on the inside. I have found his field sketches in landfill strata, in a gull's nest on Svalbard, once in the lining of a coat I bought secondhand in Tromsø, and every one of them was folded in thirds, fold-side out, like a little paper spine.

I'm telling you this because at 3 a.m. in a wrecked basecamp at the foot of a glacier, with Maren asleep ten feet away and the dead man's note sketched on tracing paper in my pocket, I stopped trying to sleep and started doing arithmetic.

The journal in my lap was water-damaged, swollen, its cover furred with mold the color of old bruises. Forty-one numbered pages survived. My father's hand marched through them—site notes, ice measurements, supply tallies, the occasional margin doodle of a ptarmigan looking judgmental—and then, between page 31 and page 32, the numbers jumped.

Pages 32 through 38 were gone. Torn out. Not ripped in panic—*removed*. The stubs were clean at the gutter, which meant they'd been taken with a blade or a very patient thumb, and the remaining stub edges had been pressed flat. Someone had wanted the absence to look like nothing.

Seven pages. And I knew—knew the way you know a chord is wrong—exactly what had been on them, because I knew how my father wrote a confession.

He'd have written it in thirds.

* * *

Here is the thing about being raised by Edmund Nakamura: you don't get bedtime stories, you get *procedures*. How to cache food against bears. How to read a glacier's surface for crevasse shadows. How to fold a letter so it survives a pocket, a river, and a search. I spent my childhood learning a man's habits instead of his heart, and for twenty years I'd called that a waste.

At 3:14 a.m., by the blue-white spill of my headlamp, I called it an inheritance.

Because if you fold a page in thirds, fold-side out, and then tear it out of a binding, the tear doesn't come clean. The fold traps the paper. You get a stub, yes—but you also get *fragments*. Little crescents of paper still glued into the gutter of the binding, on the fold lines, where the blade couldn't reach without gutting the whole notebook.

I got out my knife—the good one, the one I'd bought with midden money in a town whose name I'd already forgotten—and I opened the journal's spine as gently as I'd ever opened anything. Which, given my line of work, is saying something. I've excavated infant burials with a pastry brush. I have patience the way other people have allergies: total, involuntary, lifelong.

The stubs of pages 32 and 33 gave up their crescents first. Slivers of paper, water-stained, the size of eyelids. I laid them on my knee under the lamp and tilted the light raking, the way Felix-the-photographer had caught the dead man's fist at the right angle, and there it was—not words, not yet, but *strokes*. The ghost of handwriting, embossed into the page beneath by a man who pressed too hard when he was scared. My father always pressed too hard when he was scared. I know because I do it too, and because I once found a letter he'd written my mother with the next three pages' worth of his sentences embossed backward into the envelope.

I sat there in the ruin of the 1987 expedition, reading the negative space of my father's fear.

* * *

Maren woke at dawn, the way she always did—no transition, just eyes open and hand already finding the knife by her bedroll. She watched me work for a while before she said anything, which I'd come to understand was her version of good morning.

"You've been up all night."

"I've been up since the ice showed us a dead man holding a note," I said. "Sleep seemed presumptuous."

"That's the body in the glacier. My father's spare jacket."

"I know. I'm not working on the jacket." I turned the journal toward her, careful not to smudge the stubs. "I'm working on what's missing from it."

She crouched, and I watched her read the page numbers the way she read everything—like a ledger, like an accusation waiting to be itemized. "Seven pages."

"Pages 32 through 38. Torn out cleanly, pressed flat, made to look like nothing." I picked up one of the paper crescents with the tip of my knife. "But he folded his pages in thirds before he wrote the important things. Always did. So when the pages came out, the folds stayed behind." I tilted the light again. "And he pressed hard."

Maren was quiet a moment. "You can read that?"

"I can read *him*. It's not the same thing, but it's what I've got." I'd spent the night doing exactly that—reading him, the embossed strokes, the direction of the pen, the pressure. I'd reconstructed maybe forty words so far, and they were the wrong forty words, the ones that made my hands shake, so I hadn't shown her yet. "There's more. The note in the dead man's hand—the one in the photographs, folded small. If my father wrote it, he folded it in thirds, fold-side out. Which means when the fingers relaxed—" I stopped. "Which means when the ice gave him up, the fold lines were the outside. Exposed. That's why the ink survived at all."

"You want to go back to the body."

"I want the note. Yes."

Maren looked at me for a long time. There was frost on her eyelashes and she didn't brush it off. "That's my father's jacket on that body, Felix. Or it was his spare, which is its own kind of message. Whatever that note says, I've been waiting thirty years for it, and I want you to understand that if you go down there and come back up and lie to me about what it says, I will—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. "I won't finish that sentence. But you should know I was going to somewhere bad."

"I know," I said. "I've been somewhere bad myself. It's crowded this time of year."

* * *

We went down to the ice edge together in the flat gray light that glacier people call daytime, roped together because Maren insisted and because I'd stopped pretending I didn't want her to insist. The body lay where the melt had freed it, one arm out, the fist still half-clenched around its paper secret, frost riming the sleeve of a jacket that had once hung in a tent forty meters from where we'd slept.

I'm not going to describe the body. Not because it was terrible—it was, but the ice had been almost gentle, the way it is with everything it takes—and not because I'm squeamish. I'm going to skip it because it wasn't mine to describe. That was Maren's father's spare jacket, and the man wearing it had a name I didn't yet know, and some things you don't narrate over someone else's grief.

What I will tell you is what I took.

The note came free of the fist with a sound like a match striking. Folded in thirds, fold-side out, ink on the inside, just as advertised. My father's habits, holding the line thirty-five years after he'd walked out of my life. I stood there on the ice holding a dead man's last words in a dead man's grip and thought: *this is what you taught me instead of teaching me to fish. This. Paper-folding. Was it worth it?*

Then I opened it, and stopped asking.

* * *

The note was short. My father's hand, cramped, pressed hard, the letters leaning like people in wind:

*E. is dead. Not the ice. H. did it — I saw the camp after. The proof is at the source, sealed in the blue chamber, where I put it. I am taking the pages out of the book so no one reads them here. If they find the note they find nothing. The boy is not part of this. — E.N.*

I read it three times. Then I sat down on the ice, because my legs had filed a formal complaint.

Maren read it once. She read the way she did everything—fast, total, no second pass needed. Then she said, very quietly, "H. Who is H?"

"I don't know." That was a lie, or half of one, and she heard it, because she heard everything. I tried again. "I know who it isn't. It isn't my father. Whatever H. did, my father spent seven notebook pages making sure it stayed buried, and then he spent the rest of his life making sure *I* stayed away from it. That's not a man hiding a crime. That's a man hiding a *witness*."

"You."

"Me." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "He wrote 'the boy is not part of this' on a note he sealed into a dead man's fist at the bottom of a glacier. You know what that means? It means at some point he thought I *might be*. Part of this. Part of the price."

Maren crouched next to me. She didn't touch me. She was learning, slowly, the geography of where I let people stand. "And the pages. The seven he took out."

"I've been reconstructing them." I pulled the journal back out, showed her the crescents, the embossed ghosts. "It's slow. But I know his habits. He numbered first, he folded in thirds, he pressed hard when he was scared, and he never put the important part where you'd expect it. He'd have written the confession in the middle of a supply tally. He'd have hidden the murder inside an inventory." I tapped page 31, the last surviving page before the gap. It was, of all things, a rope count. *11 × 45m, 2 × 30m, 1 × 20m (cut), carabiners: 34.* "Look at the last line."

She looked. "*1 × 20m (cut)*."

"Thirty-five years I assumed he cut a rope in some rescue. Now I want to know what a cut rope was for." I turned to the crescents on my knee, the ghost-strokes, and read her the forty words I'd spent the night coaxing out of negative space. My voice did something I didn't authorize it to do. I kept going anyway.

The fragments said: *...he was alive when we... the crevasse was not... H. said the family... the source, the blue chamber, sealed... I am the one who buried it. Not for him. For the name. God forgive the arithmetic.*

"For the name," Maren repeated.

"For a name," I said. "Whose?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. The whole night's arithmetic came down to it. My father hadn't buried a treasure. He'd buried *evidence*—evidence that someone with an H. in his name had killed a man at the foot of this glacier in 1987, and my father had decided that the price of survival was silence, itemized: seven pages torn out, one rope cut, one note sealed in a dead man's hand, one child raised on procedures instead of stories, one family name protected at the cost of every name attached to it.

I looked up at the glacier, the blue-white wall of it, the source somewhere above us in its sealed chamber, holding whatever my father had hidden there like a bank holds a debt.

"Maren," I said. "The expedition had a financier. Somebody paid for all this." I gestured at the wrecked camp, the rusted lanterns, the tagged crates. "Somebody who could afford to make a murder look like weather. Who paid for your father's expedition?"

She'd gone very still. Stillness, on Maren, was louder than shouting.

"My grandfather," she said. "Harald Voss."

*H.*

The wind came down off the ice, and neither of us said anything for a long time, because there are sentences that you can feel rearranging the furniture of your whole life, and you let them finish before you turn the lights on.

The family that collected. The family that called its violence justice. The family whose marks were cut into the crates of this camp, whose enforcer was a day behind us on the trail, whose daughter was standing next to me holding a dead man's note in her shaking hands.

The H. had been in the family all along.
