## 16. Whose Justice

I did the arithmetic on the glacier's shoulder, in the blue hour before dawn, with my hands too cold to hold a pencil and my mind too hot to sleep.

It was simple arithmetic. That was the obscene part.

The expedition of 1987 had been financed by Halvard Voss—my grandfather, the man whose portrait hung in the family hall at Vinterbruk, whose name was carved into the lintel above the door like a benediction. The expedition had found something at the source worth killing for. My father had died for it, or near enough. And the family—the family that had spent thirty years hunting Felix Nakamura across half a country, the family that called its violence justice, the family whose enforcer had once held a knife to my throat and called it *procedure*—that family was Halvard Voss's family. His heirs. Us.

Which meant the justice we collected was not justice. It was housekeeping.

I sat with that while the light came up gray over the ice, and I turned it over the way you turn a stone to see what lives underneath, and every time I looked I found the same thing: the murder of Edmund Rask had been ordered by my grandfather, witnessed by Felix's father, and buried by both men—one in the ground, one in silence—and the family's thirty-year vendetta against the Nakamura name was not grief wearing armor. It was the armor itself. It was how you kept everyone from ever looking beneath.

We hadn't been hunting our father's killer. We'd been hunting anyone who might remember him.

* * *

Felix woke the way Felix always woke: all at once, hands first, like a diver surfacing. One moment asleep in the lee of the boulder, the next sitting up, scanning the slope, checking the rope, checking me. A person who has been running since childhood does not wake into the world gently. They arrive armed.

"You've been up all night," Felix said. Not a question. Felix read people the way I'd seen them read soil—a glance, a pressure test, a verdict.

"I've been thinking."

"That's worse." They reached for the stove, lit it, set water on. Small competent motions. "Tell me."

So I told them. I laid it out flat, no decoration, because I'd learned by then that Felix trusted plain speech the way other people trusted scripture. Grandfather financed the expedition. Edmund Rask found something—or knew something—that threatened him. Rask died on the glacier. Father's letter named Nakamura as witness, not killer. And the family's justice, the whole apparatus of it, the marks on doors and the trials in back rooms and Alder himself with his terrible gentle voice—all of it existed to keep the truth underground. We were not avengers. We were groundskeepers.

Felix listened without moving. When I finished they poured the hot water over the coffee grounds and said, quietly, "I know."

"You *know*?"

"I figured it out at the basecamp. Chapter of my own." They wrapped their hands around the cup. "Your father's letter named mine as witness. Witnesses don't get hunted for thirty years unless somebody needs them silent. And nobody spends that kind of money and blood on silence unless the thing being silenced is theirs." They blew on the coffee. "I just didn't have the last piece until you gave it to me. That it was your grandfather. Your kin."

"And now you have it," I said. "And now what?"

"Now nothing changes on this mountain. We go up, we reach the source, we find what's there."

"And then?" I heard my own voice climb. "Then what, Felix? Because I've spent thirty years building toward an ending, and every version of the ending I ever imagined had the family in it somewhere. Standing corrected. Standing ashamed. Standing *something*. If the family is the murderer—if the justice is the crime—then there is no family to face. There's only us, facing ourselves."

Felix looked at me over the rim of the cup for a long moment. Then they said, "Or there's a choice."

* * *

Here is what I will say about arguing with Felix Nakamura: they do not raise their voice. They lower it. Every point they want to land comes quieter than the last, until you find yourself leaning in, leaning so far into your own anger that you fall into their calm, and then you're arguing with yourself and losing.

"Two options," they said. "You take what's at the source—the documents, the proof, whatever survived—and you carry it down. You put it in front of the family, the law, the newspapers, whoever will look. You burn the whole rotten structure to the ground. Halvard Voss's name comes off the lintel. Alder goes to trial or goes to ruin. Everyone who built the lie watches it die in public."

"Yes," I said. "That's the option. That's the only option."

"The other option is your father's option." Felix's voice went almost soft. "He knew. He knew what your grandfather did, he knew why his friends vanished, and he chose to bury it. He took the evidence—" they stopped, corrected themselves, "—or some of it, or knowledge of it, and he sealed it away, and he let the legend grow around the cave like scar tissue. Admission, the locals call it. Payment. Maybe that was him. Maybe the story was the lock."

"He let murderers keep their name."

"He let a daughter keep her family." Felix set the cup down. "That's not the same sentence, Maren. I know it looks like the same sentence from where you're standing. But I grew up inside what happens when someone decides the truth matters more than the people holding it. My father chose the truth, in his way—he carried it, he protected it, he wouldn't sell it—and it cost him everything. It cost me everything. I was eight years old when I learned that a secret can be a roof over a house or a fire in it, and the same secret can be both."

"That's cowardice dressed as mercy."

"Maybe." Felix didn't flinch. "But it was *his* mercy, paid for with *his* life, and you don't get to spend it cheap from thirty years' distance. Neither do I."

* * *

We argued through the morning. We broke camp mid-argument, which meant we argued while roping up, while crossing the moraine, while climbing the ice ramp where the glacier's tongue met the valley wall, and the mountain did not care about either of us, which was somehow a relief.

"You want to bury it again," I said, picking my way over blue ice gone rough as concrete. "Another generation, another seal. You want to be your father."

"No." Felix planted their axe, waited for me. "I want to be the person who decides *deliberately*, instead of the person who decides out of rage. Rage is just another kind of running. Believe me. I'm an expert."

"My father is under that ice."

"I know."

"Edmund Rask is under that ice, and my grandfather put him there, and my family spent three decades sharpening knives over his grave and calling it mourning."

"I *know*, Maren." And there it was—not the calm, but under the calm, the thing Felix kept sheathed: fury, old and worn smooth from handling. "You think I don't want it? You think I haven't rehearsed this? I've had thirty years to imagine handing my father's enemies their reckoning. I used to do it falling asleep. Whole courtrooms in my head, and I always won. But every courtroom I ever built fell apart the same way yours just did—because the verdict doesn't bring anyone back, and the people who pay are never the people who decided. Alder didn't kill your father. Alder was a child. You were a child. I was a child. The man who decided is dead, and the ice is the only prison left that holds him, and you want to dig him up so the world can watch him rot."

"Yes," I said. "Yes. That's exactly what I want."

"And what does it cost?"

"Nothing! It costs the guilty!"

"It costs *you*." Felix stepped close on the narrow ramp, close enough that I could see the windburn crack their lips. "It costs you the version of your father you've carried for thirty years—the good one, the honest one, the one who wrote letters and loved you. Because if you drag this into the light, you drag him into it too. The man who watched a murder and stayed silent. The man who helped bury it. Is he a victim or an accomplice? Decide now, publicly, forever, because the world won't let him be both."

I had no answer. I stood on the ice with no answer, and the wind took my breath and gave it back colder.

* * *

We made camp early, in the shadow of a serac that groaned occasionally to itself like an old man in his sleep. Neither of us spoke through dinner. The silence between us had changed texture—it wasn't the wary silence of two strangers sharing a rope anymore. It was the silence of two people who had said true things to each other and couldn't unsay them.

That's the trouble with honesty. It's a door that opens both ways, and once it's open you can see everything in the room, including the things you liked better in the dark.

I watched Felix check the ropes by headlamp, coil after coil, fingers moving with that economy that made my chest ache in a way I refused to examine. And I thought: *this is the person I would follow onto a glacier. This is the person I already followed onto a glacier.* And this same person was asking me to consider letting my father's killers rest untried, and part of me wanted to agree simply because agreeing would keep us whole, and I hated myself for wanting that, and I hated Felix a little for making me want it, and underneath all of it I understood—I understood with a clarity that felt like falling—that Felix was not wrong about the cost. They were only wrong about who should pay it.

Because here is what I kept coming back to, turning it over like the stone it was: my father chose burial. He chose it for me. He wrote his letter, hid his knowledge, and died—or vanished—with the secret intact so that I could grow up in a family instead of a courtroom. And I had spent thirty years dishonoring that choice, digging at it, refusing it, dragging the past up the mountain behind me like a sled I couldn't unload. At what point did honoring the dead become overriding them?

At what point did my duty to the truth become a violence done to everyone the truth would wound?

"You're doing it again," Felix said.

"Doing what?"

"Deciding alone. I can hear it from here. You've got that face—you look like your father's photographs, did you know that? Same jaw set. Same stubbornness going off in the eyes like a fuse."

"Don't," I said. "Don't tell me what my own father looked like."

"Why not? You've spent this whole trip telling me what mine did." Felix set down the rope and came and sat across the stove from me, close, in the small circle of heat. "Maren. Listen to me. I'm not asking you to bury it. I'm asking you to wait until we've seen it. Whatever's at the source—we don't even know yet what form it takes. Documents rot. Ice preserves. Maybe there's nothing left to expose and the choice makes itself. Maybe there's everything, and we make it together, on the ice, looking at the actual thing instead of the idea of it. Can you give me that much? Not surrender. Just—sequence."

I looked at them across the flame. Wind moved the tent fabric. Somewhere below us, deep in the glacier, the river ran its wrong-way run toward the sea, patient as a debt.

"Sequence," I said finally. "Fine. We look first. We decide together. But Felix—if it's there, if the proof survives, I don't promise to bury it. I can't promise that. My father's silence bought thirty years of more silence and it bought Alder with his knife and it bought a town full of mothers who still set places at tables for people who aren't coming home. Silence has had its turn. It's had three decades and it produced nothing but a machine for hurting people."

Felix nodded slowly. "Okay. Then here's my term. If we open it—if we decide to open it—we open it *all* the way. Not just grandfather. Not just the murder. Your father's part in it. Mine. The whole ledger, every entry, ugly lines included. No curated truth. If we're going to burn it down, we burn it down to the foundation and stand in the ashes and let everyone see what was actually there."

"Even the parts that shame us."

"*Especially* those. Otherwise it's just revenge with better lighting."

I put out my hand, because I am my father's daughter and we shook on things in my family, and Felix took it, and their grip was warm and callused and steady, and I held on maybe a second longer than a bargain required.

"One more thing," Felix said, not letting go. "Alder is a day behind us, maybe less. When he comes—and he'll come—he'll offer you the family's version of this exact choice. Mercy if you comply, trial if you don't. And you need to decide, before you see his face, whether the family still has any claim on you. Because if the justice is the crime, then obeying it isn't loyalty. It's collaboration."

The words went into me like a splinter, and I knew I would carry them the rest of the way up the mountain.

* * *

I slept badly and woke before dawn to a sound I couldn't place—too deep for wind, too rhythmic for ice. I crawled out of the tent into the gray light and stood on the glacier's shoulder listening, and it came again: a vast muffled concussion rolling up from the direction of the source, felt in the soles of my boots before it reached my ears, like the heartbeat of something enormous turning over in its sleep.

The glacier was talking. And beneath the sound, faint but unmistakable, carried up the wrong-way river's hidden channel from miles below—from the coast, from the fishing town, from the cave under the headland—came something else. A bell. Thin and human and repeated.

Someone was ringing the calving bell a day early.

Or someone had reached the icefall ahead of us, and wanted us to know the window was closing—and that we were no longer the only ones climbing toward the source.
