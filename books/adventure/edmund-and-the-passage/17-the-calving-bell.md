## 17. The Calving Bell

The sound woke me before I understood it.

Not a sound, exactly—a *pressure*, the way you feel a door slam three rooms away in your chest rather than your ears. We were camped on a shelf of grey rock above the gorge, Maren and I, our tents pitched at the angle of people who expect to be leaving soon and don't want to admit it by making anything comfortable. The sky was doing that high-latitude thing where dawn doesn't so much arrive as seep, grey into grey, until you can't say when the day started because it never properly did.

Then it came again. A crack, long and deep and resonant, rolling down the valley like a bell struck in a cathedral made of ice—which is exactly what it was, though I didn't know that yet. I only knew that every hair on my arms stood up, and that the sound had a *voice* to it, a note that hung in the air after the crack itself had finished, the way a bell keeps singing after the hammer falls.

Maren was already out of her tent. She hadn't even bothered with her boots all the way on—one heel crushed flat, which told me she'd come out fast. She stood facing up-valley, toward the white wall at the head of the gorge where the Vinter Glacier's tongue came down to meet the world, and she was smiling.

I hated that smile immediately. It was the smile of someone whose worst fear has just confirmed itself as a schedule.

"That's it," she said. "That's the season opening."

"It sounded more like something closing."

"Same thing, out here." She crouched and pressed her palm flat against the rock, as if she could take its pulse. "Calving. The glacier's shedding weight off the face. When it does that, the meltwater channels open up—the pressure drops, the routes through the tongue clear out. My father's letter called it 'the bell.' He said when you hear it, you have days. Not weeks. Days."

"How many days?"

She looked at me with the particular patience of a person who has already done this math several times and is tired of carrying the answer around alone. "He wrote 'three, if the weather holds.' The weather hasn't held once since we started."

I did my own version of the math, which is less about numbers and more about inventory. Rope: enough, barely. Food: four days if we ate like monks, two if we ate like us. Alder Voss: one day behind, maybe less, moving with the confidence of a man who believes God audits his expenses.

"So we go now," I said. "Right now. Cold breakfast."

"Cold breakfast," she agreed, and for once neither of us argued about who deserved hot food more, which should tell you how scared we both were.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about being chased by your own deadline: the glacier doesn't hurry. That's the obscene part. The bell rings, the clock starts, and the ice itself goes on groaning and settling and dripping at exactly the pace it has kept for ten thousand years, utterly indifferent to whether two exhausted people make it through its front door or get locked outside for another decade.

We climbed. The gorge narrowed the way my father's journal—what was left of it—said it would, walls closing from a valley to a corridor to something closer to a throat. The river ran beneath us, visible through gaps in the stone, and it was running wrong, of course. Running uphill in places, or seeming to, the water climbing toward the glacier instead of away from it, feeding backward toward the source like a dog trotting home. I'd stopped trying to explain it. Some lies the land tells are just true things wearing costumes.

"Talk to me," Maren said somewhere past noon. Not a request. An instruction.

"About what?"

"Anything. You've been quiet since the bell." She picked her way along a ledge ahead of me, testing each hold with her axe before trusting it. "You get quiet when you're calculating, and I want to know what the numbers are."

"The numbers are that we're slow," I said. "I'm slow. My ankle's been lying to me since the crossing and I've been believing it because the alternative was admitting we needed a rest day we don't have."

"We're not taking a rest day."

"I know. I'm just saying the ankle knows it too, and it's resentful."

That got half a laugh out of her, which was the point. You learn things about people on rope, and one of the things I'd learned about Maren Voss was that her humor lived right next door to her terror, sharing a wall, and if you knocked politely one of them would answer and you couldn't always choose which. Thirty years she'd spent hunting this. Thirty years, and now the door was finally open and also closing, and the man who might have answers—or might have a knife—was a day behind us, and her whole life had compressed into a window measured in meltwater.

We rested anyway. Twenty minutes, no fire, backs against stone, chewing food that tasted like the idea of food. I wrapped my ankle tighter and lied to it about how much I appreciated its service.

"Felix." Maren was looking back down the gorge, the way we'd come. "Look."

I looked. Far below, at the edge of visibility, at the place where the gorge opened back into the lower valley, there was movement. Small. Deliberate. Two figures, maybe three, moving with the unhurried economy of people who know exactly where they're going and have decided everyone else will simply have to be there when they arrive.

"Alder," I said.

"And he's brought friends." Maren's voice had gone flat, which was worse than angry. "He wasn't traveling alone at the icefall either. The family doesn't send one man to do a congregation's work."

One day behind. Maybe less now, if they traveled light and slept cold, and they would, because Alder Voss struck me as a man who considered comfort a form of corruption. Which meant our lead wasn't a day. It was hours, and shrinking, and the glacier above us was ringing its bell like it enjoyed the irony.

"Then we stop being careful," I said, and stood up, and put weight on the ankle to see if it would file a formal complaint. It grumbled but held. Good enough. Everything out here was good enough or nothing at all.

* * *

The glacier's tongue appeared in the late afternoon, and I'm not going to pretend I was ready for it.

You hear "glacier" and you think white. This wasn't white. It was blue—the deep, bruised, submarine blue of ice under pressure, veined with grey moraine like marble, cracked and crevassed and folded in ways that suggested the ice had been arguing with itself for centuries and losing. It came down out of the hanging valley in a frozen waterfall, a torrent stopped mid-fall, and at its base, where it met the rock of the gorge, there was an arch.

An arch of ice, tall as a church door, glowing faintly from within, and out of its base ran the stream—the same stream, impossibly, that flowed beneath the fishing town and surfaced uphill and ran wrong all the way here. The water came out of the glacier clear as glass and cold enough to hurt, and it sang as it went, a thin constant note underneath everything else, like the cathedral had an organist who never stopped practicing.

"There it is," Maren whispered. She had her father's last letter out of its oilskin wrap, holding it like a hymnal. "The mouth. He described it exactly. 'A door in the ice, and the river keeping time.'"

"He had a flair."

"He had a daughter who was six when he wrote it." She folded the letter away with hands that weren't quite steady. "Everything he wrote, he wrote for me to find someday. Even the parts that were really confessions."

I didn't say anything to that. There's a limit to how much honesty two people can metabolize per day, and we'd hit ours somewhere around the second switchback.

We made camp within sight of the arch—not too close; the ice calved in pieces the size of buses and dropped them without apology—and I took first watch while Maren slept, actually slept, the deep dreamless sleep of the righteous and the obsessed. I sat with my back against warm stone and watched the glacier breathe.

Because it did breathe. That was the thing the photographs never captured. The ice moved. Imperceptibly, constantly, the whole mass flowing downhill at the speed of a very patient snail, and as it moved it groaned and ticked and rang, small bells answering the great one, the whole structure alive with stress and release. Every few minutes a serac would shed somewhere up high and the report would roll down the valley, softer now, echoes of echoes.

And behind us, far below, a light moved in the dark. Steady. Unhurried. Climbing.

I counted the hours between their light and our fire and did the arithmetic again, and the arithmetic said morning. Maybe earlier. Maybe they climbed through the dark, because Alder Voss had lost a teammate on the icefall—I knew that from Maren's network of informants, the scattered cousins and exiles who fed her news of the family—and men who lose people to their own decisions tend to stop sleeping.

I thought about waking Maren. I thought about pushing through the night, putting the arch between us and them, gambling everything on the route inside being passable in the dark. I thought about my father, who had stood somewhere near this spot thirty-odd years ago with blood on his conscience and pages torn out of his journal, and I wondered if he'd heard the bell too, and what he'd thought it meant. Judgment? Warning? Just weather?

Probably just weather. He was an archaeologist, not a mystic. But he'd carved his mark into a stone and left it in a sea cave like a man settling accounts, and you don't settle accounts unless you believe somebody's keeping books.

The bell rang again, far up in the dark, one long bronze note rolling down over the ice and the stone and the two of us, and I understood then that the sound wasn't a warning at all. Warnings are for things you can avoid. This was a statement of terms. *The window is open,* the glacier said. *It will not stay open. Come in or don't. I keep what I keep.*

"Morning," I said to no one, to the ice, to the light climbing patiently through the dark below. "Come on, then. Everyone's invited. Nobody gets out free."

I woke Maren an hour before dawn. Her eyes opened all the way, instantly, no fog, and when I pointed at the arch—now a black shape against the faintly luminous ice, the stream whispering out of it like a secret being told very fast—she nodded once and started breaking camp.

Behind us, down in the gorge, the light had stopped moving.

They were close enough to see us leave. Close enough to follow. And ahead of us, through that impossible blue door, lay whatever my father had buried, whatever her father had died beside, and the source of a river that ran wrong through a town full of people who still paid the cave its admission in stories.

The bell rang a third time as we stepped onto the ice.

It sounded, I thought, almost pleased.
