## 18. The Hanging Valley

The mountain did not want us there. I want to be clear about that from the start, because everything that happened afterward—the storm, the crevasse, the journal—has a way of feeling like fate in retrospect, and I refuse to let the mountain off the hook that easily. It was not fate. It was weather and bad rock and my own stubbornness, which has never once been mistaken for a virtue.

We climbed out of the gorge at first light, if you can call it light. The sky had the color of old dishwater, and the wind came down the glacier's tongue with that low, patient moan that ice makes when it wants you to know it's been waiting longer than you've been alive. Felix went first on the rope, because Felix always went first on anything technical, and I had stopped pretending this bothered me somewhere around the third day of knowing them.

"Loose rock," Felix called back, without turning around. "Everything's loose rock. Step where I step and hate your life quietly."

"I never do anything loudly," I said. "It's my defining trait."

"You're doing this entire expedition loudly. In your head. I can hear it."

They weren't wrong. Thirty years of wanting this—of chasing my father's last letter through archives and death certificates and the polite evasions of people who knew more than they said—and now the hanging valley was actually above us, a green notch in the gray wall where the river emerged from under the ice as if being born fully grown. My father had described it in 1987: *the water comes out of the mountain already old.* I'd read that sentence so many times I'd worn grooves in it. Now I could see the truth of it with my own eyes—a river pouring from a blue-black arch in the glacier's flank, cold and certain, going about its business as though thirty years of missing climbers were nothing to it.

Which, of course, they weren't. That was the thing about this landscape that I was only beginning to understand: it kept no grudges because it kept no records. It just erased. Slowly, thoroughly, without malice. The family called its violence justice, but the glacier had been committing the same crime forever and nobody had ever thought to convene a trial for it.

* * *

The ascent took us eleven hours. It should have taken six.

The problem was the moraine—or rather, the problem was that the moraine had spent three decades rearranging itself into a landscape designed by something with a grudge against bipeds. Boulders the size of cars balanced on scree that ran like water whenever we trusted it. Twice Felix had to brace a slope while I crossed, their whole body wedged against a slab the size of a door, face pressed to stone, saying nothing, doing the work. I'd watched them do this before—at the gorge crossing, at the basecamp ruins—but watching didn't dull it. There is a particular kind of awe in seeing someone handle the earth like it's a language they're fluent in while you're still sounding out the alphabet.

My father had been a good climber. Everyone said so. But he'd been a good climber the way Alder was a good judge of character—technically excellent, and wrong about the things that mattered. He'd trusted the mountain. Felix didn't trust anything, least of all the ground, and that was why Felix was still alive at hour nine when I was running on fumes and spite.

"We should consider," I said, hauling myself over a chockstone, "that my father may have exaggerated how easy this route was."

"He didn't exaggerate." Felix crouched, reading the slope ahead the way other people read newspapers. "Routes change. Ice pulls back, rock lets go. What he climbed in '87 isn't here anymore. We're climbing its ghost."

"That's either very poetic or very grim."

"It's both. Everything true is."

I was learning to hear what Felix meant underneath what Felix said. Underneath this one: *we are behind schedule, and the sky is going to kill us.*

Because the sky was, in fact, going to try. The dishwater had curdled into something darker over the western peaks, and the wind had picked up a new note—not the patient moan anymore but a sharper edge, the sound of air being shoved somewhere it didn't want to go. Storm. Not maybe-storm. Coming-storm, the kind that in this country didn't mean inconvenience, it meant the difference between a story you told later and a name on a memorial.

Felix looked at the cloud bank, then at me, and I watched them make the calculation I would have made an hour too late.

"There'll be shelter in the valley," I said. "Dad's letter mentioned—"

"Dad's letter mentioned a route that doesn't exist anymore." They said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "We don't make the valley before it hits. We need a hole in the ground."

Nobody who has never been roped to another person in failing light on a glacier can understand what those words do to you. A hole in the ground. On a glacier, holes in the ground are called crevasses, and crevasses are where the mountain keeps the people it doesn't intend to give back.

* * *

The storm arrived the way debt arrives—suddenly, and all at once, after a long time of pretending it wouldn't.

One minute we were crossing the glacier's lower tongue in gray half-light; the next, the world went white and sideways and loud. Snow didn't fall so much as arrive horizontally, with intent. Visibility collapsed to the length of the rope between us, then less. Felix moved by feel and by some internal instrument I couldn't see, probing with the axe, reading the snow's texture through their boots, and I followed the rope and hated every second of trusting someone else with my life, because trust was not a muscle I'd ever had reason to develop.

Then Felix stopped so abruptly I walked into them.

"Don't move," they said, very quietly, which in that wind meant shouting.

"What?"

"Bridge." They probed left. Probed right. Snow gave a hollow sound, a drum-skin sound, the sound of air under ice. "There's a crevasse under us. Big one. And—" they probed again, further right, and something in their posture changed, "—there's a way in. A ramp. The bridge is broken on one side but the far lip is exposed."

"You want to get *in* the crevasse."

"I want to not be on the surface when this storm finishes deciding what it thinks of us. Down there is out of the wind. Up here we're kindling." A pause. "Maren. I need you to trust me for ten minutes."

Here is the thing I have never admitted to anyone until writing this down: I almost said no. Thirty years of hunting the truth about my father's death had taught me exactly one lesson—that everyone lies eventually, that everyone's account serves themselves—and Felix Nakamura was the child of Edmund Nakamura, and every instinct I owned said *do not follow this person into a hole in the ice.*

But my hands were beyond feeling, and the wind had begun making a sound like the whole sky tearing along a seam, and Felix was standing in the whiteout looking back at me with an expression I recognized, God help me, from photographs of my father: the look of someone who knows exactly how much danger they're in and has decided to be calm about it anyway.

So I said, "Ten minutes," and followed them down into the dark.

* * *

The ramp was snow, compacted over years into a slope, and it delivered us onto a shelf inside the crevasse perhaps fifteen meters down. And I will say this for the mountain: it lied to us on the surface, but underground it kept its word. The wind became a rumor. The snow became a distant hiss. The blue light that filtered down through the bridge was the color of deep water, of church glass, of every cold thing I had ever feared, and it was, in that moment, the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, because it was still.

Felix got the stove going in the lee of an ice block, and we melted snow and ate without talking, because talking cost heat and heat was currency. My fingers came back to life in stages, each one reporting in separately, complaining. Somewhere above us the storm worked at the glacier's surface, planing it down, reshaping it, indifferent.

It was Felix who found the cache.

I heard them stop moving—they'd been pacing the shelf, the way they paced when their body needed work their mind could interrupt—and then a long silence, longer than the silence of tiredness.

"Maren." Their voice had changed. "Come look at this."

Against the crevasse wall, half-merged in refrozen meltwater, was a stack. A rucksack, its fabric gone brittle and gray with age. A coil of rope fused into a single solid loop. An aluminum pole, snapped. And beneath these, protected by a sheet of plastic that someone in 1987 had thought carefully about, a metal ammunition box, the kind expeditions used for documents, its latch crusted with ice but fundamentally intact.

Someone had hidden this here. Someone had expected it to be found—or expected it not to be, and hidden it well enough that only a storm and a broken bridge and two desperate people would ever reach it.

Felix looked at me. I looked at Felix. Neither of us touched it.

"Your father's expedition came through here," Felix said. "This is the route to the source."

"This is the route to the source," I agreed. My heart was doing something embarrassing against my ribs.

"And the family tagged the basecamp below. They searched everything. They went through his things with a fine-tooth comb—I've seen the justice-marks, they catalogued like bureaucrats." Felix's breath fogged in the blue light. "Whatever's in that box, they never found it."

"Then it belongs to whoever the dead wanted to have it," I said, "and I've been that person for thirty years."

I knelt. The latch fought me, then surrendered with a crack that echoed up the crevasse walls like a small gunshot. Inside, wrapped in a wool sock gone stiff with age: a journal. Leather cover, water-stained at the edges but dry at the heart. And on the flyleaf, in handwriting I did not recognize—smaller than my father's, neater, the hand of a man who wrote reports for a living—a name.

*Henrik Sørensen.*

I stared at it for a long time. Long enough that Felix crouched beside me and read it too, and I heard their breath catch.

"The financier," Felix said slowly. "The man who funded the '87 expedition. The rival. The one the family's whole story says died in a climbing accident before any of this started."

"The one whose death my father's letter called *not an accident* and then never explained." My voice sounded strange to me, flattened, careful, like walking on the snow-bridge itself. "Felix. This is his journal. This is the journal of the dead man. Hidden in the ice above the route to the source, in a place the family searched and missed."

"Or a place the family never thought to search," Felix said, "because the family already knew how he died. They wrote the ending first. You don't check the last chapter when you've already published the book."

I opened it with hands that shook, and I am not ashamed of that. Thirty years. Thirty years of my father's silence, of the family's verdicts, of a legend about a cave that charged admission—and here was a dead man's own voice, waiting in the cold, patient as the glacier that had kept him.

The early entries were ordinary. Supplies, weather, route notes, complaints about a colleague's snoring. Henrik Sørensen had been meticulous, dryly funny in the margins, human in the way the dead always turn out to be once you read enough of their words. I read fast, skimming toward the end, toward the dates around my father's disappearance—

And then I reached an entry dated six days before the expedition vanished, and the dry humor was gone, and the neat hand had pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the page beneath, and the first line was:

*I have to write this down somewhere no one from the family will ever look, because if I put it in my official log, I will not live to see it filed.*

Felix made a sound beside me, small and involuntary.

I turned the page. The storm hammered the glacier overhead, and the blue light held steady, and the dead man began, at last, to tell us what really happened in 1987—and the first name he wrote, in letters pressed deep enough to survive thirty years in the ice, was not my father's.

It was my grandfather's.
