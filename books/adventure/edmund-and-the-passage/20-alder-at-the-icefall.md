## 20. Alder at the Icefall

My grandfather used to say that justice was the only arithmetic that never lied. Add up what was taken, subtract what was returned, and whatever remained was the debt. Simple. Clean. He said it the way other men quoted scripture, and like scripture, nobody in the family ever asked him to show his work.

I thought about that arithmetic on the icefall, because there was nothing else to think about, and because thinking about anything softer would have been dangerous.

* * *

We'd left the gorge at first light, three of us: me, Brann Oduya, and little Sef Calloway, who was twenty-four and still believed the family's work was a calling rather than an inheritance he hadn't been consulted on. The trail up from the wrecked basecamp was the same one the 1987 expedition had cut, and thirty-eight years of weather had not improved it. The glacier's tongue had retreated since then, which meant the old route markers were stranded in absurd places—a wand planted in bare scree, a fixed line rusting into nothing above a drop that no longer existed. The mountain had rearranged its furniture and left the labels behind.

Sef read the terrain the way I'd taught him, which is to say cautiously, and Brann read it the way she always had, which is to say better than me. She'd been with the family eleven years. Before that she'd been a search-and-rescue climber in the Chugach, before that something she didn't talk about. She was the only person I knew who could look at a serac and see its biography.

"The icefall's moving," she said at midmorning, standing where the tongue began to break apart into the chaos of blocks and blue slots we'd have to thread. "You can hear it."

You could. A sound like a giant clearing his throat, deep under everything, patient. Glaciers don't hurry. They just keep their appointments.

"Then we move faster," I said.

Brann looked at me for a moment longer than the sentence deserved. Brann was good at that too—looking at you a beat past comfort, as if your words were a specimen she was checking for defects.

"And if it moves while we're on it?"

"Then we're on it," I said, "and it isn't."

She almost smiled. That was the thing about Brann; she kept her humor the way some people keep a knife, somewhere you could find it fast but not by accident.

* * *

Here is what I will not pretend: I knew the icefall was rotten. I'd stood below it the evening before and listened to it settle, counting the groans the way you count the gaps between a man's answers when you're deciding whether he's lying. Four groans in ten minutes, each one longer than the last. An honest icefall groans steadily, like a house in wind. This one was holding its breath between complaints, and things that hold their breath are storing something up.

The proper move was to rope the whole team and cross linked, one long unit, so that when the ice opened—and it would open—we'd have each other's weight to arrest the fall. That's what any guidebook says. That's what Brann would have said if I'd asked her outright instead of asking the sky.

But roping three people across a broken icefall means moving at the speed of the slowest knot, and every hour we spent threading those towers was an hour Felix Nakamura and Maren Voss spent ahead of us. They'd left the basecamp nearly a day before. They were two against our three, but they had something we didn't, which was desperation with a deadline attached. Calving season had begun. I'd heard the first great crack roll down the valley the previous afternoon, a sound like a cathedral bell dropped down a mineshaft, and after that sound the whole mountain changed its mind about what it was willing to hold.

So I did the arithmetic my grandfather would have approved of. Three roped climbers, slow, safe-ish. Three unroped climbers, fast, exposed. Weigh the risk of losing time against the risk of losing a man, and remember that the man volunteered, and remember that the debt we were collecting was thirty-eight years old and gathering interest.

I told myself it wasn't a choice between safety and speed. I told myself it was a choice between two kinds of responsibility: to the living men beside me, or to the dead who'd waited decades for someone to finish their accounting. My grandfather's creed has a genius for making itself sound like the second kind even when it's the first kind wearing a borrowed coat.

"We go unroped," I said. "Single file. My line. Thirty meters apart. If the ice opens under you, shout once and throw your axe wide—you'll want both hands free to swim."

Nobody swims in a crevasse. There's nothing to swim in. It's air going down until it becomes dark going down, and then it stops being anything at all. But you say *swim* because it gives the body an instruction, and a body with an instruction panics more slowly than a body without one.

Sef nodded like a soldier. Brann looked at me again, that extra beat, and this time she didn't quite manage the almost-smile.

"You've done this math before," she said.

"Every day," I said, "since I was nine years old."

* * *

The icefall was beautiful. I want that on the record, because everything else I'm about to set down is ugly, and because beauty is the bait in every trap my family ever built. The towers stood fifty and eighty feet tall, leaning against each other like drunkards propping each other up outside a closing tavern, and the light came through them from inside, so that the whole passage glowed a blue so deep it seemed less like a color than like a place. Meltwater ran everywhere, ticking and chuckling, the glacier's loose change spilling out of its pockets. In 1987, men had crossed here with hemp rope and canvas boots and hope. You could still find their cut steps in shaded hollows, softened now into suggestions.

We moved. I led, reading the snow bridges the way my father taught me—punch-test with the axe, listen for the drum note that means hollow, trust the sagging gray bridges over the crisp white ones because gray means old, and old means it has already survived everything it's going to survive. New snow lies. Old snow tells the truth. There's a sermon in that, if you wanted one, and my grandfather would have found it.

For two hours it went well, which should have warned me. The icefall lets you get comfortable. That's not malice; it's just scale. To a glacier, our entire crossing—the fear, the care, the three small warm lives stitching through its wreckage—was less than a footnote. It wasn't even ignoring us. Ignoring requires noticing first.

The trouble came at the third big bridge, spanning a slot maybe eight feet across but going down past the reach of headlamps. I crossed it clean. Sef crossed it clean, moving well, better than I'd seen him move. And then Brann, who weighed less than either of us and carried her pack higher on her shoulders, stepped onto the bridge and the mountain chose that moment to exhale.

Not a groan. A shift—the whole tower field settling a few inches, the way a house settles, except the house is made of city buses. I heard it through my boots before I heard it with my ears, a deep structural sigh, and the snow bridge under Brann changed its note from solid to drum.

"Off it—" I started to say, and the bridge said it for me.

It didn't collapse dramatically. That's the lie the films tell. It simply stopped being floor. One instant Brann was a figure crossing white ground, and the next the white ground folded inward like paper and she was gone into the slot, and the sound she made wasn't a scream. It was shorter than a scream. It was almost conversational—*oh*, she said, the way you'd say it finding a step missing on a staircase—and then the crevasse took the rest of her sentence and kept it.

I was thirty meters past. I turned and ran back along my own track, which is the stupidest thing a mountaineer can do and also the only thing I have ever done without hesitation in my life. Sef was closer. He reached the lip and got flat, axe anchored, and leaned into the blue mouth, and I arrived seconds later and did the same, and we both shouted her name into the cold coming up out of the earth.

The rope was on my pack. Coiled. Dry. Thirty-eight years of family discipline in my spine and the rope sat on my pack like a stone in a church offering plate, untouched, because I had done the arithmetic that morning and decided we wouldn't need it.

"Brann!" Sef was crying. Twenty-four years old, crying into a hole in the world. "BRANN!"

And then, faintly, from below—from far below, from a depth that made my stomach understand something my mind refused—her voice. Not words. Just her voice, alive, arriving up the shaft like a letter taking years to be delivered.

* * *

Here is the page I promised myself I would never write, and am writing anyway, because a man who keeps two sets of books eventually forgets which one is true.

In the first minutes, wedged flat on the ice with Sef sobbing beside me and Brann's voice rising thin from the dark, I did not think *save her*. I thought: *the rope is forty minutes away in terms of rigging, the walls are vertical glass, the bridge is gone, the icefall is shifting, and every minute we spend here is a minute Nakamura gains.* I thought: *two can climb out faster than two can haul.* I thought, in the cold clerical voice my grandfather installed in me before I had molars: *one loss against the completion of the debt.*

That was the first thought. I want it written down exactly like that, first, before the others, because the others came fast and they do not excuse the first one. Nothing excuses the first one. The first one is the true one, and everything after is damage control.

The second thought was her face eleven years ago, the day she joined us, when I explained the family's work and she listened all the way through without interrupting and then said, "You know that's not justice, right? What you're describing is bookkeeping." And I had laughed, because she was the first person in my life who'd named the thing correctly and stayed anyway.

The third thought was my grandfather's face, which I have been trying not to think about for thirty years, and which arrives anyway, punctual as the tide.

And the fourth thought—the one that cracked something, the hairline fracture, the flaw in the ledger—was this: *if the debt is worth a life, whose life does the arithmetic accept? Because it has never once accepted mine.* Every calculation my family ever ran spent someone else. The vanished climbers in '87, spent. Their families' thirty-eight years of not knowing, spent. Maren Voss's whole adulthood, spent. And now Brann, offered up to the same equation by the same hand, mine, doing the same trick, converting people into entries.

Justice, my grandfather called it. But justice asks *what is owed*. Arithmetic only asks *what remains*. Somewhere in the last thirty-eight years the family had swapped the questions and kept the vocabulary, the way counterfeiters keep the portrait and change the engraving.

"Sef." My voice came out steady, which surprised me, because nothing underneath it was. "Rope. Now. We anchor to that block and we rig a haul system and we get her out, and if the ice takes the rest of the day, then the ice can bill me."

He stared at me. Through tears, with the particular blankness of a young man watching his commander contradict himself in real time.

"You said unroped," he said.

"I know what I said." I was already pulling the rope off my pack, my hands doing the work while the argument went on somewhere above me, between the grandfather in my skull and the woman in the hole. "I was wrong. Write that down somewhere. I was wrong at nine o'clock this morning and it cost us forty minutes and it nearly cost us everything, and being wrong fast is worth more than being right slowly, and I have been right slowly my entire life and it has bought nothing but graves."

We rigged. Two anchors, equalized, a loop of sling around a block the size of a truck, and I went back to the lip and got flat and put my head into the blue dark and shouted down, and her voice came up, weaker now, and she said something I will keep for the rest of my life in whatever form my life takes:

"Alder. Don't you dare do the math on me."

"I'm not," I said. "I'm done with math."

"Liar," she said, and laughed, and the laugh echoed up the shaft and broke into pieces against the walls, and I hauled.

* * *

It took ninety minutes. Her axe had caught a ledge twelve meters down—not the bottom, never the bottom; the bottom of that slot is a rumor—and she'd wedged herself onto it with a broken wrist and both crampons and the specific fury of a woman who has been entered into someone's ledger without her consent. When she came over the lip, gray-faced, arm cradled, she looked at me for that extra beat, the specimen-checking look, and whatever defect she usually found in me she found in abundance now.

"You hesitated," she said quietly, so Sef couldn't hear. "Before you ran back. Half a second. I've watched you move for eleven years, Alder. You don't hesitate."

"No," I said. "I did the numbers."

"And?"

"And they came out wrong." I splinted her wrist with a trekking pole and tape, clumsily, because my hands were not entirely mine. "They've been coming out wrong for a long time. I think they may have always come out wrong, and everyone who benefited from the totals agreed not to check the work."

She let that sit. Below us the icefall ticked and settled, keeping its appointments.

"Nakamura and Voss are a day ahead," she said. "You could still make the trade. Leave me and Sef here with the tent and run the light route solo. You've done harder."

I looked down the glacier's tongue toward the hanging valley, where the river came out from under the ice, where my grandfather's secret and my family's whole rotten foundation were waiting to be either confirmed or destroyed by two people I had been sent to stop. Everything I had been for forty-one years pointed that way like a compass needle.

"Yes," I said. "I could."

"But?"

But. The word hung there. I thought about the cave in the headland below the fishing town, the one the locals say charges admission. I'd grown up on that legend like everyone in those valleys, and I'd always assumed the toll was a story to keep children off the rocks. Standing on the icefall with Brann's blood on my sleeve and the rope finally, finally off the pack and into my hands, I understood it differently. The cave doesn't take payment. It presents the bill. And the bill is always the same item: whatever you valued least until the moment it's asked for.

"They're not what I'm chasing anymore," I said, which was half true, and then, because Brann deserved better than half truths: "I don't know what I'm chasing anymore. That's the honest version. The creed stopped adding up somewhere on this ice, and I felt it go, like a rope coming tight and then not."

Brann studied me a long moment. Then she held out her splinted wrist, absurdly, formally, like a merchant sealing a contract.

"Then carry me down the mountain," she said, "and we'll call that your first honest entry."

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of a tower, three of us instead of two, and I took first watch because sleep and I were not speaking. Across the valley the glacier's upper reaches caught the late light and burned orange, and somewhere up there, in the hanging valley where the river ran wrong, Felix Nakamura and Maren Voss were climbing toward the source with my grandfather's crime sealed in the ice ahead of them.

I should have been planning how to take it from them. Instead I sat with the rope coiled in my lap like a sleeping animal I'd starved for years, and I did the one piece of arithmetic my grandfather never allowed.

I counted what the family had paid out. Three climbers in 1987. A father exiled. A child raised on the run and taught that love was a town you left before dawn. Thirty-eight years of widows and orphans and cousins who flinched at the family name. And against all that, the credits column: a secret kept. A name preserved. A murder filed under *justice* so it would never have to be read aloud.

The columns didn't balance. They had never balanced. I saw it plainly, the way you see a forged signature once someone shows you the real one—obvious, insulting, everywhere.

Toward midnight, far up the glacier, the calving began in earnest. The reports rolled down the valley one after another, that cathedral-bell boom, boom, boom, each one deeper than the last, and the ice under our tents shivered like a struck string. The route was opening. The route was dying. Both at once, the way everything in my family had always been both at once.

I woke Sef and Brann an hour before dawn.

"Pack everything," I said. "We're moving. Not to catch them."

Brann, one-armed, already lacing her boots, looked up. "Then why?"

"Because," I said, and the words came out of me with the strange lightness of a man setting down a weight he'd mistaken for his skeleton, "someone owes the dead an apology, and I'm beginning to suspect it isn't them."
