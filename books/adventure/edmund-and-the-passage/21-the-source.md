## 21. The Source

The river told us we were close before anything else did.

For three days it had been running beside us, under us, through us—loud, insistent, a thing with an opinion about everything. But that morning it changed its tune. It went quiet the way a crowd goes quiet, and I stopped on the moraine with my boot half-sunk in grey slush and listened to the silence where a river should have been arguing.

"Maren," I said.

She was twenty meters ahead, roped to me, bent into the wind with her hood cinched so tight only her mouth showed. "What?"

"Listen."

She straightened. Tilted her head. And then I watched thirty years of hunting move across her face like weather—the hope arriving first, too fast, followed immediately by the fear that always escorts hope when you've carried it this long.

"It's going *under*," she said. "It's going down."

"Going home," I said, because someone had to say something and she was in no state to say anything sensible.

We followed the water down. That sounds wrong—a river going down is what rivers do—but this was different. The Vinter's tongue spilled out of the hanging valley above us in a wall of dirty blue ice two hundred feet high, and the stream that had run upstream beneath a fishing town for forty years now poured itself into a slot at the glacier's base and vanished, swallowed whole, without even a splash to show for it. Like a coin dropped into a well that never hits bottom.

Maren stood at the lip of the slot and stared down into blue dark. Her breath came out in ragged flags.

"My father's letter said the source was *inside*," she said. "I thought he meant figuratively. He wasn't a figurative man."

"He was an archaeologist's colleague," I said. "Figurative is how they flirt."

She gave me a look that would have been a laugh on anyone less determined not to have one, and then she started uncoiling rope.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about glaciers: they are not white. White is what they look like from a distance, from safety, from the kind of life where ice is something in a glass. Up close a glacier is every color the sky has ever been embarrassed by—blue so deep it feels classified, green where sediment has ground into it over centuries, black bands of ash from eruptions no one alive remembers. Walking onto the Vinter was like walking into a building that predated architecture. You don't conquer a place like that. You're granted an audience.

The slot went down at a steep angle, wide enough for one person, polished smooth by forty years of running water. Maren went first because it was her father's route and her father's grief, and I didn't argue, which if you'd known me a year ago would have surprised us both. The ice around us thickened as we descended until the daylight behind us shrank to a coin and then a thread and then nothing, and our headlamps took over, two small rude circles of yellow in all that ancient blue.

And then the passage opened.

I want to describe it properly, because I've spent my whole life describing things and I know I'm going to fail, but I want the failure on record.

The chamber was maybe sixty feet across and higher than my headlamp could find, and every surface of it was ice—not snow-ice, not the grey rind you scrape off a fish hold, but glacier core, compressed until it had forgotten it was ever water. Blue light came from somewhere, from some fracture or vent I never found, and it filled the space the way incense fills a cathedral: everywhere, weightless, accusatory. The walls curved overhead in ribs. Columns of ice stood where meltwater had drilled down through centuries and refrozen, pillars like the piers of a nave. At the center of the floor, ringed by a low shelf of clear ice, was the spring.

The spring was small. That's the detail that undid me, later, when I tried to tell it. After everything—the cave, the town, the gorge, the bodies in the ice, Alder's sermon, the calving bell cracking across the valley like God clearing his throat—you'd expect the source of a river that runs uphill to be something operatic. A geyser. A chasm. Instead it was a pool perhaps four feet across, breathing. Water rose through it in slow silver ropes, hung for a moment at the surface like it was deciding, and slid away toward the slot we'd come down, beginning the long impossible trip back to the sea. The spring didn't roar. It murmured. It sounded like my father reading aloud in the next room, late, when he thought I was asleep.

"Oh," Maren said. Just that. Just *oh*, in a voice I'd only heard once before, the night she'd told me about the knock on her family's door in 1987 and the man who delivered the news like a bill.

We stood there a while. Neither of us said anything clever. There wasn't any.

Then my lamp caught the shelf of ice ringing the spring, and I saw what was sealed inside it, and the awe got a body in it, exactly as advertised.

* * *

You spend enough years digging other people's middens, you develop a professional relationship with the dead. You learn their garbage before you learn their names. You learn that a people are their broken things: the cracked pot, the snapped harpoon head, the child's toy worn smooth by hands that are now dust. I have knelt over remains more times than I can count and felt—this is the shameful part, the part I don't put in the grant applications—mostly curiosity. The dead are data. Grief is for the living, and the living aren't paying by the hour.

So I knew, intellectually, what I was looking at. The shelf of clear ice held shapes. One shape was a stack: oilcloth-wrapped bundles, a leather satchel gone stiff as board, a tin document case with a latch I could see the mechanism of through four inches of ice. Papers. Preserved the way only ice preserves—no rot, no mold, no hungry bacteria, just stillness. Forty years of stillness.

The other shape was a man.

He lay on his side, curled slightly, the way people curl when they're cold and trying to conserve, except he'd been cold for four decades and conservation hadn't worked. His clothing had kept its color better than I expected—wool doesn't quit—and on the breast of his jacket, stitched in thread that had faded from red to something like dried blood, was an expedition tag. I couldn't read the name from where I stood. I didn't need to. Maren made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, a sound with no word in it, and crossed the chamber to the ice shelf, and pressed both palms flat against the surface above him, and read the tag upside down through the ice.

"Papa," she said.

Not Edmund. Not the rival whose name I'd reconstructed from torn pages and my father's habits. Her father. Henrik Voss, who had walked out of a basecamp in September 1987 and become a legend, a debt, a wound the size of a family. He hadn't died in the gorge. He hadn't died in the crevasse field. He had walked—walked, all this way, up the route his own map described—and reached the source, and lain down beside the proof he'd come to collect, and the glacier had taken its admission.

"The cave takes payment," I said, stupidly, because my mouth does that when my chest is caving in. "That's the legend. Three climbers in '87. The glacier charges."

Maren didn't answer. Her breath was fogging the ice above her father's face, little blooms of condensation appearing and fading, appearing and fading, like she was trying to breathe life back through four inches of forty-year winter. I let her. I checked the ropes, checked the walls, did all the competent things my hands know how to do without me, and I gave her the time, because there are moments you don't interrupt even when a man called Alder Voss is a day behind you with a creed and a knife.

When she finally spoke again, her voice had been wrung out and hung back up.

"He found it." She traced the outline of the document case through the ice, careful, like touching a sleeping animal. "He found the proof. Whatever Father Nakamura buried—he found it first. He got here." A pause. "And then he stayed."

"Or was made to stay," I said. "There were three of them missing. If Edmund's body is in the ice below—if the financier's men came up after them—"

"No." She shook her head slowly. "Look at him. Look at the *position*. Nobody arranges a body like that. Nobody curls a dead man up gently and leaves the documents beside his hand." She pulled back and looked at the whole tableau, the way I should have been looking at it, the way I look at middens: as a scene, as testimony. "He got here. He collected it or confirmed it. And then—" her jaw worked "—then he decided not to leave. Or couldn't. Or—" 

"Or he understood what carrying it out would cost," I said quietly. "My father understood that too. From the other direction."

Because that was the thing assembling itself in my head, click by click, the way a dig assembles itself when the stratigraphy finally resolves. My father had taken the evidence out. Had buried it, hidden it, spent thirty-five years in exile and disgrace and my mother's silence keeping it hidden. Henrik Voss had reached the same cache and chosen—or been forced—to stay with it. Two men, one secret, opposite sentences. And between them, sealed in the blue cathedral where a river began its impossible journey home, sat the truth that had eaten two families alive.

The glacier's toll. The cave's admission. Everyone had told the story wrong. It was never the cave that charged. The cave was just the door. The glacier was the keeper, and its price was always the same: you brought your dead up the mountain, or you became one, or you paid in the only currency it accepted, which was years. Decades. Whole lives spent circling a hole in the ice.

"We need to get the case out," Maren said. Her voice had gone flat and practical, which I recognized as her version of my own digging-calm, the voice you use when feeling would kill the work. "Before the calving. Before Alder. We cut a block, we haul it—"

"Maren."

"—we wrap it, we take the documents and leave the—"

"*Maren.*" I pointed up.

Through the ceiling of the chamber—through fifty feet of glacier core—I heard it. Not a crack. A groan. Long, tectonic, patient, the sound of a mass the size of a city remembering that it is, technically, a river. The blue light in the chamber flickered as some fracture far above shifted and re-filtered the daylight coming down through the ice.

The calving season hadn't opened. But the glacier had cleared its throat, and we were standing in its mouth.

* * *

We worked fast after that, and I'll give Maren this: she dug like someone who'd grown up around diggers, even if her tools were a piton and desperation. We couldn't cut the whole block—no time, no gear, and the shelf was structural, load-bearing in the way that everything in a glacier is load-bearing right up until it isn't. So we chipped a channel around the document case alone, the tin box with the latch, working by headlamp while the glacier groaned overhead like an old house in wind.

The case came free on the third try. Maren hauled it out of its socket of ice and held it against her chest with both arms, and for a moment she was eight years old again, holding the thing her father had died beside, and I looked away to give her that privacy, because some things you don't watch even when you're the only witness.

That's when my lamp swept the far wall, and I saw the mark.

Carved into the ice near the chamber's exit slot, low, at kneeling height, was a symbol I had known my entire life without ever knowing what it meant. My father drew it on the margins of his letters. He scratched it into tent poles and tool handles and, once, memorably, into the dashboard of a truck he had no business defacing. I'd assumed it was a signature. A claim. *Nakamura was here*, the way diggers mark territory.

But this one was older than any of those. This one was weathered, rounded, cut with a different hand—and beside it, smaller, cruder, was a second mark I'd seen only once before: on the carved stone in the sea cave, the stone someone had stolen out of my rented room in a fishing town, the stone that had started all of this.

Two marks. Two men. Kneeling height.

"They were both here," I said. "At the same time. They marked it together."

Maren came to stand beside me. She looked at the carvings for a long moment, and then at the curled shape of her father in the ice, and then at the empty socket where the case had been, and I watched her arrive at the arithmetic I was still avoiding: three climbers went up in 1987. One lay in front of us. One was somewhere in the ice below, wearing a jacket her father owned a spare of. Which meant the third—

The third had walked out. The third had carried the secret down the mountain and spent a lifetime paying for it in exile and estrangement and a child who learned to stop waiting up.

The glacier groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere far above us something the size of a cathedral let go.

"Maren," I said. "We need to go. Now."

She didn't move. She was looking at her father through the ice, and her hand came up one more time, palm flat against the blue, and she said—so quietly I almost lost it under the glacier's voice:

"I'm coming back for you. When the season ends. When it's safe. I promise."

And the ice, being ice, said nothing. But the light in the chamber flickered once, blue to green to blue, like a page turning, and I chose—then and forever after—to believe it was an answer.

We climbed.
