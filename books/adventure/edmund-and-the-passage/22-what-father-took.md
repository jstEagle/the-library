## 22. What Father Took

The blue light in the source chamber didn't behave like light. It behaved like water that had forgotten how to move—frozen mid-pour, ten thousand years of it, pressing in from every side so that the spring at the center seemed to burn rather than flow. I'd spent my whole life reading ground, and I had no vocabulary for this. Ground doesn't glow. Ground doesn't hold its breath.

Maren stood with her back to me, her headlamp beam fixed on the shelf of clear ice where the documents lay sealed like insects in amber. She hadn't spoken since we'd pried the rival's satchel free of the crevasse wall two hours ago. Neither had I. Some rooms you enter talking and leave silent. This one skipped the first part.

I should have been cataloguing. That's what I do—that's what I've always done when a thing gets too big to feel: I inventory it. Two oilskin packets, one leather journal, a tin of negatives, a man's wedding ring threaded on a bootlace. The remains we did not catalogue. We covered them with Maren's bivvy sack and I said a word or two that wasn't a prayer because I don't know any prayers, just the names of soil horizons, and somehow that felt closer to right.

But my hands wouldn't start the inventory. They hung at my sides like tools whose owner had died.

"Felix." Maren's voice came out flattened by the ice, the way sound does in a place with no soft surfaces. "Say something. Say anything. If you go quiet much longer I'm going to start thinking you've frozen from the inside."

"I'm trying to figure out what he took," I said.

She turned. Her face was wind-scoured and sleepless and older than thirty years of hunting should have made it. "What?"

"My father. Everyone keeps saying it—the family says it, the town says it, Alder says it like a sermon. *He took something.* Thirty-five years of people saying *Nakamura took it* and nobody ever saying what *it* was." I looked down at the oilskin packets in their tomb of ice. "And now I'm standing here looking at it, and I finally understand, and I wish to God I didn't."

"Tell me anyway."

So I told her. Not because she needed telling—she'd read the rival's journal same as I had, by headlamp, in the crevasse shelter, our breath fogging the pages while the storm tried to bury us. But some things you have to say out loud before they're real, and this one had been waiting thirty-five years for someone to say it in its presence.

"He didn't take a treasure," I said. "There is no treasure. There never was. He took the proof that Edmund Halvorsen—" I nodded at the covered shape, at the name Maren had carried like a stone in her chest since chapter one of her own life, "—was murdered. Ordered murdered. By your grandfather. And he took it not to sell it, not to bargain with it, not even to publish it. He took it to make it *stop*."

Maren's jaw tightened. "Stop what?"

"The family. Think about it. Think about what Alder is. Think about what the family *does*." I crouched by the ice shelf, close enough that my breath bloomed and slid across the surface without melting anything. "Your grandfather has a son dead in a glacier and a rival expedition that knows why. What do you think happens next? Does he let them walk down off this mountain? Does he trust three climbers and an archaeologist to keep quiet out of decency? No. He does what the family has always done. He convenes justice. He makes the witnesses into the guilty. He erases them the way he erased Edmund—except slower, and with paperwork."

"And my father—"

"My father saw the order come through. Or found the ledger. Whatever it was—the journal's clear enough about the shape of it. And he made a choice that I have spent my entire life getting exactly backwards." My voice did something strange on the last words, went thin and high, and I pushed through it because if I stopped I wasn't sure I'd start again. "Everyone—including me, especially me—believed he ran. That he grabbed whatever was valuable and bolted and left three men dead on the mountain and his own kid to grow up in the wreckage of his name. That's the story I built my whole spine around. Every town I left, every dig I quit halfway, every fake surname—I was running from a thief. Except he wasn't running *from* anything. He was carrying something. The only copy. The only thing standing between your grandfather and a clean slate."

The spring made its small relentless sound. Water moving under ice, patient as arithmetic.

"He couldn't publish," Maren said slowly. She was working it through the way she worked through terrain, testing each hold before weight. "If he published, the family would have destroyed him and everyone who'd touched the story. There'd be no trial—the family doesn't recognize courts. So he buried it."

"He buried it and he buried himself along with it. He let the whole world believe the worst version. He let *me* believe it." I heard my voice crack on that last word and hated it and kept going. "Do you know what it's like, being the child of a man the story says abandoned everyone? You inherit the shape of the hole. You spend forty years fitting yourself into it. I became a person who leaves before she can be left, because the one man who was supposed to stay—"

I stopped.

Because here is the thing about grief: it doesn't ambush you at funerals. It waits until you are four hundred meters inside a glacier, kneeling over the answer to the only question your life has ever asked, and then it comes up through the floor like meltwater through a midden, and there is nowhere to put your foot down that isn't already flooded.

My father's handwriting was on the journal in my pack. I'd been reading it for days now—*If anything happens, it was my idea, not the boy's*—and I had read that line as guilt. As confession. As a man covering himself. But kneeling there in the blue light I heard it again the way it was actually written, and it wasn't a confession at all.

It was a shield. He knew what would happen. He knew the family would come for anyone connected to the truth, and he wrote one sentence designed to fall into a child's hands decades later and say: *whatever they tell you I did, the cost of it was mine, and you were never part of the ledger.*

He'd been protecting me the only way available to a man with no power except custody of a secret. He took the exile. He took the disgrace. He took my hatred—he took it *knowingly*, packed it up with the oilskins and the negatives and walked out of my life wearing it like a coat, because a child who hates a thief asks no questions, and a child who asks questions ends up in a crevasse with a tag on her boot.

"Oh," I said. It came out very small. "Oh, you stupid, brave, *unbelievable* old man."

And then I did the thing I had not done in this book of my life—not when my mother died, not when the university survey offer slipped through my fingers, not when Alder Voss put his polite hand on my shoulder and offered me a walk away from everything. I put my forehead against the ice, which was cold enough to hurt, and I wept.

Not gracefully. I want that on the record, wherever records end up. There was nothing luminous about it. It was ugly, hiccupping, forty-years-overdue crying, the kind where your whole body participates whether it consented to or not, and the sound of it went around the blue walls and came back to me sounding like someone else, someone younger, someone who had once believed her father would come back for her and had never quite stopped keeping a bag packed by the door in case he did.

Maren didn't touch me. I registered later that this was deliberate—she'd watched me flinch from hands my whole time on the mountain, and she understood, the way diggers understand, that you don't brace a collapse by leaning on it. She sat down beside me instead, shoulder near mine, and talked low and steady about nothing—about her father's terrible camp coffee, about how Edmund Halvorsen had supposedly cheated at cards—which gave me somewhere to be other than inside my own skull while the flood did its work.

When it was over—and it took a while; grief keeps books too, and mine had thirty-five years of unpaid entries—I wiped my face on a sleeve already ruined beyond redemption and laughed, one dry note, at the state of myself.

"Well," I said. "That's embarrassing."

"That's the most human thing I've seen you do," Maren said. "It's about time. I was starting to think you were assembled from field notes."

"I might be. Check my seams." I sat back against the ice wall and let the cold press through my jacket, grounding me the way cold always had. "You understand what this means, though. For you."

"For me?"

"Your grandfather ordered a murder. Your family's whole apparatus—Alder, the trials, the justice-marks, all of it—it was built on top of a killing and then used to hunt anyone who got close to knowing. Your father didn't vanish because of a curse or a cave that takes payment. He vanished because he was going to be the second body, and my father's theft—" I caught myself, corrected it, "—my father's *rescue* bought him maybe a decade before they closed in anyway. The legend, the admission, the whole story the town tells—it's scar tissue over what your grandfather did."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. In the blue light her face was unreadable, glacial itself, and I thought: here it comes, the collision. Because I'd done the math she was doing now, and the math was monstrous. Everything she'd hunted for thirty years—the truth of her father, the meaning of the carved stone, the reason the river ran wrong—all of it led home. And home was the thing she'd have to burn down.

"My grandfather," she said at last, "is dead. Eleven years. Buried under a stone with a very flattering inscription."

"The family isn't."

"No." She looked at the sealed documents, at the negatives, at the ring on its bootlace. "The family isn't. And Alder is coming. He's a day behind us at most, and he's spent his whole life enforcing a creed he doesn't know is a lie—or worse, half-knows." She exhaled. "What do we do with it, Felix? When he gets here. Because he will get here."

I looked around the chamber. The spring, the ice, the archive that had waited thirty-five years in the dark for someone to read it. My father had carried this alone across a lifetime so that no one else would have to die of it. And I understood, finally, sitting in the cold heart of everything he'd paid, that inheritance doesn't mean receiving what someone left you. It means deciding what to carry forward.

"We tell him the truth," I said. "All of it. Even the parts that gut him. Especially those."

"And if he chooses the creed over the truth?"

I stood, and my knees cracked, and outside—far off, muffled by a hundred meters of ancient ice—came a sound I'd been dreading since the hanging valley. A deep, resonant crack, like a cathedral bell struck once underwater. Then another. Then a long groaning silence.

The calving had begun in earnest. Somewhere above us, the glacier was opening its season, and the route behind us was already dying.

Maren and I looked at each other, and neither of us said what we were both thinking: that we were standing in the one room on earth where the truth was kept, and the building was scheduled for demolition.

"Get the documents ready to move," I said. "We may not get to choose how we leave. Only what we carry."

* * *

We worked fast after that. I won't pretend my hands were steady. Every few minutes the bell tolled again, deeper or nearer, and fine snow sifted down through a fissure overhead like flour through a sieve, catching in our headlamps. I wrapped the oilskin packets in my spare layer, tucked the journal against my ribs where a body keeps the things it cannot afford to lose, and paused one last time over the covered remains.

"You held the door," I told him quietly. "Both of you did. We'll take it from here."

Maren pretended not to hear. She was kind that way.

We were cinching the last strap when the light at the tunnel mouth changed—not the blue shifting of the ice, but a yellow human light, bobbing, approaching. Footsteps rang on the frozen streambed, unhurried, deliberate, the walk of a man who has never once needed to hurry because the ledger always balances.

Alder Voss stepped into the blue cathedral with ice in his beard and his grandfather's creed on his face, and looked at the spring, and the shelf, and us.

"So," he said. "This is where it was kept."
