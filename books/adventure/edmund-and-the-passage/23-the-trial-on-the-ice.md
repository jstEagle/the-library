## 23. The Trial on the Ice

I have presided over forty-one judgments in my life. I know the number the way a priest knows his masses, because my grandfather kept a ledger and I inherited the ledger along with the creed, and neither came with an eraser. Forty-one judgments, each conducted properly: witnesses heard, charges read, sentence weighed against precedent. The family's justice is not cruelty. It is *procedure*. That is what I have always told myself, standing in barns and boathouses and one memorable grain elevator, holding the book open while men who owed us money learned the difference between debt and sin.

None of those judgments was held on a glacier. None of them had an audience of ice. And none of them, I will admit now in the privacy of my own skull where no one can subpoena it, ever felt quite as much like theater as this one did.

But we will get to that.

* * *

I crossed the last of the moraine at dawn, alone, which was not the plan. The plan had been four of us. The plan had been ropes, radios, a disciplined line up the icefall with me at the anchor point doing what I do best, which is making other people's courage structurally sound.

The icefall took Tam. Not killed him—worse, from a procedural standpoint. It swallowed him into a crevasse I had looked at, measured with my own eyes, and chosen not to rope across, because roping would have cost us two hours and the window was closing and I did the arithmetic every man in our family does before he does anything else. Two hours against one man's margin of safety. The arithmetic said go.

The arithmetic is always right. That is the horror of it. It has never once been wrong, and it has never once been enough.

So I came up the hanging valley by myself, carrying Tam's share of the rope coiled over my shoulder like something I intended to return to him, and I found their tracks—the Nakamura child and Maren—and I followed them the way justice follows everyone eventually: slowly, and on foot, and without any particular appetite for the view.

Here is what nobody tells you about pursuing people across a glacier: you have a great deal of time to think, and thinking is not a skill the family rewards. We are a house of action. My grandfather built the creed in a single night, they say, after the law failed him for the third time, and he built it the way you build a wall—fast, load-bearing, no windows. I was raised inside that wall. I know every stone of it by touch. It was only somewhere around the second serac, listening to the glacier groan like a ship changing its mind, that I noticed the wall had never once had a door.

* * *

I found them at midday in the blue cathedral, or near it—I could see the glow of it down-valley, that impossible light the source makes, like sunlight filtered through the memory of water. They had made camp in the lee of an ice block, two small tents, a stove going. Felix Nakamura was outside it, chipping samples from the ice face with a geologist's hammer, working with those hands everyone talks about. Hands that read ground. I watched for ten minutes before I let my boot crunch, because watching Felix work is instructive even when you intend to arrest the results.

Maren heard me first. She always hears things first; she has spent thirty years listening for footsteps behind her, and it has made her ears into instruments. She stood up out of the tent with her father's old letter in her jacket pocket—I knew it was there the way you know a loaded thing is loaded—and she said my name the way you'd spit.

"Alder."

"Cousin." I came into the light with my hands visible, which is courtesy, and also doctrine. "You've led me a long walk."

"You followed." She stepped between me and Felix, which was unnecessary and which I noted, because it was the first time in thirty years she had put her body between the family and anyone. "And now you're here."

"Now I'm here."

Felix set down the hammer. Slowly. Everything Felix does is slow, deliberate, the movements of someone who learned early that sudden motion gets read as guilt. They looked at me with their father's eyes, which is to say eyes that had already calculated three exits and found all of them morally acceptable.

"You're alone," Felix said.

"I am."

"Where's your team?"

"Gone home." This was true in the way that a grave is true. "Tam fell. The others turned back with him." I let that sit. I am not a man who performs grief, but I am a man who reports facts, and the fact was that my pursuit had cost more than theirs had, and we both knew what that meant about whose cause the mountain favored. The mountain doesn't favor causes. But try telling that to a Voss.

Maren's face did something complicated. Thirty years of hating me, and grief still got in through the side door, because Tam had taught her knots when she was nine, before the family split into its two churches. I saw her hate lose its footing for half a second and then recover, furious at itself.

"You came to take us back," she said.

"I came to hold judgment." And here I did the thing I had rehearsed for eleven miles of moraine, the thing that had sounded so clean in my head against the wind. I opened the ledger. My grandfather's ledger, the physical object, oilcloth-wrapped against the cold, and I set it on an ice block between us like an altar.

"The family convenes," I said. "Here. Now. There's no barn, no boathouse, no committee. There's me, and there's the book, and there's the ice for a witness, and the ice forgets nothing, so consider yourselves formally observed."

Felix actually laughed. A short, dry sound, startled out of them, and I confess it stung worse than Maren's spitting would have. Because they were laughing at exactly the thing I had spent the whole walk trying not to notice: that a trial requires more than one man with a book, and I had brought precisely one man with a book.

"The accused," I continued, because procedure is a comfort even when it's absurd, and absurdity has never stopped a Voss yet, "are two. Maren Voss, for betrayal of the family—aiding an outsider, concealing evidence, and turning the family's own history against it. And Felix Nakamura, child of Edmund Nakamura, for the sins of the father: theft of family property, obstruction of justice, and the concealment that has cost this family thirty years of certainty."

I read it out. All of it. The charges, the precedents, the relevant entries from 1987. I did it properly. God help me, I did it *beautifully*. Standing on that glacier with the light coming down blue through fifty feet of ancient compressed snow, reading my grandfather's cramped handwriting aloud, I felt the old lift, the sense that the words were load-bearing, that I was the wall and the wall was good.

Then Maren said the thing.

* * *

She let me finish. That's important. She didn't interrupt, didn't scoff, didn't do any of the things I had prepared rebuttals for during the climb. She waited until the last entry was read and the silence came down like weather, and then she walked forward and put her hand flat on the ledger—not grabbing it, just resting it there, the way you'd rest a hand on a coffin.

"Read me the charge against the family," she said.

"There is no such charge."

"Then convene one."

"Maren—"

"You said the ice forgets nothing." Her voice didn't rise. That's the thing about Maren that Alder-the-enforcer never planned for: she fights quiet. "Fine. Then let it hear this. In 1987, Henrik Voss—" her grandfather, my grandfather, the same man, the same wall "—ordered the murder of Edmund Nakamura's colleague. You know it. I've seen the journal. Your own expedition members wrote it down before they vanished, and the family erased them, and then it erased the erasing, and for thirty years it called the erasing *justice*."

"That is an allegation—"

"It's a confession with better handwriting." She flipped the ledger open under her hand, pages rasping in the cold. "Go on. Find the entry where the family tried itself. Find the page where anyone asked what *we* owed. Forty-one judgments, Alder? How many of the judged were Voss?"

I knew the answer. Of course I knew the answer. I had known it since I was fourteen years old and found the gap in the ledger—a missing year, pages cut out with a razor, the binding re-stitched by someone who thought stitching hid anything from a boy who slept with his fingers in books. The family judged everyone except itself. The wall had no door facing inward.

"Procedure," I said, and heard how thin it came out, how it sounded less like a foundation and more like a fig leaf, "requires—"

"Requires what?" Now she did raise her voice, and the glacier took it and gave it back twice, off the ice walls, so that her question seemed to come from inside the mountain itself. "That the judge be impartial? You're the grandson of the murderer. That the accused be heard? You've already sentenced us—you posted our names in every supply town between here and the coast. That the evidence be examined? You've spent thirty years burying it. Tell me, cousin, which part of this is justice and which part is inheritance?"

And there it was. The hairline fracture, the one that had opened above Tam's crevasse and widened with every mile. She hadn't broken my creed. She'd done something worse. She'd *audited* it, in front of the only witness who keeps perfect records.

* * *

Felix spoke then, and I want it recorded—even if the only recorder is me, and I am a compromised instrument—that Felix Nakamura spoke fairly.

"You're not wrong about my father," they said. Their voice was level, their hands still, those digger's hands folded like a man at a funeral. "He took things from your family. He took the evidence and he ran and he let you all spend thirty years calling him a thief, because the alternative was a war inside your family that would have eaten the innocent along with the guilty. That wasn't clean. I've hated him for it most of my life. But he wasn't stealing treasure. He was stealing a verdict. Yours. The one your grandfather couldn't afford to have reached."

"And you'll testify to that?" I asked, because some part of me, the part still wearing the uniform of procedure, wanted it on the record. Wanted *something* on the record.

"I'll testify to everything," Felix said. "That was always the difference between us. You want the story that protects the family. I want the story that survives it."

I stood there with the ledger open in the wind and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes at altitude when the blood thins and the mind strips its insulation away, that I had walked eleven miles to conduct a trial and arrived to find myself the defendant. Maren had done it without raising a rope. She had simply refused the frame—refused to stand where I pointed, refused to be the accused while the court belonged to the criminal—and once she refused, the whole architecture came down, because a trial where the accused declines the roles assigned is not a trial. It is a conversation. And conversations were the one thing the wall was never built to contain.

"I should take you back," I said. "Both of you. By force if necessary. That's the doctrine."

"Yes," Maren said. "It is."

"But the doctrine assumes the family is worth returning people *to*."

The wind filled the pause. Somewhere below us, deep in the glacier, something shifted—a groan, a long tectonic sigh, the sound the calving season makes when it clears its throat. We all three heard it. We all three pretended we hadn't, because the window was closing and every hour we spent on jurisprudence was an hour off the route, and beneath all the righteousness, all three of us wanted the same thing: the source, the truth, the end of the thirty-year lie. We differed only on what to do when we held it. That difference had seemed enormous in the valley. Up here it seemed like a disagreement between siblings about the wording of a eulogy.

"Here is what I can offer," I said finally, and I closed the ledger, and closing it felt like closing a door on a room I'd lived in my whole life. "Not acquittal. I don't have the authority to acquit; that's rather the point Maren just made, isn't it. What I can offer is a stay. Judgment suspended until the source is reached and the record is complete. If what you find confirms the family's account, the charges stand. If it confirms yours—" I looked at my grandfather's book, at the razor-cut gap where the missing year had been, and finished the sentence honestly, which cost more than the crevasse had. "If it confirms yours, then the next trial this family holds will have a different defendant."

Maren studied me for a long time. Looking, I think, for the trick, the hook, the polite clothing over the menace. She didn't find it, because there wasn't one, and the absence frightened her more than a trap would have. People trust your cruelty. It's your mercy that makes them check the exits.

"And if the ice takes the proof before we reach it?" she said. "The calving's started. You heard it."

"Then the ice becomes the judge," I said. "And none of us will like its sentences."

Felix picked up the hammer and slung the pack. "Then we walk," they said. "Trial adjourned."

"Not adjourned," I said, falling into step behind them, because that was the strangest part of the whole day and I might as well name it: the enforcer following the accused toward the evidence. "*Continued.* There's a difference. Adjournment means it's over for today. Continuance means it's coming back, and everyone knows it."

Maren glanced back at me once, and in her eyes was not forgiveness—thirty years don't forgive in an afternoon—but something rarer. Recognition. The look of one wall acknowledging another, and wondering, for the first time, what might grow in the space between them.

Behind us, the glacier cracked again, longer this time, a bell tolling in a cathedral of blue, and the sound rolled down the valley ahead of us like a summons.

We had perhaps two days. Maybe less. And the ice, which forgets nothing, had begun remembering out loud.
