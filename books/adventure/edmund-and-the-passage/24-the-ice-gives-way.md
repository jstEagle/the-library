## 24. The Ice Gives Way

The sound came first as a feeling, the way thunder does before you hear it—a pressure in the chest, a wrongness in the teeth. Then the glacier spoke.

Not a crack. A *bell*. That's what I'd thought when I heard it from the gorge, a day and a lifetime ago: a cathedral bell struck once by a hand that didn't care who was praying. Now, standing inside the cathedral itself, I understood that the bell had only been ringing us to service.

The source chamber was everything the journals promised and nothing any of us were ready for. Blue ice arched overhead in vaults no mason could have cut, thick as fortress walls and lit from within by some refraction trick of the spring below, so the whole space glowed like the inside of a bottle held up to the sun. The river emerged from the ice at the far end—emerged *upward*, if you can believe it, pressurized out of some deep channel in the rock, fountaining a meter into the air before it gathered itself and ran. And set into the wall beside the spring, behind a skin of ice that had grown over it like scar tissue over an old wound, was the archive.

We'd spent two hours clearing it. Maren worked with the patience of someone defusing something, chipping away with an ice axe held flat, and I worked with the patience of someone digging shell middens for cash, which is to say fast and slightly angry. Between us we opened a cavity maybe half a meter deep, and there it all was: oilcloth bundles, a steel document case gone brown with rust but still latched, and Edmund Voss himself—or what forty years of glacier had left of him. He lay curled on his side like a man sleeping through a sermon he'd already heard, expedition tag still wired to his jacket, the fabric preserved so completely you could read the manufacturer's label.

Maren hadn't spoken since we uncovered him. She'd taken off one glove and pressed her bare hand flat against the ice above his shoulder, the way you'd check a fever, or say goodbye.

Alder stood apart from both of us, near the chamber's mouth, and he was doing arithmetic. I could see it moving behind his eyes—the same arithmetic he'd done at the icefall, the same ledger-keeping the whole family ran on. Proof against people. Verdict against variable. He'd come up this mountain to seize evidence and convene a trial, and here was both, laid out under blue light like exhibits already entered.

"The case first," he said quietly. "Then the remains. Then we go."

"Nobody's going anywhere yet," Maren said, not turning around. "The ice needs another hour."

"We don't have an hour."

And that's when the glacier rang its bell again.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is the only inheritance my father ever gave me that turned out to be worth anything. People will tell you a calving is loud. It isn't, not at first. At first it's a change in the quality of the silence, the way a room changes when someone stands up in the dark. The light in the chamber dimmed—not evenly, but in bands, as if something enormous had shifted between us and the sun. Dust came down fine as flour, glittering in the glow. And somewhere above us, in the vaults, a seam that had been closed for ten thousand years opened the width of a finger.

The spring responded before anything else did. The fountain stuttered, surged, threw water a full two meters, and the temperature in the chamber dropped as the meltwater found new channels and started running them hard. Under our boots, the floor—that beautiful blue floor—made a sound I've only heard once since, in a building coming down: a long structural groan, patient and total, the sound of a decision already made being announced to the people it concerned.

"It's calving," I said. "The face above us. The whole hanging tongue."

"Then we take the case and go," Alder said, and crossed to the cavity in four strides.

"No." Maren finally turned, and her face was wet, and I honestly couldn't tell you if it was spray or grief. "You don't touch him. You don't touch any of it. We carry him out or we leave him, but we don't *strip-mine* him—"

"Maren." My voice came out flatter than I meant it to. "She's right about the case. But she's right about the order too. Look at the ceiling."

They looked. The seam had widened. Through it, backlit, we could see the fracture running laterally across the entire vault—forty meters of ice deciding, all at once, that it was finished holding itself up. And beneath the fracture, directly beneath it, positioned by forty years of dumb geological luck, was the source chamber. Our cathedral. The archive. Edmund Voss in his sleeping bag of ice.

Everything we'd climbed for was about to fall into the meltwater, and the meltwater was about to fall off a mountain.

Alder did the thing I'll think about for the rest of my life, which is that he stopped. He stood there with his hands on the rusted case and he *stopped*, and I watched a man's entire operating system crash in real time. Because here was the creed, finally tested against something that couldn't be judged: the family took proof and rendered verdicts, and here was the proof, and here was the verdict, and the verdict was that none of it mattered. The glacier wasn't taking sides. The glacier was taking everything.

"The rope," he said. His voice had changed. "Felix. The rope and the litter frame. Can you anchor into that wall?"

I looked where he was looking—at the ice beside the cavity, dense old blue ice, pre-fracture, still sound—and my hands answered before my head did, because that's what digger's hands do. "If I get two good screws in. Maybe three minutes of work."

"You have less than that."

"Then stop talking to me."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about working while a glacier dies above your head: your hands stay calm. Your hands don't know. Your hands know soil and torque and the difference between ice that will hold a load and ice that will lie to you, and they go on knowing it while the rest of you is screaming in a register only dogs and dying mountains can hear. I drilled the first screw and the ice screamed back—a shriek of metal biting, then a groan from the vaults—and I drilled the second screw anyway.

Maren got Edmund free. That was her work, and she did it alone, and I'm not going to describe it because it belongs to her. What I saw was the result: a wrapped shape, heavy, carried the way you carry something that used to be a person. She brought him to the wall below my anchors and clipped him to the line, and her hands were shaking so badly she couldn't work the carabiner, and I reached past her and did it, and neither of us said anything, because there are moments when speech is just noise competing with the mountain.

Alder had the case. Of course Alder had the case—he'd have taken it into the flood itself, and for one bad moment I thought that was exactly the plan, that he'd stand there clutching thirty years of family justice while the roof came down. He looked at the case. He looked at Maren. He looked at the seam, which was now wide enough to show daylight through it, which meant the face above had opened like a mouth.

"Felix," he said. "When it goes—run the channel. Not the chamber mouth. The meltwater channel on the north side, the one the spring cut last week. It'll be open before the main collapse clears."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've been reading this ice since the icefall," he said, "and because it's the only direction that isn't down."

That was when the glacier stopped announcing and started executing.

* * *

I won't pretend to remember it in order. Memory under mortal pressure doesn't file things; it just keeps everything, unsorted, like a drawer you're afraid to open. Here is what the drawer holds:

The vault splitting along its fracture with a sound like the world's largest door being taken off its hinges, slow, almost gentle, ice the thickness of a church falling away from ice the thickness of a sky.

Maren screaming her father's name—not Edmund, her father, the other one, the one whose spare jacket is still in the ice a kilometer downslope—as if the dead could be warned.

The first block coming down. Not onto us—onto the spring. It hit the fountain head-on and the water went sideways, a white explosion, and the chamber filled with mist and roar and the light died all at once, and we were suddenly three people in the dark holding a dead man and a box of paper while a mountain committed suicide around us.

My third screw never went in. I cut the anchors loose and grabbed the line.

And Alder—I need to write this down exactly as it happened, because I know how it will look later, and I want the record to be honest—Alder dropped the case.

He dropped it. Thirty years of evidence, the murder, the cover-up, the whole rotten architecture of the family's justice, sealed in rusted steel—it hit the blue floor with a sound like a bell, one clear note under all that ruin, and he turned away from it and put his shoulder under Edmund Voss's body alongside Maren, and he shouted the only command I ever heard him give that wasn't a judgment:

"*North channel. NOW.*"

We ran. The floor tilted under us—I felt it tilt, the whole chamber settling as its foundations dissolved into meltwater—and the main mass came down behind us with a roar that wasn't sound anymore, just pressure, a fist of displaced air that picked me up and threw me into the channel mouth. Cold took my breath the way it always does, total and instant, a theft. The water was waist-deep and moving like a train and full of grinding ice, and I had the rope in one fist and Maren's collar in the other, and Alder had the body, and the channel carried us—there's no other word, we were cargo—down and out and away from the place where everything we'd come for was being buried in the dark.

* * *

We came out of the glacier's foot half-drowned, coughing meltwater, alive. Behind us the hanging valley was a white plume rising two hundred meters, dust and shattered ice catching the afternoon sun, beautiful the way avalanches are beautiful, which is to say from a distance, which is to say never.

The source was gone. I understood it before I'd finished vomiting water, lying on the gravel with my cheek against stones older than language: the chamber, the spring, the archive, the documents, Edmund Voss's remains—all of it sealed under a hundred thousand tons of fresh collapse, and beneath that, the meltwater already running, carrying whatever survived the crush down and out toward the sea. No trial. No exhibit A. No proof. Forty years of secret, solved at last by the oldest method there is: the earth closing its own case.

Maren knelt in the shallows with the wrapped body beside her, and she was laughing, or crying, or both, that terrible sound people make when the thing they climbed a mountain for dissolves in their hands. "It's gone," she kept saying. "It's all gone. Everything. There's nothing to *show* anyone."

Alder sat apart, soaked, empty-handed. I watched him look at his own palms as if checking whether the case had somehow ended up in them after all. An enforcer without evidence. A judge without a court. A man who had chosen, in the one second the land gave him to choose, a dead man's body over the truth—and who was only beginning to understand what that choice had made of him.

He caught me watching. And Alder Voss, who had chased us across a province, who had posted lies about us in supply towns, who had offered me a walk away and Maren a trial—Alder Voss said, in a voice scraped down to bedrock:

"I had it in my hands."

"I know," I said.

"And I let it go."

I looked at him—this man who had made violence into a vocation and called the bill justice—and for the first time I didn't see an enemy. I saw a man standing in the wreckage of his own arithmetic, adding up a sum that would never balance again.

"No," I said. "You didn't let it go. The mountain took it. All you let go of was—" I stopped, because I almost said *the past*, and that's too kind, too tidy. "All you chose was which weight to carry. Same as the rest of us."

Maren lifted her head. Water ran off her chin. And she looked at the plume still rising off the glacier, and then at Edmund's body, and then at the two of us—her cousin the enforcer, and the digger's kid who'd out-dug them all—and something settled in her face that scared me more than the calving had.

"There's no proof," she said slowly. "No documents. No remains but him. Nothing a court would touch." She stood up, dripping, and the wind off the ice pushed her hair straight back. "So the family gets away with it. Grandfather's murder, the cover-up, all of it—buried twice now. Once by Father, once by the glacier."

Nobody answered. The plume rose. Somewhere under all that fresh ice, the river was already finding its way down, patient as a debt.

"So we tell it," Maren said. "All of it. To everyone. And the first person who has to hear it—" she turned to Alder, and her voice didn't shake at all— "is you."

Alder looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at the mountain that had just taken everything he'd built his life on, and he said nothing at all—which, from Alder Voss, was the loudest answer there was.

Behind us, the glacier groaned once more, settling into its new shape, and began, very quietly, to melt.
