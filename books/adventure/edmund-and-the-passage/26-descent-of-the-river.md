## 26. Descent of the River

Here is what nobody tells you about watching thirty years of evidence drown: it sounds like applause.

The calving didn't announce itself politely. There was no single crack, no cinematic groan of warning. What happened instead was that the blue cathedral we'd spent nine days bleeding to reach simply *exhaled*—a sound I felt in my sternum before I heard it with my ears—and then the source chamber, the spring, the sealed shelf where Edmund Voss had waited in the ice since 1987, all of it began to come apart like a book dropped from a great height.

Alder went for the documents.

I want that on the record, wherever records still exist after today. He didn't hesitate, didn't weigh it, didn't sermonize. The chamber was failing around us—ceiling dropping in slabs the size of church pews, meltwater rising from ankle to knee to thigh in the time it took me to scream Maren's name—and Alder Voss, enforcer, judge, the man who'd hunted us across two hundred miles of bad country, turned his back on the archive his whole life had been built to protect or destroy, depending on which version of him you asked, and grabbed her instead.

Then the glacier finished its sentence, and everything went white.

* * *

I came to underwater, which is a phrase I'd like never to write again.

The flood had taken the chamber and given us to the river—the real one, the one that ran wrong, the one that flowed uphill out of the town and uphill out of every map anyone had ever drawn of this coast. Except now, with the glacier's belly torn open, the river wasn't running wrong anymore. It was running *down*, all at once, decades of impatience released in an afternoon, and we were inside it.

I don't remember deciding to grab the litter. I remember the cold—a cold that wasn't temperature so much as opinion, the water's firm conviction that I should stop existing—and I remember my hands finding the aluminum frame we'd used to carry Alder down from the icefall, and I remember holding on with the specific, unglamorous strength of a person who has dug shell middens for grocery money and knows exactly how much their body weighs and exactly how little the universe cares.

The litter became a raft because it had no other choice. So did we.

Maren surfaced beside me, one fist locked in the webbing, her other hand already checking Alder, who lay half across the frame with his ruined leg trailing in the current like something he'd finally decided to put down. His eyes were open. That surprised me more than the flood had.

"The case," he said. Water came out with the words. "The documents."

"Gone," I said.

He looked at me for a long moment. The river was doing seventy things at once around us—spinning us, lifting us, showing us the sky and then the rock walls of the gorge and then the sky again—but his face was very still.

"Good," he said.

I thought I'd misheard. Nobody says *good* over the drowning of the only proof that their family's justice was a murder's alibi. But he said it again, quieter, almost to himself, and then a wave hit us broadside and there was no more time for theology.

* * *

Riding a glacial flood through a river gorge is not like white-water rafting. I've done white-water rafting, once, badly, in a previous life under a different name, and the thing about commercial rafting is that someone has chosen the river for you, timed it, rated it, put a guide in the back with a whistle and a smile. This was none of that. This was the land settling an account of its own, and we were loose change caught in the drawer.

The gorge narrowed the way Maren's father's letter had said it would—the old route, the one that opened for weeks each decade, except now it was opening in the worst possible sense, scoured and roaring, the 1987 basecamp somewhere above us being unmade after thirty-eight years of standing patient as a crime scene. I thought of the tagged tarps. The rusted stove. The journal with its pages torn out. All of it going downstream ahead of us or behind us, I couldn't tell, everything moving at the speed of consequence.

"Feet up!" Maren shouted, when a boulder field materialized out of the spray like a decision being made against us. We got Alder's legs up—we got our own up—and the litter struck something with a sound I felt in my teeth, spun, and then the river did the thing rivers do, which is forgive you instantly and without meaning it, and set us drifting again in a long, deep channel between walls of granite older than any family's idea of justice.

For a while, nobody talked. There wasn't breath to spare.

But I'm a digger, and diggers read ground even when the ground is trying to kill them, and what I read, between the waves, was this: the water was brown with ground-up ice and grey with rock flour and threaded everywhere with pale debris—wood, fabric, metal, paper. Paper. Sheets of it, laminated by mud, tumbling past us like the world's most terrible confetti. Field notes. Maps. The ledger of a secret kept too long, finally being paid out.

One page slapped against the litter frame and stuck there, plastered flat by the current. I could see handwriting on it. My father's handwriting—I'd know that cramped, left-leaning script anywhere; I'd forged it twice as a teenager to sign my own report cards, which tells you everything about the household I grew up in.

I reached for it.

And here is the part I keep turning over, the part I suspect I'll be turning over for years: I reached for it, and then I stopped. My fingers were six inches away. The page sat there, trembling in the slipstream, offering itself. One sheet. Maybe a fragment of the confession, maybe nothing, maybe just a grocery list from 1987. But a thread. A start. Enough to say *see, it happened, we weren't liars, give us back the trial.*

I let it go.

It held on for another second, stubborn, my father's handwriting staring up at me through the water like a face at a window. Then the current took it, and it was gone, and I watched the place where it had been until there was no place anymore.

"You let it go," Maren said later—not accusingly. Wonderingly. She'd seen. Of course she'd seen. She missed nothing; she'd spent three decades missing nothing, and it had cost her every soft thing she owned.

"It was going to take my arm off at the elbow," I said, which was true and also completely beside the point, and she knew that too, and neither of us said anything else for a long time.

* * *

The gorge spat us out in the late afternoon, into the wide gravel valley below the glacier's tongue, where the river spread itself thin and exhausted across a mile of stones, suddenly shallow, suddenly almost gentle, as if embarrassed by its own behavior. We grounded on a bar of grey silt with a grinding lurch that dumped all three of us into six inches of freezing water, and I lay there on my back looking at the sky and laughing, or possibly sobbing, or possibly some third thing that doesn't have a name yet.

Maren crawled to Alder. He was conscious—had been conscious the whole way down, I realized, though he'd made no sound, not even when the litter bucked and his broken leg took the impact. He was grey as the silt and shivering in a way that wasn't voluntary, and when Maren peeled back the makeshift splint we'd fashioned from a tent pole and pack straps, we both saw the leg had gotten worse. Much worse. The flood hadn't been gentle with it.

"We need shelter," Maren said. Her voice had gone flat and practical, the voice of a woman who had learned to convert terror into logistics sometime in childhood and never found the off switch. "Fire. Dry clothes. Something for the pain."

"There." I pointed upstream along the bank, to a scatter of wreckage the flood had deposited like offerings: a section of corrugated roofing, bent double; a duffel bag, split, its contents strewn; and—because the glacier apparently had a sense of humor—a plastic cooler, intact, riding high on the gravel like a boat waiting patiently for passengers who would never come.

We dragged Alder to the lee side of the roofing, propped it against stones into something approximating a windbreak, and I got a fire going with the last dry tinder from Maren's pack and a lighter I'd carried through four identities and eleven towns because a digger learns early that fire is the difference between a camp and a corpse. The flames caught. The heat hit my hands and I nearly cried at the sheer stupid mercy of it.

Alder drank melted snow from the cooler. Maren wrapped him in everything dry we had left. And then, for a while, the three of us sat in a row against a piece of somebody's roof, watching the river run.

* * *

It was running right, now. That was the strangest part. All the way down the gorge it had run right—downhill, toward the sea, toward the town, the way rivers are supposed to—and I kept catching myself staring at it the way you'd stare at a dog walking on its hind legs, except inverted: a dog finally walking normally after a lifetime of tricks. The legend said the stream ran upstream to the source. The truth, I understood now, was simpler and stranger: the glacier had been holding the water back for decades, a reservoir behind a plug of ice, and the "wrongness" everyone had built stories on was just patience. The river hadn't been lying. It had been waiting. And today it stopped waiting, and everything it had waited for went to the sea at once.

Including the proof.

"No archive," Maren said quietly. She wasn't talking to me, exactly. She was talking to the river, or to her father, or to the girl she'd been at nineteen, standing in this same valley, waiting for a rescue that never came. "No trial. No proof."

"No proof," I agreed.

She turned and looked at me, and her face in the firelight was stripped down to something young and furious and lost. "Thirty years," she said. "I gave it thirty years. The maps, the letters, the interviews, the nights I sat in archives reading other people's grief hoping it would rhyme with mine. And it's gone. It's *water* now. In an hour it'll be indistinguishable from every other drop in the ocean."

"Yes," I said.

"And you're just—" She made a gesture that took in me, the fire, the whole wrecked evening. "*Fine.* You're sitting there like you lost a bus transfer."

I considered that. Considered what I actually felt, down under the exhaustion and the cold and the bruise blooming on my hip where the litter frame had caught me. What I felt was light. Not happy—light. Like something I'd been carrying so long I'd mistaken it for a spine had finally been set down.

"My father spent twenty years keeping that secret," I said. "Buried it, lied about it, let the world call him a fraud and a coward rather than hand it over. He lost his career, his name, and eventually his kid—that's me, hi—to protect it. And you know why? Because handing it over meant the Voss family would eat itself alive. Grandfather's crime, uncle's cover-up, cousin's crusade, all of it detonating at once. He did the math and decided the living mattered more than the truth. Wrong math, probably. Coward's math, maybe. But he did it, and it cost him everything, and today the glacier did in forty minutes what he spent a lifetime doing slowly." I fed a stick to the fire. "So no, I'm not fine. But I'm also not carrying it anymore. Nobody is. The river took the job."

Maren stared at the water. Her jaw worked.

"He chose them," she said at last. "Your father. He chose the family over Edmund. Over my father. Over the truth."

"He chose survival," I said. "It's not the same thing, but it's not nothing either. Ask Alder."

We both looked at Alder. He was half-asleep against the roofing, pain-drunk, firelit, and he opened his eyes anyway, because I think the man had trained himself never to be fully unconscious in front of witnesses.

"I heard that," he said.

"Then weigh in," Maren said, and there was something raw in it—not a challenge, exactly. A plea dressed as one.

Alder was quiet for a while. The river filled the silence, running right, running down, taking everything.

"I stood in that chamber," he said finally, each word costing him visibly, "with the documents in reach. Everything the family needs. Everything I crossed an icefall for. Lost a man for." His voice snagged on that—he'd told us about the crevasse, about the teammate he hadn't roped, about the arithmetic, and I'd watched the creed crack in his face and reseal itself, badly. "And the ceiling was coming down, and Maren was ten feet away, and I—" He stopped. Started again. "There wasn't a decision. That's what I keep coming back to. I've spent my whole life believing justice was a decision, a discipline, a thing you enforce whether it costs or not. And when it came time to choose, I didn't decide anything. I just moved. Toward her. Away from the papers." He closed his eyes. "Maybe that's the answer. Maybe justice isn't what you enforce. Maybe it's what your hands do when there's no time to think."

Nobody spoke. Downstream, the river carried the last of the glacier's hoard toward the sea—paper and bone and thirty-eight years of silence, all of it dissolving into salt water where no court would ever find it.

"So that's it," Maren said, but softer now. "It's gone. We survive, and there's nothing to show for it. No vindication. No reckoning. Just three people and a story."

"Just three people and a story," I said.

And then I saw her look change—saw the flat practical thing behind her eyes catch fire, slow and dangerous, the way it must have caught fire in 1987 when a nineteen-year-old decided that if no one was coming to save the truth, she'd go get it herself.

"No," she said. "Wait. Not *just*."

* * *

Night came down over the valley. The fire burned low. Alder slept, truly slept, for the first time since the icefall, his breathing ragged but steady, and Maren sat cross-legged across the flames from me with her notebook—the one surviving artifact of the entire expedition, waterproof, absurdly, the way only the least important things are—open on her knees.

"Testimony," she said. "That's what survives. Yours. Mine. His." She nodded at Alder without looking at him. "Three witnesses who were inside the source chamber, who read the documents before the water took them, who know what the family did in 1987 and why the 'justice' exists and whose name it launders. The glacier destroyed the evidence. Fine. Evidence was always the law's currency, and the law failed us thirty years ago anyway—that's the whole reason the family exists." She tapped the notebook. "But stories are different. Stories don't need a chain of custody. Stories need tellers."

"You're talking about going home," I said. "To the town. To the families. Telling them everything, with nothing to back it up but our word."

"And his word," Maren said. "If he'll give it."

We both looked at Alder again. Asleep. Or performing sleep. With that man it was genuinely impossible to tell.

"They'll call us liars," I said. "Some of them. The ones who need to."

"Some will," Maren agreed. "And some won't. And some will hate us for telling the truth, and some will hate us for waiting so long, and none of it will be clean, and none of it will be enough, and it's still the only thing left that's real." She looked up at me across the fire, and for the first time since I'd met her—since the road outside town, since the wary handshake of two people who trusted absolutely no one—her eyes weren't hunting anything. They were asking.

"Felix," she said. "Will you stand with me? No proof. No archive. Just the story, told together, in front of everyone. Will you do that?"

The river ran on in the dark, running right at last, carrying the past to the sea. Somewhere behind us the glacier was closing its wound, sealing the route for another decade, keeping whatever secrets remained. Ahead of us was a town full of grieving people and a family full of guilty ones and no document on earth to make any of it easier.

I thought about my father, alone for twenty years with a truth he wouldn't spend. I thought about the page in the water, and my hand that reached, and my hand that let go.

"Yeah," I said. "Together."

Maren nodded once, and wrote the date in the notebook, and under it, in careful block letters, the first line of the only evidence we had left.

Across the fire, in the dark, Alder opened his eyes.
