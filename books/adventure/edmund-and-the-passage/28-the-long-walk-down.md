## 28. The Long Walk Down

The litter took us two hours to build and cost Maren a ski pole, half a rope, and most of her patience.

We made it from the collapsed tent frame Alder's dead teammate had left at the icefall camp, lashed with cord and the last of our tape, a stretcher of tent fabric and crossed poles that sagged in the middle like a hammock with opinions. Alder lay in it with his leg strapped straight, his face the color of the glacier, and watched us work with the expression of a man being fitted for his own coffin and finding the craftsmanship adequate.

"You're doing the poles wrong," he said.

"We're doing the poles right," Maren said. "You're doing the lying still wrong."

"I've carried men out of worse country than this."

"You've *dragged* men out of worse country," Maren said, tightening a knot. "There's a difference, and the difference is what happened to your leg."

He didn't answer that. His leg was broken above the ankle, a spiral fracture from the crevasse lip he'd caught it on, and the splint we'd made from an ice axe and foam pad was a joke we were all pretending wasn't. He'd need a hospital, a surgeon, probably hardware in the bone. What he'd get was a litter ride down a river gorge, carried by the two people he'd spent the last week threatening, in weather that couldn't decide between rain and snow.

I tested the carrying handles. The frame groaned but held.

"Two of us can't carry him and move fast," I said. "We pick a pace and we keep it. Six hours to the gorge, two days down the gorge if the trail's still there, one day to the road."

"Three days," Maren said. "Maybe four. He's not light."

"I'm standing right here," Alder said.

"That's the problem," Maren said.

We lifted him. He weighed what a big man weighs when he's stopped fighting gravity, and the first hundred meters were a comedy of stumbles and corrections, Maren on the front handles and me on the back, Alder's boots pointing at the sky like a flag of surrender nobody had agreed to. Then we found the rhythm. Front steps, back steps, call the obstacles, breathe on the fours. It's the same rhythm as a dig, actually—grid work, two people moving soil in tandem—and I let my body do what it knew while my head did the arithmetic of distance and daylight.

Alder talked first. Of course he did. He'd been unconscious for six hours and he'd woken up inside a story he no longer controlled, and I was beginning to understand that silence was the one thing he couldn't stand.

"The family will come looking for me," he said, somewhere in the second hour.

"Good," Maren said, not turning around. "They can help carry."

"They won't help. They'll come to finish what I couldn't." He shifted in the litter, and I felt the frame flex. "I had a team of four. You've seen what's left of them. The family doesn't spend people lightly, but it spends them. And now it knows where you're going, and what you have, or had."

"The evidence is gone," Maren said. "It's in the river."

"The *testimony* isn't."

I stopped walking. Maren took two more steps before she noticed, and then the litter swung, and Alder made a sound through his teeth.

"Put me down if you're going to have a meeting," he said.

"We're not having a meeting," I said. "We're having a negotiation. And it starts now, because you're right—there's no proof anymore. There's just what three people say happened on this glacier. So we'd better agree on what that is before we're in a room full of people who want it to be something else."

Maren set her handles down. We lowered Alder onto a flat rock beside the trail, propped his splinted leg on my pack, and the three of us stood—or lay—in the rain, looking at each other like people who'd just realized they were in the same boat and had only one oar between them.

"Terms," Maren said.

* * *

We negotiated through the afternoon, in the way you negotiate when everyone is exhausted and one party is in shock: badly, then honestly, then badly again.

Alder wanted a full account, names and dates, delivered to the family first, privately, before any of it went public. He called it *order*. Maren wanted everything public immediately—the town, the families, the archive she'd build from whatever survived. She called it *truth*. I wanted, more than anything, for neither of them to look at me when they said the word *Nakamura*, and I said so, which was the first honest sentence anyone had produced in an hour.

"Your father's name is in this," Maren said. "It's going to come out."

"I know. I'm not asking you to hide it. I'm asking you to let me be the one who says it." I heard my own voice do something I didn't recognize. "He's still alive. He gets to hear it from me before he reads it in a newspaper, if he reads newspapers, which he probably doesn't, because he's spent forty years being a man who doesn't."

Alder watched me from the litter with an expression I'd have called clinical if it hadn't been so tired. "You want to control the narrative."

"I want to *carry my share* of it," I said. "That's all any of us are asking. That's the whole negotiation. Nobody gets to hand the whole weight to somebody else."

That, oddly, was the sentence that moved him. I saw it land—the small shift in his jaw, the way his eyes went to his broken leg and then to the sky, as if checking whether the glacier had heard him and would be taking notes.

"The family will call it a verdict," he said finally. "Whatever we agree, however we frame it. They'll want a verdict. Someone guilty, someone punished, a line drawn. That's the only grammar they have."

"Then we change the grammar," Maren said.

"You can't change grammar. You can only speak it correctly or badly."

"Watch me."

* * *

We walked until dark. Or rather, we carried until dark—Alder's weight distributed between us, swapped every hour at his insistence because, he said, uneven carrying was how people got hurt, and he would not add injury to injury. He was a nightmare patient and an excellent logistics officer, and the two identities kept colliding in him visibly, like weather fronts.

In the third hour he started telling us where the trail was washed out. In the fourth he started telling us why. In the fifth, without any transition at all, he started telling us about his grandfather.

"He was a careful man," Alder said, to the sky. "That's what nobody understands. Not a monster. A careful man. The murder—your grandfather ordered it, Maren, we've agreed on that—but my grandfather was the one who understood what it would cost if it came out. So he built the family. The justice system. The whole creed. Not because he believed in it. Because it was a vault with people inside it."

"That's worse," Maren said.

"It's more useful," Alder said. "Monsters you can kill. Architects you have to *unbuild*."

We forded a side stream, the water at our knees and furious with snowmelt, and for a while the only negotiation was with the current. Then we climbed, and Alder directed us around a slope of rotten rock he'd have sent other people over without a thought a week ago, and I noticed he was doing a thing I recognized: he was teaching us the mountain. Handing over the route, the hazards, the family's old paths, piece by piece, the way you'd hand over evidence. Every instruction was a small confession. *I know this slope because we moved a body down it. I know this crossing because we waited here for a man we were going to ruin.*

By the time we made camp under an overhang, the terms had shape.

The account would be full—everything, including the ugly parts, including Maren's grandfather, including my father's burial of the evidence, including Alder's own years of enforcement. It would go to the families of the vanished first, face to face, before anyone else. Then to the town. Then to whoever Maren could get to listen, which she predicted, correctly, would eventually be everyone.

And the weight would be split. Maren would carry her family's crime. I would carry my father's. Alder would carry his own—and his, we all understood, was the heaviest, because his was the only one still being committed.

"You understand they'll expel you," Maren said, as we ate. "The family. What you're agreeing to do. They'll erase you the way they erased the expedition."

"I've been erased before," Alder said. "It's quieter than you'd think."

I looked at him across the stove flame, this man who had hunted us for two hundred kilometers, who had lost a teammate to a crevasse he'd chosen not to rope, who had saved Maren's life with his body and paid for it with his leg, and I tried to find the villain I'd been running from and couldn't locate him. He'd never been a villain. He'd been a load-bearing wall in a building that should have come down decades ago, and now he was pulling himself out of the structure and asking the building to fall on him.

"Why?" I asked him. It came out blunter than I meant. "Why any of this? You could have walked away at the icefall. Taken the documents, taken the trial, done your job."

He was quiet long enough that the stove hissed into the silence.

"Because I did the arithmetic," he said. "For thirty years I did the arithmetic. Who threatens the family, what it costs to remove the threat, what it costs to leave them. And on the icefall I stood over that crevasse and I did it one more time—one life against the mission—and for the first time the numbers came out wrong. Not because the numbers changed." He looked at his leg. "Because I did."

* * *

The second day the rain came in sideways and the gorge narrowed until the walls pressed in and the river roared below us on a shelf of trail barely wide enough for the litter. We walked in single file, Maren calling the footing, me watching Alder's face for the gray that meant shock, and in the worst stretch—a slide scar where the mountain had shrugged off its own skin—Alder went quiet and stayed quiet for an hour, and I realized he was in pain and managing it the way he managed everything: as an arithmetic problem. Breath by breath. Cost against distance.

At the midday halt he asked to sit up.

"I want to add a term," he said.

Maren and I exchanged a look over his head. "Go on," she said.

"The families. When you tell them. Some of them will want me dead, and they'll be right to." He said it the way you'd read a shipping manifest. "I'm asking that when that day comes, nobody stands between them and me. No lawyers, no family, no—" he gestured at the two of us with a bandaged hand—"*rescuers*. Whatever they decide, I'll hear it standing. Or lying down. But I'll hear it."

"That's not a term, that's a death wish," Maren said.

"It's a *price list*," he said. "I spent my life setting prices for other people. I'd like to know mine."

I crouched by the litter. Up close his face was wrecked—dehydrated, bruised, the stubble gray at the edges—and his eyes were the clearest thing on this mountain.

"Here's my counter-term," I said. "You hear what they decide. And then you live with it. Whatever it is. Because a verdict you don't survive isn't justice, it's just an ending, and we've had enough endings on this glacier to last the town a century. You don't get to buy out of this with your life. That's the easy payment. You're going to pay the hard one."

He stared at me for a long moment. Then, to my astonishment, he laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a gate that hadn't been opened in years.

"The child out-digs the father," he said. "And out-negotiates him, I'd guess. Fine. Agreed. I live with it." He settled back against the litter frame. "You know what the worst part is? My grandfather would have respected you. That's how the rot works. It respects exactly the things that could have stopped it."

We walked on. The gorge opened by degrees, the walls stepping back, the river widening and slowing as if it too were coming down out of the high country and remembering how to be ordinary water. Somewhere below us, under the town, under the middens I'd spent weeks reading, that same water ran through its impossible uphill channel, keeping its books, keeping its dead. I thought about the carved stone in Maren's pack, the mark my father had cut, and what it had turned out to mean—not a claim, an apology—and I thought: an apology is a debt you carry instead of paying. Maybe that was what we were all doing now. Carrying it. Together, badly, at two kilometers an hour.

In the last light of the second day, the gorge spat us out onto the river flats, and we saw, far downstream, the first lights of the fishing town, small and yellow and utterly indifferent.

Maren set down her handles and stood looking at them, her hands still shaped around a weight that wasn't there anymore.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"Tomorrow," I said.

From the litter, Alder said nothing at all. But when I turned, I saw that he was watching the lights too, and that his lips were moving—counting, I realized. Houses. Families. Names he'd have to stand in front of.

He reached thirty before the dark took the town away, and then he said, quietly, in a voice I'd only heard him use once before, at the icefall:

"I'll need you both there. Not to protect me." He met my eyes. "To make sure I don't stop halfway through."

That night I didn't sleep. I sat up under the overhang with the stove between my knees and rehearsed the first sentence I'd say to the families, and threw it away, and rehearsed another, and threw that away too, until Maren, without opening her eyes, said: "There's no first sentence. There's just the truth, in order, and then you shut up and let them be angry."

So I wrote it that way instead, in my head, in the dark: the truth, in order. The tide. The cave. The stone. The journal, and the torn pages, and the man my father had been and the man he'd tried not to become. And at the end of the list, tomorrow, three people standing in front of a town that had kept a ledger for thirty-seven years, ready to read the entries out loud.

The river ran past us in the dark, down toward the sea, carrying nothing we could prove.

Carrying everything we could say.
