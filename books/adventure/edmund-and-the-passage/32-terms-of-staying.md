## 32. Terms of Staying

I have never been good at asking for things. The Voss family doesn't ask; we collect, we judge, we take our payment in silence and call it dignity. My father asked for exactly one thing in his life—a letter home from a glacier—and the family's answer was thirty years of pretending he'd never existed. So when I say I rehearsed this conversation, understand that rehearsal is as close to prayer as my people get.

I rehearsed it on the walk down from the headland. I rehearsed it over breakfast, while Felix ate toast like a man defusing it, and I lost my nerve twice and talked about midden stratigraphy instead, which Felix found suspicious enough to raise one eyebrow—the left one, the one that means *you're lying, but politely*. I rehearsed it in the shower, where all honest speeches go to die. And then, on the third evening after Alder's road took him west, I stopped rehearsing and just said it, because Felix was packing.

Not leaving-packing. That's the thing you have to understand about Felix Nakamura: there is no such thing as not-leaving-packing. There is only packing that happens to be interrupted. Their duffel lives by the door like a dog that's never quite sure it's been adopted. Their boots sit pointing outward. I once watched them sleep with their jacket folded within arm's reach, and I understood then that I wasn't courting a person so much as negotiating with a weather system.

The duffel was out because Felix had been offered the survey job again—properly this time, on paper, with the university's letterhead and a start date three weeks out—and they'd said *let me think about it*, which in Nakamura means *watch me leave without saying goodbye*.

"You're going to run," I said.

Felix didn't look up from folding a shirt that did not need folding. "I'm going to think."

"You're thinking with your boots on."

"That's how I do my best thinking." They set the shirt down, finally, and looked at me, and I saw the thing I'd seen on the glacier a dozen times: that flat, level attention, the digger's gaze, reading me like soil. "Say what you came to say, Maren."

So I said it. Not the way I'd rehearsed. Worse. Truer.

"Stay," I said. "Not because of the debt. Not because of Alder, or the archive, or the town, or your father's stone, or any of the forty reasons we've both been hiding behind since the ice let us go. Stay because I want you here. That's the whole term. There are no other terms."

* * *

You should know something about how Felix receives good news. Bad news, they're magnificent at. Bad news they fold into their pack alongside everything else and carry uphill without complaint—I've watched them carry grief up a hanging valley at four thousand meters and never once ask to put it down. But kindness undoes them. Kindness makes their hands unsteady. On the descent, when I'd told them the truth about Edmund and the stone and my father's promise, Felix had gone quiet in a way I mistook for anger until I realized it was the quiet of a person being handed something they'd already decided they'd never get.

Now they stood in the doorway of the rented room above the chandlery, holding a folded shirt like a shield, and said nothing for long enough that the tide could have turned twice.

"I don't know how to stay," they said finally.

"That's not true. You stayed through a calving season. You stayed through my family. You stayed through Alder's sermon, which would've broken most people inside of ten minutes."

"That was survival. Survival isn't the same skill." They turned the shirt over. "Survival has an end date. You survive *until*. Staying is—" They stopped. Started again, which Felix almost never does; they speak the way they dig, one clean stroke at a time. "Every place I've ever lived, I left before it could leave me first. Towns. Digs. People. My father taught me that without ever saying it out loud. He stayed somewhere once—one expedition, one source, one secret—and look what staying cost him. Everything. Me. So I learned the other lesson instead. Keep moving and nothing can be taken from you, because you were never attached to anything long enough for it to matter."

"And how's that working out?"

A ghost of a smile. Wry, tired, real. "Terribly. Thanks for asking."

* * *

We walked down to the water because neither of us can have a serious conversation indoors; we're both field creatures, and walls make us feel lied to. The tide was coming in over the flats, patient as a clerk, and the headland stood black against the last light with its cave mouth sealed beneath the swell, admission collected, ledger closed. Felix looked at it the way they always do—not with longing, exactly. With accounting.

"When I was eight," they said, "my father took me to a dig in the high desert. Three weeks. Longest he ever stayed anywhere with me. I remember thinking the tents were permanent. I remember asking him if we lived there now, and he laughed—not mean, just tired—and said, 'Nothing lives anywhere, kid. Everything's just passing through.' I built my whole life on that sentence. I thought it was wisdom." They kicked a stone down toward the waterline. "It was a wound wearing wisdom's coat."

"My father had one too," I said. "'The truth keeps better than the body.' He wrote it in his last letter. The family read it as paranoia. Turns out it was instructions."

We stood there, two orphans of two different disasters, watching the water come home.

"Here's what terrifies me," Felix said, and their voice did something I hadn't heard before—it dropped its armor mid-sentence, deliberately, like setting down a load to show you how heavy it was. "Glaciers don't scare me. Calving seasons don't scare me. Alder Voss didn't scare me, and I want that on the record. What scares me is a Tuesday. Some ordinary Tuesday a year from now, when nothing's wrong, when the survey's going fine and you're at the archive and the kettle works, and I'll feel the pull anyway. The old pull. Pack, boots, door. Because that's the shape my fear makes. It doesn't look like danger. It looks like leaving."

"So don't leave on a Tuesday."

They laughed—short, startled, genuine. "That's your plan? Scheduling?"

"It's how my family handled everything else badly. Let me handle one thing badly in a new direction." I turned to face them fully, because this part deserved eye contact, even though eye contact with Felix is like standing close to a fire—you want it, and it costs. "Listen to me. I'm not asking you to stop being someone who could leave. I know better than anyone that you can't amputate thirty years of motion and call the stump healed. I'm asking you to stay *knowing* you could go. Every day. That's the only kind of staying worth anything—the kind with the door unlocked."

Felix was quiet again. But their hands had gone still, and stillness, in Felix Nakamura, is an event. Seismic. Somewhere inland, seismographs were probably twitching.

"The university job," they said slowly, "has a three-year term."

"I saw the letter."

"They want middens catalogued. Shell horizons, seasonal occupation layers. It's—" they caught themselves, embarrassed by their own enthusiasm, and pushed through it, which cost them visibly—"it's the best work I've ever been offered. Slow work. Work that assumes you'll *finish*."

"There it is," I said. "The terrifying part. Not the glacier. The finish line."

"Don't psychoanalyze me while the tide's coming in. It's unsporting."

* * *

Here is what I did not say on the beach, because I am a coward in exactly one direction and it's the direction marked *Maren's own feelings*: that I understood the terror from the inside. That the Voss family raised me to hold ground so rigidly that I never once chose it—that staying, for me, was never courage, just inheritance, the way running was never cowardice for Felix, just inheritance. We were each handed the other's disease. Maybe that's why this works, when it works. Maybe love is just two people agreeing to swap symptoms.

What I did say, later, over the bad stove in the rented room, was smaller and harder:

"Ask me something," I said. "One question. Whatever you need answered before you can decide."

Felix considered the ceiling. The stove ticked. Outside, a gull made its argument to the night.

"If I stay," they said, "and someday I wake up and the pull wins—someday I'm gone before dawn, no note, boots pointed outward—what will you do?"

And there it was. The real question. Not *will you let me go*—they knew I would; I'd proved that much on the ice. The question was whether my staying could survive their leaving. Whether love, in our particular dialect, meant permanence or just presence, honestly given, honestly withdrawn.

"I'd be furious," I said. "For about a week. Then I'd catalogue the empty side of the closet, because that's how I grieve, and then I'd wait. Not forever. I'm not a saint and I'm not my mother. But I'd wait longer than seems sensible, and if you came back I wouldn't make you grovel, and if you didn't I'd tell the story of you the way you deserve—accurately, including the ugly parts. That's the whole vow. That's everything I've got."

Felix looked at me for a long time. Then they got up, crossed the room, and did something I will remember on my deathbed: they picked up the duffel, carried it across the room, and put it in the wardrobe. Closed the door on it. Didn't unpack it—God, no, let's not be hysterical—but closed the door.

"Not a promise," they said. "An experiment. Ninety days. If I make it ninety days without the pull winning, we renegotiate the terms."

"Ninety days," I agreed. "And the survey job?"

A pause. The longest pause yet. I watched them weigh it—not the job, I realized, but the version of themselves who takes a three-year contract, who finishes things, whose boots point inward. Watching someone decide to become a new person is like watching ice move. Nothing appears to happen, and then everything has.

"I'll sign Monday," Felix said.

* * *

They nearly broke by Thursday.

I want that recorded too, because the story people will tell—the one Maren Voss writes into her history, the tidy version—is that Felix Nakamura came in from the cold and settled like snowfall. The truth is messier. Wednesday night I woke at two a.m. to find the bed empty and the wardrobe open and the duffel half-packed, and Felix sitting on the floor beside it in the dark, not crying, not moving, just breathing the way you breathe at altitude. Deliberately. One stroke at a time.

I sat down next to them. Didn't touch them. Touch would've been a verdict.

"Tell me where you'd go," I said.

"What?"

"If you left tonight. Tell me the route. First town, first dig, first name you'd borrow."

A long silence. Then, slowly, wonderingly, like a man reading his own handwriting and finding it changed: "I don't know. I actually don't—I keep reaching for it and it's not there. The map's blank, Maren. When did the map go blank?"

"Somewhere between the icefall and the kitchen, probably. It happens gradually. Then all at once."

They repacked the duffel back into the wardrobe themselves. I watched. It took three tries, because their hands weren't steady, and I loved them so hard in that moment I had to look at the wall for a second and think about sediment.

On Friday, they signed the contract. The pen hovered over the line for maybe four seconds—an eternity, in Felix time—and then came down clean, one stroke, the digger's signature, no hesitation in the ink.

"There," they said, sounding surprised. "That's done."

"That's done," I agreed.

"I might throw up."

"Noted for the record."

* * *

Sunday evening, we walked out to the headland one more time, because Felix wanted to see the cave at high tide—wanted, I think, to stand in front of the thing that started everything and test whether it still pulled. The water was up, the passage drowned, the ledger sealed. Just black rock and green sea and the smell of salt and kelp, the same as it had been for ten thousand years, indifferent as arithmetic.

"Well?" I asked.

Felix stood there a long moment, hands in pockets, wind pulling at their hair. Reading the shore. But differently now, I thought—or reading a different shore.

"It's still beautiful," they said. "But it doesn't feel like a door anymore. It feels like a place." A pause. "Places are allowed to just be places. Nobody told me that."

"Your father, probably. By omission."

"Yeah." They didn't flinch from it this time. That's new too—the grief sitting where it sits, visited regularly instead of fled from. "Monday I start the survey. Tuesday's coming eventually. I'll let you know how it goes."

"Day by day?"

"Hour by hour, early on. Fair warning."

"Accepted." I took their hand, and Felix Nakamura—who has crossed rope bridges over floodwater, who has ridden a glacial flood itself, who has stared down my cousin Alder and lived—looked at our joined hands with the expression of a person handling something explosive.

"One more term," they said quietly. "If I ever do leave—if the pull ever wins, someday, some bad year—you come find me. Not to drag me back. Just to remind me there's a here to come back to. Deal?"

"Deal," I said. "But I'm charging admission."

Felix smiled—the full one, the rare one, the one the glacier never got. "Fair," they said. "Everything worth entering costs something."

Monday morning, they walked down to the midden site with the university crew, toolbox in hand, boots pointing forward, and I stood at the window and watched them kneel in the dirt and begin to teach a nineteen-year-old intern how to read a shell horizon, patient as tide, going nowhere.

The pull would come back. We both knew it. Fear doesn't die; it just learns manners.

But that was tomorrow's problem, and tomorrow, for the first time in either of our lives, had terms.
