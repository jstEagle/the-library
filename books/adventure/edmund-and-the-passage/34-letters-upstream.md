## 34. Letters Upstream

The stationery store in Harrow Point had exactly three kinds of paper: lined school notebooks, a pad of invoice forms left over from some defunct cannery, and one box of heavy cream-colored stock that the owner swore had been there since before the mill closed. I bought the cream stock. If you're going to build a bridge out of nothing but words, you might as well use good timber.

I'd been putting it off for eleven days. Not consciously—consciousness would have been an improvement. I'd cleaned the apartment twice. I'd reorganized my trowels by age of handle-wrap, which is a thing only diggers do and only when they are avoiding something with their whole skeleton. Maren watched me do it over the rim of her coffee mug with the patience of a woman who has waited thirty years for answers and can therefore wait another afternoon for a man to stop alphabetizing his brushes.

"You could just call him," she said.

"He doesn't have a phone."

"Everyone has a phone."

"Not him. He thinks they're listening devices with a camera bolted on as an afterthought." I set the brushes down in what I realized was alphabetical order and started over. "He's not wrong often enough to be easy to dismiss."

"So write."

"I'm getting to it."

"You've been getting to it since Tuesday." She turned back to her papers—the family records she'd been excavating all winter, box after box of correspondence that read like sediment layers, each stratum a little more careful about what it admitted. "Felix. You crossed a glacier. You rode a flood. You carried Alder Voss on your back for two days like he was a sack of wet cement and you complained less about that."

"The cement didn't talk."

"Write the letter."

So I sat down at the kitchen table under the window where the light comes in gray and generous off the water, and I took out the good paper, and I stared at it for forty minutes.

Here's the problem nobody warns you about with estrangement: it isn't silence. Silence is easy. Silence is just weather. Estrangement is a structure you build without noticing you're building it, brick by unanswered year, until one day you look up and there's a wall between you and the person who taught you to read soil, and the wall has a door in it somewhere but you've forgotten which side the hinges are on.

My father and I hadn't spoken in six years. Before that, four years. Before that, a shouting match in a parking lot in Innsbruck that I still replayed at 3 a.m. with better dialogue. The pattern was always the same: I'd find something of his—a mark on a stone, a habit in my own hands, the way I braced a collapse with my shoulder instead of my palms because he'd broken his collarbone once and taught himself around it—and I'd feel the whole architecture of absence shift, and I'd tell myself next year. Next year I'd write. Next year I'd call. Next year the wall would have a door.

Next year had arrived wearing glacier meltwater and carrying a dead man's name carved in stone, and it was done waiting.

* * *

The first draft began: *Dear Father,* and lasted one sentence before I crossed it out. Too formal. He'd hear the formality and answer it with more formality and we'd exchange four polite letters over two years and accomplish nothing, which was, historically, our record.

The second draft began: *You were right about the Vinter Glacier,* which was true—he'd written a paper in '84 arguing the river system ran upstream through the massif, and everyone had laughed at him, and he'd been right—but starting with *you were right* felt like handing him ammunition, and I wasn't sure yet whether this letter was a peace treaty or a field report or both, so that went in the pile too.

The third draft I actually wrote. All of it. Four pages, front and back, everything: the cave, the stone, Maren, Alder, the journals, the source, the collapse, the flood, the loss of the evidence, the long walk down, the town, the telling. I wrote it the way I dig—fast and honest, no smoothing, letting the stratigraphy show. Then I read it back and heard my mother's voice in my head saying, *Felix, you have just handed this man a shovel and pointed him at your chest,* and I put it in the drawer.

Not because it was wrong. Because it was mine, and I wasn't ready for it to be his.

* * *

Maren found me at the midden site the next morning, which surprised her, because I'd been avoiding the midden site too—it belonged to the university survey now, officially, my name on the permit, and there's a particular discomfort in supervising work you spent years doing for cash under tables. She came down the beach path with two coffees and the particular walk of someone pretending the coffee is the errand.

"You didn't come home last night," she said.

"I came home. I slept in the boat shed."

"That's not home."

"It has a roof."

"Felix." She handed me a coffee and looked out at the tide line, where the survey crew was already staking grids in the wet sand. The kids from the harbor school would be along at ten; I'd promised to teach them to read shell layers, and I'd been looking forward to it and dreading it in equal measure, because teaching means staying, and staying is still a language I speak with an accent. "Did you write it?"

"A version."

"What's wrong with it?"

"Nothing. That's what's wrong with it. It's a report. It's accurate and complete and it reads like a site summary." I drank the coffee. It was terrible, harbor-town coffee, burnt and proud of it. "He'll read it and he'll know every fact and he won't know a single thing about how any of it felt. And then he'll write back a report of his own, and we'll have exchanged documents, and in five years we'll have a complete archaeological record of each other and still never have spoken."

Maren considered this. She has a way of considering things that makes you wish you'd said something simpler.

"My father wrote letters too," she said finally. "Thirty years of them. To the family, to the university, to anyone who might listen. All reports. All accurate." She turned the cup in her hands. "The only time he ever told the truth about how it felt was the last one, the night before he went up the glacier. One page. *I am afraid and I am going anyway and if Nakamura's boy ever asks, tell him his father was the best digger I ever saw and I betrayed him for it.* That page did more than thirty years of reports."

I looked at her. "You never told me that part."

"You never asked how it felt." A ghost of a smile. "We're a matched set, you and I. Reports and shovels and doors we forget to open."

* * *

That night I threw out all three drafts and started over on a fresh sheet, and this time I didn't plan. I just wrote the way you talk to someone across a campfire when you're too tired to perform.

*Pop.*

He hated *Dad*. Said it sounded like a man in a commercial. Hated *Father* more—said it sounded like a summons. *Pop*, he said, was what his own father had been called, a fisherman, a man with hands like rope, and it was the only word for a parent that had never lied to him. So: *Pop.* First time I'd written it since I was nineteen.

*I'm writing from Harrow Point. I live here now, which will mean more to you than to most people, since you're the one who taught me that living somewhere is a skill and I apparently learned it late. I took the university survey. The middens along the headland. I catalogued the first grid last week and found a bone needle from before the mill, before the cannery, maybe before the town had a name, and I thought: he would want to see this. And then I thought: that's why I'm writing. Not because I forgive you and not because I don't. Because for six years I've been finding things and thinking he would want to see this, and I'm tired of mailing those thoughts to an address that doesn't exist.*

Then I told him. Not the report—the other thing. I told him about the lantern in the cave and how it smelled of rust and old smoke and how for one stupid second I'd expected to hear his voice around the bend of the passage. I told him about the stone with his mark on it and how I'd carried it for weeks believing it was an accusation, evidence, a claim—and how Maren had shown me it was none of those things. I told him about the journal, the torn pages, the line that had stopped me cold on the glacier with my gloves full of ice: *If anything happens, it was my idea, not the boy's.*

*I read that line four times before I understood it. You knew. Even then, you knew I'd come looking someday. You wrote a sentence into a journal you expected to die beside so that a stranger with a rifle would aim at you instead of me. I spent six years calling you a coward for running. You weren't running from them, Pop. You were standing between them and me the only way you could—from far away, where your shadow couldn't reach me.*

I stopped there, because my eyes had gone unreliable, and because the next part needed care.

*Here's what I found at the source, since you'll want it recorded properly: nothing. The flood took it. Every document, both bodies, the whole archive, into the meltwater, gone. There is no proof anymore. There's only what we say happened, and what Alder Voss says happened, and the story Maren is building out of her family's records. You'd think that would make me furious—all that climbing, all that blood, for the water to keep the books in the end. But I stood below the glacier while it calved and I felt lighter than I've felt since I was a kid digging in your test pits. The debt's paid, Pop. Yours, theirs, mine. The water collected. Nobody owes anybody anything now except the truth, and the truth turns out to be something you carry together or not at all.*

*This isn't forgiveness. I want to be clear about that, because you raised me to be precise. Forgiveness is a door and I'm not standing in it yet. This is a bridge. Built from one side, which is bad engineering, but I've seen worse hold. Your move is optional. I'd take it, if it were me. But I built it either way.*

*The needle's in a drawer at the survey office if you ever come look at it. Third shelf. Don't touch it with bare hands—you taught me that, among other things.*

*Felix*

* * *

I addressed the envelope and then didn't mail it for two days. This is not a metaphor. I literally carried it around in my jacket pocket, to the survey office, to the harbor, to Maren's table where we spread the family records every evening, until the corner went soft from handling and the address looked like it had been written by someone nervous, which it had.

Maren noticed on the second evening. She notices everything; it's her worst quality and the reason she's alive.

"It's written," she observed.

"It's written."

"And?"

"And it needs a post office."

She looked at me for a long moment, then stood, put on her coat, and held the door open. "Come on, then. Bridges don't build themselves."

"It's midnight."

"The postbox doesn't know that."

We walked down through the sleeping town, past the cannery ruins and the church with its leaning spire, down to the postbox on the corner by the chandlery, red paint gone pink with salt air. I stood in front of it holding the envelope like it was a bird that might startle.

"You know what's funny," I said. "I've faced down Alder Voss twice. I rode a glacial flood in a leaking skiff. I dug through a collapse with my bare hands. None of it scared me like this mailbox."

"That's because the mailbox is the one that can't be survived," Maren said. "Everything else, you either make it or you don't. This one, you make it and then you have to live with having made it." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "Drop it, Felix."

I dropped it. The little door swung, the letter fell, the sound it made was almost nothing—a whisper of paper on metal, the smallest sound in the world, and the largest transaction of my adult life concluded for the price of one stamp.

We walked back up the hill and neither of us talked, which is its own kind of talking, and at her door she paused and said, "For what it's worth—I think he kept the pages. The torn ones. Men like your father and my father, men who bury things—they always keep one copy. It's how they stand being alive."

I hadn't thought of that. I filed it away in the place where I keep hopes I'm not ready to spend.

* * *

Spring came on the way it does in Harrow Point—not arriving so much as seeping in, the light stretching, the harbor filling with boats that had wintered under tarps. The survey grew teeth: three grids opened, the school kids coming Tuesdays and Thursdays, a doctoral student named Priya who asked better questions than my professors ever had. I taught them all to read the shore the way my father taught me—every layer a ledger entry, every entry a life, nothing temporary if you write it down right.

And then, on a Thursday in late April, with the tide low and gold and the kids sieving midden dirt and shouting about a fishhook, Maren came down the beach path faster than she ever walks, holding an envelope.

Cream stock. Heavy. My name on the front in a hand I'd know anywhere—small, precise, the letters of a man who labels everything because labeling is how he loves.

My heart did something complicated.

"It came this morning," Maren said. "I didn't open it. Obviously."

"Obviously."

"Are you going to open it here or are you going to be weird about it?"

"I'm going to be weird about it."

"Good. I'd worry otherwise."

I walked out past the grids, past the kids, to the tideline where the water was coming back in over the flats, and I sat down on a drift log and turned the envelope over. Thin. One page, maybe two. No letter inside that I could feel—or something flat and small, sliding when I tilted it.

I opened it.

One page, in his hand. And folded inside it, pressed flat as pressed flowers, brittle at the creases, water-stained brown at the edges—

The torn pages. The missing journal pages. The confession he'd cut out of his own record thirty-seven years ago and carried through exile and silence and six years of my anger, kept safe the whole time, waiting, the way he waited, the way he did everything: from very far away, holding the door.

I sat on the drift log with the pages in my hands and the tide coming in around my boots, and I read the first line, and I understood that whatever I'd built—one-sided, badly engineered, a bridge from my shore only—had just been answered from the other side.

And the first line of his letter said: *Felix — I need to tell you what Edmund really was to me. Bring these to Maren. Both of you should hear it at once.*
