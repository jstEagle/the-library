## 36. Alder's Road

The doctor in the fishing town had a name I never learned and a bedside manner I will not forget. He looked at the X-rays of my leg—the one the ice tried to keep—and said, "You're lucky."

"I'm not," I said.

He shrugged, which is the honest answer most doctors give when patients argue theology. "Lucky enough," he amended. "Six weeks on that leg before you put weight on it like you mean it. Longer if you want to walk properly at fifty."

I was forty-three. I did not tell him I had no plans to be fifty anywhere he could bill me for it. I thanked him, paid him from the last of the family money I intended to spend on myself, and went to convalesce in a rented room above the chandlery, where the smell of tar and rope rose through the floorboards every morning like an apology the building made on behalf of the sea.

Six weeks is a long time to lie still when your whole life has been motion with a purpose attached. I had spent twenty years moving for the family—carrying judgments north, carrying silence south, carrying whatever needed carrying so that no one else had to know what it weighed. Now the weight had been set down in public, in front of the whole town, named out loud by my own mouth, and my body had apparently decided that having delivered its cargo it was entitled to fall apart on schedule.

The leg hurt worst at night. The rest of me hurt worst in the mornings, when I woke and remembered, in order: the crevasse, the man I did not rope, the icefall, the chamber, Maren's hand slipping from the documents, my own hands closing on her wrist instead. Some men would call that memory a wound. I had been trained to call it evidence. Either way, it did not heal faster for being examined hourly.

Maren came twice a week. She sat in the chandlery's borrowed chair with her boots crossed at the ankle and told me what the archive was becoming—her word, *becoming*, as though the thing were alive, which after a while I conceded it was. She had letters now from two of the three families of the vanished climbers. One of them wrote back with questions. One of them wrote back with nothing but a date and a name, which Maren said was its own kind of answer.

"And the third?" I asked, the second time she came.

"The third hasn't answered yet." She looked at my leg, then at my face, doing the arithmetic she was always doing. "You could write to them yourself, you know. It might land differently coming from you."

"It might land like a stone through a window."

"It might." She uncrossed her ankles, recrossed them. "Alder. You stood up in front of this town and dissolved thirty years of private justice in eleven minutes. Do you think the hard part is over?"

I thought about it honestly, because lying to Maren Voss had stopped being possible somewhere on the glacier, around the time she watched me choose her life over the proof and decided not to thank me for it, which was correct. Gratitude would have let me off too easy.

"No," I said. "The hard part is that there are branches of the family who weren't in that room. Uncles in the interior. A cousin in the port city who administers the accounts. People who learned the creed at their mothers' knees and have never once asked whose knees those were standing on. They'll hear what I did the way you hear about a death in a distant country. Distantly. And then someone will travel to them and explain that I'm a traitor, and they will believe it, because believing it costs less than the alternative."

"So go first," Maren said. "Before the explanation arrives. Be the explanation."

I should note, for the record of whoever eventually reads this, that I did not say yes immediately. I said something ungracious about missionaries and then lay awake until three in the morning composing the letter I would send to the cousin in the port city, and by dawn I understood that the letter was already a confession, and that a confession you write is worth half of one you deliver standing up, on the leg the ice gave back to you, in the rooms where the creed was taught.

That is how the road started: not as inspiration but as insomnia with a destination.

* * *

Felix found me on my last day, down at the tide line, testing the leg against wet sand. This was late September. The tourist season had died its annual small death; the town had returned to being itself, which is to say gray, competent, and unimpressed by anything, including former enforcers learning to walk.

"You're leaving tomorrow," Felix said. Not a question. Felix Nakamura read departures the way their father had read soil—one glance, full inventory.

"First light. The bus goes inland at six."

"Inland." Felix turned the word over. "How many stops?"

"That depends on how many branches of the family are still speaking to each other. Four that I know of. Possibly five, if the aunt in the valley has forgiven my grandfather yet, which she has not done in thirty years, so I won't hold my breath."

"And you're going to each one."

"I'm going to each one."

"To say what, exactly? The speech you gave here?"

"No." I picked up a piece of driftwood, weighed it, put it down. "The speech here was a verdict. Verdicts are short. What I owe the branches is longer. It's the whole account—how the creed was built, what it covered, who paid. My grandfather ordered a murder and dressed it as law, and for thirty years we enforced his costume. Every judgment I carried, every family I frightened into silence, every person who paid us 'admission'—" I heard myself use the town's word and let it stand. "They deserve to hear it from the man who collected."

Felix was quiet for a while. The tide was coming in, unhurried, keeping its books.

"They won't all listen," Felix said finally.

"No."

"Some of them will try to stop you."

"Yes. At least one will try worse than stopping." I had already written that possibility into my will, such as it was—a single page leaving my few possessions to no one, since the people I might have left them to were the people I was going to accuse. "I've thought about it."

"Have you thought about not going?"

I laughed, and the laugh surprised both of us. It came out rusty, like a gate in a house nobody lived in. "Every day since Maren suggested it. And then I think about the cousin in the port city. Tomas. When we were boys he held me under water at the swimming hole until I saw stars, and when he finally let me up he said, *Now you know what fear is for.* That was the catechism, Felix. Fear is for making people obey. I believed it for thirty years. Somebody has to swim back to Tomas and tell him the lesson was wrong."

Felix looked at me with that expression I had learned to distrust on the glacier and had come, slowly, to trust on dry land—the expression of someone deciding whether you are a problem or a person. On the glacier I had mostly been a problem. Lately the balance had shifted.

"You could just disappear," Felix said. "Start over somewhere. New name. You'd be good at it. You have the temperament."

"The temperament for what?"

"For vanishing. For carrying things alone. For thinking suffering is a job." Felix said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse, and more accurate. "Believe me, I recognize the résumé. I had it for years. It doesn't get better out there. It just gets quieter."

"And yet here you are," I said. "Staying."

"Here I am," Felix agreed, and something crossed their face—quick, private, gone—that I chose not to examine, because it belonged to Maren and it wasn't mine to see. "Staying is harder. That's the secret nobody tells you. Running takes legs. Staying takes everything else."

* * *

We walked back up from the tide line together, slow, matching my leg's pace without either of us acknowledging it. Halfway up the ramp to the street, Felix stopped.

"There's something I've been meaning to give you." They dug in the pocket of their coat and produced a small object wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth. "I found it in the cave, the first day. Before any of—" A gesture that took in the town, the glacier, the wreckage of two families. "It's the stone. Or it was. Maren took it during the break-in, obviously, and she's given it to the archive, but she had a copy cut. She wanted you to have one."

I unwrapped it. The stone was river-smooth, palm-sized, and carved with a single mark: a vertical line crossed by two shorter ones, precise as a surveyor's notation. Edmund's mark. The rival's name reduced to four strokes, cut by a dying man's friend as a promise to remember.

"Why me?" I asked.

"Because you're the one going to the places that forgot him." Felix closed my fingers around it. "You said the branches learned the creed at their mothers' knees. Fine. Carry them something else to learn. Not a verdict—you're done with those. Just the name. Let them hold it. Stone travels well."

I stood there on the ramp with a dead man's name in my fist and understood that Felix Nakamura had just done to me what I had spent my career doing to others: assigned me a burden and made it feel like an honor. The difference—which I suspect is the entire difference between us—was that theirs was a burden anyone could actually carry.

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't make it weird," Felix said, and walked on up the ramp.

* * *

That evening the three of us ate together at the chandlery—Maren, Felix, and I, around a table scarred by a century of gutting knives, eating fish stew that the chandler's wife made and refused payment for, on the grounds that we were, in her words, "the most interesting thing to happen here since the war, and the war wasn't interesting either." Maren talked about the archive's winter plans. Felix talked about the middens, the university survey they had finally accepted, the local children who were turning out to be better field students than half the graduate assistants Felix had known. I listened, and packed, and said very little, and noticed that saying very little no longer felt like discipline. It felt like peace.

Afterward, outside, the sky was doing the thing northern skies do in autumn—clearing and clouding in the same breath, as though unable to commit. The headland stood black against the last light, and beneath it, invisible, the cave waited behind its wall of returning water. Admission collected. Ledger closed. Mostly.

Maren shook my hand at the door. Hers was a workman's handshake, brief and complete. "Write," she said. "Not reports. Letters. There's a difference, and I want the difference."

"I don't know how to write letters."

"Neither does Felix. Learn together." She glanced past me, once, at Felix standing in the doorway with lamplight behind them, and whatever passed between the two of them passed silently, the way the deepest things do, and then she went inside.

Which left Felix and me alone in the street with my pack at my feet and the six o'clock bus eight hours away.

* * *

We did not talk much, that last hour. We stood by the harbor rail while the boats knocked gently against their moorings, and the lighthouse swept its beam across the water with the patience of something that has explained itself ten thousand times and expects to explain itself ten thousand more.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" Felix said at last. "The rope."

"On the icefall."

"On the icefall. You didn't rope Halvard." Halvard had been the teammate—the young one, the believer, the one who stepped onto the bridge of snow I judged sound and was wrong. I had said his name aloud exactly once since, at the town meeting, and it had cost me more than naming my grandfather. "Everyone thinks that was the coldest thing you ever did. Including you, probably. Especially you."

"It was arithmetic," I said. "Roping him slowed the crossing. Slowing the crossing risked the mission. The mission mattered more than the man. That was the creed. I didn't invent it. I just didn't question it fast enough to save him."

"But here's what I keep coming back to." Felix turned to look at me, and the lighthouse beam passed over both our faces, once, like a page being turned. "On the glacier, in the collapse. You had maybe three seconds. Documents or Maren. And you didn't do arithmetic. You didn't even hesitate—I was watching. So either the creed was always a lie you told yourself, or it broke, right there, and what was underneath it turned out to be a person."

I looked at the water for a long time. A boat's riding light doubled itself in the harbor, trembled, held.

"I've wondered the same thing," I said finally. "Whether I changed on that mountain or whether I was always this and the other thing was the costume. I don't have an answer. I think—" I stopped. Started again. "I think my grandfather built the creed because he needed one. After what he did, he needed a system that made it righteous, or he couldn't have slept. And we inherited the system instead of the crime, and a system without its reason is just habit with authority. Maybe underneath all of it, in every branch, in every house, there's just people waiting for permission to put it down."

"And you're going to give them permission."

"I'm going to give them the truth and let them decide what it permits. It's slower. But verdicts were the old way, and the old way is what got everyone killed."

Felix nodded slowly. Then, unexpectedly, they smiled—not the guarded half-smile I'd seen them ration out to Maren, but a real one, tired and crooked. "You know, the first time I saw you, you threatened me in a hotel hallway and I mentally filed you under *things that chase you forever.*"

"A fair filing."

"And now you're leaving on a bus to confess to strangers, carrying a rock." Felix shook their head. "People are unbelievable. That's the whole finding of my life. Thirty years of digging and the only artifact that matters is that people are unbelievable."

"Is that hope or despair?"

"Yes," said Felix Nakamura, and we left it there, because some answers are complete.

* * *

I left at first light, as planned. The bus was one of the small inland coaches, half freight, smelling of diesel and dried fish. My leg ached on the steps and I gripped the rail and took them anyway, one at a time, the way the doctor prescribed and the way penance is actually done—not in leaps but in stairs.

Felix was on the platform. I hadn't expected that. Maren had said her goodbye the night before; Felix had said theirs at the harbor rail, or so I assumed. But there they stood in the gray morning with their hands in their pockets, breath steaming, watching me stow my pack.

We did not embrace. Neither of us was built for it, and both of us knew the other knew. Instead Felix extended their hand, and I took it, and we held the grip a moment longer than commerce requires and a moment shorter than speech.

"The stone," Felix said quietly.

"Is in my inside pocket. Against the chest."

"Good. Keep it there. Not the bag—the chest. Things in bags get lost. Things against the chest get carried."

"I'll remember."

"And Alder." Their eyes came up to mine, and in them was everything the nod would have to hold, because neither of us was going to say it: the hotel hallway, the fire in the gorge, the sermon, the trial on the ice, the wrist I caught in the falling dark, the long litter-carry down the gorge where we negotiated truth hour by hour like merchants dividing a debt none of us could pay alone. All of it unsaid, all of it understood. "Come back through here. Whenever the road bends this way. The town keeps books, but it doesn't charge interest."

I nodded. That was the nod. It held everything, the way small containers hold large things when both parties agree not to shake them.

The bus pulled out at 6:04. Through the window I watched Felix Nakamura stand on the platform until the road bent, and then I watched the town go—the chandlery, the harbor, the black headland with its sealed cave—and then the land opened into the interior, hills and rivers and distances, and I was on the road at last.

Four branches. Possibly five. A name in my pocket and a confession in my throat and a leg that would ache in every one of them.

Tomas first. The port city, two days east. I took out my notebook and began, in the handwriting I used for reports because it was the only handwriting I trusted, the opening line of the letter I would deliver in person:

*I am writing to tell you that everything we were taught was built on a body.*

I looked at it for a long mile. Then I turned the page and kept writing, because the road was long, and the truth, unlike the creed, does not fit on a card.
