## 1. Miles, Not Years

The tide turned at 4:17, and Edmund Underhill was already in the water when it did.

He'd been down eleven minutes, working the portside rail of the Grey Wren where the growth came on thick every spring — barnacles in white ridges, the soft grey felt of hydroids that stung through a glove if you were careless. He wasn't careless. Five years of diving this hull had taught his hands to move the way water moved: patient, unhurried, finding the path of least resistance and taking it without apology. His knife scraped along the rail in long strokes, and the fouling peeled away in sheets, and beneath it the iron showed black and honest, the way it always did.

Visibility was two fathoms today. Maybe less. The river carried silt the way some men carried grudges — constantly, without any particular malice, just because it was what they were made of. Edmund had learned not to fight it. He kept one hand on the rail and let the other do its work, and he counted his progress the way he counted everything now: in feet of hull cleaned, in feet of line paid out, in fathoms of cold green water between himself and the surface.

Miles, not years. That was how he measured his life these days. A man could go mad counting years.

* * *

He surfaced at slack water into a morning the color of pewter, breathing hard through his regulator as he hung on the mooring line and looked up at the sky. Gulls wheeled over the breakwater, screaming their small grievances. The Grey Wren's buoy floated forty yards off his starboard shoulder — not the navy buoy, but his own, a rusted iron drum he'd anchored himself with three passes of chain, because a man who worked a wreck alone needed something to tie his life to, literally, or the river would take him one day without ever noticing it had done so.

The navy buoy sat further upriver, past the channel marker, a fat yellow cylinder with a radar reflector on top and a stenciled number nobody was meant to read twice. It marked nothing. That was the joke the harbor told itself about it, quietly, in the way harbors tell themselves jokes: a buoy that marks nothing, guarding a stretch of water where nothing is sunk. Edmund had been paid, once, five years ago, by a man from the naval office who wore his civilian clothes like a uniform anyway — paid to dive the Grey Wren's outer sections for "hazard survey," paid to report anything anomalous, and paid again, in cash, to stop reporting the things he found. He hadn't asked what the second payment bought. He'd taken it, and he'd kept diving, and somewhere along the way the arrangement had stopped feeling like corruption and started feeling like weather. It was simply part of the river. You didn't ask the rain why it fell.

What he'd found, in those first months, was a breach in the Wren's hull that no storm record explained — a gash torn inward, low on the starboard side, below the waterline, in a place where no rock and no collision could reasonably have reached her. He'd photographed it. He'd sent the photographs to the naval office. He'd received an envelope of cash and a note that said, in typescript: *Your survey is complete. Further work is unnecessary.* And then, a week later, another envelope, and no note at all, and that had been the beginning of the arrangement proper: a quarterly sum, left with the harbor master, for Edmund's continued silence about a hole in a ship that had been at the bottom of the river for longer than anyone alive could remember.

He'd told himself, in the early years, that he'd take the money until he understood what it was buying. Then he'd told himself he'd take it until he'd saved enough to leave. Then he'd stopped telling himself stories altogether, because the truth was simpler and worse: he took the money because the money let him keep diving, and he kept diving because the Grey Wren was the only thing in the world that asked nothing of him except attention. She didn't want him to be better. She didn't want him to explain himself. She lay in the dark with her drowned dead and her sealed compartments and her inexplicable wound, and she waited, and he came to her, and between them there was an honesty he couldn't find anywhere above the surface.

His brother would have called that morbid. His brother had been dead for six years, so Edmund felt entitled to disagree.

* * *

By half past seven the fog was burning off and Marisol Vane's skiff was chugging out toward him across the flat bright water, trailing the smell of woodsmoke and fried dough that meant breakfast, which meant she'd decided he'd worked long enough, which meant the argument was already lost before it began.

"You look like something the dredge spat out," she called, killing her engine and letting the skiff drift against his mooring buoy. She was thirty, maybe thirty-two, broad-shouldered and dark-eyed, with her mother's hands — big knuckled, salt-cracked, capable of gutting a fish or breaking a man's nose with equal efficiency. She dropped a canvas bag into his boat. "Eggs. Bread. There's butter if the gulls haven't gotten into it again."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me, it makes it charity." She lit a cigarette and squinted at him through the smoke. "How long were you down?"

"Forty minutes."

"Bottom time?"

"Twenty-five."

"Liar." But she said it without heat, the way you'd say it to a dog that stole from the table — a fact acknowledged, a punishment declined. She looked past him at the wreck buoy, then further upriver at the navy buoy, yellow and smug in the new sun. Her mouth did something complicated. "They pay you this quarter yet?"

"Harbor master has it."

"And you'll take it."

"I'll take it."

She flicked ash into the water. "One day that money's going to cost more than it pays, Edmund. Money like that always does. My mother says debt is just a story the rich tell about the poor, and the fleet tells it better than anyone."

"Your mother says a lot of things."

"My mother is right most of them." She stubbed the cigarette on the gunwale and pocketed the butt — Marisol never littered the river; even her vices were tidy. "Supper's at seven. Mother's making the eels. If you're late she'll give your portion to the Okafor boys and you know how they are about eels."

"I'll be there."

"You say that every time." She restarted her engine, and the skiff swung away, and she shouted back over the noise, not unkindly: "And eat the eggs! You're all elbows!"

He watched her go. This was the shape of his days: the dive, the food, the family that fed him without asking him to be family, the money he didn't examine, the wreck he didn't finish. The Okafor boys ran the net sheds. Old Mrs. Okonkwo mended sails in the loft above the chandlery and had opinions about everything under the sun. Danny Quill came and went with the seasons, drinking too much in winter and working too hard in summer, and owed Edmund money from a crab season gone wrong three years back that neither of them mentioned anymore. The harbor master, Bram Tull, kept the books and the fleet's envelope in the same drawer, and if that contradiction troubled him he'd never shown it. They were not blood, any of them. They were something older than blood, maybe — the kind of kinship the water makes, binding people who share a hazard. The fleet owned their moorings, their fuel, their fish prices, and their futures, and they survived it the way kelp survives a storm: by bending together.

Edmund rowed back to his mooring, ate the eggs cold in the bottom of the skiff, and spread his tide charts on the thwart to plan the afternoon.

* * *

The charts were his true religion, if he had one. He kept them in a waterproof roll in the cabin of his boat, and he annotated them obsessively in pencil — times, heights, current speeds, the little corrections that made the difference between a productive dive and a drowned man. The river here didn't have tides so much as moods: four meters at the springs, a sluggish half-meter at the neaps, currents in the narrows that could run eight knots and had swallowed swimmers whole. Every dive on the Grey Wren was a negotiation conducted weeks in advance. Slack water at the wreck lasted twenty minutes on a good day, twelve on a bad one, and the river did not extend credit.

Today's chart showed the afternoon flood building after two o'clock, which gave him a window at the turn — short, but enough to run his lines down to the cargo hold section he'd been clearing for a month. He penciled in the times, checked his tanks — 180 bar, enough, barely — and was reaching for his weight belt when the harbor radio crackled to life behind him.

"—all stations, all stations, this is *Meridian*, expedition vessel *Meridian*, inbound from the upper reach, we are—" Static. A woman's voice, clipped and foreign-accented, cutting through the interference like a blade through hydroid. "—require assistance at—" More static, then silence, then the carrier tone, empty, the sound of a channel open with nobody speaking into it.

Edmund set down the weight belt.

The transmission had come from upriver. From beyond the navy buoy, beyond the channel markers, from the long forbidden reach where the river narrowed and the charts went vague and the fleet's jurisdiction became something you could feel pressing down on the water like barometric pressure. Nobody dove up there. Nobody salvage-hunted up there. The fleet's dredges worked the lower channels, and the upper reach belonged to whatever arrangement of silence and money kept buoys floating over holes in ships.

The radio hissed. He waited, hand hovering, but nothing else came. No repeat. No position. Just the carrier tone fading into static, and then, eventually, the ordinary chatter of the harbor resuming around the interruption — Mrs. Okonkwo asking after diesel filters, the Okafor boys arranging a net haul, voices deliberately, elaborately casual, the way people talk when they've all heard the same thing and agreed not to have heard it.

He keyed the set. "Any station, this is Underhill. Did anyone copy that vessel's position? Repeat, did anyone get a position?"

Static. Then Bram Tull's voice, dry as old rope: "Underhill, harbor master. Channel's for harbor traffic. Log your dive times and stay off the air."

"Bram, that was a mayday. Or near enough."

"That was interference." A pause, long enough to fill with meaning. "Weather coming in from the east. Best secure your gear."

The channel clicked dead.

Edmund stood in his cabin with the tide chart still pinned under his elbow, the pencil in his hand, and felt the first hairline crack run through the comfortable architecture of his days. Outside, the water lay flat and silver, the gulls screamed, the navy buoy rode its anchor in the middle distance, yellow and patient, marking nothing.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he picked up the pencil and wrote, in the margin of the chart, next to the afternoon's tide times, a single word he immediately wished he hadn't: *Meridian.*

The flood tide would build after two. He had a window at the turn, twelve minutes of slack, maybe less. Enough to work the hold section. Enough to keep the routine intact, the miles accumulating, the years staying mercifully uncounted.

He pulled on his hood, and tried very hard not to think about the woman's voice, cut mid-word, still hanging in the static like a body in the water.
