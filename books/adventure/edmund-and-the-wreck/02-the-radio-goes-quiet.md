## 2. The Radio Goes Quiet

The morning Beatrix Calloway came through, the fog sat on the harbor like a lid on a pot, and Edmund Underhill knew before he opened his door that it would be a day of small wrongnesses. He had learned to read the water the way other men read faces, and the water that morning was holding its breath.

He measured his coffee by the tide chart pinned above the stove, not the clock. The ebb would begin at 7:40; slack at the narrows around 10:15; the flood turning again in the afternoon with a run of nearly three knots off the point. Five years on the Grey Wren had taught him that time was not a thing you counted in days or weeks but in water moving over a fixed place — how many gallons had passed over her deck since the last time he'd checked her plates, how much silt had crept into her holds like a slow animal settling in for winter. Miles of rope paid out and hauled back. Fathoms descended and risen. That was a life, if you wanted one.

The harbor was already awake when he stepped out. Marisol Vane — no relation to the fleet Vanes, though she spelled it the same and hated being asked — was hauling crab pots off the *Petrel* with her sleeves rolled despite the cold, her dark hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like electrical wire. Her mother, Doña Ines, stood in the doorway of the chandlery like a figurehead, arms crossed, surveying her domain: the fuel dock, the ice house, the net racks, the long wooden building where half the harbor's business got done and all of its gossip.

"Edmund." Marisol didn't look up from the pots. "You've got a customer."

"At this hour?"

"She's been here since six." A pause, and then Marisol did look up, and there was something in her face he couldn't read. "She's been asking about you."

Nobody asked about Edmund. That was the arrangement. He bought his air fills from Doña Ines, took his meals at the long table in the chandlery's back room, kept his skiff moored at the family dock, and in exchange nobody asked him questions he didn't want to answer. It was a good arrangement. It was the only kind he trusted.

The woman inside the chandlery was not from the harbor. You could tell by the way she stood — weight forward, like she was about to step onto something rather than stand on it — and by the boots, which were expedition-grade and salt-stained white above the soles, the mark of a diver who'd been working cold, silty water recently and hard. She was maybe forty, lean in the way of someone who forgot to eat when working, with gray coming in at her temples and eyes the flat gray-green of deep river water. She was reading the fleet's tide tables posted behind the counter with an expression Edmund recognized because he wore it himself sometimes: the look of a person doing arithmetic they don't like.

"You're Underhill," she said without turning around.

"I might be."

"You're the only diver on this stretch who works past the second bend." She turned. Up close her face was weathered and direct, no softness wasted anywhere. "Beatrix Calloway. I'm running an expedition upriver. Survey work."

"Survey work" covered a lot of ground. Wreck assessment, salvage prep, cargo location, things that were none of those. Edmund had used the phrase himself.

"We need gear," she went on. "Good gear. Your harbor shop is the last outfitting point before the upper reaches, and everything above there is either fleet-owned or fleet-locked. So." She gestured at the shelves. "I'll take what you have."

Doña Ines had materialized behind the counter with the unhurried menace of a woman who had been pricing out strangers for forty years. "What exactly are we talking about?"

"Two more sets of cold-water rig. Regulators, gauges, reels. Line — four hundred feet of good braided. Lift bags, the forty-pounders. And whatever compressed gas you can sell me without paperwork."

The silence that followed had texture. Compressed gas without paperwork meant nitrox or mixed gas, which meant dives deeper than sport, longer than legal, in water where mistakes didn't forgive themselves. Doña Ines's eyes flicked to Edmund — not for permission, exactly. For calibration.

"The harbor sells what the harbor has," Doña Ines said finally. "The harbor doesn't ask what it's for."

"That's why I'm here."

They negotiated the way people negotiate everywhere money matters less than information: slowly, with pauses, each side learning the other. Calloway paid cash, real cash, counted out in bills that hadn't seen a bank's fingerprints. She asked careful questions disguised as small talk — how deep did the channel run above the bend, where did the fleet's patrol launches turn around, had Edmund ever worked the old industrial reach where the river narrowed between the bluffs. He answered the way he always answered: accurately about nothing, precisely about trivia, a man built like a locked hatch.

But once — just once — she caught him. She was signing the chit Doña Ines insisted on, ceremony more than contract, and she said, casually, not looking up:

"They say there's a wreck up past the navy buoy that nobody works."

Edmund felt the words go through him like cold water through a wetsuit seal.

"There's wrecks everywhere," he said. "River's full of them."

"Not this one. This one nobody even names." She finished signing and looked up, and her eyes held his with a frankness that was almost physical. "You know which one I mean."

The fog pressed against the windows. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"I dive where I'm paid to dive," Edmund said. "And I don't get paid to talk."

"No," Calloway agreed. "You don't seem like a man who does anything he isn't paid for." She shouldered her pack. "For what it's worth, Underhill — I don't think you're paid enough."

She left. The bell over the door swung twice and settled. Doña Ines began restacking the shelf Calloway had emptied, and Marisol came in stamping fog off her boots, and the morning resumed its shape, and Edmund told himself the odd feeling in his chest was just the weather.

It wasn't. But he had five years of practice telling himself things.

* * *

Three days later the fog burned off and the river turned to hammered tin under a high pale sun, and the harbor set crackled to life at 6:12 in the evening with a voice Edmund didn't recognize.

The radio lived in the chandlery's front room, an old marine band unit bolted to the wall above the chart table, its speaker gone tinny with age. Harbor custom held that the set stayed on from dawn to dark, tuned to the working channels, and anyone within earshot was welcome to listen as long as they didn't talk over traffic. Most evenings it was Danny Quill's voice running fish counts, or the fleet dispatch office droning tide warnings, or the coast station reading notices to mariners in a voice designed to make drowning sound bureaucratic.

This transmission was different.

"—Calloway to any station, Calloway to any station." The voice was hers. Edmund knew it instantly, the way you know a voice heard underwater, stripped of everything but structure. It came through ragged, broken by static that pulsed in rhythm, like the signal was fighting through something. "We are — say again, we are —"

A long hiss. Then, fainter, as if the transmitter had been moved or dropped:

"—found it. Do you hear me? We found—" A sound that might have been laughter or might have been something else. "Manifest's intact, the whole—"

Static swallowed it. Edmund realized he had crossed the room without deciding to. His hand was on the volume knob.

"—repeat, manifest intact, we have the—" The voice changed pitch suddenly, sharpened. Someone else was speaking in the background, low and urgent, words smeared together by distance and interference. Calloway's voice came back harder. "—not now, just get it in the—"

Then, very clearly, cutting through the static like a blade through kelp:

"—they're coming. Underhill, if you're hearing this, the buoy, the wreck, it's all—"

The transmission ended. Not faded — ended, chopped clean, mid-word, the way a line parts under load. The carrier wave hissed on for three seconds, four, five, and then the channel went dead silent, that particular dense silence of a frequency where somebody had been talking and stopped.

The chandlery was full. Half the harbor seemed to be there — Danny Quill nursing a cup of tea, two of the Pell brothers mending a net across two chairs, old Tomas asleep in the corner chair he'd occupied since before Edmund arrived, Marisol by the stove. Every one of them was looking at the radio. Nobody was looking at anybody else.

"Well," said Danny Quill, into the quiet. "That'll be the weather."

"It's clear," said one of the Pell brothers. "Been clear all day."

"Clear upriver, maybe. Weather's funny up there."

Nobody laughed. The joke lay on the floor between them like something dropped.

Marisol moved first. She crossed to the set and switched it off, and the little red power light died, and somehow the room got quieter still. "Mom needs help with inventory," she announced, in the voice she used for ending arguments before they started. "Danny, your nets won't mend themselves. Pells, you're blocking the door."

There was a general shuffling, the elaborate casualness of people pretending they hadn't heard what they'd all heard. Edmund stayed by the chart table. When the room had emptied down to the two of them, Marisol turned and looked at him, and her face was doing several things at once, the way it did when she was frightened and angry about being frightened.

"You didn't hear anything," she said.

"I heard—"

"You didn't *hear* anything." She kept her voice level, but her hands, he noticed, had found each other and were gripping hard. "Edmund. Listen to me. Whatever that was, it's upriver, and it's fleet water, and it's not ours. The harbor master will say the same thing tomorrow, so you can save yourself the trouble of arguing with both of us."

"Marisol—"

"She bought out half our shop and asked about *you*, Edmund. About the buoy. About the second bend." Marisol's voice dropped. "You think that's a coincidence? You think some survey crew just happens to come through asking questions about the one thing this harbor has spent twenty years not seeing? And now her radio goes quiet mid-sentence, and you want to know what happened to her?" She shook her head. "People who ask those questions end up as stories. And the stories don't have endings."

From the doorway, Doña Ines spoke without raising her voice, which was worse. "The harbor master says the set picked up interference. Atmospheric. It happens in autumn." She came into the room carrying a tray of bread and stewed fish, as if feeding people could settle the matter, and maybe in her experience it usually could. "Eat, Edmund. You're diving tomorrow?"

"Slack's at 9:40."

"Then eat and sleep." She set the tray down. Her eyes, dark and old and kind, rested on him a moment longer than the sentence required. "The river keeps what the river keeps. That's not cruelty, mijo. That's just depth."

Later, lying in his bunk in the loft above the boatshed, Edmund listened to the harbor sounds he knew better than his own heartbeat: the halyards ticking against masts, the water lapping the pilings, the far-off clank of the fleet yard working a night shift across the bay. All of it ordinary. All of it the architecture of his life, load-bearing.

But underneath the ordinary sounds, his mind kept replaying the transmission, and specifically the moment the voice had sharpened — *Underhill, if you're hearing this* — because she had said his name the way divers say a depth number. Flatly. As a fact already accepted. As if, somewhere in the arithmetic she'd been doing behind the counter three days ago, Edmund Underhill had already been assigned a role in whatever was coming.

*The buoy, the wreck, it's all—*

All what. The sentence hung in him like a hook.

He got up, lit the lamp, and pulled his logbook from under the mattress — the real one, the one with five years of Grey Wren entries in his own cramped hand, depths and times and plate readings, miles and fathoms. He turned to a blank page and sat with the pen above it for a long time, because writing it down would make it a fact, and facts had a way of demanding things.

Outside, across the black water, the fleet yard lights burned all night, the way they always did.

He wrote one line: *Calloway. 6:12 p.m. Cut off.*

Then he closed the book, put it back, and lay in the dark listening to the tide turn, counting fathoms instead of hours, telling himself the unease in his chest was just the weather.

It wasn't. And three mornings later, when a fleet agent in a pressed coat walked up the dock looking for him, Edmund would understand exactly what it was.
