## 3. Six Tides

The agent came on the morning tide, which was fitting, because everything in Edmund's life arrived or departed according to the water.

He was mending net on the dock when the man appeared — a pressed coat, a hat with a brim too clean for harbor weather, shoes that had never once been wet. He stood at the head of the ramp the way a man stands at the edge of a churchyard, taking in the tarred pilings and the rusted cranes and the smell of diesel and brine with an expression of polite distaste, as though the whole working river were something that had happened *to* him rather than something he'd come looking for.

Edmund kept his eyes on the net. Needle through, loop, pull. Needle through, loop, pull. It was a rhythm older than any of them, and it gave him time to look without looking.

"You'd be Underhill." Not a question. The man consulted a small leather notebook anyway, as if confirming his own certainty against paper. "Salvage division. Fleet interests." He said it the way other men said *the crown* or *the bank* — as though the word itself settled all further discussion of who had authority here.

"Depends what you're salvaging," Edmund said.

The agent smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, like the coat. "That's rather the point."

* * *

His name was Pell, he said, and he wanted tea, and there was nowhere on the dock worth drinking tea, so they ended up in the back room of Marisol's mother's chandlery, where the window fogged with steam from the kettle and the shelves smelled of rope oil and pitch and forty years of salt worked into the grain of everything. Old Sefa poured three cups without being asked and retreated behind her counter with her knitting, which meant she would hear every word and forget none of them. Edmund took a small comfort in that. Whatever Pell came to say, he'd be saying it in front of a witness with a memory like a ledger.

"Upriver work," Pell began, wrapping both hands around his cup as though the harbor chill had finally reached him. "Channel survey ahead of dredging operations. The fleet means to open the upper reaches — proper navigation, proper depth. There are wrecks up there nobody's bothered with." A pause, delicate as a fishhook entering water. "We need a local man. Someone who knows the water, the marks, the mud. Someone discreet."

"I don't do surveys."

"The rate is triple your standard." Pell produced a folded paper and set it on the table between them, squared its corners with one finger. "Triple, plus a completion bonus that would buy you your own boat outright. No more borrowed skiffs, Underhill. Your own keel, your own name on the papers."

Edmund looked at the paper and did not touch it. Triple pay. He thought about what triple pay bought: a new set of tanks, a proper compressor instead of the patched one that coughed like a consumptive, a winter where the coal didn't run out in February. He thought about how easy it would be to say yes and how much easier still to stop thinking afterward.

"I know the upper river," he said carefully. "I know what's up there. Why does the fleet want it opened now? That channel's been left alone thirty years."

Pell sipped his tea. Over the rim, his eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who fished with nets rather than lines and didn't mind how long the haul took. "Commerce moves on, Mr. Underhill. Rivers exist to be used."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I'm authorized to give." He set down the cup. "Though I'll confess we've had some... complications with the preliminary work. An independent expedition went up last month without permits. Unlicensed salvage. They were asked to cease."

Edmund's hands went very still around the netting needle. Outside, a gull screamed once over the water and fell silent, as if listening too.

"Asked by whom?"

"The relevant authorities." Pell waved this away like smoke. "In any event, the matter's resolved. We expect no trouble going forward. The trouble has been—" he considered his words with the care of a man choosing which knot to cut, "—handled."

Handled.

The word went into Edmund like cold water into a lung. He kept his face still, kept his breathing even, did the thing he'd learned five years ago on the bottom of the river: when the panic comes, count. One fathom. Two. Three. His heartbeat slowed to the count.

Because Beatrix Calloway's transmission had been cut off mid-word six days ago, and the harbor master had ordered everyone to forget it, and now a fleet man sat across from him saying the trouble had been *handled*, and handled was not a word you used about a radio fault or a permit dispute. Handled was a word you used about bodies.

"They were asked to cease," Edmund repeated. "And if they didn't?"

"Then I'm sure whatever followed was regrettable." Pell's tone never changed; that was the terrible thing about it. He might have been discussing freight rates. "These independent operators, they romanticize the work. They think a wreck is a treasure chest. They don't understand that some things are sunk for good reasons." He smiled again, that well-made smile. "You'd understand that better than most, I imagine. A man dives the same ground five years, he learns which wrecks want raising and which ones want leaving."

The room seemed to tilt slightly, the way a deck tilts when a swell comes through that you didn't see coming. Five years. They knew about the Grey Wren. Of course they knew — they'd paid him not to ask about the buoy, and men who pay for silence keep accounts of what they've bought. But hearing it spoken aloud, gently, almost kindly, was different from knowing it. It was the difference between knowing the current runs hard past the point and feeling it take hold of your legs.

"I dive where I'm paid to dive," Edmund said. "That's all I am to anyone."

"Mm." Pell turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Which brings us back to my offer. Guide our survey launches upriver. Mark the shoals, flag the obstructions. Six tides from now the first dredge comes through the narrows, and after that the whole character of the upper river changes permanently. Whatever's up there will be..." He searched again. "...inaccessible. To everyone. Forever." He slid the paper another inch toward Edmund. "Triple rate. Or, of course, you can decline, and we'll find someone else, and you can go on diving your quiet little wreck for pocket money until the river forgets you. Both outcomes are acceptable to the fleet. Only one is profitable to you."

Six tides. So that was the clock. Not weeks, not a season — six turns of the water, and then the dredges would come chewing up the channel like great iron mouths, and anything — anyone — left upriver would be buried under a hundred tons of new silt and commerce, and no inquiry, no diver, no truth would ever get down to it again.

If Beatrix Calloway was alive, she had six tides.

If she was alive.

He made himself think it plainly, the way he made himself equalize his ears on descent — deliberately, painfully, before the pressure did it for him. A cut transmission. A ransacked possibility. A fleet agent using the word *handled*. The odds were not good. He knew exactly what cold water did to a broken body, how long a man lasts in the reeds and the mud in October, how far fever walks a wounded person inland before it lays them down. He had pulled enough drowned men out of this river to run the arithmetic without flinching.

But *handled* also meant they hadn't found her. You didn't say *handled* about a woman sitting in your custody. You said *handled* about a problem you believed closed — and problems were only closed when the people making them stopped moving. Which meant either she was dead in the reeds, or she was alive somewhere the fleet couldn't see, and either way the fleet wasn't certain, and uncertainty was the only door in this whole conversation that stood open.

"Underhill?" Pell prompted softly.

Edmund realized he hadn't answered. Behind her counter, old Sefa's needles had gone quiet.

"No," Edmund said.

Pell raised his eyebrows, genuinely surprised, the first honest expression he'd shown. "No?"

"I don't guide surveys."

"At triple—"

"I heard the number." Edmund stood, laid the netting needle down beside the untouched paper, and found that his voice came out level, which surprised him, because underneath the levelness something enormous was shifting, the way the riverbed shifts in a flood — slow-looking and total. "I don't have anything else to sell you, Mr. Pell. Tell your people the upper channel's bad this time of year. Weather coming. Tell them whatever keeps their dredges home longest."

For a moment Pell simply looked at him, and Edmund understood that behind those pale patient eyes a small entry was being made in a small ledger: *Underhill, E. — declined. Watch.* It didn't frighten him. It should have, and part of him noted that it should have, but mostly he felt something he hadn't felt in five years, something almost like relief, the sensation of a line finally going taut after drifting slack so long he'd forgotten it was attached to anything.

"A pity," Pell said, rising, settling his clean hat. "You strike me as a sensible man, Underhill. Sensible men live longer." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and added, without turning: "The harbor has debts, I believe. Old ones. The fleet remembers its friends, and its... obligations." Then he stepped out into the grey morning and was gone, his footsteps fading up the dock road, dry and quick, a man whose shoes had never touched mud and never would.

Sefa's needles started up again. After a while she said, without looking up from her knitting: "You'll want the strong tea. And you'll want to sit down before you decide whatever it is you just decided."

* * *

He walked the dock afterward because sitting still was impossible.

The tide was making, sliding green-grey up the pilings, covering the weed line mark by mark, patient as everything the river did. Down at the moorings the harbor boats knocked gently against their fenders — Danny Quill's old *Kestrel*, the Okafor brothers' oyster punt, Marisol's skiff with its patched gunwale — the whole ragged congregation of the place, each hull holding somebody's livelihood the way a shell holds a soft animal. The fleet owned the mortgages on half of them. Everyone knew it and nobody said it, the way you don't say the word *cancer* in a sickroom.

Six tides. He stood at the end of the dock and counted them out the only way he knew how, in water.

Tide one: tonight's ebb, wasted already unless he moved fast. Tide two: tomorrow's flood, the only window to run a shallow skiff upriver past the picket at the bend. Tides three and four: finding her, if there was a her to find, in flooded camps and drainage cuts and ten miles of reed marsh. Tide five: getting back down with whatever she had, against the current, past whatever waited. Tide six: the dredges arriving like a verdict.

It was impossible arithmetic. He ran it twice and it stayed impossible, and he found, standing there in the wind with the gulls wheeling and crying over the mudflats, that impossibility didn't feel like a wall. It felt like a depth reading. You didn't refuse to dive because the numbers were deep. You checked your air, checked your lines, and went down anyway, because the thing below was worth the risk of never coming up.

What was below, this time, was a woman who might be bleeding out in the rushes. And under that, deeper, where he didn't let himself look yet: the question of why a fleet would spend triple pay and pressed coats and careful words to keep one stretch of dead river closed. Wrecks got left alone because they were worthless. This one they guarded like a wound.

He went back to his room above the chandlery and took out the logbook — not the salvage log, the other one, the plain black notebook where he kept tide tables and air calculations and the small private mathematics of his days. He turned past the last entry, past *Calloway. 6:12 p.m. Cut off.*, and began a fresh page.

He wrote: *Slack water 0340. Picket boat at the narrows, one launch, searchlight, crew of four — changes watch on the flood, lazy on the ebb. Borrow Marisol's skiff — shallow draft, quiet engine, she'll kill me. Charts of the upper reach in the chandlery drawer, 1911 edition, pre-dredge soundings. Air: two full bottles, patch the compressor tonight.*

He stopped. Looked at what he'd written. It read like a plan and it was, mostly, but underneath every line ran a single assumption he hadn't yet said out loud, and he made himself write it, because a diver who won't name the hazard dies of it:

*She may already be dead. Go anyway.*

The pen hovered. Then he added one more line, smaller:

*Danny. Can't do it alone. Can I trust him?*

Outside, the light was going long and coppery over the water, the fleet yard lights beginning to burn across the channel, steady and indifferent as stars that had been bought and paid for. Somewhere upriver, in the darkening reeds, someone might be waiting for a tide that was running out.

A knock at the door. Two sharp raps, unmistakable, the knock of a woman who had been climbing these stairs since she was six years old and resented every one of them.

Edmund closed the notebook and put his hand flat on its cover, and knew, before he opened the door, exactly who it would be, and exactly what she'd come to say — and that he was going to have to choose, right now, between the family that fed him and the truth upstream.

He opened the door. Marisol stood in the lamplight, arms crossed, her eyes already furious.

"Pell was seen leaving," she said. "Start talking, Edmund. Now."
