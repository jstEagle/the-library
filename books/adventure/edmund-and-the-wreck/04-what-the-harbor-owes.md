## 4. What the Harbor Owes

Marisol Vey counted the boats twice that morning, because counting was how she prayed.

Fourteen moorings under her mother's ledger, fourteen hulls riding the grey water of the inner basin, and every one of them owed — not to the harbor, not to the family that had kept the quay for three generations, but to the fleet. She knew the arithmetic better than she knew her own prayers. The *Cormorant*, the *Petal*, old Hew's oyster punt with its patched gunwale: each one sat on a mooring the fleet had sold back to them at a price no honest lender would name aloud, and each year the interest ate a little more of what the harbor earned, and each year her mother signed the renewal because there was nothing else to sign.

She was twenty-six years old and she had never once seen the harbor free.

The morning fog was lifting off the water in slow rags when she saw Edmund come up from his skiff with the particular set of his shoulders that meant he'd already decided something. She had known Edmund Underhill since he was seventeen and hollow-eyed, since the night her mother put a bowl of fish stew in front of him and said, *Eat first, grieve after,* and he had eaten like a man who'd forgotten food existed. Five years he'd been theirs, more or less. Five years of dives on the wreck nobody named, of rope coiled just so, of sitting silent at their table while Marisol's mother told stories he never laughed at but always listened to.

He was carrying his dive bag over one shoulder. That was the first wrong thing. He never carried it up from the water; he carried it down.

"Edmund." She came off the bait shed steps fast, wiping her hands on her apron out of habit though they were already clean. "You're up early."

"Tide's early." He didn't stop walking.

"Edmund." She caught his sleeve, and he stopped, and for a moment they stood in the fog-thin light with the whole basin spread out behind him, fourteen boats breathing gently against their lines. "Where are you going?"

He looked at her hand on his arm, then at her face, and she saw him decide whether to lie. That was another thing about Edmund: he was bad at lying, which in a harbor full of people who lied for a living made him either a fool or a saint, and Marisol had spent five years deciding it was the second.

"Upriver," he said.

* * *

She made him come inside the bait shed to say it properly, because some things you didn't say on open water where the fleet's ears hung like gulls over everything. Inside it smelled of brine and diesel and the tar her mother used on the nets, and the single window let in a bar of light that turned the dust to drifting gold, and Edmund stood with his cap in his hands like a boy called before the schoolmistress and told her all of it.

The agent. The offer of triple pay. The word *trouble* dropped like a stone into still water, and the way the man hadn't bothered to hide his smile. And then the rest — Beatrix Calloway's transmission cut mid-word two nights ago, the harbor master's order to forget it, and Edmund standing in the dark of his bunk above the chandlery with the radio's echo in his ears until he couldn't stand himself anymore.

"So you're going to row upriver," Marisol said slowly, "into fleet water, past their picket, looking for a woman the fleet has probably already—"

"Don't." His voice was quiet but it had iron under it. "Don't finish that."

"Somebody has to." She heard her own voice rise and hated it, hated the way the shed seemed to shrink around them. "Edmund, listen to me. Listen. You don't know these people the way we do. My grandmother watched them burn a boat with a man aboard because he ran cargo without their license. They didn't even tow it out first. They did it at the quay so everyone could see, and afterward my grandfather paid for the privilege of keeping his mooring, because the fire had scorched the piling and the piling was fleet property."

"I know what they are."

"You don't. You dive. You go down into the cold and the dark where none of it can reach you, and you come up and eat our stew and think the world is a place where a man can mind his own business and be left alone. It isn't. Not here. Not on this river." She pulled the ledger from under the counter — her mother's ledger, the one with the soft leather cover worn bald at the corners — and opened it on the bait board between them. "Look at it. Go on. Look."

He looked. She watched him read the columns she knew by heart: the mooring fees, the renewal dates, the little red asterisks her mother used to mark the payments that had been *deferred* — which was the fleet's word for debt that grew in the dark like algae on a hull. Fourteen boats. Eleven asterisks.

"That's us," Marisol said. "That's every family on this quay. When my father got sick — you remember, you carried him up the ramp yourself — the clinic wouldn't take him until my mother signed the second mortgage on the *Petal*. Fleet clinic, fleet paper, fleet interest. He died anyway, eight months later, and we've been paying for the bed he died in ever since. So when you ask me why I'm begging you not to go upriver, understand that I'm not asking because I'm a coward. I'm asking because I have done the sums, and the sums say that if the fleet decides we helped you, we lose everything, and if we lose everything, there is no harbor for you to come home to. There is no stew. There is no table. There is just the water, and you in it, alone, forever."

The bar of light had moved while she talked. Dust turned in it. Edmund stood very still, the way he did underwater, she thought — a man conserving himself, letting the current pass over him.

"They might be alive," he said finally. "Her crew. Her."

"And if they are, they're alive because they're hidden, and the best thing anyone can do for hidden people is stay away from them." She reached across the ledger and took his wrist, feeling the diver's pulse beneath the skin, steady as tide. "Edmund. Five years ago my mother fed you because grief makes men stupid and someone has to stand between them and the river. I'm doing it now. Don't make me watch another man I love walk into fleet water."

The word was out before she could weigh it. *Love.* It hung in the tar-smelling air between them, and Edmund's eyes came up sharp and startled, and Marisol felt her face go hot and refused, absolutely refused, to take it back.

"You know what I mean," she said. "Family. Whatever we are."

"I know what you mean." His voice had gone rough. He turned the ledger toward himself and looked at the asterisks a long moment, and she thought — hoped — that the weight of them was settling onto him the way it settled onto all of them, generation after generation, the chain passed hand to hand. "Your mother ever try to buy them out? The moorings?"

"With what? Harbor money is fleet money by the time it reaches us. Every fish we land goes through their auction house. Every fuel drum comes off their barge. We're not tenants, Edmund, we're organs. The fleet doesn't want to own the harbor. It wants the harbor to keep working inside it, the way a heart works inside a body. You can't buy your way out of your own chest."

* * *

Her mother found them there an hour later, or maybe she'd been listening at the door the whole time; with Odile Vey it was impossible to say, and pointless to ask. She came in smelling of net tar and woodsmoke, seventy years old and built like the piling she'd been born beside, and she looked at the open ledger, and at Edmund's dive bag on the floor, and at her daughter's hand still resting near Edmund's wrist, and she sighed the sigh of a woman who had seen this exact scene before in one form or another for half a century.

"Show him," she said to Marisol. "If he's going anyway, show him. No sense sending him out blind."

"Mother—"

"Show him, girl."

So Marisol led him out the back of the bait shed, along the plank walk behind the net racks, to the low door in the seawall that her mother kept locked and that Marisol had been allowed to open exactly once before, on the day her father died. Inside was a room barely bigger than a coffin, dry-walled against the stone, and on its walls hung the harbor's true history: photographs gone sepia, a framed scrap of newspaper, and a length of chain — an actual chain, iron links as long as her forearm — bolted to the wall from floor to ceiling.

Edmund touched the chain first. Men like him always touched things before they read about them.

"What is this?"

"Read the clipping," her mother said from the doorway.

The newspaper was dated forty-one years back. Marisol knew the words the way she knew the ledger. *HARBORMEN REFUSE FLEET TERMS. QUAY CLOSED BY ORDER.* And below, smaller: *VEY FAMILY LEADS RESISTANCE; MOORINGS SEIZED.*

"My grandfather chained himself to that piling," Odile said, nodding at the iron. "Stood there through two tides and most of a third. The fleet cut him loose, took every mooring on the quay, and gave them back at terms we're still paying. Your friend the agent will tell you it was a loan. My grandfather called it a ransom, and he was right, but being right bought us nothing but the bill." She came into the little room, her shoulders filling it. "Every family here has a version of this room, Edmund. Some of them keep photographs. Some keep scars. The Ostranders keep their grandfather's teeth, what the fleet's men left of them. This is what the harbor owes, and what the harbor is owed, and the two ledgers have never once balanced."

"Why show me this?" Edmund asked. "If you want me to stay—"

"Because I don't want you to stay." Odile's voice was flat as calm water over deep rock. "I want you to win. Staying is what we do, because staying is all we've ever been able to afford. But you — you've got no mooring to seize, no boat to burn, no ledger with your name in it. You're the only free thing on this quay, Edmund Underhill, and I'll be damned if I'll spend my last years teaching another young man to kneel." She looked at Marisol then, and something passed between them that Marisol couldn't read and didn't like. "Though I notice you haven't asked the question that matters."

"What question?" Edmund said.

"The one my daughter won't ask because she's afraid of the answer." Odile folded her arms. "Ask him, Marisol."

Marisol looked at the chain on the wall, at the links her grandfather's hands had actually gripped, and made herself say it.

"If you go upriver," she said, "and the fleet finds out the harbor helped you — hid you, fed you, launched you — what happens to us? Not in general. Specifically. Because you've met their agent now. You've seen how they talk. So tell me honestly, Edmund: when they come down here asking questions, and they will, do you expect my mother to lie for you? Do you expect me to?"

The silence went on long enough that she could hear the water moving against the seawall, the small patient knocking of the basin against stone.

"No," Edmund said at last. "I don't expect anything. That's why I was leaving before dawn, before anyone—" He stopped. Ran a hand over his face. "I wasn't going to tell any of you. You weren't supposed to be part of this."

"But we are." Marisol heard her voice crack and let it. "That's the thing you've never understood in five years, Edmund. You think diving alone keeps you safe, keeps *us* safe, but it doesn't work like that. You eat at our table. You mend our nets when you think nobody sees. My mother taught you to read tide tables off her own charts. You are part of this whether you carry us or not — the only question is whether we get to choose to carry you back."

Odile made a sound that might have been a laugh. "She argues like her grandmother. God help us all."

* * *

They fed him, because that was what the harbor did; argument or no argument, no man of theirs went upriver on an empty stomach. Marisol fried mackerel and bread while her mother laid out charts on the bait table and marked the picket stations from memory — memory forty years stale, but the fleet was not a thing that changed, only a thing that grew. By the time the fog burned off entirely, the three of them stood over the chart like officers over a battle plan, and somewhere in the marking of channels and slack-water windows Marisol realized the argument was over, and that she had lost it, and that losing it felt strangely like relief.

"There," her mother said, tapping a bend above the oyster beds. "Slack water at the turn, quarter past four. If you can't slip the picket at slack, you can't slip it at all."

"I know that water," Edmund said quietly. "I've dived under their noses for five years."

"Yes," Odile said. "And that's the other thing." She straightened up, and Marisol saw her mother put on the face she wore for funerals and contract signings, the face that gave away nothing. "Five years, Edmund. Five years of quiet compliance, of paying your fees, of never asking about the buoy. The fleet has a file on you this thick." She held up two fingers, close together. "A thin file. A clean file. Right now, today, you are the most useful kind of man on this river to them: invisible. The moment you go upriver, that file starts growing. And files grow both directions — everything you've ever done for them becomes evidence of what you're capable of doing against them. Understand me? Your five years of good behavior aren't armor. They're inventory."

Marisol watched Edmund absorb that, watched his jaw tighten. "Then I'd better be quick."

"Quick." Her mother almost smiled. "Divers. Everything's fathoms and minutes with you. Sit down and eat your fish."

He sat. He ate. And Marisol, standing at the stove with her back to them both so neither could see her face, understood suddenly and completely that she was not going to change his mind, and that some part of her had known it from the moment she saw him carrying the bag the wrong direction up the quay. Grief made men stupid, her mother said. But purpose made them worse, because at least grief could be fed.

Afterward she walked him down to his skiff, past the fourteen moorings, past the *Petal* her father had died owing money on. The water lay flat and pewter-colored, holding the sky the way a mirror holds a face.

"Come back," she said. It wasn't a plea anymore. It had become, in the space of one morning, an instruction. "Flood tide Thursday. If you're not on this quay by flood tide Thursday, I'm coming upriver after you, and I'm bringing my mother, and God help the fleet."

Edmund looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something he had never done in five years: he smiled, quick and crooked, like sun through fog.

"Flood tide Thursday," he agreed.

She stood on the quay and watched the skiff grow small, watched it shrink to a fleck, to a suggestion, to nothing, out where the channel bent toward the picket and the open river. Behind her the harbor went on with its morning — hammers on hulls, the auction bell, gulls screaming over the fish guts — fourteen boats riding their chains, and her mother's voice at her shoulder, low:

"Well, girl. Now we decide what we tell the fleet when they ask."

Marisol kept her eyes on the empty water. "Nothing," she said. "We tell them nothing."

"Mm." Her mother was quiet a moment. "That's what your grandfather said too. Forty-one years ago. Day before they came for the moorings." She turned back toward the bait shed. "Start thinking past *nothing*, Marisol. Nothing is where every bad plan begins."

Downriver, where the channel bent out of sight, the fog was closing again — and through it, faint as a memory, Marisol thought she could hear an engine that wasn't one of theirs, running slow, running patient, coming up the river toward the harbor with the tide.

She went inside and took the ledger out from under the counter, and began, for the first time in her life, to look for a page with no asterisks on it.
