## 5. Upriver in the Dark

The tide turned at 3:47, and Edmund Underhill was on the water by 3:40.

He had not told anyone he was going. That was the first discipline of salvage — you never announce a dive until you're back from it, because the river keeps no secrets it isn't forced to keep, and men are worse than the river. He'd told Marisol he was going to sleep. She had looked at him the way she looked at weather coming in off the bar, and she had said nothing, which from Marisol was a whole conversation.

The skiff was Danny Quill's — borrowed without asking, which between them counted as borrowing. It sat low in the water, an old clinker-built thing with a two-stroke engine that sounded like a man clearing his throat all night, and Edmund had wrapped its exhaust pipe in sacking and old inner tube to muffle it further. It wouldn't be quiet. Nothing on this river was ever truly quiet. But it would be quiet enough at slack water, when the current stopped arguing with itself and the whole estuary held its breath for twenty minutes like a diver between strokes.

Slack water. The one moment each tide when the river forgot which way it was going. Every diver knew it as the window — the few minutes when the wreck stopped breathing, when the silt hung suspended instead of flying, when you could slip through a doorway in a hull without the water shoving you sideways into rusted steel. Edmund had built five years of dives out of those windows. Now he was building something larger out of one.

He rowed first, away from the harbor wall, oars turning in the locks with felt wrapped round the handles so they wouldn't creak. The town fell behind him in pieces: the fish sheds with their corrugated roofs ticking as they cooled, the crane at the coal wharf standing against the stars like a gallows, then the long black shoulder of the breakwater. A single lamp burned in Marisol's kitchen window. He did not look at it again after the first time. Looking made it harder, and hard was already scheduled for later.

The fog came downriver to meet him, as it always did on spring tides — a soft grey weight rolling off the flats, smelling of mud and iodine and the cold iron breath of the channel buoys. Good. Fog was a friend tonight, though friends like that could drown you as easily as hide you. Visibility would drop to a boat's length, maybe less. The fleet's patrol launches ran with radar and searchlights, but radar painted everything the same — a smudge was a smudge, whether it was a skiff or a drifting log or a seal come up to breathe. Edmund had spent five years learning how small he could make himself on the water. Tonight he intended to be very small indeed.

* * *

The Grey Wren lay six miles downriver, but Beatrix Calloway's camp was eleven miles up, past the narrows where the channel kinked like a broken finger, past the old powder hulks, into country where the river widened into reed marshes and the tide ran lazy and brown. Eleven miles against a flood tide that would begin building within the hour. He had done the arithmetic three times at Marisol's table with a pencil stub, and the arithmetic kept coming back the same: he could not go up on the flood and come back on the ebb in one tide. He would have to lie up somewhere in between, hidden, through a full tidal cycle, and run the last leg on the next slack.

Which meant the picket.

The fleet had set it two days ago, according to the talk in the fish sheds — two launches riding at anchor across the channel below the narrows, with a third running circuits. Nobody said what they were watching for. Everybody knew. A salvage broker's expedition had gone silent upriver, and the fleet wanted the river sealed like a jar. Edmund had watched the launches from the sea wall that afternoon through a pair of binoculars older than he was: grey hulls, twin engines, a searchlight on a mast amidships, and men moving about the decks with the unhurried confidence of people who owned everything the light touched.

They owned the moorings under the harbor boats. They owned the chandlery's debt and the ice plant and half the mortgages in the town, if you believed Marisol's father, and Marisol's father had spent forty years being right about things nobody thanked him for. They owned the river the way weather owned the sky. And tomorrow morning, if the agent's offer stood, they would own Edmund too — triple pay to guide their dredges upriver, and the casual, terrible phrase he had let fall like a dropped tool: *any trouble should already have been handled.*

Handled. Edmund turned the word over in his mind as he rowed, testing its edges the way he tested a corroded fitting. Handled meant Beatrix Calloway was either dead or wished dead. And handled meant someone had gone to check — which meant there was a chance, thin as monofilament, that the checking hadn't finished the job. People were bad at finishing jobs in remote marsh country. Bodies were heavy, witnesses were inconvenient, and rivers full of reeds gave hiding places to the hurt and the stubborn. Edmund knew about being stubborn in hiding places. He had been doing it for five years.

So: the picket. Two anchored launches across the channel, one running circuits. Slack water at 3:47. Fog thickening. And a skiff with a muffled engine and a man aboard who had once, years ago, threaded a line through a flooded corridor of the *Grey Wren* by feel alone, in silt so thick his lamp showed him nothing but his own reflection in the murk.

You didn't beat water. You joined it, and went where it wasn't looking.

* * *

By four o'clock the fog had swallowed the world entire.

Edmund shipped the oars and let the skiff drift while he listened. This was the part no one taught you in any manual — the listening. Sound carried strangely over water in fog; a launch engine two miles off might arrive at your ear as a soft rhythmic knocking, like someone tapping a pipe in the next room. He heard the river first: the tick of current against the skiff's planking, the occasional plop of a fish, the far-off complaint of a gull somewhere above the fog. Then, layered underneath, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall: engines. More than one. Steady, patient, idling or circling.

He started the two-stroke. It coughed twice before it caught, and each cough went through him like a shout in church. Then it settled into its muffled stutter, barely louder than the water, and he eased the tiller over and began to work upriver along the eastern shore, where the channel shallowed and the fog lay thickest over the mudflats.

He ran without lights. The chart was in his head — he had drawn it from memory at Marisol's table, every buoy and beacon and old pile field between the harbor and the narrows, and now he navigated by it the way he navigated a wreck: by feel, by count, by the accumulated knowledge of where the water deepened and where it lied. Four minutes at this throttle to the green can off Miller's Point. Six more to the sunken barge that showed only at extreme low water, its skeletal ribs just breaking the surface like the carcass of something enormous. He counted seconds in his head, the same way he counted kick-strokes on a descent, and when the count said *barge*, the black ribs slid past the skiff close enough to touch, crusted with weed, dripping in the stillness.

Somewhere out in the white blindness, a searchlight swept past. He saw it before anything else — a dim yellow bloom in the fog, growing, brightening, the beam swinging a slow arc like a lighthouse gone mad. Edmund killed the engine instantly, tilted it, and let the skiff drift, and lay down flat in the bilge among the spare fuel cans and the coiled anchor line, his cheek against wet planking that smelled of tar and Danny Quill's cigarettes.

The light came on. It passed over the fog perhaps two hundred yards out, a vast trembling column of illumination in which every droplet of moisture hung visible, swirling, beautiful and obscene — a curtain of lit water behind which Edmund lay with his heart slamming against the boards. The beam crawled across the flats. It touched the barge ribs. For one endless second the light lay along the skiff's gunwale, and Edmund watched the shadow of his own head printed on the inside of the hull, sharp-edged, unmistakable, waiting for the beam to swing and the shadow to move and give him away.

A voice carried over the water, flat and bored, words dissolving in the fog: *"—nothing on this side—"*

The beam swung away. The bloom faded, dimmed, became a rumor, became nothing.

Edmund lay in the bilge another full minute, counting his breathing down the way he'd learned to do at thirty feet with a snagged line, forcing the air slow through his nose. Fear was not the enemy. He had learned that young, on his first deep dive, when panic had come up his throat like floodwater and an old diver named Coffin Pete — dead now, most of the men he'd known were dead now — had gripped his wrist underwater and held it until the breathing slowed, and then pointed upward, and then pointed onward, letting Edmund choose. Fear was information. Fear was the body telling you the truth faster than the mind could. The trick was not to kill it but to steer it — to take all that animal urgency and aim it forward instead of backward.

Backward was the harbor, the warm kitchen, the plate Marisol would have set aside for him. Forward was eleven miles of hostile river and whatever the fleet had left of Beatrix Calloway.

He sat up, restarted the engine, and went on.

* * *

The anchored launches announced themselves by smell before sound — diesel and galley smoke hanging in the windless fog like a fence you couldn't see. Edmund shut down well short and paddled, laying the skiff along the eastern mudflat where the eelgrass grew dense, easing it into a shallow gutter between banks of samphire that scraped the hull with a dry whisper. Then he waited, chin on the gunwale, and studied the shape of the trap.

Through a rift in the fog he made them out: two grey launches riding bow-and-stern anchors athwart the channel, maybe a hundred and fifty yards apart, a hawser or boom slung between them low on the water — he could just see the dark line of it, sagging, hung with what looked like signal flags or tin reflectors to catch a propeller. A net, in other words. Not for fish. Between them and beyond them, the third launch circled, its engine note rising and falling with the patience of a sheepdog working a fold.

Any boat trying the channel would be seen, hailed, boarded, turned back or taken. Any boat trying to sneak past on the far side would find the water there shoaling onto the flats, and the circling launch waiting like a spider at the edge of its web.

But webs have edges, and Edmund had spent his life studying edges.

At slack water the tide stood still, and the fog lay lowest over the mud. On the eastern side, beyond the eelgrass, the flats ran half a mile of glistening ooze toward a raised bank where an old drainage cut — dug a century ago for the powder works, marked on no chart anyone used anymore — ran parallel to the channel behind a screen of reeds. At high slack, that cut would carry eighteen inches of water. Enough for a shallow skiff, if the man poling it didn't mind pushing through soup and silence and the certain knowledge that if he grounded, he would be a target sitting motionless in a searchlight's eye for the next six hours until the tide floated him again.

It was a bad plan. It was the only plan. Edmund had noticed long ago that these were usually the same plan.

He waited for the slack. He knew it was on him not by any instrument but by the river itself — the driftwood stilled, the eelgrass standing upright instead of leaning, the whole grey world suddenly holding its breath. Twenty minutes, maybe twenty-five, of water going nowhere. He poled the skiff out of the gutter and up onto the flooding flat, the pole sinking to its collar in soft mud, each push a slow effortful negotiation, the skiff hissing forward a boat-length at a time. The reeds closed around him. Somewhere to his left, unseen, the patrol launch made its circuit, and Edmund matched his movements to its engine note — poling when the engine rose as it turned away, freezing when it dropped as it came about. Move when they look away. Stillness when they look back. The oldest dance there is.

Twice the searchlight swept the reeds, and both times Edmund lay flat in the bilge with the pole laid alongside, the skiff motionless among ten thousand identical shadows, and let the light find nothing because there was nothing to find — just another piece of the marsh, indistinguishable from the marsh, belonging to the marsh. His hands shook on the second pass. He watched them shake with a kind of detached interest, the way he watched the depth gauge needle tremble, and waited for them to stop, and they stopped.

Then the cut took him, a straight lane of black water walled in reeds taller than his head, and the fleet's lights became a dull glow somewhere off to starboard, and the engine notes faded behind, and Edmund realized he was through.

He did not celebrate. Celebration on a job was how men died — he'd seen a diver lose a man to a collapsed bulkhead because he'd been grinning about the find instead of watching his gauge. He poled on, and the shaking in his hands turned gradually into something else, something steadier and colder and cleaner. Purpose. It felt, physically, like the moment on a descent when the silt clears and the wreck's outline comes up out of the gloom — the moment the dive stops being a risk and becomes a task.

* * *

Past the picket, the river changed. The salt thinned out of the air; the fog grew warmer, softer, smelling of rotting vegetation and fresh water and, faintly, of woodsmoke — someone's fire, far off, or the memory of one. The powder hulks loomed up out of the murk one after another, great beached iron whales rusting on the mud, their hulks furred with weed, their portholes like closed eyes. Edmund had dived none of them. There was no money in the hulks, and no permission either, and for five years he had been a man who did only what there was money in and permission for. The thought sat in him now like a swallowed stone.

Five years. He tried to reckon what else the five years contained and found the ledger nearly blank. Tides. Dives. Miles of rope paid out and hauled back. Meals at Marisol's table, eaten mostly in silence, mostly gratefully. The navy buoy he serviced and did not ask about, the checks he cashed and did not examine, the small daily surrenders that had seemed, each one, so reasonable, so survivable, so much cheaper than the alternative. Add them up and what did you get? You got a man who could hold his breath a long time. You got a man who had gotten very good at staying down.

Beatrix Calloway's voice came back to him across the weeks — she'd stood in the chandlery buying dive gear, brisk and salt-crusted, arguing prices like a woman who had never once lost an argument she cared about. *You work the Wren, don't you? The forbidden one.* And he'd said nothing, because saying nothing was his trade, and she'd laughed — not unkindly — and said, *Everything forbidden is buried for a reason, Mr. Underhill. The interesting question is whose reason.*

Whose reason. He turned the question over as the skiff crept upriver, and found, examining himself honestly the way he examined a hull, that the answer frightened him less than it should have. The fleet's reason was money. Money he understood; money was ballast, it kept things level, and he bore no particular grudge against it. But the other thing — the thing under the money, the thing that had put a slashed tent and a silenced radio and a patrol picket on a fog-bound river — that was older than money. That was the thing that had written his brother out of the company's books with a paragraph and a pension payout and a funeral where nobody met anybody's eyes. Edmund had spent five years diving around that thing the way he dove around a snagged line: carefully, respectfully, pretending it wasn't there.

No more pretending. The river was eleven miles long and he had already come five of them, and fear had sharpened itself, stroke by stroke, into purpose, the way a blade sharpens on a strop — not by adding anything, but by wearing everything unnecessary away.

* * *

Dawn found him in the reeds two miles short of the expedition anchorage, lying alongside the bank with the skiff tied into the stems, eating cold bread and canned fish and watching the fog turn from grey to pearl to a thin washed gold. The patrol launches were behind him. Ahead, upriver, the country opened into wide marshland veined with channels, and somewhere in that country — past the kink of the narrows, past the old loading stage — Beatrix Calloway's camp sat on its low bluff above the water.

He scanned it through the binoculars until his arms ached. Distance, plus dawn haze, plus the lingering fog, made certainty impossible. But the anchorage where her vessel should have swung at moorings looked empty. The bluff where the tents had stood, according to the chandler who'd provisioned them, showed shapes — pale rectangles, angles that didn't sit right, a mast or pole askew. No smoke. No movement. No laundry drying, no boat pulled up on the mud, none of the small busy litter of living people.

And then the wind shifted, coming downriver off the marsh, and brought him a smell that had no business reaching him from two miles out unless it was very large or very recent or both.

Woodsmoke. Fresh woodsmoke — but wrong, acrid, with a chemical edge beneath it, the smell of a fire that had burned things fires shouldn't burn.

Edmund lowered the binoculars. His hands had gone still again, entirely still, the diver's stillness, the stillness that came just before the descent. He untied the skiff, took up the pole, and pushed out of the reeds into the broadening morning, upriver, toward the smoke.
