## 6. The Drowned Camp

The river told him before he saw it.

Edmund had learned to read water the way other men read faces — the small tell-tale changes that meant weather, or current, or something dead upstream. All morning the Grey Wren's stretch had been running wrong: a faint sweetness in the air, a scum of pale foam riding the ebb where no foam should be, and once — unmistakable, gone before he could fix his mind on it — a scrap of canvas, sun-bleached yellow, turning slowly in an eddy behind the point.

Expedition canvas. The kind Beatrix Calloway's people had bought from Marisol's stores three weeks ago, paying too much and haggling not at all, because they were in a hurry and money was the least of their worries.

He stood at the tiller of the borrowed skiff and let the current carry him past the wreck buoy, past the navy marker with its paint so fresh it seemed to apologize for itself, past everything safe and known, and up into the country where the river narrowed and the reeds grew tall enough to swallow a man standing upright in a boat.

He had told himself a dozen stories on the way up. That the expedition had simply moved camp. That the radio failure was a burnt valve, a wet aerial, the ordinary small deaths of electrical things in damp country. That Beatrix Calloway — who bought dive gear like she was arming a war and paid for it with a smile that made Marisol mutter *trouble* under her breath — had finished whatever she'd come to finish and gone home overland, forgetting to tell anyone, because people like her forgot that harbors kept count.

The stories had lasted until the smell.

Not death, exactly. Not yet. But the sweet-rot smell of a flooded camp: canvas gone sour, kerosene spread thin across still water, food left to spoil in the heat. A human place abandoned in a hurry and then drowned by the tide, which does not care what it buries.

* * *

He found the creek mouth by following the foam backward, working the skiff up a channel barely wide enough for his oars, poling where the mud shoaled, the reeds closing over him like green water standing on end. Herons watched him pass with the indifference of creatures who had seen men come and go for ten thousand years and expected nothing better.

The camp sat on a low shelf of ground above the creek, maybe forty yards of dry-ish land between the reed beds and the rise of the old sea wall, a grass-grown embankment from some earlier century's attempt to argue with the tide. He smelled it before he saw it, and when the reeds parted he stopped poling and simply looked.

There had been four tents. He could see the ghost-marks of them in the flattened grass, the dark rectangles where canvas had kept the rain off and the ground beneath stayed bare. Two were gone entirely — struck and carried off, or washed away. One lay collapsed in a shapeless heap, its poles snapped, half-submerged in a pool of standing floodwater that reflected the sky with terrible calm.

And one — the largest, the command tent, the one that would have held the charts and the radio and whatever else Beatrix Calloway had brought upriver to find — had been slashed.

Not cut open to get inside. Slashed. Long deliberate strokes, six or seven of them, driven through the canvas deep enough to score the ground beneath, the cuts crossing each other in a pattern that had nothing to do with searching and everything to do with anger, or message, or both. The tent gaped like something disemboweled. Its contents had been dragged out and scattered across the grass: a chart case split open, paper dissolving to pulp in the dew; a lantern with its glass shattered; a coil of rope cut into pieces short enough to be useless; a typewriter — an actual typewriter, which told him something about Beatrix Calloway's opinion of modern machinery — with its keys punched inward, every single one, methodically, as though someone had taken the time to be thorough about hatred.

Edmund grounded the skiff and stepped out into ankle-deep water and did not move for a long moment.

The fleet had been here. He knew it the way he knew a storm coming — not by any single sign but by the whole pressure of the scene. This was not robbery. Robbery took the good things and left the rest. This had taken nothing. It had only destroyed, and arranged its destruction to be found.

* * *

He went through the camp the way he dove a wreck: slow, systematic, hands doing the work while the mind stayed cold and cataloguing. It was the only way he knew to stand in a place where bad things had happened — make it a job, give it edges, measure it.

The flood line was easy to read. The spring tide two nights ago had come up over the shelf, leaving a clean brown margin on the tent fabric and a tidemark of silt across the flattened grass. So the camp had been abandoned — or emptied — before that tide. Four days, maybe five, since anyone had lived here.

The fire pit held the story of the last morning. Charred wood, yes, but also unburned wood stacked ready beside it, and a kettle sitting full of rainwater where someone had set it down to boil and never come back. A tin plate with a fork still lying across it, tines crusted with dried egg. You did not leave a meal half-eaten unless you left in a hurry, or were made to.

Near the fire, pressed flat into the mud by somebody's boot heel, lay a photograph. Edmund crouched and lifted it with both hands, careful as lifting a drowned thing. Six people, squinting into sunlight, arranged in front of the very tent whose corpse he was kneeling beside. Beatrix Calloway stood at the center — he recognized her from the harbor day, tall and lean, that smile that was more decision than warmth. Beside her a heavyset man with grey stubble and a diver's build, arm slung around a young woman with cropped hair and a camera on a strap. Two more men he couldn't have named, and at the edge of the frame, half-turned away, a sixth figure whose face the light had refused.

Six. He counted the sleeping arrangements twice, reading the ghost-marks in the grass, and came up with six again.

He found the first body-sign fifty feet along the top of the old sea wall, and it stopped his heart for one full beat before his mind caught up and corrected it: blood, yes, but old and not much of it, a smear on the concrete coping and a dark stain in the grass below, days faded to the color of weak tea. Enough for a wound. Not enough — he made himself say it plainly, because vagueness was a luxury — not enough to prove anything either way.

Beside the stain, half-buried in the grass, a boot. Just one. Good leather, expedition-grade, size large. No foot went with it. He searched the slope below the wall on his hands and knees, parting the grass blade by blade, and found nothing but a button — brass, naval pattern — and a long drag-mark through the dew that led down toward the creek and ended where the water began.

He stood there a while, letting the river breathe around him, and made himself think it through like a salvage problem. Something had happened here four or five nights ago. It had been fast and violent and organized. At least one person had bled. At least one person had gone into the creek — carried, or walking, or swimming. And the fleet, having done whatever it did, had slashed the tents and scattered the gear and left the mess for the tide to tidy, expecting the river to keep its mouth shut the way it always did.

But the river had shown him the canvas scrap instead. And the river had never once, in five years, done anything Edmund hadn't eventually understood.

* * *

It was the slate that changed everything.

He found it in the third place he looked, which was the right place, because whoever had left it had wanted it found by someone careful and missed by someone quick. It was wedged under the broken leg of the collapsed tent, face down, weighted with a stone — and Edmund only turned it because he turned everything, because thoroughness was the one virtue his trade rewarded.

A diver's slate. Plastic, grease-pencil writing, the kind of thing every diver in the country had used a thousand times to communicate in silence underwater. The message on it had been written fast, the letters leaning, the pencil pressed hard enough near the end to gouge:

**CAMP TAKEN AT DUSK. THEY CAME BY BOAT — 6 OR MORE — TOOK H—**

And there it stopped. Not faded, not smudged. Stopped. The final stroke of the *H* trailed off in a long unbroken line, as if the hand writing it had been interrupted mid-letter — or had heard something, or seen something, and dropped the slate and run.

Below the unfinished word, in different handwriting — smaller, quicker, angled hard against the first message — someone had added:

**INLAND. THE CULVERT. 3 DAYS OF WATER LEFT.**

Edmund read it four times. The reeds whispered. Somewhere far downriver, so faint it might have been imagination, an engine worked against the current.

Three days of water left. Written — how long ago? The grease-pencil hadn't faded, but grease-pencil didn't fade. He crouched and studied the slate's edges, the way divers learn to date things by wear, and found the answer in the mud dried into the grain of the plastic: two nights' worth, maybe three. The spring tide had come and gone since this was written. Whoever had scratched that second message had been counting cups of water in the dark somewhere inland, and the count had started running low three days ago.

Which meant, if they were still alive, they were close to the end of it now.

* * *

He walked inland.

He told himself it was reconnaissance — read the ground, find the tracks, establish whether anyone had actually gone this way or whether the message was bait, a trap laid for exactly the kind of fool who would come poking upriver after a silent radio. And it might be bait. He turned that possibility over honestly, the way he turned over every hull before committing a line to it. The fleet knew someone would come eventually. They'd left the camp messy on purpose. Why not leave a message too?

But the second handwriting argued against it. Bait was written neat. Bait didn't say *three days of water left*, because bait didn't need urgency — it could wait forever. Only a real person, crouched in the dark with a dying pencil and a dying plan, wrote *the culvert* and meant it.

The tracks confirmed it, once he learned to see them. Going inland from the camp, through the rough grass toward the rise of the old wall, the ground told a story in the language it always told: a body dragged — the long furrow he'd already found — and then, further on, footprints. Two sets, maybe three, moving fast, toes dug in, heading away from the creek. One set favored the left foot heavily, the prints lopsided, the right one shallow. Someone hurt. Someone being helped, or helping.

They'd gone up over the sea wall and into the scrub beyond, and the scrub had swallowed them, and Edmund followed.

The land inland was old country — the bones of some earlier economy showing through the skin of the present: a ruined pumphouse, rusted field drains, the concrete spine of a road that had never been finished, all of it surrendered decades ago to birch scrub and bramble and the patient work of water. He moved carefully, reading as he went. Here a bramble stem broken at shoulder height, the break still green-edged — days old, not weeks. Here a strip of cloth, blue wool, snagged on wire. Here, on the concrete road, a dark spot the size of a palm, and another four feet on, and another, a trail of drops spaced like a man bleeding steadily while others half-carried him.

The engine sound came again, closer now, and Edmund stood very still among the birches until it faded — a patrol launch working the main channel, unhurried, the sound of a net being drawn slowly through water that the fish believed was empty.

When it was gone he kept climbing.

* * *

He heard the culvert before he saw it: a hollow note in the wind, the breath of a big pipe, the way a conch shell holds the sea. It lay where the unfinished road crossed a drainage channel — a great concrete throat, eight feet across, built by some confident generation to carry the marsh dry and abandoned by every generation since. Half its mouth was choked with silt and willow growth. The other half opened onto darkness that went back further than his eyes could follow.

The signs at its threshold were small and certain. Footprints in the drying silt, going in and not coming out. A scatter of mussel shells — someone eating what the ditch offered. And propped against the concrete lip, placed where a searcher's eye would catch it, a white triangle of plastic: a second dive slate, blank on its face, but tied to it with a frayed cord a strip of yellow canvas.

Expedition canvas. The same cloth as the scrap the river had carried past him that morning. A flag, or a marker, or a message that said only: *you are meant to find this.*

Edmund crouched at the mouth of the culvert and listened.

Water dripped somewhere in the dark. Wind moved through the willows. And underneath those sounds — so faint he might have built it out of hope, except that hope was the one material he had never trusted and did not start building with now — something shifted. A weight changing position. A breath drawn carefully, the way you breathe when you are trying not to be heard by anything, ever again.

"Hello the culvert," Edmund said.

His voice sounded enormous after the long silence, absurdly loud, bouncing off the concrete. Nothing answered. The dripping continued. He counted to thirty.

"I'm not fleet," he said. "I'm from the harbor. Underhill. I dive the Wren." He paused, and because it felt necessary — because whoever was in there had earned plain speech — he added: "Beatrix Calloway bought her gear from Marisol's stores. Paid too much. Didn't haggle. Marisol said trouble, and Marisol's usually right."

Still nothing. Then, from deep in the pipe, a voice — cracked, dry as the mud at the creek mouth, but alive, unmistakably, ferociously alive:

"How many boats did you pass coming up?"

"One patrol launch. Main channel. Heading down."

A long silence, in which he could hear the arithmetic happening in the dark.

"And the camp," the voice said. "What's left of it?"

"Tents cut. Gear smashed. Nothing taken." He hesitated. "There's blood on the sea wall. And a boot."

Another silence, longer, and when the voice came again it had changed — flatter, carrying something that had been decided in the dark over three days of shrinking water and was now simply fact.

"That'll be Hendricks." A breath. "They took him on the boat. The rest of us ran." The voice steadied itself with visible effort, the way a man steadies a load too heavy to hold for long. "Underhill. Listen to me. There are two of us left. She's got a broken collarbone and a fever, and she won't put the case down even to sleep, and we've got maybe a day of water left between us. Can your skiff take three?"

Edmund looked back the way he had come — the reeds, the creek, the drowned camp, the whole drowned question of it — and thought about the patrol launch working its way down the channel, and the dredges that would come behind it, and the harbor family whose moorings hung on his silence, and every mile of rope and fathom of cold water he had ever measured his life in.

"Yes," he said. "It can take three."

From the darkness of the culvert, low and rough with fever, the voice said: "Then get her out. Because whatever they buried in that ship — she's the only one left who knows exactly where it is."
