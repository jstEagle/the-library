## 7. A Voice in the Reeds

The scratch marks started where the mud gave way to gravel, and Edmund almost missed them.

He had been walking the bank for the better part of an hour, following the flood line of the drowned camp downstream, because a man who survives goes toward water — that was the first thing diving taught you, before knots, before tables, before anything. Water is direction. Water is drink. Water is the shape every desperate plan eventually takes. So he walked the waterline with his eyes down and his ears open, past the reed beds that stood ten feet tall along this stretch of the river, their seed heads gone silver and ragged in the autumn wind, rattling like dry bones whenever the gusts came off the channel.

The scratches were in the culvert's concrete headwall — a drainage mouth, old Works Administration concrete, poured rough and streaked black with a century of runoff. Three lines, then a gap, then three more. Not random. Random marks don't repeat. Random marks don't have that dragged, deliberate quality, like someone who could barely lift her arm pressing something sharp into stone because she had nothing else left to press it with.

Edmund crouched. The gravel crunched under his knee. He touched the grooves with one finger — they were fresh, or near enough. The edges hadn't softened. No rain since the night the camp went under, and these hadn't been rained on.

"Hello," he said, quietly, to the culvert.

Nothing answered. The river said what the river always says, which is everything and nothing at once.

He looked up and down the bank. The picket launch had passed twice since dawn, running its slow figure-eights out in the channel, close enough that he'd flattened himself into the reeds both times and counted his breaths until the engine note faded. The fleet didn't leave a search pattern like that running unless they were looking for something specific. Something alive. He'd understood that yesterday, standing in the ruined camp among the slashed tents and the floating dive masks, but understanding it here, alone, with the scratches under his hand, made it different. Made it heavier.

He drew his knife and added three scratches of his own beside hers. Then he said, louder: "I'm coming around. Don't shoot."

He meant it as a joke. It came out flat, because nothing about this stretch of river was funny anymore.

* * *

The culvert ran under the levee road, a corrugated throat maybe four feet across, half-choked with silt and the collapsed skeleton of a muskrat lodge. Daylight showed through the far end as a gray coin. Edmund went in on hands and knees, and the smell hit him immediately — standing water, rot, iron, and underneath it all the sweetish wrongness of an infected wound.

He knew that smell. He'd smelled it on his own leg once, six years ago, when a barnacle-cut went bad on him and Marisol's mother had lanced it with a sail needle and a bottle of grain spirits while he bit down on a rolled towel. You didn't forget that smell. It was the smell of a clock starting.

"Hello," he said again, into the dark. "I'm not fleet."

A sound came back. Not words. A breath drawn through teeth, and then a voice so scraped-down and hoarse he barely recognized it as human, let alone as the crisp, clipped voice that had bought dive gear from the harbor master two weeks ago and paid too much without haggling.

"If you're fleet," the voice said, "you'd have shot already. They don't talk first."

"That's true enough." He eased forward. His shoulders brushed the corrugations on both sides. "Edmund Underhill. I dive the Grey Wren. I heard your last transmission."

Silence. Then, closer now, a dry laugh with no humor in it at all. "Half a transmission. I got cut off mid—"

"—word. Yes."

"I hate that they'll have written it down that way. In some log somewhere. *Calloway, last transmission, incomplete.*" A pause, and the voice steadied itself with visible effort, the way a diver steadies on a bad descent. "Come into the light where I can see you. Slowly."

He did. The far end of the culvert opened onto a scrape of shingle below the levee, screened by reeds, and there she was.

Beatrix Calloway was propped against the concrete lip with her legs stretched out in front of her, and she looked like something the river had tried to keep and failed. Mud to the waist. Hair matted flat. Her left arm hung in a sling improvised from a shirt sleeve, knotted with the kind of quick, ugly competence Edmund recognized from harbor men who'd set their own fingers. Her right hand held a boat hook — no, he saw, a broken oar shaft, splintered end up — angled across her body like a pike. And tucked against her right side, between arm and ribs, was a hard case the size of a ship's logbook, olive-green, scuffed, with a brass clasp.

Her eyes were the worst part. Bright. Too bright. Fever-bright, the way a lamp burns just before the oil runs low.

"You're the Grey Wren diver," she said. Not a question.

"How do you know that?"

"Because I asked about you in the harbor. Because my survey man pulled the permit records before we ever came upriver, and there's one renewal, every year, five years running, for salvage rights on a wreck nobody else will touch." She swallowed; he watched the effort of it move down her throat. "And because you're the only man on this river who walks a bank alone in October looking at nothing, which is exactly what a man looks like when he's looking for something."

Edmund stayed where he was, ten feet off, weight on his heels. "Your camp's flooded. Your boats are sunk or scattered. There are fleet launches running grid patterns out in the channel, and they're not looking for wreckage." He nodded at the case. "They're looking for that."

"They can look." She shifted, and couldn't quite keep the sound inside her — a small, bitten-off noise, and her whole left side went rigid. Broken collarbone, he thought. Bad one, by the set of the shoulder. "It's waterproof to forty fathoms. It's been underwater twice already. It can wait in a ditch another week if it has to."

"It's not the case I'm worried about."

"No," she said. "I know. Me either."

* * *

He built a small fire anyway, back from the waterline in a hollow where the smoke would tangle in the reeds and come apart before it cleared the levee. It was a risk. Everything was a risk now; the arithmetic of risk had changed the moment he'd stepped out of the borrowed skiff. But fever doesn't wait, and neither does infection, and a woman with a collarbone broken in two places — he could see the deformity now, the shoulder sitting wrong, the whole arm carried like cargo — wasn't going to walk anywhere, let alone downriver.

She watched him work with those bright eyes and said nothing while he fed twigs to the flame. When he took a strip of dried fish and a flask of water from his pack and held them out, she looked at them for a long moment before taking them, and he understood the look. It was the look of someone recalculating what charity costs.

"Why did you come?" she asked. "Underhill. Nobody sent you. The harbor master ordered everyone to forget the transmission — I know how Ossian works, he'd have burned the logbook himself if he thought it helped. So why?"

Edmund considered lying. Considered the half-truths he'd prepared on the row upriver — curiosity, salvage instinct, a professional dislike of unfinished work. All of it true in the shallow sense. None of it true in the deep one.

"My brother worked a fleet job," he said finally. "Eleven years ago. Pipe-laying barge, winter run. There was an accident, and the fleet's report called it operator error, and the operator was dead, so it was easy to call it that." He turned the flask in his hands. "My brother. And I spent five years being paid, in one way or another, to look at a wreck everyone pretends isn't there, and I told myself that was a life. Then your voice came over the harbor set and stopped in the middle of a word, and I found out it wasn't."

Beatrix ate the fish slowly, in small bites, watching him the whole time. When she finished, she said: "Do you know what's in the case?"

"No. And I'm not asking yet."

"Good. Because you'd want to put it down and wash your hands after, and I need you carrying it." She leaned her head back against the concrete. For a moment the crispness dropped away and he saw the exhaustion underneath, days of it, pain stacked on pain, a woman who had crawled out of a flooded camp with a shattered collarbone and kept moving because stopping meant dying. "Here's what I'll tell you. My team found documents on the wreck site. Original documents. Nineteen-nineteen. And the fleet sank the camp the same night our tender confirmed the find, which tells you everything about what those documents say."

"The fleet didn't sink your camp."

"No," she agreed. "Men did. Men on a fleet payroll, men with fleet lanterns and a fleet launch. Which is a distinction the law will care about and I don't." Her right hand settled on the case, fingers spread, and even in fever her grip didn't loosen. "Three of my people are dead, Underhill. Two more are missing. I've been crawling through ditches for two days listening for boots. So understand me when I say the next thing I say."

She waited until he met her eyes.

"I don't trust you. I trust that you came, and I trust what you said about your brother, because nobody invents a dead brother as a gambit — it's too expensive a lie. But trust is a ledger, and yours is empty, and mine is overdrawn. We're going to be allies because we have to be, and we're going to check each other's arithmetic every step of the way, and when this is over, if we're both alive, you can decide whether you ever want to see me again."

Edmund almost smiled. It hurt his face; he'd forgotten how. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in five years."

"Don't get sentimental. Hand me that sling knot — it's slipping, and if the bone shifts again I'm going to make a noise that carries to the channel, and then neither of us gets to decide anything ever."

* * *

He reset the sling as best he could without touching the shoulder itself, working by feel and by the small sounds she permitted herself, which were fewer than most men would have managed. While he worked she talked — not about the case, but around it, giving him the shape of things the way you give a diver the shape of a wreck before the visibility drops.

"There's a tide problem," she said. "There's always a tide problem. The dredges come upriver on the spring flood — six days, maybe five now. When they arrive, this stretch of river becomes fleet water, closed water, and whatever happens after that happens behind their booms."

"Four tides," Edmund said. "Maybe five. I've been counting."

"Of course you have." Something flickered across her face that might have been approval. "Then here's your arithmetic, diver. I can't walk. I can't row. I can't even hold a tiller with this shoulder. Every plan that ends with me moving downriver runs through you, and every plan that keeps me hidden runs out of food in two days. So the question isn't whether you're in. The question is what you're willing to carry."

"The case?"

"The case, yes. Eventually. But not yet." Her fever-bright eyes held his, and beneath the brightness he saw the broker's mind still turning, cold and precise, a machine running on a dying body. "First you carry me. There's a fishing shack two miles down the levee — my survey man marked it, old Quill family structure, abandoned since before the war. If it's still standing, it has a stove, a roof, and a door that locks from inside. Get me there tonight, moving after dark, and I might live long enough to be useful."

"And after that?"

"After that," Beatrix Calloway said, and pressed the case a fraction tighter against her ribs, "we go back to your wreck. The one you've been paid to ignore for five years. Because everything the fleet is trying to drown started there, and it's going to end there — one way or another, before the dredges arrive."

Out in the channel, faint but unmistakable, a launch engine changed pitch. Both of them went still. The reeds rattled. The light was already going amber and long, the afternoon bleeding toward dusk, and somewhere upriver the fleet's search grids were tightening like a net around two people in a ditch with a fire too big and time too short.

Edmund kicked dirt over the coals and shouldered his pack.

"Can you walk two miles?" he asked.

"I can crawl two miles," Beatrix said. "Ask me again at one."
