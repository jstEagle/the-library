## 8. The Case

Fever has a way of rearranging time. Beatrix Calloway lay in the dark of the drainage culvert with her collarbone splinted against her chest and the waterproof case locked under her good arm, and the past came up through her like water through a cracked hull — not in order, not politely, but all at once, the way a wreck gives up its cargo when you finally breach the hold.

The culvert smelled of iron and old silt and the sweet rot of reeds. Somewhere beyond the mouth of it, Edmund Underhill was boiling water over a spirit stove he'd produced from some pocket of his skiff like a magician's dove, and the small domestic sound of it — the tick of metal, the gurgle — was so absurdly ordinary that it made her want to laugh, or weep, or both.

"Drink this," he said. "Slowly."

She drank. It tasted of river and tin. Her hands shook, and she hated that they shook, because she had spent eleven days being hunted and she had promised herself she would not shake until it was over.

"You found my camp," she said. It wasn't a question. He'd carried the dive slate in with him, turning it over in his fingers like a man reading scripture he didn't believe in.

"Half a message," Edmund said. "It stopped at *the manifest is*—"

"The manifest is real." She closed her eyes. The culvert darkened behind them, though it had been dark already. "That's what they killed Okafor for. That's what they drowned the rest of my people's work for. And if you're going to help me — and I think you are, because you wouldn't have boiled water for a stranger — then you need to know exactly what it is you'd be carrying. Not a rumor. Not a story. A document."

Edmund said nothing. In the grey light at the culvert's mouth his face was all angles and patience, a man accustomed to waiting out long silences underwater, where impatience kills.

So Beatrix let the fever take her back, and told him.

* * *

Her father had been an insurance man before he was anything else — before the brokerage, before the salvage charters, before the name Calloway meant boats instead of ledgers. She had grown up on the smell of actuarial paper, on dinner-table talk of premiums and payouts, and she had learned young that the sea is not merely dangerous; it is *profitable*, to certain parties, in certain arrangements. A ship lost is a tragedy. A ship lost *on purpose* is an investment.

In 1919, the Grey Wren went down in the narrows above the estuary with eleven hands aboard. The official record said storm stress — hull fatigue, cargo shift, the usual litany. The insurers paid out within four months, which even then was suspiciously fast, and the payout went to the Meridian River Fleet, the consortium of barge owners and wharf magnates whose descendants still owned every mooring, every dredge, every mile of navigable water from the head of tide to the open sea. The fleet's founding families built their dynasties on that payout and on the monopoly it financed. There were streets named after them now. There was a hospital wing.

Beatrix had come upriver in the spring with a survey crew of six and a research grant and no intention of finding anything at all. That was the joke of it, the bitter joke she would carry the rest of her life: they had been hired to do a routine hydrographic survey, depth-sounding and sediment coring, boring work, billable work. The Grey Wren wasn't even on their task list. It was forbidden water, navy-buoyed, unmarked on the public charts, and everyone in the salvage trade knew the rule — you don't ask about the buoy, you don't sound the buoy, you bill your hours and go home.

But sonar doesn't respect rules. On their third week, running a transect two hundred meters off the restricted line, Okafor's side-scan caught an anomaly. Not one anomaly. A *pattern*. Debris scattered wrong — too wide, too deliberate, hull plating flung outward from the inside rather than collapsed inward from the pressure of sinking. Beatrix had seen storm-wrecks. Storms crush. They fold a ship like a fist closing. What the sonar showed was a ship opened — blown — from within.

"I thought it was equipment error," she told Edmund, and heard her own voice go flat and strange. "I made Okafor run the pass three times. I accused him of misaligning the towfish. He just looked at me — you know how he looks, looked — that patient look, like I was the instrument that needed recalibrating." Her throat closed. She made herself keep going. "Then we ran a magnetometer over the site and found the boiler room full of ferrous material that shouldn't have been there. Charges. Old ones, but charges. Placed along the longitudinal bulkheads. Placed by someone who knew ships."

* * *

The fever took her again, and she was back in the survey tent with the rain hammering the canvas and the diesel generator coughing, and Okafor spreading the printouts across the folding table with hands that were very steady, steadier than hers were now, and saying in his quiet way: *Bea. You understand what this means. Somebody sank her.*

And she had said — she could still hear herself saying it, young and stupid and thrilled — *insurance fraud, a century old, everyone involved is dead, it's history, it's a paper for the journal.* And Okafor had shaken his head slowly, the way he did when a student missed the point entirely, and turned the printout around, and put his finger on the hull breach coordinates.

*Storm records for that night,* he said. *I pulled them. Gale force five from the southwest. The Wren was in the lee of the bluff. Bea — the weather couldn't have done this. And there's more.* He'd been to the county archive, spending his own evenings on it, spending his own money on certified copies. The crew list. Eleven names. And against each name, in the margin of the fleet's own ledger, in faded ink, a single word: *settled.*

Not *lost.* Not *drowned.* Settled. As though the men were accounts.

"We thought it was a figure of speech," Beatrix whispered to the culvert dark. "A century-old clerk's shorthand. We were wrong. God, we were so wrong." She felt Edmund shift beside her, felt the warmth of the tin cup he pressed into her hand again, and drank because her body demanded it even while her mind was drowning elsewhere.

Because the archives had given up more than a crew list. Okafor, patient Okafor, methodical as a tide table, had cross-referenced the insurance claim against the fleet's early minute books — fragments only, pages surviving in a solicitor's dead-storage vault, purchased by the county historical society in the fifties and never once read by anyone. And in those minutes, dated six weeks before the Wren sailed on her last voyage, was a resolution. Recorded in the dry language of committees. The consortium's founders, facing insolvency — the war had gutted river freight, three barges lost, creditors circling — had voted to *consolidate assets* by means left deliberately vague, and to authorize expenditure for *marine works* in an amount that matched, almost to the shilling, the cost of demolition charges and the wages of eleven men.

Eleven men who were listed as crew.

"They weren't victims," Beatrix said. "That's the thing nobody wants to hear. Most of them knew. Maybe not all of it — maybe they were told it was a salvage exercise, an insurance survey, that the charges were for a planned breakwater. But some of them knew. The bosun knew; his signature is on the stores requisition for the detonators. And when the ship went down, the fleet paid their families a settlement — generous, unprecedented, hush-money dressed as charity — and the families signed waivers. Except one family didn't sign. One widow wrote back." Beatrix's voice broke and she let it. "Her letter is in the case, Edmund. It's in the case with everything else. She wrote: *You may settle with the law, but the river keeps its own ledger, and one day it will call in the debt.*"

* * *

Here was the part she had never said aloud, the part the fever pried loose now, the part that lived in her like shrapnel:

They should have stopped. After the sonar, after the magnetometer, after the minute book — they should have packed the data, filed it with a university, published carefully, let the institutions grind it slow. That was the sane play. That was what any sensible person would have done.

Instead she had chartered a hard-hat diver out of a downriver port, a grim old man who asked no questions because his price answered all of them, and they had gone down to the Wren's stern in forty feet of black cold water, and Beatrix had gone with him, against every certification she held, because she could not ask another person to enter that hull without entering it herself.

The strongroom door had been the shock. Not that it existed — merchant vessels carried safes — but that it was *sealed from the outside*, welded, the welds fresh-looking under a century of silt. Ships seal their safes from within. Someone had sealed the Wren's strongroom after she settled on the bottom. Someone had come back.

Inside — the diver's torchlight through the gap where the weld had been chipped, the video feed crackling up the line to Beatrix's screen in the tender — inside had been the manifest. The true manifest, the one that never reached the insurers: cargo listed as ballast scrap that was actually bonded goods, values inflated fourfold for the claim, and appended to it, in the founder's own hand, a letter of instruction. *Sink her deep and clean. The witnesses sail with her. Nothing rises.*

The witnesses. Eleven men. Some complicit, some deceived, all of them sent down with the proof of the crime, because dead men countersign nothing and testify less. The fraud hadn't been only against the insurers. It had been murder — industrial, bureaucratic, laundered through ledgers and settlements and a century of respectable silence — and the fleet that ruled the river had been founded on it, capitalized by it, its great families' names carved into hospital wings with blood money.

"We got the video out," Beatrix said. "One copy. Okafor hid the original slate and gave me the case — the documents, the letter, the manifest photographs, the minute-book copies. He said—" She stopped. Pressed her splinted collarbone gently, as if testing whether the ache was still real. It was. "He said, *you're the one who can make them listen, Bea. I'm just the one who finds things.* Two days later the first launch came upriver without running lights. Four days later they burned the camp. Six days later—" Her breath went ragged. "Six days later they took him. I heard it. I was in the reeds twenty meters away and I heard it, and I ran, because running was the only thing left I could do for him, and I have hated myself every hour since."

The culvert was silent except for the drip of water and, far off, the river moving in its sleep.

"They didn't kill the others quickly either," Edmund said at last, quietly, and it wasn't a question and it wasn't comfort; it was a man confirming the shape of the thing he had stepped into.

"No. They questioned them first. About who else had copies. About who else knew. Then—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to. The slashed tent she had seen in his eyes when he described her camp, the half-message on the dive slate, told him the grammar of it.

"And the fleet," Edmund said slowly, "thinks the case came upriver with you."

"The fleet knows the case exists. They've known since Okafor's first archive request tripped a flag with their solicitors — these people have watched the paper trail for a hundred years, it's how they survived it. Everything I've done for two weeks, they've been watching. Which means—" She turned her head against the concrete, looking at him through the fever-blur, and forced the words out clearly, because this mattered more than her life and she needed him to understand it without decoration. "Which means they can't ever stop hunting me. Not because I matter. Because the case matters. As long as it's loose, their whole edifice is one document away from collapse. Insurance fraud voids the original claim retroactively in equity, the land titles chained to the payout get challenged, the monopoly charter gets reviewed — lawyers will fight it for decades, yes, but the *story* comes out, and stories are what dynasties drown in. They aren't protecting a secret anymore, Edmund. They're protecting a century of inheritance. There is no price they won't pay and no person they won't spend."

* * *

Edmund was quiet for a long time. Outside, the light at the culvert's mouth had changed — deepening toward evening, the river going bronze and then bruise-colored. She heard him move the spirit stove, heard the small efficient sounds of a man who lived alone and had organized his solitude down to the ounce.

"My brother worked a fleet job," he said finally, and his voice was different — lower, careful, like a man walking on ice he didn't trust. "Five years ago. Cover-up after. Harbor took me in after that." A pause. "I've been diving a wreck for five years that I was paid not to ask about. Your buoy. Your Wren."

Beatrix felt something turn over in her chest — not hope, hope was a luxury, but its poorer cousin, recognition. "Then you already knew something was wrong down there."

"I knew the water was wrong." He laughed, short and without humor. "Divers know. Currents that don't match the charts. Debris where it shouldn't be. I told myself it was the river. Rivers lie to you every day if you let them." He was silent a moment. "You're telling me the river wasn't lying. The charts were."

"The charts were written by the men who sank her." Beatrix shifted, wincing, and pulled the case around in front of her — the battered aluminum-and-rubber box, salt-scoured, dented along one edge where she'd fallen from the culvert bank. She worked the latches with her shaking fingers, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth, the edges of paper showed: brittle, brown, a century old, and heavier than lead. "This is the manifest. This is the founder's letter. This is the widow's reply. Eleven families' worth of grief, bought and filed. And somewhere in this box is the reason Okafor is dead and my crew is scattered and a fleet foreman has men sweeping these mudflats with lamps." She looked up at Edmund, and the fever made her vision swim, but she held his eyes anyway. "I can't carry it out alone. I can barely carry myself. But I am not giving it to anyone who hasn't decided — really decided — what they're willing to lose for it. So decide. Tonight. Because at dawn I start moving downriver whether you come or not, and the tide won't wait for either of us."

Edmund looked at the papers a long moment. Then at the darkening river beyond the culvert's mouth, where, far downstream, invisible but utterly present, the fleet's lights would be burning over its docks and dredges and its hundred years of quiet ownership.

"Tide turns at 3:40," he said. "We'll want the last of the ebb to take us down past the picket." He began, unhurried, to pack the stove. "Get some sleep. I'll wake you for the arithmetic."

*Arithmetic,* Beatrix thought, as the fever finally dragged her under — tides and hours and miles, the cold mathematics of escape, and somewhere upstream the dredges already coaling. She slept clutching the case, and dreamed of a widow's handwriting, and of a river keeping its ledger.
