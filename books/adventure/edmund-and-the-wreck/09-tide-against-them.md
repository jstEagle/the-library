## 9. Tide Against Them

The tide table lived in my head the way other men kept prayers — recited without thinking, consulted without deciding to. I lay awake in the flooded camp's one dry corner with Beatrix Calloway breathing shallow beside the dead fire, and I did the arithmetic before I'd even opened my eyes.

Five tides.

The dredges were coming on the sixth. Vane's man had said it plain enough when he'd offered me triple pay: *we break ground at the top of the spring tide, Underhill, and we'd like you standing on the deck when we do.* That had been yesterday morning, which meant the count had already eaten itself down by one while I'd been poling a borrowed skiff upriver through the dark, and another while I'd crawled through reeds following scratch marks cut into a culvert lip by a woman with a shattered collarbone and no intention of dying quietly in a drainage ditch.

So: five tides. Two days and a fraction. And between me and the Grey Wren lay eleven miles of river, a fleet picket at the narrows, a woman who couldn't lift her own arm, and a waterproof case she gripped in her good hand like it was a child she'd pulled from the water.

I got up before dawn and stood at the edge of what had been the expedition camp and let the arithmetic run its full course, because that was how I did everything — measured first, moved second.

* * *

The flood would start at 4:40. It would run hard until half past eight, slacken, then turn and drain for six hours toward the sea. A skiff with a shallow draft and a decent current under it could make the narrows on one flood if the wind held off. The narrows themselves were the problem — the picket boat sat there because the channel pinched to forty yards and every vessel on the river had to pass within hailing distance of it. Past the narrows, the river widened into the lower reaches where the mudflats began, and mudflats meant grounding at low water, which meant being stranded, exposed, lit up like a church whenever a search launch felt like sweeping its lamp across the flats.

Which meant the tides weren't just a schedule. They were a corridor that opened and shut, opened and shut, and we had five openings left and needed perhaps three of them to go right in a row.

"You're doing it again."

Beatrix's voice came from the tent wreckage, rough with fever. She'd propped herself against an overturned supply crate, her left arm bound in a sling improvised from a shirt that had belonged, judging by the name tape, to a man named Okafor who was not going to need it anymore. Her face was gray as old rope in the pre-dawn light, but her eyes tracked me the way a helmsman tracks weather.

"Doing what?"

"Counting. You've got a face like a man doing sums he doesn't like the answer to."

"I'm always doing sums." I crouched by the fire ring and fed the last dry wood to the coals. "It's what I'm for. Five tides before the dredges reach the wreck. Maybe four and a half if they push, and men like the ones paying them always push."

"And you can move me downriver in five?"

"I can move you downriver in two, if nothing goes wrong." I looked at her. "Something will go wrong."

She almost smiled. It cost her something; I could see it in the way she held her jaw. "You're a comfort, Underhill."

"I'm a diver. Comfort isn't in the job description. Accuracy is." I fed the fire again, unnecessarily, just to have something to do with my hands. "Broken collarbone. How bad is the fever?"

"Worse this morning than last night."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I've got." She shifted, and the movement pulled a sound out of her that she strangled before it became a cry. "The bone's through somewhere — I felt it grind when they dragged me out of the water. There's heat in it now, spreading up my neck. If you're asking whether I'll be alive in five tides, the honest answer is that I don't know, and I'd rather not know, because knowing would slow me down and slowing down is the same as stopping."

I had known divers like that. Men who refused to look at their gauges because looking meant acknowledging limits, and limits acknowledged became limits obeyed. They all died eventually, usually loudly. But sitting in front of me was a different animal entirely — a woman who had seen her whole expedition drowned or scattered, who had dragged herself a quarter mile inland with a shoulder full of broken gristle, and whose only concession to any of it was admitting she didn't know how long she had. That wasn't recklessness. That was bookkeeping. She was doing sums too, and she didn't like her answers either.

"We move on this morning's flood," I said. "We ride it down to the narrows, shoot them at slack, and hide above the mudflats before the ebb strands us. Tomorrow we cross the flats on the night flood and come down on the harbor from the seaward side, where nobody's watching because nobody believes anyone would be stupid enough to come that way."

"Nobody is watching," she said, "because everyone competent has already been bought. Which means the only people left are stupid and dangerous." She closed her eyes. "Go on, then. Tell me the rest. The part you haven't said yet."

"The part where moving you might kill you," I said. "A boat in chop, your arm bouncing with every swell, fever cooking you from the inside. There's a version of this where I leave you here with food and water and come back with help."

"There is." She opened her eyes. "And in that version, you come back and find this camp picked clean and me gone, because the search parties are working upstream to downstream and they'll reach this bend inside a day. You know it. I know it. Say the next part instead — the part about the case."

I looked at the waterproof case. It sat against her hip like a third passenger, scuffed green metal, salt-crusted, dented along one edge as though it had been dropped from a height or used as a weapon or both.

"If we're separated," I said, "the case goes in the water with you. Weighted. Nobody finds it by accident, and nobody finds it by searching, because searching takes time and time is the one thing the fleet can't buy more of either. Five tides cuts both ways. They have to find you before the dredges arrive, or whatever you found stays buried under a hundred feet of silt and a thousand tons of dredge spoil forever."

For a long moment she just breathed, and the fire ticked, and somewhere out in the drowned willows a bird started up its idiot dawn complaint.

"You've thought about this," she said finally. "About hiding things underwater so they stay hidden."

"It's most of what I do."

"No." She shook her head, careful, small movements only. "Most of what you do is *find* things. Hiding is just finding in reverse. Same skill. Same patience." She held my gaze, and the fever made her eyes too bright, but behind the brightness was something level and cold and completely unsentimental. "All right, Underhill. We go on this flood. But we do it my way on one point."

"Name it."

"When we shoot the narrows, you don't fight the picket boat. Whatever happens — however close they come, however much they see — you keep the skiff moving. You don't turn back for me, you don't circle, you don't get clever. A stopped boat is a caught boat, and a caught boat is two people in the fleet's hands instead of one case in the river." She said it flatly, the way you read out coordinates. "Promise me."

"I don't make promises I might want to break."

"Then make this one, because it's the only kind worth making." She leaned forward, wincing, until her face was a foot from mine and I could smell the fever-salt on her skin. "I spent three days watching better divers than you die because someone couldn't leave well enough alone. Okafor went back for Ruiz. Ruiz was already gone. Now they're both gone, and the only reason anything they found survived is that I floated downstream with it instead of staying to grieve." She settled back against the crate, spent by the speech, her breath whistling faintly. "Don't you dare go back for me."

* * *

We stripped the camp in twenty minutes. There wasn't much to strip — the expedition had traveled lean and been stripped once already by whoever had ransacked it — but I took the dive slate with its half-finished message, the coil of good line, the bailing bucket, the two intact air tanks Okafor had never gotten to use, and the canvas bag of tools that had somehow escaped the looters' interest. Tools always did. Looters wanted things that shone.

Beatrix watched me work and directed operations from her crate with the economy of a woman who had commanded crews through worse mornings than this. When I lifted her — carefully, her good arm over my shoulders, her weight lighter than it should have been and that fact frightening me more than the picket boat did — she bit down on whatever sound the movement caused and gave it to the river as silence.

The skiff was where I'd left it, tied in the willow shadow, riding low with the gear I'd brought upriver. I got her laid along the bottom boards amidships, padded with the folded tent canvas, her sling propped with the bailing bucket turned on its side. She looked, I thought, like cargo. Like salvage. The comparison arrived uninvited and I pushed it away, because I knew exactly what it was to be handled like cargo, and I wasn't going to be the one doing the handling.

"Flood's starting," I said. The current had begun its slow reversal, the willow tips leaning downstream now instead of up, the surface skin of the river smoothing as the tide took command. "Four hours of fair water. Sleep if you can."

"Where did you learn tide tables?" she asked, as I pushed us out into the current and took up the oars. "Really learn them. Not the harbor-master's chart versions. The real ones."

"My father." The word came out before I could decide whether to spend it. "He ran a mud pilot boat. He used to say the tide was the only honest thing on the river — it told you exactly what it was going to do, every day, twice a day, and asked nothing for it. Everything else lied." I rowed. "He was wrong about that, as it turns out. But it took me thirty years to prove it."

"What else did he say?"

"Why do you want to know?"

"Because talking keeps me from counting my own pulse," she said simply, "and because a man who measures his life in fathoms learned it from somebody, and I want to know who built you before I trust you with what's in this case."

The current took us around the first bend, and the drowned camp slid away behind the willows, and I found myself telling her things I hadn't said aloud in years. Not the big things — not yet, not those — but the small architecture of a diving life. How my father taught me to read water by the way birds sat on it. How he died, ordinary as rain, when his heart quit halfway through a winter crossing, and how the harbor family had folded me in afterward the way harbors fold in anything that floats. Marisol's mother feeding me at their table until I stopped flinching at the word *family*. The Grey Wren, and the five years of quiet miles I'd measured across her hull, and the navy buoy I'd been paid not to ask about.

Beatrix listened the way good officers listen — completely, without interrupting, filing everything. Twice she asked a question that showed she'd heard more than I'd said. Once, near the end, she said, "Your father sounds like he'd have liked this river," and I understood that she was keeping me talking too, keeping both of us above the waterline of our own fear, and I let her, because it worked.

* * *

The narrows announced themselves an hour before we reached them — a change in the river's voice, the broad mutter of open water tightening to a low urgent hiss between the bluffs. I shipped the oars and let the current carry us, and I got down flat in the bilge beside Beatrix and pulled the canvas tarp over us both, leaving a slit to watch through.

"Slack water in ten minutes," I whispered. "The picket sits on the east bluff. They'll have a lamp, maybe a line across the channel at night, but at slack in daylight they'll be bored. Bored is good. Bored looks at its own boots."

"And if they're not bored?"

"Then we find out how fast this skiff is."

She made a sound that was almost a laugh. "You have a plan for everything except the parts that matter."

"Nobody has a plan for the parts that matter. You just pick your heading and hold it."

Through the slit in the tarp I watched the bluffs slide closer, gray stone streaked white with bird lime, the channel between them narrowing to its forty yards of green troubled water. The picket boat sat where I'd known it would sit, a fleet launch with a mounted lamp and two figures aboard, one of them smoking, the smoke rising straight up in the windless air. Slack water. The river hung between tides like a held breath.

"Hold still," I murmured, though she hadn't moved. "Whatever happens."

We entered the narrows at the speed of the dying current, which was barely any speed at all, and for ninety seconds the world consisted of the launch growing larger off the starboard bow and the sound of my own blood in my ears. The smoker flicked his cigarette into the water. His companion said something I couldn't hear. Neither of them looked down. Why would they? The narrows saw a hundred boats a week, fishermen and mud-pilots and fuel barges, and the fleet's war was with expeditions and radio transmissions, not with a single tarped skiff drifting through at the pace of floating weed.

We cleared the bluffs. The river opened. My lungs unlocked.

Then, from behind us, the launch's engine coughed into life.

Not alarm — I told myself it wasn't alarm, it was shift change, it was routine, it was a bored man deciding to patrol rather than sit. But engines at the narrows meant wake, and wake meant the picket was coming downchannel, and coming downchannel meant passing us, and passing us meant a look at a tarped skiff carrying two shapes instead of one fisherman.

"Underhill." Beatrix's whisper was steady. "Heading. Hold it."

"Holding."

The launch came up the channel behind us at idle speed, its wake rolling under our keel and lifting the skiff in a long slow swell that I heard Beatrix absorb through her teeth. It drew alongside, fifteen yards off, close enough that I could see the crewman's face, young, sunburned, chewing something. He glanced at us. The tarp covered us to the gunwales. To him we were a mud-flat skiff doing what mud-flat skiffs did, riding the slack down toward the fishing grounds.

Fifteen yards. Ten. He looked away. The launch drew ahead, its wake slapping the banks, and rounded the next bend, and was gone.

I counted to a hundred before I spoke. "That's one."

"One what?"

"One thing that went right today. I'm keeping score. It helps the arithmetic."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "What's the score?"

"One right, several wrong, and the tide turning against us in about three hours." I took up the oars. "There's a creek mouth two miles down, past the old oyster beds. Deep enough to hide a skiff, shallow enough that nothing bigger follows us in. We lie up there through the ebb, and tomorrow night we cross the flats on the flood."

"Lying up through the ebb," she repeated. "Six hours in a hole in the marsh with a fever."

"With a fever and a case and a diver who knows every creek on this stretch of river better than the charts do." I pulled, and the skiff answered, and the lower reaches opened ahead of us, wide and brown and glittering, the mudflats showing their dark shoulders as the tide began to fall. "You wanted accuracy. That's the accurate version."

"Good," Beatrix said, and lay back against the canvas, and closed her eyes, and I genuinely could not tell whether she slept.

* * *

The creek, when we reached it, was exactly as I remembered — a slot of shadowed water threading back into the marsh, overhung with reeds, its entrance masked by a curtain of last year's dead growth. I poled us in until the skiff grounded softly on the falling tide, and I rigged the tarp as an awning, and I got Beatrix settled as comfortably as the bottom boards allowed, and then I sat in the stern and watched the water drop and did the arithmetic again, because the arithmetic was the only thing I could control.

Five tides had become four. By tomorrow night, three. The dredges would be loading at the fleet yard by now — I could picture it, the great chains going aboard, the spoil barges lashed alongside, Vane's men working with the unhurried competence of people destroying something on schedule. And somewhere between here and there, Danny Quill was moored in the harbor basin with a boat that could make the flats crossing safely, if he could be bought, and buying him would cost money I didn't have and trust I wasn't sure existed anymore.

"You've gone quiet," Beatrix said, from under the awning. "Quieter."

"I'm figuring the next move."

"Which is?"

"Money," I said. "And an old friend who'll take it, and what happens after he does."

The ebb drained the creek around us, exposing the ribbed brown mud, and the marsh smelled of salt and rot and the particular mineral cold of exposed ground, and far off across the flats a search launch swept its lamp across the falling water, once, twice, patient as a lighthouse, looking for exactly two people in exactly the wrong place.

Beatrix's hand found the case in the dark and rested on it.

"Three tides," she said. "Then we'll see what the river gives us."

The lamp swung away. The tide kept falling. Somewhere downriver, an old friend was sleeping with a price on his conscience, and by the next flood I intended to find out what it was.
