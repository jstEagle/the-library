## 10. Old Friends, New Prices

Danny Quill lived where the river forgot to be pretty — a lean-to of corrugated iron and old boat planks wedged between the tar works and the last mooring before the mudflats took over entirely. The smoke from his chimney smelled of kelp and diesel, the way it had for fifteen years, and the path to his door was paved with broken oyster shell that crunched like small bones underfoot.

Edmund walked it slowly, one hand steadying Beatrix at his elbow. She had refused the wheelbarrow he'd offered at dawn, and refused again the crutch he'd lashed together from a boat hook and a length of inner tube, so she came down the shell path in a series of deliberate, furious hops, her arm clamped over his shoulders, her breath whistling through her teeth with each landing. Her collarbone had swollen into a ridge beneath her borrowed shirt, and the fever had come back in the night — not the raving kind, but the low, banked kind, the kind that sat behind the eyes and made everything look slightly too bright.

"You said this man owed you," she said, between hops.

"He does."

"For what?"

"His life. Twice." Edmund watched the lean-to grow closer. "The second time he didn't know it was happening."

"That's the best kind," Beatrix said. "Debts you don't know you owe are the only ones people pay without haggling."

The door opened before Edmund could knock. Danny Quill filled it the way he always had — a big man gone soft at the edges, beard shot through with grey he'd stopped fighting years ago, hands like knotted rope hanging at his sides as if they didn't quite belong to him when they weren't holding something. He looked at Edmund for a long moment. Then past him, at the woman leaning on his shoulder, at the bandages visible at her collar, at the mud dried up both their shins to the knee.

"Well," Danny said. "You always did bring your trouble home wet."

"I need the *Kestrel*."

Danny didn't move out of the doorway. "Hello to you too, Eddie."

"Downriver. Tonight if we can, tomorrow's tide if we can't. I need someone who knows the flats well enough to run them dark, and I need them not to ask me why until we're clear of the picket line."

"And after they're clear?"

"After that, whatever questions you've got saved up."

Danny scratched under his beard. His eyes went to Beatrix again, and stayed there, and Edmund saw the exact moment recognition landed — not of her face, because Danny wouldn't know her face, but of her *kind*. Salvage broker. Expedition lead. The sort of person who arrived in harbors with money and left with wrecks and sometimes with men.

"You'd better come in," Danny said. "Both of you. Before somebody sees you standing on my shell like an advertisement."

* * *

Inside, the lean-to was one room and half another, crowded with the archaeology of Danny Quill's life: dive helmets on nails, a harpoon gun mounted above the stove like a hunting trophy, charts curling on every wall, most of them annotated in three colors of grease pencil going back decades. A kettle was already on. It was always already on at Danny's; Edmund suspected the man slept with it lit.

He sat Beatrix in the good chair — the one with actual springs — and she sank into it with a sound that was almost a groan and almost a laugh, and Danny put tea in front of her without being asked, and then stood over Edmund with his arms crossed while the kettle ticked itself quiet.

"The *Kestrel*," Danny said. "That's not a favor, Eddie. That's my hull. That's my engine, what's left of her. That's how I eat."

"I'll pay."

"With what?"

Edmund put the roll of notes on the table. He'd taken it from the expedition camp's strongbox, buried under the flooded mess tent — Beatrix's own money, or her backers', and she'd told him to take it without a flicker of shame. It wasn't much. It was enough to start a conversation.

Danny looked at the roll the way a man looks at a snake on his floor. Then he laughed, short and hard, and turned away to pour his own tea.

"You know what's funny? Five years ago you'd have walked in here and I'd have handed you the keys before you finished asking. You remember that winter we worked the *Marguerite*, both of us on air lines in water cold enough to crack teeth, and we split the salvage fifty-fifty even though I did half the work and all the complaining?" He shook his head. "Fifty-fifty. You were the only man on this river who ever gave me fifty-fifty."

"Danny—"

"So don't put money on my table." He said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. "Not yet. First tell me why there's a navy launch doing grid searches off the mudflats since Tuesday, and why Marisol sent her boy down yesterday with a message that said, and I quote, *if a tall man comes asking about boats, feed him and don't tell anyone he ate.*"

Edmund glanced at Beatrix. She was watching Danny over the rim of her cup with the flat, assessing calm of a woman running numbers through fever. She gave him nothing. This was his debt to spend, not hers.

So he told him. Not all of it — not the culvert, not the case, not the manifest — but the shape of it. An expedition upriver. A camp destroyed. People missing. A woman with a broken collarbone who knew something the fleet would burn the harbor to keep buried. Dredges coming. Tides counted on fingers.

Danny listened the way he did everything, heavily and completely, and when Edmund finished he sat down across from Beatrix and studied her for a long time.

"They say you're Calloway," he said.

Beatrix lowered her cup. "They say correctly."

"There's a paper going around." Danny's voice had gone careful, the way it went careful when he was measuring a depth by feel instead of by gauge. "Harbor office has it pinned up. Fleet printed it. There's a picture — bad one, taken from a distance, but it's you all right. And a number underneath."

"How much?" Beatrix asked. No fear in it. Curiosity, almost professional.

"Three hundred." Danny let it sit. "Three hundred pounds for information leading to the recovery of one B. Calloway, whereabouts unknown, last seen upriver. *Recovery.*" He smiled without any humor in it. "They chose that word careful. Not arrest. Recovery. Like you're a lost dinghy."

The kettle ticked. Somewhere outside, a gull argued with the wind. Beatrix set her cup down and folded her hands, and Edmund watched her do the arithmetic he was already doing: three hundred pounds was two years of Danny's diving. Three hundred pounds was his daughter's school fees and his roof mended and his engine rebuilt and a winter with meat in it. Three hundred pounds was the price of the *Kestrel* twice over, and all Danny had to do was walk to the harbor office and open his mouth.

"I see," Beatrix said. "Then perhaps I should be direct, Mr. Quill. I can't offer you more than they can. Anyone who tells you otherwise is lying. What I can offer is this: help us, and in a month's time the thing they're paying to bury will be public, and papers like theirs won't be worth printing anymore. Help us and you'll have helped sink the fleet's monopoly, and there isn't a diver on this river whose rates won't go up because of it. That's not three hundred pounds. That's not anything I can hand you tonight. But it's real, and it doesn't rot."

Danny looked at her for a long moment. Then at Edmund.

"She always talk like that?"

"Since I've known her, which is four days."

"It's exhausting," Danny said, but there was something in his voice that wasn't exhaustion at all.

* * *

They walked the mooring while the light went long and copper over the flats. The *Kestrel* sat low on her mooring buoy, a thirty-foot converted lifeboat with a cabin like a coffin and an engine Danny had rebuilt eleven times and swore at daily. She was ugly and slow and utterly reliable, and Edmund felt something in his chest loosen at the sight of her, the way a man feels seeing an old dog.

"She'll need fuel," Danny said, crouching at the edge of the slipway, testing the water temperature with two fingers like a cook testing a pot. "And she'll need me. You can't run the flats alone, Eddie, not at night, not with a wounded woman aboard. The channel moves. What was deep in March is a sandbar now."

"I know."

"Do you?" Danny straightened up. "Because here's my problem, and I'm going to say it plain because you've never once lied to me and I won't start lying to you." He turned, and in the copper light his face was all shadow and grey beard and two steady eyes. "I've got Nell. Eleven years old. Bad hip since birth, getting worse, and the fleet clinic will fix it free — free, Eddie, no charge, no waiting list — because her father holds a fleet tender license. The day I stop being useful to them, that clinic door gets heavy. You understand? Every favor on this river comes out of somebody's hide, and mine happens to be attached to my daughter."

Edmund said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either a promise he couldn't keep or a lie he wouldn't tell.

"So when I look at that paper in the harbor office," Danny went on, "with that number on it, I don't see three hundred pounds. I see Nell walking straight by twenty-five. And when I look at you—" he exhaled through his nose, long and slow, "—I see a man who pulled me out from under a collapsed hold in the *Marguerite* with his last ten minutes of air, and never once mentioned it after, not even when I was drinking and stupid and deserved mentioning. Both those things are true at the same time. That's what nobody tells you about this river. It's not that men are loyal or bought. It's that both live in the same chest, and some mornings you don't know which one's going to answer when you knock."

Behind them, Beatrix had lowered herself onto a bollard, conserving herself, watching the exchange with her fever-bright eyes and saying nothing. Letting it happen. Edmund understood that about her now — she didn't argue when argument would cheapen the thing being decided.

"What do you want, Danny?" Edmund asked finally. "Name it. If I can give it, it's yours."

Danny was quiet a while. The tide was turning below them; he could hear it, that faint muscular whisper of the flats beginning to drink.

"Fifty-fifty," Danny said at last.

Edmund blinked. "What?"

"Whatever this is. Whatever you're carrying, whatever she knows, however it ends — you split it with me fifty-fifty. Not the money. The *truth of it*. If it goes wrong, I stand where you stand. If it goes right, I get to say I was there. No more of this—" he waved a hand at the roll of notes still sitting untouched on his table inside, "—paying me to be a boat. I'm not a boat, Eddie. I'm the other diver. Always have been. You just went five years without noticing you needed one."

The copper light was dying. Out on the flats, the first of the evening birds came in low over the mud, and somewhere far downriver — too far to see, close enough to feel — Edmund imagined the dredges assembling, black hulls and chain buckets, eating their way toward the Grey Wren with the patience of machinery.

"Fifty-fifty," Edmund said, and put out his hand.

Danny took it. His grip was still the grip Edmund remembered, rope and iron and no give at all.

"One condition," Danny added. "We sail on the morning tide, not tonight. Tonight I fuel her, load her, and chart the flats, and you two sleep — her especially, because if that fever climbs on my boat I'll have to choose between turning back and watching her die in my coffin-cabin, and I don't fancy either. Morning flood takes us down clean. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

Danny nodded, satisfied, and started up the slip toward his lean-to, then stopped. He didn't turn around.

"Eddie. That paper in the harbor office. It went up Tuesday." A pause, filled with river sounds. "There's a second line under the number. Small print. I nearly missed it." He turned his head just enough that Edmund caught his profile, grim as a figurehead. "*Information leading to recovery, or confirmation of death.* They're paying the same either way. Whoever wrote that paper doesn't much care which one they get."

He walked on toward the light of his window. Behind Edmund, on the bollard, Beatrix rose slowly to her feet, one hand pressed to her collarbone, and looked downriver at the darkening water as though she could count the tides left in it by eye.

"Morning, then," she said quietly. "Let's hope the fleet reads its own small print slower than Mr. Quill does."
