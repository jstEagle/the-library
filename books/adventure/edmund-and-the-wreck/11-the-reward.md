## 11. The Reward

Danny Quill had never been a man who slept well on land, and that night in the loft above his boatyard he did not sleep at all.

He lay on the cot he kept for apprentices who never stayed, staring up at the rafters where the tar smell lived, and turned the fleet's offer over and over like a stone in his pocket. *The Calloway woman. Five hundred crowns for word of her position. A thousand for her person.* The agent — a soft-spoken man named Pell, with hands too clean for the river — had said it the way you'd quote a freight rate. No threat in it. No need for any. The number itself was the threat; it did its own work.

Five hundred crowns. Danny had earned four hundred and eleven last year, all told, mending hulls and running salvage jobs the fleet hadn't bothered to claim. His daughter Nell's arm had been broken in two places by a winch cable in March, and the fleet clinic in Greyharbor had set it well enough, but the bone had knitted crooked, and the surgeon who could straighten it charged ninety crowns a visit, three visits minimum, money Danny did not have and would not have before the arm set wrong forever. The fleet clinic would do the surgery free. All it cost was a name spoken into a radio handset, or a direction pointed with a chin, or even just a failure to warn.

He got up before dawn because lying still was worse than moving, and went down the outside stairs into the blue-grey cold of the yard. The river was doing its early-morning trick, the one where it looked like hammered pewter and smelled of weed and iron, and the mist stood on it in slow columns like the ghosts of masts. His breath smoked. Somewhere upstream, past the bend where the channel narrowed, Edmund Underhill was running a borrowed skiff through water no honest man used before full light, carrying a woman with a broken collarbone and a case she wouldn't let out of her reach, toward a wreck the navy forbade anyone to name.

Danny knew this because twelve hours earlier they had stood in his boathouse, lamplight swinging, and asked him for passage downriver, and he had said yes, and then he had gone inside and found Pell waiting on his own dock like a bill come due.

* * *

Pell had arrived at dusk, stepping off a fleet launch so quietly that Danny only noticed him when the man cleared his throat. He wore a grey coat that shed river spray like waxed paper, and he stood with his boots precisely at the edge of the boards, not touching the oil stain Danny had never managed to scrub out, as if dirt were something that happened to other men.

"Quill," Pell said. "You're a hard man to find after dark."

"I'm easy to find. I'm just not often worth finding."

That got a smile, thin as ice on a puddle. Pell reached into his coat and produced a folded sheet of paper, and Danny felt his stomach drop before the man even opened it, because he already knew what kind of paper it was. He'd seen them nailed to the customs house in Greyharbor during the strike year, back when he was young enough to think notices were about other people.

"An expedition went upriver three weeks ago," Pell said. "Unlicensed survey. Unregistered divers. The harbor master has been instructed to consider it a matter of navigation hazard." He smoothed the paper against the boathouse door, and the lamplight from inside fell across it, and there she was: a photograph of Beatrix Calloway, younger than the woman Danny had fed stew an hour ago, but unmistakable — the same level eyes, the same mouth set like a cleat. Below the photograph, in type so clean it looked apologetic: *INFORMATION LEADING TO THE RECOVERY OF FLEET PROPERTY. REWARD.*

"Fleet property," Danny said.

"The expedition's records are fleet property. Salvage conducted without license is salvage conducted against the fleet's interest, and what's against the fleet's interest is, by charter, the fleet's property." Pell said it smoothly, the way a man recites a prayer he stopped hearing years ago. "You'll notice the reward schedule. Five hundred for verified information. One thousand for recovery of the person."

"And if she's hurt?"

"Then the fleet's surgeons will see to her. We're not barbarians, Quill."

"No," Danny said. "You're accountants."

Pell didn't rise to it. That was the worst part, looking back — how little it took to move him, how patiently he waited, as though the river always came around to the fleet eventually and he was simply standing where it would arrive. "Your daughter's arm," he said. "The corrective work. Dr. Imray at the Greyharbor clinic is very good. He trained in the capital." A pause, measured out like line paid off a reel. "Fleet dependents receive such care at no charge. It's one of the benefits of association."

"I'm not fleet."

"You run a yard on fleet water. You buy your rope from the fleet chandlery at rates the independents can't touch. You've taken fleet contracts every winter since the *Merrilees* job." Pell folded the notice along its crease and held it out, and Danny took it because refusing it would have been a statement, and statements were things a man with a crooked-boned daughter couldn't afford either. "I'm not asking you to do anything tonight. I'm telling you what the water's worth this season. Prices change. Good men keep their options open." He stepped back onto the launch, and before the engine caught he added, almost kindly: "They're heading downriver, aren't they? Toward the Wren. Think about which side of the channel you want to be dredging on."

Then he was gone, and the wake slapped the pilings for a long time afterward, like slow applause, or like counting.

* * *

By the time Edmund and Beatrix had eaten and rolled into their blankets in the boathouse loft, Danny had walked the length of his yard four times and smoked half a tin of the cheap shag that made Nell wrinkle her nose whenever he kissed the top of her head.

Nell was asleep in the house with his sister, who kept the girl on the weeks Danny worked late, which was most weeks. Through the window he could see the shape of her under the quilt, one arm outside the covers — the left one, the bad one, laid straight as a plank because crooked bones ached in the cold and she'd learned to keep it still even sleeping. She was nine. She wanted to be a rigger, up in the tall work, and she had her mother's head for knots and her father's stubbornness, and the surgeon in Greyharbor had turned her hand palm-up and pressed along the forearm and said, in the mild voice doctors use for ruin, *well, we could have done better with more time.*

More time. Ninety crowns a visit. Three visits.

Danny stood in the yard with the fleet's notice in his pocket and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water closing over his head, exactly what kind of man the fleet thought he was. Not a traitor. Traitors were dramatic. What the fleet counted on was arithmetic — good men, tired men, men with daughters, doing sums in the dark until the sum came out the way the fleet needed it to. They didn't buy souls. They bought options, and let a man sell his own.

And the terrible thing, the thing that made him spit into the water and hate himself for the taste of it, was that the sum kept coming out their way. Five hundred crowns. Nell's arm straight. A winter without choosing between rope and bread. And what did it cost? A woman he'd known for one evening, feverish, hurt, carrying a case full of paper about a ship that went down before his grandfather could walk. Paper. Names of dead men dead eighty years. Who bled for that? Nobody living. Nobody he'd ever have to look at across a table.

Except Edmund.

Edmund Underhill, who had dived beside him for six years back when they were both young and poor and stupid enough to love the bottom of the river. Edmund, who had pulled him out of the silt collapse on the *Coriander* with a grip like a mooring bitt and never once mentioned it afterward, because mentioning it would have made it a debt and Edmund didn't believe in those between friends. Edmund, whose brother had gone down on a fleet job and stayed down while the company wrote *equipment loss* in a ledger somewhere. Edmund, who had spent five years diving a forbidden wreck for wages nobody could explain, and Danny had never asked why, because some questions you don't ask a diver before he goes down.

If Danny sold them, he wasn't selling a stranger. He was selling the man who had held his life in his fist in forty feet of black water and given it back without a word.

He went inside and sat by the cold stove until the window went grey.

* * *

In the morning he cooked enough for six, because that was how much he always cooked, and because feeding people was the one transaction he still understood cleanly. Beatrix came down the ladder one-handed, favoring the strapped shoulder, and ate like someone who had learned hunger as a discipline rather than an event. Edmund ate the way he did everything — steadily, completely, without comment — and watched Danny over the rim of his mug with those flat grey eyes that saw more than they admitted to.

"We'll want to be off with the ebb," Edmund said. "Past the narrows before it turns, or we're mudbound in front of the picket station."

"Mudbound's not the worst thing," Danny said. "Mud hides a skiff better than water does."

"That's the diver talking."

"That's the man who's grounded on the Coriander flats twice talking." He refilled their mugs. His hands were steady. He was proud of that later, and ashamed of being proud. "There's a stretch below Quill's Point — my namesake, family joke, don't ask — where the channel runs close under the reeds and the patrol launches can't swing wide. If you hug the bank at slack you'll pass the picket inside their sweep interval. Four minutes. I've timed it."

Beatrix looked up. "You've timed the fleet's patrols."

"I time everything. It's how I know what anything costs." He heard his own voice and wished it sounded less like Pell's.

Edmund set his mug down. Slowly, the way he did everything. "Danny."

"Don't." He turned to the stove because he couldn't say the next part facing them. "I'm not coming with you. I've got Nell, and a yard, and a sister who'd murder me, in that order. But there's a skiff behind the net shed, the *Kestrel*, new canvas on her seams, and I'll put fuel in her and food for three days, and you take her and don't log her anywhere."

Silence behind him. Then Beatrix, in that dry level voice: "What does it cost?"

And there it was. The question he'd been circling all night, arriving at last in daylight, in front of witnesses, wearing its true clothes. He could have lied. God knew he'd earned the right to one lie. He could have said *nothing, call it old times,* and let the debt sit unspoken like a body in deep water.

Instead he took the fleet's notice out of his pocket and laid it on the table between the mugs.

Edmund read it. Danny watched his face do very little, which was how he knew how much it was doing. Beatrix read it upside down, then turned it around with her good hand and studied her own younger face the way you'd study a chart of a coast you once wrecked on.

"One thousand for my person," she said. "I should be flattered. Last year I was worth eight hundred."

"This isn't a joke."

"No," she agreed. "It's a price list. Those are different things." She pushed the notice back across the wood toward him, deliberately, until it touched his knuckles. "So. Which are you, Danny Quill? The five hundred or the thousand?"

"I'm the man who didn't take either."

"Not yet." She said it without heat, without accusation, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "You kept the notice. Men throw away what they've decided against. You folded yours along the crease."

Danny looked at Edmund. Edmund looked back, and there was nothing in his face but the old patience, the patience of a man who had waited at the end of a lifeline in the dark for a friend to decide whether to climb or cut. He didn't plead. He didn't forgive in advance. He just waited, and the waiting was the whole argument.

"Nell's arm is broke crooked," Danny said, to the stove, to the floor, to the river through the door. "The fleet will fix it free if I'm helpful. That's the shape of it. That's everything. I'm not going to dress it up."

Neither of them said anything for a moment. Then Beatrix said, quietly, "How old is she?"

"Nine."

"My daughter would have been eleven." She said it the way Edmund dove — steadily, completely, no flourish — and then she picked up her mug and drank her tea, and let the sentence sit there in the room like a stone dropped in still water, rings going out and out.

Danny hadn't known that. There was no ring on the woman's finger, no photographs in the flooded camp he'd helped Edmund provision for, nothing. Just that one sentence, offered like ballast, and he understood suddenly that everyone on this river was carrying a weight they'd chosen not to show, and that the fleet's whole genius was weighing people one at a time so none of them ever saw the others' loads.

* * *

He fueled the *Kestrel* himself, and packed bread and cheese and a jar of his sister's pickle, and a spare coil of line he pretended was for the boat and knew was for whatever mess they made of her. While Edmund loaded the gear, Danny took the camera out of the drawer under the chart bench.

It was a cheap box camera, the kind chandleries gave away with a purchase of rope, and he'd won it off a dredge-hand at cards three years ago and never used it. He took it out now because his hands wanted something to hold that wasn't the notice, and because a plan had been forming in him all night, low and cold, the way fog forms — not decided, just *present*, the way weather is present.

He photographed the notice first, laid flat on the chart table, reward schedule legible. Then, when Beatrix was at the rail checking the tide gauge and Edmund was bent over the engine, he raised the camera and took two more. One of each of them. Candid, quick, the shutter's click swallowed by the river's morning noise. Edmund never turned. Beatrix did, at the second click, and looked at him across the deck with those level eyes, and Danny felt the camera weigh forty pounds in his hand.

"Insurance," he said. It wasn't an apology. It was the truth, and the truth was all he had left that wasn't for sale.

Beatrix nodded slowly. "Against what?"

"Against the day I have to choose with the clock running." He lowered the camera. "If I turn you in, I'll want proof I did it under pressure — that I tried first, that I fed you, armed you, timed the patrols for you. A man who helps first and reports last gets leniency. A man who reports first gets a bonus and a reputation." He heard it laid out plain and hated how sane it sounded. "And if the fleet comes for me anyway — if they take Nell's care off the table, if they burn my yard over some invented infraction — then I've got pictures of the two people they're spending a thousand crowns to find, and a story about why, and maybe a lawyer in the capital who likes lost causes."

"So you're hedging," Beatrix said.

"I'm a harbor man. Hedging is what we do instead of praying."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where one would go if she could afford one. "For what it's worth," she said, "I'd have done the same. I *have* done the same. Half my expedition's equipment is insurance against the other half." She glanced at the waterproof case lashed to her chest by a strap over the good shoulder, the case she slept holding, the case that had started all of this. "The difference between compromise and betrayal isn't the act. It's whether you still know which side you meant to land on."

"And do you know?" Danny asked. "Which side you mean to land on?"

"I know what I'm carrying," she said, and put her hand flat on the case, and that was answer enough.

* * *

They cast off on the ebb, the *Kestrel* sliding out of the yard's shadow into the pewter light, Edmund at the tiller with the economy of motion Danny remembered from a hundred shared dives — no wasted gesture, everything held in reserve. Beatrix sat amidships, small and upright, wrapped in a blanket against the chill, and as the current took her she raised her good hand, not waving exactly. Acknowledging. The way one skipper acknowledges another in a narrow channel: *I see you. Hold your course.*

Danny stood on the dock and watched them downstream until the mist took them, and the sound of the little engine faded into the long wash of the river against the piles, and then it was just him and the yard and the empty mug on the chart table and the fleet's notice, which he did not throw away.

He told himself the photographs were insurance. He told himself the notice was evidence of coercion, a thing to wave at a magistrate someday. He told himself that a man who fed them, armed them, timed the patrols, and gave them his own namesake point to hide behind was, whatever else, not yet a traitor — that betrayal was a door you walked through, not a threshold you stood on, and he was still standing on the threshold, and thresholds were honest places, weren't they, places where a man could still go either way?

He cleaned the camera and put it back in the drawer, and his hand paused on the drawer's edge, because underneath the camera, under a false bottom he'd forgotten he'd built twenty years ago, lay the tin he kept his father's things in — and on top of the tin, where it had lain since the funeral, a photograph of his older brother Tom in his fleet coveralls, grinning on the deck of a tender, nineteen years old and three weeks from shipping out on a job the company would later file under *equipment loss.*

Danny looked at his brother's grin for a long moment. Then he closed the drawer, gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping.

Downriver, past the narrows, past the picket station with its four-minute sweep, a skiff was hugging the reeds with two hundred miles of cold water ahead of it and a wreck at the end of the run that the fleet would spend any price to keep buried. And here in the yard, in a drawer, in a cheap box camera, sat two photographs that were worth five hundred crowns apiece to the wrong people and nothing at all to the right ones.

Danny went to find his sister and ask if she could watch Nell through the weekend.

He told himself it was for the yard work. He was almost sure that was a lie, and he was almost sure he was going downriver after them — but which way he'd be sailing when he got there, even the river didn't know yet.
