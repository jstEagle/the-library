## 12. Night on the Mudflats

The tide went out from under them like a thief leaving through a window.

One moment the skiff was riding easy in six feet of brown water, Danny Quill's borrowed engine ticking as it cooled, and the next the keel kissed mud with a sound like a kiss and a curse combined, and the river simply walked away. Edmund felt it happen in his spine before his ears — that faint settling, the whole hull leaning a degree to port — and he knew before he looked at the chart that they'd missed their window by minutes. Maybe ten. Maybe less.

"Grounded," Beatrix said.

"Grounded."

She said it the way she said everything now, one hand pressed flat against the splinted collarbone, her voice sanded down to its grain by fever and pain and three days of running. She didn't ask what came next. She'd been a salvage broker long enough to know that when a man who knew water said *grounded*, there was nothing to negotiate.

Edmund stood up in the skiff and looked at what the ebb had given them: miles of mudflats opening on every side, glistening under a half-moon, ribbed like the inside of a shell. The channel they'd been following was retreating downriver in a silver thread, and somewhere beyond the dark line of the far bank, the fleet's search launches were burning fuel and light looking for them.

"We can't float until the flood," he said. "Four hours. Maybe five."

"Then we're a landmark." Beatrix's eyes were clear even when the rest of her wasn't. "A grounded skiff, sitting in moonlight, exactly where anyone with a chart would expect a fool to run aground."

He'd already thought of it. He hated that she'd thought of it too, faster, with a broken collarbone and an infection climbing her arm like ivy. "We don't leave the skiff," he said. "We leave the *shape* of the skiff."

* * *

They worked in silence for twenty minutes, and it was the best work Edmund had ever done with another person.

He poled the skiff a hundred yards across the wet flats — each push of the stake into the mud making a sound like a slow kiss, muffled, intimate, obscene if you listened too hard — until they reached a channel scar left by some old spring tide, a depression in the mud deep enough to lie a hull in. Then he cut alder boughs from the scrub at the flat's edge, and they laid them over the boat in layers, and then, kneeling side by side in the cold ooze, they scooped mud over the branches with their hands until the skiff became a low mound among low mounds, indistinguishable from a hundred other hummocks the river built and abandoned.

Beatrix worked one-armed and did not complain once. Twice she swayed, and twice Edmund caught her elbow without either of them remarking on it. By the end they were both gray to the thighs, caked past the wrists, mud in her hair and along the line of her jaw, and when she looked at him — really looked, her face pale as fish-belly above the filth — she started to laugh. It was a small sound, quickly smothered against her good shoulder, but it was the first laugh he'd heard from her since the culvert, and it did something to his chest he didn't have a name for.

"What?" he said.

"You," she managed. "Five years diving a wreck you won't name, and your grand plan is to *become a mudbank*."

"It's a good plan."

"It's a terrible plan. It's the plan of a man who has spent so long being invisible he's forgotten how to be anything else." She wiped her hands on her ruined trousers, leaving them no cleaner. "Fortunately for us both, invisible is exactly what we need tonight."

They settled into the hollow beneath the boughs, backs against the hull, shoulders almost touching. The mud around them was already breathing out its cold — that particular river-flat cold that comes up from below, older than the water, patient as debt. Edmund pulled the tarp over their legs and told himself the shiver running through Beatrix was only the temperature.

Above them the sky was doing its enormous indifferent work. Clouds moved upriver in ragged ranks, shredding across the moon, and between the gaps the stars came out in numbers that always embarrassed him, as if the sky were showing off precisely because nobody honest ever looked. Somewhere out in the black a curlew cried, that rising note like a question asked by something that had been asking since before the fleet existed.

"How long," Beatrix said quietly, "until you start counting?"

"I'm not counting."

"Liar. You've checked the sky four times since we lay down. You're doing tide arithmetic in your head. I can hear it. It sounds like an abacus falling downstairs."

Edmund almost smiled. Almost. "Flood's around two. Search launch passed the point an hour ago going downriver. If they come back up this reach, they'll do it on the flood, same as us."

"So we have until two o'clock to be mud."

"To be mud," he agreed.

She was quiet for a while. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into a different register, lower, the conversational equivalent of lying flat. "Talk to me, Underhill."

"About what?"

"Anything. The wreck. The harbor. What mud tastes like. If that light comes over that rise and finds us, I would rather spend my last free hour learning something about you than listening to you breathe like a man defusing a bomb."

"I am not—"

"You are. In through the nose, hold, out slow. Very disciplined. Very alone." She turned her head, and in the broken moonlight her eyes caught a sliver of shine. "I've read men underwater before, Edmund. It's the same skill as reading wrecks. You look for what doesn't move."

* * *

The light came at half past midnight.

It came the way he'd known it would, sweeping up the reach on the young flood, a white blade laid across the mudflats, and Edmund heard it before he saw it — the flat diesel knock of a fleet patrol launch, that engine note stamped into his childhood the way a church bell is stamped into other people's. He put his hand on Beatrix's wrist and squeezed once, and she went still with a competence that frightened him more than the light did.

The beam climbed the far bank. Crossed the channel. Started toward them.

There is a quality to being searched for that Edmund had never known until that night, lying in cold mud under cut alder with a feverish woman's breath quickening beside him. It is not fear exactly. Fear is fast and clean. This was slower — a pressure, like deep water, like the moment thirty fathoms down when your light fails and the dark doesn't rush in so much as settle on you, taking your weight. The beam swung toward their hummock and Edmund did the only thing there was to do, which was nothing, absolutely nothing, becoming with total commitment a piece of ground that had never been anything else.

The light touched the edge of their hiding place and slid on.

It came back. Patrols always sweep in overlapping arcs; he knew the pattern from five years of watching navy boats question harbor traffic. First pass wide, second pass closer, third pass — if the operator was thorough — slow, quartering. The beam returned and crawled across the mud twenty feet short of them, close enough that Edmund could see individual blades of drowned grass standing in the ooze, lit like exhibits.

Beside him, Beatrix began to talk.

Not loudly. A murmur, barely voiced, pitched under the engine noise, meant for him alone — and he understood suddenly that it was meant for exactly that, that she had read the situation the way she claimed to read wrecks and found the thing that didn't move, the rigid place in him, and was working it loose the only way she could with her arm splinted and her body shaking.

"—my father took me out on a survey barge when I was nine," she breathed. "River work, nothing glamorous. He let me hold the lead line. Did you know the old leads had tallow in the bottom? Arming the lead, it's called. You press it into the bottom and whatever sticks tells you what's down there. Mud, shell, sand, wreck timber." Her voice stayed level as the light crawled nearer. "He said most people go their whole lives without knowing what's under them. Said the river keeps everyone's secrets and charges no interest. I believed him for about eleven years."

The beam lifted, swept the far flats, began its return.

"Then I found my first falsified manifest. A grain ship, scuttled for the insurance, six crew written off as lost to weather. My father's firm had brokered the policy." Her breath caught — pain or memory, impossible to say. "I was twenty-two. I sat in his office with the paper in my hand and watched him decide, right in front of me, which kind of man he was. He chose the firm. He chose it so smoothly I almost admired him."

The light was coming back. Closer this time. Edmund could hear voices on the launch now, two men, bored, professional, the boredom of men who have searched a hundred miles of river for people who are probably dead anyway.

"He died before I could ask him why," she whispered. "So I ask the rivers instead. Every wreck I raise, I'm asking him. *Why. Why does the paper matter more than the people.*"

The beam crossed them again — near enough that warmthless white light leaked through the alder boughs and lay in stripes across Edmund's knees — and moved on, and the launch's wake came lapping against the flats a minute later, small tired waves, and receded.

"They're turning at the bend," Edmund murmured. "They'll run the reach once more, then drop back to the picket line."

"Then I have time for one more confession." She shifted, winced, settled. "Your turn, diver. Five years on a wreck you won't name. Tell me about her."

And here is the thing Edmund had never told anyone, not Marisol, not Danny, not the harbor priest who heard confessions in a shed that smelled of creosote: the Grey Wren was not the first wreck he'd loved.

* * *

"There was a trawler," he said. His voice came out rusty, like a hinge in salt air. "Before the Wren. The *Kittiwake*. My brother Thomas crewed on her."

Beatrix went very still, the stillness of someone who understands that a door is opening that has been shut a long time.

"Fleet contract job, winter run, five years back. Storm came up faster than the forecast and the *Kittiwake*'s radio was — there were questions about the radio. There were questions about a lot of things. Thomas and four others went down off the shallows." He watched the moonlight move on the mud. "The fleet paid out compensation in eleven days. Eleven days, Beatrix. I've seen probate take longer over a fence dispute. They paid fast because paying fast closes doors. And the inquiry — there wasn't one. There was a report. A report is what they give you instead of an inquiry when the answer might cost them something."

"And you started diving."

"I started diving." The words tasted strange after five years of not saying them. "First it was just — the *Kittiwake* site. Diving her. Learning her. Some part of me thought if I knew the wreck well enough I'd understand how he died. That's not how it works. Wrecks don't explain themselves. They just wait." He paused. "Then the harbor gave me the Grey Wren contract. Survey and salvage assessment, routine work, except for the buoy."

"The buoy."

"Navy buoy on the upstream section. Marked restricted. The fleet pays the harbor a supplement every year, and the supplement buys silence, and the silence buys my contract, and my contract buys Marisol's mother her heart medicine and the Quill kids their school shoes. Everyone in the chain gets fed. That's how it works. Nobody has to be evil. The system just needs everyone a little hungry." He realized his hand had closed into a fist against the mud and made himself open it. "I dove the Wren for five years and never once dove past the buoy line. Not because I wasn't curious. Because curiosity, in a harbor, is a luxury item."

"And now?"

"Now I know what the buoy is for." He looked at her — fever-bright, mud-caked, holding a waterproof case against her body like a second heartbeat. "Now I know the Wren isn't a wreck. She's a grave somebody dug on purpose and then hired the whole river to forget. Same as the *Kittiwake*. Same as your grain ship. The fleet's been sinking its own ships for a hundred years and calling it weather."

The curlew called again, farther off, and the tide had begun its long invisible turn somewhere out beyond the bar. He could feel it in the mud, a faint loosening, the flats deciding to forgive.

"Why didn't you ever tell anyone?" Beatrix asked softly. "About Thomas. About the questions. Five years, Edmund. You had grief and you had suspicion and you had a whole harbor full of people who loved you."

"Because telling costs." He said it flatly, without self-pity, the way you'd state a depth on a gauge. "Telling got a man I knew run off the quay. Telling got a journalist's boat burned at its moorings — officially an accident. And because—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because if I said it out loud, it became real, and then I'd have to do something, and everything I love is downstream of the fleet's goodwill. Or it was."

"And now?"

"Now everything I love is lying in the mud next to me with a broken collarbone and a case full of paper that can burn the fleet to the waterline." He heard himself say it and was surprised — not by the truth of it, but by how little it cost. "So I suppose I'm done being hungry."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, in the dark, her hand found his forearm — mud-slick, freezing, steady — and gripped it once, hard, and let go.

"That," she said, "is the first honest thing anyone's said to me since my expedition stopped answering its own radio."

* * *

They talked after that in the low endless way people talk when the truth has been opened and can't be repacked — about harbors and fathers, about the specific loneliness of being competent, about whether the moon looked better from thirty fathoms down (it did, Beatrix refused to believe this, Edmund promised to prove it someday, and the promise sat between them glowing like a coal neither of them would acknowledge). Twice the launch came back up the reach and twice they became mud, and the second time Beatrix whispered the names of the *Kittiwake*'s lost crew as if memorizing them, and Edmund, who had never heard anyone but himself say those names aloud, had to turn his face into the cold tarp until his breathing steadied.

At ten past one, the eastern sky developed a seam of gray.

"Flood's making," Edmund said. "Hour to float. We should dig her out."

Neither of them moved immediately. The mud was beginning to gleam, the flats exhaling, and downriver the channel was filling like a vein refilling with blood.

"Edmund." Beatrix's voice had changed — alert, sharp, the broker surfacing through the woman. "When we float. Where do we go? Not the harbor — they'll be watching the harbor. Not downriver — the dredges are coming up it."

He had been doing the arithmetic all night, underneath everything, the abacus falling downstairs. And every path led to the same place, the place he'd sworn for five years he'd never dive, the place the buoy marked, the place where the answer to Thomas's report and Beatrix's case and the fleet's whole rotten century lay rusting behind a sealed door.

"The Wren," he said. "We go to the Wren."

Beatrix smiled — feverish, fierce, mud-streaked, entirely unafraid. "I was hoping you'd say that."

Out beyond the bar, unseen, the tide turned, and the river began, patiently, to give everything back.
