## 13. Why Edmund Dives

The launch passed over them at half past two, and after it was gone the river went back to being a river — that vast indifferent breathing thing that Edmund had loved longer than he had loved any person alive.

They lay in the mud of the channel shelf with only their faces above the silt, Beatrix beside him, her broken collarbone strapped down with tape and prayer, and the cold came up through the riverbed like a slow argument against everything they were doing. Edmund had been cold before. He had been cold in ways most men never learned existed: cold at forty fathoms with his regulator icing, cold on the Grey Wren's deck with his gloves rotted through, cold the winter he was eleven and slept in the net loft because there was nowhere else. This was not the worst cold. But it was the first cold in five years that had made him want to talk.

Beatrix had talked first. She was good at it — filling silence the way other people filled ballast tanks, deliberately, to keep something from rolling over. She told him about her father, who had taught salvage law at a university Edmund couldn't have found on a chart. She told him about the first wreck she'd ever seen raised, a trawler off the shallows, and how the crowd on the quay had cheered when the hull broke water, and how she had understood even then, at nine years old, that they weren't cheering for the ship. They were cheering for the answer. Ships were just questions that sank.

Edmund listened. And somewhere between the second searchlight sweep and the third, when the tide had begun its long imperceptible turn beneath them, he heard himself start to answer her — really answer, not the flat harbor version of himself he gave strangers, but the whole account, the one he hadn't spoken aloud since Marisol's mother died.

He told her about his brother.

* * *

His brother's name was Aled, and he had been four years older and infinitely braver, which is what Edmund had believed until the night Aled died, and what he had understood to be exactly backwards ever since.

Aled Underhill went out on fleet jobs the way other men went to church — regularly, gratefully, without examination. He was nineteen and already rated as a tender-diver, which on the river meant he did the work nobody else would take: hull inspections under loaded barges, snag-clearing in the shipping lanes, the deep maintenance dives where the fleet's insurance men wanted photographs and didn't much care what the photographs cost. He came home smelling of diesel and river mud, and he would stand in the doorway of whatever room Edmund was in and drip on the floorboards and say, *Well, little brother, the bottom's still there,* as if this were news worth reporting, and somehow it always was.

Their parents were gone by then — mother to fever, father to the sea in the ordinary way, a capsize, a body recovered three tides later. The harbor took them in the way harbors do, which is to say imperfectly and completely at once. Marisol's mother fed them from a pot that never seemed to empty. Old Tam Quill — Danny's uncle, back when the Quills were still one family instead of two estranged ones — taught them both to dive off the breakwater with a garden hose and a hand pump, which should have killed them and instead made them the strongest swimmers on the river. There was no paperwork to any of it. No one signed anything. You were cold and someone fed you, and that was the whole contract, and Edmund had spent his entire adult life trying to honor terms no one had ever written down.

Aled died on a Tuesday in March, on a fleet job, in water no deeper than the harbor mouth.

That was the part Edmund could never make landlubber people understand. Not a deep dive. Not some heroic catastrophe at the edge of the possible. A routine inspection of a mooring dolphin — a cluster of pilings, thirty feet of green water, twenty minutes of work. Aled went down at nine in the morning with a working diver named Corrigan watching his line. At nine-twenty the line stopped moving. At nine-forty Corrigan went down after him and came up alone, saying the visibility had closed like a door.

They pulled Aled out an hour later. His air was still half full. His mask was on. There was no entanglement on his body that the fleet's report could find, no equipment failure, no medical cause the coroner would sign. The official finding was *diver error — disorientation in poor visibility.* The fleet paid for the funeral. The fleet sent flowers. The fleet's local man, a smooth gray person whose name Edmund has spent years deliberately forgetting, shook Edmund's hand at the graveside and said the company stood behind its people, and Edmund, sixteen years old and hollowed out, had actually thanked him.

It took him eleven years to learn what had happened on that dolphin, and he learned it the way you learn anything true on the river — sideways, drunk, from a dying man.

Corrigan. Two winters ago, in the Harbor Light, with his hands shaking so badly he had to hold his glass in both palms like a man warming himself at a fire too small. He was dying of the lungs, the way old divers die, and dying men on the river develop a sudden appetite for arithmetic. He told Edmund that the dolphin's pilings had been rotten below the waterline — eaten through, hollow as reeds — and that the fleet had known for a season. There was a survey. There was a memo. The repair order had been written up and then quietly killed, because the dolphin served a berth the fleet was planning to abandon anyway, and why spend money on a pier you intended to let fall into the river? Aled had gone down to inspect a structure the fleet already knew was failing, wearing gear the fleet already knew was adequate, on a job the fleet already knew was unnecessary — except for the photograph, except for the file, except for the appearance of diligence.

And then, Corrigan said, and here his voice dropped to the register men use for things they're ashamed of, the survey itself had disappeared. He had looked for it, afterward, once the guilt got its teeth in. Asked around. The memo was gone from the files. The engineer who wrote it had left the river within the year, gone inland, untraceable. The paper trail had been swept the way you sweep a deck after a bad landing — thoroughly, professionally, without malice. That was the thing Corrigan kept coming back to, weeping now into his beer, the thing Edmund had carried out of that bar and turned over every night since: *there was no villain, boy. There was just a company, doing what companies do. Nobody decided your brother should die. Everybody just agreed it would be cheaper if nobody looked.*

Nobody looked. Except Edmund. Except, it turned out, Beatrix Calloway, upstream, with a team of divers and a waterproof case.

* * *

The tide was making. Edmund felt it before he saw it — a faint pressure against his cheek where it lay above the mud, the river beginning to lean inland, gathering itself for the flood. Somewhere far downriver, muffled by miles of dark water and reed bank, a dredge engine turned over and settled into its work rhythm, patient as digestion. Four tides. Maybe fewer now. Vane would not be idle either.

But lying in the cold with the mud holding him like a hand, Edmund found that for the first time since Corrigan's confession, the anger wasn't the loudest thing in him. Something quieter had come up underneath it, the way the big fish come up under the small ones feeding.

He had spent five years diving the Grey Wren for money, and then, when the money stopped mattering, for habit, and then — though he'd never have put it in words — for company. A wreck is a patient companion. It asks nothing. It holds its secrets the way the dead hold their breath: indefinitely, without complaint. He had measured his life in rope and fathoms because rope and fathoms don't lie to you and don't leave you. A hundred feet of line is a hundred feet whether your brother is alive or dead. The bottom is still there. Well, little brother. The bottom's still there.

He had thought he was staying away from the truth all these years. Staying small, staying quiet, taking the navy's money for the buoy he didn't ask about, keeping his head below the surface where the fleet's attention couldn't reach. He had called it survival. He had even, on his better days, called it wisdom.

Lying in the mudflat with Beatrix Calloway bleeding warmth into the silt beside him, Edmund finally saw it plain: he hadn't been avoiding the fleet. He had been avoiding the question. Because the moment you asked *why did Aled die*, you had to ask *who benefits from my not knowing*, and the moment you asked that, you were no longer a diver, you were a threat, and the smooth gray men would come for you the way they had come for the engineer, the way they had come for Beatrix's camp, the way they had come — mid-word, mid-sentence, mid-breath — for her radio transmission. The harbor family had fed him and housed him and asked nothing, and the price of their kindness was that he ask nothing, and he had paid it gladly for fifteen years because the alternative was to be alone in the world with nothing but a question.

But the question hadn't gone anywhere. Questions sunk are like wrecks sunk. They wait. They rust, but they wait. And the tide comes back — the tide always comes back — and what the river took, the river returns, changed perhaps, crusted over, but returning.

The Grey Wren was not his brother. Edmund knew that with a diver's precision. The Wren had gone down in 1919, forty years before Aled was born, carrying a manifest and a crime and a hold full of drowned witnesses, and the men who scuttled her had built the fleet that built the dolphin that killed Aled, and the chain between those two facts was made of nothing but paper and money and other people's willingness not to look. But a chain is a chain. Pull any link and the whole length of it comes off the seabed. That was what Beatrix had understood from the beginning, and what Edmund was only now, in the mud, in the cold, letting himself understand: this was never about one ship. It was about the machinery of not-looking, the great smooth machine that ground up Aled Underhill and a crew of forty in 1919 and God knew how many others between and since, and ran on, day after day, humming, profitable, respectable, fed on silence.

You couldn't fight a machine like that with grief. Grief was what the machine ran on.

You fought it with paper. With a manifest raised into daylight. With names read aloud on a quay. With the one thing the machine could not digest: the record, witnessed, public, undeniable. Salvage law, Beatrix had said, was the strangest and most beautiful thing the law had ever built — the idea that what lies lost belongs, in the end, to whoever brings it home. And the truth of 1919 was lying lost, and the fleet was coming with dredges to bury it deeper, and Edmund Underhill, who had spent five years being paid not to look, was going to look. Not for the money. Not even, anymore, for Aled. For the looking itself. For the principle that the bottom gives up what it holds, that nothing stays sunken forever, that the river keeps faith with the drowned better than the living do and it was time the living learned from it.

Justice outside the law — that was what this was, and he knew it, and he was done pretending otherwise. The law had signed *diver error* on his brother's death. The law had buried the survey and pensioned off the engineer. The law was a fine boat, but the fleet owned the harbor it was moored in, and some mornings you had to swim.

* * *

Beside him, Beatrix stirred. Her breathing had gone shallow and careful, the way it did when the cold reached the bone, and Edmund rolled toward her, mud sucking at his chest, and put his palm flat against her cheek to check for fever.

"Still here," she whispered.

"Still here," he said.

"You went somewhere," she said. "Just now. Your face changed."

"I'll tell you on the boat."

She almost smiled. "Divers. Everything deferred until the boat." But her eyes held on his, and in the last gray hour before dawn he saw that she understood anyway — that she had known since the culvert, probably, since the moment a stranger's hands had lifted her out of the dark and asked no questions first. Some debts you recognize on sight.

The sky over the eastern shore went the color of pewter, then oyster, then the faint bruised gold that meant the river was about to become visible, and visibility was death. Edmund counted the tide in his head the way he counted everything, in fathoms and minutes: the flood would float them off the shelf within the hour; Danny's boat would be waiting at the cut reeds if Danny was the man Edmund had believed he was, and if he wasn't, then the next hour would teach them that too. Downriver, faint as a heartbeat, the dredge engines kept their rhythm. Closer than yesterday. Four tides had become three in the night, and somewhere behind them Aldous Vane was learning — Vane would learn it today, Edmund was sure of it, the way the river teaches everyone eventually — that the woman was alive, and that the diver who knew the Grey Wren better than any living man had gone over to her side.

Edmund slid his arm under Beatrix's good shoulder and waited for the water to lift them.

"Well, little brother," he said, so quietly that only the mud and the turning tide could hear it. "The bottom's still there. But not for long."

The flood took them.
