## 14. The Fleet's Dredges

Aldous Vane kept a tide clock on his office wall the way other men kept portraits of their fathers. It had come with the room, the way the room had come with the job, and in nineteen years he had never once wound it wrong. The brass pendulum swung its slow half-circle behind glass gone amber with river damp, and twice a day it told him what the water was doing out there beyond the fleet yard's cranes — flooding, slacking, turning — and Aldous Vane had built his whole life on the principle that a man who knew when the water turned would never be caught standing in the wrong place.

The report came up to him at four minutes past six in the morning, carried by a runner who was breathing hard enough that Vane knew before he opened the envelope.

He read it standing at his window, looking down at the dredge *Consolidation* moored at the fitting berth, her bucket ladder folded against her hull like a sleeping arm. The morning fog was burning off the river in long rags. Somewhere below, the night shift was knocking off, and he could hear their boots on the iron stairs, and the smell of coal smoke and tar and the green rot of the mudflats came up through the window gap the same as it had every morning of his working life.

*The Calloway woman is alive,* the report said. *Seen at a drainage culvert above Miller's Bend by the search party's spotter. Feverish, injured, traveling with a man not yet identified. Search party declined to close without further instruction.*

Vane set the paper down on his desk, squared its edges with the blotter, and stood for a while doing nothing at all.

He was sixty-one years old and he had spent forty-four of those years on this river. He had started as a bucket-tender's boy on a dredge called the *Perseverance*, back when the fleet still painted its vessels white so you could see them coming from three bends away, and he had worked every grade between mud and management: deckhand, winchman, foreman of channels, superintendent of works, and now this — a room with a tide clock and a telephone and the whole grey weight of the Consolidated River & Harbor Trust settling onto his shoulders each morning like a coat he could never quite take off.

People in the harbor towns said things about him. He knew that. He'd heard the word *hangman* once, spoken by a boy on the Coalhaven quay who hadn't known whose son he was talking in front of, and Vane had let it go, because a foreman who answered every insult became a foreman who answered nothing else. But the word sat wrong with him, all the same. Not because it stung — because it was lazy. A hangman takes a life that the law has already decided about. What Vane did was harder and lonelier than that. He made the decisions the law was too squeamish to make, and then he lived inside them, every one, for years, in a room with a window over the water.

He poured himself coffee from the pot his secretary kept filled and drank it standing, because sitting down felt like agreement, and he had not yet agreed to anything.

The woman was alive.

He had assumed otherwise. That was the honest arithmetic of it, and Vane prided himself on honest arithmetic even when it shamed him. When the Calloway expedition's radio had gone quiet eleven days ago, when the search parties had come back reporting a flooded camp and no bodies, he had done the sums the way the river taught you to do them: cold water, cold nights, an injured party, no supplies. The river keeps what it takes. He had written *presumed drowned* in his own hand on the incident summary, and he had slept that night, because the alternative to sleeping was lying awake being a different kind of man, and he had tried being that kind once, in his forties, and it had nearly broken him.

Now she was alive, and feverish, and moving downriver — or trying to — with some unidentified man, and somewhere in a waterproof case or a buried cache or the stubborn head of Beatrix Calloway herself was the thing she had come upriver to find.

Vane crossed to the filing cabinet — real steel, real locks; he trusted paper more than he would ever trust wire — and took out the folder marked only with a date: *September 1919.* It was thin. That was the point of it. Forty-one pages for the drowning of a ship and twenty-six men, and most of those pages were insurance correspondence in beautiful copperplate, men in London writing to men in the port city about *the regrettable total loss of the S.S. Grey Wren, valued at £48,000, cargo and hull.*

He had read the file perhaps two hundred times. He knew which sentences mattered. He knew that the *Grey Wren*'s last manifest listed machinery and pig iron, and that the salvage surveys of 1921 found neither, and that the survey was signed off by a fleet founder named Ambrose Pell, whose grandson now chaired the board that paid Vane's salary. He knew that three of the crew's widows had asked questions and been quietly settled, and that the fourth — a Mrs. Harkess, of Coalhaven — had asked questions until she died asking them, and that her letters were the reason the file existed at all, because someone in 1934 had thought it prudent to keep the evidence of what had been survived rather than risk it surfacing elsewhere.

Order, Vane thought, closing the cabinet. That was the whole of it. Order.

He was not a cruel man. He wanted that understood, if not by the harbor towns, then at least by himself, in the privacy of his own skull, where a man had to live with whatever he allowed. He fed his sister's cat when she went to the coast. He had paid for the schooling of two bucket-tenders' orphans out of his own pocket and told no one, because charity announced is just advertising. He believed — genuinely, wearily, with the conviction of a man who had watched the alternative — that the river towns ate themselves alive without the fleet. That before the Trust, the channel silted, the trade died, the towns starved and turned on each other, and men like Ambrose Pell did worse things in the open air than anyone had ever done in the dark. The fleet was a dam holding back something older and hungrier than water. And a dam, to be a dam, could not have holes in it. Could not have women crawling out of drainage culverts carrying forty-year-old questions in waterproof cases.

If the truth came up, the dam cracked. If the dam cracked, it wasn't one town that drowned. It was the credit, the contracts, the dredging leases, the clinic in Coalhaven that treated dockworkers' children for nothing, the pension fund that had kept four hundred widows fed through the lean years. All of it stood on the same footing, and the footing was silence. That was not villainy. That was engineering.

He picked up the telephone.

* * *

"Four tides," he said. "I want the *Consolidation* under way on tomorrow's flood."

There was a pause on the line, the particular quality of pause that meant Harbormaster Creel was taking off his glasses. "Aldous. She's not due upriver until the week after next. The channel survey isn't finished above Stonecut, and the—"

"The channel survey is finished. I finished it." Vane looked at the chart pinned above his desk, the river drawn in ink and correction-pen, every shoal and snag annotated in his own cramped hand across thirty years. "You'll take her up on the flood, work the narrows on the ebb, and be anchored off the wreck site by the fourth tide from now."

"Aldous—"

"There is a woman on my river, Tom, who should be dead, and she is not, and every hour she breathes she is either dying of fever or getting stronger, and I cannot know which, and I do not intend to find out by waiting." He heard his own voice go flat and level, the voice he used on dredge decks in weather, and he let it. "Take her up."

Creel was quiet a moment longer. Then, carefully: "And if we find her? Or the man she's with?"

Vane looked out the window. The fog had burned off entirely now, and the river lay bright and indifferent in the morning light, running seaward with everything it carried — leaves, oil-slick, the slow brown memory of the hills. On the fitting berth below, the *Consolidation*'s crew were beginning their day, small figures in canvas, and one of them waved up at the office window the way they always did, not knowing or caring who watched, and Vane did not wave back because he never had.

"You'll bring her to me," he said. "Alive, if it can be managed. Unharmed, if it can be managed. I want to talk to her before anyone else does." He paused. "And Tom — if there's a diver with her, a local man, someone who knows the wreck site — I want his name before noon."

He rang off and stood a moment with his hand still on the receiver, because there was one call left to make and he had been avoiding it since dawn, and a man could put off a board chairman for exactly as long as it took to pour a second cup of coffee.

* * *

Pell the younger received him in the boardroom at nine, all long windows and polished mahogany and the smell of money that had been money so long it had forgotten being anything else. Young Pell was fifty-eight and looked forty-five, the way well-kept men do, and he listened to Vane's report with his fingertips steepled and his face arranged into an expression of mild concern that Vane had learned to read as fury.

"She's alive," Pell said at last. "Injured, feverish, and she has the case."

"We don't know she has the case."

"We know she came upriver for it, and we know she's alive, and we know your people lost her." Pell unfolded his hands and laid them flat on the table, a gesture of reasonableness that Vane had seen precede three careers ending. "Aldous, I'm going to speak plainly, because you're the one man in this building I can speak plainly to. My grandfather built this company. Whatever happened in 1919 happened before any of us, in a world with different rules, and I will not have it dragged up and waved about on courthouse steps by some salvage broker with a grudge and a press list. The company employs eleven thousand people. Eleven thousand, Aldous. You've seen the pension rolls. You've signed them."

"I sign them every quarter," Vane said. "That's why I called you instead of handling this alone."

Something flickered in Pell's face — relief, quickly banked. Men like Pell needed to believe the dirty work was delegated, not chosen, and Vane had spent nineteen years letting them believe it, because the belief kept the board steady and a steady board kept the clinics open and the pensions paid, and Vane had long ago stopped pretending to himself that the ledger didn't balance that way. It balanced. It balanced every single day. It was just that the entries were written in a hand he sometimes didn't recognize as his own.

"What do you need?" Pell asked.

"The dredge moves tomorrow. Four tides to the site. Once the *Consolidation* is anchored over that wreck, nothing comes up out of it that doesn't pass through my hands." Vane held the old man's gaze. "But I'll tell you now what I told your father, and his father before him, and I'll keep telling it until one of you hears me. If that manifest surfaces — in a courtroom, in a newspaper, anywhere with witnesses — this company does not survive the scandal by fighting it. It survives by surrendering fast and sacrificing the dead. There are ways to do that. I've drafted them. They're in the safe. I hope to God we burn them unread."

Pell smiled the smile of a man who intended to burn nothing. "Get the case, Aldous."

Vane walked back down through the marble lobby past the founders' portraits — Pell, Crane, Ashworth, the three faces that watched every employee cross the floor, painted in the year of the *Grey Wren*'s loss, all three of them prosperous and serene — and out into the street, where the harbor noise came up around him like water, gulls and hammers and the bell buoy clanking its idiot patience, and he stood on the steps a moment breathing it in.

He thought about Mrs. Harkess again. He often did, lately. Her letters were in the file — four of them, the last one barely legible, written the winter before she died. *My Thomas went down with that ship and nobody will tell me why the machinery wasn't in the hold. I am seventy-one years old. I do not want money. I want one sentence that is true.* The company had sent a clerk with a settlement offer. The clerk had been turned away. And Vane, reading it in 1961 with a cup of tea going cold beside him, had felt something move in his chest that he had spent the years since refusing to name, because naming it would have required him to act on it, and acting on it would have cracked the dam, and the dam held back the sea.

One sentence that is true.

She had been right, of course. That was the unbearable part. The truth was not a flood. It was a sentence. It was one sentence, and it had been available for forty years to any man with the courage to say it aloud, and instead the company had buried it deeper than the ship, and Vane had shoveled the earth himself, and called it order, and mostly slept at night.

He got into the fleet car and gave the driver the yard address.

* * *

By evening the *Consolidation* was coaling. Vane walked the fitting berth in the last light, checking with his own eyes what he could have read in reports, because reports were how mistakes propagated and he had promised himself, forty years ago on the *Perseverance*, that he would never run a vessel he hadn't walked. The bucket ladder gleamed with fresh grease. The anchor windlass turned sweet under its tarpaulin. Creel moved along the deck beside him, saying little, and Vane appreciated that in the man — a harbormaster who filled silence with reassurance was a harbormaster who hadn't understood the river yet.

"Spotter says the man with her is a diver," Creel offered finally, as they reached the stern. "Local, by his boatwork. The picket at Stonecut logged a skiff running dark through the slack last night. Description matches a harbor man. We're pulling the lists now."

"Do it quietly. No reward posted — not yet. A reward turns every fool on the river into a search party, and search parties spook divers, and a spooked diver goes to ground where I can't reach him." Vane watched the coaling barges nudge alongside, black dust rising in the lamplight. "Besides. If he's a harbor diver, he'll surface on his own. They always do. The water pulls them back."

"And the woman?"

Vane was quiet for the length of three swings of the crane. Downriver, somewhere past the darkening bends, a feverish woman with a broken collarbone was drifting toward the sea with a dead man's secret in a waterproof case, and a diver who knew these waters better than any chart was pulling her along, and four tides from now the biggest machine the fleet owned would drop its anchors over the grave both of them were racing toward.

"Bring me the case, Tom," he said. "Whatever else happens — however it happens — the case comes to me first. Not to the board. To me." He turned to go, then stopped. "Because if it has to be opened, I'd rather it was opened by someone who remembers there were twenty-six men aboard."

Creel looked at him for a long moment in the lamplight, and whatever he saw in Vane's face, he nodded slowly and said nothing, which was the correct answer, and Vane climbed the iron stairs to his office feeling the weight settle onto his shoulders the way it did every night, familiar as a coat, heavier than a coat, and stood at the window watching the navigation lights kindle one by one down the darkening river.

On the wall, the tide clock ticked toward the turn.

Four tides, he thought. Enough time for a fever to kill her, or a river to hide her, or a diver to do something no diver in the history of this harbor had ever been foolish enough to try.

He wound the clock, though it didn't need winding, and went home to eat dinner alone.
