## 15. Salvage Rights

Pain was a country Beatrix Calloway had visited before, but never one she'd planned to stay in.

She lay in the stern of Danny Quill's boat under a tarpaulin the color of old fish scales, and she took an inventory of herself the way she'd once taken inventory of cargo holds: what was sound, what was compromised, what could be written off entirely. Left arm — useless, the collarbone grinding like a rusted hinge whenever the boat rolled. Right hand — hers, thank God, and steady enough to hold a pencil if someone wedged it between her fingers. Ribs on the left side — two of them singing a high note with every breath. Fever — down since Edmund had forced water and willow bark into her at the culvert, though it still came back at dusk like a tide that hadn't gotten the message about turning.

Everything else was intact. That was more than her crew could say, and she did not let herself think about their names for longer than a breath each. A breath each was all she'd allow until this was finished. Grief was a luxury of people who had time, and time was the one thing the river was not giving them.

The boat moved downriver through the grey morning, and Beatrix watched the world upside-down from under the tarp's edge — sky below, mud above, gulls wheeling in the reflection off the water. It suited her. She'd spent three days in a drainage culvert learning to see the world sideways, breathing shallow so the broken bone wouldn't shift, listening to reeds talk to each other in the wind while men with boots walked the levee overhead calling her name in voices that pretended to be concerned.

*Dr. Calloway? Dr. Calloway, we only want to help you.*

They had said *help* the way her father used to say *investment*. She'd grown up in rooms where words were load-bearing structures, and she knew a false wall when she heard one.

Edmund sat at the tiller with his collar up, saying nothing, which she was beginning to understand was his native language. He spoke fluent silence. He could hold a whole conversation in the set of his shoulders. She'd catalogued some of it already: shoulders square meant he'd made a decision; shoulders dropped meant he was grieving something he wouldn't name; shoulders turned slightly away from her meant he was deciding whether to trust her, and the angle of the turn measured how much.

Right now they were mostly square, angled maybe ten degrees toward her. Progress.

"Danny says there's a fleet launch doing grid searches past the second bend," he said, without looking back. "We'll be under the tarp when we pass. Don't cough."

"I hadn't planned to."

"You coughed twice in the last hour."

"Then I'll unplan it." The joke cost her a rib-twinge and was worth it, because she heard him exhale through his nose — the closest thing to a laugh she'd gotten out of him yet, and she filed it away like a chart correction.

Danny Quill was at the wheel forward, a broad man with river-mud permanently worked into the creases of his knuckles, steering them down channels he clearly knew by feel rather than by chart. He kept glancing back at her when he thought she couldn't see, and she let him think it. She'd been reading people across salvage tables for fifteen years; Danny Quill was a man doing arithmetic he didn't like, weighing something against something else, and she suspected she knew both sides of his equation. There was a reward out on her. There always would be, from now until this ended. She was, at present, the most valuable piece of floating wreckage on the river, and every soul who looked at her was doing sums.

Let them sum. She had one card left, and it was bigger than any reward.

The pain pulsed in time with the engine, and she closed her eyes and went back — because there was nowhere else to go, and because the memory was the map now — to the hold of the *Merrow*, nine days ago, with her dive lamp burning a cone through water the color of strong tea.

* * *

Her diver Nadeem had gone through the breach first, into the section of the Grey Wren no chart acknowledged, and when his voice came up the hardline it was stripped down to single syllables. *"Beatrix. You need to come down here."*

She'd gone. Of course she'd gone. She'd been chasing this wreck for six years, ever since a dying archivist in Rotterdam pressed a photostat into her hands and said, *the manifest was never filed, and the men who should have filed it built an empire on the gap.* Six years of grants denied, permits refused, harbors closing to her one by one with polite letters that all smelled of the same law firm. Six years of being told, in the language of insurance and admiralty, that the Grey Wren was a closed file.

The strongroom door had been sealed with a shipyard stamp — not storm damage, not panic. Deliberate. Riveted from outside, painted over twice, and beneath the paint, scratched by some dying man with a belt buckle or a ring: *WE ARE LOCKED IN. GOD SEE THE PAPER.*

Nadeem had cut the rivets with a torch while she held the light, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth and tarred canvas, dry as the day it was hidden — hidden, not lost, *hidden*, by someone who'd known the ship was going down and had chosen paper over lifeboat space — lay the manifest.

Not the manifest the insurers received. The real one.

She'd read it by lamplight in the flooded hold, her hands shaking in cold-water gloves, and the numbers had rearranged themselves into a sentence: the Grey Wren had sailed overloaded, insured for triple her worth, carrying not just cargo but eleven stowaways — witnesses, workers, a clerk who was going to testify about falsified tonnage returns — and her sea valves had been opened by hand. There was a page of engine-room notes in a second engineer's hand, dated the night before the sinking. *Valves turned. Mr. Ashcombe gave the order. We are told it is company business. Marta is aboard and does not know.*

Marta. He'd signed it with a name. A wife, a daughter, someone waiting at a dock that would never see him again.

And beneath the manifest, a confession in a founder's own hand — Aldous Vane's grandfather's grandfather, the man whose bronze statue stood in the fleet's headquarters court — admitting everything, written to be opened after all the witnesses were dead, because even monsters keep ledgers.

Beatrix had surfaced into the grey dawn of the camp knowing two things with absolute clarity. First: this document, authenticated and raised under lawful salvage procedure, would break the fleet the way ice breaks a hull — slowly, then all at once. Second: they were going to kill her crew for it.

They'd come within thirty hours. She'd been lucky in ways that still made her sick — ashore checking a bearing when the first launch arrived, far enough to see the tents slashed, to hear the shots stop, to fall down the levee embankment and crack her collarbone and crawl into the culvert while the river rose over her ankles and the searchers' lights swept the reeds.

Three days in the dark, holding a waterproof case against her chest with her one working arm, reciting the manifest's serial numbers to herself to stay sane. If she died in that culvert, the numbers would die with her. So she didn't die. She got angry instead, and anger floats better than fear.

* * *

"Quill's going to put in at the eel stakes," Edmund said, pulling her back. "Hour from now. He wants payment settled before we go farther, and frankly so do I. Whatever you're going to tell us, tell us there, where he can hear it too. He's risking his boat."

"He's risking more than his boat," Beatrix said. "I know about the reward."

Edmund's head turned a degree. "How?"

"Because I posted bounties myself, in my broker years. I know what the notices look like, and I saw the way Danny looked at me this morning. It wasn't pity. Pity doesn't do arithmetic." She shifted carefully against the gunwale. "He'll take me or he won't. But either way, you need to hear what I have, because it changes what we're running *toward*, not just what we're running from."

The tarp lifted briefly as Edmund checked the sky, and grey light fell across her like cold water. "You've been saving it," he said. Not accusing. Noting.

"I've been *rationing* it. Information spent too early buys nothing." She met his eyes. They were the color of the estuary in February, and just as hard to read depth on. "You've been diving that wreck for five years, Edmund. Paid to ignore it. I need you to understand that the ignoring was the crime. Everything else follows from that."

The eel stakes came up out of the mist like the masts of a drowned forest — black poles leaning at drunk angles, hung with weed, marking a channel that shifted every season. Danny brought the boat alongside a rotting platform with the tenderness of a man docking beside his own mother, and while he tied off, Beatrix sat up against the gunwale, wedged herself upright, and prepared to spend her last coin.

* * *

Danny crouched on the platform planks across from her, arms folded, rain beading on his oilskin. Edmund stood at the rail where he could watch both the channel and her face, and she noted — with the part of her mind that never stopped taking notes, the part that had kept her alive in the culvert — that he positioned himself exactly between her and the open water. Whether that was guarding her or guarding against her, she genuinely could not tell. Perhaps neither could he.

"Salvage law," she began, "is older than the fleet, older than the navy charter, older than every court on this coast. It comes from the wrecks themselves. Find a derelict on open water, raise her, and she's yours — that's the root of it. But there's a clause. An old one. My lawyers call it the *derelict of record* provision, and the fleet has spent eighty years pretending it doesn't exist."

Danny spat over the side. "Lawyers. Last time a lawyer helped anyone on this river, it was the lawyer."

"This one helps us. Listen." She pulled the pencil from her jacket with her good hand, held it out, and Edmund — after a heartbeat's hesitation — wedged it between her fingers. She drew on the flat of a supply crate: a line for the river, a cross for the wreck. "When a vessel sinks carrying evidence of a felony — fraud, murder, anything a court can hang a name on — and that evidence is recovered by a salvor acting in good faith, the salvor holds it as *officer of the inquiry*. Not owner. Custodian. And a custodian cannot be silenced, bought, or searched without triggering a public hearing. Automatically. Within fourteen days. It's in the Admiralty Code, section eleven, and it predates the fleet's founding charter by forty years, which means their lawyers can't touch it without admitting the code applies — and the moment they admit the code applies, they admit the wreck is a *derelict of record*, which means they admit there's something to record."

She let that sit. Rain ticked on the crate around her pencil lines.

"So whoever raises that manifest," Edmund said slowly, "doesn't just have proof. They have a *process*. The proof drags the courtroom behind it like an anchor chain."

"Exactly. It's the one door the fleet can't lock, because they'd have to unlock it publicly to do it. Fourteen days from recovery, a commissioner must convene, the document must be read into the record, and the record is public. No sealed courts. No 'closed files.' Their whole empire runs on the gap between what happened in 1919 and what was written down. Salvage rights close the gap."

Danny had gone very still. "And the fleet knows this."

"The fleet's founders wrote half the modern code trying to bury that clause. It survived anyway. Law is like ballast — you can shift it, but you can't throw it all overboard without capsizing." She looked from one man to the other. "Which brings us to the arithmetic. Vane's dredges arrive in five tides. When they do, they'll strip the site — legally, they'll claim the fleet holds title as successor-in-interest to the original owners, and they'll shred the strongroom along with everything else, and no clause will matter because the evidence won't exist. Unless the manifest is raised *first*. Raised, documented, witnessed, and in a custodian's hands before their crane hooks touch the hull."

"Five tides," Edmund said. "To cut into a sealed strongroom, sixty feet down, in water nobody's dived clean in eighty years."

"With cutting gear we don't have, on air we barely have, with a crew of—" she gestured at the three of them with her bandaged arm and winced, "—two and a half."

Danny laughed, short and harsh, but there was something underneath it that wasn't laughter. "You're not talking about running anymore. You're talking about going *back*."

"I'm talking about winning. Running just means they find us later, somewhere with fewer witnesses." She leaned forward despite her ribs, and the fever lit her voice from underneath. "Danny Quill, they've put a price on my head, and I'm telling you it's less than half what your silence is worth, because my silence ends in fourteen days the moment that manifest surfaces, and theirs lasts forever. Edmund Underhill — you've measured five years in fathoms on that wreck, paid to look away. This is the tide where looking is worth more than any wage the fleet will ever pay you. Whoever raises her first owns the truth. I intend for it to be us."

The rain thickened. Out in the channel, somewhere behind the mist, a fleet launch sounded its horn — two long notes, routine, a vessel announcing itself to the river the way the fleet announced itself to everything: *we are here, we are large, move.*

Edmund stared at the pencil marks on the crate. The line of the river. The cross of the wreck. She watched him the way she'd once watched a gauge needle climb — waiting to see if it would settle in the green or bury itself in the red.

"My brother dove for them," he said at last, so quietly Danny leaned in to hear. "That's not why I'm telling you. I'm telling you because I know the strongroom. I've swum that corridor fifty times. I know where the hull's thinnest, where the silt runs, where the old air pockets might still hold." His jaw worked. "Five tides isn't enough. But four might be, if we start now."

Beatrix felt something loosen in her chest that had nothing to do with the bones — hope, or its dangerous cousin. "Then we don't run downriver."

"No," Edmund said, and squared his shoulders fully toward her for the first time. "We run back."

Danny looked at the mist, at the horn-note fading, at the two of them, and swore with great feeling and considerable variety. Then he untied the line.

"Tide's wasting," he said.
