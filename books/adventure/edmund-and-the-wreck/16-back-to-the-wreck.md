## 16. Back to the Wreck

The tide turned at four minutes past two, and Edmund felt it before he saw it — a long slow shudder running up the anchor line and into the bones of the skiff, as if the river itself had cleared its throat. He'd been reading water for twenty years. It was the first honest thing he'd ever learned, and it had never once lied to him.

Beatrix lay propped against the gunwale with her arm strapped across her chest in a sling made from Danny's spare shirt, her face grey under the freckles, watching him coil rope.

"You're doing it wrong," she said.

"I'm doing it my way."

"Your way is wrong."

"My way has kept me alive for five years on a wreck everyone else is afraid to name." He snugged the coil down and set it in the bow. "Your way got your camp flooded."

She almost smiled. It cost her something — he could see the pain of it pull at the collarbone — but she paid it anyway, and that told him more about her than anything she'd said since the culvert. "Fair," she said. "But we're not diving your wreck yet. We're rowing toward it. Different arithmetic."

He didn't answer. Downriver, past the bend where the channel narrowed and the reeds gave way to grey shingle, the Grey Wren waited under forty feet of cold green water, exactly where she'd been for a hundred and six years, exactly where he'd left her yesterday morning with his slate wiped clean and his mind wiped cleaner. He knew every rib of her. He knew the way the current combed her deck planking into long silver furrows, the way the mussels had colonized her rail in black ridges, the way her masts had rotted off at the partners so that only stumps remained, soft as candle wax. He knew her better than he'd ever known a living thing, and he had been paid, every month for five years, in cash left in a bait box under the third piling of the fleet wharf, to know her and say nothing.

That was the part that sat in his chest now like swallowed ballast. Not fear. Shame.

* * *

They ran downriver through the last of the ebb, Beatrix steering while Edmund bailed, because her arm was useless and his hands were better suited to work than thought. The narrows opened around them, brown water sliding fast over drowned alder, and twice they passed moorings where boats sat empty, their owners no doubt warned off by the same rumor that had emptied the upper reaches: trouble upriver, fleet business, keep clear. The river had gone quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone important walks in.

At Gull Point Danny's skiff came out from behind the breakwater to meet them, Danny standing at the tiller with his cap pulled low and his jaw working on nothing.

"You're mad," Danny called across the water. "Both of you."

"Probably," Beatrix said.

"The dredge is coming. Vane's people were fueling it at the yard this morning. Two days, maybe less if they push the tide schedule." Danny brought his boat alongside, close enough that Edmund could see the sleeplessness ground into the skin under his eyes. "You can't cut into a strongroom with hand tools and good intentions."

"We're not cutting yet," Edmund said. "We're looking. I need to see the door again. I need to know what we're asking the charge to do."

Danny looked at him for a long moment. "You've been diving her five years," he said quietly. "You never once went near the restricted section. Never. I asked you once, remember? Offhand, years back, and you looked at me like I'd asked to rob a church."

"I was paid to look away."

"I know what you were paid." Danny's voice dropped. "I'm saying it's different, going back on purpose. It changes how the water feels."

Edmund had no argument for that, because Danny was right. Every diver knew a site carried a kind of weather, and the weather of the Grey Wren's forward section had always been the weather of a held breath. For five years he had skirted it the way you skirt a neighbor's grief — politely, thoroughly, without ever letting your eye rest too long.

"Three tides," Beatrix said. "Then the dredge arrives and whatever's in that strongroom gets chewed into gravel and silt and nobody living will ever read it. So yes. We go back on purpose."

Danny rubbed his face with both hands. "I'll run lookout at the point," he said finally, which was not agreement and was not refusal, and Edmund took it for what it was — a man keeping one foot on each bank of a widening river.

* * *

They anchored above the wreck at slack water, in the dead-calm hour when the river stops moving and hangs suspended between its two opinions. From the surface the Grey Wren showed only as a darkening in the green, a shadow with edges, the way a whale shows under a boat. A hundred yards upstream, the navy buoy rode its mooring, orange paint faded to the color of an old wound, its radar reflector turning slowly in no wind. Edmund had spent five years not looking at that buoy. Now he looked at it straight, and it seemed to look back.

"You could still take me back to the harbor," Beatrix said. "No shame in it. I'd find another way."

"No, you wouldn't. You can barely hold a cup."

"Then it's fortunate I have you." She said it lightly, but her eyes stayed serious. "Edmund. Before you go down. Tell me what you're walking into."

So he told her, because she'd earned it and because saying it aloud steadied him the way reciting a chart steadies a pilot in fog.

"She went down in November 1919, stern first, in water that wasn't deep enough to drown her. That's the first wrong thing. A ship like that doesn't sink in nine fathoms unless someone opens her. Second wrong thing: the breach. Forward lower section, port side, below the waterline. It's torn outward, not inward. Pressure blows steel in. Explosives blow it out. I've had my hands on that edge. It curls away from the hull like a flower opening." He paused. "Third wrong thing is the buoy. Navy keeps a marker on a civilian wreck in a civilian channel and pays the harbor to forget why. Fourth wrong thing is me."

"And now?"

"Now I stop being the fourth wrong thing."

He kitted up in silence after that, checking each piece of gear the way a priest checks his vestments — regulator, gauge, knife, slate, the two spare lights Danny had brought taped to his chest strap. The water took him at the gunwale with its usual indifference, cold closing over his head like a door.

* * *

Down, then. The green light thickened around him, and the river's noise fell away into the particular hush of the wreck zone — that acoustic blanket of silt and iron where even your own bubbles sound apologetic. He descended along the shot line he'd placed years ago and never moved, because moving it would mean admitting he intended to return, and he had spent five years refusing to admit anything at all.

The Grey Wren rose out of the murk the way she always did: gradually, then all at once. First the mast stumps, furred white with bacterial bloom. Then the rail, and the great dark slot of the cargo hatch, and the deck beneath it lying in its coat of settled silt, unmarked except by his own old fin prints from previous dives, faint crescents pressed into the grey like fossil birds' tracks. His own history, written on a dead ship, waiting for him.

He swam her length slowly, touching nothing. This was the ritual he'd never named: the approach, the courtesy call, the diver's equivalent of taking off your hat. Her hull rose beside him, plated and riveted, furred with weed, home now to blennies and wandering crabs and the occasional lost eel that poured itself out of a scupper like spilled ink. Somewhere aft, in the engine room he knew by heart, the light fell in shafts through a skylight gone to lace, and he did not go there today. Today he turned forward, toward the section he had never entered, and felt the water change.

It was subtle. Warmer by half a degree, maybe — decay running hotter where the sealed compartments held their rot like a stove holds heat. Quieter. The current didn't comb here; the hull's broken shoulder broke its force, and inside that lee the silt hung suspended, so that his lights carved tunnels through drifting grey instead of finding clean water. And there was the smell — divers know wrecks have smells, oil and rust and old wood — but here, seeping from the breach, was something sweeter and older, the smell of a hold that had been closed for a century with things inside it that had never finished dying.

The breach itself appeared out of the murk like a mouth.

He'd seen it a dozen times from outside, catalogued it, measured it, photographed it once with a camera he'd later buried in the mud rather than explain. But he'd never come at it straight, never hovered before it with his lights full on, and seeing it plainly now he understood that everything Beatrix had said was true and had always been true and he had known it in his hands for five years. The steel curled outward. The fracture radiated in a starburst pattern no storm could write. At the star's center, fused into the plate itself, was a small irregular cavity where the charge had sat — he could see the ghost of its shape, a socket in the metal, the way a bullet leaves its mark in bone.

Someone had knelt on this hull in the dark, a hundred and six years ago, and set a charge against a sleeping ship, and then rowed away, and the ship had gone down with all hands, and the river had kept the secret, and the navy had planted a buoy on the secret, and the fleet had paid a boy in cash to swim past it forever without looking.

His breath rasped in his own ears. He made himself put his gloved hand flat against the curled steel, the way you'd touch a wound to confirm it's real.

It was real.

* * *

The strongroom door was ten feet inside the breach, on the port side of the forward lower passage, behind a collapse that had taken him three dives to map from the outside. He squeezed through the gap between a fallen bulkhead and the overhead, fins knocking loose a fall of silt that bloomed around him and erased the world, and he hung motionless in the cloud he'd made, blind, breathing, counting, until it settled. Visibility returned in layers: first his own hands, then the passage walls, then, at the edge of his lights, the door.

Iron. Round-ported, wheel-operated, the kind fitted to strongrooms and magazine spaces on merchant ships of that era when the owners wanted something kept very safe or very secret. The wheel was seized with corrosion, furred orange, beautiful in the way ruined things are beautiful. Across its face, stamped into a brass plate gone green, letters he had to rub with his thumb to read:

GREY WREN — STRONGROOM NO. 1 — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY

And beneath, smaller, almost worn away:

FLEET & RIVER NAVIGATION CO.

The company name. On the door. Sealed inside a ship the company had sworn for a century was lost to weather.

Edmund hung there in the green silence with his heart going hard against his ribs, and he understood the shape of the whole thing now, top to bottom, the way you understand a knot once you find the end of the rope. They hadn't just sunk her. They'd sealed her. Whatever was in this room — manifest, papers, proof — they had locked it behind their own name and blown the ship around it, believing the river would be the second lock and time the third. No one would ever dive this deep in this cold in this current. No one would ever get paid enough to look.

Except they hadn't counted on a harbor orphan who measured his life in miles of line, who had nothing else to want, and who had been given, month after month, the exact price of his own blindness — until a woman with a broken collarbone washed up in a culvert and made the price too high.

He worked the wheel gently, testing. Nothing. He tried the dogs — the locking pins around the rim — and found them frozen solid, fused into their sockets by a century of rust, exactly as he'd expected and dreaded. No hand tools were opening this. It would take shaped charge, precise placement, a cut clean enough not to crush what lay beyond. It would take the explosives from Beatrix's flooded cache, dried, primed, and placed by someone who understood steel well enough to talk to it.

It would take a miracle with a fuse, and they had three tides to build one.

He copied the door onto his slate — the stamping, the dogs, the hinge side, the plate thickness estimated by eye and confirmed by knuckle-tap — and as he wrote, his light caught something he'd missed. Scratched into the paint beside the wheel, low down, small, deliberate: four short lines crossed by a fifth. A tally mark. One person's count, made from inside or outside, of days or dives or years, the same way Edmund himself had once counted miles instead of time.

Someone had stood here before him, in the dark, and marked that they had been here. Maybe a crewman, sealing the room before the end. Maybe another diver, decades ago, who had also found the door and also failed to open it and also left without telling anyone. The mark was old. The paint had grown over its edges like skin.

He touched it once, lightly, the way you'd touch a stranger's grave, and turned for the surface.

* * *

He broke water into late gold light, the sun already leaning west, and Beatrix's hand was on his arm before he'd fully cleared the gunwale, hauling, steadying, her face sharp with a worry she didn't bother hiding.

"Well?" she said.

"It's real." He pulled his hood back and sat heavily on the thwart, water running off him in ropes. "The breach is a charge. Outward curl, seat cavity, the whole signature. And the strongroom door is right where your team's survey said it would be. Fleet & River Navigation stamped on the plate. Sealed from outside." He looked at her. "They locked their own name inside the evidence, Beatrix. They were so sure the river would keep it, they signed it."

Something moved through her face — not triumph; something rawer, closer to grief arriving late. "One hundred and six years," she said quietly. "My grandfather's grandfather probably helped pay for that charge."

"Then you're the right person to open it."

"The charge won't be me. It'll be the cache from our camp — gelignite in waterproof tubes, if any of it survived the flooding. It'll need drying, priming, and placing by someone who knows hull steel." She looked at him steadily. "By you."

Edmund looked back downriver, toward the wreck's darkening position, marked on the surface by nothing at all. Three tides. The flood would begin within the hour, and after it the ebb, and after that one more cycle, and then the dredges would come up the channel with their cables and their buckets and their lawful orders, and the Grey Wren would become gravel, and the names on her manifest would stay drowned another century.

Behind them, far off at the point, Danny's skiff swung at anchor, a small dark shape pretending to be fishing. Watching. Deciding.

"One tide to prepare," Edmund said. "One tide to place. One tide to fire and recover." He flexed his stiff fingers, feeling the cold still living in them. "And somewhere in there, the fleet is going to notice us."

As if the river had heard him, the navy buoy a hundred yards upstream began, very softly, to ring — its bell tripped by the first push of the flood tide, tolling once, twice, three times across the empty water, like something waking up.
