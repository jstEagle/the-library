## 17. The Navy Buoy

The fog came off the estuary in the last hour before dawn, and Edmund steered into it like a man walking into a room where he knows the furniture by heart.

He had run this water a thousand times. He knew the way the channel bent east of the old oyster beds, knew where the mud gave way to gravel, knew the exact spot — he could have found it blindfolded, and nearly was now, in the grey wool of the mist — where the navy buoy sat on its mooring chain, riding the flood with the patient arrogance of something that had never once been asked to move. *K-7*, stencilled in flaking white paint on its flank. He'd been diving beside it for five years. He'd been paid, in small unmarked envelopes left under the fuel dock's till, to dive beside it and ask nothing.

Beatrix lay wedged in the skiff's stern among the netting and the spare fuel cans, her broken collarbone strapped down with Danny Quill's best canvas and a length of rubber innertube. She was grey around the mouth but her eyes were open, tracking him, and every few minutes she said something — about the current, about the fog, about the angle of the light that wasn't there yet — just to prove her voice still worked.

"You're going to pass it," she said now. "The buoy."

"I'm going to pass it," Edmund agreed.

"Close?"

"Close enough." He kept his eyes on the compass card, which glowed faintly green in its binnacle like a held breath. "That's the point. If I give it a wide berth at this hour, in this fog, it looks like what it is: a man sneaking past. If I go by the way I've gone by it four hundred mornings, it looks like a man going to work."

"And are you?"

Edmund didn't answer that. The engine ticked over at half-throttle, a sound so familiar he barely heard it anymore, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. The fog closed around them until the world was thirty feet wide and made entirely of water and wool.

* * *

They heard the patrol boat before they saw it, because that was how the navy worked: sound first, presence second, permission never. A diesel thudding somewhere out in the grey, steady as a metronome, and then a white cone of light swinging through the fog and finding them the way a hand finds a shoulder in a dark room.

"Skiff. Kill your engine."

Edmund killed it. The silence afterward was enormous. He could hear the water working against the hull, hear Beatrix shift behind him and go very deliberately still, hear his own breath moving in and out of his chest with the slow discipline of a man who had learned, over five years, to make his body boring.

The launch came alongside with the smoothness of long practice. Two sailors, young, bored, cold. And behind them, in the cockpit, a lieutenant with a face like a folded chart — all creases and no expression — and a name tape that read *HARROW*.

"Morning," Edmund said.

"Morning." Lieutenant Harrow looked at him for a long moment. "Underhill. Isn't it."

It was not a question, and that was the first wrong thing. Edmund let two seconds pass, the way you let a knot settle before you trust it. "You know me?"

"I know the name." Harrow's eyes went past him, unhurried, cataloguing the skiff: the fuel cans, the netting, the canvas-wrapped shape in the stern that any fool could see was a person and that Edmund was betting everything no one would look at closely enough to see was an injured woman. "You work the Grey Wren."

"I do salvage survey. Under contract."

"Under contract." Harrow repeated it the way a man tastes wine he suspects has been watered. "Five years, isn't it? Same wreck. Most salvors work a site six months and strip it or quit. You've been down there longer than some of my officers have been in the service." He paused. "Very thorough."

"The river doesn't give up its dead quickly," Edmund said. "You have to be patient."

Something moved behind Harrow's eyes — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of one professional acknowledging another's craft without endorsing it.

"Where are you bound?"

"Up to the Wren. Tide's right. Fog'll lift by seven. I want two hours on the bottom before the visibility goes."

"In this?" One of the young sailors glanced at the fog and away again, quickly, as if the lieutenant might punish him for noticing it.

"Fog's the best time," Edmund said. "Nobody else is on the water. Nobody else is dropping lines near my site. I can work."

"Mm." Harrow stepped to the rail. He was close enough now that Edmund could smell the coffee on him, real coffee, navy-issue, and see that his uniform was dry while everyone else's was soaked with river damp — the man had been below, in the warm, making decisions, and had come up only when the decision required a face. "You'll forgive the questions. There's been a report of unauthorized activity upriver. An expedition. Unlicensed."

"Is that so," Edmund said, and put exactly nothing into it.

"A salvage broker. Calloway, the name was." Harrow watched him the way a man watches a gauge needle. "Her permits were irregular. Her radio went quiet nine days ago. The fleet's asked us to keep an eye out for survivors."

"The fleet asks you for a lot," Edmund said.

For the first time, Harrow smiled. It was a small smile, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes; it lived in the creases of his face the way water lives in the folds of a chart, present, mapped, and meaning nothing good. "Yes," he said. "It does."

* * *

The silence stretched. Out in the fog, the diesel idled. Somewhere far off, a bird started up on the marsh — the flat, complaining cry of a curlew, the sound the river made when it wanted to say *empty, empty, empty*.

Beatrix hadn't moved. Edmund could feel her stillness behind him like a second heartbeat, and he understood suddenly that she was doing what he was doing: making herself boring. Making her breathing shallow and even. Being cargo.

"You've got someone back there," Harrow said.

"My gear," Edmund said. "Wrapped against the spray."

"Your gear." Harton — Harrow — tilted his head. "Your gear breathes, does it?"

Edmund turned and looked at him directly for the first time. It was a calculated risk and he took it slowly, the way you take a breath at depth, metered, controlled. "Lieutenant. Five years I've dived that wreck under your service's nose. Five years I've filed my surveys, paid my fees, kept my mouth shut about whatever it is K-7 marks, and never once given your board a reason to write me a letter. I'm a man going to work. If there's a drowned expedition upriver, that's fleet business, and the fleet and I have no business together. Now — are you going to search my skiff, or are we going to stand here getting wet while the tide I came out on runs out from under both of us?"

The younger sailor shifted. Harrow didn't. He studied Edmund with the patience of a man who had all morning and a thermos, and Edmund understood that this was the actual test — not the questions, not the answers, but who would blink first while the tide drained.

Then Harrow said, quietly, almost conversationally: "Do you know why the buoy is there, Underhill?"

"No."

"No," Harrow agreed. "And in five years you haven't tried to find out."

"It wasn't in my contract."

"No. It wasn't." Harrow looked past him, at the wrapped shape in the stern, and his voice dropped half a register, down to something that wasn't for the sailors' ears. "There was a storm in 1919. The records call it a storm. The charts call it a hazard. My orders call it nothing at all, because my orders don't mention it, which is itself a kind of mention, if you follow me." He straightened. "I followed those orders for eleven years. I intend to follow them for eleven more. Whatever's upriver, whoever's in that canvas — it isn't mine to find. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I think," Edmund said carefully, "you're telling me to be on my way."

"I'm telling you I didn't see anyone." Harrow stepped back from the rail. "The fog's thick. Visibility, what — thirty yards? Forty? A man could hide a whole crew in fog like this." He raised his voice slightly, letting it carry to the sailors, official again. "Log it: commercial skiff, single operator, E. Underhill, licensed salvor, routine transit to contracted site. No anomalies." He looked at Edmund one last time. "Mind the channel. The dredges will be coming up soon — fleet charter, big as churches. They don't stop for anything smaller than they are."

"They won't find much to stop for," Edmund said.

"No," Harrow said. "I imagine they won't." And then, as the launch began to fall away, he added one more thing, pitched low, almost lost under the diesel: "The strongroom's aft of the third bulkhead, Underhill. Port side. In case your survey missed it."

* * *

Edmund didn't move until the light had swung away and the diesel had faded to a rumor and then to nothing, and the fog had folded itself back over the water like a bandage over a wound.

Then Beatrix sat up — slowly, wincing, one-handed — and said, "He knew."

"He knew."

"He told you where the strongroom is." Her voice was rough with fever and disbelief. "A naval officer just handed you the location of a restricted compartment on a wreck his own service has spent a century pretending doesn't exist. Why?"

Edmund restarted the engine. It caught on the second pull, and the sound filled the fog, and he turned the bow toward the place where the buoy must be, though he couldn't see it yet.

"Because he's been following orders for eleven years," Edmund said, "and he wants somebody to break them for him. That's not help. That's a man handing a lit fuse across a fence so his own hands stay clean."

"That's complicity."

"That's the river," Edmund said. "Everybody in it knows something. Nobody says it. They just leave doors unlatched and buoys unlit and let the water carry the rest." He found the buoy then, looming up out of the grey, K-7 riding its chain, and he passed it close, closer than he needed to, close enough to touch, the way he had four hundred mornings, because the habit was the cover and the cover was the truth and both of them were lies now.

Behind him, Beatrix was quiet for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — harder, faster, the broker surfacing through the fever.

"Three tides," she said. "If Vane's dredge comes early, less. We dive at slack water today, and we cut tomorrow, and we're gone before the next flood. And Edmund—" she waited until he glanced back, "—if Harrow told you where the strongroom is, someone above Harrow already knows we're coming. Officers don't disobey alone. They get permission."

Edmund thought about that all the way in, through the thinning fog, past the oyster beds, toward the wreck he had loved for five years precisely because it had never asked him anything. The buoy fell astern, unlit, patient, keeping its secret the way the whole river kept its secrets — by everyone agreeing, silently, forever, not to say them out loud.

But the tide was turning. He could feel it under the hull, the long slow reversal of all that weight, and somewhere upriver, he knew, a dredge the size of a church was taking on fuel.

The water had started talking. All they had to do now was get to the bottom before it changed its mind.
