## 18. Marisol's Choice

The message came in on the morning tide, the way bad news always did at Greyharbor — riding in on the water, uninvited, smelling of diesel and official ink.

Marisol Vane-Ortiz was in the net loft when the harbor runner arrived, a boy of fourteen named Pip Calloway-no-relation who took the fleet's dispatches as a paid errand and read them first as a private vice. He came up the loft ladder two rungs at a time, and she knew before he spoke, because his face had gone the particular grey of a boy carrying something too heavy for his pockets.

"From the fleet office," he said. "Marked *urgent — harbor master's eyes only.* But the harbor master's down at the careenage with a broken winch, and—" He stopped. Swallowed. "And I thought you'd want to know it came."

She took the envelope. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. Fleet stationery, the crest embossed in the corner — the crossed dredge and anchor, the motto her mother used to read aloud in a mocking voice: *Ordo ex Profundo.* Order from the Deep. Her mother had laughed at it for forty years and paid their mooring fees to it for forty years too, and that, Marisol thought, was Greyharbor in a single sentence.

"You didn't read it?" she asked.

Pip looked at his boots. "I read the first line."

"Which was?"

"*Re: the diver Underhill.*" He said the name carefully, the way you'd carry a live coal. Then he was gone down the ladder, and Marisol was alone with the envelope and the sound of the harbor breathing outside — halyards ticking against masts, the long sigh of water under the loft floor, a gull arguing with the world about something that would never change.

She broke the seal.

*To the Harbor Master, Greyharbor. The diver Edmund Underhill is sought in connection with interference in fleet operations on the upper river. Any person with knowledge of his position or intentions is required to report such knowledge to the fleet office within one tide of receipt. Harbor moorings, fuel allocations, and winter lay-up berths remain contingent upon the full cooperation of this office. A reward is offered. Failure to report will be considered—*

She stopped reading there, because her hands had started to shake, and she did not want to read the rest of the sentence while shaking. She read it anyway, later, a hundred times, in memory: *will be considered an act against the fleet.*

Not against the law. Against the fleet. As if the two were the same thing. As if the river itself belonged to them, and every man, woman, and child who earned a living from it were tenants on their own water.

She sat down on a coil of netting and thought about Edmund Underhill.

* * *

She had known him since she was eleven years old, when he came up the harbor stairs with salt in his hair and nothing in his hands and a look on his face like a boy who had been put out with the morning's garbage. Her mother, Odile — the matriarch, the woman whose word in Greyharbor was worth more than any fleet stamp — had looked at him for a long moment, looked at the empty hands, and said only, "There's a pot on the stove and a bunk behind the net loft. You'll muck the bilges till your hands stop shaking."

His hands had shaken for nine days. She had counted. On the tenth day they were steady, and he went out with her father on the eel boat, and he never spoke about whatever he had come from, and the harbor never asked, because that was the bargain Greyharbor made with its broken things: *we will not ask where you came from if you will not pretend you were not broken.*

Five years he had dived the Grey Wren. Five years of quiet miles of rope, of fathoms, of tide charts annotated in his small precise hand. Five years of sitting at their table on Sundays, eating Odile's fish stew, saying maybe forty words all evening and somehow, with those forty words, being more present than men who filled rooms with noise. Marisol had stopped noticing his silences years ago. That was the thing about family — you stopped noticing the shapes of the people in it, the way you stopped noticing the harbor bell, until the night it didn't ring.

And now the fleet wanted her to sell him.

* * *

Her mother came up the loft stairs at noon, slow on the ladder the way she was slow everywhere now, her hands thick-knuckled from fifty years of rope and cold water. She was seventy-one and could still splice a wire cable by feel, and she read the message over Marisol's shoulder without asking, because privacy in Greyharbor was a rumor people had heard of but never met.

"Well," Odile said.

"Well," Marisol said.

Her mother sat down across from her, on the old sea chest that had come over with Marisol's great-grandmother and held, as far as anyone knew, nothing but a dead woman's oilskins and a rosary nobody prayed on. The loft was quiet. Below them, the harbor went about its business — Danny Quill's boat coming in on the young flood, the careenage hammering, children daring each other along the pontoon edges.

"They'll know we got it," Marisol said. "They log every dispatch. If there's no reply from Greyharbor by tomorrow's tide, they'll send someone."

"They always send someone." Odile took off her reading spectacles and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "When your grandfather ran this harbor, they sent a man about the mooring fees. When I took over, they sent a man about the fuel allocations. It's always a man, and it's always about the same thing — which is, do we understand that the water is theirs and we are only permitted to float on it."

"And if we don't reply?"

"Then they'll take the moorings. The fuel. The winter berths." Her mother said it flatly, the way she read a tide table. "Eleven boats winter in this harbor. Eleven families. The Okafo boat, the two Halloran boats, the careenage, the ice house. You know the arithmetic, Marisol. I taught it to you before I taught you to swim."

Marisol did know the arithmetic. She had done it every winter of her life, the same sum the whole harbor did: *what the fleet gives, the fleet can take, and what the fleet takes is everything.* The moorings were not just iron and chain. They were the right to exist on the water. The fuel allocation was not just diesel. It was the difference between a working winter and a starving one. The fleet had spent eighty years building a harbor that could not survive without it, and they called that *order*, and they called that *generosity*, and the truth was it was a chain, and everyone in Greyharlor wore it, and everyone had agreed to pretend it was a necklace.

"He went upriver for her," Marisol said. "For the Calloway woman. For the truth, or whatever it is. And they'll have sent dredges after him, and now they want us to hand him the last mile." She looked at her mother. "He's dived for us five years. He's mucked every bilge, he's pulled three people out of the water that I know of — Tom Halloran's boy, and the Okafo girl, and that tourist nobody liked. He fixed the ice house compressor with a bent spoon and a prayer. He has never once asked this harbor for anything except a bunk and a pot on the stove."

"I know what he's done for this harbor," Odile said. "I fed him, didn't I?"

"Then—"

"Then you know what the fleet will do to us, and you know it better than you're saying." Her mother's eyes were sharp and old and not fooled by anything, least of all her daughter. "So say the rest of it. Say what you're actually asking me."

Marisol took a breath. The loft smelled of tar and hemp and the ghost of a thousand fish, and outside the tide was turning, and somewhere upriver Edmund Underhill was running, and the message in her hands was the last lock on the trap they'd built around him.

"I'm asking what we owe him," she said. "That's all. What does the harbor owe?"

Her mother was quiet for a long time. A gull landed on the loft's open sill, regarded them both with the contempt of its kind, and left.

"When your father drowned," Odile said at last, "it was on a fleet job. You were four. And the fleet office sent a man — not with money, mind, they sent a man with a *form*. Compensation schedule, schedule B, deckhand, no dependents' uplift. And I signed it, because I had you and no boat and no choice, and I have hated that signature for thirty years, not because I signed it, but because they made me grateful for the smallness of what they gave." She folded the spectacles and put them in her apron pocket. "The harbor owes Edmund nothing, Marisol. That's the truth. We fed him; he worked. Fair trade. But the fleet doesn't owe us either — it *takes* from us, every year, a little more, and calls it cooperation. And there comes a tide in every harbor's life when it has to choose which debt it's going to carry. The one to the people who fed it, or the one to the thing that owns its chains."

"And you've chosen?"

"I'm seventy-one years old," her mother said. "I've been choosing my whole life, and I always chose the safe thing, and the safe thing never once made me proud." She stood, and took the message out of Marisol's hands, and looked at it. "You'll burn it, then."

It was not a question, and it was not permission either. It was something rarer — a mother telling her daughter *I see what you already are.*

* * *

They burned it in the loft stove, with the day's net-mending fire already going, because a fire that burns nothing new burns nothing suspicious. Odile fed the envelope in a corner at a time, the fleet crest curling first, the crossed dredge and anchor going black and then to flakes of ash that rose on the stove's draft and vanished up the flue toward a sky that belonged to no one.

"Tomorrow," Odile said, "a fleet man will come, and he will be polite, and he will ask whether the dispatch arrived, and you will say yes, and he will ask what it said, and you will show him—" she paused, feeding in the last corner, "—nothing, because there is nothing to show. And he will look at you the way they look at everyone, like a man pricing a fish. And you will look back at him like a woman who has sold fish to better men than him. And then he will leave, and then we will see what we have bought with our silence."

"And what have we bought?"

Her mother watched the last of the ash go up the flue.

"A war," she said. "A small one. But ours."

* * *

The fleet man came on the afternoon tide, as promised, and he was polite, and his name was Renwick, and he wore a grey coat too good for the harbor and shoes that had never been wet. Marisol met him on the quay because her mother's hands ached on cold days and because — she understood this only as she stepped out of the loft door — because this was hers now. The choice had been made at the stove, but the choice was not real until someone stood on the quay and paid for it.

"Miss Vane-Ortiz." He had her name right, which meant he had done his reading, which meant the fleet's file on Greyharbor was thicker than anyone liked to think. "The harbor master's dispatch. Regarding the diver Underhill."

"The harbor master is repairing a winch," Marisol said. "I handle the paper."

"Of course." He smiled with the bottom half of his face. "The dispatch marked urgent. Yesterday's tide."

"There was no dispatch."

The smile stayed where it was. "Our log shows it delivered."

"Then your log is better than our post," Marisol said. "Pip Calloway-no-relation runs our dispatches, and Pip loses more mail than the sea does. Ask anyone. Ask him." She held the man's eyes, and thought of her mother's voice: *look at him like a woman who has sold fish to better men.* "You know how it is, Mr. Renwick. Paper comes up the harbor, paper goes in the stove, paper comes out as ash. Nothing in Greyharbor survives a winter, including paperwork."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger; anger would have been almost comfortable. It was the look of a man recalculating. She had seen it on buyers' faces when a fisher quoted a price that broke the market.

"I see," he said. "Then I'll say this plainly, since plainly seems to be the harbor's dialect. The fleet values cooperation. The fleet also remembers its friends. And its—" he considered, "—its *less* cooperative acquaintances. Eleven boats winter here, I believe."

"You believe correctly."

"And the ice house. And the careenage. All of it contingent, as you know, on the harbor's good standing." He let that sit in the cold air between them, then produced, from his grey coat, a card. "If the diver contacts the harbor — and he will, they always do — the number is on the card. One tide. That's the window. After that, we stop assuming goodwill."

Marisol took the card. She did not look at it. "I'll pass it to the harbor master," she said, "if he ever finishes that winch."

Renwick looked at her a moment longer, and then at the harbor behind her — at the boats, the loft, the smoke rising straight from the stove flue, carrying, he could not have known, the last molecules of his own dispatch up into the clean grey sky — and he touched the brim of nothing in particular and walked back down the quay to his launch.

Marisol stood until the sound of his engine faded upriver. Then she opened her hand and looked at the card. The fleet office number, embossed. The crossed dredge and anchor.

She did not burn this one. She put it in her pocket, because her mother had taught her one thing above all others on the water: *never throw away a tool, even the enemy's.* A phone number was a line, and a line could be fed. A line could be fed lies, on a schedule, in a voice that trembled just enough to be believed.

She was still standing there, the tide lapping at the harbor steps, when Pip came running again — down the pontoon this time, from the direction of the radio shack, his face no longer grey but lit, lit like a lamp.

"Marisol." He was breathless, grabbing the rail. "The set. The harbor set. It's Edmund — he's coming downriver, he's got the Calloway woman with her, with her collarbone broke, and he's asking—" Pip gulped air. "He's asking if the harbor will stand behind him. His words. *Will the harbor stand behind me.*"

Marisol looked upriver, where the water ran grey and heavy toward the sea, carrying on it somewhere a borrowed skiff, a broken woman, a waterproof case, and five tides' worth of running. She put her hand in her pocket and closed it around the fleet's card until the embossed crest bit her palm.

"Pip," she said. "Get every boat owner on the pontoon within the hour. And get me the radio shack."

She was already moving toward the stairs. "Tell him yes."
