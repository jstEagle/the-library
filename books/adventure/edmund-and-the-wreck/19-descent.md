## 19. Descent

The Grey Wren had never let me see her whole.

Five years of diving her and I knew her the way you know a lover in the dark — by touch, by the particular give of her plating under a gloved hand, by the way the current sang through her railings at half flood. I knew the stern where the propeller blades hung like folded wings. I knew the cargo hold with its silted floor and its ribs of rusted steel arching overhead like the bones of some enormous fish that had swallowed the ship whole. I knew the wheelhouse, or what the river had left of it, the brass fittings gone green as pond weed, the telegraph lever still frozen on *Full Ahead*.

But I had never gone past the bulkhead at frame forty-two. Nobody did. The navy buoy moored forty yards upstream said so in the language of buoys, which is to say it said nothing at all and expected everyone to understand. I'd been paid not to understand for five years, and I had kept my side of that bargain the way you keep any bargain that lets you sleep at night: without looking at it too closely.

Tonight I was going to look.

* * *

We anchored above her at slack water, which is the only hour the river allows anyone anything. Slack water on the Grey Wren's stretch was a lie the tide told twice a day — the current didn't stop so much as hold its breath, gathering itself for the next pull, and you had maybe twenty minutes of that held breath to work in before the water remembered what it wanted.

Beatrix sat on the skiff's thwart with her arm strapped across her chest, the broken collarbone knitted wrong and knitting anyway, and watched me dress in. She'd insisted on coming out. Danny had argued against it — *you can barely lift a kettle, Bea* — and she'd looked at him with that flat grey patience of hers until he stopped talking.

"I need to see it," she said, when I asked if she was sure. "I've read about it for two years. Charts, manifests, the insurance filings. I've built the whole wreck in my head out of paper." Her eyes went to the dark water sliding past the hull, brown-green, opaque as tea. "I need to know how wrong I was."

"You'll be wrong," I said, checking my gauge. "You always are, first time. The river doesn't match anybody's paperwork."

"Then show me how."

Danny held the boat on the anchor while I ran my checks. He'd come back the night before with the cutting gear lashed under tarpaulin and a face like a man who'd swallowed something that hadn't agreed with him, and he hadn't said one word about why, and none of us had asked, because asking would have made it a thing that could be taken back. He worked the winch now with quick competent hands and wouldn't meet my eye.

"Visibility will be nothing," I told Beatrix. "This time of year, this far down, you get maybe an arm's length on a good day. Today isn't a good day. The upriver rain has the water carrying topsoil."

"So you'll be diving blind."

"Diving by touch. It's different." I pulled the hood over my head. "Blind is when there's nothing to find. Touch is when everything tells you where it is, if you listen with your hands."

"And the restricted section?" She kept her voice level but I heard the wire underneath it. "The part you've been paid to ignore for five years. What do your hands know about that?"

"Nothing." I spat into my mask and rinsed it, a ritual older than me, older than diving probably. "That's the point."

* * *

The river took me the way it always took me, all at once, cold closing over my head like a door.

At three fathoms the light gave out. Not faded — gave out, the way a candle gives out, all at once, and then there was only the brown murk and the beam of my lamp pushing a cone of yellow through it that showed me nothing except the silt hanging in the water like weather. I checked my line, clipped onto the descent rope, and went down hand over hand into the dark, counting fathoms the way other men count years.

Eight fathoms to the deck. I knew it in my body before my depth gauge confirmed it — the pressure settling on my shoulders like a passenger, the cold changing key around twelve feet down where the thermocline lived, a band of water colder than the water above and below it, a river inside the river. I passed through it and shivered inside the suit and kept going.

My glove touched steel.

There she was. The Grey Wren's deck came up under my palm, crusted with a century of river life — mussels in ridged black clusters, algae slick as wet leather, the ghostly softness of rotting wood where the decking had been. My lamp showed me a wall of brown nothing and then, suddenly, the curve of a bollard six inches from my face, furred green, and beyond it the long slope of the deck falling away toward the holds.

I'd missed her. That was the thing nobody understood who hadn't done it — you don't find a wreck, you arrive on her, all at once, like waking up in a strange bed. One moment the river is the whole world and the next moment the world has a floor and the floor is a ship.

I moved aft along the rail, following my guideline, toward frame forty-two.

* * *

The bulkhead at frame forty-two was a doorway once. Now it was a wall of collapsed plating, twisted inward by whatever had happened to her — and I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only thing I have left to offer this story: in five years I had never once tried that opening. Not out of obedience. Obedience I could have managed to break; I'd broken plenty of smaller rules. It was out of something older than obedience, something the body does when it walks past a house where somebody died.

The fleet agent in Chapter Three of my life — no, that's not right, that was last week, time has started behaving strangely since Beatrix came back into the world — the fleet agent had offered me triple pay to guide dredges upriver, and I'd refused, and refusing had cost me whatever comfortable fiction I'd been living in. But even after refusing, even after deciding, I had stayed clear of the forward section. Habit is a kind of loyalty too, and mine had been loyal to the wrong people for five years.

I squeezed through the breach.

The plating caught my tank valve and I froze, heart hammering, working myself free inch by inch, the way you free a hook from a fish's mouth — gently, gently, because panic underwater kills faster than drowning does. Then I was through, and my lamp opened up a space I had never seen, and I stopped moving entirely.

The forward section was intact.

Not damaged-intact. *Intact*-intact. The corridor ran ahead of me, straight and true, doors standing open on either side, and the silt lay over everything like fallen snow, undisturbed, smooth, holding the shapes of things beneath it — a boot here, the curve of a chair back there, a shape near the wall that I made myself not look at too closely because I knew what it was and I wasn't ready. A century of river had poured through every other part of this ship and torn her apart, and here it had laid everything down soft as a blanket.

And the breach itself — I turned back to look at it, really look, the way Beatrix had taught me to look at documents, asking not *what is this* but *what does this say*.

It didn't say storm.

I've read storm damage my whole life. I grew up on a harbor where the winter gales wrote their signatures on every hull that survived them, and storm damage has a grammar. Storms tear outward. They peel plating away from the structure, they open seams along the lines of least resistance, they leave ragged edges bent in the direction the water was running. This breach was punched *inward*, clean-edged at the corners, and the plating around it carried stress fractures radiating out in a star pattern — the signature of an explosion contained just long enough to focus itself, or of something very heavy driven through from outside with the ship already settled and helpless.

No storm record matched this. Beatrix had shown me the records herself, spread across the skiff's thwart: the gale of November 1919, the official findings, the surveyor's report with its tidy conclusion — *vessel lost to weather, hull failure consistent with sustained heavy seas*. Tidy. Tidy as a locked drawer.

Somebody had written that report without ever getting wet.

* * *

I went deeper into the ship, and the ship closed behind me.

This is the part I'll try to tell straight, though telling it straight makes it sound simpler than it was. Diving inside a wreck is claustrophobia with a clock attached. Every breath off the tank is a breath you don't get back. Every turn of the corridor is a turn you'll have to retrace, and the silt hangs in the water waiting for you to stir it, and when you stir it the visibility goes from bad to nothing, and then you are a blind man inside a steel box eighty feet under a river that wants you dead. Good divers die in wrecks all the time. Not from the water. From the small decisions, each reasonable, each a foot closer to the trap.

I ran my line properly. Clipped it at the breach, paid it out as I went, kept my lamp low so the beam cut under the silt-haze instead of bouncing back into my eyes. The corridor doors passed on either side — cabins, probably, third class by the size, four berths to a room, the berths still bolted to the walls with their frames furred white with bacterial mats that glowed faintly in my lamplight like frost.

And everywhere the silt, holding its shapes. Holding them the way snow holds the shapes of things that walked through it and never walked back.

I found the galley by smell — absurd, impossible, but the river had gotten into the old coal and the old grease and something of them still rose when my fins stirred the layer, a ghost-smell of engine rooms and boiled coffee that put a lump in my throat I had to swallow past. I found a child's shoe. I left it where it lay. Some things you don't collect. Some things are not salvage.

Then the corridor ended, and there was the door.

* * *

It was steel, and it was shut.

Every other door on this ship stood open — the river had seen to that, or the crew had, fleeing aft toward the stern and the life that wasn't there. But this door was shut, and it was not a cabin door. It was heavy, riveted at the edges, with a wheel-lock in the center like a submarine's, and above it, stamped into the steel plate in letters the rust had only gentled, not erased:

STRONGROOM — AUTHORIZED ACCESS ONLY

AUTHORIZED BY WHOM? I thought. And I put my gloved hand flat against the steel, and the steel was cold in a way the rest of the wreck wasn't, cold like the idea of cold, and I felt — I swear I felt, though I know it was only my own pulse in my fingertips — something answer from the other side.

The manifest was behind this door. Whatever Beatrix's people had found proof of, whatever the fleet had drowned a ship and a crew and five years of my silence to bury, it was behind eighteen inches of riveted steel at the bottom of the Grey Wren, and the company that owned the river had never sent divers to retrieve it. Think about that. Think about what it means. They sank her with the evidence sealed inside, and then for a hundred years they guarded the wreck — buoyed her, patrolled her, bought the silence of every diver who worked her — and never once opened the strongroom.

Because opening it meant admitting it existed. Because the door was the confession. As long as it stayed shut, the report stayed tidy, the sea stayed guilty, and the founders' heirs stayed respectable. The truth didn't need guarding. It needed only to be *not touched*, and they had hired men like me to not touch it, and I had taken the money, and called it work.

My air gauge read a third. The silt I'd stirred on the way in was settling now, the corridor behind me fading back into brown sleep. I should have turned. Every rule I had, every habit that had kept me alive through five years and four hundred dives, said *turn now, go up slow, come back tomorrow with cutting gear and a plan.*

Instead I put my shoulder against the wheel-lock and pushed.

It didn't move. Of course it didn't move. A century of river had grown its own welds through the mechanism, corrosion sealing steel to steel, the lock fused into a single mass harder than the hull around it. I pushed until my vision spotted at the edges, until the regulator rasped with my breathing, and the wheel gave exactly nothing, and somewhere in the dark behind me my guideline shifted — a small sound, a whisper of rope on rusted metal — and I went cold all over in a way the water hadn't managed yet.

I turned. The line was fouled. Somewhere back along the corridor, in the dark I'd stirred and lost, my lifeline to the surface had snagged on something, and the slack was gone out of it, and the line led off at an angle I didn't recognize into a blackness my lamp couldn't finish.

Twenty minutes of bottom time. A third of a tank. A fouled line. And behind the steel in front of me, close enough to touch, the reason for all of it — the manifest, the letters, the confession, the names.

I put my hand on the wheel-lock one more time. Not to turn it. Just to feel it. To know it was real, that I hadn't dreamed it, that the door was there and the door was shut and the door could be opened by a man with the right tools and enough air.

Then I turned back to the dark, and started untangling my way home.
