## 20. Two Tides

The tide table was a small cruelty. Edmund had known it since he was a boy — the way the river kept its own arithmetic, indifferent to what you needed from it. He sat in the skiff with the chart spread across his knees and Beatrix propped against the gunwale beside him, her arm strapped flat with a strip of Danny's spare canvas, and he did the numbers twice because he wanted them to come out different the second time.

They didn't.

"Say it," Beatrix said. She had a way of asking for the worst of a thing as though it were a favor. Fever still lived in her, banked but not out; her voice had a rasp in it like a rope drawn over stone.

"Two tides," Edmund said. "Maybe two and a half if the wind backs around and holds the flood a little past the turn. After that, the channel over the wreck silts out. You can't dive it, you can't even anchor over it. The dredges come on the third tide after that." He looked up. The river lay grey and flat under a sky the color of wet slate, and somewhere upriver, beyond the bend, the fleet's machines were already moving. "So we have two tides to cut into a strongroom door that's rated to keep out everything but God."

"And cutting gear?"

"A hacksaw and a cold chisel."

Beatrix laughed — a short, cracked sound, more cough than laugh. "The great salvage industry of this river."

"The great salvage industry of this river," Edmund said, "has spent eighty years making sure nobody ever needs to cut anything open. That's the point of it. You keep the water quiet, and quiet water doesn't need tools."

The wreck lay beneath them as he said it. He could feel it, the way you feel a tooth that's been aching for years — the Grey Wren sitting in her grave of silt forty feet down, her decks collapsed into a soft grey dark, her corridors hung with the curtains of old nets and the slow white bloom of freshwater mussels. Five years he had worked her, measuring his life in the length of his lines. He knew her the way you know a house you grew up in, and he had never once been through the door that mattered.

The strongroom sat under the aft deckhouse, behind a steel plate door with a lock the size of a dinner plate. He had found it in his second year, run his glove along the seam of it, felt the cold of it through three fingers of neoprene, and turned away, because turning away was what he was paid for. Now he lay awake at night seeing that seam. A hairline of darker dark. A door in a house he'd lived in for five years and never opened.

"Show me again," Beatrix said.

He turned the chart toward her. He'd drawn the wreck from memory — not the salvage-company plan, which showed only what the fleet wanted known, but his own, the one that lived in his hands. The hull, the breach, the corridor, the deckhouse, the door.

"Here. Aft deckhouse, port side. The deck above her is gone — collapsed in the second winter, I think. That's our way in, if the silt hasn't drifted back over it. It's a drop of maybe eight feet down inside, into the passageway. Then the door."

"Steel?"

"Armored. This was a company ship; they carried payrolls and papers worth killing for. The door was built to survive the ship sinking." He paused. "And it did."

Beatrix studied the drawing with her good hand, tracing the corridor with one finger, and he watched her do it and understood something about her he hadn't understood in the culvert, or on the mudflats, or in the long cold hours since. She wasn't afraid of the wreck. She looked at his drawing the way he looked at tide tables — as arithmetic to be beaten.

"Explosives," she said.

Edmund looked at her. "What?"

"Her crew cached explosives. For the survey work — stump charges, seismic charges, small stuff, for sounding the riverbed up the shallows where the sonar couldn't read." She was already moving, awkward with the strapped arm, digging one-handed into the duffel they'd recovered from the drowned camp. "When the fleet came for us, we scattered the gear. I told them to. I thought — if we couldn't take the evidence out, at least they wouldn't get our instruments, our records. I had them sink the crates in the reeds off the east bank, weighted, marked." She came up with a grease pencil and a scrap of dive slate, and began to write, her letters slanting with the fever. "Forty feet of water, no. Eight feet. Shallow enough to free-dive. Shallow enough to find."

"You want to blow the door," Edmund said.

"I want to *cut* the door. Blowing it would be stupid — the charge would go through the door and through whatever's behind it. Paper, Edmund. Nineteen nineteen paper, sitting in river water for eighty years. It'll be pulp if we're lucky and soup if we're not. We need a cutting charge. Linear. Shaped. Something that peels the seam open instead of punching through it." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and too hot, fever-bright, but the mind behind them was running clean. "Your brother divers — the harbor men — did they ever use cutting charges?"

"Once. A snagged barge on the bar, years back. Danny Quill set it." He stopped. Danny's name sat in his mouth like a stone.

"Danny," Beatrix said, and read his face the way she read charts. "He let us go."

"He let us go and he took photographs of us doing it. Insurance. If the fleet squeezes him, he's covered." Edmund turned back to the chart. "He won't come back. He can't. His daughter—"

"His daughter needs the fleet's clinic. I know. I did my homework on this river before I ever launched an expedition on it." She said it without apology. "So not Danny. Who else can set a charge underwater?"

Edmund thought about the harbor, its crooked lanes and its small loyalties, everyone owed and owing, everyone watching. "Nobody I'd trust with my life and yours in the same breath."

"Then we do it ourselves." She said it flatly, the way you'd say *then we eat* or *then we sleep*. "I've set seismic charges. Not underwater, not on a wreck, but the physics don't change. You want a shaped charge — a ribbon of explosive against the door seam, tamped with something dense so the force goes into the steel and not into the water. Water's the problem. Water eats shock waves, steals them. So we pack the seam, we tape the line, we fire it from the surface on a long cord, and we pray the hull takes it."

"The hull is eighty years of rust holding hands," Edmund said. "You set off a charge inside her and the whole deckhouse could come down. On top of the door. On top of the corridor. On top of me, if I'm in there when it goes."

"You won't be in there when it goes."

"Someone has to place it."

Silence, then. The river made its small sounds against the hull — the lap and suck of water, a bird crying somewhere up the marsh. Beatrix looked at him for a long moment, and he saw her doing the arithmetic he'd just done, and refusing to arrive at his answer.

"Two tides," she said finally. "One to place and fire. One to go in and get it out. And the second tide is the one that's dying — we'd be working the tail of the flood into the start of the ebb, in the dark, with the current building." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her good hand. "And we have maybe three tanks of air between us, and one of them is yours and half-empty."

"Two and a half," Edmund said. "The flood runs long when the wind's out of the southwest. It's out of the southwest."

"You've been counting tides your whole life, haven't you."

"It's what I have instead of years."

She looked at him then — really looked, the fever receding behind her eyes for a moment like water off a flat — and something passed between them that had no words in it. Two people who measured time in water.

* * *

They found the cache an hour before slack, at the edge of the reeds off the east bank, where the water went from brown to green-brown and the light came down in slanted bars. Edmund free-dived it, no tank, no weights but the stones in his pockets, following Beatrix's directions shouted down through two feet of river.

"Left! Left of the dead alder — there's a fence post, the old wire fence, follow the wire down—"

The wire was still there, furred with algae, running down into the murk like a line someone had drawn and then drowned. He followed it. Eight feet, then nine, the pressure closing on his ears, the cold reaching through the wetsuit's seams and finding the warm skin beneath. And there, in a hollow the current had scoured, three crates lashed to a length of chain and a mooring block, the wood gone soft and grey, the stenciling still legible under a skin of silt: **CALLOWAY SURVEY — SEISMIC — HANDLE WITH CARE**.

He worked the lashings loose. The knots had swollen; he used his knife. The first crate came up light — instruments, ruined or not, he didn't check. The second was heavier, and when he broke the lid on the surface, gasping, Beatrix was there with her good arm to steady it, and they looked down together at the sticks of explosive nested in waxed paper, and the coil of det cord, and the small brass firing assembly, and the rolls of waterproof tape.

"Seismic charges," Beatrix said. "Forty-gram sticks. Pentolite." She lifted one out and turned it in the light, and her hands were steady, which surprised them both. "Small. Too small, one at a time. But taped end to end along the seam, tamped—" She stopped. Looked at the wreck's direction, invisible from here, forty feet down under grey water. "Tamped with what?"

Edmund was already looking at the first crate. At the instruments. At the brass fittings, the lead-acid battery cells, the dense grey plates inside.

"Lead," he said. "Battery plates. We melt them down or we just break them up and pack them in bags. Clay would be better. There's clay on the cut bank, a mile downstream — the harbor kids dig it for fishing weights."

"Clay and lead and tape and prayer," Beatrix said. "That's our cutting charge."

"That's the whole expedition," Edmund said, and she laughed again, that cracked sound, and this time it went on a little longer.

They worked through the afternoon in the skiff, anchored in the lee of the east bank with the reeds hiding them from the channel. Beatrix directed from the stern, one-armed, her voice gaining strength as the fever broke and ran out of her in sweat; Edmund built. He broke the batteries open and stripped the plates, grey and heavy, and cut them into strips with the cold chisel. He dug clay from the bank with his bare hands and kneaded it with river water until it was a dense grey dough. He taped seismic sticks end to end into a ribbon four feet long, then pressed the clay-lead putty along its back in a ridge as thick as his thumb, and wrapped the whole thing in tape until it was a rigid, ugly, heavy serpent of a thing that no ordnance man in the fleet would have looked at twice except to condemn it.

It was, by every measure he knew, a terrible cutting charge.

It was also the only one on the river.

"Placement is everything," Beatrix said, watching him work. "The seam of the door — you said you've run your hand along it. You know it by feel?"

"I know it like my own jawline."

"Then the charge goes against the seam, clay side out, the whole length. You brace it — wedges, anything, the clay will stick a little if you press it in wet. Then det cord back to the firing assembly, and the assembly up your line to the surface. You fire it from the boat. Not from in the water, not from the wreck. From the boat, with a hundred feet of cord between you and it." Her voice had gone quiet and precise, the voice of a woman who had lost a crew and would not lose another. "You go down, you place it, you come back up. You do not linger. You do not check your work twice. The wreck has waited eighty years; it can wait the ninety seconds it takes you to climb a line."

"And if the charge doesn't cut?"

"Then we've spent one tide and a ribbon of explosives, and we do it again with what's left, better." She looked at the remaining sticks in the crate. "There's enough for two attempts. Not three."

"And if the charge cuts and the deckhouse comes down?"

She didn't answer that one, and he didn't ask her to. Some arithmetic you did alone, in the dark, with your eyes closed, and then you opened your eyes and went to work anyway.

* * *

The first of the two tides began to turn near dusk.

They came downriver in the last light, running the skiff quiet along the eastern shallows where the fleet's patrol launches didn't bother to look, and anchored over the Grey Wren as the sky went from slate to ash to nothing. No lights. Edmund had wrapped the skiff's lantern in canvas so that only a coin of glow fell on his hands as he checked his gear, and Beatrix sat across from him in the dark like a figure carved out of the night, her strapped arm held against her chest, watching him with eyes that had adjusted until they caught even that coin of light.

"Say the sequence back to me," she said.

"Descend the anchor line. Cross to the deckhouse. Drop through the collapsed deck, eight feet, into the corridor. Door on the port side. Feel the seam. Place the charge, clay out, full length of the seam. Brace it with the wedges. Run the det cord back along the corridor, up through the deck gap, tie off to the anchor line. Ascend. Ninety seconds of bottom time, no more. Then we clear the anchor, back off a hundred feet, and fire from here." He buckled the weight belt. The ugly clay-backed serpent of the charge lay coiled in a canvas bag like something drowned and resurrected. "Then we wait for the water to settle, and if she's still standing, I go back down and see what eighty years kept."

"And if the deckhouse comes down on the door—"

"Then the fleet's dredges can dig her up with everything else, and the truth stays buried another eighty years, and we'll have lost nothing but a ribbon of explosives and my one good chance at a long life." He said it lightly, and saw that she hated him for saying it lightly, and understood that she hated it because she had already lost a crew to exactly this kind of arithmetic and had sworn, in the flooded dark of a culvert, never to spend another person's life as though it were line off a spool.

"Edmund." Her voice stopped him with his mask half on. "I'm not going to tell you to be careful. You're a salvage diver; careful is your whole religion. I'm going to tell you something else." She leaned forward, and the coin of lantern light found the fever-flush on her face and the absolute steadiness of her eyes. "What's behind that door isn't paper. I've read the fragments my team pulled from the purser's cabin before the fleet came. It's nineteen men who drowned believing their own company had abandoned them, and it's the proof that they were right, and it's the name of every man who decided they were cheaper dead than alive. That's what you're diving for. Not a manifest. A *verdict*."

Edmund looked at her. The river breathed against the hull. Somewhere upriver, faint as a heartbeat, he could almost believe he heard the dredges.

"Ninety seconds," he said, and rolled backward off the gunwale into the black water.

The cold took him the way it always did, a full-body closing, a hand squeezing the breath out of him and then, as the suit sealed and the body's fire caught, giving it back. He pulled himself down the anchor line hand over hand into the dark, and the dark was total — no moon reaching this deep, no light but the small glow of his wrist compass and the phosphorescent ghosts the water made when he moved, little cold sparks of blue dying around his gloves.

The Grey Wren rose to meet him.

He felt her before he saw her, the way he always did — a change in the water, a stillness, a presence like a hand laid flat on his back. Then the rail came out of the blackness, furred white with mussels, and he crossed it the way he'd crossed it a thousand times, and the wreck opened around him like a throat.

He dropped through the gap in the deck. Eight feet. The corridor received him, its walls hung with net and silt-shadow, and his light — a shuttered lamp, a coin of beam — found the door.

Steel. Armored. Eighty years of river had given it a skin of rust and silt, but the seam was there, the hairline of darker dark he had run his glove along a hundred nights and always turned away from. He pressed his palm to it one last time. Cold as a grave lid. It was.

Then he worked. The charge came out of its bag stiff and heavy, and he pressed it wet against the seam and felt the clay take, just barely, just enough, and he drove the wedges in above and below with the flat of the chisel, and his air hissed, and the compass on his wrist said what it said, and he made himself not count the seconds because counting was how divers died, rushing, rushing. He ran the det cord back along the corridor the way Beatrix had made him rehearse on the deck of the skiff, over the collapsed deck, up to the anchor line, tied off with a knot he could tie blind and had, in fact, just done blind.

He climbed.

The surface took him like a birth, gasping, and Beatrix's hands — one hand — hauled him over the gunwale, and for a moment they crouched together in the dark of the skiff, two people breathing hard over a coil of detonation cord that ran down into the river and into the dark and into the hands of the dead.

"Clear the anchor," Edmund said. "Back off."

They pulled. The skiff slid back across the black water, fifty feet, a hundred, the reeds rising around them, and Beatrix took the firing assembly in her good hand and looked at him across it, and asked him nothing, and he nodded once.

The charge went with a sound he felt more than heard — a deep *whump* that came up through the hull of the skiff and through the bones of his spine, and the river over the wreck bulged and shivered, and a single bubble of displaced air broke the surface and was gone.

Then the water lay flat again, and the night lay flat again, and somewhere forty feet down, behind eighty years of rust and silence, a door stood either open or not.

Beatrix exhaled, a long shaky breath. "Two tides," she whispered. "One left."

Edmund was already pulling his mask back on.

"Then we'd better not waste it," he said, and went down to see what the dead had kept for him — and whether the deckhouse had come down on top of it.
