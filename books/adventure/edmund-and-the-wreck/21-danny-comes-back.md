## 21. Danny Comes Back

The photograph wouldn't stay in the drawer.

Danny Quill had put it there three times since the tide turned — under the spare fuses, beneath the tin of hooks, wedged behind the ledger where he kept what people owed him and what he owed them, which was a longer list than he liked to admit. Three times he'd slid it into the dark and shut the drawer on it, and three times he'd found himself back at the desk with his hand on the handle as if the drawer were calling him by name.

In the photograph, Edmund Underhill stood on the deck of the *Maggie Bright* with a wrench in one hand and a look on his face that Danny hadn't seen in six years: the look of a man who trusted the person holding the camera. They'd been younger then. The river had been kinder, or they'd been too stupid to know otherwise. Behind them both, out of focus, the Grey Wren's buoy bobbed like a blind eye.

Danny had taken four more photographs yesterday, down at the mudflats, with the long lens he used for survey work. Edmund crouched over a woman wrapped in oilskins. A skiff beached on the flats like something dead. The Calloway woman, alive, when half the river said she was fish food and the other half knew better than to say anything at all.

Four photographs. Insurance, he'd told himself. That was all. You didn't hand a man to the fleet; you just made sure that if the fleet came asking, you could buy your daughter's spine with somebody else's trouble.

He looked at the drawer again. Then at the corner of the room where Nell's brace hung from its hook, the new one, the one the clinic man had measured her for with hands that smelled of carbolic and money. *The fleet takes care of its own,* the man had said, smiling at Nell like she was a puppy at a fair. *And family of men who help the fleet.* He'd left a card. The card was still on the table, face up, because Danny couldn't bring himself to turn it over and couldn't bring himself to throw it in the stove either.

Nell was asleep in the back room. He could hear her breathing through the wall, that faint whistle the doctors called restriction and Danny called the sound of everything he didn't have. Her mother had died owing the clinic eleven pounds. Eleven pounds, and they'd carried it forward against the girl like a debt of honor, like a blood price, and now here was a way to clear it, laid out neat as a chart, and all it cost was a photograph of two people freezing on a mudflat while a launch hunted them with a light.

Danny put his head in his hands. Outside, the ebb was running hard past the pilings, and the water talked the way it always did, in that low gossiping voice that never told you anything you wanted to hear.

*You let them go,* he reminded himself. *That counts.*

It counted for nothing and he knew it. Letting them go wasn't the same as helping them. There was a whole ocean between watching a man drown and pushing him under, but there was also a whole ocean between watching him drown and throwing him a line, and Danny Quill had spent his life floating exactly in the middle of it, telling himself the middle was safe water.

Except his brother had drowned in the middle too. And nobody had thrown anybody anything.

He stood up so fast the chair went over behind him. In the back room Nell stirred, murmured, went quiet again. Danny took the lantern down off its nail, and then, after a moment, took the canvas tool roll off the shelf above it — the heavy one, the one he hadn't opened since the last contract job, three years gone. He unrolled it on the table beside the fleet's card.

Cutting gear. Oxy-hydrogen torch, the little brass regulator, the spare tips wrapped in waxed cloth, the striking lighter with its flint worn to a nub. His father's gear before it was his. His father had cut ships open for a living and never once asked what was inside them, and Danny had followed him into that trade the way you follow a man into church, without ever deciding to.

He rolled it up again, slow, and slung it over his shoulder. Then he turned the fleet's card face down, which was not the same as burning it, and went out into the dark.

* * *

The river at slack-before-flood was a held breath. Danny ran his old launch up the channel with the lamp doused, steering by the shapes of things he'd known since he was small enough to fall in — the leaning alder, the stumps of the old ferry pier, the mudbank that shifted every winter and never shifted quite enough to fool him. Fog sat on the water in rags. Somewhere upstream a bird cried once and thought better of it.

He'd told himself he was going to deliver the gear and leave. That was the story he'd built on the run upriver, the story he could live inside: *I'm not taking sides. I'm returning borrowed property. Edmund can cut his door or drown trying, and I'll be home before the flood makes, and none of it will have my name on it.*

But stories are like boats; they carry only what you load them with, and Danny knew what he'd loaded. He'd loaded the oxy bottle and the regulator and the spare tips, and underneath all of it, wrapped in waxed cloth where no one could see, he'd loaded 1919.

Because that was the thing he couldn't say yet. Not to Edmund, not to the Calloway woman, not to God or the river or the drawer full of photographs. There was a name on the Grey Wren's lost crew list that Danny Quill had known since before he could read it — a name his mother had said once a year, every year, standing at the window on the anniversary with her back straight and her voice flat, saying it the way you say a prayer you've stopped believing will be answered. A name the fleet's compensation board had paid out on at fourteen shillings a week for a widow's lifetime, and then stopped paying early, citing clerical error, citing the fire that took the records, citing whatever needed citing.

His mother had died still waiting for the truth of it. Danny had inherited the waiting along with the tools.

So when the fleet man had come to his slip yesterday morning — soft shoes, soft voice, a coat too good for the weather — and named a figure that would clear Nell's spine and Nell's spine's grandchildren, Danny had heard the figure and nodded the way you nod at weather. And underneath the nod, some part of him that had been underwater for thirty years turned over and began, slowly, to swim toward the light.

He found the skiff tucked in the reeds below the old drainage cut, exactly where the mudflat photographs said it would be, and he brought his launch alongside with barely a kiss of timber, the way you come alongside a boat you respect. No one hailed him. For a long moment he just stood at his own rail with the fog beading on his beard, listening.

There. Under the bank, in the black seam between water and land: a thread of lamplight, hooded, the kind of light you show only when you've decided to trust whoever's looking for it.

"Edmund," he said quietly. "It's Danny. Alone."

The silence went on long enough that he started composing apologies, and then the reeds parted and Edmund was there in the bow of the skiff, a boathook held low and ready, and behind him, propped against the thwart with her arm bound across her chest, a woman with fever-bright eyes who watched Danny the way a hawk watches a field mouse that has approached voluntarily.

"You're a long way from your mooring," Edmund said. His voice was rough as rust. He hadn't slept. He'd been sleeping in watches, if at all, for days, and it showed in the gray of him.

"I'm a long way from a lot of things." Danny held up the tool roll so the lantern glow caught the brass fittings. "Brought the torch. Regulator's sound. Two spare tips and a fresh flint."

Edmund didn't move. That was the worst of it — not anger, not relief, just that stillness, a man weighing cargo. "Why?"

"Because you'll need it to cut that strongroom, and you haven't got one."

"That's what. I asked why."

The woman shifted against the thwart. "Let the man answer, Mr. Underhill," she said, and her voice was hoarse but level, and Danny understood in one cold drop why half the river feared her. "I'd like to hear it myself."

Danny looked at Edmund. Six years of not-looking ended right there, in the fog, with the flood tide beginning to whisper against the hulls. "I can't tell you yet," he said. "I know how that sounds. I know what I did — took the pictures, took the meeting, let you think I'd sold you. I'm not asking you to forgive it tonight. I'm asking you to use my torch and hate me after, if it comes to that. But I can't say why. Not yet. When it's done — if we're all still above water when it's done — I'll tell you the whole of it, and you can do what you like with me."

"Fever talking?" Edmund said to the woman.

"No," Beatrix Calloway said. She studied Danny with those bright, burning eyes, and Danny felt catalogued, weighed, entered into a ledger he suspected had no forgiving column. "Fever doesn't lie this carefully. Mr. Quill. You understand that if this is a trap, I'll make sure the fleet knows you led them to us? Dead men's testimony carries weight in an inquiry. It's about the only weight it carries."

"It's not a trap."

"People say that."

"People say everything." He set the tool roll down on the skiff's thwart, deliberate, letting go of it in a way he hoped meant something. "The regulator needs bleeding before you strike it. Don't crack the valve fast or you'll freeze the seat. Tips are marked — the number two is the one for plate that thick." He heard his own voice going into the old rhythm, the diving-partner rhythm, briefing before a job, and it hurt somewhere under the ribs, sweet and awful, like pressing on a bruise to prove it's yours.

Edmund looked at the tool roll a long time. Then he looked at Danny, and whatever moved behind his eyes stayed behind his eyes, the way it always had; five years of measuring his life in fathoms teaches a man to keep his soundings private. "Board's rotted over the strongroom hatch," he said at last. "I got the first dive done at slack. Door's steel, riveted frame, maybe three-eighths plate. Can your torch take it?"

"With the number two tip, yes. Slowly. You'll want to cut a panel, not the frame — frame's probably backed with concrete, they did that on the merchant ships. Panel first, feel for the void, then decide." He paused. "You'll need bottom time to do it clean. How much air have you got?"

"Two bottles. One good, one doubtful."

"One good and one doubtful." Danny laughed, and it came out wrong, half a cough. "Same as ever. You always dove on faith and fumes."

"And you always dove on arithmetic," Edmund said. "Some things hold."

They looked at each other across the thwart, across six years and four photographs and a name neither of them had spoken aloud, and the flood tide lifted both hulls gently, as if the river itself were trying to bring the boats together.

"The dredge is coming early," Danny said. The words came out of him before he'd decided to spend them, and once spent, there was no taking them back. "Not six tides. My information says four. Maybe less. Vane pushed the schedule up when word got out the Calloway woman was breathing."

Beatrix went very still. "Your information."

"I have ears in places." He met her eyes. "Places I'm not proud of, this week especially. Four tides, madam. Maybe three and a bit, if the weather serves them. You need to know that before you plan around anything I've said."

She absorbed it the way a hull absorbs a sea — visibly taking the strain, visibly holding. "Then we cut tomorrow's slack water," she said. "Not the day after. Whatever it costs in bottom time."

"Whatever it costs," Edmund echoed, and Danny heard what lived under the words, the same thing that had lived under them six years ago on the *Maggie Bright*: a man prepared to trade his own body against the clock, and call it fair.

"There's one more thing." Danny reached into his coat, and saw Edmund's hand drift toward the boathook, and almost smiled at the fairness of it. He brought out not a photograph but a folded page torn from a shipping register, old paper gone the color of tea, and set it on the thwart beside the tool roll. "Crew list. Grey Wren, final voyage. I've had it since I was nineteen. There's a name on it I'll tell you about when it's done. Not before. But I wanted it aboard this boat tonight, even if I don't explain it. Consider it — ballast."

Edmund picked it up. Even in the hooded lamplight Danny saw him read the names, saw him stop on one, saw the question rise and — miracle of restraint — stay unasked. Five years of being paid not to ask questions had given Edmund Underhill the finest discipline on the river: he could hold a question the way other men held their breath.

"Ballast," Edmund said finally. "Aye. We're short of ballast and long of secrets. Might as well trim the boat properly."

Beatrix extended her good hand, and after a heartbeat Danny took it. Her grip was hot and dry, fever living in it, and her eyes over the handshake were doing sums he couldn't see. "Tomorrow's slack," she said. "Bring yourself, Mr. Quill. Not just the torch. A cutting job goes faster with four hands, and I don't mean to spend Edmund's air watching him work alone."

"I didn't say I'd dive."

"I didn't ask what you said."

Danny looked at Edmund. Edmund looked back, and gave the smallest shrug, the shrug of a man who has decided to trust someone against all his training and refuses to discuss it. "Slack's at half seven," Edmund said. "Come up the cut before light. If you're coming to sell us, sell us early — I'd rather get it over with before breakfast."

"That's the warmest thing you've said to me in six years."

"Don't get used to it."

Danny stepped back onto his own deck. The flood was making now, real and steady, the river turning its great slow mind toward the sea, and he cast off and let it take him, standing at the tiller without lighting his lamp, watching the hooded light in the reeds go out behind him as though it had never been.

He thought about the drawer. He thought about the card lying face-down on his table, and Nell breathing through the wall, and the name on the crew list riding now in Edmund's pocket like a stone from a grave. He thought about how redemption isn't a door you walk through; it's a tide — it comes whether you're ready or not, and all you can choose is whether to be standing in the water when it does.

Halfway home, in the thickest of the fog, he passed a shape running dark down the far channel: long, low, engines muffled, moving against every rule of the river at night. A patrol launch, headed upriver. Toward the cut. Toward the reeds. Toward the hooded light that had already gone out — he prayed it had already gone out.

Danny put his tiller over and killed his engine, drifting, listening to the other boat's wake slap the banks until it faded. Then he sat alone in the fog with his heart going like a pump underwater, and understood that whatever he'd decided tonight, the fleet had decided something too, and the two decisions were now running at each other up the same dark channel, and dawn was four hours off, and slack water was at half seven, and there was no longer any middle water anywhere on the river at all.
