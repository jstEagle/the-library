## 22. The Cut

The charge sat against the strongroom door like a sleeping animal, all its danger folded inside a bundle of waxed paper and wire. Edmund had made it himself, kneeling in the bilge of the Grey Wren with Danny Quill's cutting gear spread around him on a canvas sheet, and he had made it the way his brother had taught him to make things: slow, twice-checked, no shortcuts. The primer cord. The tamping mud packed into the seam above the door to throw the blast *inward*, not out into the corridor where it would do nothing but stir the silt. The det cord run back along the deck plates to the junction where Danny waited at the surface, his thumb on the firing box, his breath going in and out through a regulator he wasn't wearing because he'd refused the dive and wouldn't explain why.

Edmund checked the wire a fourth time. Then a fifth. The water around him was the color of strong tea left too long, visibility measured in arm's lengths, and every few seconds the hull above him ticked and groaned with the pressure of the river leaning on its back. Ninety years of leaning. He put his glove against the strongroom door one last time — iron, riveted, furred with a century of rust that came away on his fingers like dried blood — and felt, or imagined he felt, the faintest give. As if the door had been waiting.

He backed away down the corridor, counting his kicks. Twelve to the junction. Fifteen to the corner. Twenty to the companionway where the det cord ran up through the broken skylight to the world of air and light where Beatrix Calloway was watching the tide gauge and hating every second of it.

He stopped at the corner, pressed himself against the bulkhead, and gave the signal: three sharp tugs on the line.

Above him, muffled by ninety feet of river, the Grey Wren held its breath.

* * *

He had told Beatrix the truth that morning, or as much of it as he understood himself, standing on the deck of Danny's boat with the fog coming off the water in ropes. Two tides left. The dredge was coming, Vane had moved it up, and the strongroom door would not open with a crowbar or a prayer. It needed a shaped charge, small and precise, and it needed a diver who knew the wreck's insides the way a man knows the rooms of a house he grew up in.

"You've been down there five years," Beatrix had said. Not a question. Her collarbone was strapped, her color still wrong, but her eyes had that flat bright quality he was learning to dread, the look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't intend to share. "Five years, and you never opened it."

"I was paid not to."

"And now?"

He had looked at the water, at the grey seam of the horizon where the dredge would appear, and said, "Now I'm paying myself."

Danny had said almost nothing at all. He'd brought the gear at dawn, crates of it, the good stuff, the stuff that costs money and questions, and he'd loaded it aboard without meeting anyone's eyes. When Edmund asked him where he'd gotten the primer cord, Danny said, "Don't ask," and when Edmund asked him why he was helping, Danny said, "Don't ask that either," and went forward to check the anchor line as if it were the most interesting thing in the world.

Now Edmund hung in the dark at the corner of a drowned corridor, and the whole wreck seemed to lean in around him, waiting for the answer to a question it had been asking since 1919.

The blast, when it came, was not the roar he'd braced for. It was a *push*. A great soft fist of pressure that came down the corridor and struck his chest and ears all at once, and the water went white — not dark, *white*, a blinding churn of silt and rust and shattered rivets — and the wreck screamed. Every plate, every frame, every riveted seam of her hull spoke at once, a groan so vast and so human that Edmund's first thought, absurdly, was *I've hurt her*, and his second was the far more practical one: *the bulkheads, the old girl's been dead ninety years, she doesn't care what you feel.*

He counted. Ten seconds. Twenty. The white began to thin at the edges, silt drifting down like the snowfall of some terrible country. He gave the line two tugs — *I'm moving* — and swam for the door.

* * *

The strongroom had been a strongroom for a reason.

The charge had done its work well — Danny's gear, Danny's craft, and somewhere in that thought a question Edmund refused, for now, to finish — and the door hung inward on its frame like a jaw knocked loose. Beyond it, a room perhaps twelve feet by eight, shelved along two walls, and on the shelves: boxes. Tin boxes, zinc-lined, the kind shipping companies used for papers they could not afford to lose. Some had burst with the pressure of the years, their contents fanned out and frozen mid-drift in the silt. Some had held.

Edmund's light moved across the room and his heart moved with it, climbing.

He went in low and slow, the way you enter any space underwater, the way his brother had drilled into him before the fleet ever got its hands on either of them: *the wreck decides, Edmund. You just ask nicely.* He lifted the nearest tin. The lid came away in a bloom of rust, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job against all odds, was paper. Real paper. He unrolled the first sheet with hands that would not entirely steady.

A manifest. *S.S. Grey Wren, manifest of cargo, final voyage, March 1919.* He read it by the circle of his lamp, and the numbers went into him like cold water into a boot. Cargo listed as machinery and scrap — cargo actually logged, in a second hand, in a second column, as bullion, bonds, and *one sealed strongbox, contents per attached*. The attached pages were here too, brittle as moth wings. And beneath the manifest, a sheaf of letters, dozens of them, tied with wire, each envelope addressed in a different hand to the same place: the offices of Calloway & Vane Shipping. Letters from the families of the crew. Letters dated *after* the sinking. *We are told there was no cargo of value, and therefore no salvage claim, and therefore no compensation. We are told the loss was an act of God. We are told to be grateful for the twenty pounds.*

Twenty pounds. For a husband. For two sons, in one envelope, the handwriting gone ragged halfway through the second name.

Edmund hung there in the flooded room, ninety feet under a river that had been paid to forget, and read the letter from a woman named Ada Merrick whose handwriting was very fine and very controlled, the handwriting of someone holding herself together with thread. *I do not believe it was an act of God,* she had written. *My husband told me the cargo was wrong. He told me they loaded at night. He told me if anything happened to him I should ask why the insurance was tripled in the last week. I am asking why. Nobody will answer me. I will keep asking.*

The lamp trembled in his hand. He thought of his brother's file, the one the fleet had sealed, the one that said *equipment failure, no further inquiry warranted*, and thought: *Ada Merrick, I know exactly what your kitchen looked like when you wrote that.*

There was one more thing. At the back of the lowest shelf, in a box heavier than the rest, a ledger bound in leather gone the texture of old skin. He opened it to the marked page and the name at the top of the page was *Vane* — not Aldous, an earlier Vane, the founder himself — and it was not a ledger at all but a private book, a confession written in a hand that started confident and deteriorated page by page into something cramped and hunted. *The Wren was lost by design,* the hand had written. *The inspectors were paid. The crew were not meant to survive, and the ones who did were paid to be silent, and the ones who would not be silent are at the bottom of the channel, and I have written the word "silence" so many times it no longer looks like a word. God has stopped answering. I no longer blame Him.*

Edmund got it all into the case. The manifest, the letters, the book — into the waterproof case Beatrix had given him, the one with the folding clasp, the one she'd carried out of the drowned camp with a broken collarbone and a fever and refused, flatly, to let him open until now. He sealed it, clipped it to his harness with two clips because one clip was for cargo and this was not cargo, and turned for the door.

The wreck shifted.

* * *

It was not dramatic, at first. It never is. The Grey Wren had been settling for ninety years, and a charge against one bulkhead is a hand on the shoulder of an old, old woman — she moves, and the moving continues. Edmund felt it as a change in the water, a pressure in his ears, a sound below hearing, and then the silt around him began to run sideways, a current where no current had been, pouring through the corridor from the direction of the breach.

He got to the doorframe as the deck plates behind him let go. Not exploded — *sagged*, the way a floor sags, and then folded, and the shelving wall came down across the room he'd just left in a slow-motion avalanche of tin and rust and ninety years of settled river, and the silt cloud it raised was total, a wall of brown night.

His line was still on. He could feel it running back over his shoulder, up through the companionway, to air, to Danny, to the world. He gave the signal for *coming up* — two slow pulls — and felt the line go slack.

Not cut. *Slack.* He pulled again, harder, and the line paid out toward him, weightless, and his stomach understood before his mind did: somewhere above, in the collapse, in the surge, the line had parted or been buried under a ton of shifted hull, and the thing that had connected him to the surface was now just a length of rope ending in nothing.

He checked his gauge. The needle said what it said, and what it said was *not enough*. Not nearly enough. He'd burned his bottom time finding the papers, and the decompression window Beatrix had drilled into him for three days — *you miss it, Edmund, you die of it, slowly and stupidly, on the surface or in the water, either way* — was already closing like a door.

He had a spare bottle on the wreck. Stage bottle, staged at the companionway two days ago, and the companionway was — he turned his lamp into the brown murk and the murk gave back nothing — somewhere. The corridor had been a corridor. Now it was a suggestion. The surge was still running, pushing silt in long ropes through the wreck, and every surface looked like every other surface, and somewhere in the white dark was a stairway and a bottle of air and a way out, or wasn't.

He thought about Ada Merrick's handwriting, fine and controlled, holding.

He clipped the case tighter, found his line, and followed it not up but *down* to its end, because the end would tell him where it broke, and where it broke would tell him where the collapse was, and away from the collapse was the only direction that mattered.

The line ended in a snarl of rusted plating, pinned under a crossbeam that hadn't been a crossbeam yesterday. Fine. He'd known the wreck would fight. He'd told Beatrix once, in the culvert, fever-night, that the Grey Wren and he had an arrangement: he left her mostly alone, she let him mostly live. He put his glove on the cold plating.

"All right, old girl," he said, and the words came out as a stream of bubbles, and the wreck heard them anyway. "You've kept it ninety years. Help me carry it up."

He pushed off into the blind brown water, gauge in his left hand, lamp in his right, compass in his head, and began to navigate a shipwreck by memory, by touch, and by the sound of his own breathing getting shorter — and above him, ninety feet up, at the edge of the light, the surface of the river went on glittering like something from another world, where a woman with a broken collarbone was watching a line go slack.
