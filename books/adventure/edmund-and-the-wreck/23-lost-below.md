## 23. Lost Below

The water gave him back nothing.

Beatrix stood at the rail of Danny Quill's boat with the shot line burning in her fist and watched the place where Edmund had gone under, as if the river were a door that might reopen if she only kept her eyes on it. The current ran brown-green and opaque, streaked with foam where the tide had begun to turn against them. Somewhere down there — forty fathoms down, in water cold enough to stop a heart's argument — was a man who had missed his decompression window by four minutes.

Four minutes. She had counted them herself, aloud, into the wind, as though her voice could reach him through the hull of the Grey Wren. *Edmund, come up. Edmund, your window.* The radio in his helmet had crackled its small dead static, and then his line had gone taut and singing, and then slack, and then still.

"He's not answering," she said, and heard how flat her voice came out, how much it cost to keep it flat.

Danny didn't look up from the winch. His hands moved over the brake and the drum with the economy of a man who had done this arithmetic before and hated every number in it. "Line's still attached. That's something. Means he didn't drift."

"Or means he's snagged."

"Or means he's snagged." Danny spat over the side, an old habit that looked suddenly like prayer. "Either way we don't fix it standing here talking about it."

She knew what he was going to say before he said it, because she had read the same manuals, sat through the same safety briefings, signed the same waivers that promised, in careful legal language, that no one would ever do what they were about to do. A diver who misses decompression is already dying by inches — nitrogen blooming in his blood like frost on glass. You bring him up slow. You put him in the chamber. You give him hours of measured ascent while his body renegotiates its terms with the pressure. What you do *not* do —

"We can't run him up the line," Beatrix said. "It'll kill him faster than staying down."

"Staying down kills him for certain. He's got maybe twenty minutes of air left in that bottle, and the surge's coming." Danny nodded at the water, at the long swell rolling upriver ahead of the flood tide, the same surge that had parted Edmund's line from the wreck and set all this in motion. "So we've got one bad choice and one worse one, and I have been diving this river since I was fourteen, and I will tell you which one I'm picking."

He picked up the spare regulator.

* * *

Later — years later, if there were later — Beatrix would try to reconstruct those minutes and find them gone, dissolved like sugar in river water. What remained were fragments: Danny stripping off his jacket, checking his own gauges twice, three times; the collarbone she'd broken eight days ago screaming when she hauled on the line; the smell of diesel and cold iron and her own sweat; the way the light went coppery on the water as the clouds tore open above the narrows, as if the sky were leaning in to watch.

"You're injured," Danny said. It wasn't an objection. It was a fact being entered into the record.

"I'm the best free-swimmer on this boat, and you need someone on the surface running the rope." She took the coil from the cleat and wrapped it once around her palm. Her hand shook. She let it shake. There was no time to be ashamed of fear, and besides — fear was useful. Fear was the body telling the mind to pay attention. "You go down fast, you find him, you get him on the line. I haul. We don't stop to be gentle. Gentle is for people with a chamber."

"There's no chamber within ninety miles."

"Then we make our own." She said it and heard how mad it sounded and said it again anyway, louder, because some part of her had understood already what they would have to do: bring him up fast and keep him alive on the surface, breathe for him, warm him, force the nitrogen out of him with pure oxygen and prayer and whatever the harbor had instead of medicine. It violated everything. Every manual, every rule written in blood by divers who had learned the hard way. And the alternative was a hole in the river where Edmund Underhill used to be.

Danny looked at her for a long moment. Something passed across his face — not quite trust, not yet, but the decision to act as if trust existed, which Beatrix had learned was sometimes the same thing.

"If he's dead down there," Danny said quietly, "we don't tell anyone different. Not the fleet, not the inquiry, nobody. He gets to be a man who died diving, not a man the river ate."

"He's not dead."

"You don't know that."

"I know it the way you know a chart is wrong," she said. "Not because I can prove it. Because everything else stops making sense if it isn't."

He went over the side without another word, a clean entry, barely a splash, and the river closed over him the way it had closed over Edmund, and Beatrix was alone on the deck with a rope in her hands and a broken collarbone and the whole grey weight of the afternoon pressing down.

* * *

Hauling a body up forty fathoms is not like hauling anything else. The line comes alive in your hands. It has moods. It hums with the current, jerks with the surge, goes heavy as sin when the load shifts. Beatrix wrapped it around her hips — the proper way, the way Edmund had shown her during that first wary week, back when he'd spoken to her in single sentences and looked at her like a woman reading a contract — and leaned back against it, and pulled.

Her shoulder screamed. She pulled anyway.

The minutes went by in rope-lengths. Ten fathoms. Fifteen. The wind picked up out of the southwest and pushed little white horses across the channel, and far downstream she could see the dredge's mast lights coming on in the failing light, patient and obscene, like eyes opening. Four tides, Vane had said. They were spending the third one here, hanging on a rope above a drowned ship, betting a man's life against the physics of gas and blood.

Twenty fathoms. The line went slack — a horrible, gut-empty slack — and her heart stopped with it, and then it went taut again with a double pull, two sharp tugs, the signal they'd agreed on. *Found. Alive.*

Alive.

She made a sound she would deny making for the rest of her life, something between a sob and a laugh, and hauled harder, and the rope burned through her glove and into her palm and she let it, because pain was just information and the information she needed was *up, up, get him up.*

Thirty fathoms. Thirty-five. The light was going now, the copper gone out of the water, the channel turning the color of gunmetal. She could see shapes below — a pale bloom that might have been bubbles, a darker shape beneath it that resolved, slowly, agonizingly, into two men rising through the green dark like figures in a photograph developing. Danny below, arms locked around Edmund's chest, Edmund's head lolling, his mask fogged, his regulator trailing loose from his mouth on its hose like a severed umbilical.

They broke the surface ten feet off the beam.

"Here! Here, give me — " She was over the rail before she'd decided to be, dropping to her knees on the low platform Danny had welded onto the transom years ago, reaching down into water so cold it felt like a held breath. Together they got Edmund's torso over the gunwale, and then the rest of him, and then he was lying on the deck of the boat, streaming river water, gray-lipped, eyes half-open and fixed on nothing.

"No pulse in the wrist," Danny said, climbing aboard, coughing. "Check the neck."

She checked the neck. For three seconds she found nothing, and in those three seconds the entire world contracted to the size of two fingers pressed against wet skin — and then, faintly, stubbornly, a beat. Then another. Slow as a dripping tap, but there.

"He's alive," she said. "He's alive, he's — Edmund. Edmund, can you hear me?"

His eyes moved. Found her face. Focused with visible effort, the way a lens focuses, hunting and settling. His lips moved around the regulator mouthpiece he wasn't wearing anymore, shaping something.

She bent close.

"*Case,*" he breathed. Water and words together, barely a sound at all. "*Got the case.*"

And there it was, strapped to his chest under the buoyancy vest, the waterproof case from the strongroom, dented and scraped and lashed to his body with a length of signal line tied in knots she recognized — knots he'd taught her, weeks ago, in another life. He had missed his decompression window because he'd stopped to tie it. He had hung in the surge with his air bleeding down and spent four irreplaceable minutes lashing a hundred-year-old manifest to his own ribs.

"You idiot," she said, and her voice cracked clean through the middle of the word. "You beautiful idiot."

* * *

What they did next had no name in any manual, so Beatrix named it herself, silently, as she worked: *the harbor method.* Danny had oxygen — a small medical bottle he kept for welding accidents, half full. They taped the mask to Edmund's face and cracked the valve and told him, over and over, *breathe, just breathe, slow, slow.* They stripped his wet suit to the waist and wrapped him in every dry thing on the boat — the canvas tarp, Danny's coat, the wool blanket that smelled of dog and diesel. They laid him flat and raised his feet on a crate. And when his breathing went shallow and his lips went blue-gray and his eyes started to roll, Beatrix did the thing she had read about in a salvage journal eleven years ago and never thought she'd use: she talked him through it.

"Stay with me," she said, her mouth close to his ear, her good hand pressed flat against his sternum. "I need you to stay with me, Edmund. Listen to my voice. You're on the surface. The dive is over. Everything below is finished. You did it. You got it. Now your only job is breathing — in through the nose, out through the mouth, that's it, that's all the work left in the world."

His eyes found hers again. This time they stayed.

"The case," he whispered again. Always the case. Even now, even with the nitrogen writing fireworks behind his eyes, the broker's instinct or the diver's discipline or whatever it was that made Edmund Underhill himself.

"It's safe. It's lashed to the mast. Nobody's touching it but me." She had moved it while Danny stripped the gear off him — couldn't help herself, had untied it with shaking hands and carried it to the mast step and wrapped the whole thing in the tarp's corner, and now she lied to Edmund gently, because the truth was that it lay six inches from his hip and she wanted him looking at her face, not at it. "It's safe. *You're* the thing that isn't safe yet. So breathe."

Danny crouched at Edmund's other side, working the cranks of the little winch with one hand out of pure habit, though there was nothing left on the hook. He looked older in the last light, Beatrix thought. Older and more decided, as if something had settled in him during the descent that he hadn't agreed to on the surface.

"His pulse is picking up," Danny said. "Color's coming back to his face. He might actually live, the stubborn bastard."

"He'd better." Beatrix pushed the wet hair off Edmund's forehead. Her hand was steady now, finally, now that there was work in it. "He owes me a salvage claim. He owes me thirty-one chapters of trouble. He doesn't get to die owing me."

Edmund's mouth twitched. It might have been a smile. It might have been the nitrogen. With Edmund it had always been impossible to say.

* * *

The dredge's lights grew brighter as the dark came down. They sat in the shadow of the point with the engine off and the current carrying them nowhere in particular, and Beatrix kept her hand on Edmund's chest and counted his breaths the way she had once counted tides — as a form of prayer, as a way of measuring time that didn't feel like losing it.

"He asked about the case," she said quietly, after a long while.

"They all do, at the end." Danny was at the tiller, watching the lights. "Divers, miners, men trapped in things. Last words are always about the work." He paused. "Except my brother's weren't."

Beatrix turned. In the gloom she could see Danny's profile, hard-edged against the distant glow, and something in the set of his jaw told her the sentence had cost him more than the descent had.

"Danny—"

"Not tonight." He said it without heat. Almost gently. "Tonight we get him downriver past the picket, and tomorrow somebody puts an oxygen mask on him properly, and the day after that you two walk into a courthouse and burn the fleet to the waterline. Then maybe I'll tell you why I came back with the cutting gear when I'd already taken their money's worth of doubt." He looked at her, and in the dark his eyes were the same grey as the river. "One tide left, Calloway. One. And that dredge is going to be sitting on the wreck by morning."

"Then we don't sleep."

"No," Danny agreed. "We don't sleep."

Below them, faint through the hull, Edmund murmured something in his half-dream — a word, a name, lost in the wash against the boards. Beatrix leaned closer to hear it, and the river carried the boat on toward the narrows, toward the last tide, toward whatever waited on the other side of the dark.
