## 24. Surfacing

The first thing that comes back is the cold.

Not the cold of the river, which he knows the way a man knows his own pulse — the cold that comes after, the cold of the body remembering it was ever meant to be warm. It arrives in his chest first, a fist of it, then spreads down his arms like water finding the low places in a hull. Edmund's eyes are open. He knows this because the grey light of the overcast is there, wavering above him, and because someone is shouting his name in a voice that has been shouting it for some time.

"—Edmund. *Edmund.* Breathe, damn you, breathe—"

Hands on his shoulders. The deck of Danny Quill's boat under his back, planking slick with river water, and the whole world tilting with the swell in a way the world is not supposed to tilt. He has spent five years learning the difference between the river moving and himself moving, and right now he cannot tell them apart, and that terrifies him more than the dark did.

He turns his head and is sick, river water and bile, most of it over the side because some animal part of him has kept the manners the rest of him has lost. Then he can breathe. It is not a good breath — it is a tearing, whistling thing that hurts all the way down — but it is his, and he takes another, and another, each one a small theft from a river that had decided it was owed everything.

"There you are." Beatrix Calloway's face comes into view above him, haloed in overcast, her collarbone strapped and her skin grey as ash, and she is laughing — a cracked, disbelieving sound, half sob. "There you are, you stubborn son of a—"

"The case." His voice comes out as gravel dragged through water. He tries to sit up and the world spins and he lets it, because his hands have already answered the question his body hasn't finished asking. They are closed around the strap of the waterproof case, knuckles white, and they have not loosened. Not through the surge. Not through the long blind climb up the shot line with his lungs full of fire. Not through whatever happened between the moment his line parted and the moment hands hauled him over the gunwale. His hands know what they are for, even if the rest of him has forgotten. "The case. Tell me I have it."

"You have it." Beatrix's hand closes over his knuckles, over the strap, as if to make the statement true by force of touch. "You've had it the whole way up. You wouldn't let Danny pry it loose to get your jacket off."

"Good." He lies back. The planking is hard and real and smells of tar and old fish and diesel, and he loves it the way a man loves a thing he nearly lost. "Good."

* * *

They do not let him move for a while. Danny wraps him in a blanket that smells of engine oil and forces sweet tea between his lips, and Edmund drinks because refusing Danny Quill has never once worked and because his body is a debt collector now, demanding everything at once. His ears are still ringing. There is a high thin note in them that he suspects will live there for a while, a souvenir from the deep.

He takes inventory the way he takes inventory of a dive, methodically, item by item, because the method is the thing that keeps a man from screaming. Lungs: bruised, working. Ears: ringing. Left shoulder: wrenched where the surge took him and the line held and then didn't. Hands: cut where he'd gripped the shot line bare-fisted on the way up, because there hadn't been time for gloves and he'd known it and done it anyway. He flexes them now and watches the blood well thin and pink in the rain-damp. Five years of rope and fathom have taught his hands to be honest. They tell him he is hurt, not finished.

Above him the sky is the colour of old zinc, and the rain comes fine and steady, beading on the canvas dodger, ticking on the water. Somewhere downriver a foghorn speaks once, low and patient, and falls silent.

"Talk to me," Beatrix says. She has settled herself beside him, back against the wheelhouse, and she looks worse than he feels, which he would not have believed possible an hour ago. The fever is still in her eyes. The strap across her collarbone is dark with sweat. "I've been talking to you for twenty minutes and getting nothing but bubbles. It's a bad habit to fall into, talking to a man who doesn't answer. Danny does it and look how he turned out."

"I heard you." He hadn't, not as words. But there had been a voice down there in the grey, in the last hundred feet when his air ran thin and his head went strange and the wreck's shadow opened beneath him like a mouth — a voice, and light above, and he had climbed toward both because the alternative was to stay down with the letters and the confession and the drowned crew of 1919, who had waited a hundred years and could wait a little longer now that the paper was safe. "Mostly."

"Liar." But her voice gentles. She looks out at the water, and he watches her do the arithmetic he can feel her doing, the same arithmetic that lives in every harbor skull like a second heartbeat. "One tide, Edmund."

He knows. He has known since the strongroom, since the charge went off and the river shuddered and the manifest case came into his hands like something handed across a century. One tide. The flood is running now; he can read it in the way the mooring lines of the far pilings stand out from the piles, in the drift of the rain-slanted current past the hull. The flood will turn. And when it turns, when the ebb begins, the dredge *Vane's Pride* — or whatever name the fleet has painted on her this season — will come up the channel with her suction heads swinging, and the Grey Wren will become mud, and the strongroom will become less than mud, and everything in Edmund's hands will become the only proof in the world.

One tide. Six hours, maybe seven, if the river is generous, and the river has never once been generous.

"Then we don't waste it sitting here watching me drip," he says, and tries to sit up again, and this time Beatrix's hand on his chest holds him down without effort, which tells him something about how much strength he actually has, which is approximately none.

"You'll sit," she says, "and you'll drink your tea, and you'll tell me what you found down there. All of it. Because if we're going to do what I think we're going to do, I need to know exactly what we're carrying."

* * *

So he tells her.

He tells her about the breach in the hull, the one that matches no storm record, the one with the plate edges bent *inward*, cut from the inside of the river's memory by hands and charges rather than weather. He tells her about the strongroom door, the fleet's maker's mark still bright under a century of silt where the cutting charge had peeled it open like a tin. He tells her about the manifest, dry and legible in its oilskin wrapper, the cargo listed in the fleet founders' own hands — and then the second page, the true manifest, the one that was never supposed to exist, listing what was actually loaded that night and who ordered the loading.

He tells her about the letters. This part is harder. His voice keeps snagging on it, the way a hook snags on old netting, and he has to stop twice and drink tea and start again. Forty-one letters, bundled in oilskin with the manifest, written by the families of the crew in the weeks after the sinking. Petitions. Pleas. A widow's letter asking the fleet, with terrible politeness, why her husband's pay had stopped the day the ship went down when the ship had been insured for three times her worth. A father asking why the fleet would not say where on the river his son's body had been found, or whether it had been found at all. The last letter in the bundle is from a woman named Ada Quill, and when Edmund reads the name aloud, Danny Quill, at the wheel, goes very still.

"Ada Quill," Danny says, not turning around. "That's — my grandmother's name was Ada. Her first husband went down on a fleet ship before she married my grandfather. She never talked about him. Not once, my whole childhood."

"Her letter asks," Edmund says quietly, "why the fleet's inquiry took eleven days to convene, and why no crew families were called to give evidence."

The rain ticks on the canvas. The foghorn speaks again, further off now. Danny stares at the water ahead of the bow for a long moment, and his hands on the wheel are steady in the way of hands that have decided to be steady by force.

"Get us to the courthouse steps," he says finally. "That's all. Get us there, and let her read it out."

* * *

The flood is making strongly now, and Danny brings the boat around and upriver, and Edmund, wrapped in his blanket in the shelter of the wheelhouse, feels the strangeness of moving *toward* the wreck instead of away from it. All night and all morning the whole shape of his life has been a running — upriver in the dark, downriver with Beatrix, out to the wreck and back — and now the running has a direction with a name on it, and the name is *truth*, and it sits in his hands in an oilskin case weighing no more than a gull.

Beatrix spreads her charts on the deck boards and weights the corners with winch handles, and the three of them bend over them like conspirators, which is what they are.

"The dredge is coming up the main channel," Beatrix says. Her finger moves on the chart, sure despite the tremor in the rest of her. "Vane won't risk the narrows with a hull that size, not on a flood tide with this wind. So we know where he is and where he's going, and he doesn't know where we are — or he thinks we're mud at the bottom of the wreck site, which amounts to the same thing." Her finger stops, and taps. "The courthouse quay. High water. If the manifest is read aloud in public — if it's read before the fleet's lawyers can get an injunction, before Vane can buy or bully or drown it — then it's out. You can't un-ring a bell. You can't un-read a letter. Half the river will have heard it, and the other half will hear it by nightfall."

"And the fleet will know exactly where we are," Edmund says.

"Yes."

"And they'll come."

"Yes." She looks at him then, and there is no apology in her face, and he is glad of it, because he does not want an apology. He wants her exactly as she is: a woman with a broken collarbone and a fever and a case of paper that will burn the fleet to the waterline, planning the last hour of the race as if she were planning a Sunday picnic. "One tide, Edmund. There's no version of this where we're safe. There's only the version where we're right, and fast."

He looks down at his hands. The cuts have stopped bleeding; the rain has washed them pink and clean. He thinks of the strongroom, of the oilskin bundle in his hands in the dark, of the moment the surge took his line and the wreck let him go — no, not let him go, *gave him up*, the way the river gives up a thing it has decided has been carried far enough. He thinks of his brother, whose name is in no letter and no manifest and no inquiry record anywhere, whose death the fleet explained in four sentences and a cheque, and he feels the resolve in his chest settle and harden like concrete poured in cold water.

"Then we're right and fast," he says.

* * *

The narrows come up to meet them an hour later, and with them comes the sound.

It is a sound Edmund has heard in nightmares for five years without knowing its name: the deep, rhythmic, grinding pulse of a dredge at work, carried over the water the way thunder is carried, felt in the chest before it is heard in the ears. But the narrows are empty. The sound is behind them, downriver, at the wreck — no. He gets his bearings wrong for a moment, the ringing in his ears tilting the world, and Beatrix's hand finds his arm and steadies him and points.

Downriver. At the Grey Wren. Already there.

"He's early," Danny says. "Four tides out, he said, and he's here with three to spare. Man doesn't trust the river to do his killing on schedule."

Through the rain-haze, far off, they can see her: the fleet dredge, low and black, her suction booms swinging in their slow patient arc, her working lights burning yellow even in the daylight, because Vane wants the wreck found and broken and gone before anyone can say he wasn't thorough. The grinding pulse comes again, and Edmund feels it in his teeth. Somewhere under that boom, the hull he has known for five years — every plate, every fathom, every mile of line he has run through her — is being taken apart by men who were paid to never say her name.

He should feel rage. What he feels instead is colder and more useful: *time*, draining out of the day like water out of a holed skiff.

"How long," he says, "before she works down to the strongroom."

Beatrix doesn't answer at once. She is looking at the dredge, and doing sums, and her lips are moving slightly, and Edmund realizes he is watching a woman spend the last of her strength on arithmetic because arithmetic is the only weapon left that works.

"Two hours," she says. "Three, if the silt slows them. The strongroom's under the collapsed section — they'll have to clear it, and Vane doesn't know exactly where it is, or he'd have started there." She rolls up the charts. "Which means we have two hours to reach the quay, and however many people we can put between that dredge and this boat."

"Who's going to stand between us and a fleet dredge?" Danny asks, and it is not a real question, it is the question of a man who already knows the harbor, and Beatrix smiles — a thin, feverish, dangerous smile.

"Everyone," she says. "If Marisol does what I think she's already done."

* * *

The rain thickens as they run up the last reach, and the river closes around them, grey and featureless, and for a while there is nothing in the world but the engine's beat and the rain's hiss and the case in Edmund's hands. He holds it in his lap now, both hands, the way a man holds a sleeping child he is afraid to wake. Through the oilskin he can feel the edges of the paper inside, the manifest, the letters, the confession in a dead man's handwriting that admits everything and absolves nothing.

He thinks about what it weighs. Nothing, really. A pound, maybe two. Five years of his life, and a hundred years of silence, and forty-one families' worth of grief, and his brother's four-sentence death, and all of it weighs less than the anchor chain he has hauled a thousand times without thinking.

The river bends, and the harbor comes into view through the rain, and Edmund stops breathing.

There are boats on the water. Not two, not five. Dozens. Fishing boats and oyster boats and mud scows and a paddle-steamer that has not left her mooring since before the war, their engines coughing and their sails half-set and their decks crowded with people in oilskins, and they are not coming out to fight. They are coming out to *be there*, to lie across the channel in a loose, stubborn, unbreakable line between the mouth of the harbor and the sea, and at their head, standing in the bow of the harbor launch with her hair plastered flat by the rain, is Marisol.

"Look at that," Danny says softly. His voice has gone strange. "Look at what she did."

Edmund cannot speak. Something is happening in his chest that has nothing to do with the near-drowning, a pressure behind the sternum, a heat in the eyes that he blames on the salt spray and will go on blaming it on for years. Five years he has kept himself a mile of rope and a fathom of cold water away from every living soul in this harbor, and here they all are, come out into the rain for him, for the paper in his hands, for a truth older than any of them — and he understands, finally, in the wordless way a man understands a thing in the moment it is almost too late, that he was never as alone as he had made himself.

"High water in forty minutes," Beatrix says. Her hand finds his shoulder, and grips, and her voice is rough. "One tide, Edmund. Let's spend it well."

The harbor boats part ahead of them, a slow lane opening through the rain, and Danny puts the wheel over, and they run for the quay.
