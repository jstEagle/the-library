## 25. Vane Arrives

The dredge *Iron Meridian* smelled of diesel and old iron and the particular staleness of men who had been awake too long, and Aldous Vane stood at her rail in the grey hour before dawn and watched the river give up its secrets one fathom at a time.

He had not slept. Sleep was a currency he had stopped spending years ago; there were better uses for the hours between midnight and morning, and most of them involved charts. The chart spread on the chart table behind him — he could see it through the wheelhouse glass without turning, because he had drawn half of it himself — showed the reach of river below Harrow Point with a precision the Admiralty would have envied and the fleet would have paid him double to lose. Every snag. Every drowned piling. Every hull that had gone down in ninety years of traffic and been left where it fell, because the fleet preferred its dead where they lay.

And now, marked in his own hand in red grease pencil, a circle around a shallow patch of water that no honest chart had ever named.

*Grey Wren.*

"Foreman." The mate, a broad man called Hesk whose face had been weathered into something like driftwood, came up the ladder with two mugs of tea and the careful deference of a crewman who has learned that Vane's mornings are not to be interrupted with anything less than catastrophe. "Soundings say we're two miles off the mark. Tide'll put us over it by seven."

"Two miles early." Vane took the mug. It was too hot to drink and he held it anyway, for the warmth against his palms. "I told the board four tides."

"You told them four tides. You told us three." Hesk permitted himself the ghost of a smile. "We made it in two and a half. She's a good boat when you let her run."

She was a good boat. *Iron Meridian* was the newest dredge in the fleet's working fleet, forty meters of riveted appetite with a bucket chain that could eat a sunken barge in a shift, and Vane had supervised her fitting-out personally, down to the thickness of the plates. He knew her the way he knew all the machines he'd built: completely, and without affection. Affection was for things that could disappoint you. Machines only failed, and failure had causes, and causes could be found.

What he did not know — what had kept him at this rail since midnight, watching the black water slide past — was what a salvage broker and a harbor diver had managed to do with six tides and no equipment worth the name.

"Any sign of them?" he asked.

Hesk shook his head. "Search launch swept the narrows twice overnight. Nothing. No skiff, no campfire, nothing on the flats." He hesitated, which Hesk almost never did. "Either they're gone downriver past us—"

"They haven't." Vane said it flatly, and Hesk, wisely, did not ask how he knew. He knew the way he knew the soundings: by arithmetic. A wounded woman and a diver in a borrowed skiff could not outrun a dredge that had left a day ahead of schedule. Which meant they were behind him, or hidden, or — and this was the possibility he turned over most carefully, the way a man turns over a coin to check for shaving — already at the wreck.

The river ran brown-green under the first light, carrying last night's rain out toward the sea. Gulls worked the mudflats. Somewhere upstream, invisible, the town of Greyharbor was waking to kettles and gulls and the smell of tar, and none of those people knew that the largest machine the fleet had ever sent upriver was sitting on their doorstep like a debt come due.

Vane drank his tea at last. It had gone lukewarm and tasted of the tin. He thought about the board meeting, nine days ago, when old Mrs. Renwick-Cole — granddaughter of a founder, voice like a drawer of cutlery — had said, *Mr. Vane, we are paying you to ensure that nothing surfaces.* Not the wreck. Nothing. He had signed the paper they slid across the table without reading it closely, because he already knew what it said. It said: whatever happens, it is yours.

He had spent thirty-one years making himself useful to the fleet precisely so that someday, when the past came knocking, he would be the one holding the door. That was what order meant. That was the whole of his philosophy, learned young and never revised: the river keeps what it takes, and men who go dragging the bottom for grievances drown there, and the living deserve clean water. His father had helped build the first dredges. His grandfather had helped sink the pilings for the first coaling wharf. Three generations of Vanes had made the river navigable, predictable, safe, and if a certain amount of history had to be paved over to make it so — well. Paving over was the price of every road.

"Foreman," Hesk said again, quieter. "There's a boat."

* * *

It was a skiff, and it was riding at anchor fifty yards off the wreck site as though it had grown there, and the man standing in it was Edmund Underhill.

Vane had done his reading. He always did. He knew the diver's file the way he knew the soundings: five years on the permit list for the outer channel, five years of quiet, compliant, unremarkable work dives on a wreck the navy had fenced with a buoy and the fleet had fenced with silence. No violations. No questions asked, and — this was the detail Vane had underlined in pencil — no questions *answered*, either. Underhill was the kind of man who noticed everything and reported nothing, which made him either the safest man on the river or the most dangerous, depending entirely on which side of him you stood.

The skiff bobbed on the flood tide's first push. Underhill stood in it with the stillness of a heron, dive gear stacked at his feet, and he watched the dredge come on with an expression Vane could not read at fifty yards and suspected he could not have read at five.

"Run out the spuds," Vane said. "Hold here. Nobody touches the bucket chain until I say."

Hesk looked at him. "You're going over to talk to him."

"I'm going over to talk to him."

"With what?"

Vane set down the empty mug. "With what I brought him for."

The launch went across the water low and fast, throwing a bow wave that slapped against the skiff when they swung alongside, and Vane stepped from gunwale to gunwale with the ease of a man who had been stepping between boats since he was nine years old. Up close, Underhill was younger than his file photographs and more tired than his age allowed, with the salt-bleached look of someone who had spent half a decade being slowly rinsed. There was blood on his sleeve, dried brown, not his own by the way he ignored it. And lashed down under the thwart, wrapped in canvas, was a case. Steel-edged. Waterproof. The kind of case that cost money and carried things that mattered.

"Mr. Underhill," Vane said. "Aldous Vane. Fleet operations."

"I know who you are." Underhill's voice was flat river-water, no current showing. "Everyone on this river knows who you are. They just don't usually get to see you."

"No," Vane agreed. "They don't." He looked past the diver, at the water over the wreck — green-grey, opaque, hiding the hull that had lain there for twenty-six years while the fleet paid a navy buoy to keep divers honest and a harbor family to keep one diver quiet. "You've been diving my ship for five years."

"Not your ship. The navy's buoy. The harbor's moorings. The river's wreck." Underhill shifted his weight, and Vane saw the diver's hands — scarred, rope-calloused, absolutely steady. "I never asked whose it was. That was the arrangement."

"And now?"

"Now there's a woman with a broken collarbone asleep under a tarp in the bottom of this skiff, and a case full of paper that says your founders murdered their own crew, and a dredge the size of a church sitting on top of both." Underhill met his eyes. "Now the arrangement's over."

Below them, faintly, through the hull of the world, Vane could feel the *Meridian*'s engines idling, a vibration in the water like a held breath. Overhead the gulls wheeled and screamed their small hungers. The flood tide was building; he could read it in the angle of the skiff's anchor line, in the slow creep of foam along the flat. One tide. Perhaps two, if the weather held, before the channel closed and the dredge could work and everything could be settled the old way — steel and silt and silence.

He had rehearsed this conversation on the run upriver, in the wheelhouse, alone, at three in the morning. He had rehearsed threats and dismissed them — threats were for men who lacked imagination, and besides, threatening a man who had already decided to die for a piece of paper only clarified his resolve. He had rehearsed money and dismissed that too; Underhill had spent five years proving he couldn't be bought cheap, which meant he couldn't be bought at all. What remained was the thing Vane himself would want, in Underhill's place. What remained was the truth — but a truth shaped and issued and certified, which was a different animal from a truth loose in the world.

"Your brother," Vane said.

Underhill went very still. The gulls screamed. The tide pushed.

"Tobias Underhill," Vane continued, quietly, the way a surgeon speaks near an open wound. "Diver, second class, fleet contract 4471. Died eleven years ago on the Collier Deep job, when the cofferdam let go. The inquiry found operator error. The file was closed. The compensation was paid to the harbor trust, because there was no next of kin on record." He paused. "There was a next of kin on record. You'd changed your name to your mother's. I've known that for eleven years. I signed the form that buried it."

Underhill's breathing had changed — slower, deeper, the breathing of a man managing something enormous. "Why are you telling me this."

"Because I'm going to un-bury it." Vane let the words sit in the cold air between them. "Official acknowledgment. Public record. Tobias Underhill, killed by a dam the fleet's own engineers flagged as unsafe six weeks before the job — I've read the flag memo, Mr. Underhill, it exists, it's in a vault in Renwick House, and I can put it on a courthouse table inside a month. Full inquiry. Named responsibility. Compensation to you, personally, with interest across eleven years. Your brother's name read aloud at the quayside with the other men the river took."

The tide hauled at the skiff. Somewhere beneath them, twenty fathoms down or ten — the charts lied, the wrecks moved, the river rearranged its dead — the *Grey Wren* waited with her secret in her belly.

"And in exchange," Underhill said.

"In exchange, the case comes to me. Unopened is preferable. Opened is survivable. Missing is not." Vane held up a hand before the diver could speak. "Before you tell me what I already know is written in it — yes. I've seen fragments. Enough to know the shape. Founders' names. Insurance fraud. A scuttling. Crew who weren't meant to survive the towing accident." He said the words evenly, without flinching, because flinching was a luxury for men who hadn't spent three decades keeping this river orderly. "I'm not going to insult you by pretending it isn't true. It's true. My grandfather helped draft the insurance claim. I found that out when I was forty-one and it cost me a year of sleep I never got back."

"Then why—" Underhill's jaw worked. "Why keep it down. Why any of it. If you know."

"Because of what happens when it comes up." Vane turned and gestured, one arm taking in the whole grey sweep of the river: the mudflats, the moorings, the town smoke rising thin and blue, the dredge, the distant cranes of the fleet works where four hundred families drew wages. "Three thousand jobs. Every mooring fee, every chandlery, every pension the fleet pays out. The cooperatives, the clinics, the pilot service. All of it stands on the company charter, and the company charter stands on the founders, and the founders stand on that manifest. You pull it up, the whole structure doesn't just wobble — it drowns. Not the guilty ones. The guilty ones are dead, Mr. Underhill, every last one of them, comfortably, decades ago. What drowns is the harbor. Your harbor. Marisol Vega's mooring rights. Danny Quill's daughter's clinic card. The families that fed you for five years — you think the board will keep paying into Greyharbor once the charter's dragged through an inquiry? They'll liquidate. They'll sell the works to interests downcoast and the river will belong to men who never learned its tides."

"You don't know that."

"I know exactly that. I've sat in the room where they decide such things." Vane lowered his arm. "I am offering you the only version of justice this river can survive. Your brother's name in daylight, paid for with a piece of paper about men who have been dead longer than you've been alive. Or you surface that case at flood tide, and you get your truth, and eighteen months later this harbor is a memory and the dredges belong to strangers who won't even leave the wrecks lying respectful."

From under the tarp in the stern, a voice, rough with fever: "Don't listen to him, Edmund. He's offering you your grief back at retail."

Vane looked down. Beatrix Calloway had pushed the tarp back from her face — grey-lipped, collarbone strapped, eyes burning like the last coals of a fire — and she was looking at him with an expression he recognized, because he had seen it in mirrors across thirty years of hard decisions: the look of a person who has already counted the cost and found it acceptable.

"Miss Calloway." He inclined his head slightly. "You should be in a hospital."

"What I should be," she said, "is dead, according to your search launches. Instead I'm going to be at the courthouse steps at high water tomorrow with that case open in front of the press and whatever navy officer you can't keep away. Salvage rights, Vane. First to raise holds title. Look it up. It's older than your fleet." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "You can't buy salvage rights. You can only beat them."

"The law," Vane said, "is a thing the fleet wrote."

"Then it will be very interesting," Beatrix Calloway said, "watching you enforce it."

For a long moment nobody spoke. The tide lifted the skiff and let it fall. Down in the green water, patient as arithmetic, the wreck waited with its dead and its paper, and above it two men stood in two boats measuring each other across a gap of three feet of grey river, and each of them understood, with perfect clarity, that the other could not be moved.

Vane stepped back onto the launch. At the rail he paused, and did not look back, and said the last thing he had rehearsed at three in the morning, the thing he had hoped not to need:

"The offer stands until the tide turns, Mr. Underhill. After that, I do my job, and my job is the bucket chain." He signaled the helmsman. "Think about your brother. Think about which of us is actually going to say his name out loud."

The launch swung back toward the dredge. Behind him he heard the splash of an anchor coming up, and the creak of a small boat turning into the flood, and — faint, carried flat across the water — the sound of Beatrix Calloway beginning, hoarsely, patiently, to explain to Edmund Underhill exactly what his brother would have wanted surfaced.

Vane stood at the rail of the *Iron Meridian* and watched the skiff grow small, heading not downriver, not upriver, but straight out over the circle of red grease pencil, toward the wreck itself.

"Hesk," he said. "Start the chain."
