## 28. Last Tide

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning.

I know because I was watching the water when it did — watching the way a man watches a door he knows is about to close. The ebb had been running all night, dragging the river's heart out toward the sea, and for six hours the current in the narrows had been our enemy, shoving the skiff's stern around, making Danny's borrowed engine labor like an old horse pulling uphill. Then, almost without ceremony, the surface went still. A long green slick of slack water, no more than twenty minutes wide, lying across the channel like a held breath.

Then the first eddies began to spin backward. The flood had begun.

"Four hours," Beatrix said beside me. Her voice was rough from fever and salt and two days of being jounced downriver with a collarbone that hadn't been set properly, and she'd stopped apologizing for it somewhere past the mudflats. "Maybe four and a half before it goes slack again. After that the channel closes until tomorrow's flood."

"And the dredge arrives on tomorrow's flood," I said.

"So we don't be here when it does."

I looked back once, the way you're not supposed to. Behind us, maybe three miles downriver where the estuary widened into grey steel, the dredge *Ironhold* sat low and black against the last of the night, her deck lights burning like a row of yellow eyes. She'd gotten under way early — Vane had seen to that, four tides out instead of five — and she moved with the patient, unhurried certainty of something that has never once needed to hurry. She didn't have to run us down. She only had to arrive. The river would do the rest; the fleet owned the river.

But she was behind us now, and the tide was with us, and there were four hours of water between her bow and whatever we could become by then.

"Danny," I said.

"I see it." He had his hands full of wheel and throttle, knuckles white, hair plastered flat under the hood of his oilskin. He'd taken the helm an hour ago and hadn't given it up, even when his arms started shaking. "Narrows are dead ahead. You two get down low."

* * *

The narrows were a mile of channel the width of a country lane, cut between mudbanks that the flood was just beginning to drown. At high water they were nothing — a pleasant stretch of brown river with reeds along the margins. At low water they were a graveyard of masts and keels, every wreck and snag and sunken barge from a century of careless commerce standing proud of the mud like the ribs of drowned animals. There was one navigable line through them, maybe forty feet wide, marked by nothing but my memory and two leaning stakes that some forgotten waterman had driven into the bank before either of us was born.

I knew the line because I'd run it a hundred times, mostly at night, mostly with cargo I wasn't asked to describe. The harbor had always known the narrows. The fleet's charts showed them as unnavigable — which suited everyone fine, because it meant the fleet's patrols stayed out of them too.

That was the whole of our plan, if you could call it a plan: the biggest thing they had couldn't follow us through the smallest place we knew.

The trouble was that the tide was flooding *into* the narrows, and a mile of constricted channel turns a rising tide into something with opinions. By the time we reached the mouth, the current through the gap was already visible — a long seam of ruffled water down the middle, flanked by glass-smooth eddies spinning off the mudbanks, and here and there a boil where the flood came over a submerged point and stood up in the air like something breathing.

"Flood's making better than three knots in there," I said, reading the water the way other men read print. "We'll make six over the ground running with it. But it'll shove us sideways off the line if you let it."

"Then don't let me," Danny said.

Beatrix gripped the gunwale with her good hand and said nothing at all, which from her was a kind of trust.

We went in.

* * *

There is a speed that water reaches where it stops being water and becomes a place. You feel it in your hands first, through the hull, a trembling like a struck string. Then in your ears, the hiss building to a roar as the skiff's bow wave stands up and the banks start to blur. We hit that speed thirty seconds into the narrows and never lost it.

The mudbanks came at us like walls. Black on both sides, streaming, higher than the gunwales in places where the flood hadn't reached them yet, close enough that I could smell them — that rich rotten-egg breath of disturbed silt, the smell of everything the river has ever swallowed. Reeds whipped past overhead. A heron exploded off the bank not six feet from my face, all wings and outrage, and was gone behind us before my heart finished its next beat.

"Stakes!" I shouted, and pointed left. "Line runs inside them!"

Danny saw them — two grey posts leaning together like conspirators — and put the nose of the skiff at the gap between them and the boiling water beyond. The current took us the moment we committed. It grabbed the stern and swung it out and for one long second we were broadside to the stream, sliding, the near mudbank coming up fast enough to read the individual shells embedded in it —

— and then Danny did the thing I will remember about him for the rest of my life, which was to give it *more*. Full throttle into the swing, letting the engine's thrust fight the current for ownership of the hull, and the skiff came round like a dog shaking off water and found the line again with ten feet to spare on either side.

"Sorry," Danny yelled, though there was nothing to be sorry for.

"You're a menace," I yelled back, and meant it as the highest compliment I own.

Behind us, faint under the roar of the channel, I heard a new sound: a horn. One long blast, deep as a church bell, rolling up the river and bouncing off the mudbanks so that it seemed to come from everywhere at once. The *Ironhold*, announcing herself at the mouth of the estuary. Fleet procedure. They always announced themselves. Order required it.

"She can't follow us in," Beatrix shouted against the wind. She'd twisted around despite the collarbone, and her face was grey with pain and lit up anyway. "She draws nine feet loaded. The narrows won't carry six."

"She doesn't need to follow us." I watched the water ahead, calling the line. "She needs to be waiting when we come out. And she knows exactly where we come out, because there's nowhere else to come out."

The horn sounded again, closer, and this time I heard something underneath it that made my stomach drop: engines. Not one engine. Several, small and fast, the particular ragged buzz of fleet patrol launches at full power.

"They've got boats above the narrows," I said. "They ran them upriver last night while we slept. They'll be at the far end."

"How many?" Danny asked.

"At least two. Maybe three."

Beatrix was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again her voice had changed — flattened out, cold and clear, the voice I imagined she used on salvage crews when the money was gone and the diving was bad and someone had to say the arithmetic out loud.

"Then we don't stop at the far end," she said. "We come out of the narrows at full speed and we keep going, and we hope their nerve is worse than ours."

"That's not a plan," Danny said.

"It's a tide," she said. "It's the last one. Plans are what people have when they've got time."

* * *

The middle of the narrows was the worst of it and the best of it, though I couldn't have told you which at the time. The channel kinked hard to starboard around a point where an old coal barge lay sunk with her ribs just kissing the surface, and the flood piled against that obstruction and stood up in a standing wave, three feet of green water holding perfectly still in the middle of the current like a sculpture of itself. Running with the tide, you hit it at eight knots over the ground and went weightless.

We went weightless.

For the length of one heartbeat the skiff flew — actually flew, hull clear of the river, engine screaming at the sky — and in that heartbeat I had the strangest sensation of looking down at the whole river at once: the narrows unspooling behind us, the drowned barge sliding away beneath, and far off through the pre-dawn grey the *Ironhold*'s lights, and nearer, much nearer, two sets of running lights swinging out from the far end of the channel to block it. Everything laid out at once, the whole board visible, the way I imagine it looks to gulls and to the dead.

Then we landed, hard, and the world was water and noise again.

"Two launches at the exit," I gasped, hauling myself back onto the thwart. "Port side and center. There's a gap to starboard, along the north bank, but it shoals — eighteen inches over the oyster spit at low water."

"The tide's been making two hours," Beatrix said. "What's over it now?"

"Three feet. Maybe less in the troughs."

"Our draft?"

"With Danny driving? Six inches, if we're lucky, and luckier still if we stay lucky."

Danny laughed — a short, wild bark of a laugh, the laugh of a man who has passed through fear and out the other side into something brighter and more dangerous. "Three feet of water and two patrol boats. Is that all?"

"That's all," I said.

"Then hold on to something you love," he said, and put the bow down the throat of the narrows' last reach.

* * *

The last half-mile was the fastest thing I have ever been part of. The channel straightened and narrowed until the banks were close enough to touch from the gunwales, and the flood, compressed, simply ran — a brown-green torrent with the skiff surfing down its face, spray sheeting off the bow, the reeds whipping past in a continuous hiss like the river whispering *go, go, go*. My eyes streamed. Beatrix had her good arm locked around a cleat and her face turned into the wind and she was, I swear it, smiling. Danny stood at the wheel with his whole body, knees bent, riding the hull's every twitch, steering by something older than sight.

And ahead, growing out of the grey, the two patrol launches. They'd anchored themselves across the channel mouth — lazy, confident, a rope and a lantern apiece, men in fleet oilskins standing in their wells with the easy posture of people who have never once had to chase anything. One of them raised a hand-lamp and blinked it at us: the signal to heave to. As if we might. As if anyone in the history of water had ever seen a signal like that and obeyed it at eight knots down a flooding tide.

"Starboard," I said. "Now."

Danny put us over. The skiff slewed, dug a shoulder into the current, and shot out of the main channel's throat into the shallows along the north bank — and the bottom came up to meet us. I felt it before I heard it, a change in the engine's note as the screw began to chew at water that was thinking about becoming mud. Three feet. Two and a half. The oyster spit's shells rattling somewhere beneath our keel like teeth.

The nearest launch crew finally understood what we were doing. I heard shouting — thin, furious, absurd over the wind — and saw men scrambling to raise their anchor, to bring their bow around, and I knew with a cold clean certainty that they were too late. An anchored launch needs two minutes to get under way. We needed eleven seconds to cross the spit.

Ten.

Nine.

The skiff touched. Once — a grinding shudder through the hull, shell and sand shrieking along the garboard, Danny easing the throttle a hair and then giving it back, lifting the bow with the wave of his own wake the way you lift a heavy door over a threshold —

— and we were through. Deep water on the far side took us like a hand opening, and the engine's note climbed back to its full cry, and the narrows fell away behind us with the two launches still fouled in their own anchor lines, still shouting, the hand-lamp blinking its useless command at nobody.

* * *

We came out into the wide lower reach with the sky going from black to iron to the first bruised suggestion of pearl in the east, and for perhaps half a minute none of us said anything. The engine roared. The wind tore at us. And ahead, bending away toward the sea, the river opened out all silver and moving, alive with the flood, carrying us.

Then Beatrix said, quietly, in the voice of a woman doing sums she doesn't like: "Look ahead. Not behind."

I looked ahead, and my stomach went cold.

The harbor wall of boats was there — Marisol's doing, God bless her and every soul who'd answered her — a strung-out line of moorings, punts, oyster dredgers, and one ancient steam launch that hadn't moved under its own power since before the war, spread across the lower reach between us and the *Ironhold*. Ordinary boats. Ordinary people standing in them, lanterns lit, holding position in the flooding tide with the calm stubbornness of a family refusing to leave a graveside. They weren't there to fight. They were there to be in the way, which is sometimes braver.

But the *Ironhold* was closer than I'd reckoned. Much closer. In the growing light I could see her clearly now: the great black hull, the dredge gear slung along her flank like a fistful of folded cranes, the wheelhouse windows glowing, and — my chest tightened — a launch swung in davits on each quarter, lowered, manned, sitting on the water alongside her like wasps ready off a nest.

And she had seen us. A searchlight snapped on atop her wheelhouse and swept the water, found us, and pinned us — a circle of brutal white light with our little skiff trembling in the middle of it, every plank and rivet and exhausted face suddenly as legible as a page.

"She's making way," Danny said. "She's actually — she's coming up the reach."

"She doesn't need the narrows," Beatrix said. "This reach carries her whole draft. She's going to meet us head-on, and those launches are going to come off her the moment she's within a mile." She turned and looked at me, and the searchlight made her eyes into two bright holes in a grey face, and she smiled the way people smile at funerals when they mean it. "Well, Edmund Underhill. Four hours of tide left, a wall of fishing boats, and a dredger the size of a chapel bearing down on us. What does the water say?"

I listened to it. That's the truth of what I did, though it sounds like poetry and isn't — I shut my eyes for two seconds and listened to the river the way I'd listened to it every day for five years, and the river said what it has always said, which is that it is bigger than every man who claims it, and it favors whoever moves.

"The wall parts," I said. "Marisol will have it planned — they'll open a lane for us down the middle and close it behind. We run the lane straight at the *Ironhold*'s bow and turn hard across her stem at the last possible second, into her lee, where her own launches can't see us against her hull. Then we run for the Grey Wren roads with her searchlight blind on her own starboard side."

"That's insane," Danny said.

"It's a tide," Beatrix said, and there was the grin again, fierce and fever-bright. "Last one. Hold on to something you love."

Ahead of us, the wall of harbor boats began — slowly, deliberately, lantern by lantern — to move apart.

And behind us, out of the dark mouth of the narrows, came the ragged buzz of patrol engines, two of them, three, running with the flood at full power, closing fast.
