## 29. Collision

The narrows took us the way a throat takes water.

One moment we were in open river with room to breathe, and the next the banks closed in — black stone on either side, slick with spray, the channel threading between them like wire through a needle's eye. The flood tide ran through that gap at eight knots if it ran an inch, and our little skiff, overloaded past all sense, surfed down the middle of it with the engine screaming and Beatrix's good arm locked around the case between her knees.

"Harbor boats are parting," Danny shouted from the wheel. He'd taken it without being asked, without ceremony, the way he did everything — as if steering a stolen skiff through a tidal race under patrol engines was simply the next job on the list. "Look at them go."

I looked. Ahead of us the wall of lanterns was opening like a gate, each boat sliding aside just far enough, no farther, their hulls grinding against one another in the dark, and beyond them I could see the quay lights of home strung out along the water like a rosary someone had dropped.

Behind us, the patrol engines grew teeth.

"They'll try to ram," Beatrix said. She had to shout over the engine and the tide both, and her voice cracked doing it, fever still living in her throat. "They won't board. Boarding means witnesses, paperwork. They'll put us in the rocks and call it weather."

"Then we don't give them rocks," Danny said.

The first searchlight found us then.

It came off the lead launch like a thrown spear, white and absolute, and for one full second the whole narrows stood revealed — the wet black stone, the boiling water, the harbor boats flanking us with their crews standing silent at their rails, forty faces turned toward the light without a single one of them flinching from it. Then the beam swung off us and the dark came back, and in the dark I heard the patrol launch's engine change pitch.

It was coming to kill us. There was no other word for what that sound meant.

* * *

I have been underwater when things went wrong. I know the particular silence of it, the way panic arrives not as noise but as a kind of narrowing, the world contracting to the width of your next breath. What happened in the narrows felt like that — everything narrowing to steel and water and the space between them.

"They're flanking," I said. I'd gone forward, low, one hand on the gunwale, watching the two launches split behind us like a pair of jaws. "One on each side. They mean to close the gap."

"Then we go where the gap isn't." Danny hauled the wheel over and the skiff heeled so hard Beatrix cried out, clutching the case, and we shot toward the eastern bank where the tide eddied backward off the stone in long white ropes. It was madness. It was the only water the launches couldn't follow us into without tearing their own bottoms out.

The light caught us again. In its glare I saw the near launch clearly for the first time — fleet grey, deck guns stripped, six men at the rail, and at the bow a figure in a long coat with his hands folded behind him, watching us with the terrible calm of a man who has already done his arithmetic.

Vane.

He wasn't shouting. He wasn't gesturing. He simply stood there while his launch bore down on us out of the flood, and I understood, in the last clear moment before everything went wrong, that Aldous Vane had come to see this done himself. That whatever orders had been given, whatever men he'd brought, he considered this his own work, his own weight to carry — the foreman closing out the job.

"Danny—"

"I see him."

The launch came on. The narrows narrowed. And the tide, which had carried us all night like something precious, chose that moment to remind us it had never once been ours.

* * *

Later, people would ask me how fast it happened, and I would tell them the truth: faster than telling. Faster than fear. There was the launch bearing down off our starboard quarter with Vane motionless at its bow, and there was Danny hauling the wheel hard to port to keep us out of the eddy line, and there was the moment — half a heartbeat, no more — when the two courses crossed.

The dredge wasn't even supposed to be there.

That's the thing I couldn't stop seeing afterward. Behind the launches, filling the mouth of the narrows from bank to bank, came the *Iron Meridian* herself — Vane's dredge, running with the flood, her great bucket ladder stowed but her hull riding heavy and black as a church. He hadn't waited four tides after all. He'd brought the machine up with him, because machines don't leave witnesses and neither, tonight, did he.

Her bow wave reached us first. A wall of displaced water, three feet high, rolling out ahead of thirty thousand tons of iron, and it picked our skiff up like a leaf and threw us sideways across the channel, directly into the launch's path.

I remember Beatrix's hand finding mine. Not grabbing — finding, deliberate, her fingers lacing through my fingers with the case wedged between us, as if to say: whatever comes, this comes with me holding you.

I remember Danny.

He didn't shout. He didn't look back. He saw the launch's bow rising off our starboard side like a cliff coming down, saw that the wheel alone wouldn't save us, saw — God knows how, in half a second, in the dark, in the spray — that the only way to turn us clear was to hold the skiff into the collision instead of away from it, to take the blow at an angle that would glance rather than crush, and to hold that angle with his own body on the wheel until it was done.

"Edmund." His voice, very level. "Get her down."

"Down where—"

"Flat. Both of you. Now."

And then he turned his back to us, planted himself against the wheel with both arms locked, and became the rudder.

* * *

Steel met wood at eleven knots.

The sound is still in my ears. Not a crash — crashes end. This was a long tearing shriek, the launch's stem ploughing into our stern quarter, wood splintering in sheets, the whole skiff twisting under us like a living thing being killed, and through all of it Danny held. I could see him from where I lay flat across Beatrix and the case, could see his shoulders set against the wheel, his boots braced against the deck boards as they buckled under him, holding the angle, holding it, holding it — giving us the extra yard, the two yards, the three yards that put the launch's bow across our stern instead of through our hearts.

The skiff slewed. The launch ground past us close enough that I could have touched its paint, close enough that I saw the rivets, saw a sailor's face staring down at me with something like horror. And then the flood tide took us both, and the launch was swept on ahead, and we were spinning, shipping water, alive.

"Danny—" I got my knees under me. "Danny!"

He was still at the wheel. That was the first thing, and for one merciful second I thought he'd done it, thought he'd walked away from it the way he walked away from everything, grinning, already talking about the bar tab.

Then I saw the wheel post.

It had come through. When the launch hit, the whole helm assembly had driven forward and inward, and the steel post that should have been stopped by the deck had gone through Danny instead — below the ribs, angling up, pinning him to the wheel he refused to let go of. His hands were still on it. His knuckles were white. His face was the color of the foam going past us, and when he spoke, blood came with the words.

"Steer," he said. "She's shipping water. Steer, you great useless eel, or we all drown a hundred yards from home."

* * *

There is a version of this night where I freeze. I've met it since, in dreams — the version where I kneel beside him and hold his hands and say his name over and over while the river takes the boat and the case and Beatrix and everything, the version where love does nothing because love arrived too late to be useful. I know that version exists because I lived inside it for perhaps four seconds.

Then Beatrix moved.

Feverish, one-armed, collarbone broken and barely a week mended, she crawled past me to the wheel, took one look at the wound, and said, in the voice of a woman who has run salvage crews through storms for twenty years: "Don't touch it. Edmund — the tiller lines. There's an emergency tiller aft, every working boat has one, find it and steer us in. I'll stay with him."

"He needs—"

"He needs the boat steady more than he needs anything you can do. Go."

So I went. God forgive me, I went. I crawled aft over the splintered deck with the river coming in over the transom in cold handfuls, and I found the emergency tiller socketed under a hatch Danny had shown me a hundred years ago when we were young and stupid and diving together for honest money, and I fitted it, and I steered.

Ahead of us the harbor boats had seen it all. I could hear them now — voices carrying across the water, the whole flotilla turning, coming back through the parted wall with their lanterns swinging, Marisol's voice somewhere among them calling my name, calling Danny's. The quay lights grew. The narrows fell behind. And behind us, in the dark, the *Iron Meridian* slowed and stopped in the channel, her engines idling, because Vane would not bring his machine into the harbor's own water with forty boats and a hundred witnesses watching. Whatever else that man believed, he believed in the appearance of order, and we had dragged him into the one place where the appearance had failed.

"We're going to make it," Beatrix said. She had her coat off and wadded against Danny's wound, her good hand pressed there, her forehead nearly touching his. "You hear me, Danny Quill? You're going to make it, and I'm going to owe you a debt so large your daughter will be sick of hearing about it."

"That true?" Danny said. His voice was smaller now. His hands had slipped off the wheel; there was nothing left to hold. "About Nell? You'd see to her?"

"The crews' fund. The inquiry. Every penny that comes out of that manifest — some of it's hers. I'll write it into the claim myself."

"Nell," Danny said, and smiled, and it was the old grin, the one from the good years, the one that used to make the whole dive boat feel like a festival. "She'd like that. She'd lord it over everyone."

"Hold on to something you love," Beatrix whispered. "Hold on."

* * *

We grounded on the slipway below the harbor master's shed with the tide still lifting us, and hands were there before the hull stopped moving — Marisol's hands, her father's, a dozen others, hauling us out of the dying skiff onto the wet stones. Someone had sent for the doctor. Someone had cleared a path. The harbor family, which had spent five years teaching me that help arrives quietly or not at all, arrived all at once, loud and terrified and magnificent.

They lifted Danny onto a door pried from the harbor master's shed, and I walked beside him the whole way up the quay, holding his hand, which squeezed mine once, weakly, and let go.

At the surgery door he caught my sleeve.

"Edmund." Blood at his lips, but his eyes clear, ferociously clear. "The case. Don't let it out of your sight. Not for me, not for anyone. Not even for me — especially not for me. Understand?"

"I understand."

"And Edmund." His grip tightened, spent the last of what he had. "I took their photographs. At the mudflats. Insurance, I told myself. If they come asking—" He coughed. "Burn them. First thing. Before the inquiry, before anything. Burn them and never let them use me against you."

"There's nothing in those photos that matters more than what you did tonight."

"There's everything in those photos." His eyes were closing. "That's how they work. That's always how they work. Burn them, Ed. Do it for me, since I never managed to do anything right for myself."

The doors closed between us.

* * *

I stood on the quay with the case under my arm and the flood tide turning beneath the pilings, and I watched the lights of the *Iron Meridian* hanging in the narrows like a held breath. Somewhere on her deck Aldous Vane was deciding what came next. Somewhere in the surgery above me, Danny Quill was fighting for the life he'd spent the night buying for strangers.

Beatrix came to stand beside me. Her hand found the case, rested on it, did not take it.

"One tide," she said quietly. "It's turning now. By morning slack, the lawyers will be here, and the press, and the navy officer who asked too many questions at the buoy. Everything that happens next happens in daylight, in front of witnesses, on stone." She looked up at me, and the fever was still in her face, but underneath it was something harder and calmer than fever. "We made it to shore, Edmund. Now we make it matter."

Above us, in the surgery window, a lamp burned. Below us, the last of the flood slid past the harbor mouth, carrying the dark out to sea.

I climbed the courthouse steps.
