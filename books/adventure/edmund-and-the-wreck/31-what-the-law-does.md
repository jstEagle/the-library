## 31. What the Law Does

They came down on the courthouse like gulls on a dropped fish.

Edmund watched them from the steps, where the press had been pushed back behind a rope that hadn't been there yesterday. Three black motorcars idled at the quay, and out of them came men in grey suits with the particular smoothness of people who had never once been cold. Their shoes were wrong for the stones — too thin-soled, too clean — and they walked across the cobbles the way you'd walk across a deck you didn't trust, testing each step before committing your weight to it.

The lead one was called Mr. Fenwick. Edmund learned this because Fenwick introduced himself twice, once to Beatrix and once to the navy officer standing at the top of the steps with his cap under his arm and his face arranged into official blankness. Fenwick's handshake was dry as old rope. His eyes went over Edmund the way an assessor goes over a hull — checking for damage, estimating what it would cost to write him off.

"Mr. Underhill." He said the name correctly, which told Edmund someone had briefed him. "You've had quite a week."

"I've had five years," Edmund said. "The week was just when it all came up."

Something flickered behind Fenwick's glasses — not amusement, exactly. Recognition of a man who was going to be more trouble than he looked. "Perhaps we could speak somewhere quieter."

"There's nowhere quieter than here," Edmund said. The harbor wind came off the water and moved through the crowd, carrying diesel and brine and the smell of mudflats at low tide. Behind him, through the courthouse doors, he could hear the inquiry clerks moving furniture, setting up the room where the next three days of his life would be decided by men who'd never been below the surface in their lives. "Say what you've come to say."

Fenwick glanced at the press rope. At the navy officer. At Beatrix, who stood with her arm still in its sling, her collarbone knitting crooked because she'd refused the clinic, refusing anything the fleet touched. She was watching Fenwick with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she already knew the answer to.

"Very well." Fenwick folded his hands. "First, let me say that the fleet categorically disavows the actions of Mr. Aldous Vane. He acted alone, without authorization, in a manner wholly inconsistent with the fleet's values—"

"He rammed us," Edmund said. "With a dredge. In front of forty boats."

"—in a manner," Fenwick continued, as if Edmund hadn't spoken, "wholly inconsistent with the fleet's values. The board has accepted his resignation. Criminal proceedings against him are, of course, a matter for the authorities, and the fleet will cooperate fully." A small pause, the kind a diver takes before dropping down past the light. "Second. The fleet is prepared to offer you immunity, Mr. Underhill. Full immunity. For the salvage operation, for the explosives, for the obstruction of the dredge — everything. You walk away clean. No charges, no fines, no questions about how you spent five years diving a restricted wreck."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, your testimony concerns piracy only." Fenwick's voice stayed level, reasonable, the voice of a man explaining tide tables to a child. "Vane's assault on your vessel. The reward posted on Ms. Calloway. The interference with navigation. These are serious matters, and your account would be invaluable. What we ask is that you confine yourself to events *after* the sinking. The Grey Wren went down in 1919 in a storm. That is the historical record. It is not the subject of this inquiry."

"The storm record doesn't match the breach in her hull," Edmund said. "I've had my hands inside it. I know what a storm does to steel and what a charge does, and that breach was a charge."

Fenwick smiled, gently, the way you smile at a man who has just said something expensive. "Mr. Underhill. I understand you found documents. Documents can be forged. Old paper is old paper; provenance is everything. The fleet's archivists have reviewed photographs of the material and have significant concerns about authenticity." He spread his hands. "Whereas what happened last week — the dredge, the reward, the gunfire at the picket — that is documented, witnessed, undeniable. Testify to that, and you are untouchable. Testify to the rest, and you become a man making claims about events seventy years dead, supported by papers no court has authenticated, against institutions with the finest counsel money buys. Do you see the difference? One path makes you a victim. The other makes you a crank."

* * *

They put the offer in writing, because lawyers always do, and the paper itself was a lesson. Edmund read it on the courthouse steps while the gulls argued over something in the tideline. Immunity, comprehensive. Protection for Marisol, for Danny Quill, for every harbor boat that had formed the wall. Even protection for Beatrix — the fleet would drop its countersuit over the expedition's permits, eat the costs, let her walk away whole.

It was a good offer. That was the thing about it that sat in his chest like swallowed ballast. It was genuinely, carefully, expensively good. Somewhere in a tall office a team of men had worked out precisely what Edmund Underhill's silence cost, and they were willing to pay it, and the number was generous.

"They're buying the sinking," Beatrix said beside him. She hadn't touched the paper. "Not you. They don't care about you. You're the delivery mechanism. They're purchasing the version of history where thirty-one men drowned in a storm instead of being locked below decks and sent to the bottom with a hole blown in their ship."

"You don't know they were locked below."

"No," she agreed. "But the letters were in the strongroom, Edmund. Families' letters, undelivered, sealed in with the confession. Why would the company seal correspondence from drowned crewmen's families in a strongroom unless the correspondence was evidence?" Her voice was quiet, stripped of argument. She wasn't trying to convince him. She was just laying the facts out on the table between them like tools, the way you lay out dive gear before a descent, so that whatever you needed was within reach. "I'm not going to tell you what to do. I told you that on the mudflats and I meant it."

He thought about the mudflats. The searchlight sweeping overhead, her voice steady in the dark, talking to keep his breathing even. *Tell me about her,* she'd said, meaning the wreck. And he had, there in the stinking cold mud with death sliding past fifty yards off, he had talked about the Grey Wren like she was a person, because by then she was.

"What happens to Vane either way?" he asked.

Fenwick had answered that one already, on the steps: Vane would face charges for the collision, for the reward, for the rest of last week's theater. The fleet would not obstruct it. They might even fund his defense — a defense designed, Edmund understood now, to keep Vane talking only about last week. A trial about a dredge and a skiff. Nothing earlier. Nothing about 1919.

And Vane, Edmund suspected, would take that deal. Because Vane believed in order, and a trial confined to recent events preserved order. Vane had told him so on the dredge's deck, offering him everything — partnership, legitimacy, his brother's name cleared, officially acknowledged, a pension carved out of fleet money with fleet apologies attached. Edmund had almost taken it. He'd stood there with the case in his hand and wanted it with a hunger that frightened him, because the acknowledgment of Joseph Underhill was the one thing five years of miles and fathoms had never bought him.

Beatrix hadn't argued then either. She'd asked him one question: *what would your brother have wanted surfaced?*

He still didn't have a full answer. But he knew what Joseph had died of. Not the sea. The sea was honest. Joseph had died of a company deciding its schedule mattered more than its divers' air, and then deciding its reputation mattered more than its diver, and paying the family off in an office that smelled of varnish while a junior clerk wrote *misadventure* in a ledger. Misadventure. As if the river had acted alone. As if the river had ever once acted alone in the history of that coast.

* * *

The clerk called them in at two o'clock, and the courtroom smelled of floor wax and cold tea. The inquiry commissioner was a retired admiralty man named Hargreaves with a face like a mooring bollard — nothing about it gave, but you could tie things to it. The fleet's counsel filled two benches. Beatrix's side filled one and a half, mostly harbor people who'd come up from the moorings in their working clothes and sat among the pews like ballast, solid and silent.

Fenwick opened for the fleet with the elegance of a man laying out fine china. He did not deny the dredge. He did not deny the reward. He condemned them, warmly, thoroughly, and asked the commissioner to note that these acts were those of a single rogue officer since departed from the fleet's employ. Then he turned, smoothly, to the matter of scope.

"The commission's mandate," Fenwick said, "as I understand it, covers the events of the past fortnight. We submit that the 1919 loss of the Grey Wren falls outside it, and further, that evidence purporting to revise that event is unauthenticated and prejudicial."

Hargreaves looked at Beatrix. "Ms. Calloway. Your response?"

She stood, sling and all, and Edmund watched her do the thing she did best, which was to make a room feel smaller around her opponents. "Commissioner, the manifest and the founder's confession were recovered from a sealed strongroom aboard the wreck, behind a plate cut open in front of three witnesses whose statements you have. The paper is contemporary — the ink, the watermark, the binding, all consistent with 1919 stationery, per the analysis you commissioned Tuesday. If the fleet wishes to argue its own founders forged documents and then sank them in their own ship's safe, seventy years ago, in anticipation of this exact week — well. That would be the most remarkable salvage operation in maritime history, and I'd admire it."

A sound went through the benches — not laughter, exactly, but the pressure of it, held.

Hargreaves almost smiled. Almost. "The commission will hear testimony on the full circumstances of the wreck's loss," he said, and Fenwick's china arrangement developed a hairline crack you could see from the gallery. "Proceed."

* * *

They broke for the evening, and the lawyers descended properly then.

They came to the harbor in ones and twos, in hired cars, and Edmund understood that the real inquiry was happening now, in doorways and on quaysides, away from the commissioner's ears. Fenwick found him at Marisol's kitchen table, of all places — the fleet's counsel sitting with his hat on his knee under the drying nets, drinking tea Marisol poured him because Marisol poured tea for anyone who sat down, it was a reflex older than her grudges.

"Mr. Underhill. The offer stands, and I'm authorized to improve it." Fenwick laid a second paper beside the first. "Immunity for everyone, as before. Additionally: the fleet will fund a proper memorial for the crew of the Grey Wren. Stone, names, dedication ceremony, the full dignity. And a compensation fund for the descendants — administered jointly, we'd suggest, with Ms. Calloway's oversight."

Edmund set down his cup. "You'll pay the families."

"We'll compensate the families. Generously."

"But the sinking stays a storm."

"The sinking remains what the record says it is." Fenwick's voice didn't harden; that was the terrible skill of him. He sounded like a man discussing weather. "Mr. Underhill, consider what you actually want. You want the dead honored. We will honor them, in stone, publicly, with their names — which is more than any inquiry will do. You want the living provided for. We will provide. What you would give up is—" he paused, weighing words like lead shot, "—a version of events. Seventy years old. Against which we would give you everything you have spent five years wanting."

Marisol, at the stove, stopped stirring.

"And my brother?" Edmund said. "Joseph. Fleet job, eleven years back. Air line failure, covered as misadventure. Does the memorial stone cover him?"

Fenwick's composure held, but Edmund saw the small recalibration behind the glasses, the assessor finding a flaw in the hull he hadn't charted. "That predates my brief."

"It predates everyone's brief," Edmund said. "That's the whole point. Everything inconvenient predates somebody's brief. The sinking predates yours. My brother predates mine. You're offering me a law that only reaches backward far enough to be cheap."

"Law always reaches backward," Fenwick said quietly, and for one moment the salesman fell away and something tired looked out of him, something that had perhaps once been a young lawyer who believed in things. "That is both its nature and its limit. It cannot raise the dead, Mr. Underhill. It can only decide what the living owe them. We are offering to owe them a great deal."

"And asking them to have drowned honestly."

Fenwick gathered his papers. At the door he paused. "The offer expires when the commission reconvenes. Nine o'clock." He put his hat on. "For what it's worth — and I know it isn't much — I hope you refuse. A lawyer hopes to win. A man hopes otherwise."

Then he was gone into the evening, and the harbor sounds came in behind him — halyards ticking against masts, the fleet's dredge groaning at its anchorage where it waited under writ, the long sigh of the tide coming in over the flats.

* * *

Edmund walked out to the harbor mouth after dark, because that was where he did his thinking, and thinking was what he owed himself tonight.

The flood tide was running, gray-black water pouring through the narrows, and out past the light the Grey Wren's buoy-marked hulk sat where it had sat for seventy years, holding its dead, holding its paper, holding the truth the way a hull holds water — until someone cuts it open.

He thought about what the law could do. It could give him immunity. It could give the harbor boats protection, Danny's daughter her surgery through some settlement clause if he asked, Marisol's moorings security. It could put a stone on the quay with thirty-one names cut deep and hold a ceremony with a band. It could probably, if Beatrix rode it hard enough, wring out a compensation fund with real money in it.

What it could not do, on Fenwick's terms, was say what happened. And Edmund had come to understand — out there in the dark, with the current pulling at his ankles like a hand — that the saying was the whole of it. A stone with the wrong story underneath was just a lid. Compensation paid against a lie was hush money with flowers on it. His brother's ledger entry, *misadventure*, had been legal. Every word of it. The law had looked at Joseph's death and found nothing to object to, because the law saw only what was written down for it, and the fleet had owned the pen for a hundred years.

Justice outside the law, Beatrix had called it, back when it was just the two of them and a waterproof case. He'd thought she meant breaking rules. He saw now she meant something harder: insisting that the record itself be true, whatever the machinery of the law preferred to record. Salvage rights weren't really about ownership. They were about who got to write the wreck down.

Nine o'clock. He had until nine o'clock.

Behind him, up the hill, the courthouse windows were dark, but the harbor was not — lamps in Marisol's kitchen, lamplight on Danny's boat where Danny sat up late, lamplight in Beatrix's borrowed room where she was presumably drafting something merciless. Ordinary lights. People who had put their boats between him and a dredge without being asked, without any promise of immunity at all.

The tide turned below him, right on schedule, the way truth was supposed to. He stood until the ebb began to run, and then he went back up the hill to find Beatrix, because there was a question he needed to ask her before morning — not about the law, but about what you do with a truth once the law finally, grudgingly, agrees to hear it.

Her lamp was still burning. Through the window he could see her at the table, and across from her, hat in his hands, sat a man Edmund knew: the navy officer from the patrol boat, the one who had known more than he said.

Edmund climbed the stairs and reached for the door.
