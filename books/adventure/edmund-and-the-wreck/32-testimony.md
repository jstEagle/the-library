## 32. Testimony

The courthouse had been a customs house once, back when the river still paid taxes to a crown nobody remembered. You could see it in the bones of the place — the high windows meant for counting cargo, the iron rings bolted into the floor where bales had been tied down against the tide of men. Now the rings held nothing, and the room filled instead with chairs, and lawyers, and the particular silence of people waiting to hear something they already suspected.

Edmund sat with his hands flat on his knees and watched the light come through the east glass in long amber bars, moving across the floorboards at exactly the speed the morning moved everywhere else. It was strange, he thought, how the world kept its own schedule regardless. The flood would be turning at the harbor mouth right now. Somewhere out on the water the Grey Wren lay where he'd left her, lighter by one manifest, heavier by everything that manifest meant.

Beatrix sat beside him, her arm still bound in its sling, collarbone knitting crooked under the plaster like a river changing course around an obstruction. She wore a coat that had been pressed for her by someone at Marisol's — pressed badly, over a stove, the way harbor women did things — and she looked, Edmund thought, like what she was: a woman who had been drowned halfway and come up anyway.

"You don't have to say it all," she murmured, not looking at him. Her eyes were on the commissioners' bench, three men and a woman behind a rail of dark oak, the navy officer from the patrol boat standing at parade rest along the wall as though he'd been nailed there. "The claim is yours. If you keep it, you keep it. Nobody in this room will think less."

"Nobody in this room thinks anything yet," Edmund said. "That's the trouble."

* * *

They'd come downriver two days ago under escort — actual navy escort, which was its own kind of joke, the same service whose buoy he'd spent five years diving beneath now running picket for him like he was ammunition. The harbor boats had followed as far as the narrows, then turned back, Marisol's skiff last, her hand raised in something between a wave and a salute. He hadn't looked back after that. Looking back was for people who thought they might not return.

The night before the inquiry they'd slept in a boarding house above a chandlery that smelled of tar and old rope, and Edmund had lain awake listening to the tide slap the pilings and rehearsed nothing. That was the difference between him and Beatrix, he'd decided somewhere around the third bell. She rehearsed. She had arguments filed away like charts, each one folded to its crease. He just knew what he knew, the way a diver knows the weight of his own belt, and when the moment came he would either say it or he wouldn't.

In the morning there had been lawyers. Fleet lawyers, four of them, gray as gulls, waiting on the courthouse steps with faces arranged into sympathy the way a wreck is arranged into salvage — everything useful stripped, everything dangerous sunk. One of them, a soft-voiced man named Pell who smelled of bay rum and money, had taken Edmund's elbow gently, the way you take the elbow of a man you're steering.

"Mr. Underhill. A word before you go in." And he'd laid it out plain, no insult intended, none taken: testify to the piracy, the dredge, the collision, Danny Quill crushed at his wheel holding the line — testify to all of it, name Vane if he liked, and the fleet would stand aside. Immunity. Protection. But the sinking itself, 1919, the scuttling, the founders — leave that water uncharted. It was old. It was complicated. It involved estates and trusts and families who were, after all, only heirs to things done before they were born.

"And my salvage claim?" Edmund had asked.

Pell had smiled. "Yours entirely. First raising, uncontested. The manifest case comes to you as property, Mr. Underhill. What you do with your property afterward is, of course, your own affair." A pause, delicate as a hook set in a fish's lip. "Though property can be purchased. At fair value. Very fair."

Edmund had looked past him at the river, brown and patient, going about its business. "I'll let you know," he said, which was true, and walked inside.

* * *

The presiding commissioner was a woman named Hargrave with a voice like a hatch cover closing. She read the terms of the inquiry aloud — the loss of the expedition, the death of Beatrix's crew, the collision in the narrows, the conduct of Salvage Foreman Aldous Vane — and then she looked at Edmund over her spectacles and asked him to state his name and trade for the record.

"Edmund Underhill. Salvage diver."

"Mr. Underhill, you are here voluntarily?"

"I am."

"You hold a salvage claim arising from the recovery of certain documents from the vessel known in these proceedings as the *Grey Wren*?"

Here it was, then. Sooner than he'd expected. The whole room shifted slightly, the way water shifts when something big moves underneath it — not visibly, but every man in it feeling the pressure change. The fleet lawyers leaned in. Pell uncapped a pen he didn't need.

"That's correct," Edmund said. "I do."

"And you understand that this commission has no authority over questions of salvage title, which lie with the admiralty court—"

"I understand it fine," Edmund said. "But I'd like to give mine up anyway. If I may. While everyone's listening."

Hargrave's eyebrows went up a quarter inch, which from her was an exclamation. "You wish to *relinquish* a claim of that value? Voluntarily?"

"Yes, ma'am."

There was a sound from the gallery — not a gasp, harbors don't gasp, but a long collective exhale, like the river deciding something. Edmund found Marisol's face in the benches, third row, arms crossed, chin up, watching him with an expression he couldn't read and didn't try to. Beside her sat Danny, propped on a cane, ribs strapped, face the color of old rope but eyes clear. He'd come off his sickbed for this. Edmund had told him not to. Danny had come anyway, because that was Danny: he never did what you told him until after he'd done it.

"State for the record," Hargrave said slowly, "the nature of the claim you are relinquishing."

"The manifest of the *Grey Wren*," Edmund said. "Raised by me from the strongroom on the flood tide, six days ago. Plus the letters that were with it, and the confession. All of it. I'm giving it to the record. To this inquiry. To whoever wants to read it out loud."

"Why?" It was Pell, on his feet, silk-smooth, genuinely puzzled — and that genuine puzzlement was the most honest thing Edmund had heard from the fleet in five years. "Mr. Underhill, that claim is worth — conservatively — more than you would earn in forty years of diving. Why would you surrender it?"

Edmund considered the question honestly, because it deserved honesty. He thought about miles. Five years of them, paid out hand over hand into cold black water, measured not because he loved measuring but because measuring kept other thoughts down. He thought about his brother, and the company letter that had called the accident *regrettable operational circumstances*, and the check that came with it, and the way the check had felt in his hand — heavy, official, worthless. Money was how the fleet said *be quiet*. He'd taken their quiet once. Everyone in the harbor had taken it, in one form or another; that was what the moorings cost, what the debt cost, what the buoy cost. And the thing about being paid to be quiet was that eventually you couldn't hear yourself anymore.

"Because I've been paid to stay quiet before," Edmund said. "By your principals. Not you personally, maybe, but the same money, the same hands. They paid me triple to guide their dredges upriver and bury whatever Miss Calloway found. I said no. Now they're offering me a fortune to keep what I found. It's the same offer wearing different clothes, Mr. Pell, and I've finally learned to recognize the tailor." He looked at the commissioners. "The manifest isn't mine. It never was. It belongs to thirty-one people who drowned in 1919 because the men who owned their ship decided they were cheaper dead than alive. Their families wrote them letters. Those letters went down with them. I'm just the man who happened to be holding the rope when they came back up. You don't get to buy the rope."

* * *

Then they swore him in, and the real work began.

He testified for four hours. He gave them the radio transmission cut mid-word, and the harbor master's order to forget it, and the fleet agent with the soft voice offering triple pay who had let slip the word *handled*. He gave them the flooded camp, the slashed tent, the dive slate with half a message scratched into it. He gave them the culvert, and Beatrix feverish with her broken collarbone, and the waterproof case she'd guarded with her good arm like a mother bird. He gave them the arithmetic of tides — five, then four, then three — until the numbers themselves became witnesses, ticking down in the record like a clock nobody could stop.

He gave them the descent. This was the part where his voice changed, and he felt it change and didn't fight it, because some things should sound like what they were. The bad visibility. The snagged lines. The breach in the hull that matched no storm in any log he'd ever read — torn outward, not inward, steel peeled back from the inside like something escaping. The sealed strongroom door, brass-bound, intact, waiting fifty years in the dark for someone with a reason to open it.

"When I put my hand on that door," Edmund said, "what did I feel?" Commissioner Hargrave had asked him to describe it, and he'd stood there in the well of the courtroom trying to find words for a thing that lived below words, down where divers live. "Cold," he said finally. "And then not cold. It's hard to explain. Down there everything is pressure and dark and your own heartbeat, and then you touch something a man built, and touched, and locked, and walked away from knowing what it held — and for half a second the water feels *occupied*. Like swimming through someone's unfinished sentence. I've dove that wreck five years and never felt it. That day I felt it. The ship knew what it was carrying."

The fleet lawyers objected to that — speculation, atmosphere, not evidence — and Hargrave sustained it, and Edmund almost smiled, because of course they objected. Of course. The whole crime had been committed in exactly that register: the register of things that were true but not provable, feelings that didn't count, dead men who couldn't testify. They'd built a company on the difference between what happened and what could be entered into evidence. But the paper couldn't object. The paper was already in the record.

Because Beatrix had opened the case that morning, publicly, on the courthouse steps, before the press and the cameras and the navy officer standing at parade rest pretending not to hear his own institution's name spoken like a curse. The manifest was read aloud. The confession was read aloud — Aldous Merrick Vane's grandfather, or great-grandfather, the ink gone brown, the handwriting steady right up until the last lines where it wasn't. And then the letters. Thirty-one letters from wives and mothers and sons, written to men who were already dead when the envelopes were sealed, read out into the amber morning while the river ran on outside, oblivious, faithful, keeping its own counsel the way it always had.

Edmund had listened to every word. He had not looked at the fleet lawyers. He had looked at Danny, who had gone very still at the fourth letter, and stayed still through the rest, and Edmund had understood without being told — understood the way you understand a current shift, by the feel of it — that something in that stack of names belonged to Danny too. That would come later. Everything came later. That was the tide's whole lesson.

* * *

Cross-examination began after the recess, and Pell took it, and he was good, Edmund granted him that. Good the way a snag is good at catching lines.

He tried the money first. *You were offered triple pay by the fleet and refused — why?* Because it was wrong. *You refused three times your annual wage on principle?* Yes. *Yet you accepted fleet wages for five years to dive a restricted wreck.* Yes. *So your principles are recent.* No, Edmund said. My principles are old. My courage is recent. There's a difference, and the difference has a body count.

He tried the loyalty next. *You were raised by the harbor family. The harbor owes the fleet its moorings. Isn't it true you acted against the interest of the people who fed you?* And here Edmund paused, because Marisol was in the room, and because the question was a knife dressed as a concern. "The fleet owns our moorings the way a current owns a swimmer," he said. "It doesn't follow that the swimmer loves the current. We stayed quiet because quiet was what we could afford. I'm testifying because I've learned what quiet costs. Ask the thirty-one."

He tried Beatrix last, obliquely, delicately — *Miss Calloway led an unauthorized commercial expedition into restricted waters, did she not? An illegal dive?* — trying to make her lawlessness the frame for everything, so that the truth would arrive pre-tainted. And Edmund felt the old anger come up in him, the deep-water anger, the kind that doesn't boil but presses, and he answered carefully:

"She broke a regulation. Your principals broke thirty-one necks. When this inquiry is ready to weigh those on the same scale, I'll wait."

Hargrave let that stand. Pell sat down. Outside, through the high windows, the light had gone from amber to white, meaning noon, meaning the flood was making hard at the harbor mouth, meaning the world was still keeping its schedule.

* * *

It ended the way such things end — not with thunder but with paperwork. Hargrave announced the commission would sit in closed session, that further witnesses would be called, that the documents entered into evidence would be preserved under seal pending findings. Under seal. Edmund heard the phrase and felt the old cold come back, the strongroom cold, the sense of doors that lock from the outside.

But then Beatrix rose — uninvited, unsworn, sling and all — and her voice carried the way voices carry when they've been used to wind and engines. "Under seal," she said, "means sealed from the public. It does not mean sealed from history. There are thirty-one families on that manifest, madam commissioner, and I have their addresses, because I spent two years finding them. Whatever this commission decides, they will know what was done to their grandparents. The reading on the steps this morning was not evidence. It was a promise."

Nobody stopped her. Nobody dared, quite. Hargrave wrote something down.

Afterward, on the steps, the press broke around them like water around a piling, and Pell materialized one last time at Edmund's shoulder, bay rum and defeat, though he hid the defeat well. "My clients note your testimony with interest," he said. "And wish me to convey that the offer regarding your — former claim — remains open. Sentiment fades, Mr. Underhill. Value doesn't."

"Tell them sentiment is the only thing that doesn't," Edmund said. "Everything else rusts. I dive for a living; I'd know."

Pell left. The crowd thinned. And then, at the bottom of the steps, a car that had no business looking so ordinary drew up at the curb, and a man got out of it who was not a lawyer, and the crowd noise changed key.

Aldous Vane stood at the foot of the courthouse steps with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral, and he looked up at Edmund with an expression Edmund had seen once before — years ago, on the face of the fleet supervisor who had handed him the check for his brother, regret and relief fighting openly behind the eyes, both losing.

"They're meeting tonight," Vane said, loud enough for the nearest reporters to hear, calm enough to make it strange. "My board. About me." He climbed one step, two, unhurried, a foreman inspecting weather damage. "I've been in that industry thirty years, Mr. Underhill, and I want to tell you something while I still outrank the men telling me to shut up." He reached into his coat, and half the courtyard flinched, and what came out was a leather folder, worn soft at the corners, thick as a chart book. "Order requires the past stay buried. I believed that my whole life. I dispatched a dredge on that belief." His jaw worked. "I've spent today listening to thirty-one letters. And I find I can no longer say what my belief is *for*."

He held the folder out. Reporters surged. Beatrix's hand closed on Edmund's sleeve.

"This is what I was going to burn tomorrow," Vane said. "Decide fast."
