## 34. Danny's Bill

The hospital smelled of carbolic and river water, which was to say it smelled of the fleet — of everything the fleet had ever touched and never quite cleaned. Danny Quill lay in a narrow bed on the third floor with his left arm strapped to his chest like cargo lashed down for heavy weather, and he counted the ceiling tiles because counting was what divers did when the body wasn't theirs to command anymore.

Forty-one tiles. He'd counted them eleven times now. The number never changed, and there was a strange comfort in that. Numbers held still. People didn't.

The blow had taken him across the ribs and through the shoulder, a grinding crunch he felt more than heard over the scream of the dredge's hull scraping past, and then the cold had come up around him like a hand closing. He remembered holding the wheel. He remembered Edmund's face going away from him downriver, small and getting smaller, Beatrix's arm outstretched. He remembered thinking: *this is the bill coming due*, and being surprised at how calm he felt about paying it.

He'd been running tabs his whole life. Everyone in the harbor ran tabs — with the chandlery, with the fleet clinic, with each other. But some debts you don't run up yourself. Some debts are inherited, passed down like a boat's papers or a bad knee, signed for by men long dead and collected from men not yet born.

His brother's name was Aloysius Quill. Nobody living called him that; they'd called him Wish, because as a boy he'd wished on everything — stars, pennies, gulls, the wake behind the ferry — and because their mother said wishing was the only prayer a poor family could afford. Wish had been twenty-three years old in 1919, an able seaman aboard a coastal freighter out of Greyharbor, signing articles under a company name that no longer appeared anywhere except in the fine print of insurance ledgers.

The *Grey Wren*.

Danny had grown up with the name the way you grow up with a scar — knowing it's there, not remembering the cut. Their mother had kept one photograph: Wish in his shore clothes, hat tipped back, grinning like the river belonged to him. On the back, in her handwriting gone brown with age: *Lost with all hands, October 1919. Storm.*

Storm. Even as a boy Danny had known the word didn't fit. His mother had known it too. She'd gone to the company offices three times, hat in her hands, asking how a storm could sink a ship in weather the pilot boats had logged as moderate. The third time, a clerk had slid an envelope across the counter — compensation, final settlement, signature required — and looked at her with something that wasn't quite pity and wasn't quite threat, and she'd signed, because there were two boys at home and winter coming, and grief doesn't keep the stove lit.

She never spoke of Wish again. But every October, on the anniversary, she'd walk down to the harbor mouth at slack water and stand there until dark, and Danny would stand with her, and neither of them would say a word, and the river would move beneath them carrying whatever it carried.

* * *

They let him have visitors on the fourth day, and the first was not who he expected.

Marisol came in with her mother's posture and her own stubbornness, set a jar of fish stew on the bedside table without asking whether he wanted it, and looked him over the way she'd look over a hull — checking for damage below the waterline.

"You held the wheel," she said.

"Someone had to."

"Edmund says the dredge would've run you down clean if you'd flinched."

"Dredges don't flinch either." He tried a grin and found half of it worked. "How is he?"

"Grieving Danny Quill, apparently. Keeps looking at your empty chair at Marisol's table like it might explain itself." She sat down on the edge of the bed, careful of the strapped arm. "Beatrix sits with him sometimes. They don't talk much. It's better than talking."

"And the manifest?"

"Read aloud on the courthouse steps with the whole quay listening. You were still under the ether." Her voice did something complicated on the last words. "You should have heard it, Danny. The names. All forty-one names read out, and half the harbor knew someone who knew someone, and old Mrs. Tarnow fainted dead away when they read her grandfather's name, and when they got to—" She stopped. Looked at him. "You know whose name they read."

He knew. He'd known since the night on the mudflats, since he'd stood in the shallows with his camera full of photographs he'd told himself were insurance and understood, finally, that they were something else entirely — a man trying to price a debt so he wouldn't have to feel its weight.

"Aloysius Quill," Danny said. "Able seaman. Lost with all hands."

"Lost with all hands," Marisol repeated softly. "Except one hand came back. Sitting right here eating my mother's stew."

"I haven't eaten it yet."

"You will. My mother made it. Refusing is not an option she recognizes."

* * *

The second visitor came on the sixth day, and this one Danny had been expecting the way you expect weather.

Edmund came in quiet, the way he did everything, filling the doorway first and then the room by degrees, like tide into a basin. He looked terrible in the way that meant he hadn't slept, and he carried a paper bag that smelled of hot bread, and he set it down next to the stew and then just stood there, hands in his pockets, looking at Danny with an expression Danny had spent thirty years learning to read and still couldn't, quite.

"You're supposed to be resting," Edmund said.

"So are you."

"I rest underwater. It's the only place nothing follows me."

Danny laughed, and the laugh pulled at the shoulder, and he stopped laughing. Edmund crossed the room in three steps and eased him back against the pillows with those salvage-diver's hands, gentle as a man handling something raised off the bottom after a long time down.

"Lie still, you great fool."

"Took a dredge hit for you," Danny said. "Least you can do is let me insult you comfortably."

Edmund sat down in the chair by the window. For a while neither of them spoke. Below, the river ran silver in the late light, and somewhere out on it a work barge thumped and clanged, and the sound came up through the hospital walls like a heartbeat through a hull.

"The fund wrote to me," Edmund said at last. He said it carefully, the way he said everything that mattered, laying each word down like a diver laying line in poor visibility. "The crews' descendants' fund. It was set up yesterday — Beatrix's lawyers, the inquiry's interim order, money released from the frozen estate accounts. First disbursements went out this morning." He paused. "Your daughter's surgery is paid. Fully. At any surgeon she chooses, here or upcountry. There's a letter for you in your coat."

Danny looked at him for a long moment.

"I know about the letter," he said. "It came this morning. I had them read it twice because I thought the nurse was making it up."

"And?"

"And I cried like a child and told the nurse it was the morphine." He turned his head toward the window so Edmund couldn't see his face properly. "Nellie sees Doctor Ashworth Monday. Ashworth. Not the clinic butcher with his queue and his clipboard. Ashworth, who fixed the harbormaster's hip." His voice cracked on the name and he let it. "You know what the fleet clinic quoted me? Before? Eleven years of harbor wages. Eleven years, and Nellie growing worse every winter, and me taking fleet jobs I hated because the pay was the only pay, and telling myself it was for her—"

"It was for her."

"It was for her," Danny agreed, "and it was also a chain, and I knew it was a chain, and I wore it anyway because chains are easier than choices." He wiped his eyes with his good hand, roughly, angry at them. "And then a hundred-year-old piece of paper surfaces off the bottom, and inside a week my girl's surgery is paid by men who've been dead since before my grandmother was born. You want to talk about tides, Edmund? That's a tide. That comes round once."

Edmund was quiet. Then: "Wish," he said.

Danny looked at him sharply. "Who told you?"

"Beatrix found the crew list weeks ago. She's been cross-referencing harbor families since the courthouse. She said—" Edmund hesitated, and Danny realized with a strange floating feeling that Edmund Underhill, who feared conversation the way other men feared deep water, was doing it anyway, deliberately, for him. "She said to tell you that the letters in the strongroom included one addressed to a Mrs. Quill, Greyharbor. It was never delivered. It's in the inquiry's custody now, but she says you can read it whenever you want. It's from Wish. Written the week before the ship went down."

The room tilted very slightly. Danny gripped the blanket.

"He wrote to her?"

"He wrote to everyone. The letters were going to be evidence even then — Wish suspected something. Beatrix thinks he and two others kept copies of the loading orders, hid them in the strongroom against exactly what happened." Edmund's voice dropped. "He knew, Danny. Or he feared enough to act like he knew. He put the truth in a steel box and trusted the river to keep it, and the river kept it a hundred years, and we're the ones who finally opened it."

Danny stared at the ceiling tiles. Forty-one. Of course it was forty-one. Forty-one names read on the courthouse steps, forty-one families owed, and one of them his, the debt come home across a century to land on a hospital bed in the form of a letter and a surgeon's appointment.

"My mother waited her whole life for word," he said. "Stood at the harbor mouth every October like the river owed her an explanation. And the whole time—" His voice failed. He tried again. "The whole time the answer was sitting in the mud, twenty fathoms down, four miles upriver. Waiting for someone patient enough to go get it."

"Five years," Edmund said quietly. "I dove that wreck five years and never asked why the navy wanted it forgotten. You took fleet money to photograph a dying woman's rescuers and couldn't make yourself send the pictures. We were both keeping faith with something before we knew what it was."

"That's either very wise or very stupid."

"With us it's usually both."

* * *

They talked until the light went copper and then grey, about small things mostly — the harbor, the boats, whether Marisol's mother's stew could legally be classified as a weapon — and it was only when the nurse came to chase Edmund out that Danny caught his sleeve.

"There's a bill," Danny said. "I want it settled proper. Not the surgery — that's settled, that's grace. The other bill. The mudflats. The photographs. The night I stood there with a camera and a choice and let you both walk away on trust I hadn't earned."

Edmund considered him from the doorway, silhouetted against the corridor light.

"What do you figure it costs?"

"Work," Danny said. "Whatever needs doing. Whatever nobody else will do. When the *Grey Wren* gets raised — and she will, the inquiry's already talking about it — I want to be on the crew. Not for pay. For the names. I want to be there when they read them off the quayside, and I want my hands on the lines that bring my brother's ship into the light."

Something moved through Edmund's face then, slow as current under ice — and Danny understood that he had just offered the one thing Edmund valued above money, above safety, above sense: company on the bottom. A partner on the line.

"She goes up in six weeks," Edmund said. "Spring tide, calm weather, navy escort and half the harbor watching. Beatrix is organizing it. Salvage rights are hers, but she's been asking after good men." A pause, and the ghost of the old grin. "I may have mentioned a name."

"You mentioned a name."

"Best diver I ever worked with," Edmund said. "Even if he does cry at hospitals."

"Get out," Danny said, laughing, holding his ribs, "get out of my ward, you barnacled hermit, before I throw something—"

But Edmund was already gone, quiet as tide, and Danny lay back against the pillows and listened to the river working below the windows, and took out the letter from his coat pocket, and read it again by the last of the light.

*Dear Mother [Wish had written, in a schoolboy's careful hand grown hurried at the edges], If anything happens to us it will not be the weather, whatever they tell you. The holds are wrong. The weights are wrong. Mr. Ellery has shown me the papers and I have put copies where no storm can reach them. Do not sign anything they bring you. Make them say her name out loud. Make them say all our names.*

*Your loving son, Wish.*

Below the window, far downriver, the channel buoy bell rang once in the evening breeze — and Danny Quill, able seaman's brother, debtor across a century, closed his eyes and began, at last, to count something other than ceiling tiles.

Six weeks. Spring tide. He'd be on the water when they brought her up.
