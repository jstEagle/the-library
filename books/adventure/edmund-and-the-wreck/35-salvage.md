## 35. Salvage

They raised her on a morning tide so calm the river looked like poured pewter.

Edmund stood on the deck of the salvage barge *Honest Measure* — a name that had made Beatrix laugh out loud when she'd chartered it — and watched the cranes take up their slack one inch at a time. Four of them, positioned at the corners of a lattice of new chain that divers had spent three weeks threading through and under the Grey Wren's hull. Not the fleet's brutal dredge method, not the great clawing buckets that tore a wreck into scrap and silt. This was slow work. Respectful work. The kind of work you did when what you were lifting off the bottom was not cargo but people.

The air bags went down first, forty of them, each one folded flat as a dead man's shirt until the pumps filled them and they bloomed in the green dark like enormous pale flowers. Edmund had helped position half of them himself, working from memory now instead of charts. He knew the Wren's hull the way he knew his own hands: every rivet line, every fathom of her keel, the long tear along her port side where the water had come in on the night of March 1919 and never left.

"Stand by," called the lift master, a broad woman named Okafor who had worked honest wrecks from here to the Sound and treated every one of them like a funeral procession.

"Standing by," said everyone.

The tide was at half flood. Beatrix had insisted on that. *She should come up on a rising tide,* she'd said, *not an ebb. She's been going down for a hundred years. She can go up for once.*

So they waited for the water to begin its slow climb, and then Okafor gave the signal, and the pumps roared, and the Grey Wren began to rise.

Edmund felt it before he saw it — a shudder through the barge's deck plates, a deep groan transmitted up four hundred feet of chain, a sound like something vast clearing its throat after a century of silence. Then the river itself changed. A ring of ripples spread outward from the lift point, smooth and deliberate, as if the water were bowing away from her. And then the mud let go — a great brown bloom of displaced silt rising around the hull like breath — and the first part of the Grey Wren broke the surface.

Her stern rail. Just that, at first: a corroded iron rail, furred with weed, streaming water, catching the early light.

Nobody cheered. That had been agreed beforehand, all of them, without anyone having to say it aloud. Danny Quill took his cap off. Marisol, standing beside him at the rail of the harbor skiff that had come out uninvited and welcome, crossed herself with a hand that wasn't quite steady. Old Tomas from the chandlery, who had sworn for forty years he'd never set foot on open water again, was there too, gripping the gunwale with both fists.

And then more of her came, foot by foot, yard by yard, as the tide lifted and the air bags held and the cranes kept their patient tension. Her stern rose first, then the long curve of her midsection, black-green with a century of river growth, barnacled and beautiful in the way that only drowned things are beautiful. Her name was still legible beneath the fouling where a diver — Edmund, two weeks ago, with a soft brush and an hour of bottom time he'd paid for out of his own tank — had cleaned the letters by hand.

GREY WREN

Out of the water at last. Into the light she hadn't seen since the year the war ended and the men who owned her decided she was worth more dead than alive.

Edmund found he couldn't watch the whole thing. He looked away, out toward the harbor mouth, and his eyes were burning, and he let them burn, because there was no one on this water who would think less of him for it.

* * *

They brought her to the quay at slack water that evening, and the whole harbor came.

Not because anyone organized it. Because word travels in harbors the way tide does — silently, under everything, arriving everywhere at once. By the time the tugs nudged the Wren against the fenders at the old commercial berth, there must have been six hundred people on the stones. Fishermen in their oilskins. Dock families with children on their shoulders. The cooperatives' people, the ones who used to be fleet crews, wearing their new armbands. Even a few navy ratings, out of uniform, standing awkwardly at the back like men attending a funeral for someone they'd been ordered to dislike.

Beatrix stood on the quay steps with her arm still in its brace, her collarbone healed crooked — the navy surgeon had offered to re-break and reset it properly, and she'd declined with a look that ended the conversation. She held a single sheet of paper. On it, in her own handwriting, were ninety-one names.

"The crew of the Grey Wren," she said, and her voice carried without any help, the way voices do when a crowd has gone completely still. "Sunk with her on the nineteenth of March, 1919. Deliberately. By order of the men whose names are on the fleet's founding charter. They were told the ship was unsafe. Some of them signed on anyway because they needed the wage. Some of them were promised bonuses for the run. All of them were lied to."

She read the names.

She read them slowly, giving each one its full weight, and the harbor read them with her, murmuring the unfamiliar ones, and then — this was the thing Edmund would remember for the rest of his life — the murmuring changed. Somewhere around the thirtieth name, an old woman near the fish market began to say *yes* quietly after each one, and then others picked it up, and by the sixties it was a response, a congregation answering: *yes. yes. yes.* Present. Still present. A hundred years drowned and still counted.

"...Aldous Quill," Beatrix read.

Danny made a sound beside Edmund, low, like a man taking a blow he'd been bracing for since the courthouse. His brother's grandfather. Edmund put a hand on his shoulder and left it there.

"...Thomas Underhill."

Marisol's hand found Edmund's other arm. He hadn't known his brother's name was on the Wren's crew list until the manifest came up out of the strongroom — no, that wasn't true, and he made himself be exact about it even now, even in grief. He had known. He had known for five years, ever since the night he'd pulled the fleet's employment ledger from a flooded records office on a contract job and found Thomas Underhill listed as a deckhand on a vessel the fleet's own charts marked only as *restricted*. He had known, and he had said nothing to anyone, and he had gone diving on her grave five days a week for five years because it was the closest he could get to his brother without drowning too.

"Yes," said the harbor, six hundred voices, and Edmund's among them, cracked and quiet.

When the last name was read, Beatrix folded the paper and held it up.

"These names go on the memorial stone. These names go in the schoolbooks. And these names—" she turned slightly, toward the navy officer standing at the edge of the crowd, the same one from the patrol boat, the one who had looked at Edmund that day with eyes that knew too much and said too little — "these names are going to have the inquiry's finding attached to them. Murder. Written plainly. No more *lost with all hands due to weather*. There was no weather that night but greed."

The officer didn't look away. After a moment, he removed his cap. Around him, one by one, so did the ratings.

The tide turned. The bells of the mission church rang the hour, and nobody moved for a long time after that either.

* * *

The Wren sat on the slipway cradle for eleven days while the archaeologists did their work — cataloguing, photographing, treating everything like evidence and relic both, which is exactly what it was. Edmund stayed away for nine of those days. He told himself it was because there was work to do at the harbor, moorings to repair, the cooperative's boats to service now that the fleet's monopoly was dissolving into a dozen small honest companies. All true. None of it the reason.

The reason was that he didn't know how to stand on her deck in daylight.

He had known her in the dark, in the cold, in the green silence where she had been his alone — his secret, his penance, his church. Five years of dives, and in all that time he had spoken to her. Not aloud, mostly. But he had talked to the Wren the way some men talk to God or to the dead, and she had answered in her way: the creak of her plates shifting with the tide, the sudden silver startle of a fish school in his torchlight, the long patience of a thing that waits.

Now she was out of the water, lit by work lamps, crawling with strangers in coveralls, and he could not make the two versions of her fit together.

On the eleventh day, Beatrix found him mending net on the quay and sat down beside him without asking, which was one of the hundred things he'd come to love about her.

"She's coming apart next week," she said. "Structurally. The corrosion's past saving. The conservators want to preserve sections for the museum — the stern, the strongroom bulkhead, the wheelhouse. The rest goes to scrap, respectfully." She paused. "There's a window tomorrow. Before the cutting starts. The site's closed to everyone else." She looked at him sideways. "I thought you might want your hour."

"My hour," Edmund repeated.

"You've been saying goodbye to that ship for five years, Edmund Underhill. I've watched you do it. You just never got to finish." She stood, brushed off her trousers, and added, walking away: "Flood tide's at seven. I'll hold the boat."

* * *

He went down at first light, alone, on scuba for once instead of surface-supplied, because this dive wasn't work and he wanted nothing between himself and the water but his own breath.

The flooded dry dock was still and green-gold, the morning sun coming down through it in long slanted columns, and the Grey Wren lay on the cradle below him like a cathedral fallen on its side. He descended along her port rail, past the cleaned letters of her name, and the visibility — miraculous, unnatural, the dock water settling after days of disturbance — let him see forty feet in every direction. He could see her whole. He had never seen her whole. For five years she had existed for him in fragments: a bulkhead here, a companionway there, a corridor of black water and torch beam. Now she was simply herself, entire, and smaller than he had imagined, and it broke his heart gently, the way you'd break bread.

He swam her length.

Through the broken section where the sea cocks had been opened — he'd found them in the final survey, opened by hand, the wheels still turned, the fingerprints of murder preserved in brass. Through the crew quarters, where the archaeologists had left the deck plates bare and he could see, in the arrangement of shadow and stain, where bunks had been, where men had slept and gamed and written letters home on the last night of their lives. Through the galley, where a single tin cup sat upright on a shelf, catalogued and tagged, waiting for its glass case.

At the strongroom bulkhead — the section they'd save, the steel plate with the ragged star-shaped hole Danny's charge had cut — he stopped, and hung in the water, and let the current of his own breathing carry him.

"Tom," he said. Aloud. The regulator muffled it; the bubbles carried it up anyway, in silver strings, toward the light.

His brother had been twenty-two. Edmund had been nineteen, and had begged to come along on that job, and Tom had laughed and said *not this one, little brother, this one's a milk run, you stay and mind the nets.* A milk run. The fleet's euphemism for a job they didn't want witnesses young enough to have consciences. Thomas Underhill had died in the flooding dark of a ship deliberately sunk, and the company had paid the family a settlement with a confidentiality clause, and their mother had signed it because the alternative was nothing, and Edmund had grown up inside that silence like a pearl grows inside a wound.

For five years he had dived this wreck believing he was keeping vigil. Standing watch over his brother's grave. It had felt like devotion. It had felt like the only honest thing left.

But hanging here now, in the clear gold light, looking at the whole of her at last, he understood what Beatrix had understood on the mudflats, what the harbor had understood on the quay, what even Vane in his ruined dignity had finally understood: that the vigil was over. That the watch had been kept. That the dead don't want guarding — they want naming. They want the *yes*, the answering voice, the name read aloud in daylight. Tom hadn't needed Edmund to haunt the place of his death. He had needed him to walk up the courthouse steps and say the truth into the light, and Edmund had done it, had burned his own salvage claim to do it, and it was finished.

"You can rest," he said to the water, to the ship, to the boy in the bunk he'd never find. "I've got it from here."

The bubbles rose. The light shifted. Somewhere far above, a gull cried, and the sound came down through the water thin and high, like a thread pulling him home.

He swam out along the rail one last time, trailing his fingertips over the fouled iron — not clinging, just touching, the way you touch a doorframe leaving a house you grew up in — and ascended slowly, watching the Wren grow small and whole and peaceful beneath him, a ship at anchor at last, her crew named, her cargo truth, her voyage over.

When he broke the surface, Beatrix was leaning on the boat's rail with two thermos cups, and she didn't ask him anything, and he loved her for it.

"Well?" she said finally, handing him the coffee.

"She's ready," Edmund said. And found that it was true, and that he was too.

* * *

The cutting crews started Monday. By Friday the Wren was sections and memory, and the museum took her stern and her wheelhouse and the strongroom plate with its star-shaped wound, and the scrap went to the cooperative foundry to be beaten into fittings for the new boats — harpoons of the old greed reforged into honest iron, Marisol said, and everyone pretended that wasn't poetry.

On Saturday evening, Edmund stood at the harbor mouth where the water ran deep and clean, watching the flood tide come in, and Beatrix came to stand beside him with her hands in her pockets and a look on her face he'd learned to read as the beginning of a proposal.

"So," she said. "I've got the survey contract for the northern coast. Honest work. Wrecks nobody died covering up. Bad coffee, worse weather, and a crew of eight, one of whom—" she glanced at him, "—I'm told can hold his breath longer than anyone on the river."

Edmund looked out at the water. Harbors are hard to leave. Every rope he'd ever tied was tied here; every mile he'd ever measured started from this shore.

"I'll want my own bunk," he said.

Beatrix smiled. "You'll want your own boat, knowing you."

"That too," Edmund admitted, and turned toward home, and the tide came in behind him like a promise kept.
