## 36. Anchorage

The scar on my collarbone has stopped aching in the mornings, which is how I know the season has turned. Pain keeps its own calendar better than any almanac; for four months it told me the weather before the gulls did, a dull barometric throb that woke me at slack water and reminded me, in case I'd somehow forgotten, that a man had carried me out of a drainage culvert on his back through freezing reeds while a fleet launch swept its searchlight over our heads.

Now the scar only speaks when I reach too far overhead, a small tug like a fish testing a line. I have decided to think of it as a reminder rather than a wound. Some things you want to remember.

I was standing on the quay outside what used to be the fleet's survey office — ours now, though the ink on the lease was barely dry and the sign still bore the ghost of the old letters beneath the new paint, CALL— showing through under CALLOWAY & UNDERHILL, COASTAL SURVEY like a bruise under skin. Danny had painted the new name himself, badly, the U leaning as if it were about to step off the board and walk into the river. Marisol had laughed until she had to sit down on a bollard. Edmund had looked at it for a long time without saying anything, which was his way of saying quite a lot.

That was three weeks ago. Since then we had surveyed eleven miles of channel, charted two unmarked shoals that had claimed boats for a century, and taken on one apprentice — a harbor boy of sixteen named Fen who could hold his breath longer than any diver I'd worked with and who worshipped Edmund with the mute, hopeless devotion of a dog watching a door.

And since then, Edmund had not once said yes.

* * *

He came to the office at dusk, as he always did, letting himself in without knocking because the door had never learned to be closed against him. I heard his boots on the stairs — he still climbed them slowly, favoring the knee the dredge's wake had wrenched when Danny took the wheel — and I kept my back to him, pretending to study the new chart of the narrows pinned above my desk.

"You've been standing there twenty minutes," he said.

"I'm working."

"You're waiting."

I turned. He filled the doorway the way he always did, not large so much as *present*, a man who had spent five years underwater learning how to take up space quietly. His hair was going grey at the temples early, salt and cold water doing their slow work, and there was mud from the day's survey dried on his forearms in pale crescents where his sleeves had been rolled.

"The Vane inquiry transcripts came," I said, and tapped the folder on my desk. "Final annex. The heirs are contesting everything, of course, but the judge let the crews' fund stand. The descendants get the pension rights. All of them. Backdated."

"I heard." He came to the window beside me instead of the desk, which meant he didn't want to talk about the fund. The river below was turning copper with the last of the light, the moored boats knocking gently against each other like old friends greeting after a long separation. "Marisol says the cooperatives want us to run their sounding work come spring. Forty miles of channel."

"Forty-two," I said. "I counted twice."

"That's a year's work."

"Two years, if we do it properly." I made myself turn and face him fully, because this was the conversation we had been circling for weeks the way a diver circles a snagged line, looking for the clean place to cut. "Edmund. Sit down."

"I'm fine standing."

"Sit down anyway."

He sat, perched on the edge of the chair like a man at a dock waiting for a boat he wasn't sure was coming. I pulled my own chair around so we were knee to knee, and outside the window a curlew called across the mudflats, that long rising note that always sounds to me like a question being asked by someone who already knows the answer.

"Here's what I have," I said. "A lease. A boat — the *Kestrel*, refitted, sound as anything Danny's ever built, which is saying something. A contract with the cooperatives. An apprentice who follows you around like a shadow with boots. And a name on the door that I put there without asking you, which I'll apologize for if you want me to, but I won't take off, because you earned it in a strongroom ninety feet down with your air running out."

His jaw worked. He looked at the window, at the river, at the place on the floor between his boots where the light from the doorway made a long thin parallelogram that was slowly sliding toward dark.

"And here's what I don't have," I went on, softer. "Your answer. You've been diving for us all autumn. You've drawn half the charts yourself. You sleep in the harbor and eat at Marisol's table and you've never once said the word *yes* out loud, and I've decided I'm too old and too nearly-drowned to keep guessing."

"It's not—" He stopped. Started again. "It's not that simple, Bea."

"Nothing worth having is. That's not an argument, it's a platitude. Give me something better."

He almost smiled. It was there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "Harbors are hard to leave," he said. "You wouldn't know. You're the kind of person who arrives."

"And you're the kind who stays," I said. "I know. I've watched you stay for five years on a wreck everyone else was paid to forget. Staying is what you do, Edmund. It's the finest thing about you. But staying isn't the same as belonging, and you've never once believed you were allowed the second one."

The curlew called again. Somewhere down the quay, Fen's laugh rang out, high and cracked, and a woman's voice — Marisol's, unmistakable even at distance — told him to mind the paint.

"My brother dove for the fleet," Edmund said at last, to the floor. "Eleven years. He thought he belonged there. They wrote him off as equipment loss inside a week, and the harbor took me in because there was nowhere else, and I've spent every year since measuring my life in rope and fathoms because fathoms don't lie to you and people do." He looked up. His eyes were the flat grey-green of deep water, and they held mine the way he held a line: steadily, without bravado. "If I say yes to this, I'm saying yes to people again. To charts that matter to somebody. To a name on a door. To—" he gestured vaguely upward, meaning the office, the town, the whole improbable architecture of the life I was offering him, "—to all of it. And everything I've ever said yes to got sunk."

"Not everything," I said. "You said yes to coming upriver for me. Six tides, a borrowed skiff, a patrol launch, a flooded camp, and a feverish woman with a broken collarbone who wouldn't let go of a case. You said yes to all of it before you knew why."

"That was different."

"Why?"

"Because—" He stopped. I watched him reach for the old answers — duty, debt, the harbor's honor — and find them worn through, like line that's been run over the same fairlead too many times. "Because it was you," he said finally, very quietly, as if the words might break if he handled them roughly. "Because you were in the reeds and nobody else was going."

The room was very still. Down on the water the tide had begun to make, the mooring lines creaking as the boats swung to meet it, and I thought: *there it is.* Not a declaration. Not a promise. Just a man admitting, in the smallest possible units, that he had already left the harbor he thought he couldn't leave — that he'd left it the night he rowed past the picket in the dark, and everything since had only been the long swim home.

"Edmund," I said. "I'm not asking you to stop being a man who stays. I'm asking you to stay *here*. With a chart that has your name on it and a crew that would follow you into a strongroom and an outfit that tells the truth because telling the truth is the whole business we're in." I reached out and put my hand over his where it gripped the arm of the chair, knuckles pale. "You've spent five years diving a grave. Come dive living water instead."

For a long moment he didn't move. Then his hand turned under mine — not pulling away, just turning, the way a man turns his palm up to catch rain — and he looked at our hands together and breathed out something that might have been a laugh or might have been the end of holding his breath for five years.

"Fen will want to know if he gets a raise," he said.

"He'll get a raise."

"Danny will want his cut renegotiated."

"Danny can want whatever he likes. He'll take it."

"And Marisol—" He stopped. His voice did something complicated. "Marisol set another plate tonight. She does it every night. Never says whose it is. Just sets it, and if I don't come she puts the food in a tin and leaves it on my step, and if I do come she pretends it was always for two."

"Have you ever asked her why?"

"No." He met my eyes. "I've been afraid the answer was pity."

"It's not pity," I said. "I've eaten at that table. Pity doesn't argue with you about tide tables for an hour. Pity doesn't threaten to feed your portion to the gulls if you mope. That woman has been setting a plate for family, Edmund, and the only person at that table who doesn't know it is you."

He bowed his head. I saw his shoulders move once, a single long shudder, the kind a man makes when he surfaces from a deep dive and the air is suddenly enormous. When he looked up again his eyes were wet and he made no apology for it, and I loved him for that — not the soft, sudden love of a moment, but the slower thing that had been building since a culvert in the reeds, since a voice hoarse with fever reading me tide arithmetic, since a hand hauling me up a muddy bank while the world tried to swallow us both.

"All right," he said.

"All right?"

"Yes." He said it the way he did everything — plainly, without ornament, a word dropped into deep water and allowed to sink to the bottom and rest there. "Yes. To the outfit. To the name on the door. To the plate." A breath. "To anchoring."

I squeezed his hand once and let go, because some moments you honor by not gripping them too hard, and stood, and went to the window where the last light was dying on the water. Below, the harbor lamps were coming on one by one along the quay, little pools of gold reflected in the black flood tide, and I could see Marisol's kitchen window glowing warm above the chandlery, and Fen's silhouette moving on the deck of the *Kestrel*, doing some evening task he'd invented purely to be near the boat.

"There's one more thing," Edmund said behind me.

"Oh?"

"The spring survey. Forty-two miles." He came to stand beside me, and his reflection in the dark glass overlapped mine, two figures merged into the river's image. "Where does it start?"

"At the harbor mouth," I said. "Working upriver."

"Upriver." He was quiet a moment. "Past the Grey Wren."

"She'll be raised by then. Respectfully. Properly. The crews' fund has hired the cooperatives to do it — our bid, if we want it." I turned to look at him. "Do we want it?"

He was silent for a long time, watching the water. When he answered, his voice had the quality it got underwater, on the radio, through the crackle and hiss — calm, and certain, and carrying further than it should.

"I want to be there," he said, "on the day they read the names. Every one of them. Out loud, on the quay, where the whole river can hear." He straightened, and something in his face settled, the way silt settles after a storm, leaving the water clear. "And afterward — I want to dive her one last time. Empty hull, clean water. There's a ghost down there that isn't mine, and I think it's time somebody told it the wait is over."

Outside, the flood tide turned the moored boats gently toward the sea and held them there, patient, ready, going nowhere until morning.

* * *

We walked down to Marisol's together as full dark came on, and neither of us spoke, because there was nothing left that needed saying and the silence itself was a kind of speech — the comfortable silence of two people who have been underwater together and know each other's breathing.

The kitchen smelled of bread and lamp oil and the particular brine of a house that has lived beside a river for a hundred years. Marisol took one look at us — at Edmund's face, at whatever was written on mine — and reached into the cupboard without a word and set down a third plate beside the second, and a fourth for Fen when he tumbled in smelling of pitch and triumph, and nobody remarked on the arithmetic, because in that house the plates had always known what the people were only now learning.

Edmund sat. He ate. And halfway through the meal, when Marisol teased him about the crooked U on the sign and Fen laughed so hard he choked on his bread, I watched Edmund Underhill laugh too — really laugh, from somewhere deep and long-unused, a sound like a wreck giving up its dead, like a bell rung under water, like a man dropping the weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was there.

Afterward, walking home along the quay under a sky rinsed clean of cloud, he stopped at the harbor mouth where the flood tide ran silver under the lamps, and stood a while looking out at the open water beyond the point.

"Bea," he said. "In the morning — before the survey work. There's a wreck two miles downcoast. Nothing important. A coaster, forty years down, nobody's claim, nobody's grief. Pretty as anything, Danny says, in the afternoon light."

I looked at him. He wasn't quite smiling, but the lines of his face had rearranged themselves around something that hadn't lived there before, and I understood that I was being offered something rarer than salvage rights or a name on a door.

"A dive," I said. "For no reason at all."

"For no reason at all," he agreed, and turned toward home, and for the first time since I'd known him, he walked like a man who knew exactly where that was.
