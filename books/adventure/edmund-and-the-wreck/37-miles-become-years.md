## 37. Miles Become Years

The first thing Edmund noticed was that he had stopped counting.

It happened without ceremony, the way most true things do. For five years — no, longer than that, if he was honest, all the way back to the day the fleet's launch had come back to the quay without his brother aboard — he had measured his life in units that could not lie to him. Miles of rope paid out and hauled back. Fathoms of cold water between him and whatever waited below. Tides, always tides, sixty-two of them to a month if you counted the small neaps, each one a tick of a clock that didn't care whether he lived in it or merely beside it. A man could survive almost anything, he'd learned, if he broke it into rope-lengths. Grief included. Especially grief.

But this morning, standing at the sink in the room above Marisol's boathouse with a chipped mug of tea going lukewarm in his hands, he realized he hadn't checked the tide table in three days. It hung on the nail by the door where it had always hung, the paper gone soft at the corners from years of damp, and it might as well have been a recipe for bread for all the attention he'd paid it. The Grey Wren sat raised and gutted on the marine railway, her bones open to the sky, her secrets long since spilled across a courthouse floor and into every newspaper from here to the capital. There was nothing down there anymore that needed him. Nothing that needed counting.

He drank the tea. He watched the light come up over the moorings, gilding the mastheads one by one like someone walking down a row of candles with a taper. And he thought: I don't know what today is.

The thought should have frightened him. For years the numbers had been the railing he held onto — so many fathoms since Tom died, so many dives since the last time anyone said his name gently, so many tides until the next job, the next payment, the next reason to keep breathing on schedule. Take away the numbers and what was left? Just a man in a room above a boathouse, with tea going cold and a whole unmeasured day opening out in front of him like floodwater.

What was left, it turned out, was the smell of frying onions coming up through the floorboards, and Marisol's voice carrying up the stairwell with the particular sharpness she saved for mornings when there was good news she refused to deliver kindly.

"Edmund Underhill! If you're awake up there, you're late, and if you're not awake, you're later!"

* * *

The kitchen behind the chandlery was the same kitchen that had fed him since he was seventeen years old and too hollowed-out by grief to argue about being taken in. Same scarred deal table. Same iron stove that Marisol's mother had cooked on before her, and her mother before that, its black surface seasoned by a century of fish stew and burnt porridge and, once, memorably, a whole leg of mutton that Old Nan had sworn was ruined and everyone else had sworn was the best thing they'd ever eaten. Same low ceiling darkened with smoke and steam, same window over the sink fogged even now, though it was only October and the stove had been lit barely an hour.

But there were differences, if you knew how to look. A second chair pulled up to the table that hadn't been there in the spring — a proper chair, not a crate, with a cushion on it that nobody would admit to having sewn. A jar of the good honey on the shelf, the kind that came from upriver farms, the kind the harbor had stopped buying years ago when the fleet's prices squeezed everything smaller than itself out of the market. A coil of new line hanging by the door, bright and unbleached, bought not because the old line was worn but because the old line had been part of a life that was over.

Marisol stood at the stove with her back to him, spatula in hand, and did not turn around.

"You're late," she said again.

"For what?"

"That's the spirit. Sit down."

He sat. The table was set for four. He looked at the fourth place — plate, cup, knife and fork laid straight, the way Marisol laid anything she cared about — and felt something move in his chest that he decided not to examine too closely, because examining things too closely had been his profession for five years and he was trying, these days, to be an amateur at everything.

"Four," he said.

"You can count after all."

"Who's the fourth?"

Marisol turned then, and her face was doing the thing it did when she was pleased and determined that no one should have the satisfaction of seeing it. "The Calloway woman rang the harbor set at seven this morning. From the survey office, the new one, the one she's renting off the Pilots' Guild." She flipped something in the pan with unnecessary violence. "She says she'll be down on the noon boat. She says—" and here Marisol's voice shifted, very slightly, into an imitation so accurate it could only have been rehearsed, "'—tell Edmund there's no obligation, but the tide's favorable and I've got charts that need checking and I'd rather check them with him than without him.'"

"She said that?"

"She said exactly that. Word for word. I've got a memory like a ledger, me." Marisol slid eggs onto two plates, then a third, then filled the fourth from the pan and covered it with an upside-down bowl to keep it warm. "No obligation," she repeated, setting his plate down hard enough to rattle the cutlery. "That woman has been saying 'no obligation' to you since the day she crawled out of a drainage culvert half-dead and you carried her three miles through reeds. You know what I think?"

"I expect you'll tell me."

"I think 'no obligation' is what people say when they mean the opposite and haven't worked up the nerve." She sat down across from him, tucked her napkin into her collar like a child, and pointed her fork at him. "And I think you've been up in that room for eleven days telling yourself you're thinking it over, and I think thinking it over looks a lot like hiding, and I think—"

"Marisol."

"—I think the eggs are getting cold," she finished, undeterred, "so eat."

They ate. Through the fogged window the harbor went about its morning: the ferry bell, the gulls arguing over the fish waste, old Pietro's grandsons hauling crab pots with the easy rhythm of boys who had grown up on a working quay and never doubted they'd work one themselves. Before the inquiry, before the fleet came apart, half those pots would have flown the fleet's colors. Now they flew whatever the boys liked. One of them, Edmund noticed, flew a scrap of faded canvas that might once have been part of an expedition flag, salvaged from some skip or other, run up a pot-buoy for no reason except that it was theirs to fly.

Nobody had thrown a party. That was the thing about this harbor that he'd understood only slowly, the way you understand deep water — not all at once but by degrees, by the pressure of it. When the verdicts came down, when the fleet's charter was dissolved and the dredges were sold for scrap and the founders' heirs discovered that a century of accumulated money buys excellent lawyers but cannot buy back drowned men, the harbor had not celebrated. There had been no bonfire on the shingle, no ringing of bells. What there had been was this: Nan's kitchen setting an extra place. Pietro repainting his ferry's name in fresh white. Boys flying scraps of expedition flag off their buoys. The whole harbor performing, quietly and without announcement, the ceremony of continuing.

Edmund had spent five years learning how a wreck tells the truth — how the hull remembers the storm, how the cargo records the greed, how the smallest fitting, a valve or a rivet, will testify when everything human stays silent. He had thought truth was a thing you raised. It had taken him until this autumn to learn it was also a thing you set a plate for.

* * *

After breakfast there was work, because there was always work, and because work was the language this harbor spoke when feeling ran too high for words. The marine railway needed greasing before winter. The Grey Wren herself — raised in June, pumped out, patched, and now awaiting the museum trust's slow machinery of permits and funding — wanted her covering boards checked before the rains came, and Edmund went down to her alone in the grey middle of the day and walked her deck like a man visiting a grave that had finally been given a stone.

She looked different in the air. Every diver knows this: a ship underwater is a body, weighted, dreaming, hers; a ship on the ways is a skeleton, honest and slightly embarrassing, all her secrets visible as rust streaks and sprung seams. He found the patch of hull where the cutting charge had gone in — Danny's charge, placed by Danny's hands, though Danny still wouldn't say why he'd come back that night, and Edmund had stopped asking, because some debts announce themselves when they're ready and not before. He found the strongroom, empty now, its door leaning against the coaming where the salvage crew had left it, a door that had kept its secret for a hundred years and given it up in eleven minutes of Edmund's desperate, surge-blind work.

He put his palm flat against the steel. Cold, even in October sun. Under his hand the ship felt — he knew how it sounded, and he said it anyway, to himself, in the privacy of his own skull — relieved.

"You talk to her yet?" said a voice behind him, and there was Marisol, who had followed him down without appearing to follow him, carrying two mugs and wearing the expression of a woman who had been waiting all day for her moment.

"Some."

"My mother used to say ships hear better than churches." She handed him a mug. Tea, heavily sugared, the way she made it when she thought he needed looking after and would deny needing it. "So. Are you going to stand on decks all autumn, or are you going to answer her?"

"Answer who?"

"The Calloway woman. Don't play the fool, Edmund, you're bad at it." She leaned against the rail, looking out at the river rather than at him, which was her mercy and her method both. "She asked you to come on with the survey outfit. Upriver charting, proper hydrography, honest contracts — I heard the offer myself, sitting right there in my own kitchen while you two did your long silences at each other. And you said you'd think about it. And it's been—" she counted on her fingers with theatrical slowness, "—eleven days."

"It's been busy."

"It's been October. Nothing's busy in October. Even the eels have given up." She turned to look at him then, full-on, and her eyes were her mother's eyes, the ones that had taken in a hollow-eyed boy twenty years ago and never once let him feel like charity. "Here's what I know about you, Edmund Underhill. You measure everything because measuring keeps things at arm's length. Fathoms. Miles. Tides. You spent five years diving a wreck you weren't allowed to name because a named thing can ask something of you and a number can't. And now the numbers are done — the wreck's raised, the truth's out, the fleet's gone to the lawyers' graveyard — and you're standing here with nothing left to count, and it terrifies you worse than any surge ever did."

Edmund drank his tea. The river moved. Somewhere upstream a train whistled, a thin sound that took a long time dying.

"You done?" he said.

"No. Because here's the other thing I know." Her voice dropped, lost its tease, and became just Marisol, thirty-eight years old, who had burned a fleet message in her own kitchen and never regretted it for one hour of one night. "You already stayed. Did you notice? Eleven days, and you never packed your kit. Your dive gear's in my boathouse loft where it's been since June. Your tide tables are on the nail. You fixed my mother's gate hinge Tuesday without being asked. You're not deciding whether to stay, Edmund. You're deciding whether to admit it."

He looked at her. She looked back, chin up, daring him to argue, and he found — this was the strange part, the part he would turn over for many unnumbered days afterward — that he couldn't. Not because she'd won the argument but because there was nothing left in him that wanted to win it.

"When did you get wise?" he said.

"Born wise. You were too busy counting to notice." She drained her mug and pushed off the rail. "Noon boat. Kitchen. There'll be a plate. If you're on it, fine. If you're not—" she shrugged, magnificent, "—more mutton for us."

* * *

He went up to his room in the afternoon, meaning to pack, or meaning to decide not to pack, or meaning at least to perform some gesture that would qualify as a decision. Instead he found himself taking down the things that had traveled with him for twenty years, the whole portable archive of a man who had arranged his life to be leaveable at an hour's notice: the duffel with the broken zip. The spare regulator. His father's compass, which he had given away in September to a woman with a broken collarbone and a waterproof case, and which had come back to him last week by post, wrapped in newspaper, with a note in Beatrix Calloway's upright hand: *Loaned, not given. Come collect the rest of the interest.*

He sat on the bed with the compass in his palm. Brass, worn smooth at the poles, the glass scratched where his father had — so the story went — pried it open with a knife to fix the card during the '48 gale, and never gotten the bezel seated quite right again. It had pointed at nothing useful for most of Edmund's life. His father had used it to find fishing grounds; Tom had used it to prove points in arguments; Edmund had used it, for five years, as a thing to hold in the dark when the numbers got thin. North, south, it hadn't mattered. It was the weight that helped.

Now he turned it over and read the engraving on the caseback, the one he'd needed lamplight and squinting for since he was a boy: *E. UNDERHILL — FROM THE RIVER, FOR THE RIVER.* His father had never explained it. Edmund had never asked. But standing in the small room above the boathouse, with the harbor sounds coming through the glass — the ferry, the gulls, Pietro's grandsons shouting about a crab — the words read differently than they ever had. Not a motto. An instruction. The river gives; the river expects you back. Everything else — the miles, the fathoms, the careful arithmetic of staying unattached — had been him refusing the second half of the sentence.

Below, the kitchen door banged. Voices. Nan's laugh, older and thinner than it used to be but unmistakable, and under it the clatter of the good plates, the ones with the blue rim that came out only when the occasion outranked the risk of chipping.

He looked at the duffel. He looked at the compass.

Then he put the compass in his pocket, left the duffel where it lay, and went downstairs.

* * *

There was no party. Of course there was no party. There was a kitchen with the stove ticking and the window steamed, and Nan installed in her chair by the fire with her knitting, and Pietro because Pietro appeared wherever food appeared, and Danny Quill — limping still, and always would be, the surgeons said, but limping toward things now instead of away from them — propping up the doorframe with a bottle of something that had clearly been intended for a larger occasion and had been waiting years for a smaller one. And at the stove, sleeves rolled, arguing with Marisol about whether onions belonged in a fish stew or constituted an act of vandalism, Beatrix Calloway, who had arrived on the noon boat and been absorbed into the kitchen the way driftwood gets absorbed into a beach: gradually, permanently, as though she'd always been part of the shoreline.

She saw him in the doorway and stopped mid-argument. For a moment neither of them said anything, and the kitchen held its breath around them — Nan's needles clicking, the stew muttering, the fire settling — and then Beatrix wiped her hands on her trousers, crossed the room, and held out his father's compass, which he was already holding, so that they stood there for an absurd second, each offering the other the same object.

"You brought it back," he said.

"You sent it to me."

"I sent it to be repaired."

"It was repaired. By you. In September. On a mudflat, at two in the morning, with a searchlight sweeping overhead." Her mouth twitched. "Best service it's ever had. I'm told the man does good work under pressure."

"He's slow with paperwork," Marisol called from the stove. "Eleven days to answer a simple question."

"What question?" Beatrix asked, turning, and Edmund understood suddenly that she had known — had known since the note, since the loan, since the culvert perhaps — that the answer was yes, and had simply declined to hurry him, because hurrying a diver off his decompression stops kills them, and she was a woman who respected the stops.

"The survey," he said. "Upriver. The charts." He cleared his throat. The whole kitchen pretended, with tremendous discipline, not to listen. "Whether there's a berth."

"There's a berth," Beatrix said. "Has been since June. There's also a second berth, if the harbor wants to lend us its best line-handler for the spring runs — I'm told she burns messages beautifully."

Marisol threw a dish towel at her. Danny laughed his rusty laugh and poured drinks all round, including one for Nan, who knocked it back like a stevedore and returned to her knitting. Nobody made a speech. Nobody proposed a toast. Pietro said grace in rapid, mumbled Italian that everyone politely ignored, and they ate the stew — with onions, vindicated — off the good plates with the blue rims, and not one of them chipped.

Later, much later, when the others had gone and the stove was banked and the kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and the ghost of onions, Edmund stood at the back step with Beatrix, looking out at the moorings under a high cold moon. The tide was making — he could tell without checking, could tell by the smell and the sound and the pull of it, the way you can tell a friend's footsteps on a stair — sliding green and silver up the channel, filling the harbor, lifting every hull in the anchorage an inch closer to the sky.

"You stopped counting," Beatrix said. It wasn't a question. "I noticed it on the boat this morning. You didn't check the tide once, and we crossed the bar on a slack that would've made you sweat in June."

"Marisol says I'm deciding whether to admit I've stayed."

"And have you decided?"

Edmund took out the compass. Held it up between them, moonlight running along the brass. *From the river, for the river.*

"I stopped deciding," he said. "Turns out that's different."

Beatrix looked at the compass, then at him, and whatever she saw made her smile the way she had smiled in the culvert, feverish and cracked-ribbed, when he'd told her she was going to live: not joy exactly, but recognition. The look of a person finding the thing they'd been searching for in the last place they'd dared to dig.

Inside, behind them, Marisol's voice floated out through the open window, pitched to carry, aimed with love:

"Edmund! Your plate's still on the table. It'll keep — but it won't keep forever, and neither will we, so come in out of the moonlight before I have to set another place!"

He laughed. Beatrix laughed. And Edmund Underhill, who had measured his life in miles and fathoms and tides for twenty years, went inside to sit down at a table that expected him, and stayed.
