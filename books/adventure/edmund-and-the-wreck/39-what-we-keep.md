## 39. What We Keep

The wreck we chose had no name worth keeping.

It lay in the shallows off Gull Point, where the river widened and forgot itself, a coastal trader gone down in some ordinary winter sixty years ago — ballast shifted, hawser parted, crew all saved by the lifeboat from Marsh End. There was nothing aboard but silt and soft iron and the slow architecture of mussels. No manifest. No strongroom. No crime. The navy had never bothered to mark it; the fleet had never bothered to claim it. It was nobody's wreck, which was exactly why I loved it.

Beatrix found it the way she found everything — by refusing to accept that a chart was finished. She'd been going through the old survey sheets from the harbor office, the ones nobody had updated since before the war, running her finger down soundings like a woman reading letters addressed to someone else. "Here," she'd said one evening over Marisol's table, tapping a depth figure that didn't match anything modern. "Something's sitting four fathoms up where the chart says six."

"Could be a shoal," I said.

"Could be." She smiled the way she did when she was letting me talk myself out of an argument she'd already won. "Or could be worth a look."

We'd been back three months then. Long enough for my shoulder to stop aching when the weather turned, long enough for her collarbone to knit straight, long enough for the inquiry to become something people talked about in the past tense, the way they talked about storms. Long enough for me to learn what her laugh sounded like when it wasn't armored.

So on a morning in late September, with the tide making gentle and the sky the color of hammered tin, we took Danny's skiff out past the point and dropped anchor over four fathoms of nothing in particular.

* * *

I went down first, out of habit more than need. Five years of diving alone will build habits into you like ribs.

The water at Gull Point was different from the Grey Wren's water. Warmer by degrees, greener, lit all the way down on a bright day so that the bottom came up to meet you instead of swallowing you. Visibility ran ten feet, fifteen, sometimes twenty if the wind had been kind. After the brown blindness of the restricted section — after that hull breach I still saw when I closed my eyes too long — it felt like diving through glass.

The wreck announced itself as a shadow, then a shape, then a ship.

She was small, maybe ninety feet, a coaster with a single hold and a wheelhouse aft, lying on her starboard side in the clean grey mud as though she'd simply lain down to rest. Silt had claimed her to the rail on the buried side. The exposed planking wore a pelt of hydroids that moved in the current like pale grass in wind. A school of young pollack hung above her deck in a loose, turning column, and when my bubbles reached them they parted without alarm and closed again behind me, unbothered, unhurried. Nothing down here had ever been frightened of anything.

I hung in the water above her rail and just looked.

That was the thing I'd never done, not once in five years on the Grey Wren. Work diving doesn't allow looking. You go down with a task — check a line, clear a snag, run a measurement — and every second of bottom time is spent against the clock, your attention narrowed to a cone of lamplight and the job inside it. You surface having seen perhaps a tenth of what passed before your eyes. The rest goes into the dark unrecorded.

But this morning there was no task. Beatrix had been explicit about it, almost stern, the night before: *No lines. No measurements. No slate. We're not salvaging anything, Edmund. We're visiting.*

Visiting. I hadn't known wrecks could be visited. I'd thought they were only ever worked, or feared, or mourned.

I swam her length slowly. Her name was still legible on the bow, or half of it — *...ORA MAY*, the first letters eaten by gribble and time — and I decided, arbitrarily and permanently, that she had been the Flora May, because a ship should have a name even if history couldn't be bothered to keep it. Her wheelhouse windows were long gone but the frames remained, square and patient, and through them I could see the brass of the engine telegraph lying in the silt, its face green with age, its lever frozen at STOP. Had been stopped, I supposed, for sixty years. Would stay stopped forever. There was something restful in that — a machine that had finished its work and knew it.

A lobster had taken up residence under the fallen wheel. It came out to the edge of the shadow and raised its claws at me, indignant, magnificent, and I laughed into my regulator, a great useless cloud of bubbles that scattered the pollack and accomplished nothing whatsoever. It was the best minute of diving I'd ever done.

Beatrix came down the anchor line while I was watching him. She descended the way she did everything now — carefully, economically, favoring the left arm that still told the truth about the culvert — and when her fins leveled out beside mine she turned and looked at the wreck with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it myself a moment earlier.

Wonder has a particular face. I'd seen it on hers across a courthouse steps and a harbor full of boats, but never underwater, never this unguarded. Down here her face was bare of everything but light.

We swam together along the deck, side by side, our bubbles rising in two threads that braided and unwove and braided again. She pointed at a china cup sitting upright in the silt outside the galley door, impossibly intact, impossibly placed — someone's cup, set down sixty years ago on a deck that was about to become a seabed, waiting all this time for no one. She picked it up, turned it in the green light, and set it back exactly where it lay, in its own little crater, undisturbed. That was the difference between us and everyone who'd ever dived the Grey Wren before us, I thought. We could afford to leave things where they were.

At the stern she found the crew's names on a brass plate screwed inside the companionway, tarnished nearly black but legible under her torch: seven men, one dog, the dog listed last with his own line, as though the master had insisted. *SHIP'S COMPANY, WINTER 19—*. The year was corroded away. Beatrix copied the names onto her wrist slate anyway — breaking her own rule, and glancing at me guiltily when I caught her at it — and I understood why. Some things you don't salvage. Some things you carry.

Her air ran low before mine, the way it always did; she breathed a wreck the way she read a chart, thoroughly, hungrily. We ascended together, slow, hanging at fifteen feet for the length of a prayer neither of us said aloud, and broke the surface into a world that seemed, for the first few breaths, unnecessarily loud.

* * *

Danny's skiff rode easy on the flood. We climbed aboard, stripped masks, sat dripping in the September sun with the point behind us and the whole bright river ahead, and neither of us spoke for a while. There is a silence between divers that isn't empty. It's full of everything the other person just saw.

"There's a lobster," I finally said, "who believes he owns that wheelhouse."

"Good," Beatrix said. "Someone should."

She had brought a canvas bag aboard, and I'd noticed it without asking about it, the way I'd learned to notice most things about her — peripherally, patiently, trusting they'd resolve. Now she opened it and took out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it with the deliberateness she reserved for things that mattered.

It was a flag. The expedition's flag — the ragged blue-and-white banner that had flown over her camp upriver, the one I'd assumed burned or drowned with everything else the fleet had torn apart. It was scorched along one edge and stained with river water that would never fully come out, and one corner had been torn away entirely, and someone — she, I realized; she must have carried it out of that camp herself, feverish, collarbone broken, wading through reeds in the dark — someone had stitched the worst of the tears closed with thread that didn't match.

"I'm not much of a seamstress," she said, anticipating the question. "Marisol offered to fix it properly. I told her no." She smoothed it across her knees, and the wind took the loose corner and worried it gently. "It should stay ugly. Ugly is part of what it means."

"What does it mean?" I asked, though I suspected I knew.

"That we were there," she said. "That we came upriver with eleven people and a question, and the river tried to keep us, and it didn't. That the answer got out." She looked at me then, directly, the way she did when she wanted a thing understood the first time. "Flags are for claiming territory. This one claims a different kind. I want you to have it."

I didn't say anything for a moment. Words felt like the wrong tool, the way a crowbar is the wrong tool for a hairline crack.

Instead I reached under the thwart, where I'd stowed my own bundle that morning before she was awake, and brought out the compass.

My father's compass. Brass-cased, gimbal-mounted, the card yellowed to the color of old teeth, with a hairline crack across the crystal that he'd never explained and I'd never asked about. He'd carried it on fleet jobs for twenty years, back when the fleet still pretended to be honest, and he'd given it to me the week before the job that killed him — pressed it into my hands on the quay and said, *Keep this where the company can't inventory it.* I was fourteen. I hadn't understood until much later what he was telling me: that some things belong to you and not to your employers, and that the difference might someday matter more than wages.

For five years on the Grey Wren I'd kept it in a tin box under my bunk and never once taken it diving. Compasses fail underwater anyway; you navigate by rope and reckoning and the lie of the current. But that wasn't the reason. The reason was that taking it down meant risking it, and risking it meant admitting it was mine to risk, and admitting that meant my father was really gone, really finished, reduced at last to an object that could be lost. So I kept it dry and safe and unclaimed, the way I kept everything.

I put it in Beatrix's hands.

"It doesn't work well anymore," I said. "The card sticks near north-northeast. You have to tap it."

"Most true things do," she said.

"He used it on fleet surveys. Before." I looked at the river rather than at her, because some sentences you can only deliver sideways. "He'd have liked knowing it ended up pointing at honest work."

Beatrix held the compass the way she'd held the china cup — turned it once in the light, read whatever needed reading, and closed her hands around it. She didn't thank me. Thanking would have made it a transaction, and we had both spent enough of the summer learning that what we were building wasn't built out of transactions.

Then she leaned over and kissed me, salt-lipped and unhurried, with the flood tide lifting the skiff beneath us and the gulls arguing over the point and the whole ordinary world going on around us as though nothing remarkable were happening — which, I suppose, was the point. Nothing remarkable was happening. Just two people on a boat, trading the only currency that had ever held its value.

* * *

We ran back downriver with the tide under us, the skiff surfing the last of the flood through the narrows where the dredge had clipped us, where Danny had held the wheel. Danny himself was on the quay when we tied up, sitting on a bollard in the sun with his daughter's drawings spread beside him, convalescent and content and pretending to supervise.

"Any good?" he called.

"Lovely wreck," Beatrix said. "No crimes at all."

"Disappointing."

"Restful," I said, and Danny looked at me a beat too long, and grinned, and let it go.

Marisol was waiting at the top of the ladder with a basket, because Marisol was always waiting somewhere with a basket; it was how the harbor said the things it couldn't say aloud. She fed us bread and smoked fish and strong tea on the seawall, and she teased me — she always teased me now — about how a man who'd spent five years eating alone at odd hours had gotten slow at defending his plate. Beatrix laughed at that, the unarmored laugh, and Marisol heard it and gave me a look over the teacups that said more than either of us would have tolerated being said directly.

Later, walking the quay as the light went long and copper, Beatrix slipped her arm through mine and asked, casually, terribly casually, whether I'd ever been out to the open sea. Past the bar. Past the river altogether.

"Once or twice," I said. "Fleet jobs. Hated it."

"And now?"

I considered. The harbor mouth stood open ahead of us, the bar a faint white line beyond, and past it the grey-gold plain of the sea going out to the edge of the world. For five years that line had been a wall. Harbors are hard to leave; I'd said so, and meant it, and mostly still meant it. But walls and horizons are different things, and I was beginning, finally, to know which was which.

"Now," I said, "it looks less like an ending."

She squeezed my arm and said nothing, which from Beatrix was a whole paragraph.

That night I hung the expedition's flag from the beam in my room, ugly stitches and all, where the lamp threw its shadow large against the wall. And I set my father's compass on the sill of Beatrix's window across the yard, where I could see it from my bed, its cracked crystal catching the harbor lights, its stubborn card resting — stuck, tapped, true — toward north-northeast.

Toward the sea. Toward whatever came next.

In the morning, Marisol said, there was a letter come on the packet boat, postmarked from the Admiralty, and it had my name on it, and Beatrix's underneath, and neither of us slept particularly well wondering what the navy wanted with two people who had finally learned to rest.
