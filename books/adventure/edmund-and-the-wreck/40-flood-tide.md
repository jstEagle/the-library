## 40. Flood Tide

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and Edmund was there to see it.

He hadn't planned to be. He'd woken in the dark the way he still sometimes did — five years of tide charts written into his body like scripture, the old habit of rising before slack water to check the current off the point — and instead of lying back down he'd pulled on his coat and walked out through the sleeping harbor to the mouth, where the stone jetty ran out into the channel like a broken sentence.

Now he stood at the end of it with his hands in his pockets and watched the river come home.

The flood came up from the sea the way it always did: not all at once but everywhere at once, a slow green muscle flexing under the surface, the water climbing the barnacled stones inch by patient inch. Out past the point the first light was coming up grey and then pearl, and the channel buoys that had lain over on their ebb moorings all night swung upright one by one as the new current took them, nodding like men agreeing to something after long argument. The sound of it was the oldest sound he knew — that low, ceaseless conversation between water and stone, the river breathing in.

Five years ago he had stood on this same jetty and measured the tide in fathoms and minutes because fathoms and minutes asked nothing of him. A man could live inside arithmetic. A man could dive a wreck two hundred times and never once have to say out loud why. He had told himself it was work. He had told himself it was loyalty. Both were true, and neither was the whole of it, and the whole of it had taken a feverish woman in a drainage culvert and a dead brother's name read aloud on courthouse steps before he could look at it straight.

The Grey Wren was gone now. Raised in September, hull patched and pumped, lifted onto the marine railway at the old government yard while half the county stood on the seawall with thermoses and children on their shoulders. He had been in the water when she broke the surface for the last time — the only man alive who'd seen her both ways, drowned and breathing — and when her keel cleared the mud he'd felt something leave his chest that he'd been carrying so long he'd mistaken it for a rib.

They'd read the names at the quayside. All thirty-one. It took eleven minutes. Marisol had counted, because Marisol counted everything, and afterward she'd said, *Eleven minutes, Edmund. That's what they got. Eleven minutes.* And he'd said, *Better than none,* and she'd looked at him with her mother's eyes and said, *Yes. Better than none,* and that was the closest either of them ever came to weeping in public.

The fleet was gone. Not vanished — nothing that big vanishes; it just comes apart into smaller truer things, the way a wreck comes apart into salvage and story. The dredges sat rusting at their moorings until the cooperatives bought them and put them to honest work pulling silt off the channel every spring. Vane's board dissolved itself with the efficiency of men who had always known which way the water ran. And the navy buoy — the buoy he'd been paid five years not to ask about — came up on a grey morning in October, crusted and dripping, hauled aboard by sailors who wouldn't meet anyone's eye, and the chart was corrected, and the channel was marked true, and no one ever again would be paid to look away from that stretch of water.

Edmund had kept one thing from the wreck. Not the manifest — that belonged to the inquiry, then to the museum, then to the families. Not the founder's confession, though he'd read it four times, that flat bureaucratic voice describing murder in the language of accounting. What he'd kept was small: a brass fitting off the crew's mess door, green with a century of sea, shaped like nothing much. It sat on his windowsill now where the morning light found it. Some men kept trophies. He kept a door handle, because doors were how you left, and this one had stayed shut too long against thirty-one men who'd wanted only to go home.

The flood tide reached his boots. Cold through the leather, familiar as a handshake. Down the jetty behind him the harbor was waking — gulls arguing over the fish shed roof, the smell of woodsmoke from Marisol's kitchen chimney, Danny Quill's boat already moving out toward the grounds with its new paint and its old scars. Danny worked the shallows now, mostly, easy water, and took his daughter to the clinic twice a month in a car the crews' descendants' fund paid for, and never once mentioned the photographs he'd taken on the mudflats, and Edmund never once mentioned that he knew about them. Some debts you settle by letting them rest.

Beatrix's collarbone had healed crooked. She said she could feel the weather in it now, which she claimed made her better at forecasting than the barometer, and the barometer agreed. Her survey outfit — three boats, eleven people, no lies in the ledgers — had spent the winter running honest charts of the coast, and in the spring they'd started taking on harbor kids who wanted to learn diving, and Edmund had taught the first class standing in three feet of water off the slipway with six teenagers looking at him like he was speaking a language they'd only heard in stories.

*You don't fight the water,* he'd told them. *You listen to it. It tells you everything, if you're quiet enough.*

Marisol had watched from the seawall with her arms crossed and said afterward, *Listen to yourself. Five years ago you didn't say ten words a week.*

*Five years ago nobody was listening.*

*I was.*

She'd said it lightly, the way she said most true things, and then gone back inside before he could answer, which was also the way she said most true things.

The light was strengthening now. The flood had the channel full, the buoys riding high, and out past the point the open water lay flat and pewter-bright all the way to the horizon. Somewhere out there was the little coaster Beatrix had flagged in the re-charted survey — a harmless wreck, forty feet down on clean sand, an honest ship that had simply grown old — and tomorrow, or the day after, the two of them would go down to her for no reason at all. No manifest. No crime. Just a ship to visit, the way you visit an old friend's grave and find yourself smiling instead of grieving. He'd bring the compass. His father's compass, the one that pointed true north and slightly toward forgiveness. She'd given him the expedition flag already, salt-stained and torn at one corner, and it hung in his room above the brass door handle, and between them those two objects said everything the two of them had never managed to say in words, which suited them both fine.

A footstep on the stones behind him. He didn't turn. There was only one person in the harbor who walked that quietly and pretended it was accidental.

"You're up early," Beatrix said, arriving beside him, hands jammed in the pockets of a coat that had once been expedition-issue and was now more patch than cloth.

"Tide turned."

"I noticed." She looked out at the water. Her breath smoked. "I couldn't sleep. Kept thinking about the culvert."

"Bad memory?"

"No," she said, and considered it honestly, the way she considered everything. "Strange one. The worst week of my life and I keep catching myself being fond of it. Is that wrong?"

"It's Tuesday," Edmund said.

She laughed — short, surprised, real — and the sound went out over the water and came back changed, the way sounds do at flood tide, fuller than they left.

They stood together while the sun cleared the headland and laid a long gold road down the middle of the channel, and the harbor boats began to move along it, one after another, out to the grounds, out to the survey lines, out to whatever the day held. Ordinary boats doing ordinary work on water that had finally been allowed to tell the truth about itself.

"You know what I keep thinking," Beatrix said. "That night on the mudflats. You talked about the Grey Wren for two hours. Two hours, Edmund. I thought you'd never stop."

"I nearly didn't."

"Why?"

He watched the current slide past the jetty, carrying leaf-mold and kelp and one lost crab pot buoy out toward the sea, everything the river had borrowed finally being returned. "Because I'd been holding it alone so long I forgot it was heavy," he said. "Then someone else had hold of it and I could feel the weight again. And it was lighter anyway. Both things at once. That's what I couldn't figure out for the longest time."

"That's just what it's like," Beatrix said. "Having people."

"Mm."

"You're staying, then." Not quite a question. She'd asked it properly once, in the autumn, offered him a berth on the survey outfit with wages and a bunk and a place at the table, and he'd said *let me think,* and she'd said *take all winter,* and the winter was nearly done.

"The harbor throws no parties," Edmund said. "It just sets another plate."

"And yours has been set since November."

"I know. I've been eating off it."

She smiled and bumped her shoulder against his, careful of nothing, because the collarbone was healed and some things didn't need care anymore, and after a while she said she'd see him at Marisol's table tonight, and went back up the jetty toward the smell of woodsmoke and frying bread, her shadow long and crooked-shouldered and entirely unbroken on the stones.

Edmund stayed.

This was the thing he had learned, standing here at the mouth of the river where all his arithmetic had lived and died: truth was not a thing you dug up once and were finished with. Truth was tidal. It went out — behind walls, under oaths, into the deep water where powerful men paid to have it sunk — and it seemed gone, seemed buried past any finding, and the world above went on as if the bottom of the river were nobody's business. But the moon kept its count. The pressure built somewhere out beyond the bar. And then, on a morning like this one, without drama, without even much noise, the whole vast weight of it turned and came home — up the channel, over the flats, into every creek and culvert and drowned camp, touching everything, asking nothing, refusing absolutely to be stopped.

You didn't have to force it. You couldn't, anyway; he'd tried, with cutting charges and borrowed skiffs and a broken woman in a waterproof case, and the truth of it was that all he'd really done was be present at the turning. Been there when it came. Held the line steady and let the water do what water does.

His brother had been out there somewhere under the flood all these years too, in the deep ledger of the drowned, waiting with the other thirty-one. And the tide had brought them all home.

Edmund Underhill stood at the harbor mouth until the sun was fully up and the flood stood high and green and certain in the channel, and then he turned from the sea, and walked back up the jetty, past the fish sheds and the mended nets and the fresh-painted boats, through the gate with its worn hinge, up the lane that smelled of bread and smoke and tar, toward the house where another plate was set and someone was already calling his name.

He went in, and stayed.
