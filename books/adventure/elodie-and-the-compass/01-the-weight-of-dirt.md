## 1. The Weight of Dirt

There are three rules to digging where no one wants you digging. One: never bring a lamp you can't replace. Two: never dig deeper than your exit. Three: never, under any circumstances, want what you find.

I'd broken the third rule exactly once in my life, and I was eleven years old, and the thing I'd wanted was my mother's attention. It didn't come up out of the ground either. Some caches stay buried.

The borderlands at night smell like wet iron and old arguments. I knelt in the spoil heap behind the tannery at Corvess Hollow, three fingers into a soil cut that had been opened by someone else and abandoned by everyone, and I listened the way other people pray. Wind through the fence slats. A dog two properties over with opinions. The tannery vats burping their slow chemical digestion. Nothing human. Good.

The man paying me—Rennick, a fence with the moral flexibility of a wet rope—wanted whatever the grave robbers had cached before the constable caught one of them and the other two scattered. He'd said *relics*, said it the way men say *relics* when they mean *coins* but want to feel educated about it. He'd paid half up front, which in Rennick's economy meant he expected me to die before collection.

I worked by touch. That's not romance; that's arithmetic. Lamplight carries over broken country like gossip, and the borderlands have more watchers than stars. So: hands, knees, a short-handled spade worn down to something between a tool and an apology. I read soil the way literate people read paper. This cut had been refilled twice—the robbers had gone back for their cache once already, which told me they were nervous, which told me there was more than coins down there, because nobody gets nervous over coins.

Two feet down, the fill changed texture. Looser. Recent. I widened the hole with my palms instead of the spade, because a spade has a voice and palms don't, and I lifted out a waxed canvas bundle wrapped the way soldiers wrap things they expect to run with.

Inside: forty-one coins, minted on the wrong side of a border that had moved twice since they were struck. A silver hand-mirror with a green patina eating its face. And a small stone box, lidded, heavy past its size, carved all over with lines that weren't decoration. Lines that were *writing*, or had been before whoever wrote them died and took the alphabet with them.

I sat back on my heels in the dark and let myself do the thing I never let myself do. I imagined keeping it.

Not selling it. Keeping it. Setting it on a shelf in a room that belonged to me, in a town where people knew my name and used it without checking it against a list first. I imagined turning it over in lamplight—I could own a lamp, if I kept it—and reading what couldn't be read, and being the kind of person whose life accumulates objects instead of exits.

Then I put the lid back on the box, because wanting is how people get found.

Here's what nobody tells you about erasing yourself: it works. That's the trap of it. You spend years sanding your name off leases and ledgers and family correspondence, and one morning you realize the sanding went all the way through—you're not hidden anymore, you're *gone*, and the only person who notices the absence is you, and even you have to squint.

My name isn't Jude Fairweather. Not officially. Officially I'm a sequence of aliases so well-tended they've begun to develop their own personalities. Fairweather is the name I keep folded up inside, the way you keep a letter you'll never send, because throwing it away would mean admitting you read it.

I wrapped the cache back up, all of it, even the coins. Forty-one coins is a fortune out here and a death sentence anywhere Rennick could reach, and both of those facts fit in a waxed canvas bundle small enough to forget.

I filled the hole. I tamped the soil with the flat of the spade until even the rain wouldn't know we'd been introduced. Then I carried the bundle forty yards east, past the fence line, to the root cellar of a house that had burned eleven years ago—a house I knew about because I'd made it my business to know about every empty place within a day's walk, the way other men make it their business to know women or drink—and I buried it under the collapsed floor joists where the roots had made a lattice no shovel would thank you for disturbing.

Then I walked back to Rennick's and lied to him beautifully.

"Robbers got clever," I said. "Cached it in the river. River's been greedy this season."

Rennick looked at me the way he looked at everything, which was like an appraisal with legs. "You're certain."

"I'm certain the river's been greedy." I held out my hand for the second half anyway, because a liar who doesn't collect looks like a liar who's lying badly. He paid. He always pays; that's his one virtue, and virtues in fences are like teeth in hens.

"You'll hear if it surfaces," he said.

"You won't hear from me," I said, and left before he could decide whether that was insolence or professionalism. With Rennick it was both, and he respected both, which is the closest thing to affection the borderlands offer.

* * *

My camp was a mile north, in the lee of a limestone shelf that broke the wind the way a shoulder breaks a fall. I'd chosen it for three reasons: no sightline from the road, water seeping from the rock face into a basin I'd lined with tarpaulin, and nothing worth stealing, which is the best lock ever devised.

I boiled tea I didn't taste and ran the night back through my head, looking for mistakes. This is not anxiety. Anxiety is for people who have something to lose. This is maintenance. You check your work the way a smith checks a weld—not because you doubt yourself, but because the ground doesn't forgive, and neither does the constabulary, and neither, most reliably, does memory.

The mistake arrived on schedule, because mistakes always do, and it arrived wearing boots.

I heard him fifty yards out—wrong gait for the terrain, too much heel, a man who'd learned to walk on pavement and was impersonating a walker of wild country. I banked the fire down to coals and moved to the shelf's edge where the shadow is deepest, and I waited, and I did not want anything, including for him to leave, because wanting things is how you telegraph them.

He came into the coal-light coughing.

That was wrong too. Everything about him was wrong. He was big, gray-bearded, built like something quarried rather than born, and he was holding his side below the ribs, and his hand came away black in the coal-light, and he looked at my camp—my bedroll, my kettle, my one spare shirt drying on a line—as though cataloguing it for a report he'd never file.

"Fairweather," he said.

The name hit me like cold water down the collar. Nobody had said it aloud in six years. I'd said it myself maybe twice, testing it, the way you test a floorboard you suspect is rotten.

"You've got the wrong camp," I said.

"No," he said, and sat down without permission, which takes a particular kind of dying man, "I've got the right one. Took me nine days to find you. Nine days and most of my blood." He laughed, and the laugh cost him, and I watched him pay. "Callum Voss. You don't know me. Nobody knows me anymore, that's rather the point of us, isn't it?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"You erased yourself off a survey crew roster in Harrowgate four years ago. Before that, a shipping manifest out of Dunmore. Before that—" he waved a bloody hand, generous, "—well. I did the same trick, just worse. I erased myself and left debts standing. Debts with guns." He leaned back against the limestone and breathed in a way I recognized from field hospitals and bad nights: shallow, counted, rationed. "I need you to carry something across the Ashkettle Range."

"No."

"You haven't asked what it is."

"The answer doesn't improve with details."

He smiled. There was blood on his teeth and amusement in his eyes and those two things together made something I hadn't seen in years, which was a person entirely unimpressed by me. "No," he agreed. "It doesn't. But you'll say yes anyway, because I'm going to tell you why I picked you, and you're going to hate how well I picked."

I should have walked out then. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them. I should have taken my kettle and my spare shirt and my nine aliases and dissolved into the hills, and Callum Voss could have bled out against my limestone with his secrets intact, and everything after—the riders, the range, the ice, the girl who sells passes, the whole long ledger of it—would never have been written against my account.

Instead I crouched down across the coals from him and said, "Why?"

"Because you walk away from treasure." He said it simply, like reporting weather. "Rennick's been telling anyone who'll buy him drink about the fool who found the Hollow cache and buried it again. Forty-one coins, Jude. I've killed a man for less than forty-one coins, and here you are, leaving them under a burned house like a cat hiding a bird it doesn't intend to eat." His breath whistled. "A man who doesn't want things is the only man alive I can trust to deliver a thing."

"That's not trust. That's just poverty of ambition."

"It's rarer than trust." He fumbled at his coat, and I let him, because a gut-shot man going for a weapon is a problem for later and curiosity, I am ashamed to report, is patient. He drew out a leather packet, soft with handling, tied with a cord, and laid it on the stone between us like a card played face-up. "Map. Letters. Both yours if you do the job. Map goes across the Ashkettle to a place I'll show you. Letters go to Merrow's Landing. To my daughter."

"I'm not a courier."

"No. You're a ghost with good knees. It's the same trade, better rates." He closed his eyes for a moment, and I thought—he's going now, right here, on my limestone, and I'll have a body to explain—but he opened them again, stubborn as weather. "Nineteen days before the high passes close. Maybe less, this year. The snow doesn't negotiate."

"Voss." I tried the name out loud, to see if saying it would make him less real. It didn't. "Whoever shot you will follow the wound. Wounds leave sign. You've led them straight to me."

"They were always going to find somebody," he said. "Better a ghost than a daughter."

And there it was. The hook, set clean through the soft part. I have spent six years making sure that nothing anyone did to anyone else could be laid at my door. Voss looked at me across twelve inches of coal-light and handed me a stranger's guilt like it was change from a purchase.

"I'll think about it," I said, which is what cowards say instead of no.

"Think fast," he said, and slept, or did the thing dying men do instead.

* * *

He lasted until dawn. Dawn is when the honest light comes, the kind that shows you the truth about faces, and his was a map of its own: every year of hard country drawn in lines, every debt inked around the eyes. Near the end he talked—not to me, exactly, more to the middle distance where daughters live—and I learned fragments. Three partners. A fourth man. A crevasse. Words dropped like tools down a shaft, and I didn't pick them up, because picking things up is wanting, and I had a rule.

At the very end he gripped my wrist with a strength that surprised us both and said, "See it home," and I still don't know whether he meant the map or himself, and I suppose I'll spend the rest of this story finding out.

I dug his grave myself, in the gravel above the seep, deep enough that the dogs and the dishonest would pass him by. No marker. Markers are addresses. I said no words over him, because words are also addresses, and I stood there in the gray light feeling the specific loneliness of the self-erased: not the loneliness of having no one, but of having made sure of it.

Then I went back to camp and untied the leather packet, because I hated myself, and hatred makes you curious.

The map was hand-drawn, beautiful and mad, ink over pencil, corrected obsessively. It showed the Ashkettle Range—which appears on no official chart, which the Survey denies, which cartographers omit the way families omit uncles—and at its center, drawn with a care that bordered on devotion, a small ring of stones around a shaft, and in the shaft, rendered in a single confident stroke, an object like a compass. Except the needle wasn't drawn pointing anywhere. It was drawn *hollow*. An empty circle, waiting.

Underneath, in Voss's cramped hand: *It doesn't point north. It points at what you want. God help you.*

I sat with that for a long time while the tea went cold and the borderlands went about their indifferent morning.

The letters I didn't read. Not yet. Some part of me understood, even then, that the letters were the real cargo—that the map was bait and the confession was the freight—and I wasn't ready to be paid in a dead man's regrets. I retied the cord. I sewed the packet into the lining of my coat, over the left breast, where a heart would go if I'd kept mine where it was issued.

I was threading the needle when Sefa—I didn't know her name yet, she was still just a rumor of trouble on the road ahead—no. Let me keep the order straight. I was threading the needle when I remembered the newspaper.

Rennick wraps his goods in old print; it's cheaper than cloth. Last week's wrapping had still been lying in his yard, and I'd taken it for kindling without reading it, because reading is a door and doors are dangerous. Now, with Voss's blood still on my wrist, I unfolded the crumpled sheet by the dead fire and made myself look.

Page seven. The academic notices, dry as dust, the section nobody reads, which is why I'd always read it—old habit, an inheritance I couldn't bury. And there it was, in a paragraph about a lecture tour:

*Dr. Imogen Fairweather, whose controversial monograph on pre-Survey borderland cultures has drawn both acclaim and censure, will present new findings concerning a relic she terms the "Hollow Compass"—an artifact she argues was never a navigational instrument at all, but something older, and stranger...*

I read it four times. The wind moved the page. Somewhere north, a range that officially didn't exist waited under its snow, and somewhere south, a woman who had taught me to read soil and then failed to notice when I stopped speaking had put my word—*hollow*, her word, our word, a word from a childhood kitchen—in print, attached to the thing currently sewn against my chest.

She'd spent twenty years digging toward everything I'd spent six years digging away from. And now, by the oldest law of the borderlands—the law that says what you refuse to want comes looking for you anyway—her handwriting and Callum Voss's blood had met in the same week, on the same page of my life.

I folded the newspaper along the crease. I packed my kit with the economy of long practice: kettle, tarp, spade, the coat with its secret passenger.

Nineteen days before the passes close. A map to a place that doesn't exist. Letters to a daughter. A mother's voice rising out of newsprint like something exhumed.

I put out the last coal with my boot heel, the way you close a grave, and started walking west.

Toward the range. Toward the thing I refused to want.

Which, as any ghost could tell you, is the surest way of all to find it.
