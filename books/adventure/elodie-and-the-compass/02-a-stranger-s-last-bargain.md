## 2. A Stranger's Last Bargain

I was boiling creek water for coffee when the man came out of the trees, and I want it on the record that my first instinct was to put out the fire and leave.

That's not cruelty. That's arithmetic. A stranger staggering into your camp in the borderlands is either a dying man or a decoy, and both of them cost you something. The first costs you time. The second costs you everything else. I'd spent six years learning to be a rounding error on other people's ledgers, and rounding errors don't stop to help.

But he fell before I could decide, which is how most of my bad decisions get made—by the world simply moving faster than my caution.

He came down through the alder scrub like a sack of tools dropped from a height, caught himself on one knee, and stayed there, breathing in a way I recognized from dig sites and disaster reports: wet, rattling, each breath a negotiation he was losing. His coat was good leather gone gray with trail dust, his boots were expensive and ruined, and there was blood on his left side in an amount that stopped being dramatic and started being administrative.

"Water," he said. "Please."

I gave him the water. Not because I'm kind—I've had that particular delusion beaten out of me—but because a man who dies in your camp is a problem, and a man you've given water to is slightly less of one. Or so I told myself at the time. Looking back, I think the truth arrived earlier than my excuses did.

He drank half the cup, then looked at me with eyes the color of old ice and said, "You're the Fairweather kid."

The cup went cold in my hands. "Nobody's called me that in years."

"I know." He tried to smile and got about a third of the way. "Your mother's paper made the rounds last spring. *On Anomalous Bearing Instruments of the Northern Ranges.* There was a sketch of her field notes in the margin. Your handwriting, annotating hers. She kept the page."

"That's not possible," I said, and heard how young I sounded saying it.

"She keeps a lot of things she doesn't display." He shifted, and the breath he took afterward had a sound like a boot coming out of deep mud. "Callum Voss. Prospector. Formerly of several places, currently of nowhere much."

"Jude," I said. Just Jude. No surname offered, no history volunteered. Six years of erasure doesn't break easily, even for a bleeding man.

"Jude." He tested it like a coin between his teeth. "Good name. Anonymous. You chose well."

"I didn't choose anything. It's just a name."

"Everything's just a name until someone's willing to kill for it." He said this mildly, the way a man comments on weather, and then he coughed, and the cough had blood in it, and I stopped pretending I hadn't already decided to stay.

* * *

Here's what I knew by full dark, after I'd gotten him onto my bedroll and done what field medicine allows, which is mostly watching and lying:

Callum Voss had been shot somewhere south of the Sere River, two days' hard walk back, by men he'd once called partners. He'd walked those two days anyway, following a rumor of a borderlands digger who could move without leaving tracks—a rumor, he informed me, that had cost him three drinks and a favor I didn't want to know the price of.

"You came looking for me specifically," I said.

"I came looking for anyone who could do what you do. You happened to be findable, which I understand is against your religion."

"It is, actually."

"Well." He settled back against my pack, gray-faced but composed, a man conducting business from a deathbed because the deathbed was the only office left to him. "Then consider this my formal apology for the inconvenience."

The bullet had gone in below his ribs and, as far as I could tell, nowhere out. I cleaned it, packed it, gave him willow bark and the last of my decent whiskey, and told him the truth, because lying to dying men seemed like a waste of a good lie: he might see morning, and he probably wouldn't see another one after that.

He nodded like I'd confirmed a train schedule. "That matches my own assessment. Good. We understand each other. Now let's talk terms."

"There are no terms. You're dying in my camp. That's not a negotiation, it's a misfortune."

"Everything's a negotiation." He fumbled at his coat with hands that had started shaking, and I helped him, because refusing would have cost more than helping. Inside the lining, sewn flat against the leather, was a packet wrapped in oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth were two things: a bundle of letters tied with waxed string, and a map.

I should describe the map properly, because everything that followed—the riders, the river, the crevasse, all of it—lives inside its ink lines.

It was hand-drawn on survey linen, the kind cartographers use when they mean a drawing to outlast them. The Ashkettle Range ran across it north to south, rendered in a draftsman's hand so precise it looked printed. But the range itself was wrong. Officially, the Ashkettes don't exist. They're a blank on every government chart, a surveyor's gap, a place the maps politely decline to mention. Voss had drawn them anyway, ridge by ridge, pass by pass, with elevations noted in a cramped sure script—and at the center of the range, where nothing official lived, he'd drawn a small circle of stones around a shaft, and beside it, in letters smaller than any other marking on the page, two words:

*Hollow Compass.*

"You know what it is," he said. Not a question.

"I know what my mother thinks it is. A bearing instrument of unknown provenance. She wrote eleven pages speculating and concluded, in her careful academic way, that it probably doesn't exist."

"Your mother," Voss said, "is a very intelligent woman who has never wanted anything badly enough to be dangerous. That's why they let her publish." He tapped the map with one finger. "This exists. I found it. And the men who shot me want it, and they have already killed for less than knowing where it lies, and now, unfortunately for you, so do you."

"I don't want it."

"Nobody wants it," he said, with sudden fierceness that made him cough again. "That's the whole point of it. Nobody sane wants it. But wanting isn't required. Knowing is enough. Knowing is the disease." He lay back, spent, and for a while there was only the fire working through the alder wood and his breathing doing its losing negotiation.

I should have burned the map that night. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is apparently the theme of my life now. The oilcloth was in my hands. The fire was right there. One gesture and I go back to being nobody, which was the only thing I had ever worked for.

Instead I folded it along its original creases and put it back in the lining of his coat, because it was his, and because—even then, even before the promise—I understood that some things aren't property. They're debts wearing property's clothes.

* * *

He talked through the middle hours of the night, the way dying men do, in loops and fragments, and I learned to sort the sense from the fever.

There had been four of them, twelve years ago: Voss and three partners, financed quietly by a company whose name he never said aloud but whose shape I recognized from the emptied villages and bought magistrates of the border country. They'd come into the Ashkettes chasing a survey anomaly—an instrument needle that wouldn't settle, that pointed wrong in ways instruments don't. They'd found the ring of stones. Three of them went down into the shaft.

"One came up," Voss said, and his voice did something complicated on the word *one*, and I didn't ask, and he didn't offer, and we both let it lie there between us like a tool neither of us wanted to pick up.

What he offered instead was the letters. He touched the waxed string with two fingers, the way you'd touch a wound that wasn't yours.

"My daughter. Mara. Merrow's Landing, on the east coast, past the range. She'll be—" he calculated, wincing, "—twenty-six now. I left when she was nine. I sent money for four years and then I stopped sending anything, including money, including words. These are twelve years of unsent letters. Every year on her birthday I wrote one. Then I couldn't stop writing them, which should have told me something."

"Why not send them?"

"Because a letter is a claim," he said. "A letter says *remember me*. And I hadn't earned remembering. So they piled up." He looked at me with that terrible composure dying men manufacture out of nothing. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not asking you to explain me. I'm asking you to carry paper across a mountain range and put it in a woman's hands. That's all. It's a courier's errand. You're the best courier I could find, because you're the only man alive who can cross the Ashkettes without being seen, and the men who shot me will be looking."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I die in your camp, and you burn the map and the letters, and you go back to being a rumor, and I will haunt you moderately. Nothing dramatic. Doors left open. Coffee gone cold."

Despite everything—the blood, the smell, the arithmetic screaming in my skull—I laughed. He looked pleased about that, like he'd gotten a signature on something.

"The map," he said, "is payment. Take it or leave it; it's worthless unless carried. The promise is the actual transaction. I need to hear you say it."

"Don't do this."

"I've been doing it for two days. Walking toward a stranger because I trusted a drunk's gossip, because the alternative was trusting my partners, and look how that went." His hand found my wrist. His grip was still strong; the rest of him was negotiating surrender terms with gravity. "Say it, Jude. Say you'll carry it home. Say you'll see it delivered. Those words. Exact words matter. I've had a lot of time to draft this."

And here is the part I keep circling, the part I'd cut from any story I told in a tavern: I said it.

Not because I was moved. Not because of some buried goodness finally surfacing. I said it because he was dying in front of me and the words were free and I have never in my life learned how to watch a man beg and keep my mouth shut. Six years of invisibility, and it turns out the foundation of it was always going to crack under the weight of one bleeding stranger with a daughter's name in his mouth.

"I'll carry it," I said. "Across the range. To Merrow's Landing. To your daughter. I'll see it delivered."

Something went out of him when I said it—not tension, exactly. More like a man setting down a load he'd been told no one else could lift, and discovering his arms still remembered the shape of it.

"Thank you," he said. Then, wry to the end: "Worst bargain you ever made, and you weren't even buying anything."

* * *

He died at dawn.

Not dramatically. The stories all give dying men a final burst of clarity, a last message, a hand rising to point at the horizon. What actually happened was quieter and worse. He slept near morning, and his breathing stretched its intervals longer and longer, like a clock winding down, and at some point between one grey minute and the next the negotiations ended and there was just a man-shaped quiet in my camp.

I sat with him until the sun cleared the ridgeline. Then I did the work. There's always work. I dug, because I'm good at digging—it's the one skill my mother gave me that I never resented—and I buried Callum Voss under a cairn of creek stones with no name on it, because names attract the wrong kind of attention, and because I suspected he'd have appreciated the irony of a professional eraser handling his arrangements.

Before I filled the grave, I took the oilcloth packet from his coat. The letters. The map. Everything.

I stood there a long time with the fire rebuilt and fed, telling myself the sensible story: burn it. Burn the map, scatter the letters, tell no one, go back to being a rounding error. The men who shot him would chase a dead man's ghost for a season and lose interest. The daughter in Merrow's Landing would live her whole life unbothered by a father's handwriting, which might honestly be a mercy. Burning it was the kind choice. Burning it was the smart choice. Burning it was, by every measure I'd built my life on, correct.

I held the first letter over the flames long enough to feel the waxed string start to soften.

Then I pulled it back.

Because—and this is the thought that arrived uninvited, the way all true thoughts do—I had promised. Not to the map, not to the relic, not to whatever black-glass needle lay in a shaft at the center of a range that officially didn't exist. I'd promised *him*, a man who had walked two days on a gut shot because a drunk's rumor was the only thing left he trusted. And I had spent six years perfecting the art of owing nothing to anyone, and here was a debt, the first one I'd taken on voluntarily since I erased myself, and I discovered that some stubborn animal part of me did not want to wriggle out of it. Wanted, in fact, the opposite. Wanted to carry it. Wanted to finish something.

I hated that. I want that understood. I hated it the way you hate a door swinging open in a house you'd sealed shut.

I kept everything. The map went into the lining of my own coat, sewn flat with waxed thread from my kit, needle work I'd learned from a woman in a border town whose name I also never gave. The letters went into an oilskin pouch against my spine, where they rode like a second heartbeat. I burned his coat, because coats carry lice and sometimes trackers, and I marked his grave with a cairn and no name, and I spoke over it the only words I trust, which were: "I'll see it delivered."

Then I broke camp, because standing still is how invisible people become visible.

I was two miles down the valley, walking north toward the staging town of Bracken Ford where guides could be bought and questions asked carefully, when I saw it: dust on the southern trail, too deliberate for weather, too organized for traders. Riders. Three of them, moving at the pace of men following a line rather than searching an area.

They were a day behind him. Which meant they were, at most, two days behind me.

I adjusted my pack, checked the weight of the letters against my spine, and did the thing I'd sworn off years ago.

I started wanting to arrive.
