## 3. Men Who Follow

The border town of Gilt Row had exactly one virtue: nobody in it cared who you were, because everybody in it was busy not caring about themselves. It was a town built on the principle that questions were a form of theft—questions took something from you that you could never get back—and so the whole population had agreed, silently and permanently, to stop asking them.

I'd been there four days when the riders came.

I knew they were trouble before I saw them, which is the sort of sentence that sounds like boasting but is really just bookkeeping. Trouble announces itself in small ways first. The dog outside the Dry Ledger went quiet mid-bark, the way dogs do when they smell something that outranks them. The card game in the corner of the saloon lost its rhythm—not stopped, just went soft, the way water goes still before it freezes. And Marta, who owned the place and had never once looked directly at me in two years of selling me coffee, looked directly at me.

"Back room," she said, without moving her lips much. "Now."

I want to be clear that I am not a brave person. Bravery is a luxury good, like silk or honesty, and I had spent years learning to live without luxuries. So I did what she said, taking my coffee with me because leaving a full cup behind is wasteful, and settled onto a crate between a barrel of pickled eggs and a stack of chairs that had given up on being furniture.

Through the gap where the plaster had crumbled away from the lath, I watched three men come through the front door.

They came in wrong. That's the only way to describe it. Everything in Gilt Row came in slouched and apologetic, shoulders down, eyes on the floor, performing poverty so convincingly that most of the time it wasn't a performance anymore. These three came in like the room owed them money. The one in front was big through the chest and narrow through the patience, wearing a canvas duster that had been oiled recently enough to still shine. The other two flanked him the way well-trained dogs flank, wide enough to cover the exits without appearing to.

The big one put his hands flat on the bar. Not slammed—flat. Placed. A man who wanted you to notice how controlled he was.

"We're looking for a man," he said. "Came through here maybe a week back. Prospector type. Older. He'd have been hurt."

Marta poured him a drink he hadn't asked for. "Lots of prospectors."

"This one would've been bleeding."

"Lots of those too." She set the glass down. "It's the border country. Everything bleeds eventually."

I liked her enormously in that moment, in the way you can like someone you've known for years and never once spoken to properly. There's an intimacy in shared silence that conversation ruins.

The big man drank. He didn't thank her. "He might have been traveling with someone. Younger. Keeps to themselves. Might have bought supplies."

"You're describing half this town," Marta said. "And the other half wishes it were them."

One of the flanking men laughed—a short, ugly bark—and then stopped laughing when the big man glanced at him. The glance lasted less than a second, but I saw what lived inside it, and it made my hands go cold around the coffee cup. It wasn't anger. Anger is hot and stupid and passes. This was arithmetic. The look of a man calculating whether Marta was worth the cost of killing her, and finding the sum close enough to be interesting.

"He'd have had a map," the big man said, quieter now. "Hand-drawn. If anyone's seen it, or heard talk of it, there's money in it for them. Honest money." He set coins on the bar, one at a time, so we could all hear them ring. "More honest than most of what gets earned here."

Nobody moved. In Gilt Row, that was practically a civic statement.

The big man waited a moment longer, reading the room the way I read spoil heaps—for what was buried under the surface. Then he turned, and here is the thing I will remember until I die: as he turned, his eyes passed over the crack in the plaster. Passed over me. Held for half a heartbeat on the gap where a woman sat alone among barrels, watching him through a hole in a wall, and then moved on.

He knew. Or he suspected. And he let it go, because a suspicion in a back room wasn't worth a scene, and because men like him prefer to follow rather than flush. You learn more from what runs than from what fights.

The door closed behind them. The dog started barking again. Somewhere in the corner, the cards resumed their rhythm, and the whole town exhaled and pretended nothing had happened, which is the closest thing Gilt Row has to a prayer.

Marta appeared in the back room doorway thirty seconds later, arms crossed.

"You know them?" I asked.

"I know the coat," she said. "Ashkettle Mining and Survey buys that oilcloth by the hundred. Their men wear it up and down the range." She looked at me with the first direct interest she'd ever shown me, and I understood that my invisibility, the armor I'd worn so long I'd forgotten it was armor, had just developed its first crack. "Whatever that dying man gave you, girl—it's worth more than you told yourself it was."

"It's letters," I said. "Apologies. To a daughter."

"Mm." She didn't believe me, which was fair, since I barely believed myself. "Back door's through the cold store. Don't use the main road east; they'll have a man on it within the hour. They always do." She paused. "You were never in my back room."

"No," I agreed. "I've never been anywhere."

She almost smiled. "That used to work better, I think. Before today."

* * *

Here is what I did in the next six hours, and I record it because later, when people asked—and people did ask—I wanted to remember that I had done it deliberately, step by step, like a ritual, and not simply run like an animal.

First, I went back to my camp above the slag fields and burned everything that could burn. My spare shirt. The false papers in the name of T. Merrick, surveyor's assistant, which had served me faithfully for two years and deserved better than fire but got it anyway. The notebook with my dig notes, six months of careful stratigraphy, gone in eleven minutes of orange light. I kept the trowel. Some things are not negotiable.

Second, I took Voss's map out of the oilskin packet where I'd kept it since dawn broke over his body, and I looked at it properly for the first time.

I'd looked at it before, of course. I'd traced the route with my finger a dozen times—the switchbacks, the river crossing marked with a terse *floods*, the waystation drawn as a square with a broken roofline. But I'd looked at it the way you look at a bill, not the way you look at a document. Now I spread it flat on a rock and forced myself to actually see it.

Voss had drawn with a surveyor's hand and a drunkard's heart. The lines were precise and the annotations were furious. Along the margins ran notes in a cramped script: distances in paces rather than miles, water sources marked with symbols I didn't recognize, warnings in the imperative mood—*do not trust the ice below the second saddle*, *the stones lie, count your own steps*. And at the center of everything, drawn larger than any landmark, rendered in careful cross-hatching that must have taken hours:

A compass. Not pointing north. Pointing inward, its needle folded back against itself, into the center of its own rose.

And beneath it, the range itself. The Ashkettle Range—or what should have been the Ashkettle Range—drawn in full, peak after peak after peak, with elevations noted beside each summit.

I sat back on my heels.

Because here is the thing every schoolchild in three territories knows, the thing printed on every government map issued in the last forty years: there is no Ashkettle Range. There is the Ashkettle Uplands, a modest line of hills, surveyed, named, and dismissed. The official maps show grassland and low stone beyond the Sere River, good grazing country sloping gently toward the northern wastes. I had grown up on those maps. I had memorized them, the way I memorized everything, because maps were the first language I ever learned to speak fluently and the only one I ever trusted.

But Voss had drawn mountains. Real ones, with snowlines and glaciers and a pass through the shoulder of the highest ridge, and he had drawn them with the confidence of a man who had stood on them.

Either Callum Voss had died lying to me about geography, which seemed like an odd final project for a man spending his last night on apologies—

—or the maps were wrong. All of them. Deliberately.

I thought about the paper. My mother's paper, the one that had reached me through three intermediaries and a year's delay, the one I'd read twice and told myself meant nothing. *On Anomalous Cartographic Suppression in the Northern Territories.* She'd written it in her usual style, which was to say like a woman politely accusing an entire government of forgery while apologizing for the inconvenience. She'd listed discrepancies. Elevations reported by early expeditions that matched no surviving terrain. Place names in old mining ledgers that appeared on no chart. A pattern of erasure so consistent it couldn't be accident, and so thorough it had to be policy.

I had read it and felt the old familiar sickness—the pull of her voice across all that distance, reaching even here, even to me—and I had put it out of my mind the way I put everything of hers out of my mind.

Now her evidence was sitting on a rock in front of me, hand-drawn by a dead man, and three riders in mining-company oilcloth were asking after it in a town where nobody asks anything.

"Well," I said aloud, to the slag fields, to the wind, to nobody. "Damn."

Third—and this was the part that took the longest, the part that hurt—I sewed the map into my coat lining.

Not folded into a pocket. Not strapped to my body where a search would find it. Sewn. Flat against the canvas between the outer cloth and the lining, left side, below the ribs, distributed so it wouldn't print as a shape. I used waxed thread and a curved needle I kept for repairing gear, and I worked by the last of the light with my fingers going clumsy, and somewhere around the twentieth stitch I realized I was crying, which annoyed me considerably, because I hadn't cried when Voss died and I hadn't cried burning my notebook and I resented the map for earning tears that people hadn't.

But that's not why I was crying, if I'm honest, and I was trying, in those years, to be honest with myself at least once per crisis.

I was crying because the sewing was a surrender. A pocket is temporary; a pocket says *I'll deal with this later*. Stitching a thing into your coat means you've decided to carry it all the way. Every wash, every mile, every border. I had spent nine years making sure I carried nothing that couldn't be abandoned in under ten seconds, and here I was, anchoring a dead stranger's guilt to my ribs with waxed thread.

*See it home,* he'd said. *The rest doesn't matter. Just see it home.*

"You could burn it," I told myself. Out loud. The wind took it away, which felt appropriate. "You could burn it right now and be nobody again by morning."

I threaded the needle instead.

* * *

Dane Kessler had a theory about towns like Gilt Row, and the theory was this: every town is a lock, and every lock has a key, and the key is never force. Force is what you use when you've already failed. No—you found the person in the town who wanted something, and you paid for it. Everyone wanted something. In twenty-two years of finding people who didn't want to be found, Kessler had met exactly one who genuinely wanted nothing, and he'd shot that one in the back outside Corvane, mostly out of professional irritation.

So he sat in the Dry Ledger's best room—he'd taken it, and the town had adjusted around the fact the way water adjusts around a stone—and he listened to his men report.

"Barkeep knows more than she's saying," said Ferro, the younger one. "Woman like that, running a place like this? She knows everything."

"She knows everything," Kessler agreed. "Which means she also knows what happens to people who sell. We'll come back to her when she's had time to weigh it." He turned a coin across his knuckles, an old habit, thinking tool. "The digger. What do we know?"

"Registered nowhere. Pays cash. Been here two years, give or take. Works the slag claims east of town, alone. Nobody eats with them, nobody drinks with them." Ferro shrugged. "Could be anyone."

"Could be," Kessler said. "Except." He laid it out for them, because laying it out was how he thought, and because his men were better instruments when they understood the music. "Voss comes off the range with a bullet in him and a story in his mouth. He doesn't go to a doctor—there isn't one—but he finds somebody. Somebody who digs for a living, alone, at the edge of town, and who has spent two years being perfectly unremarkable. Voss was many things, but he wasn't stupid. If you're dying and carrying the most valuable piece of paper in the northern territories, you don't give it to a chatterbox. You give it to a ghost." He caught the coin flat on his palm. "Our ghost was in that building tonight. Behind the wall. Watching."

Ferro shifted. "You're sure?"

"I'm sure the plaster had a fresh crack in it and the draft was coming from the wrong direction. I'm sure our hostess poured me a drink she charges triple for without blinking, which means she wanted the interview short." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, because Kessler's smiles always reached his eyes; that was the terrible thing about him. "I let it go because ghosts run. Running tells you their route. Fighting tells you nothing but noise."

"And when we catch them?"

Kessler considered the question honestly, because he considered all questions honestly; dishonesty was inefficient. "Then we talk. Nine chances out of ten, whoever Voss picked up doesn't even know what they're carrying. They think it's a treasure map. People do foolish, brave things for treasure maps, and foolish brave things leave trails." He stood, buttoning the oiled coat, feeling the pleasant weight of the day arranging itself. "And one chance in ten, they understand what it really is. And then—" he paused at the door, savoring it— "then it gets interesting."

He believed, entirely, that he was the reasonable one in this story. He offered payment before violence, terms before threats, truth before lies. He had never once enjoyed cruelty. He simply understood, with the calm of a man reading weather, that the company wanted the Compass found and the loose ends tied, and that he was very good at both, and that the world did not run on mercy but on competence, and he intended to be competent all the way to the summit.

Outside, the night smelled of slag and woodsmoke. Somewhere east, a ghost was running.

Kessler smiled at it, the way you smile at a lock.

* * *

I went out through the cold store at midnight, past hanging sides of meat that swung slightly in the draft like slow pendulums counting down something, and took the drainage cut south of town that Sefa-type smugglers used and that I knew about only because I'd once spent a week mapping Gilt Row's exits out of pure habit. Old habits. The invisible life leaves its marks too.

By dawn I was eight miles out, walking the long way toward Bracken Ford, avoiding the eastern road as instructed, telling myself the whole time that I was heading to the staging town merely to buy passage west and disappear, that the map in my ribs was cargo and not commitment, that a promise made to a dying man in the dark was the easiest kind to break because the dead collect no debts.

The sun came up over the low hills to the east—hills that were, according to every map I'd ever trusted, supposed to keep going, gentle and grassy, all the way to the horizon.

They didn't. Far off, hazed blue with distance, something rose against the sky that had no business existing. Peaks. A wall of them, white-capped even in autumn, standing where the world officially insisted there was nothing but grass.

I stopped on the trail and looked at them for a long time.

Nineteen days, Marta had said the miners' gossip claimed, before the high passes closed. I didn't know yet that she was right down to the day. I didn't know anything yet, except that I was walking toward mountains that didn't exist, carrying a dead man's confession stitched against my ribs, followed by men who wore coats bought by the hundred.

I adjusted my pack, checked my trowel, and kept walking.

Invisibility, I was beginning to suspect, was like everything else in the borderlands. It held until it didn't.
