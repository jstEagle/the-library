## 4. The Girl Who Sells Passes

Bracken Ford smelled like wet wool and bad decisions, which is to say it smelled like every staging town I'd ever passed through, and I felt a brief, unearned nostalgia for all of them.

The town sat where the Sere River bent lazy around a shelf of granite, strung along its bank like laundry nobody had bothered to take in. Timber camps upstream, freight yards downstream, and in between: saloons, outfitters, a livery that doubled as a rumor exchange, and the particular species of person who made their living standing at the seam between the settled country and the Ashkettle Range, selling passage to fools who wanted to cross it.

I was one of those fools now. I was still getting used to the feeling.

Three days out of the border town, three days of riding hard with a dead man's map sewn against my ribs and his letters in my saddlebag, and I'd learned two things. First: nobody had followed me across the lowlands, or if they had, they were better at it than anyone I wanted to meet. Second: the map didn't make sense without a guide, because half the terrain it described wasn't on any survey I'd ever seen—and I'd grown up eating dinner off surveys, back when I still ate dinner with anyone.

The range on Voss's hand-drawn parchment sat at the center of the page like a spider in a web, and the passes threading through it were marked in a cramped, careful script that named things no government cartographer had ever named. The Hollow Shoulder. The Weeping Stair. A pass he'd labeled only *the honest way*, underlined twice, as though he'd been arguing with himself about it for years.

I needed someone who knew what those words meant.

Which is how I came to be leaning against a rail outside the Ferryman's Rest, watching a girl who couldn't have been more than sixteen conduct business like a customs officer.

* * *

She worked the corner near the livery gate, and she worked it well. Not begging, not hawking—negotiating. She stood with her arms crossed and her weight on one hip, and men came to her the way they come to a toll bridge: resentful, but paying.

I watched her turn away a freight man who tried to haggle. Watched her take coin from a pair of hunters who clearly thought they were robbing her, then give them directions with such cheerful precision that I suspected they'd end up walking an extra day for their savings. She wore a coat two sizes too big with the sleeves rolled in thick cuffs, a scarf up to her chin, and boots that had been resoled so many times they were mostly stitching and stubbornness. Her hair was dark and cut short in the way of someone who'd done it herself with a knife and no mirror.

When the hunters moved off, arguing, she caught me looking at her and looked right back. She had the flat, patient stare of a cat deciding whether something was worth the energy.

"You've been there twenty minutes," she said. "Either you're lost or you're cheap."

"Both, probably."

"Lost I can fix. Cheap's your own funeral." She uncrossed her arms. "You want a pass west."

It wasn't a question, and I didn't confirm it, and we both pretended that meant something. "What makes you think that?"

"Your boots are wrong for the lowlands and right for scree. You bought salt pork and waxed canvas this morning at Ferro's, and you asked him about rope, not line, which means you're going high, not floating. And you keep touching your left side like you're checking a pocket." She shrugged inside the enormous coat. "Also everyone who comes here wants a pass west. It's the only thing Bracken Ford sells that isn't whiskey."

"And you sell them?"

"I sell *passes*. Whether they're worth what you pay depends on how honest you are with me." She tilted her head. "So. Where are you crossing?"

"The Ashkettles."

Everyone within earshot went very slightly still—the way a room does when someone says a name they've agreed not to say. A woman sweeping the boardwalk swept a little faster. The freight man who'd been refused earlier suddenly found urgent business elsewhere.

The girl didn't blink. "Nobody crosses the Ashkettles."

"Somebody drew a map of them."

That landed. I saw it land—a flicker behind her eyes, quick as a fish turning. She glanced past my shoulder, down the street, then back, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into a different register. Lower. Business.

"Not here. The Rest has ears in the walls, and the walls repeat things for money." She pushed off the rail. "There's a drying shed behind the tannery. Smells terrible, which keeps people away. Ten minutes."

"What's your name?"

"Sefa." She was already walking. "You can decide whether to tell me yours while you're trying not to vomit from the smell."

* * *

The tannery shed was every bit as advertised. Hides hung in ranks like flayed ghosts, and the air was thick enough to chew. Sefa lit a stub of candle, set it on a barrel, and turned to face me with the unhurried confidence of someone who owned the room despite owning nothing in it.

"Now," she said. "Talk."

I gave her the shape of it without the heart of it. A dead man's obligation. A delivery to make on the far side. A window before winter. I left out the Compass, the riders, the name Kessler, and everything else that would either frighten her or price her services beyond my purse. I'm told lying by omission is still lying. I've decided I can live with that.

Sefa listened with her chin down and her eyes up, which I'd learn was how she did everything—watching over the top of whatever she pretended to be doing.

"A courier job," she said finally.

"Of a kind."

"For a dead man."

"He was alive when he hired me."

"That's usually how it goes." She picked up the candle and walked a slow circle around me, and I let her, because being appraised by a teenager in a tannery was far from the strangest indignity of my month. She stopped in front of me. "The Ashkettle Range isn't on maps because the company paid to have it taken off. You know that?"

"I'd heard."

"Heard isn't knowing. Knowing is: there are valleys in there with towns in them that don't exist anymore. There are trails my family used before the company decided the whole range was theirs, and the survey crews burned the records, and the people got moved or worse. The passes close with the first real snow—not the first snow, the first *real* one, the one that comes down sideways and doesn't stop—and after that, nothing goes over until thaw. Nothing. Not smugglers, not soldiers, not saints." She set the candle down. "Nineteen days. Maybe eighteen, if the wind turns early. That's your window, and it's shrinking while we stand here smelling dead cow."

"Nineteen days," I repeated. "Can you get me across in nineteen?"

"I can get us across in twelve, if the weather holds and you do exactly what I say, when I say it, without asking why." She held up a finger. "First question: how many people know you're making this trip?"

I hesitated a half-second too long, and she saw it. Of course she saw it.

"At least one too many," she said. "Good. Honest-ish. That's better than most clients." Another finger. "Second question: is what you're carrying worth killing for? And think carefully, because I'll know if you lie, and I charge double for silence, triple for lies."

I looked at her—that level, unblinking stare, older than the face holding it—and I thought about Callum Voss bleeding out in my camp with his hand pressed to his gut, trading the last thing he owned for a promise from a stranger. I thought about three riders asking questions in a border town. I thought about the fact that I'd sewn a map into my coat lining like a man smuggling contraband, which, in a sense, I was.

"Yes," I said. "Worth killing for. Which is why I'd rather it never be killed over."

Something shifted in her expression—not softening, exactly. Recognition. The look of someone checking a lock and finding it sound.

"Double for silence," she said. "Fifteen now, fifteen at the treeline on the far side. I choose the route. We move at my pace. If I say drop, you drop. If I say run, you run, and you don't run back for anything—not gear, not the mule, not me." She said that last part evenly, without drama, the way you'd read terms off a contract. "And one more thing."

"Name it."

"If anyone asks, you're a surveyor and I'm your local hire. Surveyors go into the Ashkettles sometimes. Everyone else who goes in is either running from something or toward it, and both kinds attract attention." She stuck out a small, chapped hand. "Deal?"

I shook it. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

"Jude," I said. "Since you asked."

"Did I?" But she smiled, briefly, crookedly, and for a second she looked her actual age, and then it was gone again, filed away somewhere safe. "We leave tomorrow, an hour before light. Sleep with your boots on. Everybody does their first night in Bracken Ford."

"Why's that?"

"Because half this town makes its living off people leaving, and the other half makes its living off what they leave behind." She pinched out the candle with her fingers, unbothered by the heat. "Welcome to commerce."

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing the things you do when you're about to walk into mountains that officially don't exist: buying feed, checking tack, sitting in a corner of the Ferryman's Rest with my back to a wall, listening.

Towns like Bracken Ford talk in their sleep, and if you're quiet, they talk to you. By dusk I'd pieced together a picture in fragments. Freight rates west had doubled in a season. The company—Ashkettle Consolidated, though nobody said the full name any more than they said the range's—had agents in town, quiet ones, asking after travelers. And once, from a drunk teamster who thought I was buying him drinks out of friendship rather than professional interest:

"—bought a pass already, some fella. West, couple days back. Big money, too. Girl wouldn't take him herself, I heard. Referred him on to the Kestrel brothers." He hiccupped. "Man had a look about him. Like a bill collector."

I kept my voice bored. "Lots of people heading west?"

"Lots of people *running* west. Different animal." He drained his glass and looked at me with the sudden, terrible clarity of the briefly sober. "You want my advice? Whatever's out there ain't worth it. Nothing out there but weather and ghosts."

"Then why does everyone go?"

"Same reason everyone always goes." He grinned, gap-toothed and sad. "They think they're the exception."

I bought him one more and went to find Sefa.

* * *

She was in the livery loft, sitting cross-legged on a hay bale with a battered tin lamp, mending a pack strap with the concentration of a surgeon. A mule dozed in the stall below—gray, long-eared, radiating the profound moral indifference unique to its kind.

"That's yours?" I asked.

"Biscuit. She comes with the fee. She carries more than you and complains less." Sefa didn't look up. "You found out about the other one."

Not a question. I climbed the ladder and sat on the bale opposite her. "The man who bought a pass west. Two days ago."

"Kestrels took him up the north fork trail yesterday morning." She bit the thread, tied it off. "He asked about the range specifically. Asked about the high passes. Asked—" she paused, choosing, "—asked whether anyone else had come through asking about the high passes."

The loft felt colder. Outside, the river muttered over its rocks, patient as time.

"So he knows someone's coming behind him."

"Or ahead of him. Or he's guessing." She finally looked up, and the lamplight took the angles of her face and made her look carved instead of young. "Here's what I know, Jude, since we're partners now, allegedly. Two days ago a man with money and a bill collector's face bought passage west and asked questions about you—or somebody like you. Yesterday he went up the mountain with the Kestrel brothers, who are competent, which is worse than being incompetent, because incompetents lose people in the Ashkettles and competents don't. And today you showed up asking me for the same passes." She folded the strap over, tucked the needle away. "I don't need to know what you're carrying. But I need to know: when he finds out you're behind him, what does he do?"

I thought about Dane Kessler's voice on the edge of the border town, polite as a knife laid flat on a table. I thought about what Voss's partners had already done for a fraction of the truth.

"He sends people," I said. "And if sending doesn't work, he comes himself."

Sefa nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something she'd already priced in. She reached under the hay bale and came out with a coil of good rope, a signal lantern, and a short-barreled rifle that had seen honest use.

"Then we don't take the main passes," she said. "We take mine."

"Your passes aren't on any map."

"No," she agreed, and there was something fierce and old in it, something that had nothing to do with commerce. "They're on mine."

Below us, Biscuit opened one eye, considered us both, and went back to sleep. Nineteen days, Sefa had said. Standing in that loft, listening to the river and the wind starting to think about the peaks, I understood for the first time that the number wasn't a deadline.

It was a countdown.
