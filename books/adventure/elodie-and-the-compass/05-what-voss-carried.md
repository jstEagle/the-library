## 5. What Voss Carried

The letters were wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth was tied with a knot I didn't recognize, and I sat there for a good ten minutes deciding whether a dead man's knots counted as locks.

They did, I decided. But dead men don't enforce their own rules, and besides, he'd given me the whole bundle. *See it home,* he'd said. *Home* was doing a lot of work in that sentence. Home could mean the map. Home could mean the Compass. Home could mean Merrow's Landing and a daughter who'd spent however many years learning to spell a word like *abandonment* without crying.

I built the fire up before I touched them. That was deliberate. If I was going to read a stranger's mail—and I want to be clear that every instinct I had said don't, that the Jude Fairweather who'd spent six years sanding themselves off every ledger in three territories would have fed these pages to the flames unread—that Jude needed witnesses. The fire would do. Fire sees everything and tells no one.

There were nine letters. Nine envelopes, each addressed in a careful, schoolroom hand: *Mara Voss, Merrow's Landing.* No street. No house number. As if he'd known the town well enough to trust it with her, or as if he'd been gone so long the town itself had become her address.

None of them had stamps.

I noticed that first, before I even broke the seal on the earliest one—the paper gone soft at the folds, foxed with age, handled so many times the corners had started to felt. He'd carried these for years. Not months. Years. He'd written them, sealed them, and then carried them around the borderlands like stones in his pack, taking them out and putting them back, taking them out and putting them back, until the envelopes knew his hands better than his daughter ever had.

I know something about not sending things. I have a drawer full of unsent letters myself, if you can call a hollowed-out floorboard under a rented cot a drawer. So when I say I understood what I was holding, understand that I understood it the way you understand a trapdoor—by falling through it.

* * *

The first letter was dated eleven years back. The ink had faded to the color of weak tea.

*Mara—if this reaches you it means I finally found the nerve, which will surprise you more than the contents. You'll have questions. Start with the easy one: yes, I'm alive. I know that's not the easy one. I know it's the cruelest one.*

He wrote about small things. The weather in the range country. A mule he'd liked. A stew he'd eaten in Bracken Ford that he was still thinking about two years later, which told me either the stew was remarkable or his life was very thin. He never once said where he was. He circled it, the way you circle a hole in the ice. *Out west of the Sere. Further than the maps bother with.*

Then, near the bottom, the thing that made me sit forward:

*You asked your mother once—I heard about it secondhand, everything comes to me secondhand now—what I died of. She told you exposure, I'm told. It's as good a word as any. What I actually died of was a crevasse on the Ashkettle glacier, and the fact that I climbed out of it and three other men didn't.*

I read that sentence four times. Then I read it aloud, because some sentences need air in them to be believed.

Three other men.

Voss's map showed the range, the pass, the ring of stones, the shaft. What it didn't show—what no map could show—was that the Hollow Compass hadn't been found by one lucky prospector. It had been found by four, and only one had walked out, and the one who walked out had spent eleven years writing apologies to a girl who couldn't read them.

* * *

The second letter was angrier. Or rather, he was angrier at himself, which reads differently but lands harder.

*I've tried to write this as an accident and it won't hold its shape. An accident is when nobody could have done anything. I could have done something. Halloran went down first and I had the rope end and I held it, Mara, I want you to know that I held it, and then the ice moved and the rope went slack and I told myself the rope went slack, and there are days I believe that and days I don't, and the days I don't are getting more frequent, which I suppose is what conscience is—a debt collector who gets more persistent the longer you ignore him.*

Halloran. I turned the name over. It meant nothing to me, which meant everything, because names that mean nothing are the ones that get buried deepest.

The third letter mentioned the others. Breck. Osei. Three men down a crevasse, one man up and out, and between those facts a gap exactly the width of a rope going slack.

*Here is what I took from the ice, since I'm confessing everything tonight: I took the needle. Osei found it first—it was sitting in a niche in the wall like it had been set there, like someone had put it away carefully, and he laughed, we all laughed, we thought it was a surveyor's toy. Then the shelf gave. And when I got out—when I'd gotten out, alone, which is a sentence I can barely write—I went back down the next season with proper gear and I took it. I told myself I was retrieving what belonged to all of us. I told myself I'd bring it out and sell it and send you the money and build us each a monument in our heads. What I actually did was carry it for eleven years and let it point at things.*

*It points, Mara. That sounds mad. I know how that sounds. But it points, and it pointed at you, every time, from anywhere, and I could not make myself follow it home because following it home meant arriving, and arriving meant explaining, and explaining meant saying the word slack out loud to your face.*

I put the letter down. My hands weren't steady, and I resented them for it.

A compass that points at what you most want. Callum Voss had carried a needle aimed at his daughter across half a continent for eleven years, and the distance between him and her had never once been miles.

* * *

By the fourth letter the dates stopped being regular. Some came eight months apart. One had two dates on it, crossed out and rewritten, as if he'd started it, failed, and come back a year later to fail again in the same place.

The middle letters were the worst, because they were the best. He wrote her growing up from a distance—he'd heard she was tall now, that she had her mother's way of standing with her weight on one hip like she was bored by gravity itself. He wrote that he'd seen a dress in a shop window in some border town and stood there so long the shopkeeper asked if he was robbing the place. He wrote jokes. Bad ones. The kind that only work on children, sent to a child who was no longer a child, which he seemed to realize each time and press on anyway, because the alternative was the crevasse again and he'd already lived in the crevasse long enough.

And threaded through all of it, always, the needle:

*I keep it wrapped in my spare shirt at the bottom of the pack. Some nights I take it out and hold it and let it point at you and I pretend that's the same as being a father. It isn't. I know it isn't. But it's the closest thing I have to a direction, and a man with no direction will walk off any edge you put in front of him.*

I fed the fire. Outside, the wind was working at the tent seams, patient, professional. Somewhere south of me, presumably, three riders were asking after a dying man, and here I was reading his soul by firelight like it was a service I provided.

Here's the thing I couldn't shake: I'd known Callum Voss for one night. He'd bled into my camp, traded me a map and a promise, and died at dawn with his boots on. A stranger. By every measure I keep, a stranger.

But grief doesn't check references. Grief saw a man who'd spent eleven years writing letters to a hole in his life, sealing them, carrying them, failing to post them, and grief said: *you know this man. You have a floorboard full of this man.* Every unsent letter I owned was addressed to a woman in the capital who published papers under a name I used to answer to. Imogen Fairweather dug up the past for a living and had never once dug toward me. I'd stopped writing to her years ago—not because I ran out of things to say, but because I ran out of nerve, and nerve, it turns out, is the one supply no dig restocks.

So I sat there reading a dead man's apologies and recognizing the handwriting of my own silence, and I want to tell you I kept my composure, because that's the brand I trade under. I did not keep my composure. There's a stain on the fifth letter that's either grease from my fingers or something else, and I've decided it's grease, and I'd thank you not to investigate further.

* * *

The last letter was recent. Weeks old, maybe. The ink was still dark.

*Mara. I'm dying. Not dramatically—my heart, the doctor says, though the doctor also says I should stop walking, and we both laughed, because stopping walking is death too, just slower and indoors. I met a young person at a dig site. They didn't give me a name and I didn't ask, and I think that's why I trusted them. People who don't ask names aren't looking to sell you anything.*

*I've given them the map. They've promised to carry it across the range and bring you my letters. I don't know if they'll do it. I paid them the only coin I had left, which was watching a stranger decide whether to be decent. They decided. I saw it happen. It takes about four seconds and most people never do it at all.*

*If they're reading this to you, or handing it to you, or you're reading it alone in a room I'll never see—Mara, I'm sorry. Not for leaving, though that too. For the slack rope. For every year I chose the needle over the door. For making you learn the word father as a past tense.*

*The compass is real. Whatever they tell you it does, it does that. But I'll tell you what it really is, since I'm the only man alive who knows: it doesn't grant anything. It just shows you. It shows you what you want, and then it leaves you alone with the knowing, and the knowing is the punishment. Three men went into that ice wanting gold and it showed them gold and they died reaching for it. I wanted you. It showed me you for eleven years and I never once followed it all the way. Don't be me, Mara. When it points, go.*

*That's the whole inheritance. A needle, a confession, and advice I never took myself. Your loving father, who was neither, mostly. Callum.*

I sat back from the fire. My eyes were wet and I let them be, because there was no one to see, and then I corrected myself—there was no one to see *yet*, and the whole point of the last three days was that this was changing whether I liked it or not.

Nine letters. Nine attempts at the same sentence: *I did a terrible thing and I loved you anyway and both of those are true and I never learned how to hold them at once.*

That's not a treasure map. That's not a relic run. I'd spent three days telling myself I was hauling cargo—map, promise, delivery, done, back to being nobody by spring. Sitting there in the firelight with a dead man's failures spread out on my knees, I finally understood what I was actually carrying.

Not a compass. A confession. Nine copies of it, sealed and stamped with nothing, waiting on a girl in Merrow's Landing who deserved every one of them and none of the burden.

And the men riding north asking after a dying prospector—they weren't hunting a map. Maps burn. Confessions hang you. Whatever Voss's former partners thought was in my pack, they'd killed before for less than the truth of that crevasse, and the truth was folded into my coat lining in nine envelopes, and there was no version of this where I handed it over and walked away clean.

I banked the fire. I sewed the letters in beside the map, one layer deeper, against the skin.

Outside, far off to the southeast, too far to be sure and too regular to be wind, I heard what might have been a whistle. Three notes. The kind a rider uses to find another rider in the dark.

Nineteen days to the high passes, Sefa had said. I had a feeling we wouldn't need all of them.
