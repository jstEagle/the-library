## 6. The River That Forgets

There are three rules to the Sere River, and I learned all of them the year I turned eleven, which is also the year I learned that rules are mostly just stories people tell you before something goes wrong.

Rule one: never cross at flood stage.

Rule two: if you must cross at flood stage, do it in the morning, because the melt comes down off the Ashkettes by noon and turns a bad idea into a fatal one.

Rule three: there is no rule three. Nobody who needed rule three lived long enough to write it down.

I stood on the south bank at dawn with my boots laced double and my pack strapped high, watching the river move like it had somewhere important to be and no patience for anyone in its way. The Sere in spring isn't water so much as an argument — brown, muscular, full of trees it had stolen from upstream and was carrying off like loot. It had eaten the bridge at Miller's Crossing two years ago and nobody had rebuilt it, partly because timber was expensive and mostly because the company that owned everything worth owning had decided a bridge wasn't profitable. So you forded. Or you didn't. The river didn't care which.

"Tell me we're not doing this," Fairweather said beside me.

Fairweather — Jude, though they hadn't offered the name yet and I hadn't asked, because asking questions is how you end up knowing things about people, and knowing things about people is how you end up owing them — stood with their hands jammed in their coat pockets, looking at the water the way a man looks at a dog he's been told is friendly.

"We're doing this," I said.

"It looks fast."

"It is fast."

"It looks *deep*."

"It's deep."

They looked at me. "You're really selling this crossing, you know that? Very persuasive. I can see why you charge double."

I charge double for silence, actually, but I didn't say that out loud, because the silence I sell includes my own. What I said was: "The pass closes in nineteen days. We go around the Sere, we add six days, and then we're climbing the north face in snow. You want to explain to me why that's better?"

"I want to explain to you that I can't swim."

I looked at them. Long coat, long silences, hands that moved like they were used to being careful with things. Of course they couldn't swim. People who erase themselves from maps don't learn to swim; swimming implies other people might see you flailing.

"Then stay on the rope," I said. "And don't let go of the rope."

"That's your professional guidance? Don't let go?"

"It's what my father charged extra for." My father, who had crossed this river four hundred times and drowned in a creek two counties over, because the river doesn't care how good you are, only how lucky, and luck runs out faster than rivers do. But I didn't say that either. "Rope's tied to the willow. We go diagonal, let the current push us downstream to the gravel bar. You fall, you hold the rope, I come get you. Simple."

"And if *you* fall?"

"Then you hold the rope and think very hard about finding another guide."

That got half a smile out of them, which for Fairweather was practically a parade. They'd been three days of monosyllables and careful eyes, checking every trail behind us without ever turning their head, which is a skill, and I respected it, and I also noticed it, and noticing things is how I make my living. Somebody was following them. Somebody had asked about them in Bracken Ford, paid too much attention, worn boots too good for the borderlands. I hadn't mentioned it yet. Information is currency, and I wasn't sure yet whose bank I wanted to deposit it in.

We roped up. The willow took the knot the way old willows do, grudgingly, like it had held worse. I went first, because I always go first, because if the river takes someone it should take the person who knows what she's doing and not the client, because clients sue, or worse, they grieve at you.

The first ten steps were fine. Cold enough to make your bones file complaints, but fine. Gravel underfoot, water at mid-thigh, current shoving at my hips like a drunk uncle. Then the bottom dropped away where the channel cut deep, and the water hit me at the chest, and the argument the river had been having with itself all morning suddenly included me.

Here is the thing about flood-stage water that nobody tells you: it doesn't pull. Everyone says *the current pulls you under*, and everyone is wrong. It doesn't pull. It *lifts*. It gets underneath you and decides you belong to it now, and it carries you off like you were always its property and everyone else was just borrowing you.

One step, the bottom gone. Two steps, my feet gone. Three steps, and I was flying sideways through brown water with my pack trying to become an anchor and my body disagreeing, and the rope snapped taut behind me and did absolutely nothing useful except remind me that physics has a sense of humor.

The world became very simple. Water. Cold. The white noise roar of it filling my ears. Somewhere above and behind, muffled like it was happening in another country, Fairweather shouting my name — my actual name, Sefa, which I hadn't given them either, which meant they'd read it off my license papers while I slept, which meant I should have been angrier about that than I was, and I filed the anger away for later because later was looking increasingly theoretical.

The river took me downstream. It bounced me off a boulder — shoulder, hard, I'd feel that tomorrow assuming there was a tomorrow, which felt like a coin flip — and spun me, and for one clean second I saw sky, gray and indifferent, and then water again, and my pack dragged me down and the gravel bar I'd promised Fairweather slid past overhead like a rumor.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the theme of whatever this trip is becoming: I panicked. Not the screaming kind. The quiet kind, the kind where your brain, which has always been your best asset, your sharpest tool, the thing that keeps you alive and paid, simply says, *well, this is embarrassing,* and starts shutting departments. My hands stopped working. My lungs started making decisions without consulting me. Some animal part of me wanted to breathe the river in just to make the cold stop, and I understood, in a distant academic way, how people drown on purpose.

Then the rope went tight again — properly tight this time, jerking, hauling — and a hand closed on my collar.

Fairweather. In the water. Who couldn't swim. Who had, when the rope went slack around my waist because the current had stripped it loose, done the single stupidest thing available and waded in after me, boots and coat and all, and caught my pack strap as I cartwheeled past, and was now being dragged toward the deep channel by a soaked teenager and her stubborn luggage.

"Let go of the *pack*," they shouted, and I heard it twice, once through the water and once through the roaring in my skull, and I let go of the pack, and the river took it — my bedroll, my spare clothes, half my dried meat, my mother's tin of tea leaves that I'd carried four years without drinking a leaf of it — and I would like to report that I felt noble about the trade, but mostly I felt the loss of the tea, sharp and stupid, even as Fairweather hauled me hand over hand along the rope toward the shallows.

We came out on the gravel bar on our knees, both of us, coughing, shaking, the river roaring past close enough to touch, having taken its toll and wanting more. Fairweather collapsed onto their back in the wet stones, breathing like a bellows, staring straight up at nothing.

"You," they wheezed, "can't swim," they wheezed harder, "and you jumped in."

"You were holding the rope wrong," I said.

It came out before I could stop it, and it was such an absurd thing to say that Fairweather laughed — actually laughed, a short rusty sound like a gate that hadn't been opened in years, and then kept laughing, lying flat in the gravel with river water running out of their sleeves, and after a while I laughed too, the shaky hysterical kind, the kind that's just fear wearing a costume.

* * *

We built a fire on the bar because we had to — wet to the bone in air that still thought it was winter — and because the far bank was a wall of pine and shadow and neither of us wanted to look at it yet. My remaining gear hung on branches like strange fruit. Fairweather had lost less: their kit rode high and their coat, soaked as it was, had kept the map dry in its lining, sewn flat against their ribs where I'd pretended not to notice them checking it three times a day.

I noticed everything. It's the job.

"You pulled the rope loose," Fairweather said, after a while. They were feeding the fire small sticks, precise about it, like everything else they did. "When it caught on the rock. You unwrapped it."

"The rock was going to snap it. Better loose than broken."

"So you chose to drown rather than lose the rope."

"I chose to keep the rope *available*. There's a difference."

"There really isn't."

I threw a wet twig at them. They caught it without looking, which annoyed me more than the drowning had.

The fire crackled. The river roared its one long note. And I sat there steaming like a dumpling and realized I was going to tell them. I could feel it coming the way you feel a sneeze, inevitable and slightly humiliating, and I hated it, because I had a whole architecture of silence built up over years and this soggy stranger with their sewn-in secrets had knocked a load-bearing wall out of it just by jumping into a river after someone they'd known for three days.

"My family's from Hollowmere," I said.

Fairweather didn't move. Didn't prompt. Just waited, which is either politeness or strategy, and with them it's probably both.

"It was a mining village. North fork of the Ashkettes. Company town — Meridian Consolidated, if you've heard the name, and if you haven't, count yourself lucky. My grandfather dug for them. My mother ran the boarding house. I was born there." I turned a stick in the fire. "When I was nine, the company decided the vein was played out and the town was worth more empty than occupied. There was a paper. There's always a paper. Compensation, relocation assistance, all of it signed by men in offices a thousand miles from any snow. Most families got enough to move somewhere worse. Mine got nothing, because my father had signed on as a guide for the survey crews the year before, and guiding was contract labor, and contract labor didn't count as residency. A clerk explained this to my mother. She asked him where her children were supposed to sleep. He said that was outside the scope of his department."

The fire popped. Fairweather said nothing, and their saying nothing was somehow louder than most people's sympathy.

"Hollowmere's been empty twenty years. You can still see the church steeple from the high trail. Everything else is falling down. The company pulled the rails and left the rest to rot, and now it's a ghost village that officially never existed, same as half the range." I shrugged, and my shoulder reminded me about the boulder, and I winced. "I guide because I know every unmarked trail in these mountains, and I know them because my father mapped them, and he mapped them because the company paid him to, and the company used his maps to decide which villages were worth keeping and which weren't. So. There it is. I charge double for silence because I grew up in a place that got erased, and I figure somebody ought to profit from keeping quiet instead of getting nothing for it."

I expected — I don't know what I expected. Pity, maybe, which I'd have hated. Questions, which I'd have deflected.

What Fairweather said was: "Your father's maps. Are they still around?"

"That's your response? Not — I don't know. Condolences. Outrage. A speech."

"Speeches don't reopen villages." They poked the fire. "Maps might. If the company buried something at Hollowmere — records, payrolls, anything proving who lived there and what they were owed — a map tells you where to dig. I'm told digging is a specialty of mine."

I stared at them. Here was the thing I couldn't square about Fairweather, the thing that had been itching at me since Bracken Ford: they talked about wanting nothing, moving like nothing, needing nothing, and yet they'd walked into a flooded river for a girl they'd hired off a tavern wall. People who truly wanted nothing stayed on the bank. People who truly wanted nothing didn't carry a dead man's letters across a mountain range for free.

"What's in it for you?" I asked. "The map. The letters. The dying man's promise. You could've walked away. You walk away from everything — I've watched you check every road twice, you travel like a man being followed by his own shadow. Why not drop Voss's promise in the first ditch and vanish?"

For a long moment I thought they wouldn't answer. The fire settled. Across the river, on the far bank, the pines stood black and patient.

"Because I've spent six years being no one," Fairweather said finally, quietly, to the fire more than to me. "And it turns out no one can't keep a promise. There's no one there to keep it." They looked up. "Voss made *me* promise. Not a stranger. Me. First time in six years anybody's asked anything of me specifically, by name, expecting me to exist afterward." A pause. "It's a terrible reason to cross a mountain range. It's the only one I've got."

Well. I didn't have anything to say to that, so I said nothing, and we sat in the kind of silence that isn't empty, which was new for me, and I found I didn't mind it, which was newer.

* * *

We crossed the rest before noon, on the rope, slow and ugly and successful, and I recovered my pack from a snag fifty yards downstream minus the tea tin, which the river could keep, and I told myself I wasn't sad about the tea tin, and I was lying, and I let myself lie, because some griefs are allowed to be small.

It was Fairweather who saw them first. We were climbing the far bank, hands and boots in the wet scree, and they froze with one knee up and their head cocked, the way they did, listening to a distance I couldn't hear.

"Riders," they said. "South bank. Four of them. Where we camped last night."

I wormed up beside them into the cover of a fallen spruce and looked back across the Sere, and there they were: four dots on the south shore, moving along the bank with the unhurried confidence of men who owned horses and knew exactly what they were looking for. One of them dismounted at our old fire ring. Even at this distance I could read the body language — crouching, touching, pointing downstream to the gravel bar, to the snag where my pack had hung.

"They'll find the crossing," I said. "Willow's still knotted. Any tracker reads it in a minute."

"How fast can they cross?"

"Not today. Not with those horses, at this water. Tonight, maybe, if the melt drops. Tomorrow for certain." I chewed on it. "Which means we've got a day, maybe less, on riders who know the range better than I hoped and worse than I feared."

Fairweather watched the dots a moment longer. Something in their face had gone flat and old, the look of a person seeing a bill arrive that they'd been pretending wouldn't.

"There's a waystation," I said, before I'd fully decided to say it. "Old company relay station, two days north, off the switchbacks. Abandoned since the rails came out. There's shelter, a well, maybe a stove that works. And—" I hesitated. This was the part where a smart guide kept her mouth shut and her knowledge priced. "—there may be people there already. Squatters. Travelers. The kind of folk who winter in places that don't officially exist."

Fairweather looked at me. "Friends of yours?"

"No," I said honestly. "But I've heard stories. A cartographer who got run out of the capital for drawing true lines in the wrong places. A medic who left the army the wrong way." I shouldered my dripping pack and winced and grinned anyway, because apparently nearly drowning buys you a kind of recklessness. "Figured a party of erased people ought to stick together. Strength in numbers. Also, I lost half our food to the river, and I hear medics share."

Below us, across the brown roar of the Sere, one of the riders mounted up and turned his horse north along the bank, toward the ford at Graystone, the next crossing down — a day's ride, a night's wait, and then our trail, our tracks, our scent, laid out plain as a signature.

Fairweather saw it too. I watched them see it.

"Two days to the waystation," they said. "Let's make it one and a half."
