## 7. The Company of the Broken

The waystation announced itself a half-mile before we reached it: a crooked chimney standing in the pines like a man who'd wandered off a funeral and forgotten why. Sefa spotted it first from the ridgeline and made a sound I'd come to learn meant *trouble* in at least three languages.

"Smoke," she said. "Thin. Someone's trying not to be seen."

"Someone's bad at it."

"Or someone's hurt." She was already adjusting her pack straps, that quick economy she had, like every decision was a door she'd already walked through. "Hurt people are either grateful or dangerous. Sometimes both. Usually both."

"We could go around."

"We could," she agreed, and kept walking toward the smoke.

I've learned that about her in the four days since the Sere River tried to keep her. Sefa Ilin treats danger the way other people treat weather—acknowledged, unimpressed, occasionally useful. When she'd said *I'm guiding you partly because I owe money to men who don't count days*, she'd said it while wringing river water out of her braid, as if admitting a debt were the same as paying one. It wasn't. But it was more than most people gave me, so I counted it.

The waystation had been a relay stop once, back when the company ran freight through these hills and called the emptied villages along the route *progress*. Now it was a roof with opinions about gravity. The smoke came from a fire built inside a ring of stones in what had been the stable, because whoever lived here understood that a chimney draws attention and a stable wall hides light.

Two people sat by the fire like they'd been expecting us for years and were mildly disappointed it took so long.

The first was a woman past fifty, gray-shot hair pinned with what looked like a pencil, spectacles mended with wire, a surveyor's transit set up on a tripod beside her as if she were taking the measure of the mule stalls. She didn't look up when we came in. She was drawing.

The second was a man asleep sitting up, which is a skill only soldiers and new fathers have. He was big through the shoulders, soft through the middle, with a medic's satchel open at his feet and his hands folded over it like a man guarding a church collection plate even in his dreams.

"Don't step on the instruments," the woman said, still drawing. "Or do. They're worthless now anyway. Everything's worthless now. That's rather freeing, when you get used to it."

"We're passing through," I said.

"Everyone's passing through. That's the whole trouble with people." She finished her line, blew on the ink, and looked up. Her eyes did something I recognized—the quick inventory, the cataloguing, the filing of me under a heading I wouldn't get to read. Cartographers' eyes. My mother had the same look. I felt the old flinch and hated it. "You'll want the fire. Sit down before you fall down; falling down damages my floor and my composure equally."

Sefa had her knife out, casual as a woman scratching an itch. "Who are you?"

"Odile Marchand." The name landed and waited. Sefa's knife hand went very still, which told me the name had traveled further than its owner had. "Yes. *That* Odile Marchand. You can put the blade away; I've been thoroughly punished by professionals. Whatever you're considering would be amateur work."

*That* Odile Marchand. Even out here, even in the borderlands where news arrived late and pre-rotted, the name carried weight. Seven years ago the company's survey maps—her maps, drawn under her seal—had placed a rebel village inside a military exclusion zone it had no business being in. The shelling that followed killed forty-one people. The inquiry found the maps accurate. The inquiry was run by the company. Odile Marchand had spent the years since insisting the maps had been altered, and the years since had spent themselves making sure nobody believed her.

"I don't care who you are," Sefa said, which was a lie delivered beautifully, and Odile smiled like a woman who graded such things for a living.

"You care enormously. You simply haven't decided whether to care safely or dangerously." She gestured at the sleeping man. "That's Tomas Reyes. He deserted from the Ninth Garrison with a wagon full of army morphine and a conscience he couldn't fit in the wagon. He pulled two bullets out of me last winter and I've been failing to leave him ever since."

From the ground, without opening his eyes: "She says that like it's my fault."

"It is entirely your fault. You made yourself medically indispensable, which is the most manipulative thing a person can do."

Tomas Reyes opened his eyes and looked at us—looked at *me*, specifically, the way doctors look, checking color, posture, the set of the mouth. "You're favoring your left side," he said. "Ribs?"

"Old."

"Old ribs still crack. Come here."

"I'm fine."

"He's fine," Sefa confirmed.

"He's lying," Odile said pleasantly, "and so are you, and this is all very tedious. Sit down, boy. The fire's paid for."

I want to say I resisted. What actually happened is that my ribs—which had been complaining since the river, since before the river, honestly since Voss leaned his dying weight against me—and my exhaustion held a brief committee meeting and outvoted my caution unanimously. I sat. Tomas crawled over, pressed two fingers between my ribs with the tenderness of a customs official, and pronounced a hairline fracture wrapped in spectacular bruising.

"Strapped, not bound," he said, already digging in the satchel. "You need to breathe deep or it heals wrong and you cough blood in ten years. I've seen it. It's disgusting."

"I wasn't planning on ten years."

"Nobody is. That's how they end up with ten." He worked the wrap around me with hands that were gentle the way heavy things can be gentle—careful because they knew exactly what they could break. "Where are you headed?"

"Nowhere."

"Everyone's headed nowhere," Odile said. "It's the destination of the age. But nowhere has coordinates, dear, everything does. Where?"

I looked at Sefa. Sefa looked at the ceiling with the innocence of a professional liar. And I thought about Voss's map sewn into my coat lining, about the range that officially didn't exist, about three riders asking questions in a border town, and I lied the way I'd been lying my whole life: completely, and badly, at the exact moment it mattered.

"West," I said. "Over the range. Before snow."

Odile's pencil stopped moving.

Not dramatically. It just stopped, the way a heart stops—a small private catastrophe. She set it down parallel to her paper with the precision of ritual and took off her spectacles and cleaned them on her sleeve, though they were clean.

"The Ashkettle Range," she said. Not a question.

"There's no such place," Sefa said automatically, reciting the official line, and then heard herself, and shut her mouth.

"No," Odile agreed softly. "There isn't. There never was. That was rather the point of my life's great work." She put the spectacles back on and looked at me, and behind the wire and the weariness there was something bright and terrible, the look of a woman who has spent seven years staring at a locked door and has just heard a key turn in it. "I surveyed those mountains. Every switchback, every watershed, every pass. And then I watched my own lines get moved on paper until a village sat where it shouldn't, and forty-one people died of my cartography, and I have spent seven years unable to prove the alteration because the original plates were destroyed." Her voice stayed level. That was the frightening part. "Except they weren't. Were they? Because you're going over that range, and nobody goes over that range unless they're following something older than the company's fiction. So. Who gave you your map?"

The fire popped. Tomas's hands had gone still on the strap.

"Sefa," I said, "you're being paid to guide, not to talk."

"I'm being paid double for silence," she said. "This is me earning it."

Which left me, sitting in a ruined stable with my ribs strapped and my secret itching under my coat seam, doing arithmetic I didn't like. A cartographer who knew the range better than any living soul. A medic with army supplies and a deserter's reasons to stay ahead of patrols. Two people who owed nothing to Kessler or Voss's partners, who had every reason to hate the company, and who were—this was the part I kept circling back to—already bound to each other by something neither of them would name.

Every instinct I owned said: travel alone, die alone, owe no one. That creed had kept me alive and kept me hollow, and I'd mistaken the hollowness for safety for so long I'd forgotten it was a wound.

But Odile Marchand knew passes that weren't on any map, including mine. And the men following me had already killed for less than the truth.

"Voss," I said finally. "A prospector named Callum Voss. He drew it himself."

Odile closed her eyes. "Callum Voss. Of course it was Callum. He was on my survey crew, the first season—he was the one who found the old shaft, up near the summit glacier. The company sealed the site and burned the field notes and Callum quit the same week and went strange." She opened her eyes. "What's in the shaft?"

"A compass," I said. "Maybe."

"*Maybe,*" she repeated, tasting it. Then, briskly, catastrophically, as if she hadn't just detonated seven years of exile: "Well. You'll need a route. The western approaches are watched—I mapped the watch posts myself, God forgive me—and the low trail floods after rain. Which leaves the high line through the saddle, and the high line will kill anyone who doesn't know it, and you clearly don't know it, because you're planning to cross a glaciated range with a cracked rib and a teenager."

"I'm eighteen," Sefa said.

"After the season, you'll be lucky to be sixteen. No. No, this won't do." Odile was already pulling fresh paper toward her, already sketching, the pencil moving like it had been waiting seven years for permission. "Reyes. We're going."

Tomas didn't even look up from repacking his satchel. "We're not."

"We are."

"I'm not crossing the Ashkettles. Nobody crosses the Ashkettles. People *attempt* the Ashkettles and we bury what's left."

"Then you'll bury me closer to the truth than I've stood in seven years, and you'll do it with your own hands, because you are not staying in this drafty barn without me and we both know it."

There it was. I watched it happen and understood I was seeing something older than their argument: two people who had each, separately, decided the other couldn't survive alone, and had built an entire stalemate on top of that decision and called it companionship. Neither would leave. Neither would say why. The waystation wasn't a home; it was a truce with a chimney.

"That's sentimental nonsense," Tomas said, with the flat certainty of a man losing.

"Yes," Odile agreed. "All the important things are."

* * *

They argued for another hour. I mean that literally—Sefa timed it, leaning against the stall door with her arms crossed, wearing the expression of someone watching a slow-motion accident she'd already bet on. Odile argued geography. Tomas argued medicine. Neither mentioned the actual subject, which was that Odile Marchand had been given, after seven years, a road back to the scene of her ruin, and Tomas Reyes could not bear the idea of her walking it without him, and could bear even less the idea of saying so.

In the end it was settled the way most things are settled: by a mule.

She appeared at dusk out of the treeline, a dun-colored, knock-kneed, profoundly unimpressive mule with a company brand half-burned off her haunch and a broken lead rope trailing from her halter. She walked into the stable, stepped over Tomas's satchel, ate a portion of our oats without apology, and lay down against the warm side of the fire ring like a dowager claiming a box seat.

Sefa stared at her. "Is she yours?"

"Not remotely," I said.

"She's ours now," Sefa said, with the finality of law. "That's how it works. If an animal chooses you, you're chosen. Ask anyone."

"Ask anyone what?" said Tomas.

"Whether we're keeping the mule."

"We are not keeping the mule. Mules eat. Mules are loud. Mules attract—" He stopped. He looked at the mule. The mule looked back at him with the ancient, liquid patience of her kind, and something in the medic's face simply surrendered, the way a garrison surrenders: formally, with honors, long after the battle was lost. "She needs shoeing," he muttered. "And she's got a rub on her shoulder somebody left untreated, the bastard. I'll make a poultice."

"And that," Odile said to me, folding her maps with a flourish, "is how it's done. You don't argue a man into a family, dear. You give him something smaller than himself to take care of, and the rest follows."

"We're not a family," I said.

"Of course not. We're five strangers and a mule sharing a fire in a haunted barn, heading into a mountain range that doesn't exist, pursued by men who kill for less than what you're carrying." She smiled, and for a moment the smile was young and sharp and utterly without fear. "Families are exactly the same, only they lie about it less."

* * *

We left at first light, because Sefa doesn't believe in second lights. The party that climbed out of the waystation clearing looked, I imagine, like a joke with a punchline still pending: a smuggler girl navigating, a disgraced cartographer correcting her navigation, a deserter medic leading a stolen mule, and me, strapped and silent, carrying a dead man's map and a dead man's letters and a promise shaped like a debt.

I should have felt crowded. For years, crowding had meant exposure, and exposure meant graves—other people's, mostly, but I'd always assumed mine eventually. Instead, somewhere past the second ridge, with Odile arguing elevation with Sefa and Tomas telling the mule about her diet in the tone men use for children and wives, I caught myself almost laughing at something, and the feeling that came with it was so foreign I had to name it before I trusted it.

Safety. Not the safety of hiding. The other kind. The kind that comes from being surrounded.

I hated how good it felt. I hated more that some buried, starved part of me had known it would.

We made camp that night in the shadow of the foothills, the true ascent beginning tomorrow. Odile spread her charts across a flat rock and traced the high line with one finger, and her finger stopped at a notch in the ridge she'd marked with a tiny, private symbol—not a pass, not a camp. Something else.

"There's a waterhole here," she said. "Spring-fed. Reliable. Any party crossing west will stop there." Her finger rested on it a beat too long. "Anyone might be there."

She didn't look at me when she said it. Sefa didn't either. But I felt the sentence settle over the fire like a change in the wind, and I lay awake a long time listening to the mule breathing, understanding that tomorrow, at that waterhole, we might not be alone.
