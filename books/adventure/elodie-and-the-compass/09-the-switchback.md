## 9. The Switchback

There is a particular kind of shame that lives in the body rather than the mind. I carried mine in my hands for eleven years—eleven years of looking at my own fingers and remembering what they had drawn.

The switchbacks began where the treeline gave up, a stitched seam of trail climbing the mountain's flank like sutures over an old wound. Sefa led, mule behind her, then Reyes, then me, with Jude at the rear watching our backtrail the way other people watch a door in a room they've decided not to trust. The morning was cold enough to make breathing a deliberate act. Somewhere above us, cloud sat on the ridgeline like a lid on a pot.

I had been on this flank before. That was the problem.

Not this exact trail—the Ashkettle Range has more trails than it admits to, most of them made by goats or smugglers or men running from something—but the terrain itself. The way the eastern face broke into terraces of grey shale. The way a hanging valley opened at two thousand feet like a cupped hand. I knew these mountains the way you know a face from a photograph of a crime: intimately, and against your will.

In 1879 I had surveyed this range. Not walked it—surveyed it, which is a different and worse intimacy. Walking a country teaches you its manners. Surveying it teaches you its weaknesses. For fourteen months I had triangulated peaks, measured grades, calculated how many pack animals a column could move per day over which saddles, and all of it had gone into a folio of maps so precise, so beautiful, that the Meridian Consolidated Mining Company had wept with gratitude and paid me triple my asking rate.

I told myself then what every cartographer tells herself: maps are neutral. A map is a knife lying on a table; what is done with it is not the knife's affair.

Then Meridian used my folio to plan the approach to Corrin Hollow, and Corrin Hollow stopped existing, and forty-one people were buried under a rockslide that my contour lines said could be triggered with ninety pounds of powder placed at three specific points. My points. My elevations. My neat, confident little crosses marking load-bearing seams in the mountainside.

They called it a natural collapse. They gave me a medal first—a fellowship, a dinner, my name engraved—and took it back later, quietly, when a journalist started asking questions. By then I had already taken my own name off everything else. Odile Marchand, cartographer, became a woman who drew harbor charts in a port town two thousand miles away and drank enough to blur her own hand into someone else's.

And now here I was, climbing my own disgrace one switchback at a time.

"You're limping," Reyes said, falling into step beside me. He said it the way he said most things—flatly, as a diagnosis awaiting confirmation.

"I'm favoring," I corrected. "Limping implies complaint."

"Your left knee's been locking since the second terrace."

"My left knee has been locking since 1881. It predates your medical license, Doctor, and it will outlast your interest."

He grunted, which from Reyes was a full conversation, and dropped back to check the mule's hooves. I was grateful. Kindness, that morning, would have cracked me open like river ice.

* * *

We made the third terrace by noon and ate hardtack and dried apples in the shadow of an overhang. Sefa sat apart, as she always did when we stopped, chewing slowly and watching the route ahead with an expression I recognized: the look of someone reading a page only she could see. She was nineteen and read mountains better than I ever read paper, which should have humbled me more than it did. Mostly it just frightened me, because it meant she'd notice.

She noticed everything. That was the trouble with hiring a guide who charged double for silence. You got the silence. You also got the attention.

Jude sat with their back to the rock, map-less now—the original burned with half our food on the glacier approach still days below us, though Jude carried Voss's letters in an oilcloth packet against their chest like a second heartbeat. They ate without pleasure, eyes moving constantly: backtrail, skyline, Sefa's face, backtrail again.

"The pass ahead," Sefa said finally, to no one and everyone. "The one the map showed. The low one, due west."

"What about it?" Jude asked.

"It's wrong."

I felt my stomach drop through the shale beneath me. "Wrong how?" I asked, and hated how quickly the question came, how much it sounded like a man objecting before the evidence is presented.

Sefa shrugged. "Too easy. A pass that low, that direct, on the only range in the territory nobody officially surveys? Somebody keeps it that way on purpose. Either it's watched, or it's a trap, or both." She looked at Jude. "You paid me to get you across alive, not across fast."

"And the alternative?"

"North fork of the valley. Higher, longer, ugly. Adds two days." She said *two days* the way other people say *plague*, because two days against the closing snows was a currency we did not have.

Jude looked at me. "Odile? You've seen these hills."

There it was. The question I had been dreading since Bracken Ford, asked casually, innocently, by a person who did not know what they were asking. *You've seen these hills.* Yes. From above. Through a theodolite. With a pencil that later killed forty-one people.

"Only on charts," I said. "Secondhand."

It was not even a lie, exactly. It was a lie wearing truth's coat, which is the most comfortable kind.

* * *

That night we camped in a fold of the slope where the wind couldn't reach, and I lay awake doing arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Because Sefa was right. The western pass was watched—I knew it the way I knew my own signature, because I had drawn the watch-post into the margins of my own folio. *Recommended position for supply depot and checkpoint,* I had written, aged thirty-one and proud of myself, beside a saddle I had graded as ideal for exactly that purpose. Meridian built it there within the year. Kessler's employers owned the ground my lines had blessed. If we went through the western pass, we would walk into the arms of the men hunting us, invited by my own hand.

And the north fork—the ugly, long, two-day detour Sefa proposed—ran through a corridor I had never sold. There was a reason. In my last season on the range, before the Corrin Hollow contract came and ended everything, I had surveyed the north fork privately, on my own time, out of pure cartographer's greed, because it was the one piece of the range no company wanted and therefore the one piece that belonged to me. I had mapped a high route through it, a pass above the glaciers that didn't appear on any folio, any ledger, any ledger's burning. I called it the Lark's Saddle in my private notes, after nothing at all; I just liked the sound. Then Corrin Hollow happened, and I burned my private work along with everything else, or thought I did.

But you cannot burn a mountain. That is the cartographer's curse and, tonight, possibly the party's salvation. The paper dies; the terrain remains. And the terrain remembered itself to me all day—the hanging valley, the shale terraces, the way the ridge broke north at a shoulder I had once measured at nine thousand one hundred and forty feet. My knee remembered too. My whole traitorous body was a survey instrument I couldn't dismantle.

So: I could say nothing, let Sefa take us through the western pass, and watch us walk into a checkpoint I had designed. Or I could speak, redirect us, and answer questions about how a disgraced harbor-chart artist knew an unsurveyed pass in a range she claimed to know only secondhand.

Or—and this was the option my hands favored, my guilty, clever, ruinous hands—I could do neither. I could simply steer. A word here. A doubt there. *That trail looks washed out. The scree on that line will kill the mule.* Nudge the party north by degrees until we were committed to the fork, and let Sefa believe the choice was hers, and keep my secret another day, and another, the way I had kept it for eleven years.

That was the option shame preferred. Shame is a conservative investor. It never risks the principal.

I lay in the dark listening to Reyes breathe and the mule stamp and the wind combing the high rocks, and I understood, with the clarity that comes only to the sleepless, that steering was just complicity with better manners. I had spent eleven years telling myself the knife-on-the-table story, and here was the table again, and here was my hand, reaching.

* * *

Morning came grey and windless, the worst kind of morning in high country—it meant weather was thinking about it. We packed in silence. Sefa stood at the head of the trail, looking west toward the low pass, and I saw her hesitate, weighing her own advice against the two days it cost.

"Sefa," I said.

She turned.

"Your instinct about the western pass is right. But the north fork is better than you think." I heard my own voice come out steady, which surprised me; inside, everything was sliding like scree. "There's a route through it. High, above the glacier line, through a saddle. It's steep but it holds, and no one watches it because—" I stopped. Started again. "Because almost no one knows it exists."

Jude's eyes came up slowly. Reyes set down the pannier he was loading. Even the mule seemed to stop chewing.

"How," Sefa said, in a voice with no inflection at all, "do you know that?"

The question hung there. I could have lied. God knows I had practice. I could have said I'd heard it from a trapper, read it in a dead man's journal, dreamed it. Instead I looked at my hands—those hands, those instruments—and told the truth, or the shape of it.

"Because I helped map this range," I said. "Not harbor charts. This. All of it. Fourteen months, 1879, for Meridian Consolidated. Every grade, every saddle, every place a road could run or a camp could hide. Including the checkpoint positions." I swallowed. "Including the one on your western pass. I put it there. On paper. Before they built it."

Silence. The wind moved somewhere above us, patient.

"They used my maps at Corrin Hollow," I went on, because now that the dam had a crack, the whole flood intended to come through. "Forty-one people. My contour lines told them where to put the powder. I've spent eleven years being no one, drawing coastlines, and I told myself the knife-on-the-table story the entire time, and then you all handed me a reason to climb this range and I discovered the terrain never forgets even when I desperately have." I looked at each of them in turn. "The pass I'm offering you is one I never sold. I surveyed it for myself, before—before all of it. It isn't on any folio. It can't be. I burned the only copy."

"Why tell us now?" Jude asked quietly. Not hostile. Worse than hostile—careful.

"Because yesterday I was going to steer you there without telling you," I said. "Nudge you north by inches and let your guide think it was her idea. Because that's what I do. What I did. I let other people pull triggers on information I provided and told myself I hadn't touched the gun." I took a breath that hurt. "I'm done steering. So. Two days we may not have, through a pass only I can find, offered by a woman whose maps have killed before. That's what's on the table. Decide."

Reyes spoke first, which surprised me. "Corrin Hollow," he said. "I treated survivors. Miners' families, walked out over the passes with burns and broken bones. I always wondered how the company found the villages so easily." He looked at me, and his face did something complicated—not forgiveness, not yet, but the ground forgiveness is quarried from. "You're telling me it was you."

"It was my lines. What they did with them was theirs. Both things are true and I have hidden behind the second one for eleven years."

Sefa spat, deliberatively, into the shale. "Two days," she said. "Snows close in twelve now. You're spending margin we don't have on a pass nobody's seen." She glared at me with the full fury of the young, who have not yet learned that fury is a luxury. "If this saddle of yours doesn't exist—"

"It exists."

"—then we die on a mountain because a mapmaker felt guilty."

"Yes," I said. "That is accurately the situation."

Jude stood a long moment, looking north up the fork, where the valley bent toward the high country and the clouds sat on the ridgeline like a held breath. I watched them weigh it—not the route, I realized, but me. Whether a woman capable of what I'd confessed was worth trusting with anything, even a direction.

"We go north," Jude said at last. "Odile leads until the saddle. Sefa corrects her if the mountain disagrees." A pause. "And Odile—if there's anything else in that head of yours that belongs to Meridian, I want it before it costs us. All of it. Tonight, by the fire."

"There's more," I admitted. "There's always more. You'll have it tonight."

"Good." Jude shouldered their pack and nodded up-valley. "Then let's spend some trust."

We climbed. The switchbacks fell away beneath us, stitch by stitch, and the north fork opened ahead—grey water, grey stone, a corridor no company had ever wanted and no map but mine had ever recorded. My knee ached with every step, keeping its old measurements, and I let it. For the first time in eleven years I was walking through my own work with my eyes open, and I could not decide whether that was penance or pilgrimage.

By late afternoon the light had gone strange and yellow, the kind of light that makes cartographers nervous, and Sefa stopped on a rise, shading her eyes westward.

"Smoke," she said.

We all looked. Far behind us and below, back along the line of our morning's trail, a thin thread of it rose from the trees near the western pass—the pass we had not taken. Someone was there. Someone had been waiting where my old contour lines said waiting would be easy.

"They know the route," Sefa said softly. "Someone sold them the same map you burned."

And I felt the cold come up through my boots, because there was only one other copy of that folio in the world, and I had delivered it personally, in 1880, into the hands of Meridian's chief of surveys—a man named Aldous Wren, who had shaken my hand and smiled, and whom I had spent eleven years praying was dead.
