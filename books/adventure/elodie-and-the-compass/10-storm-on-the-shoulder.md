## 10. Storm on the Shoulder

The storm came up from the south like a debt collector, and like most debts, it arrived earlier than anyone had budgeted for.

I'd been watching the sky all morning, the way you watch a patient whose color has gone wrong. The clouds over the Ashkettles weren't building so much as *curdling*—that gray-green bruise of a light I'd learned to read in another life, in another uniform, in country I still couldn't name without my hands going quiet. Sefa called it "the mountain clearing its throat." Odile, squinting at her instruments, said something more clinical and considerably more alarming. Jude just looked at the sky for a long moment and said, "We're not making the saddle today."

Which was how the five of us—and the mule, who deserves mention because he was smarter than three of us—came to be pinned on a shoulder of bare granite with maybe an hour of daylight left and no shelter worth the name.

"We could push," Sefa said. She always said that. It wasn't bravado exactly; it was more like a reflex, the way some people flinch. "There's a hollow below the ridge line, maybe forty minutes."

"Forty minutes in this," Odile said, "is forty minutes too many." She was holding her left arm against her chest in a way she probably thought was casual and I knew was compensation. Her wrist had been swelling since noon. She kept flexing it when she thought nobody was looking, which is what people do right up until they can't.

"Then we dig," Jude said.

So we dug. There's a particular kind of labor that happens when the weather turns—everyone suddenly agreeable, everyone suddenly useful, words rationed like hardtack. We cut blocks of snow and stacked them into a windbreak against the prevailing gusts, and I'll say this for our little company: we were good at it. Sefa worked like she'd been born in a quarry. Jude moved with that terrible efficiency they have, all economy, no waste, as if effort itself were something to be ashamed of. Even the mule stood where he was told, which was more than I could say for any soldier I'd ever served with.

By the time the wind hit full voice, we had a wall, a lean-to of oilcloth and pack frames, and a fire going in a scooped hollow behind stone. Small victory. The kind you take.

The temperature fell the way a stone falls—no negotiation, no mercy, just physics doing its work. We huddled close enough that our shoulders touched, which none of us would have permitted a week ago. That's the thing about cold: it negotiates boundaries better than any treaty ever drafted.

* * *

Odile's wrist went first.

She held out until the fire was established, until the wind found its rhythm, and then her hand simply stopped obeying her. I saw it happen—the small failure, the fingers curling wrong, the way she looked at them with something worse than pain. Betrayal. Your own body siding with the enemy.

"Let me see it," I said.

"It's nothing."

"It's a distal radius fracture, or I'm a mule's uncle, and I've been called worse." I held out my hands. "Arm."

She gave it to me the way you surrender a weapon—with the understanding that refusing would cost more than complying. I palpated gently, watching her face rather than the limb. Cartographers, in my limited experience, are the worst patients. They spend their lives being precise about everything except themselves.

"Hairline," I said. "Clean, if I'm any judge, and I'm told I am. Splint it, keep it immobilized, and in six weeks you'll be drawing maps again. Assuming," I added, "we're somewhere with paper in six weeks."

"You're a medic," she said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"I was."

"Army?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters," Jude said quietly, from across the fire, "because you've been letting us believe you were running from something ordinary."

I looked at him. He was feeding the fire stick by stick, deliberate, his face half-lit and half-dark, and there was something in his expression I recognized—not judgment. Invitation. The rarest thing in the world, offered by someone who clearly hated offering it.

"All right," I said. And then, because the wind was screaming and the truth seemed suddenly cheaper than the lie, I told them.

* * *

"There was a mine," I said. "Copper, mostly. Owned by one of the big combines—I never knew which shell owned which shell; that was rather the point of shells. I was attached to a garrison company that provided 'security' for their operations, which is a polite way of saying we made sure men who wanted to strike didn't strike, and men who wanted to leave couldn't."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke. Sefa had gone very still, the way young people go still when a story starts sounding like theirs.

"There was a collapse in the deep workings. Eleven men. Maybe twelve—the company's count and the miners' count never agreed, because dead men are cheaper by the dozen. The shaft didn't kill them outright. It trapped them. There was air. There was time. There was even, if you want the whole ugly inventory, a chance—a slim one, but the engineers thought it was real—if we started digging immediately."

I fed the fire another stick. My hands were steady. They're always steady now; that's part of what I paid.

"The order came down before we'd finished rigging the lines. No rescue. The seam was unstable, the company was already writing off the level, and a recovery operation would have cost more than eleven miners' back pay. We were to hold position, disperse any families who gathered, and file a report citing natural causes." I paused. "I remember the exact phrasing. *Unfortunate geological event.* As though the mountain had acted alone. As though it hadn't been asked to."

Sefa's voice came out small. "What did you do?"

"What does anyone do?" I said. "I treated the men I could reach—which was none—and then I walked away from my post during second watch and didn't stop walking for nine days." I shrugged, and the shrug cost me something, though I don't think it showed. "They call it desertion. I've made my peace with the word. What I've never made peace with is the arithmetic. Eleven men weighed against operating costs, and the scale tipped the wrong way, and every man above me slept fine that night. I've met a lot of men who sleep fine. It's the defining characteristic of power, in my experience. The ones who hold it never lose a minute of sleep over what it costs the rest of us."

"That's why you won't let me splint alone," Odile said softly. "Why you check everyone's hands every night."

"Frostbite takes fingers," I said. "Fingers are how we hold things. I've seen enough taken. I count what's left."

Nobody said anything for a while. The wind did the talking, hammering our wall of snow, finding the gaps, complaining about them. Then Odile reached over with her good hand and took one of mine and squeezed it once, briefly, the way you'd test a rope.

"Everyone here has paid something," she said. It wasn't comfort. It was more like a roll call.

* * *

It was past midnight when Jude finally spoke again.

He'd been sitting apart, the way he always sat apart, wrapped in that coat with the strange stiff lining he never explained, staring into the fire with the look of a man doing sums he hates. I'd learned to read him a little by then—or thought I had. Jude Fairweather gave away nothing voluntarily. Everything you got from them, you got by accident.

But the storm had changed the terms. Storms do that. There's something about being pinned under a screaming sky with four near-strangers and a mule that makes silence feel less like privacy and more like cowardice.

"I should tell you what Voss gave me," Jude said. "All of it. Before anyone else asks and I have to decide whether to lie."

Sefa sat up. Odile shifted. I found myself leaning forward despite myself, and I noted, clinically, that my pulse had picked up, which was interesting, because I'd thought that part of me had been excised years ago along with the rest.

Jude laid it out plainly, the way you'd set out instruments before a procedure. The prospector Callum Voss, gut-shot, collapsing into their camp. The map traded for a promise. The letters to a daughter in Merrow's Landing. And then the relic itself—the Hollow Compass, the thing that supposedly points to what its bearer wants most.

"And you believe that?" Sefa asked. "Really?"

"I believe Voss believed it," Jude said. "And I believe the men hunting us killed for less than certainty. That's belief enough to climb a mountain."

"Why tell us now?" Odile asked. Her voice was careful. I understood why. A secret shared under a storm is either trust or strategy, and it takes a long time to learn which.

"Because Kessler will find each of you eventually," Jude said. "Men like him always do. And when he does, he'll offer you something—money, safety, whatever wound you're carrying, salved. I'd rather you hear the shape of the thing from me first, so you know what you're standing next to." A pause. The firelight moved on their face. "Also because Tomas is right. Everyone here has paid something. You deserve to know what you're carrying for me."

I watched the group absorb it. Sefa's jaw was set in a way that suggested she was recalculating every mile behind them. Odile was staring at the fire with the expression of a woman re-plotting a route. And I was thinking about Voss himself—a man who'd crawled out of a crevasse while three others didn't, and spent whatever years remained trying to mail apologies to a daughter.

We were, I reflected, quite a company. A deserter, a disgraced mapmaker, a smuggler child, a ghost who'd erased themselves from every record in creation, and a mule. All of us carrying things we couldn't put down.

"Well," I said at last, because someone had to, and gallows humor is the only medicine I stock in quantity, "at least now the madness has a diagnosis."

That earned a sound from Sefa that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Even Odile's mouth twitched.

Jude almost smiled. Almost. On Jude's face that's practically a parade.

* * *

Near dawn the wind dropped, all at once, the way storms sometimes quit—not fading but resigning. In the sudden quiet I went around the shelter checking hands and feet, my nightly liturgy, and found what I'd been dreading: Sefa's left foot, two toes gone waxy white past feeling.

Early frostbite. Shallow, if I was lucky, and I have never in my life needed luck more professionally.

I worked on it in the gray light while she bit down on a strap of leather and cursed me with impressive fluency in two languages. Rewarming, slow and controlled, the ache that means the blood coming back. By the time the sun cleared the ridge her toes had gone from white to angry red, which in my trade counts as a sunrise.

"They'll blister," I told her. "You'll walk anyway. But Sefa—" I caught her chin, made her look at me. "You tell me. The moment anything goes numb again. The moment. Not an hour later. Not when it's convenient. I've buried enough of stubborn."

She nodded, and for once didn't argue, and I understood that I'd just been promoted—from hired stranger to something else. It sat strangely on me. Most of my life I'd been demoted from things.

We broke camp as the light strengthened, and it was Odile, cradling her splinted arm, who stopped us all with a map spread against her knee and a frown spreading across her face.

"This storm came early," she said. "Weeks early. Too early for the season, too early for sense." She tapped the western horizon, where the clouds had broken to reveal a smear of wood smoke rising thin and straight from a valley we'd have crossed tomorrow.

Someone was camped in our path. Someone whose fires didn't care what the weather did.

Kessler had stopped waiting for the mountain to do his work.
