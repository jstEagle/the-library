## 11. The Hollow Village

There are two kinds of empty places in the mountains. The kind that was never filled—scree fields, dead cirques, the high bald country where nothing has ever bothered to grow—and the kind that was emptied. You can tell them apart from a long way off if you know what to look for. Never-filled places don't apologize. Emptied ones do. They leave their doors hanging open like men caught mid-sentence. They leave fence posts standing in rows for livestock that were sold or shot forty years ago. They leave a church bell in a tower with no congregation, and when the wind moves through it, it makes a sound like a question nobody came back to answer.

I knew what kind of place we were walking into an hour before we reached it, and I didn't say anything, because saying it would have made it true out loud.

"Smoke ahead," Odile said, because Odile says everything, including things everyone can already see. She was riding the mule—His Excellency, Tomas had named him, over my objections, which I withdrew on day three because the mule answered to it now and there was nothing to be done about a mule with delusions of rank. "Or chimney smoke. Or mist."

"It's not mist," I said. "Mist doesn't come out of chimneys."

Tomas looked at me sideways. He does that. He's got a medic's habit of reading people like symptoms, and I could feel him taking my pulse without touching me. "You know this place."

"I know *of* it," I said, which is a different thing, and he knew it was a different thing, and let it be.

Jude rode up beside me. Jude Fairweather has exactly one mode of conversation, and it's *tell me only what I need to not die today*, so when they asked, "What do we need to not die today?" I almost laughed.

"A roof," I said. "The storm behind us is going to catch us by dark, and the pass above us is two days' climb through country with no shelter at all. There's a village down in that fold." I pointed with my chin, because pointing with your hand wastes energy at altitude. "Karagöl. Or it was. Nobody's lived there since before I was born."

"Emptied?" Jude asked.

I looked at them. Jude Fairweather, who had spent years scrubbing themselves off the face of the earth, who flinched when you used their name too warmly, asking me that question with a face like still water.

"You already know the word," I said.

They nodded once.

"Then yes," I said. "Emptied."

* * *

We came down into Karagöl as the light went the color of old brass, and the village did what emptied villages do: it apologized. Doors open. A cradle in one doorway, small enough to hold with two hands, left behind because whoever packed the cart that day had run out of room or run out of time or run out of the belief that they'd ever want it again. A well with its bucket still on the chain. Laundry lines gone to rust with clothespins still clipped to them, holding nothing, holding the shape of nothing, for decades.

The company that did it—the Ashkettle Consolidated, though they've changed their name twice since, the way a man changes his shirt after something spills on it—called it a relocation. There are documents. There are always documents. Compensation schedules, land transfers, signatures from men who signed other men's names. My grandmother kept her copy of her own eviction notice folded inside the cover of her prayer book until the day she died, which tells you everything about where she filed it in her theology.

"You've been here before," Tomas said. We were clearing the largest house near the well—a long stone building with a good hearth—and he was saying it quietly, just to me, while Jude and Odile saw to His Excellency in the lean-to.

"No."

"But your family—"

"My family is from a valley two ranges east of here," I said. "Different valley, same company, same year, same paperwork. They didn't burn our village. They didn't have to. They just stopped the road maintenance and bought the mill and closed it, and within two winters there was nothing to stay *for*. Karagöl was the one that got the soldiers. This was the example they made." I swept ash out of the hearth with the side of my boot. It came up in gray clouds and settled on everything, including, I felt, my insides. "My grandmother came through here once, years later, on the way to somewhere else. She wouldn't stop. Stood at the top of that road all night rather than sleep under these roofs. I asked her why once and she told me the dead don't haunt houses, they haunt the *leaving*."

Tomas was quiet a moment. "And you?"

"And me what?"

"Do you want to stop?"

I wanted to throw a rock at him. That's how I knew he'd asked the right question.

"We're stopping," I said. "It's snowing. I'm not sentimental enough to freeze to death on principle."

"No," he agreed mildly. "You're sentimental enough to charge double for silence and then never once mention your family for six days on the trail."

"Keep talking and I'll start charging you for conversation."

He smiled and went back to work, and I stood there in the gray ash-light of a stranger's kitchen, in a village that had been murdered politely, and hated how much it felt like home. Not this home. Any home. The whole idea of home, sitting there in the middle of the floor with its doors hanging open.

* * *

Odile found the school records.

Of course she did. Odile Marchand cannot walk past a piece of paper the way other people can't walk past a mirror; she has to check whether it's lying. She came back to the fire that night with a tin box she'd pried out of the schoolhouse rubble, sat down cross-legged, and started reading names aloud in the firelight like a priest administering last rites to a parish that had died of paperwork.

"Sefa." She'd stopped mid-list. She was looking at me. "There's an Ilin family in these rolls. Three children. 1871 through 1876."

"That would be my mother's mother's family," I said. "Before they moved east. Before." I fed a stick to the fire because my hands wanted a job. "Small mountains, big paper trail. The company wrote everybody down. That's the joke of it. They erase you and keep the receipt."

Nobody laughed. Jude was watching me across the fire with that look they get—not pity, thank every saint, Jude doesn't do pity—but recognition. Like looking at a map of terrain they'd walked themselves.

"The schoolmaster's name was Demir," I said, mostly to the fire. "My grandmother talked about him. He refused to hand over the attendance rolls when the company men came, so they took the school itself. Lock, stock, and bell tower. He walked out with the children and taught them in barns for two more years until—" I shrugged. "Until the company bought the barns too. You can't fight a thing that buys everything. You can only leave, or stay and be bought."

"Or," Odile said quietly, still turning pages, "you can write it all down and wait."

She held up the roll book. Water-stained, mouse-nibbled, but legible. Every child of Karagöl, 1868 to 1881, in a dead schoolmaster's careful hand. Dates of birth. Dates of withdrawal—the company's word, *withdrawal*, like the families had pulled out of a bank. In the back pages, in different ink, someone had continued the record past the end: names of the displaced, destinations, some marked *returned*, most marked nothing at all, just names trailing off into blank paper like footprints ending at the edge of a cliff.

"He kept counting them," Tomas said. "After everyone left. He kept the list going."

"Somebody had to," I said, and heard my grandmother's voice come out of my mouth, and shut my mouth very fast.

* * *

I couldn't sleep, which surprised nobody, least of all me. I took first watch partly out of duty and partly because the loft of that house had a window facing west, and west was the direction my grandmother had always refused to look.

The snow came down soft and businesslike, filling the streets, erasing the apology. And I sat there with the schoolmaster's roll book in my lap—I'd taken it from Odile's pack without asking, which she'd have approved of, cartographers being great believers in theft for archival purposes—and I read names.

Halfway down the second page I found my great-aunt. Vered Ilin, age nine, withdrawn October 1879, destination east. My grandmother's older sister, who I'd known as a hard old woman with a laugh like a door hinge, and who had been, once, a line of ink in a murdered village, age nine, destination east.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about being displaced. You spend your whole life thinking you're from nowhere, because nowhere is easier than being from somewhere that was taken. You make a virtue of it. You learn every back trail in the range and you charge double for silence and you tell yourself you're free, unattached, mobile as smoke. And then you sit in the ruin of somebody else's identical story and find your own name in it, and you understand that you were never from nowhere. You were from *here*, and everywhere like here, and the company has been keeping the receipts the entire time.

I'm seventeen years old and I have been running from a debt of my own since I was fourteen, and I told myself it was money. It was never money. Money is just the shape the debt takes so I don't have to look at what it actually is.

What it actually is, is a valley two ranges east with no road maintenance and a closed mill and a grandmother who stood all night in the cold rather than sleep under a roof that had been stolen honestly.

I went down the ladder. Jude was on second watch, sitting by the banked fire with Voss's letters, because Jude reads those letters the way other people pray. I sat down across from them without being invited, which Jude permits, barely.

"I'm changing the price," I said.

Jude looked up.

"You hired me to guide you across the range. Double rate, silence included. That contract's done as of tonight." I put the roll book on the floor between us. "New terms. Same route, same rate, but the silence clause is void. When this is finished—whatever's at the summit, whatever Kessler wants, whatever the Compass turns out to be—I want it written down. All of it. What the company buried up there, what they cleared these valleys for, what's worth killing a prospector and hunting five strangers across a mountain range. Names, dates, paper. A record."

"Why?" Jude asked. Not suspiciously. Genuinely. Which is the most dangerous way to be asked a question, because it deserves a real answer.

"Because there's a man named Demir who kept counting after everyone left," I said, "and somebody has to be the next one, and apparently it's going to be me, because I seem to be the only person in this party who can't walk away from anything." I jerked my chin at the letters in Jude's hands. "You're carrying a dead man's apologies across a mountain. Don't tell me you don't understand the job."

For a long moment Jude said nothing. The fire ticked. Somewhere out in the snow, His Excellency complained about the cold in the voice of a mule who believes himself grievously misunderstood.

"All right," Jude said finally. "Written down. Everything we find."

"Witnessed," I said, and spat in my palm, and offered it, and after a pause long enough to be funny, Jude shook it, because Jude Fairweather keeps promises even the stupid ones, especially the stupid ones, and I was counting on that.

I climbed back up to the loft and slept better than I had any right to, in a stolen house, in a dead village, with my great-aunt's name folded under my bedroll like a compass that pointed east.

* * *

Morning came white and silent, and it should have been the best morning of the trip. The storm had passed. The sky over the upper passes was that hard clean blue that means good climbing weather, and the snow would hold the trails firm, and we were one day from the saddle and two from the glacier.

I was packing His Excellency's load when Odile came out of the schoolhouse with her face doing something complicated.

"Tomas," she called, and then, louder, sharper, "Tomas. Where's Reyes?"

Tomas emerged from the lean-to wiping his hands. "Sleeping loft. Why?"

"The loft's empty. His kit's gone." She was already moving, checking, the way she checks everything, twice. "And the copy of the map—the one he was making, the tracing he did at Bracken Ford—he kept it rolled in his bedroll, I saw it last night when—"

She stopped. We all stopped. The village held its breath around us, forty open doors, and the snow squeaked under nobody's boots, because Reyes's boots were already a mile gone.

Jude came out of the house slowly, and I watched them take it in—the empty loft, the gap in the gear, the westward trail with no tracks in it because Reyes, damn him, damn him, had walked out on bare rock where the snow couldn't hold his print, and a deserter medic knows exactly how to walk so that nothing holds his print.

"He took it in the night," Odile said. Her voice had gone flat, professional, the voice she uses for coordinates. "While we slept. He copied the map and he took it and he's gone."

I stood there with my hand on the mule's halter, and I thought about contracts, new ones and old ones, and about how every single person in this party was running from something except possibly the mule, and even the mule had delusions.

Jude looked west, toward the saddle, toward the glacier, toward wherever a man goes when he's sold something he hasn't finished stealing yet.

Then they said, very quietly, "Nobody follows," and turned back toward the mountain, and my stomach dropped through the snow.
