## 12. Betrayal at the Saddle

I've slept in enough bad places to know the sound a camp makes when it's honest. The mule shifting weight. Odile's maps rustling in her sleep like she's still drawing. Sefa talking to the mule, because she claims he's the only one who listens without arguing. Tomas breathing the slow, deliberate breaths of a man who learned to sleep in field hospitals, where you never fully surrendered to it.

That night at the saddle, the camp was honest right up until it wasn't.

I'd taken the last watch. That was the arrangement—Tomas first, then Sefa, then me, with Odile excused on account of her wrist, which she'd splinted herself and refused to discuss. The saddle was a shallow notch between two shoulders of the range, a wind-scoured bowl of gray rock where nothing grew and the stars came down close enough to feel like an audience. We'd made camp in a fold of stone that broke the wind, and I sat with my back against the rock and the map's weight against my ribs, sewn into my coat where it had lived since Bracken Ford, and I watched the fire burn down to a red eye, and I thought: this is as safe as we get.

That's the joke, isn't it. The safest place is always the one you haven't been robbed in yet.

I must have drifted. I'll own that. Not fully—never fully, I don't have that luxury—but the kind of half-sleep where your eyes close and your ears keep working, and the ears get tired too, eventually. The wind in the saddle has only one note. It's a lullaby if you let it be. I let it be for maybe an hour, and when I opened my eyes the fire was down to ash and Tomas's bedroll was empty.

Not alarming, at first. Men walk into the dark to piss. But his pack was gone, and his pack was not the kind of thing a man leaves behind to piss. I'd watched him pack it every morning with a field medic's economy—everything in its place, everything counted. A man like that doesn't abandon his kit. A man like that *is* his kit.

I got up quietly, which is a skill I've had since I was fourteen, and I went to the edge of the firelight, and I looked down the trail we'd climbed, and I saw nothing, because the night was doing its job. But the mule was restless—stamping, turning his head down-trail—and mules know. Mules always know.

I checked the others first. Odile, asleep with her splinted wrist cradled like something borrowed. Sefa, curled tight, one hand under her cheek, looking for all the world like the teenager she technically was and almost never acted like. Both breathing. Both accounted for.

Then I went back to Tomas's bedroll and I did the arithmetic I didn't want to do.

His bedroll was there. His kit was gone. And on the flat stone where he'd been sitting when I took the watch, there was a piece of paper, weighted down with a stone so it wouldn't blow away.

I knew what it was before I picked it up. I knew, because I've left notes like it myself—notes slipped under doors, notes tucked into returned books, all the small coward's paperwork of a man who can't bear to be watched while he leaves. I've written *don't look for me* in three countries. I know the handwriting it's done in.

I picked up the stone and I read it.

*I'm sorry. I've had word my family is alive. There's a man who can get them papers and passage if I bring him what he wants. I copied the map—you'll find the original untouched in your coat, check if you don't trust me, and you shouldn't, I'd not blame you. I couldn't ask you to share this risk. It was never yours. Don't follow. The route down the north face is bad this season, take Odile's pass. Tomas.*

I sat on the cold stone and read it three times, and here is the thing I'm not proud of: the first thing I felt wasn't anger. It was relief. A small, filthy, human relief—*at least he copied it, at least he didn't cut it out of my coat while I slept, at least I still have the original*—and I hated myself for it, and then I felt the anger come in behind the relief like a tide coming in behind a seawall, and then, worst of all, under both of them, I felt something I recognized from the inside.

I felt *understanding.*

Because I knew what that note was. I'd written its cousins. It's the note you write when you've finally found a thing worth more than your pride, and the thing isn't a map. It's a face. It's a name spoken by someone who isn't here. Kessler hadn't bought Tomas with amnesty. He'd bought him with the only currency that's ever moved me off any hill I've stood on: *someone is waiting, and you can reach them if you just give up the thing you were protecting.*

I'd have done it. That's the truth I sat with in the dark, my breath steaming, the mule watching me with his idiot patient eyes. If someone had come to me at that waterhole and said *your mother, alive, one condition*—I'd have walked into the night too, and I'd have left a note with the same apologetic, self-flagellating, unbearably decent handwriting, because Tomas Reyes was a decent man doing a rotten thing, which is the most common tragedy there is.

I checked my coat. The map was there, original, intact, the stitches he'd have had to unpick still holding. He'd told the truth about that much. He'd copied it—when? I thought back through the days. The waystations. The nights he'd sat up with Odile's wrist. He'd had hours, whole dark quiet hours, and none of us had thought anything of a man sitting up with a candle, because that was Tomas, the man who never slept quite as deep as the rest of us. A medic's habit. A deserter's habit. A man who'd already lost everything once, keeping watch.

He'd been making his copy the whole time we were learning to trust him.

And I still couldn't hate him. That was the worst part. I sat there trying to manufacture anger and it kept curdling into something else—grief, mostly. Because the camp had been honest. I'd have sworn it. The way he'd held Odile's wrist steady while Sefa splinted it. The way he'd given Sefa the bigger portion and lied about why. The way he'd looked at me when I told them the truth about the Compass on that storm-bound shoulder—not greedy, not hungry, just *afraid*, the way a man looks at a thing that has already killed everyone who touched it.

He'd been afraid the whole time. And he'd stayed anyway, as long as he could, until the offer got specific enough that staying became a luxury.

* * *

Sefa woke at first gray light, took one look at my face, and said, "Who's gone?"

Not *what's wrong.* She'd grown up in a town that emptied out one family at a time. She knew the shape of a departure.

"Tomas," I said.

Her face did something complicated and then went flat, which is what she does with complicated things. "The medic."

"The medic."

"Kessler?"

"His family," I said, and handed her the note.

She read it fast, the way she reads everything, and then she read it again slowly, and I watched her jaw work. "He copied the map."

"Original's here."

"Let me see it." I let her see it. She held the coat open and looked at the stitching for a long time, and then she said, "He could've just taken it. We'd have been dead in the crevasse field without the map and he'd have known it. He left us the original." Her voice was doing the thing where it wanted to be angry and couldn't quite find the floor. "Why would he do that? If you're going to betray someone, betray them. Don't do it *politely.*"

"Because he's Tomas," Odile said.

We both turned. She was sitting up in her bedroll, splinted wrist against her chest, and she'd been awake long enough to have heard everything, or enough of it. She looked older in the gray light. Cartographers' faces do that—weathered by other people's distances.

"He left us the map because he's the kind of man who can't do a thing halfway rotten," she said. "I've met his kind. I've *been* his kind. He'll have told himself he wasn't really betraying us—Kessler already had the route, or could get it, and the copy changes nothing, and his family is *alive*, don't you understand, they're alive and he buried them in his head years ago and now some man in a clean coat has dug them up and handed them back and all it costs is a piece of paper that isn't even his." She said it all in one breath, and then, quieter: "That's how it works. You never betray anyone for the thing itself. You betray them for the story you can live inside."

Sefa stared at her. "You're defending him."

"I'm *explaining* him. There's a difference, and you'll want to know it someday, when it's your turn." Odile got up, wincing, and looked down the north trail, where the dark was peeling off the rocks. "Which way did he go?"

"Down," I said. "Toward Kessler's route. The low saddle."

"Then he'll reach their camp by midday. And Kessler will have the copy by midday. And Kessler will know the copy is a copy, because a man like Kessler always knows, and he'll wonder why Reyes didn't bring him the original, and he'll wonder what else Reyes didn't bring him." She said it like she was reading terrain. "The amnesty will hold exactly as long as the map is useful."

"Then we should go after him," Sefa said. "Catch him before—"

"No," I said.

They both looked at me.

I'd been thinking about it since the arithmetic, turning it over the way you turn a stone to see what's under it. Here's what was under it: we were four, minus Odile's wrist, plus a mule, with food for six days if we ate like monks. The pass closed in five. Kessler had riders, horses, and a head start on the only other route. If we went down after Tomas, we spent our margin—our whole margin, everything—on a man who had already made his choice, to arrive at a camp where we'd be outnumbered and outgunned and known.

And if we caught him? What then. Tie him up? He'd have left us anyway, in his heart, the moment the offer was made. You can't rope a man to a promise. I'd tried it with myself for years and I was the only one I'd ever managed to hold.

"We don't pursue," I said.

"He sold us," Sefa said.

"He sold the *map*. We're still here." I looked down the trail, at the place where the dark had been, where a decent man was walking toward a bad bargain with his heart full of his family's faces. "And he left us the original. And he left a note. And he told us the north face is bad, which he didn't have to do. That's not a man selling us. That's a man trying to be in two places at once, which no one has ever managed."

"So we just let him go." Sefa's voice cracked on it, and she hated that it cracked, and I pretended not to notice, because that's the only kindness she accepts.

"We let him go," I said. "And we move. Now, fast, before the weather reads the same note Kessler's reading."

Odile was already rolling her bedroll with one hand, which is a thing I'd watched her get frighteningly good at. "The pass I never sold," she said. "It adds a day. Maybe two."

"Then we don't have five days," I said. "We have the pass, or we have nothing."

Sefa stood there a moment longer, looking down the trail after a man who wasn't there anymore. Then she said, to the mule, not to me: "Everyone leaves. At least he left a note," and she went to pack.

* * *

We climbed.

The pass Odile never sold was a cartographer's sin and a traveler's mercy—a shelf of stone that threaded the shoulder of the range a thousand feet above the official route, narrow enough that we walked the mule through single file with the drop on his left and the wall on his right, wide enough that Odile, navigating by memory and guilt, kept us off the scree slopes that would have announced us to every glass in the valley below.

She didn't talk while she navigated. Sefa didn't talk at all. I brought up the rear, which meant I spent the day looking back, which meant I spent the day looking down the north face, at the thread of the low route, at the place where a man with a copied map was walking toward a promise that had already expired.

I thought about what I'd tell him if he came back. I thought about what I'd have written in his place. I thought about my mother, who I hadn't thought about in years by design, and how the Compass, when I finally held it, had pointed not at treasure but at Merrow's Landing, and how Tomas's needle, if he'd had one, would have pointed at a kitchen somewhere, a door, a voice. We were the same, all of us. That was the whole rotten secret of the range. Nobody climbs it for gold. They climb it for the thing they left at the bottom.

We made the high shelf by late afternoon, and from there we could see down into the valley on the far side, and that was when Sefa, who had gone ahead to scout the descent, came back up the trail at a pace that made my stomach drop before she said a word.

"Smoke," she said.

"How much?"

"One camp. Big. Down in the valley, on the route below us." She was breathing hard. "And Jude—there are horses. More than before. And they're not moving. They're *waiting*."

Waiting. Camped on the low route, between Tomas and the summit, patient as a trap. And I understood, then, what Odile had predicted by midday and the day had confirmed by dusk: Kessler had the copy, and the copy had told him everything he needed, and Tomas Reyes had walked into that camp carrying the only thing he had left to trade—himself—and Kessler's idea of amnesty had been waiting there all along, warm and lit, like a fire built for one.

Sefa watched my face. "We could go around," she said. "High route. Lose a day we don't have."

"We could," I said. I looked at the smoke, at the small, ordinary campfire of men who had killed for less than a map, and I thought about a note weighted down with a stone, and a man who'd left us the original because he couldn't help being decent even while he betrayed us.

"He'll talk," I said. "When Kessler's done with him. He'll tell them everything—the crevasse, the letters, all of it. He knows the route we're taking. He's the only one who does besides Odile."

Odile came up beside me, wrist against her chest, and followed my gaze down to the smoke. "Then we'd best hope," she said quietly, "that he lies as well as he copies."

Below us, the fire burned on, patient as a held breath, and above us the first clouds of the season were already stacking up against the summit like something being saved for later.

Five days to the pass. Four, if Odile's mercy held. And somewhere down in that valley, a man who'd chosen his family over us was learning what Kessler's family values were worth.

I turned up the trail. "We climb through the night," I said.
