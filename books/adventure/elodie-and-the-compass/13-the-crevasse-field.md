## 13. The Crevasse Field

The glacier announced itself a mile before we reached it, the way bad news does—not with a roar but with a change in the quality of the silence. The wind stopped carrying the smell of pine. The light went white and flat and honest in the way light is honest about nothing, showing you everything and telling you nothing. Odile stopped at the edge of the ice and looked at it the way a woman looks at a house she was once happy in and then ruined.

"I surveyed this," she said. "Eleven years ago. We called it the Meridian Shelf, because the company liked names that sounded like progress." She adjusted her glasses with a gloved knuckle. "There's no meridian here. There never was. But the men paying for the survey liked the word, so the word stayed, and the glacier didn't care, because glaciers don't read."

"Comforting," Sefa said.

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be a warning. I mapped this ice, and maps lie, and ice lies worse, and I am the only one of the three of us qualified to tell you how."

The mule, whose name was Biscuit because Sefa had named him on the second day and refused to be talked out of it, stamped and blew steam through his nostrils. He had carried half our food, the tent, the rope, and Odile's survey case up nine days of switchbacks, and he had done it with the air of a creature performing a favor he intended to mention later, at length.

"Stay roped," Odile said. "Stay spread. And whatever you hear under you, don't stop to listen."

We went out onto the ice.

* * *

A glacier, I learned that morning, is not a thing. It is a slow argument between weight and time, and the surface you walk on is just the latest sentence. Underneath, the argument had opened gaps you could drop a church into. The crevasses ran in long diagonals, some bridged with snow that looked exactly like the snow that wasn't bridged, which is the glacier's oldest joke, and one it never tires of telling.

Voss's letters had described this place. I had read them by firelight until I knew the sentences the way you know a scar—*the ice opened like a mouth, and I went in, and what I found in there I will not write down, not even here.* He had walked out of a crevasse on this shelf with a needle of black glass in his pocket and three men unaccounted for. I had spent the whole crossing telling myself the letters were the fevered geography of a dying man.

Then Odile stopped, crouched, and brushed snow from a seam in the ice, and I saw the diagonal of it running away into blue dark, and I understood that the letters had been a map too, and we were standing on the margin.

"There," she said quietly. "The big one. It runs northwest. If my memory of my own sin is accurate, it goes down two hundred feet and turns under the serac wall. We can cross above it, or we can go around by the lateral moraine and lose four hours."

Four hours. We had maybe one day of margin before the pass closed, and Kessler had a day less of sentiment.

"Above it," Sefa said. "Biscuit hates moraine."

"Biscuit," I said, "has never expressed an opinion in his life."

"He expresses it constantly. You just don't listen."

We crossed above it. For an hour, the ice held us the way a sleeping animal holds its breath, and I let myself believe the morning would stay boring, which is the last thing I let myself believe before every bad thing that has ever happened to me.

* * *

The ambush came from the rocks on the eastern flank, where the glacier met the mountain in a jumble of stone that had been calving off the slope since before there were names for any of it. I heard the mule first—Biscuit screamed, a sound I had not known a mule could make, high and human and wrong—and then the first rifle shot cracked off the ice walls and went somewhere none of us wanted to imagine.

"Down!" Odile shouted, and then, absurdly, "Biscuit—!"

I hit the ice and slid. Sefa had the mule's lead and was hauling him behind a serac the size of a chapel, and Tomas was dragging Odile by the collar of her coat, and the second shot came and took something from the world that I felt before I understood it—a wet sound, a cry cut short, the particular silence a person makes when they stop being loud.

"Odile's hit!" Tomas roared, in the voice of a man who had spent years being ordered not to treat people and had decided, somewhere on the switchbacks, that he was done with orders.

"Go!" Sefa screamed at me. "The crevasse—the big one—get them in it!"

It is a strange thing, to be told to hide in the mouth that ate three men. But the mouth was the only door, and Kessler's men held the rocks, and the arithmetic of survival does not care about your feelings.

I got Odile's arm over my shoulder. She was conscious, which Tomas later said was the only lucky thing about the whole hour, and she was talking—of course she was talking—giving me instructions about her own bleeding. "Pressure here, not there, the tourniquet is in the left case, the *left*, Fairweather, do you ever listen—" The blood was coming from her thigh, high up, and it was soaking through my glove, warm in a place where nothing should be warm.

Sefa cut Biscuit loose.

I want to be exact about this, because she has never let me make it smaller than it was, and she shouldn't. She cut the lead rope with her knife, slapped his flank, and drove him out onto open ice toward the rocks and the gunfire, packed panniers and all, because a panicked mule runs straight and a shot mule is a distraction, and half our food was a price she paid in one motion and never once mentioned again. Biscuit ran. The shooting followed him. The last I saw of that mule was his ears, going away from us over the blue ice with half our winter on his back, and then the ice shelf groaned, and a bridge of snow that had looked exactly like the other snow gave way beneath his hind hooves, and the glacier's oldest joke landed, and he was gone, and the shooting stopped, because even Kessler's men apparently had a line, or because they'd gotten what the shots were for.

Half the food. The tent. The rope.

"Move," I said, and my voice didn't sound like mine.

* * *

The crevasse mouth was a slot you could have driven a cart into, and we went in on a rope Odile had insisted we still carried coiled at her own belt—her survey rope, the one she'd used eleven years ago, the one she said she'd kept the way a drunk keeps one bottle, for honesty's sake. Tomas went down first with her in a harness of tied coats. Sefa came third. I came last, and as I went over the lip I looked back once at the rocks on the eastern flank and saw, very clearly, a man in a grey coat lowering a rifle, and I saw him see me.

Then the blue took me, and the world above became a strip of white sky like a wound in the ceiling of everything.

* * *

Here is what the inside of a glacier looks like: it looks like the inside of a jewel that has decided to drown you. The light came down through the ice in shafts of blue so deep it felt less like color than like weather. The walls were ribbed and flowing, frozen argument, and the floor was old snow packed to the density of stone. It was, Tomas announced, after checking Odile's wound by the light of a single candle stub, the warmest place for forty miles—out of the wind, out of the rifle lines, thirty degrees above the surface by the simple mercy of being underground.

It was also the crevasse from the letters. I knew it the way you know a room from a description. The serac wall at the northwest turn. The shelf of ice at head height where the light pooled. Voss had written, *I lay on the shelf and watched the blue and thought, this is what the inside of wanting looks like.* I put my hand on the shelf and left it there a moment, like touching a grave marker, which is what it was.

"Is it true," Sefa said quietly. She had wedged herself against the wall with her knees drawn up, and she was looking at me with an expression I hadn't seen on her before—past the wariness, past the transaction, into something rawer. "What's in the letters. Three men came in here and one walked out."

"Four came in," I said. "Voss wrote that the ice took one on the way in. Three were in here. He walked out alone."

Tomas's hands didn't pause in their work. He had Odile's boot off and the wound packed and was tying off the dressing with the economy of a man who had done this by lantern light in worse places. "Then we're sheltering in a grave," he said.

"We're sheltering in the only room in the range," I said. "The grave part was already true before we arrived."

Odile laughed—a thin, bloodless sound, from a woman conserving everything, including her humor. "At least," she said, "it's a well-mapped grave. I did the survey. I can tell you exactly how far it is to the turn." Her eyes found mine in the candlelight. "Fairweather. The men on the rocks. They'll have spotters on the rim by dark. They know we're in here. They're not stupid enough to come down, but they're cruel enough to wait."

"How long can we wait?" Sefa asked.

Tomas and I looked at the panniers we'd carried down on our backs, the quarter of our supplies that had been on our persons and not on a mule. Two days of food for five, minus one. A candle stub. A flask of the medicinal brandy that Tomas guarded like a relic.

"Two days," Tomas said. "Three if we don't move. Longer if it snows, because snow means they can't see us and we can't be seen, which cuts both ways."

"And if it doesn't snow," Sefa said, "we come up out of this hole into their guns."

Nobody answered that, because the answer was yes, and we all knew it, and there is a kind of honesty in a crevasse that doesn't exist anywhere else on earth. The blue light made us all look like the dead already. Odile's face was grey under the candle flame. Tomas's hands were steady, which I understood now was not calm but discipline, the steadiness of a man who had once been steady on an order and had paid for it with his uniform and his country and had decided the steadiness would from now on belong only to himself and to whoever lay in front of him bleeding.

* * *

In the night watch, while the others slept in a heap of shared coats that would have been sentimental anywhere else and was merely thermal here, I sat on the shelf Voss had written about and held the candle and looked at the walls.

The ice keeps things. That's the truth no one tells you about glaciers—they are not erasers, they are archives. Everything that falls in, they file. And the walls of this room had been filed thoroughly. Twenty feet up, where the light shaft hit, I could see a dark line woven through the ice: a rope, old, its fibers frozen mid-strain, still tied in a bowline some dead man's hands had tied. Lower, near the floor, a boot sole, the stitching intact, pointing at nothing. And near the shelf, at the height of a kneeling man, scratched into the ice with something harder than a fingernail—maybe a knife tip, maybe a crampon—four names.

I read them twice. I won't write them here. They weren't Voss's. Voss had told the truth in his letters, at least about that: he had come in with three men and walked out alone, and the three had stayed, and two of them had left their names the way you leave a name anywhere—so that someone, someday, would know you had been a person and not merely a loss.

The third name was scratched lower, alone, half-flooded by later ice, as if cut by a man lying down.

I sat there a long time with the candle and understood something that had been circling me since Bracken Ford and finally landed. The Compass wasn't a treasure. It had never been a treasure. It was a thing that had been *found here*, among this, and everything that had happened since—Voss's flight, the partners, the killings, Kessler, all of it—was men hunting an object that had been pulled out of a room full of evidence, and the evidence said: *this is what wanting does. It brings you here. It leaves your name in the ice.*

"Fairweather." Odile's voice, low, from the floor. She wasn't asleep. I don't think she'd slept at all. "You're staring at the wall like it's a mirror."

"It might be," I said.

She was quiet a moment. "When we get out of here," she said, "I'm going to redraw the survey. All of it. Every lie I sold them, corrected. My name on it. Let them come for me." A breath. "Assuming I keep enough blood to walk."

"You'll keep the blood," Tomas said from the dark, not opening his eyes. "I've decided. I've had enough of men deciding otherwise."

Above us, faint through thirty feet of ice, came a sound I had been waiting for and dreading: boots on the rim. One set, pacing. Then a second. Then, distantly, a shout in a voice I knew—Kessler's voice, carrying that terrible reasonableness of his even through a glacier, even muffled, even aimed at men who had no reason left to believe him.

And then the snow came. I felt it before I heard it—a softening of the wind's voice, a change in pressure, the whole mountain drawing a blanket over itself. Sefa woke, and looked up at the wound of white sky, and said the truest thing anyone said that night:

"It's snowing. They can't watch the hole."

"Yes," I said, "and we can't see the pass, and we've got one day of margin and a quarter of our food, and Odile can't walk fast."

"Then we'd better think of something," she said, "before the snow stops."

I was still holding the candle, and the light caught the scratched names one more time, and I thought: Voss, you walked out of here with the only thing in this room that points anywhere, and you spent your whole life trying to give it away. I'm starting to understand why.

What I didn't know—what none of us knew, huddled in the blue dark with one candle and a quarter of our food—was that up on the rim, in the falling snow, one of Kessler's men was already having second thoughts. And his name was Tomas Reyes.
