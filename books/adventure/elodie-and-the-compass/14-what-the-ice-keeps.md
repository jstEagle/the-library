## 14. What the Ice Keeps

The crevasse was not a shelter so much as a wound in the glacier that we had the poor manners to occupy.

Sefa and Reyes lowered Odile down on the rope in a series of small disasters—her boot catching the lip, her breath coming out in a white scream she tried to swallow, the litter swinging once against the blue wall hard enough that I heard her teeth click shut on whatever sound wanted out. Then Sefa dropped after her, then the packs, then me last, because I'd decided years ago that if anything went wrong at the bottom of a hole, I preferred to be the one who saw it first.

The mule was already gone. So was half our food, the tent, the map, and Odile's left forearm, which sat under Tomas's splint doing something no forearm should do. The bullet had gone through clean, which everyone kept saying like it was good news, as though flesh were a door you could simply walk through if you didn't linger.

"Cozy," Sefa said, looking around.

The crevasse ran maybe forty feet before it narrowed to a throat of packed ice too tight for any of us but Sefa, and even she shook her head when she tested it. Above us, the sky showed as a jagged ribbon of grey, snow sifting down through it in slow, indifferent spirals. The walls curved inward overhead, which meant the storm couldn't find us, and also meant we were sitting inside the glacier's closed fist.

"Nobody climbs out until this stops," Reyes said. He said it to all of us, but he was looking at Odile, who had gone the particular grey of someone deciding how much pain to admit to.

"I've been in worse holes," Odile said.

"You haven't," I said.

"No," she admitted. "But I've drawn them."

Tomas worked by lamplight while Sefa melted snow over the little spirit stove and I did what I do best, which is nothing, usefully. I checked the rope. I counted the food twice and got the same depressing number both times. I arranged myself against the ice wall where the cold came through my coat slow and patient, like a debt collector who knows exactly where you live.

Odile slept—or lay with her eyes closed, which from her was nearly the same thing—and eventually Sefa slept too, curled against the pack with her hood up, seventeen years old and running on nothing. Reyes stayed awake out of professional habit, his hands moving over his kit, rewrapping things that didn't need wrapping.

That left me and the wall.

I want to say I noticed the niche right away. I didn't. What I noticed first was a scratch, low down where the blue light pooled—a line cut into the ice, deliberate, man-made, the kind of mark you make when you have a knife and time and no other way to say *I was here.*

I crouched. My knees announced themselves. Behind me, Reyes asked what I was looking at, and I didn't answer, because I wasn't sure yet.

It was a name. Or the start of one: **HALLOR** — the rest lost where the ice had flowed and healed over decades, smearing the letters like a thumb across wet ink. Below it, another scratch, smaller: a date. A year that was older than I was.

"Halloran," Reyes said quietly behind me. "There was a Halloran on Voss's crew. One of the three."

"One of the three who never walked out," I said.

The lamp threw our shadows up the wall, huge and wavering, and above the name the ice went on rising, blue going to green going to black, forty feet of frozen time pressed into glass. And now that I knew what to look for, I saw more.

A rope. That was the thing that stopped my breath for a second—an actual rope, or what was left of one, sealed a hand's depth inside the wall like a fly in amber, its fibers gone dark, its end frayed where it had parted under load. Someone had anchored here. Someone had climbed here. Someone had fallen here, and the rope had told them the truth about their weight, and the glacier had taken the rest.

And lower still, half-melted into the floor near the niche, a boot. Leather, rotted soft, the sole gone, laces still tied. Whoever it belonged to had stepped out of it, or been stepped out of it, and the ice had folded over the place where they'd stood and kept the shape.

"Jude," Reyes said.

"I see it."

"Do you see—" He stopped. He was a medic; he'd seen bodies do everything bodies can do. But there's a difference between a body and a grave that's been open for thirty years, holding its dead politely in glass, waiting for someone to look.

Voss's letters were in my coat, sewn where the map used to be, because apparently I'd developed a habit of sewing things into coats and never learning from it. I didn't take them out. I knew what they said. I'd read them enough times by now to recite them: *We found it in the ice, in a crack below the saddle, and Marta said it pointed true, and we laughed.* Four men had come up onto this glacier laughing. One had walked down.

I put my hand flat against the ice beside Halloran's name. It was cold the way stone is cold, impersonal, and underneath my palm, deep in the blue, I could just make out the shadow of the niche itself—a hollow the size of a loaf of bread, smoothed oddly round, as though something had rested there long enough for the ice to learn its shape.

The Compass had lain there. Years. Decades. In the dark, in the cold, in a grave with three men's names scratched around it like a fence.

"It's not a treasure," I said. I hadn't meant to say it out loud.

Reyes looked at me.

"Think about it." My voice came out low, because Sefa was asleep and Odile might be, and some things shouldn't be said loudly inside a tomb. "Voss found it here. With them. Three men dead around it and him alive with a relic in his pocket. And what does he do? He doesn't sell it. He doesn't use it. He spends twenty years running from his own partners and dies gut-shot in the borderlands handing a stranger a map and a stack of unsent letters." I turned back to the wall. "You don't hide treasure for twenty years. You hide evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

"Of what it does." I touched the frayed ghost of the rope again, careful, the way you touch a wound. "Marta said it pointed true. That's in the letters. Pointed true at what? At what each of them wanted most—that's the story, isn't it, the story everyone kills for. But four men found it together and only one walked out. You don't kill each other over a compass that points north. You kill each other when it points somewhere different for every hand that holds it. When it shows every man exactly where his wanting lives, and none of you can bear what the others' wanting looks like."

Reyes was quiet a long moment. Down the crevasse, the wind made a sound through the upper gap like breath drawn through teeth.

"And Voss?" he said finally. "What did it show him?"

"That's the part he wrote about," I said. "Sort of. He wrote around it. Twenty pages of circling it like a sore tooth. The closest he comes is—" I closed my eyes; I didn't need the letters, I had them by heart. *'It showed me the road home and I understood I had burned it myself, and that knowing the way does not unburn it.'*

"He had a daughter," Reyes said.

"He had a daughter he left. And a crew he left. Half of them in the ground." I looked at the names again, at Hallor- and the date, and thought about a man kneeling here with a knife, cutting letters into ice with his hands shaking, while his partners lay wherever the boot's owner lay, and the thing that had done it sat in its little hollow pointing—at what? At home? At gold? At each other's throats?

At nothing, maybe. Maybe for Voss it had pointed at nothing, and nothing was the honest answer, and he'd carried that honesty out of the ice like a stone in his boot for the rest of his life.

"They weren't killed for it," I said slowly, working it through as I spoke, the way you dig. "Or not only. Kessler's people killed a fourth man for less than the truth, Voss told me that much. But these three—" I nodded at the wall. "These three might have done it themselves. Over it. Because of what it showed them. And Voss walked away knowing that, carrying it, and he couldn't send a letter, couldn't face a daughter, couldn't even die without paying a stranger to finish his confession for him."

"You're saying it's not magic."

"I'm saying it doesn't matter whether it's magic." I sat back on my heels. "It shows you what you want. Truly shows you. And men who see that clearly stop being able to lie to themselves, and men who can't lie to themselves start doing arithmetic about the people around them—who's between them and the wanting, who's worth more alive or dead. Kessler's men will hold it and see their own hearts, and they'll hate what they see, and they'll hate whoever made them see it. We don't need to understand it. We just need to understand what it costs."

Above us, the storm thickened. Snow stopped sifting and started pouring through the gap, a white rope of it, and the temperature in the crevasse dropped the way it does when the mountain decides to remind you whose house this is. Reyes got the stove shielded and Sefa woke without being shaken, the way smugglers wake, all at once and silently, her hand already finding her knife.

"Storm's set in," she said, stating the obvious, because someone had to.

"Tomas?" Odile's voice, from the litter, thin and level. "How bad?"

"Your arm's bad. Your color's worse. Nobody's dying tonight if I have opinions about it." He glanced at me. "Which I do."

So we settled in. There's a particular intimacy to being trapped overnight in a hole in a glacier with three people you've known for days and would now, apparently, die for—it arrives without discussion, the way weather does. Sefa took first watch at the throat of the crevasse. Reyes dozed sitting up with his hand on Odile's good wrist, counting her pulse in his sleep, probably. Odile drifted in and out, and once, surfacing, she looked at the wall with the names and said, "I surveyed this glacier. In '09. For Marchand and Vale—the company. We mapped the surface only. Standard practice. You don't sound the ice." Her eyes found mine in the lamplight. "They paid us not to ask what was under it."

"Did you ask?"

"I drew very straight lines," she said, and closed her eyes again.

I didn't sleep. I sat with my back to the wall an arm's length from Halloran's name and let the cold work on me and turned the night's arithmetic over and over.

Here is what I had built my whole life on: that wanting was the trap. Want a thing and it owns you; want nothing and you're free. I'd erased myself from every record, every family table, every map that mattered, because a man who wants nothing can't be bought, can't be hunted, can't be held. Callum Voss had died proving the opposite—that a man can carry a thing he refuses to want and be owned by it anyway, owned by the promise, by the guilt, by the names in the ice.

And here was the clarity, arriving at two in the morning in a graveyard, as clarity prefers to: the Compass had never been the trap. The Compass was a mirror. The trap was what we brought to it. Kessler wanted power and would feed his own men to the glacier to get it. Voss had wanted forgiveness and couldn't afford the postage. And I—

I sat in the dark with a dead man's letters against my ribs and admitted it, since the ice had gone to the trouble of providing witnesses.

I wanted to be known. Not found—there's a difference, and I'd spent years confusing them. Found is what hunters do. Known is what you choose. I wanted my mother to know where I'd gone and why, and I wanted her to have to sit with it, and I wanted—I could feel it pull, faint and constant as the needle Voss described—to go south, to Merrow's Landing, to deliver these letters and stand in front of Voss's daughter and say *your father sent these* and watch what knowing does to a person's face.

The Compass, if I held it, would point there. I didn't need black glass to tell me. That was the joke of it, the cruel little joke: the relic was redundant. Everyone who climbed this mountain already knew what they wanted. The Compass just refused to let them pretend otherwise, and pretending was the only thing keeping most of us upright.

Somewhere past the worst hour of the night, Sefa came and crouched beside me. She looked at the names. She'd seen them earlier and said nothing, which is her way—she files things.

"My grandmother used to say the mountain keeps accounts," she said. "Everything taken gets written down somewhere. She meant it as a warning about the company." She nodded at the wall. "Turns out it's literal."

"Your grandmother sounds smarter than most cartographers."

"She'd have liked yours." Sefa hesitated, then: "The men who scratched those names. You think they knew each other? Before?"

"Voss's letters say they were friends. Eight years in the ranges together."

She absorbed that. Seventeen years old, and she absorbed it the way she absorbs everything—quietly, and with interest accruing. "Then it's not the ice that kills you," she said. "It's the wanting."

"That's the theory."

"Good theory." She stood, stretched, went back to her watch. From the throat of the crevasse she said, without turning around: "When we get to the top, and Kessler's men find it—you're going to give it away, aren't you. I can tell. You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face of someone planning to lose on purpose."

I didn't answer. Above her, through the pouring snow, the sky had begun—if you could call it that—to lighten, a bruise-grey suggestion of dawn, which meant the storm had spent its night and so had we, and the mountain would expect us back on its surface soon, walking its switchbacks toward a ring of stones and a shaft and whatever waited at the bottom of both.

One day before the passes closed. One day, and below us somewhere, Kessler's camp.

I got up, stiff as the ice around me, and took out Voss's letters—not to read them again, but to count them, the way you'd count ammunition. Eleven letters. Eleven confessions, addressed and stamped and never sent, weighing less than the boot in the wall.

Whatever the Compass was—relic, mirror, evidence—it had spent decades in a grave with honest men's names around it, and the least I could do was not add mine.

"Everyone up," I said. "We're burning daylight we borrowed."

Odile opened her eyes. Reyes was already moving. And far off, muffled by a hundred feet of ice and snow, came a sound none of us wanted to hear: voices. Human voices, on the glacier above, and more than one of them, moving toward the crevasse field with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly where we'd gone to ground.
