## 15. Reyes's Reckoning

I have lied to a lot of people in my life. Commanding officers, customs men, three separate women named Margaret, and once — memorably — a bishop. But I had never before lied to myself with quite the craftsmanship I brought to Kessler's camp.

The lie was simple: that amnesty was a real thing, that paper and promises worked like sutures, that a man could stitch himself back into the world by handing over a map he'd copied by candlelight while his companions slept. I told myself the story so many times it wore smooth as river stone. A deserter's pardon. A clean record. My mother's name cleared of the shame of me. All I had to do was walk out of the storm, deliver a piece of paper to Dane Kessler, and let him do whatever he was going to do to the people who'd fed me from their own dwindling stores.

You'll notice the flaw in the story now. So did I, eventually. It just took the storm two days to shake it loose.

* * *

Kessler's camp sat in a bowl below the saddle, half a dozen canvas shelters staked against the wind like tents at a field hospital after a battle nobody won. When I walked in — frostbitten, snow-blind, hands shaking in a way I blamed on the cold — they took my rifle, patted me down with the thoroughness of men who'd been robbed before, and marched me to the largest tent, where Kessler sat behind a camp table doing arithmetic.

That's what struck me first, before anything else. Not the guards, not the maps pinned under tin cups, not the smell of wet wool and gun oil. The arithmetic. He was counting tins of food with a pencil stub, lips moving, and when he looked up at me there was nothing in his face at all. No triumph. No hatred. Just a man checking his inventory, and I had just become part of it.

"Doctor Reyes." He said it pleasantly. "Tomas, isn't it? Sit down. You look half-dead."

"I feel worse than I look," I said, "which is saying something."

He laughed — a real laugh, warm even — and poured me tea from a dented kettle. That was the thing about Dane Kessler that made him dangerous, and I understood it fully only later: he was genuinely likable. There was no mask. What you saw was what there was, and what there was happened to be a reasonable man who would bury you in an unmarked grave if the arithmetic called for it.

"You copied the map," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Every line."

"And Fairweather doesn't know you're here."

"Fairweather thinks I went for firewood."

"Good." He nodded, satisfied, and set the pencil down. "Then we can be efficient. You'll tell me where they're crossing the crevasse field, you'll rest tonight, and tomorrow you'll walk with us to the summit. After that—" he spread his hands, generous, "—you're free. Papers, passage, money enough to reach the coast. Your family hears you died honorably, or never hears from you again, whichever you prefer. I keep my word, Doctor. Ask anyone who's alive to vouch for me."

There it was. The joke arrived in my head unbidden, the way jokes do at funerals, and I nearly said it aloud: ask anyone who's alive. But I drank my tea instead, because my hands needed something to hold, and because a medic learns early that the body tells the truth before the mouth does, and my body was telling me to sit still and stay warm and live until morning.

They gave me a tent to myself. Small mercy, or large one, depending on how the night went. I slept four hours and woke to voices.

* * *

Here is what I know about eavesdropping: it is the coward's confession booth. You learn things you can't unknow, and then you have to decide what kind of person the knowing makes you. I crouched in the dark between the supply tent and the cook shelter, wind covering my steps, and listened to Kessler talk to a man named Brannigan — Voss's old partner, the one with the scarred knuckles and the laugh like a hinge.

"The doctor walks up with us," Kessler was saying.

"And then?"

"And then he doesn't walk back down."

A pause. Wind. Somewhere a mule — their mule, a mean-eyed gray — stamped and complained.

"He led us to them," Brannigan said. "That's worth something."

"It's worth exactly what I paid for it. He's a liability. He's seen our numbers, he knows the route, and he's a deserter with a conscience, which means eventually he'll grow one back and start talking. Men with consciences always do." Kessler's voice didn't harden. That was the horror of it. He might have been discussing feed rations. "We give him the papers on the summit. He signs, he turns around to admire the view, and it's quick. I'm not a butcher, Brannigan."

"No," Brannigan agreed. "You're worse. You're tidy."

I stood there in the dark with the snow coming through my collar and felt something I hadn't felt since the mines at Corvel: clarity. Clean, terrible, surgical clarity. At Corvel I'd been ordered to seal a shaft with forty men still breathing inside it, and I'd obeyed, and I'd spent every day since telling myself the order wasn't mine, the choice wasn't mine, the guilt was a weight distributed across so many shoulders that mine carried almost none of it. Distributed guilt. I could have published a paper on it. Chapter one: the body keeps the ledger anyway.

But this — this was different. This was a choice arriving whole, unwrapped, addressed to me by name. Walk away and let them kill Jude Fairweather and the girl and Odile Marchand, who'd taken a bullet already and joked about her own bad cartography while Tomas stitched her. Or walk back into the storm with nothing, warn them, and spend whatever remained of my life being hunted by the most patient man in the Ashkettles.

The arithmetic, for once, was simple. I'd just spent two days pretending it wasn't.

* * *

My copy of the map lived inside my boot, folded to the size of a prayer. I'd drawn it fair — every switchback, every waterhole, the ring of stones at the summit rendered in my careful medic's hand, the hand that had written so many death notices it had stopped shaking years ago. I sat in my tent by the light of a stub of candle and looked at it for a long time.

Burning it felt theatrical. I want to be honest about that. Some part of me — the part that survived Corvel by being useful — wanted to keep it, wanted the insurance, wanted the little folded escape hatch against a future where warning them went wrong and I needed leverage again. Leverage. That's what survival had taught me to call it. The map was leverage, and leverage was life, and life was the thing I'd been hoarding like a miser ever since I'd walked away from forty men underground.

I held the corner to the flame.

It caught slow, the way paper does when it's been living in a damp boot, and I watched Jude's mountain burn in miniature — the switchbacks going orange, the crevasse field curling black, the summit stones flaking into ash that smelled like every bad decision I'd ever made. And underneath the drama of it, underneath the theater, there was something quiet happening in my chest, which was this: for the first time since Corvel, I was carrying nothing I couldn't afford to lose.

The guard outside heard the candle gutter and asked if I wanted another. I said no thank you, I was turning in. Lying to a stranger is easy. It's the ones who feed you soup that cost you.

I waited until the watch changed — the third hour, when the new sentry spends his first twenty minutes finding a comfortable rock and the last twenty fighting sleep. Then I took what I could carry, which was nothing, and walked out of the camp the way smoke leaves a room: quietly, and leaving everything behind.

* * *

The storm had opinions about my plan.

It came off the ridge in long white slaps, erasing the trail behind me as fast as I made it, which meant that if I fell, I fell alone and permanently. I navigated by the slope of the wind and the memory of a map I'd burned an hour earlier, which is either madness or poetry depending on whether you survive it. Twice I went down. The second time I lay in the snow for what felt like a season and considered, seriously, the elegance of simply staying. No more choices. No more ledgers. The ice keeps what it's given; I'd seen that much in Voss's letters, read by firelight while Odile argued about contour lines and Sefa stole biscuits off my plate.

But the dead at Corvel hadn't gotten to choose, and I had, and I was choosing, damn it all, even if the choosing hurt. I got up. One knee, then the other. The way you do.

Somewhere past midnight the storm eased just enough to show me stars, mean and far away, and I used them to correct my bearing toward the waystation where I'd left them. I thought about what I'd say. *I'm sorry* seemed insufficient. *I burned your map* had a certain honesty but poor timing. In the end I settled on the truth, plain: *Kessler has six men, he knows your route, and he means to kill you all at the summit. Also I'm freezing. Also I'm sorry. Also, if any of you are medics, which one of you is, actually, I appear to be losing feeling in my feet.*

By dawn I was stumbling more than walking, and the world had gone soft at the edges, the way it does when the body starts closing accounts. I remember thinking, absurdly, about the bishop I'd lied to — how he'd told me that repentance was a road, not a door, and I'd thought it was nonsense, and here I was on a literal road, or a mountainside approximating one, doing my penance at altitude in weather designed by a hostile God.

And then, through the white: woodsmoke. And a shape. And a voice I knew, sharp and young and furious, cutting through the storm like the girl herself.

"That's far enough. Hands where I can—"

"Sefa." My voice cracked on it. "It's Reyes. I need to talk to Fairweather."

She came closer, rifle up, snow crusted in her eyebrows, and looked at me the way you look at a dog that's bitten someone. Behind her, at the mouth of the ruined waystation, other shapes were stirring. Odile's voice, rough with pain. Jude's silhouette filling the doorway, still as a held breath.

I tried to stand straight and mostly failed. My hands wouldn't work. My feet had filed their resignation somewhere on the last ridge. Everything I owned was ash in a stove in Kessler's camp, and everything I knew was in my head, and my head was starting to go soft and strange at the borders.

So I gave them the only thing I'd carried out of that camp, the only thing the storm hadn't taken:

"He's coming," I said. "Six men, two days behind me, and he knows the pass. And Fairweather — I burned the map. I burned it before I left. It's just us now, and what we remember, and the snow."

Jude stepped out into the storm, and the look on their face was not forgiveness, and it was not welcome, and it was not, God help me, surprise.

It was the look of someone deciding, right there in the falling snow, whether I was worth the food it would take to keep me alive.

Behind them, Odile's voice drifted from the dark of the waystation, dry as paper: "Well. Somebody put the kettle on. We're going to need it."
