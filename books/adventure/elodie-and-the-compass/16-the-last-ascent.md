## 16. The Last Ascent

We left before the sky had decided what it wanted to be. That's the honest way to put it—gray, undecided, holding its breath. Sefa said the pass would close tomorrow, and Sefa had been right about everything so far except her own name, which she'd given us three versions of and I'd stopped keeping count.

"Today," she said, standing over the dead fire with her arms crossed, breath steaming, "or we winter on this mountain and eat Odile's boots."

"My boots are the only thing I own that still works," Odile said from inside the litter.

The litter was Reyes's masterpiece and my guilt made solid. Two tent poles lashed into a frame, a tarp stretched between them, rope harnesses fore and aft. We took turns carrying it in pairs, two hours each, because a woman with a bullet through her thigh cannot walk up a ridge line, and because leaving her was a sentence none of us would say out loud and all of us had thought anyway at three in the morning when the wind came through the rocks like something hunting.

I carried front-left for the first shift. From there I could see her face, or what showed of it above the blankets: gray, set, awake. She hadn't slept since the crevasse. People who've been shot either sleep like the dead or don't sleep at all, and Odile Marchand had chosen the second option as if choosing were still something she got to do.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm navigating."

"You're staring at my leg."

"I'm checking your bandage."

"It's fine."

"It's not fine. It's seeping."

"Then change it at the top of the switchback and stop lying to me about the navigation." Her eyes closed. Opened again. "Jude. How far?"

Sefa answered from behind us, where she always walked now, watching our backtrail like it owed her money. "The saddle by dark. The summit by noon tomorrow if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then we learn what Kessler's men eat for breakfast."

* * *

Reyes walked like a man whose hands had been taken away from him. Frostbite does that—it takes the fingers first, the instruments, the whole identity of a medic—and he kept flexing them inside his mittens, opening and closing, testing a door that might not open anymore. He could still grip. He couldn't feel. He told me this at the first rest stop with the flat voice of a man reading his own inventory.

"I can tie a knot," he said. "I can't tell you if I'm tying it tight."

"Tie it twice."

"That's not how knots work."

"It's how ours work."

He almost smiled. Almost. He'd been doing that since he came stumbling back into camp half-frozen with nothing but a warning—Kessler never meant amnesty, Kessler meant a grave with paperwork—and none of us had thanked him properly because thanking him properly would have meant saying out loud that he'd betrayed us first, and grief and gratitude don't share a room well. So we just let him carry the litter's rear harness, and he let us, and that was its own kind of ledger being settled.

The map had burned with the mule. I want to write that sentence plainly because it deserves to be plain: Voss's hand-drawn map, the thing a dying man traded me, the thing three riders and a company of killers had chased across a border—the mule went down in the ambush, the oil stove went with him, and the map went into the fire along with half our food and any illusion that the mountain was neutral ground.

So Sefa navigated by memory. Not her memory—her mother's, her grandmother's, the smugglers' trails passed down like recipes through a family the company had scattered to the four winds. She'd stop sometimes in the middle of a slope, close her eyes, and move her lips like she was asking a dead woman for directions. Then she'd point, and we'd climb, and she'd be right.

"How do you know this?" I asked her once, on a shelf of rock narrow enough that we moved single file with the litter tilted like a coffin being argued over.

"I don't know it. I remember being told about it." She didn't look at me. "My grandmother ran salt over this ridge before I was born. She used to say the mountain keeps two kinds of paths—the ones on maps, for people who trust paper, and the ones in stories, for people who trust grandmothers."

"Which do you trust?"

She looked at me then, and there was frost in her lashes and something older than frost underneath. "Right now? The one who isn't on fire."

* * *

By midday the wind found its voice. It came off the ice fields to the north in long, flat gusts that didn't howl so much as insist—a sound like a door being held shut against something patient. We roped together above the tree line, five of us and no mule, and the litter went last with Reyes and me hauling while Sefa scouted ahead and Odile gave directions nobody asked for.

"Left," she said. "Bear left. There's a seam in the rock."

"There's a lot of seams in the rock."

"The one that doesn't have snow in it. Snow-filled seams mean water gets in, water freezes, the face spalls. You'll know it because the rock under it will be clean, almost polished." A pause. "I surveyed this range, Jude. Before. For them."

Nobody said anything. What was there to say—that we forgave her? Forgiveness is a currency and she'd paid enough already, in blood, on a glacier, carrying a bullet some hired man had put there because of a survey she'd drawn a decade ago. Instead Reyes said, "Left it is," and we bore left, and the seam was exactly where she said, and the rock beneath it was smooth as river stone, and I thought: even her guilt is useful. Even her shame knows the terrain.

That's the thing about this party of mine, if it was mine—I'd stopped pretending I'd hired any of them somewhere around the time they started saving each other's lives. Sefa, guiding for reasons she'd stopped calling wages. Reyes, walking back into the storm that nearly killed him because the hard right was the only direction he had left. Odile, mapping our route out loud from a litter, spending the knowledge that ruined her like it was the only coin she had left to spend. And me, Jude Fairweather, professional nonentity, carrying a dead man's letters and a promise I couldn't put down any more than I could put down the litter.

Exhausted resolve. That's what Tomas would have called it, if Tomas had been the sort to name things. We were past tired. Tired is a place you visit. This was somewhere else—a country where your legs belong to the mountain and your mind belongs to the promise and the only thing left that's yours is the decision to keep going, renewed every hundred steps like a lease neither party wants but both keep signing.

* * *

We made the saddle an hour after dark, which Sefa called "fine" in the tone other people reserve for catastrophes. There was a hollow below a leaning slab, deep enough to sit in, shallow enough that sitting in it was mostly a way of lying down in public. We melted snow over a flame the size of a thumbnail. We ate the last of the smoked fish, which tasted like rope and salt and survival. Odile's wound had stopped seeping and started smelling faintly wrong, and Reyes changed the dressing by feel, tying every knot twice, and lied to her the way she'd lied to me: "It's fine."

"It's not fine," she said.

"No," he agreed quietly. "But it will hold until tomorrow. After tomorrow, one way or another, it won't matter."

That's the kind of comfort field medics give. It works better than it should.

I took last watch. The others slept in a heap the way people sleep when warmth is arithmetic—Sefa curled smallest, Reyes around her like a wall, Odile propped against the slab with her bad leg straight. Above us the stars came out hard and close, the thin-air stars that make you feel like you could reach up and be disappointed.

Below us, down the southern shoulder, there was a light.

One light. Steady. Camp-colored, fire-colored, wrong-colored—because we should have been the only ones on this side of the range, and the pass closed tomorrow, and whatever burned down there had gotten there first.

Kessler's camp. At the site. Already there.

I sat with that for a long time, doing the arithmetic I didn't want to do. They had fresh men, fresh supplies, a day's head start on the final approach. We had a wounded cartographer, a frostbitten medic, a teenage guide running on memory and spite, and me. If I'd been the man I was six months ago—the one who erased himself from records, who walked away from a grave robbers' cache because wanting things gets people followed—I'd have turned around. I'd have gone down the east slope alone, fast and light, invisible, alive.

Instead I counted the hours until dawn and found, to my complete lack of surprise, that I wasn't going anywhere.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old tin. We climbed.

The final ridge was a spine of black rock with wind-scoured ice in the joints, and we went up it the way you go up anything impossible—one lie at a time. *Almost there.* *Just past this crest.* *The angle eases after the next tower.* None of it true, all of it necessary. Sefa led. Reyes and I carried the litter in relays that shortened as the air thinned, until the relays were fifty steps and then thirty, until Odile said, "Put me down and rest or put me down and admit we're done," and Reyes said, "Neither," and we kept going.

Somewhere near the top my hands stopped being hands. I remember looking at them on the litter ropes—white, clumsy, someone else's—and thinking distantly that Voss had died with hands like that, gut-shot in the dirt, trading his last possession to a stranger because a stranger was the only kind of person left who might keep a promise. I hadn't understood it then. Lying in the dirt myself, gasping at an altitude that resented me, I understood it completely. You give the heavy thing to whoever's still walking.

The summit, when it came, arrived without ceremony. One moment the ridge was climbing; the next it wasn't; the world opened southward like a hand unclenching.

And there it was.

A bowl below us, ringed by peaks, holding a circle of standing stones around a dark shaft in the earth—exactly as Voss had drawn it, exactly as the letters described, a ring like a keyhole's eye. The stones stood black against the snowfield. The shaft gaped at their center, breathing a faint steam, as if the mountain had a throat and we'd finally found it.

And around it, pitched neat as a military review: tents. Maybe twenty. Cook fires. A flag I recognized from the company seals on Kessler's papers. Men moving between the stones with the unhurried confidence of people who believe the outcome is already written.

"They beat us here," Reyes said.

"Of course they did." Odile's voice was thin with pain and thick with something else. "They had the survey. My survey. I drew that access route myself, ten years ago, for a road that was never built." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "I built their road to this place. I just didn't know what it led to."

Sefa stood at the edge of the drop, small and still, looking down at the camp with an expression I couldn't read until I realized I could—because it was the same expression she'd worn in the hollow village, standing in the doorway of a house that had belonged to her family before the company emptied it. It was hunger. Not for gold. For answers.

"So what now?" she asked. "We're five. They're twenty. We're on top of the world and they're sitting on the prize."

I looked at the ring of stones. At the shaft. At the tiny figures moving within it, and at one figure apart from the others, standing at the ring's edge with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the horizon the way a man watches a door he expects someone to come through.

Kessler. Waiting for us. He'd known we'd come—he'd always known. A man who offers deals at waterholes doesn't stop offering them at summits.

"Now," I said, and heard my own voice come out steadier than I had any right to expect, "we go down and see what the Compass wants."

Because that was the truth I'd been carrying since the crevasse, since the names scratched in the ice, since the boot and the rope and the evidence of everyone who'd ever wanted something badly enough to die in a hole for it: the Compass didn't grant wishes. It revealed them. And there were twenty men down there about to have theirs revealed, and not one of them—not Kessler, not his best shooter, not the cook or the muleskinner—was going to like what the needle showed them.

I adjusted the litter straps. Reyes flexed his dead hands. Sefa spat over the edge, for luck or contempt, possibly both.

"One day before the snows close," Odile murmured. "Make it count."

We started down.
