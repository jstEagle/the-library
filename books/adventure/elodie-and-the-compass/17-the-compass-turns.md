## 17. The Compass Turns

I have been called many things in my life—thief, butcher, reasonable man—and I will tell you plainly that the third is the one that stings least and fits best. A thief takes what isn't his. A butcher kills without arithmetic. But a reasonable man? A reasonable man looks at a bad situation and finds the cheapest way through it, and if other men's blood is cheaper than his own, well, that is simply the market speaking.

The market had been speaking to me for eleven days up this mountain, and it was saying: you are losing money.

We'd lost two men to the river crossing—drowned, or swept, I never confirmed which, because confirmation costs wages. We'd lost a day to a rockslide that Odile Marchand's old survey maps swore wasn't there, which told me either her maps were wrong or she'd sold me honest ones and kept the true ones for herself. We'd lost Reyes, my medic, my amnesty-seeker, who walked out of camp three nights ago with a copy of the map folded against his chest like a love letter. And we'd lost something less countable but more expensive: the certainty of my men. You can buy a man's back for coin. His certainty has to be managed daily, like livestock.

So when Brannock came down from the ring of stones at the summit shouting my name in a voice cracked down the middle, I confess my first thought was not triumph. It was: *what has it cost us now.*

"Sir." He skidded the last few yards of scree and caught himself on my shoulder, which no one does twice. "Sir, we found it. The shaft. There's a shaft under the stones, and at the bottom—" He stopped. He was breathing like a man who'd run from something rather than toward it.

"At the bottom what?"

"There's a box. Iron, old, sealed with wax. And sir—the wax is broken. It was broken before we got there."

That stopped me. Voss had drawn the map forty years ago and never come back—that much Kessler's money had established beyond argument, bought from tavern keepers and jailers across three territories. If the seal was broken, someone else had been down there. Someone else had held whatever waited in that box.

Or Voss himself had lied about never returning.

Men lie about everything, in my experience, except their debts. Even those they lie about, but with worse grammar.

* * *

The shaft was exactly as the map promised: a ring of standing stones around a throat of cut rock, maybe nine feet across, dropping into blackness deeper than a lantern could argue with. Voss's map showed iron rungs driven into one wall. They were still there, furred with frost, and they rang when Brannock tested them—a sound like a struck tooth.

My men gathered at the rim and looked down, and I watched them look, and I want to be honest about what I saw, because honesty is the luxury of men who've already decided everything else. I saw six grown killers lean over a hole in the world and go quiet as children at a funeral. Not afraid, exactly. Something before fear. The way you feel standing outside a house where something important happened long ago and everyone involved is dead.

"Well," I said. "Someone fetch it up."

Nobody moved.

It wasn't cowardice. These were men who'd shot at each other for practice. It was something older and stupider: none of them wanted to be second. Whoever went down would find it first, and whoever found it first would hold it, and holding, we all understood without discussing it, was its own kind of power. I have spent thirty years making sure my men understand that power flows from me and only me, and here was a hole in the ground teaching them otherwise for free.

"I'll go," said Halloran, which surprised me, because Halloran was the newest and the dumbest, and dumb men are usually the most sensible about holes.

He went down the rungs with the lantern swinging, and the rest of us stood in the wind listening to his boots tick away into the dark, and I noticed Quill had drifted a step closer to the rim than the others. Quill is my best shot and my worst liar, and he was watching Halloran descend the way a card player watches the deck.

The lantern light shrank. Then it flared—Halloran must have set it down—and then his voice came up the shaft, thin and strange, thinned further by stone:

"It's a needle."

"A needle?" Brannock called down.

"Black glass. Long as my forearm. There's writing on the case. Sir—" and here his voice did something I didn't like, something wet, "—sir, it's pointing at me. It's pointing right at me."

"Everything points at you when you pick it up, you idiot," Quill shouted down, but his voice cracked on *idiot*, and I heard it, and so did he, and so did everyone.

Halloran climbed back up slower than he'd gone down, and when his head cleared the rim I saw his face and understood we were in trouble. Not the trouble of ambush or avalanche. The trouble of a man who has seen something he cannot put down.

He held it out flat on both palms like an offering, and I'll describe it as precisely as I can, because precision is all I have left of that afternoon. A needle of black glass, yes—black the way deep water is black, black that seemed to have depth rather than color. It sat in a case of tarnished iron lined with rotted velvet, and the needle moved inside it, slow, deliberate, the way a snake moves when it isn't hunting yet. And it was pointing. Not north. Not at anything in the landscape. It pointed at Halloran's chest, dead center, steady as a nail.

"That's a trick of balance," I said. "The pivot's weighted toward whoever holds it. Some jeweler's joke."

"Then you hold it, sir," Halloran said, and there was relief all over him, and eagerness too, and the combination frightened me more than either alone.

I took it.

And it pointed at nothing.

Not at me. Not away from me. It swung once, lazily, like a door deciding whether to close, and then it settled on a direction that was no direction—out past the summit ridge, out past the range, out into the empty air above the Ashkettle wilderness where there was nothing, nothing at all, no town, no road, no grave, no woman, no gold. It pointed at blankness with the confidence of a compass that has never once been wrong.

And I want to tell you what I felt, because this is the part of the story where a lesser man would lie. I felt seen. That's the only word. Every man in my employ believes he knows what I want—it's written in my ledgers, in my orders, in the graves I've paid for. Land. Leverage. The company charter that lets me sign other men's deaths into legality. But the needle looked at all of that, weighed it, and declined. It turned its black face toward the empty sky and said: *this man wants nothing that exists.*

I laughed. I couldn't help it. The men stepped back.

"Pass it around," I said. "Everyone holds it. That's an order."

Because here is what I understood in that moment, standing in the wind with a dead thing in my hands: if it pointed at nothing for me, then it wasn't broken. It was reading. And I needed to know what it read in my men, because a leader who doesn't know what his men want is not a leader. He's a treasurer.

* * *

Quill took it first, since he was closest, and I watched the needle swing and settle, and I watched Quill's face do something complicated and terrible.

It pointed south. Hard south, off the mountain, down through the ranges toward the lowlands, and Quill stared along its line like a man reading his own sentence, and then he said, very quietly, "There's nothing south but the prison at Corvel."

"Who's in the prison at Corvel, Quill?"

"My brother." He said it like a confession extracted under torture. "He's been inside nine years. I keep meaning to—" He stopped. The needle trembled, eager, insistent, straining south like a dog at a rope. "Nine years," he said again, and his voice had gone somewhere young.

Brannock snatched it from him. "Give it here."

For a moment—one full breath—I thought Quill wouldn't let go. His knuckles were white around the case, and Brannock's hand was on it, and the two of them stood locked over a piece of black glass while the wind screamed past the stones, and I understood that we were one heartbeat from shooting each other over a needle that pointed at a prison.

Brannock won the tug. The needle spun, steadied, and pointed east. Down-range, east, toward the coast, toward Merrow's Landing, though of course Brannock didn't know that; he just stared east with his mouth open and said, "My daughter. She lives—" and then shut his mouth so fast I heard his teeth click, because he'd remembered whose company he was keeping, and what we say about men with families. Soft. Exploitable. Gone.

But the needle knew. The needle had gone straight through Brannock's armor, through thirty years of hired violence, and found the girl in Merrow's Landing he sends money to and never visits, and it pointed at her with the patience of something that had waited forty years in the dark to say so.

One by one they held it. One by one it found them. Pell's needle pointed at a valley two territories west where, it emerged, he'd buried a wife he told us had died in a fire she hadn't died in. Dree's pointed straight down—at the shaft itself, at the ice below, and Dree went gray and handed it back without a word, and I did not ask, because some arithmetic you don't want done aloud. Each man's wanting was impossible. Too far. Too late. Too buried. Too ashamed. The Compass didn't offer these things. It only named them, the way a doctor names a disease, and naming, we all discovered within the hour, is worse than having.

Because here is what happens to a company of armed men when each learns what the others lack: the others become levers.

I saw it happen in real time. Quill looking at Brannock's daughter and doing sums. Brannock watching Dree stare at the shaft and filing it away. Pell drifting closer to Quill, casual as a pickpocket, asking him about Corvel, how far, how many guards, whether a man could ride it in four days—and Quill answering, helplessly, gratefully, because nobody had asked him about his brother in nine years, and the asking felt like kindness even as it was a knife being sharpened.

I should have ended it there. Taken the thing, dropped it down the shaft, put a bullet in the first man who reached for it, and marched us home poorer and intact. That was the reasonable move. I have made moves like it all my life.

Instead I gave an order I will regret into whatever afterlife consents to hold me.

"We rest here tonight," I said. "Tomorrow we go down and finish the job. Fairweather and his ragtag congregation are coming up that ridge, and when they arrive, I want them to find this company disciplined, armed, and in possession of that needle. Nobody holds it overnight. It goes in the case, the case goes in my pack, and my pack sleeps under my head. Am I understood?"

They said yes, sir, in six different voices, and every voice was lying, and I knew it, and I let it stand, because I told myself I could manage them. I have always told myself that. It is the founding fiction of my entire career: that wanting can be managed, that men are ledgers, that everything balances if you keep the columns yourself.

The needle lay in its case by the fire, and I swear to you it did not point north, or at the fire, or at any of us. It pointed at the empty air past the ridge, patient as debt, and I caught every one of my men looking at it when they thought the others weren't looking, and I caught myself looking longest of all.

* * *

It came apart a little after midnight.

I woke to voices—not shouting, worse, the low urgent murmur of conspiracy—and rolled out of my bedroll with my pistol already clear, which is why I'm alive to narrate this. The fire had burned to coals. The case was open. The case was open and empty, and Quill was gone, and Pell was gone, and when I came around the rocks with Brannock and Dree at my shoulders, guns out, hearts going like drumlines, we found them at the rim of the shaft.

Both of them. Standing at the edge in the starlight, the needle held between them, each with a fist on the case, neither pulling. Just standing. The needle pointed down into the dark, and both men were looking along its line into the black like it was a window instead of a hole.

"Step back," I said.

Neither moved.

"Pell. Quill. Step back from the edge."

"He wants to go down again," Pell said. His voice was dreamy, ruined. "Says it pointed at the ice. Says whatever it wants of him is down there."

"It doesn't *want*, you fool, it's a piece of glass—"

"You held it, sir." Quill turned and looked at me, and his eyes were wet and furious and free, freer than I'd ever made any man feel, and that was the worst of it. "You held it and it pointed at nothing. You don't get a vote on what it means."

And that was the whole of it, laid bare in one sentence by my worst liar. The needle had audited every man on this mountain and found him wanting—literally, specifically, unbearably wanting—and the only man it had acquitted was me, and an acquittal from that thing felt less like mercy than like being pronounced irrelevant.

Dree raised his rifle. I'll give him this: he followed orders even now. "Drop it," he said, "or I drop you both," and it might have worked, it might have held, except that was the moment the stars went out.

Not all of them. Just along the eastern ridge, where a line of lanterns had appeared—five lights, maybe six, descending through the switchbacks in a slow, deliberate procession, unhurried as a funeral. Fairweather. The deserter, the guide, the cartographer, all of them, coming up the last mile with their wounds and their mule-less poverty and their unbearable solidarity, walking straight toward six armed men and a hole in the world.

Pell and Quill forgot each other instantly. That's the thing about men like mine—they can turn from brotherhood to tactics in half a heartbeat. They came off the rim, rifles up, and Brannock was shouting positions, and I stood there with an empty iron case in my hand and the wind rising out of the west carrying the smell of snow.

Snow. Early. Weeks early. The pass would close behind them and trap us all together on this summit, and I understood, with the clarity the needle had refused to grant me, that everything from this hour forward would happen in a shrinking room.

"Let them come," I heard myself say.

The case hung at my side, empty, and my thumb kept moving over the place where the needle had lain, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. Below us, the lanterns climbed. Somewhere under our feet, down at the bottom of a shaft older than any map worth trusting, the ice held the shape of everything my men wanted and the shape of my own absence, and I could not stop wondering—which is the truth, the whole ugly truth—which of those two things frightened me more.

Behind me, soft as snowfall, Dree began to laugh.
