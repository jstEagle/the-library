## 18. The Summit of Wanting

The last hundred yards down from the ridge, I learned that a man can be too tired to be afraid.

Not brave. Brave is a choice. This was something past fear, the way hunger stops hurting after long enough, and your body simply files a complaint it knows will go unanswered. We came down the final slope in single file—Sefa first, reading the mountain out of her head because the map had burned with the mule; Reyes half-carrying Odile's litter with frostbitten hands he refused to complain about, which was its own kind of complaint; and me at the back, doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for two days.

One day before the snows closed the pass. Kessler's camp already in the hollow below. And somewhere between those two facts, us.

"Smoke," Sefa said, flattening against a boulder. She said it the way other people say *sorry for your loss*—flat, practiced, meaning everything.

I looked past her shoulder. Below us, the ring of stones Voss had drawn sat exactly where his shaking hand had put it, a circle of standing rocks around a dark shaft like an eye that had been crying mud for a century. Kessler's tents were pitched just outside the ring, tidy as a company town. Six men. Maybe seven. Cook fire going. They'd beaten us here by hours, and they hadn't gone into the shaft yet.

"Why not?" Reyes whispered. "If they've got men to spare, why are they waiting?"

Odile answered from the litter, her voice thinned by blood loss but still carrying that cartographer's certainty, the tone of a woman who had once believed maps could be honest. "Because they're afraid of it."

"They're afraid of a hole," Sefa said.

"They're afraid of what's in the hole." Odile turned her head carefully, the way you do when your body has stopped being trustworthy. "I surveyed this range for them, children. I know how Kessler's people work. They don't dig until they've priced the digging. And nobody can price this."

I could have told them then. Should have, maybe. But there's a difference between telling the truth and knowing which truth to tell when, and I'd spent my whole life learning the second one while failing the first.

"We go around," I said. "East rim, above their camp. We watch. If they find nothing, they leave, and we've lost nothing but time we didn't have anyway."

"And if they find something?" Sefa asked.

I didn't answer, because the wind did—a long moan off the glacier, the sound the range made when it wanted you to know it was bigger than your plans—and because we both knew the answer. If they found something, none of my careful arithmetic would matter anymore.

We moved along the east rim, small and quiet, five people who had no business being alive, and below us the ring of stones waited with the patience of things that have outlasted everyone who ever drew them.

* * *

It went wrong the way most things do: quickly, stupidly, and because of a mule.

Not our mule—that one was glacier meat now, along with half our food. One of Kessler's pack animals spooked at the shaft's edge, hauling back on its rope, and the nearest man cursed and struck it, and the noise carried up the rim like a thrown stone. A sentry turned. Looked straight up. And whatever he saw—or thought he saw—he shouted, and the tidy camp became a kicked anthill.

"Down," Sefa hissed, but the slope was scree and the scree had opinions, and before we'd gone ten feet the whole hillside was announcing us. There was no more hiding. There was only downhill, fast, toward the one place on this mountain worse than the men chasing us.

I remember thinking, absurdly, of Voss's hands. How they'd shook when he pressed the map into mine. *See it home,* he'd said, and I'd promised a dying stranger a thing I didn't believe in, and now here I was delivering on it at gunpoint, which felt about right. Every promise I'd ever kept had cost more than the thing was worth.

Then we were in the ring of stones, and the shooting started, and it was the worst kind of shooting—the kind where nobody wants to hit anybody yet because everybody wants something first.

Kessler stood at the shaft's mouth with two men behind him and a pistol held low, the way a reasonable man holds a pistol, and he looked at me across forty feet of broken ground with genuine, infuriating disappointment, like a teacher watching a promising student throw it all away.

"Fairweather." He said it almost warmly. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy," I said. "There wasn't one. The mountain handled it."

"I offered you a clean deal at the waterhole. I'm offering it again, and this is the last time I'll be able to offer anything, because my associates—" he tipped his head at the men behind him, and something flickered across his face, some doubt he'd been carrying since the crevasse field, "—are getting impatient. The map. Or what's left of it. Tell me where it leads, and you walk. All of you. I give you my word."

"The word of a man whose partners killed a fourth man over less than this," Reyes said from beside the litter. His voice shook. His feet didn't move. "I heard you promise amnesty once already, Kessler. Ask me how that went."

Kessler's jaw tightened. For a moment—one real moment—I watched him genuinely not understand. That was the thing about Dane Kessler that made him worse than the killers he employed: he believed himself reasonable. He believed every horror he'd caused was somebody else's bad arithmetic, and that if everyone would just sit down like adults, he could explain why the bodies were necessary. Men like him don't lie to themselves. That's the trick. They build a self so clean the lies slide right off it.

"It doesn't have to end badly," he said.

"It ended badly years ago," I told him. "You're just the last one to hear about it."

And that's when the shaft spoke.

* * *

Later, everyone would tell it differently. Sefa swore the ground shook. Odile said she heard singing, which I put down to blood loss until I heard it myself, years after, in dreams. What actually happened, I think, is simpler and worse: one of Kessler's men lost his nerve waiting for orders, dropped a rope into the shaft, and went down alone.

What came back up came back up changed.

He climbed out slow, one-handed, and in his fist was a needle of black glass maybe the length of a forearm, wrapped at one end in corroded silver, and even from thirty feet away I felt it—not saw it, *felt* it, the way you feel someone watching you in a dark room. The air around the ring of stones went strange and still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"Hollow Compass," the man breathed. He was laughing and crying at once. "It's real. It's real, it's—"

He pointed it, the way you'd point any compass, and we all watched his face come apart.

"No," he said. "No, that's not—it's wrong, it has to be wrong—"

"What does it show you?" Kessler demanded, stepping forward, and I saw the hunger on him now, the reasonableness cracking like river ice.

"She's alive," the man whispered. "My daughter. The letter said she died. It points—she's alive, she's in Corvane, she's—" He turned the needle as if testing it, and it swung, steady, certain, pointing south and east, toward a city and a grave that wasn't a grave, and the man began to walk. Just walked, compass out, past Kessler, past his rifle leaning against a stone, toward the edge of the world with tears running down his face.

Two others grabbed for him. One wrenched the Compass free and the needle snapped around instantly, violently, to point somewhere west, and this man went white and dropped it like it had bitten him. It landed in the dirt between us all, spinning slowly, and every person in that ring of stones watched it like it was a snake.

Except it wasn't spinning to settle. It was spinning to choose.

"Nobody move," Kessler said, and his voice had lost its warmth entirely.

Too late. The third man dove for it, and the fourth, and it was ugly and brief and human—two grown men wrestling in the dirt over a stick of black glass while their employer screamed orders nobody heard. The needle settled under the third man's grip and pointed north, into the range, at a pass that officially did not exist, and he stared at it and said, very quietly, "That's where my brother went," and got up and started walking, and this time nobody stopped him, because half the men present were remembering things they'd buried deeper than any shaft.

In the chaos—in the shouting and the walking-away and Kessler roaring like a man watching his life's work dissolve into individual wants—I did the only smart thing I've ever done under fire.

I picked it up.

* * *

Here is what I expected: gold. Direction. Some grand shining *yes*, the way treasure works in the stories Imogen used to read me before she stopped reading me anything at all.

Here is what happened instead.

The needle swung once, twice, and settled. South. Down out of the mountains, through the passes, along the coast road, to a gray harbor town where gulls argue over fish heads and the fog comes in like a debt collector.

Merrow's Landing.

My hands knew before my head did. My hands went light. Twenty years of armor—every erased record, every burned alias, every border crossed under a name that wasn't mine—all of it suddenly weighed nothing, because the needle wasn't pointing at treasure. It was pointing at a woman in a house with a blue door who had signed papers letting a child disappear rather than fight for her, and the child had spent two decades making sure she couldn't be found, and the needle didn't care. The needle just pointed. *There. That way. The thing you left.*

"That's it," I said aloud, to nobody, to the ice, to Voss wherever he'd ended up. "That's the whole trick."

It grants nothing. It reveals everything. It's not a treasure map. It's a confession you hold in your hand, and it points, always, at what you abandoned—and it never stops pointing, and it never lets you pretend you don't know.

Voss had carried this across a range and never opened the shaft again, because he already knew what it would say. *Merrow's Landing,* it would have told him. *A girl named Mara. The letters you wrote and never sent.* He'd traded it to me not to be rid of a relic but to be rid of the pointing—and I'd carried it home for him anyway, because a promise is a promise, and because some part of me had known since chapter one of this disaster that the Compass and I were the same kind of coward.

"Fairweather." Kessler's voice, close now. Very close. "Give it to me."

I looked up. The ring of stones had emptied of everyone but us—his men scattered to the four winds, each following a needle only they could see, or sitting against the standing rocks staring at nothing. Sefa, Odile, Reyes stood together near the litter, watching me with expressions I couldn't read and didn't dare try.

Kessler held out his hand. And here's the thing—I believed him. When he said, quietly, "Name your price. Land, money, a new name so clean not even you'd recognize it. Your mother's debts, paid. Whatever Fairweather wants, Fairweather gets"—I believed he meant it, and I believed he could deliver it, and that was the most frightening part. Not the gun. The offer. Because for one full breath, I stood there holding a needle that pointed at everything I'd spent my life pretending not to want, and I let myself want it.

A mother who might explain. An apology twenty years late. A door I could knock on without erasing myself first.

The needle trembled in my grip, patient as ice.

"No," I said.

"You haven't even heard—"

"I know what it shows you." I turned it so he could see the needle, steady, merciless, aimed south at a harbor town that meant nothing to him and everything to me. "It doesn't grant anything, Kessler. It just tells you the truth about what you threw away. That's all it does. That's all it's ever done." I looked at him—at the reasonable man, the clean man, the man who'd built a fortress out of other people's losses and called it business. "So tell me. What's it going to point at for you?"

And Dane Kessler—who had crossed a mountain range, killed for less, burned villages by proxy and slept fine—looked at the black glass in my hands and, for the first time since the border town, had nothing reasonable to say.

Behind him, the storm finally came over the summit ridge, white and total, and the temperature fell like a verdict.

I backed toward the crevasse edge, the Compass heavy as a sin in my hands, and Kessler's gun came up—not aiming, not yet, but asking—and behind him, three battered, frozen, impossible people stepped out of the snow to stand where I could see them.

"Well," I said. "Since we're all here."
