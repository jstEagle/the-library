## 19. The Giving

The needle of black glass sat in my palm like a held breath.

Around me, the ring of stones at the summit was coming apart. Kessler's men had breached the shaft an hour ago and found what Voss's letters promised: the Hollow Compass, whole after all these years, waiting in its niche of ice like something patient and unforgiving. And now they were learning what it did. Not granting. Revealing.

I watched it happen from twenty feet away, crouched behind a fallen stone with Sefa's hand gripping my sleeve, and I watched men come undone.

The big one—Harrow, Kessler called him, though his men called him nothing so familiar—held it first. He stared down at the needle for a long time, and then he laughed, one short bark that had no humor in it anywhere, and said, "That's not possible," in the voice of a man reading his own death warrant. He passed it along quickly, the way you pass along something hot.

The next man looked longer. He didn't say anything at all. He just turned the Compass slowly in his hands, following the needle with his eyes as it swung toward the southeast, toward some town or some grave or some woman three hundred miles away who probably hadn't thought about him in a decade—and his face went through grief like weather. When Kessler took it from him, he let it go without a fight. That frightened me more than a fight would have.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about relics: they don't need to be magical to ruin you. They only need to be honest.

* * *

Kessler held it last.

He stood at the shaft's edge with the storm building behind him, gray light going silver, snow beginning to come down in fat, deliberate flakes—the first true snows, nineteen days late by Sefa's count but arriving exactly on time for somebody's purposes, and I suspected the range itself counted among them. He turned the Compass over twice. He read the needle.

And I saw it point at nothing.

Not north, not south, not at him, not away from him. It drifted. It wandered in its housing like a drunk looking for a door, and Kessler's face did something complicated and terrible. He'd climbed a mountain for this. He'd killed for less than this. He'd offered me clean deals and dirty ones, amnesty and bullets, and now the thing in his hands was telling him—gently, the way ice tells you—that there was nothing left in him that wanted anything at all.

"Mr. Kessler," I called out, because apparently I'd decided to die witty.

His head came up. The gun came up with it, faster than I liked, and the six men around the shaft remembered I existed all at once.

"You've got it backwards," I said. "It doesn't grant. It shows. That's all it ever did. Voss told me—or he wrote it, anyway, in letters he never sent, which is the most honest kind of telling there is." I stood up slowly, hands visible, the Compass still in mine. "You're holding the truth about yourself, Mr. Kessler. Most people pay good money to avoid that."

"Give it here."

"I'm going to do something better than that."

I want to record, for whoever finds this account—if anyone does, if anyone cares—that in that moment I was not brave. My knees were doing arithmetic against each other. The gun barrel looked large enough to walk into. But I had spent the night before last in a crevasse full of dead men's names scratched into ice, and I had learned something down there that I couldn't unlearn: the Compass was never treasure. It was evidence. Evidence of what people wanted badly enough to die in a hole in a glacier for, and evidence, too, of what wanting costs when you feed it everything you are.

Voss walked out of that crevasse alone. He spent thirty years paying for it in unsent letters. I wasn't going to spend thirty years paying for this.

So I walked.

Not toward Kessler. Toward the crevasse.

There's a second opening on the summit side—a split in the glacier where the ice had calved back years ago, leaving a blue-black seam maybe four feet wide, dropping into that same cold dark where Odile lay two nights ago with a bullet in her shoulder while I read dead men's handwriting off the walls. The storm wind funneled through it and made a sound like a low note held on an instrument nobody could see.

Every eye followed me. Good. That was the entire plan, such as it was: make them watch.

"The map's burned," I said, walking backward toward the seam, raising my voice over the wind. "The mule took it over the ice with half our food, and I'll tell you what else the ice has, since you're all standing on it. Names. Scratched into the walls, down where your relic lived. Four men went in. One came out. Your Compass isn't worth anything, gentlemen—it's a witness. And witnesses belong with their dead."

Kessler started forward. "Fairweather—"

"It points at what you want most," I said. "Yours pointed at nothing. Think about what that means before you take another step. Because I've thought about it, and here's what I think: somewhere between the borderlands and this summit, you stopped wanting things and started wanting *winning*, and winning isn't a direction. There's nowhere for the needle to go."

He stopped. For one heartbeat, one full heartbeat, I thought I'd reached him—not to mercy, nothing so useful, but to that terrible naked place where a man sees himself clearly and can't look away.

Then Harrow moved.

I don't know what the Compass showed Harrow. I never asked and he never said. But I saw him look at Kessler holding it, and I saw the arithmetic change in his face—the same arithmetic every man in that company must have been running since the shaft came open: *if it shows what you want most, and we all want the same prize, then the prize can only be held by one.* Kessler had been reasonable right up until reason stopped being profitable. His men knew it. They'd always known it. They'd just needed the object in their hands to know it out loud.

Harrow grabbed for the Compass. Kessler twisted away. The gun swung wide, then up, then pointed—at Harrow, at the sky, at nothing, everywhere—and the alliance that had held together across four hundred miles of bad country came apart in the space of two breaths, the way alliances do, quietly and then all at once.

And in that space, I turned and dropped the Hollow Compass into the ice.

* * *

It made almost no sound. That's the part I'll remember longest. All of this—all of Voss's dying bargain, the riders, the river, the switchbacks, the storm, Odile bleeding, Reyes walking back through whiteout to warn us, Sefa navigating by memory because the map was gone, five people dragged across a mountain by a promise to a dead man—all of it, and the ending was a small black shape falling four feet into blue shadow, turning once, and settling into the dark with barely a click. Like a coin in a well. Like it had been listening for exactly that sound for forty years and finally got it.

The fighting stopped.

Not because of the sound. Because of the silence after it.

Six armed men stood in falling snow around an empty shaft, and the thing they'd crossed a mountain for was gone, and there was nothing left to point a weapon at except each other and me. The wind filled the pause. Somewhere below us, deep in the glacier, I imagined—I chose to imagine—the needle swinging gently, pointing at whatever the ice wants most, which is presumably more ice.

Kessler found me first. Of course he did. He came across the stones with his coat open and snow melting on his shoulders and his eyes gone flat and bright, and he put the gun against my chest the way you'd lean a tool against a wall, casually, like it was tired of being held.

"Do you know what I gave up for that?" he said. Quietly. That was the worst part—he wasn't shouting. "Do you have any idea what I traded to stand on this rock?"

"A great deal, I'd guess," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "But here's the thing, Mr. Kessler. You didn't give it up for the Compass. You gave it up for the *wanting*. And the wanting's still yours. You can keep it. It's the only part that survived the trip."

The gun pressed harder. I felt it through my coat, through the lining where a map used to be sewn, and I thought—this is absurd, this is genuinely absurd—that the coat had cost eleven crowns in Bracken Ford and Sefa had haggled the seller down from fourteen, and that it would be a stupid coat to die in, and that there were no good coats to die in, and that I would very much prefer not to test any of it.

Then Sefa stepped up beside me.

She didn't say anything. She just planted herself at my left shoulder, small and soaked and seventeen, arms crossed, chin up, wearing the expression she used on customs officers and grown men who underestimated her, which was nearly all of them.

Then Odile, leaning on her makeshift litter-pole, gray-faced, shoulder bound, standing when standing should have been impossible, taking her place at my right like she was joining a receiving line.

Then Reyes, frostbitten fingers tucked into his armpits, half-dead on his feet, who had every reason in the world to hate me and no reason at all to be standing in a gunsight on a mountain—he stood anyway.

Nobody raised a weapon. Nobody shouted. That was the astonishing thing. Three wounded, exhausted people simply stood next to me in the snow, and Kessler's gun arm began, very slightly, to shake.

"Shoot," Sefa said. She said it almost kindly. "Go on. Six of you, one shot, and then what? You think any of your men follow a man who murders a stranger over a hole in the ice?" She tilted her head at the others. "Look at them. Look at their faces. They already know what the Compass showed them. You think they want to die for your nothing?"

Harrow spat into the snow. Another man lowered his rifle all the way to the ground, deliberately, the way you set down something you're done carrying. A third just turned and walked a few steps away and stood with his back to all of us, looking east, toward wherever his needle had pointed.

Kessler looked at his own men. I watched him do it. I watched him see what I'd seen: that power isn't taken, it's *lent*, and his men had quietly recalled the loan.

"You could have sold it," he said to me. Almost a whisper. "You could have named a price. Anyone sane would have named a price."

"Yes," I said. "That's rather the point."

He lowered the gun. Slowly, joint by joint, like a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying, and when it hung at his side he looked older by ten years and smaller by twenty pounds, and I understood that I was watching a man die without the courtesy of falling over. Whatever he'd been before the range—a surveyor's muscle, a company man, someone's son—he wasn't recoverable from where he stood.

"Get off my mountain," he said.

"It was never yours," Odile said. Her voice was hoarse and it carried anyway. "I mapped these passes, Mr. Kessler. I drew the lines your company erased villages along. If anyone here owns this ground, it's the people you displaced. And they're not asking. They're telling."

For a moment I thought he might raise the gun again out of pure habit. Then the storm decided for all of us. It came off the ridge in a white wall, sudden and total, and the world shrank to six feet in every direction, and when it thinned enough to see again, Kessler and two of his men were moving downslope toward the west, bent double against the wind, and the others were simply gone—scattered into the white like seeds.

Sefa caught my arm. "East," she said. "Now. While it's still a race and not a burial."

* * *

We went down through the storm in a rope line, Reyes first because his hands still worked better than his pride, then Odile between Sefa and me, the litter-pole lashed across our shoulders, her boots dragging when she let them. The snow came harder with every hundred feet of descent, and the trail disappeared under it within minutes of our passing, which felt less like misfortune and more like the mountain tidying up after us.

Somewhere above and behind, faint through the wind, I heard a shout—Kessler's voice or one of his men's, angry, lost, calling a name that the wind took apart before it arrived. I didn't look back. Looking back is how the range collects its toll.

At the shoulder below the summit ring, where the stones gave way to scree, Sefa pulled up short and pointed with her chin. Down-slope, half-buried already, lay the mule.

Or what the ice had left of the mule. And lashed to the wreckage, frozen stiff, half of our food stores, intact.

"Well," Sefa said. "The mountain gives refunds."

We loaded what we could carry. Odile laughed—actually laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound, the first laugh any of us had managed in two days—and Reyes checked her bandage by feel because his fingers were too numb to trust his eyes, and we kept descending, and the storm fell behind us by degrees like a debt being paid off.

Near the treeline, where the snow turned to rain and the rain turned to the ordinary gray drizzle of the lower world, Sefa fell into step beside me. She was quiet for a long time. Then:

"What did it show you?" she asked. "When you held it. On the summit. You held it, didn't you?"

"I did."

"And?"

I thought about lying to her. I'd gotten very good at lying; it had been my profession for years, erasing myself, and a lie would have fit neatly into the old habits. But she'd stood in front of a gun for me, and Odile had stood on a broken shoulder, and Reyes had walked back through a blizzard, and none of them had lied about anything that mattered.

"It pointed south," I said. "Toward Merrow's Landing. Toward a woman who let me disappear and never came looking." I watched the treeline darken ahead of us, the first honest trees in weeks. "It didn't show me treasure, Sefa. It showed me the address of everything I ran from. Turns out I've been carrying it the whole time. The Compass just wrote it down."

She nodded slowly, the way she did when she was filing something away for later, which was most of the time. "Mara Voss is in Merrow's Landing," she said. "So's your mother, if the paper's right about her field station." A pause, sly as anything. "Funny how both your needles point the same direction."

"It's not funny at all," I said.

"No," she agreed. "It's worse. It means you can't get lost anymore, can you?"

Ahead of us, through the rain, the valley opened up—the long green descent toward the coast road, toward Merrow's Landing, toward a daughter who didn't know yet that her father's letters were folded against my ribs, and a mother who didn't know yet that her child was coming down out of the mountains with a name and a face and five days' beard, visible, findable, *known*. Behind us the snow closed over the summit, over the empty shaft, over the crevasse and its scratched names, keeping what it keeps.

Sefa was right. I couldn't get lost anymore.

God help me, I wasn't sure I wanted to.
