## 20. Descent Before Snow

The snow came down like a verdict.

Not the soft, apologetic flakes of a Bracken Ford winter—the kind you could shrug off with a shoulder and a curse—but big, wet, deliberate clumps that landed on my eyelashes and stayed there, melting down my cheeks like the mountain itself was crying on my behalf. I didn't have time to be moved by it.

"Left!" I shouted over the wind. "Odile says left!"

Odile couldn't say much of anything anymore. She was strapped into the litter Tomas and Jude had rigged from a tent pole and two bedrolls, her leg splinted, her face gray as slate, but her eyes were open and tracking, and every few minutes she'd lift one hand—the good one—and point without a word, like a weathervane made of guilt and genius. Left meant away from the cornice. Left meant away from the crevasse field where we'd buried half our food and all of our luck under an avalanche of Kessler's men.

I took us left.

Behind us, up at the ring of stones around the shaft, the mountain was still arguing with itself. We'd heard it start as we clipped the last of our gear together—shouting, then a shot, then nothing but wind. Whatever the Compass had shown those men when they finally cracked it open, it had been enough to turn them from a company into a mob. Kessler's voice had carried down the ridge for a while, that reasonable, patient voice of his, trying to talk his own people back into being employees. Then it stopped carrying. I didn't wonder about it. Wondering is a luxury of people walking downhill with full stomachs.

We were walking downhill. Our stomachs were not full. The arithmetic of survival is very simple and very cruel, and I've never once seen it come out in anyone's favor who deserved it.

* * *

You should understand something about me before you hear how this descent went, because everyone always gets it wrong. They think guides love mountains. They think we're drawn to the high places, that there's some romance in it, some calling.

I hate the Ashkettle Range. I have hated it since I was nine years old, since the morning my mother woke me before dawn and put me on a cart with the good pots and my grandmother's quilt, and we rolled out of Hollowmere while the company's surveyors were still drinking their coffee, and by noon the village I was born in belonged to a man in a gray coat who had never seen it and never would. My father stayed behind to argue about the deed. The deed was paper. The company had guns. You can finish that sum yourself; children of displaced villages always can.

So no. I don't love mountains. I know them the way you know a house you were thrown out of—which is to say better than anyone alive, and with a bitterness that keeps you warm at night.

For eleven years I ran from that knowledge. Sold passes, ran contraband, learned every goat track and smuggler's cut through the range because knowing it paid and because staying close to something that hurt you is its own kind of sickness. I told myself I was guiding for money. Money was true, the way a map is true—it shows you the shape of things without telling you why anyone drew it.

Then we sheltered in the hollow village, and I stood in a house with the same door-latch my grandmother's house had, and I understood that I hadn't been running *from* the range at all. I'd been circling it. Like the Compass's needle, if the stories are right. Pointing, always pointing, at the thing I wanted most and refusing, always refusing, to walk toward it.

Well. I was walking toward it now, at speed, in a blizzard, with four broken people and a dead mule's worth of food behind me.

The east side of the range doesn't switchback. That's the west, built by engineers for carts and compromise. The east side is what the mountain does when nobody's watching: scree fields that move under your boots like living things, gullies that funnel wind into a scream, and then, if the mountain permits, the treeline—stunted pines bent sideways by forty years of weather, growing like they're apologizing for existing.

That was the prize. The treeline. Below it, wood for fire, water that wasn't ice, slopes shallow enough that a stumble didn't mean a two-hundred-foot lesson in gravity.

Above it, we were exposed, five small dark shapes on a white page, and somewhere behind us the story of Kessler's camp was still writing itself in gunsmoke and scattered men.

* * *

Reyes went first. He was the steadiest on scree even frostbitten—he said it was field training, that you learn to walk on unstable ground when the ground is made of people worse off than you—and he called the footing back to us in a low, flat voice, the medic's voice, the one that has decided panic is a symptom to be treated later.

"Firm shelf, ten feet. Then loose. Test everything."

Jude came after him with the litter's front ropes, and I brought up the rear with Odile's pack and whatever remained of our food, which fit in a bag I could have hidden under a hat. Sacks of salt pork gone with the mule down the crevasse. Half the hardtack. A tin of tea Odile refused to let anyone jettison, on the grounds that morale was a supply like any other. She was right, which was annoying, which was typical.

The litter was the problem. Two people hauling a third across scree in falling snow is not travel; it's surgery performed with geography. Every twenty minutes we stopped, rewrapped the ropes, breathed steam at each other, and started again. Jude never complained. I kept waiting for it—I'd spent three weeks cataloguing this person's silences, and I thought I had them all mapped—but the complaint never came. There was just this thing Jude did instead, this small stubborn motion of resetting their grip and leaning into the slope, over and over, like the mountain was a debt collector and Jude intended to outlast it.

"You could put me down," Odile said at one stop. She said it lightly. Lightly, from Odile Marchand, was a dialect all its own.

"No," said Jude.

"I'm telling you the load math doesn't favor—"

"No," said Jude again, and then, softer, in that voice they probably thought nobody heard: "Nobody else gets left on this mountain."

Nobody argued. There are arguments you win and arguments you survive, and then there are arguments where someone has already decided the shape of their soul, and you just stand there in the snow feeling lucky to witness it.

* * *

The first of Kessler's men found us at the second hour.

Don't picture a fight. Picture the opposite. He came stumbling down a gully above us, weaponless—or having abandoned the weapon, which amounts to the same thing—with his coat torn and his eyes doing something I recognized immediately, because I'd seen it in mirrors: the look of a person whose map of the world just got redrawn without their consent.

He saw us. We saw him. Five exhausted nobodies and one broken soldier of a broken company, all of us hanging off the same mountainside like laundry.

He raised both hands. "I'm done," he said. His voice was wrecked. "Whatever it showed you—it showed me my brother. Dead eight years. Standing in my mother's kitchen, asking me why." He laughed, or something near it. "Kessler's men are scattering. Half of them won't even go back to camp. You want to know what beats a company? Show a man what he actually wants. Ruins him for wage work forever."

Jude looked at him a long moment. I watched them decide something. I've watched Jude decide a lot of things on this trip—mostly whether to speak, mostly deciding no—but this time they reached into the food bag and pulled out a fistful of hardtack and held it out.

"Eat," Jude said. "Then go down ahead of us and tell anyone you meet at the treeline that there's a party coming with a wounded woman. Tell them we're not worth following."

The man took the bread like it might bite him. He ate standing in the snow, crying without any noise about it, and then he went, fast, down the slope, and I don't know if he ever told anyone anything. But I know what it cost Jude to feed a man who'd helped hunt us across a range, and I know what it looked like, and I'm writing it here so somebody remembers it was possible.

"He'll sell our position," I said, when he was gone.

"Maybe," Jude said. "Probably."

"And you fed him anyway."

Jude shrugged, that infuriating shrug, the one that means *yes, and I'd do it again, and I don't want to discuss it.* Three weeks ago that shrug would have made me want to throw them off a cliff. Now it just made me tired in a way that felt suspiciously like affection.

* * *

The storm thickened. Of course it did. Mountains are dramatists.

By the fourth hour I was navigating by memory alone—the map had gone down the crevasse with the mule, along with half our food and, in a sense, my belief that I was only ever in this for the pay. Memory is a strange instrument. It's less accurate than a map and far more honest. I knew this slope because my father had hunted it, because my mother had pointed out the safe line down to me when I was small enough to ride her shoulders, because Hollowmere's people had used this eastern descent for generations before anyone drew it on paper anyone official would accept.

Unmapped ground. The best kind. The kind that belongs to the people who carry it in their bodies.

There was a moment—I mark it because moments like it come maybe twice in a life—when the cloud tore open below us, just for thirty seconds, and I saw the whole east face laid out: the scree, the gullies, and far below, a dark ragged band that could only be the treeline, and beyond it, faint as breath on glass, the valley. Our valley. Not mine-by-inheritance-from-a-cart. Mine by blood and grudge and eleven years of circling.

"There," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I didn't care. "That's the way. That's my way."

Odile lifted her head from the litter and looked at me with an expression I'd learn later she reserved for maps she wished she'd drawn. "You've walked this?"

"My family did. Before it was taken."

"Then you're not a guide anymore," she said. "You're a primary source."

It was the nicest thing a cartographer has ever said to me, and I include the man in Bracken Ford who once called my trail notes "the only honest documents in the district."

* * *

The treeline, when we reached it, was not triumphant. Nothing about that day was triumphant. It was more like drowning people touching bottom—not safety, exactly, but a floor, and the sudden shocking discovery that your legs still work.

We came down into the pines as the light failed, all five of us, and I counted us twice because I didn't believe it the first time. Jude, snow-crusted, moving like every joint was a negotiation. Reyes, hands wrapped in rags, still somehow checking Odile's pulse before he'd check his own. Odile, conscious, swearing at the litter with real eloquence, which Tomas said was the best sign yet. And Jude, again, because I counted them twice. Because part of me still expected the world to take them back, the way it takes back everything quiet and careful and unclaimed.

We got a fire going. Small, screened, illegal by every rule of mountain craft, and absolutely necessary. Reyes melted snow. Odile drank tea and dictated corrections to a route she'd never see again, out of pure habit, and we listened, because her corrections were always right and because listening to her was cheaper than arguing.

And then, sitting there in the orange circle of that too-big fire, with the snow coming down through the branches like the sky emptying its pockets, I felt it happen.

Not a revelation. Revelations are loud. This was quiet, like a latch clicking.

I had spent eleven years running from Hollowmere—from the taken house, the lost deed, the father who stayed to argue with paper and guns and lost. Running, I told myself, toward money, toward independence, toward the sharp free life of a girl who owed nothing to anybody. And here's the joke, the one the mountain had been saving for me: I hadn't been running from the loss at all. I'd been running from the weight of carrying it. Carrying grief is heavy. Carrying a home that was stolen is heavier. So I'd set it down every morning and picked it up every night and told myself the ache in my shoulders was just the work.

But you can't guide people through a country you refuse to belong to. You can only lead them in circles. I know, because I checked—I led myself in circles for a decade, and the Compass, that black-glass liar, would have found my needle pointing at a door-latch in an empty village every single time.

Now I was carrying it. All of it. The village, the displacement, my mother's voice saying *this is the safe line*, the whole unmapped inheritance of it. And yes, it was heavy. But it was also, for the first time, *mine*, and there is a difference between weight you're fleeing and weight you've chosen, and the difference is that the second one lets you stand up straight.

"Sefa." Jude's voice, from across the fire. "You got us down. Off the map, off the record, through a storm. How?"

I looked at them—at this person who'd spent years erasing themselves so thoroughly that being known nearly killed them, and who'd chosen, today, to be seen by four strangers and a mountain.

"Family knowledge," I said. And then, testing the words like new boots: "My family's. From before."

Jude nodded slowly, and something passed between us that neither of us needed to name. Two people who'd spent years pretending to be nobody, discovering that nobody was the one lie that never held.

* * *

We slept in shifts. Real sleep, some of it, the kind with dreams in it.

In mine, my grandmother's quilt was on a bed in Hollowmere, and the door had the same latch, and when I opened it, the village wasn't empty. It was full of people I'd never met, descendants of the scattered, and they looked up when I came in and one of them said, "Oh good, you brought the trails with you," and handed me tea.

I woke before dawn with frost on my eyelashes and the fire down to a red heart, and Jude was already awake, sitting up, looking east through the trees at a thin gold line where the valley opened out.

"Merrow's Landing?" I asked.

"Three days, if the snow holds off." Jude paused. "There's a daughter there. Voss's daughter. I owe her some letters."

"I know."

"And after that—" Jude stopped. Started again, which they almost never did. "After that, I have to write to someone I haven't written to in years."

"Your mother," I said.

Jude looked at me, startled, and I shrugged—my own shrug, learned from theirs, improved upon.

"I guide people," I said. "I can tell where someone's needle points."

Below us, the valley waited, gold-edged and ordinary and real. Above us, the storm was burying the summit, the shaft, the ring of stones, and whatever black glass truth lay inside it—burying it deep, the way ice keeps things, until someone comes looking who's ready to carry what they find.

I shouldered my pack. It was heavier than it had been three weeks ago.

I wouldn't have traded a gram of it.
