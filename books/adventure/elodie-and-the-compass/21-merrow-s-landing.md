## 21. Merrow's Landing

Merrow's Landing smelled of fish and tar and the particular despair of towns that had once been important. It sat at the mouth of a river the color of old pewter, its docks rotting in elegant curves, its streets laid out by someone who had clearly expected a city to happen here and then died disappointed.

We came down off the range on the ninth day after the summit, five people and no mule, looking like what we were: survivors of something nobody would believe. Odile walked with a stick and a permanent scowl she claimed was therapeutic. Reyes's frostbitten fingers were wrapped in his own improvised dressings, which he critiqued constantly. Sefa hadn't stopped talking since the treeline—a torrent of words about her mother's village, about the company ledgers she intended to find, about whether gulls could be eaten and how many were too many. She was carrying her family's displacement like a pack she'd finally decided to shoulder instead of abandon, and it made her loud.

I didn't mind. Loud was better than the silence I'd carried for years. Loud meant known.

The letters rode against my ribs in an oilskin packet, as they had for two thousand miles. Voss's handwriting, cramped and apologetic even before you read a word: *To Mara Voss, if she'll have it.* Eleven letters. Unsent. I'd read them all by now, some twice, and every reading had felt like eavesdropping on a man drowning slowly in his own cowardice.

I'd told myself the whole way down the mountain that I'd hand them over and go. That was the deal. See it home, deliver the letters, done. Debt paid, obligation discharged, back to being nobody from nowhere.

Sefa saw me checking the packet for the hundredth time and said nothing, which from Sefa was practically a sermon.

* * *

We found Mara Voss where the town's one respectable street met the water, in a chandlery with a sign so faded it might have said anything. She was behind the counter arguing with a supplier about rope prices, and I knew her before anyone said a name—because she had Callum Voss's exact jaw, set at exactly the stubborn angle he'd worn in the hour before he died.

"Mara Voss?" I asked, when the supplier had been seen off with threats of future business.

She looked up. Middle-aged, sun-browned, hands like a working woman's. "Who's asking?"

"My name is Jude Fairweather. Your father sent me."

Something crossed her face—too fast to name, too slow to miss. "My father," she said carefully, "has been dead to this town for twenty-two years."

"He's dead everywhere now," I said. "Three weeks, give or take. A gut wound, in the borderlands. He traded me a map for a promise." I set the oilskin packet on the counter between us. "These are yours. He wrote them himself. He never sent them because—" I stopped, because I'd rehearsed this part and it still sounded cruel. "Because he couldn't make himself do it. That's not an excuse. It's just what happened."

Mara Voss did not touch the packet. She looked at it the way you look at a snake that might be asleep.

"Is there money in it?" she asked finally.

"No."

"Then why bring it?"

"Because he asked," I said. "And because I promised. And because—" I gestured vaguely at the packet, at the whole rotten architecture of the thing. "Because somebody should know what he carried. Even if it's only paper."

She closed the shop. Turned the sign herself, with hands that weren't quite steady, and led us through the back to a room above it that smelled of lamp oil and dried cod, and there, over tea strong enough to strip varnish, she opened her father's letters for the first time.

* * *

She read them aloud. Not all of them—at first just fragments, her voice flattening out as she went, the way voices do when they're holding something heavy at arm's length.

*Dear Mara. Today I found the thing we were looking for, and I want to tell you everything, but first I have to tell you about Tomas Aldous.*

"Who's Tomas Aldous?" Sefa asked.

"One of the three," I said. "The men who went into the crevasse field with him."

Mara read on. The letters got worse as they got older—or better, depending on how you measure honesty. The early ones were excuses dressed as explanations. The later ones dropped the costume entirely. In the sixth letter, written in a hand shakier than the rest, Voss finally said it plainly:

*Mara. I need you to understand what kind of man your father is, so you can stop wondering. There were four of us who went onto the glacier. We found the Compass together. And when the weather turned, when Kessler's people came up the mountain behind us, I ran. I left Aldous and the Brandt brothers on the ice. They were slower than me. That's the whole truth of it—they were slower, and I wanted to live more than I wanted to be decent. I've spent twenty years telling myself there was nothing else I could have done. There was always something else. There is always something else.*

The room was very quiet. Outside, a gull screamed at another gull about something neither would ever win.

"He walked away," Mara said. Not a question. She'd reached the same sentence I had, months ago, by firelight, alone.

"He walked away," I said. "And then he spent the rest of his life writing letters he couldn't send, carrying a relic he couldn't use, and dying alone in a place where nobody knew his name. I don't know if that's penance. I don't know if penance works that way. But he kept those letters dry through two thousand miles of hell because he wanted you to have them, and wanting something and failing to reach for it—that part I understand."

Mara set down the letter she was holding and looked at me properly for the first time. "You talk like someone who's practiced not reaching for things."

"I'm very good at it," I said. "Award-winning, almost."

That almost got a laugh out of her. Almost. Her father's jaw again, but this time with something underneath it that was hers alone.

* * *

They stayed. None of us discussed it; it simply happened, the way things happen with people who've been freezing together on a mountain. Odile commandeered the chandlery's back table and started redrawing maps of the Ashkettle Range from memory—the true ones, the ones she'd never sold, annotating them in a small furious hand. Reyes took over the medical care of half the fishing fleet within four days, mostly by fixing things other doctors had given up on and complaining about their technique. Sefa vanished into the town records office and emerged each evening smelling of dust and triumph, building a case file against the company brick by brick.

And I sat in a room above a chandlery with a woman I'd never met, while she read her dead father's mail, and discovered that I had nowhere I urgently needed to be.

It was the strangest feeling I'd had in years. Possibly ever.

On the third evening, Mara finished the last letter—the eleventh, the shortest—and put it down, and looked at me across the lamplight.

"You knew him for less than a day," she said.

"A night and a morning."

"And you crossed a mountain range for him. People died trying to stop you. You gave away the thing everyone wanted." She shook her head slowly. "Why? Really. Not the promise. Why keep it?"

I thought about lying. I'm excellent at lying; it's practically a trade skill. But there was something about that room—the lamp, the cod smell, the woman with her father's stubborn jaw holding eleven unsent apologies—that made lying feel like a waste of good material.

"Because I've spent ten years making sure nobody could find me," I said. "No records, no letters, no forwarding address. I erased myself so thoroughly that when my own mother published a paper mentioning my field of work, I found out about it secondhand, from a dying stranger. And somewhere on that mountain I figured out that Voss wasn't asking me to carry a map. He was asking me to carry a confession to the one person alive who deserved to hear it. Because he couldn't face doing it himself, and I—" I turned my cup on the table. "I knew what it cost to be unreachable. I was the world's leading expert. So I thought, fine. Somebody has to be reachable. Might as well be me. Once."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in Merrow's Landing drinking terrible tea with a woman whose father I buried under stones in the borderlands," I said. "So apparently the expertise is fading."

Mara studied me for a long moment. Then she slid the eleventh letter across the table.

"This one isn't addressed to me," she said. "Read it."

I picked it up. The salutation said only *To whoever brings these.* Voss's handwriting, steadier than I remembered it, as if he'd saved his best ink for the stranger.

*If you're reading this, you kept the promise, which means you're either honest or stubborn, and in my experience the two are indistinguishable. I don't know what you're running from. I only know that I ran for twenty years and it didn't work, and the letters I never sent weighed more than any treasure I ever dug up. Whatever you're carrying—put it down somewhere it can be found. That's the only advice I have left, and I stole most of it from mistakes.*

Mara watched me read it. "He wrote that the week before he left," she said. "For you. For whoever came. He knew someone would come, eventually. He just didn't believe it enough to send the rest."

"That sounds like him," I said.

"It does," she agreed, and there was grief in it, but also something gentler—the grief of a woman meeting her father honestly for the first time, twenty-two years late, and finding him exactly as flawed and exactly as human as everyone else. "Jude Fairweather. That's your real name?"

"It's the one I was issued."

"Your mother. Dr. Imogen Fairweather. The archaeologist." She said it lightly, but I felt it land anyway, the way a mention of home always lands on people without one. "Sefa talks. She talks a great deal."

"She does," I said. "It's one of her many features."

"She says you're going to write to her."

"Sefa says a lot of things."

"Are you?"

I looked at the letter in my hands. *Put it down somewhere it can be found.* An old man's stolen advice, bought with twenty years of silence and three graves on a glacier.

"I don't know," I said, which was the most honest answer available, and Mara Voss—her father's daughter to the bone—accepted it without pushing, the way you accept weather.

* * *

That night I stood at the window of the room I'd been given—it had a bed, an actual bed, and I'd slept in it with the suspicious gratitude of a feral animal—and watched the harbor lights bob on black water. Behind me, the oilskin packet lay empty on the table, eleven letters lighter, and I felt the absence of them like the absence of a tooth: smaller, and somehow more noticeable.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow I'd write to Imogen Fairweather. Not forgiveness—I wasn't sure forgiveness was a thing you could post—but coordinates. An offer. *There's a range that doesn't exist on your maps, and I know where it is, and I think you and I should dig something up together.* It was terrifying in a way the crevasse field hadn't been. Glaciers can't reject you.

Downstairs, faintly, I heard Sefa laughing at something Odile had said, and Reyes telling them both to quiet down, patients sleeping, and the sound settled over the house like snowfall—soft, insistent, accumulating.

Someone knocked. Mara, wrapped in a shawl, holding two cups of the varnish-stripper tea.

"You'll want to leave before dawn," she said. "People like you always do. My father did."

"People like me," I repeated.

"Runners." She handed me a cup. "But the tide doesn't turn until mid-morning, and the road east is mud until noon regardless, and I've learned you can't outrun weather or yourself, so." She shrugged, an eloquent shrug, a whole philosophy in one gesture. "Stay for breakfast. Stay for the day. Leave tomorrow, or the day after, or don't. Nobody in this town will ask you questions, and if they do, I'll lie beautifully. It runs in the family."

I took the cup. Outside, the gulls had gone quiet and the harbor breathed against its pilings, and somewhere south my mother was presumably awake too, not knowing yet that a letter was coming, not knowing that her invisible child had decided, provisionally, tentatively, with several escape routes still mapped—to be visible.

"For breakfast," I said. "And we'll see about the rest."

Mara smiled, and it was her father's smile, and it wasn't, and I drank the terrible tea, and for the first time in ten years, I did not leave before dawn.
