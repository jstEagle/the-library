## 1. The Weight of a Name

Greyhollow smelled of tar and herring and the particular rot that came when a town had given up pretending it would ever be more than what the sea left behind. Zephyrine Eriksen had been back eleven days, and in those eleven days she had learned three things with great precision: that her room above the chandlery cost four coppers a night and leaked rain directly onto her pillow; that the harbormaster's clerk would not meet her eye; and that her name, spoken aloud in any establishment that served food or sold rope, had the effect of a dropped glass — a small bright silence, then everyone very carefully continuing.

She sat now at the end of the Saltcellar's long counter, furthest from the fire, nursing a cup of barley tea she had paid for with the last coin that still bore her own thumbprint on its worn face. The tea was terrible. She drank it anyway, because warmth was warmth, and because sitting in a tavern without buying anything was the kind of behavior that got a blacklisted guide thrown out on her ear, and being thrown out meant walking past the notice board twice.

The notice board was why she was here. Tacked to it among the usual detritus — lost dogs, missing boats, a reward for information about a stolen figurehead — was a single sheet of good paper, written in a hand that shook only slightly:

*Guide wanted. Kestrel Escarpment crossing, west approach. One traveler, light gear. Triple standard rate. Inquire Callum Voss, Room 4, the Saltcellar.*

Triple rate. Nobody offered triple rate for an escarpment crossing unless they were fools, liars, or dying. Zephyrine had spent enough years reading mountains to know that men who wrote such notices were usually all three at once.

She also knew what the Kestrel Escarpment did to guides who took contracts there. It was not the worst route on the coast — that honor belonged to the Serrated Teeth, where her mother had made her name and then, two years later, unmade it — but it was close. Collapsing passes. Weather that turned like a key in a lock. A ridge line that narrowed in places to the width of a coffin lid. Three guides had lost clients on the Kestrel in the last five years, and two of those guides had lost their licenses along with them, their names struck from the rolls in ink that never dried.

Zephyrine's name had been struck from different rolls, for different reasons, but ink was ink. Blacklisted was blacklisted. Officially she could no longer guide at all; unofficially, nobody hired her for anything, which amounted to the same starvation with less paperwork.

So she drank her terrible tea and listened to the stairs at the back of the tavern, waiting for the man from Room 4 to come down and prove himself a fool, a liar, or dying.

He came down at dusk.

She heard him before she saw him — a wet, rattling cough that started somewhere below his ribs and seemed to go on longer than any breath should reasonably last. When he finally appeared in the doorway between stairwell and common room, she understood immediately which of the three he was, and it was none of them, exactly. He was all of them. He was a man whose body had begun making arrangements without consulting him.

Callum Voss was sixty if he was a day, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who had once carried heavy things up steep things, though the weight had gone off him recently and unevenly, like snow sliding from a roof. His hands were enormous, scarred across every knuckle, and they gripped the doorframe as though the floor might be persuaded to tilt. His eyes, when they found hers across the room, were the pale grey of glacial ice — the color that rock went when it had been under pressure for ten thousand years.

He crossed to her without asking anyone which chair she occupied. Everyone else in the Saltcellar had, by unspoken agreement, left the bench at her end of the counter empty, and Voss settled onto it as naturally as if it had been kept warm for him.

"You're the Ravensworth girl," he said.

"Zephyrine Ravensworth." She said it flatly, daring him to flinch at the name. Most people did. *Ravensworth* had been a word that opened doors once — her mother's doing, a reputation built over thirty years of bringing people home alive from places that had no business letting anyone live. Then the survey scandal, the forged charts, the inquiry, the disgrace, and now *Ravensworth* closed doors so fast it could take fingers off.

Voss didn't flinch. He ordered two more cups of the barley tea from the pot-boy, paid with a coin that gleamed, and studied her with the unhurried attention of a man appraising a climbing iron for hairline cracks.

"They say you're the best guide on this coast," he said.

"They say wrong. I'm not permitted to guide."

"I'm not hiring you through the guild. I'm hiring *you*." He tapped the counter with one scarred finger. "The escarpment doesn't check credentials."

"The weather does."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "Good. I want someone who respects it." The cough took him then, folding him forward over his forearms, and Zephyrine watched the muscles of his back work uselessly against whatever was growing in his chest, and felt something old and cold turn over inside her. She knew that sound. She had heard it in her mother's tent, in the last season, before the disgrace killed her faster than any mountain could have.

When Voss straightened, there was blood-bright color high on his cheekbones and his eyes had watered clear. "Kestrel crossing," he said, when he could speak. "West approach, over the Shoulder, down into the fjord country beyond. I'm told it's six days in fair weather."

"It's six days in fair weather and a death sentence otherwise. And it hasn't been fair weather on the Kestrel since the thaw."

"So we wait for a window. I have time." He said it lightly, and then ruined the lie by coughing again, shorter this time, angrier. "I have some time."

"Why?" Zephyrine asked. Not *why the escarpment* — she'd get to that. *Why me.* "You could hire half a dozen licensed guides in Greyhollow for a quarter of what you're offering. Men with clean names. Why pay triple for a woman the guild won't even let carry a rope?"

Voss wrapped both huge hands around his fresh cup and looked into it as though reading tea leaves in barley.

"Because a clean-named guide will turn me back at the first bad sign," he said. "Because a clean-named guide has a career to protect, a family to answer to, a guild breathing down her neck. Because a clean-named guide, when I tell her what I actually need, will refuse — and be right to." He raised his eyes to hers. "I need someone with nothing left to lose."

The words landed harder than he could have known. Zephyrine looked away, toward the fogged window, where Greyhollow's few lamps were blooming yellow in the murk. Beyond the harbor wall the sea worked at the rocks with its patient, mindless hatred. Somewhere out there, under cold green water, lay everything her mother had loved and everything that had destroyed her, and here sat Zephyrine with four coppers' worth of debt and a compass she couldn't bring herself to sell.

"That's a cruel thing to shop for," she said quietly.

"Aye," said Voss. "It is. I've been crueler lately. Dying does that." He slid something across the counter: a purse. Heavy. The clink of it was obscene in the quiet tavern. "Half now. Half on the far side. Triple your old rate — I asked around, discreetly, before I asked for you."

"And what you actually need," Zephyrine said. "The thing a clean-named guide would refuse."

Voss was quiet for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a bed creaked; the tide turned outside with a long sigh against the harbor stones.

"There's something I have to fetch," he said at last. "Past the escarpment. On the other side. And there's something I have to give away, after." His hand went briefly, unconsciously, to his breast pocket, pressing flat whatever lay against his heart. "That's all you need to know to walk me over. The rest—" Another cough, bitten short. "The rest is mine to carry. You just get me up the mountain and down again alive. Can you do that?"

Every prudent instinct Zephyrine owned stood up in a body and voted no. A dying client on the Kestrel. An undisclosed errand. Triple pay — which meant trouble cubed, her father used to say, and her father had died sober and poor and right about most things. If she took this contract and Voss died on the ridge, her name wouldn't merely stay black; it would be buried, stamped into the guild rolls beside the other guides who'd lost people on the Kestrel, another Ravensworth ruined by the mountains, proof that the family curse ran true.

And if she refused, she'd be out of coppers by week's end, out of the chandlery room soon after, and out of Greyhollow entirely — drifting down the coast with nothing but a dead woman's compass and a dead woman's shame.

Weary pragmatism won, as it always did. It was the only muscle she had left that still worked reliably.

"One condition," Zephyrine said. "If your lungs fail on the ridge — if I judge you can't make the descent — we turn around. No argument. No triple pay owed, no curses. We turn around and you die somewhere with a roof, and I keep my record clean of you. Agreed?"

Voss considered her for a moment, and she saw the appraisal finish behind his eyes — the last hairline crack checked for, and not found, or found and accepted.

"Agreed," he said. "Though I'll warn you now, guide: I don't intend to die on any ridge. I intend to die *after*. There's a difference, and it matters a great deal to me."

"Everyone intends that."

"Aye." He pushed the purse toward her. "But I've got further to go than most."

She took the purse. It was heavier than it should have been, the way promises are, and she counted nothing, because counting would have made it real and real things could be lost.

Upstairs, in her narrow room with its rain-stained ceiling, Zephyrine unpacked the one possession she had never once considered selling. Her mother's compass: brass-cased, palm-sized, its needle housing clouded with age, the lid engraved with a line of mountain range rendered so finely it looked like stitching. Mara Ravensworth had carried it over forty ranges and brought it home forty times. On the forty-first, the compass had come home alone, mailed back from the inquiry office with the rest of her effects, its needle bent two degrees off true — damaged, the accompanying note suggested, in circumstances relevant to the investigation.

Two degrees. Enough to put a survey line in the wrong valley. Enough, if a magistrate's forged report was to be believed, to ruin a map, a reputation, a life.

Zephyrine strapped the compass to her wrist the way her mother had taught her — snug, case inward, always against the pulse side — and felt the familiar cold weight settle over her like a second skin. Old grief, worn smooth by handling. She checked her rope, her pitons, her storm shelter, her rations. She counted the purse's coins once, finally, and stopped breathing for a moment at the sum.

Below the window, Greyhollow's lamps guttered in the rising wind, and beyond the harbor the Kestrel Escarpment stood invisible in the dark, a wall of black air where the stars didn't shine. Six days of it. A dying man with a secret pressed to his chest.

At dawn, Callum Voss was waiting at the west road with a pack lighter than any prospector's she'd ever seen, and when she reached him he smiled with cracked lips and said, "There's something you should see before we start walking," and drew from his breast pocket a folded sheet of paper stained with grease and years — and Zephyrine, looking down at the hand-drawn lines of a coastline she did not recognize, felt the compass on her wrist go suddenly, impossibly cold.
