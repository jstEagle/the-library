## 2. A Map for a Promise

The mountain took its toll in small payments first. A bootlace gone stiff with frost. The taste of iron in the wind off the scree. Callum Voss stopping to breathe every forty steps, then thirty, then twenty, until Zephyrine stopped counting and simply watched the man's shoulders for the tremor that meant his lungs were closing like a fist.

They had made the second shelf by midmorning, where the Kestrel Escarpment shrugged off its pine mantle and showed its true face — a wall of grey granite seamed with ice, rising into cloud the color of unwashed wool. Below them, Greyhollow had shrunk to a scatter of roof-slates beside its grey harbor, a town small enough to cover with a thumbnail. Zephyrine did not look at it long. Places you were leaving had a way of looking back.

"Guide." Voss's voice came out papery. He had lowered himself onto a boulder the size of a chapel pew, and the cough that followed him down was deep and wet, the cough of a man drowning on dry land. When it passed, he spat pink into the snow and grinned at her with a prospector's shameless optimism. "How far to the notch?"

"Three hours, if the weather holds." She crouched at the edge of the shelf, testing a handhold with her glove. "It won't. Cloud's coming down off the col by afternoon. We'll shelter at the notch and cross at first light."

"First light." He said it the way other men said a prayer, or a debt they meant to honor. "Good. I want to see the fjord before I die."

"You're not going to die on my mountain, Voss."

"Your mountain." He laughed, and the laugh cost him; she heard the wheeze beneath it, the rattle. "That's a fine thing for a blacklisted guide to say. Whose name's on it, then? Yours, or your mother's?"

Zephyrine's hand stilled on the rock. She had told him nothing of her mother. He was a stranger who had walked into the Salt and Anchor with a contract and triple pay and eyes that catalogued people the way a customs officer catalogues cargo. She should have known he'd done his reading.

"Nobody's name is on a mountain," she said. "That's the whole point of mountains."

"Mm." He fumbled at the collar of his coat, and she turned back to the route, giving him the privacy men always wanted when they were doing something they thought no one could see. Behind her she heard the click of a clasp, the rustle of oilcloth. "Then let me tell you about a name that is on something. *Meridian.* Salvage brig, lost in the Sable Fjord nine years back. Officially a wreck of no account. Unofficially—" the wheeze again, "—unofficially she went down carrying cargo worth more than Greyhollow."

"Salvage stories are like fish stories," Zephyrine said. "They get bigger every time the water's crossed."

"And yet you took my coin."

She had. Three months of arrears at her lodging house, and a name so black that harbormasters spat when they heard it. She had taken his coin the way a drowning woman takes a rope — without asking where it was anchored.

The cloud came down while they ate, as she had promised it would, swallowing the escarpment in a soft grey blindness. The temperature fell with it, the kind of cold that got into the joints of things. Voss grew quiet. She told herself it was the weather, the effort, the thin air. She told herself a lot of things that morning.

It was an hour past noon, on the long traverse below the notch where the path ran along a shelf barely wider than a coffin, that he stopped.

Not the stopping of a man catching his breath. The stopping of a man whose body has simply declined to continue. He put one hand flat against the rock face and stood there, and the sound he made was small and terrible — a whistling pull of air that never seemed to reach his chest.

Zephyrine had him under the arms and lowered to the shelf before she had consciously decided to move. She got his pack off, got him propped against the stone, got the brandy flask to his lips. His lips were the color of the slate around them.

"Voss. Look at me. Breathe with me. In—" she demonstrated, exaggerating, "—and out. Slow. Slow, damn you."

"I'm—" A cough tore through him, and he folded forward, and she caught his weight against her shoulder and held it there, this dying stranger heavier than any pack she'd ever carried. "I'm not going to make the notch, guide."

"You'll make it."

"No." He said it gently, which was worse. He lifted his head, and his eyes were clear — horribly clear, the clarity of a man whose body had already settled its accounts and was waiting only on the paperwork. "Listen to me. I've been dying since the spring. The mountain air was a gamble. I knew the odds. I took them anyway." His hand found her wrist, grip still strong, the grip of a man who had hauled ore out of the earth for forty years. "Which means the last thing I do on this earth is my business, and my business is now in your hands."

"Voss—"

"*Listen.*" The word came out cracked but absolute. "The map. In the oilcloth. Take it."

His pack lay against the rock, and she did not want to reach for it. Reaching for it was an admission. But his hand tightened on her wrist, and she was a pragmatic woman, and pragmatism, she had learned, was just grief wearing work clothes.

She drew out the oilcloth packet. Inside was a sheet of heavy paper, folded twice, stained with grease and salt and years of handling — and beneath it, a smaller square of the same, wrapped around something that clinked faintly. She opened the map. It was drawn in a sure, cramped hand: the Sable Fjord rendered in ink lines, soundings marked in fathoms, and there, in a narrow arm of the fjord where the cliffs pinched together, a small careful cross and two words in the same hand: *the Meridian.* The margins were crowded with annotations — depths, tide tables, bearings, and strings of figures that meant nothing to her.

"Six tides," Voss said. He had to stop twice to get it out. "The dry season's sixth tide. After that, the water drops and the wreck's ribs show, and every scavenger from here to the Salt Reaches will be on her like gulls on a gutted fish. The fleet's already fitting out. Corvane Hale's fleet."

The name went through her like a splinter driven under the nail. She kept her face still. Years of practice. *Magistrate Corvane Hale*, whose survey had closed the high passes, whose testimony had ended her mother's guiding license, whose signature sat at the bottom of a document Zephyrine had once read so many times she could recite it in the dark. She said nothing. The mountain wind said it for her, keening along the shelf.

"You know the name," Voss observed, watching her with those clear dying eyes. "Good. Then you understand why it can't be Hale who reaches her first. There's something aboard the Meridian worth more than gold, guide. Worth more than the cargo. Worth dying for, if it comes to that, and it seems it has." He coughed, wet and long, and when he could speak again his voice had dropped to a thread. "I'm not asking you to carry it for pay. I'm asking you to carry it for a promise. Take the map to my daughter. Wren Voss. She works the docks at Saltmere, the rigging yards. Tell her—" his breath hitched, "—tell her her father wasn't running from her. Tell him— tell her I was running *toward* something. For her. She'll want to spit on that. Let her spit. But give her the map."

"Give it to her yourself," Zephyrine said. "You'll live to Saltmere."

"Guide." He almost smiled. "You're a poor liar for a woman in your trade. You knew before I did. I saw you watching my shoulders this morning."

She had nothing to say to that. The cloud pressed down around them, and the shelf was narrow, and somewhere below the drop fell away into white nothing.

"Swear it," Voss said. "Not to me. I'll be past caring. Swear it to the mountain, if you've got any superstition left in you. Swear it on that compass you keep touching like a rosary."

Her hand had gone, without her permission, to the brass compass at her belt — her mother's, the one object she'd kept when the bailiffs took the rest. She looked down at it, at the worn case, the needle trembling toward a north no one could see through the cloud.

"I don't make promises I can't keep," she said at last. "I've got a black name and no money and a mountain between here and Saltmere, and a magistrate's fleet between Saltmere and your fjord. You're asking me to swear to a thing that might be impossible."

"I'm asking you to swear to a thing that's *worth* it," Voss said. "There's a difference, and you know it, or you'd never have strapped on that compass and come up here with a dead man."

The wind moved through the col above them with a sound like the whole escarpment exhaling. Zephyrine knelt in the snow beside Callum Voss, and the cold came up through her knees, and she thought of her mother's name dragged through the mud of a courtroom, of promises made to the dead that no one had ever kept.

"Bring it to Wren Voss," she said, "before the dry season's sixth tide. On my name. Such as it is."

"Your name's worth more than you think," Voss whispered. "That's the secret I was going to sell you at the top. The map's margins — the figures. They're not treasure weights, guide. They're *dates and signatures.*" His eyes were going dim now, but the urgency in them burned to the last. "Hale's own hand. What sank the Meridian wasn't the rocks. It was a man who needed her at the bottom of the sea. Find out why, and you'll find out what he did to—"

The cough took him whole. It went on and on, and Zephyrine held him against her shoulder, and the snow began to fall, fine and dry as ash, and by the time it stopped, Callum Voss had stopped with it.

She sat there a long while, the map in her hands, grease-stained and warm from its dead owner's coat. The weight of it was nothing — a few ounces of paper. The weight of it was everything — a daughter in Saltmere, a fleet under Corvane Hale, six tides, and a stranger's last breath spent on her name.

Far below, through a tear in the cloud, the sea showed grey and glittering, and on it, small as a fingernail, a sail.

Zephyrine folded the map, strapped Voss's pack beside her own, and turned her face toward the fjord.

* * *

The descent to Saltmere took her a day and a night, and she did not sleep in either of them. She buried Voss above the treeline under a cairn she built stone by stone with bleeding hands, and she marked the cairn with his own knife, and she spoke no words over it because there were none that seemed big enough.

The rigging yards at Saltmere stank of tar and hemp. She found the girl where the harbor-master said she'd be, halfway up a mast's worth of scaffolding, a wrench in her hand and her father's stubborn jaw set against the world.

"Wren Voss," Zephyrine called up. "I've come from your father. I've come from the Kestrel Escarpment. And I've brought you something he made me swear to deliver before the sixth tide."

The wrench stopped. The girl looked down, and her eyes were already hardening into something Zephyrine recognized — the look of someone who blamed guides for every absence, every empty chair, every promise a mountain had ever eaten.

"I don't want anything from a guide," Wren Voss said.

"You will," Zephyrine said, and held up the map, "when you see his handwriting."
