## 3. The Girl Who Hates Guides

The winch on Pier Nine had been screaming for three days, and Wren Voss had finally had enough of it.

She knelt inside the housing with her sleeves rolled past her elbows and her hands black to the wrist, listening the way other people prayed. A winch told you everything if you listened. This one was telling her the drum bearing had gone dry as a sermon, that someone — probably Oskar, probably because Oskar never read a maintenance slate in his life — had been hauling loads forty pounds over rating. She could hear the metal's complaint like a voice. *Fix me or bury me.* Machines were honest that way. Machines never left.

"Voss." Harl's boots stopped at the edge of the housing, blocking her light. "There's a woman asking for you."

"Tell her I'm elbow-deep in a crab pot."

"She says it's about your father."

Wren's wrench stopped mid-turn. The harbor noise went on around her — gulls, halyards, the wet slap of hulls against pilings — but it all seemed to arrive from very far away, through water, through years. Her father's name came to Greyhollow the way weather came: rarely, and usually bad.

She climbed out of the housing without wiping her hands.

The woman stood at the pier's landward end where the planking gave way to cobbles, and Wren knew her for a guide before she'd said a word. It was in how she stood — weight even, eyes moving over the rooftops and rigging the way other people checked exits. Grey coat, salt-faded, patched at the shoulder with thread that didn't match. A compass hung at her throat on a leather cord, old brass gone green at the seams, and she kept one thumb resting against it like a man keeps his hand on a lucky coin. Tall. Younger than Wren expected. Eyes the grey of the escarpment stone they said she'd been raised on.

Zephyrine Eriksen. Wren knew the name the way every soul in the coast towns knew it. The guide who'd walked a paying party into the White Scream pass and walked out alone. The name on the blacklist posted in every charterhouse from here to Saltmere. *Eriksen, Z. — revoked, do not engage.*

Her father had hired her anyway. Of course he had. Callum Voss had never met a rule he wouldn't spend.

"You're the daughter," the woman said. Not a question.

"I'm a mechanic," Wren said. "The daughter is a rumor my father spread so people would stop asking why he was never home."

Something moved behind the guide's eyes — not quite sympathy; Wren would have bitten sympathy off at the root. Something more like recognition. "My name is Zephyrine Eriksen."

"I know who you are. There's a poster of you in the harbormaster's office. They use it to scare apprentices." Wren crossed her arms, and only then noticed the grease still blackening her fingers, and hated herself for noticing. "Whatever he promised you, whatever he owes you, I can't pay it. He's been gone eleven months. He's always gone. You guides bleed men dry and call it a living, and when the mountain takes them you're all very sorry and there's nothing anyone can do."

The woman took that full in the face and did not flinch. That was the first thing Wren learned about her, standing in the fish-smell and the wind off the grey water: she absorbed blows like ballast. "He's dead," Zephyrine said. "On the Kestrel ridge, four days ago. His lungs. He couldn't get enough air to walk, let alone climb. I buried him under a cairn above the tree line, facing the fjord, because he asked me to face him toward the water."

The words landed one after another, flat stones dropped down a well, and Wren stood waiting for the splash that never came. Dead. The word was too small. Eleven months of silence she could have filled with a hundred ordinary explanations — a strike, a woman, a vein of gold too good to leave, another daughter in another port town, God knew the man collected obligations like other men collected debts. But *dead* closed every door at once.

"No," Wren said.

"I'm sorry—"

"No. You don't get to say that. You don't get to stand there with his blood practically on your boots—" Her voice cracked and she hated that worse than anything, hated the whole pier for hearing it. "Guides take the money and then they lose them. Every time. My mother used to say the escarpment eats one Voss a generation and the guides hold the door open. He swore this was the last contract. He *swore*."

"He did swear," Zephyrine said quietly. "I made him swear it twice. He laughed both times."

Wren turned away. She gripped the piling until the barnacles bit her palm, and looked out at the harbor because looking at anything else was impossible. Boats. Honest, stupid boats, going nowhere fast. Somewhere north of all that grey water was a ridge with a pile of rocks on it, and under the rocks was the man who'd taught her to solder by holding the iron steady while her small hands shook, who'd missed her sixteenth birthday and her mother's funeral and every winter solstice for nine years running, who had sent money wrapped in notes that always began *Wrennie, forgive me* and never once explained what she was forgiving.

"I need to show you something," the guide said behind her. "Not for me. For him."

* * *

They sat in the back of the Salt Cellar, the tavern where the dock crews drank and the owner knew better than to ask questions, and Zephyrine Eriksen laid a piece of oilcloth on the table between them and unwrapped it with the care of a surgeon.

It was a map. Hand-drawn, grease-stained, creased into soft ruin along fold lines worn nearly to lace. Wren knew the handwriting before she knew the geography — the cramped forward slant of it, the way he crossed his sevens, the little anchor doodled in the corner of every chart he'd ever touched because he thought it was funny. Her father's hand. She'd have known it blind, by smell. It smelled of him, faintly, of pipe smoke and machine oil, and Wren put her fingertips on the paper and could not make herself lift them away.

"The Meridian," Zephyrine said. "A salvage wreck sunk in the Meridian Fjord, up past the lawless water. He drew it himself. He said he'd found the location years ago and never had the means to reach it. He traded me this map for passage over the Kestrel. And he made me promise—" the guide paused, and Wren watched her gather the words like someone gathering stones, "—he made me swear I'd bring it to you before the sixth tide of the dry season. Before the salvage fleets reach it."

"Why?" Wren's voice came out strange. Small. "Why does it matter? It's a wreck. There are ten thousand wrecks."

"Not this one." Zephyrine's thumb went again to the compass at her throat, an unconscious motion, a tic worn smooth by repetition. "He wouldn't tell me what was aboard. Only that it mattered more than gold, and that no one else could be allowed to reach it first. He said—" and here the guide's voice changed, took on an odd careful weight, as though she were handling something fragile, "—he said, *tell my girl the ledger's worth more than the lode.*"

The tavern noise receded. Wren stared at the map, at the fjord inked in her father's sure strokes, at the little X that wasn't an X at all but a tiny drawn anchor, and felt the ground tilt under a truth she hadn't agreed to carry. Her father hadn't been chasing gold. Callum Voss, who'd spent thirty years chasing gold and finding mostly trouble, had died climbing toward something else entirely — something worth more than the lode — and he'd trusted its delivery not to a lawyer or a friend but to a disgraced stranger with a blacklisted name, because apparently even dying, the man believed guides got things done.

Or because he had no one else. The thought arrived cold and stayed.

"When's the sixth tide?" Wren asked.

"Nine days. Eight now, maybe, depending on the moon tables." Zephyrine hesitated. "There's more. The fleet that's racing us — the big one, out of Saltmere — it's chartered under Magistrate Corvane Hale."

The name went through Wren like a fishhook. Even she knew Hale; everyone on the coast knew Hale, the magistrate with the salvage monopoly and the smile like a knife being sheathed. But the guide across the table had gone white in a way that had nothing to do with grief for Callum Voss. Whatever history lived between Zephyrine Eriksen and Corvane Hale, it was older and sharper than this journey.

"So we're faster," Wren said.

"*We?*" Zephyrine leaned back. "No. No, absolutely not. I made a promise to deliver a map. I didn't sign on to drag a dock mechanic over the passes in the back half of the season. Do you know what the Kestrel does to people who don't know what they're doing?"

"Do you know what I do to people who tell me what I can't do?" Wren heard her own voice go low and flat, the voice she used on owners who tried to short her pay. "You want to hand me a map and a dead father and then walk away? Try it. I'll follow you. I'll follow you over the Kestrel, around the Kestrel, through the bottom of the sea if I have to. I've rebuilt engines older than you with a spoon and spite. The mountain doesn't scare me."

"It should."

"Everything should scare me. It doesn't. That's my father's fault too." She pulled the map toward her, and Zephyrine's hand shot out and covered hers — quick, hard, reflexive — and for a moment they were both just two women gripping the same greased paper across a scarred table, breathing at each other like rivals.

Then Zephyrine let go. Slowly. "Half the routes are washed out. The passes are coming apart early this year. And the man leading the fleet wants what's on that wreck badly enough to kill for it — I'd stake my name on that, and my name is the only thing I have left." She said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "If you come, you do exactly as I say on the mountain. Exactly. The moment you argue with me above the tree line, I turn us both around. Swear it."

"On my father's grave?"

"On your own life. It's the only currency the escarpment accepts."

Wren looked at her — really looked, past the blacklist and the reputation and the flint-grey eyes — and saw what she'd missed at the pier: exhaustion carved deep under the composure, and underneath that, something stubborn and grieving and familiar. This woman had carried her father four days up a mountain on her back. Had buried him facing the water because he'd asked. Hated her as she did, Wren could not hate her entirely, and that was its own kind of wound.

"Fine," Wren said. "I swear. Now swear me something back."

"What?"

"That you didn't kill him. That whatever happened on that ridge, you did everything a person could do." She held the guide's gaze and refused to blink. "Because if I find out otherwise, halfway up that mountain, there won't be a magistrate or a mountain high enough to save you."

For a long moment Zephyrine Eriksen said nothing. Then her hand rose to the brass compass at her throat and closed around it, knuckles whitening, and she said, "Sworn. On my mother's grave — and she's got less left than yours does."

Wren almost smiled. Almost. Outside, the tide was turning, the harbor sighing against the pilings, and somewhere north a fleet was already cutting black water under Corvane Hale's flag.

"I need an hour," Wren said, standing, wiping her hands on her thighs out of habit. "To close out my work. Tell my landlord I'm gone. And Eriksen—" she paused at the end of the table, map folded safe against her ribs like a second heartbeat, "—the next time someone tells me my father's dead, you'd better be the one carrying good news instead. Because I am done receiving letters from mountains."

Zephyrine rose too, shouldering her pack, and at the door she glanced back once — at the map, at Wren, at some calculation still unfinished behind her eyes.

"There's something else you should know before we sail," she said. "About why my mother's name is mud and why Hale's fleet sails under a survey seal. If your father's ledger is what I think it is, then the Meridian isn't carrying treasure."

"Then what's it carrying?"

"A confession," Zephyrine said, and pushed open the door onto the rain.
