## 4. Six Tides

The descent from the escarpment's shoulder was a study in letting go. Zephyrine had spent two days climbing, and now the mountain asked her to unspool all that hard-won height in a single afternoon, trading altitude for distance, the thin cold air for the thicker, wetter breath of the lowlands. Behind them the Kestrel Escarpment receded into cloud, its teeth still bared, as if it resented being escaped.

Wren Voss walked ahead of her on the switchbacks, and she walked like someone who had never once in her life asked a mountain for permission. She took the scree slopes at an angle that made Zephyrine's calves ache in sympathy, boots striking stone with a mechanic's blunt certainty — no delicacy, no reverence. Where Zephyrine read the rock, Wren simply refused it.

"You're drifting left," Zephyrine said. "The trail cuts right below the overhang."

"I see the trail."

"The trail sees you back. It's been waiting all season for someone to trust it too much."

Wren shot her a look over one shoulder — that flint-grey look, her father's look, though she didn't know it yet, didn't know how much of Callum lived in the set of her jaw when she was being stubborn about something small. "My father crossed this range eleven times," she said. "Alone, mostly. He said guides were for people afraid of their own feet."

"Your father," Zephyrine said quietly, "crossed it eleven times because he couldn't afford to cross it twelve."

That shut the girl up for a hundred yards of switchback. The wind came up the valley in long grey gusts, carrying the smell of salt and kelp and something colder underneath — the fjord announcing itself before they could see it, the way grief announces itself before you understand what has died.

They came down out of the last of the pine at dusk, onto the shelf of land above Saltmere, the fishing village that clung to the fjord's northern lip like a barnacle to a hull. Below them the water lay flat and pewter-dark, stitched with the lights of the village, and beyond the headland — Zephyrine stopped walking.

Beyond the headland, riding at anchor in the deep channel where the fjord opened toward open sea, sat ships.

Not fishing boats. Not coastal traders. Warships — or something dressed as them: black-hulled, low-slung, masts bare of canvas but heavy with rigging, lanterns burning at their rails in disciplined rows. Three of them, maybe four, their silhouettes stacked against the last bruised light of the sky. And at the foremast of the largest flew a pennant Zephyrine knew the way you know a scar — not by looking at it often, but by how it aches when the weather turns.

The silver kestrel of Magistrate Corvane Hale.

Her hand went to the compass at her throat without her deciding anything. Her mother's compass, brass worn to gold at the edges, the needle trembling in its housing like a small captive bird. It had hung there through every contract since the blacklist, through every job too dangerous or too shameful for anyone else to take, and it had never once trembled like this.

"What is it?" Wren had come up beside her. Then she saw it too, and Zephyrine watched the color leave the girl's face by degrees, watched her hands curl around the strap of her tool-bag the way a drowning woman curls around driftwood. "That's — those are salvage ships. That's a *fleet*."

"It's Hale's fleet." The name came out of Zephyrine flat and cold, a stone dropped down a well.

"You know him?"

"I know *of* him." She kept her eyes on the ships. "Everyone in Greyhollow knows of him. He doesn't need to be known closer. Being known closer is how careers end."

She started down toward the village, and after a moment Wren followed, both of them moving faster now, the descent gone urgent beneath their feet.

* * *

Saltmere smelled of fish oil and woodsmoke and the particular sourness of a town that had learned to keep its shutters closed. The inn — the only one, the Drowned Kestrel, which Zephyrine found either providence or mockery — was half-empty and full of listening. She felt the listening the moment she crossed the threshold: the way conversation thinned to a thread and then resumed too loudly, the way eyes slid toward the window and the ships beyond it and then away again.

The innkeeper was a broad woman with forearms like mooring line and a face that gave away nothing except exhaustion. She poured their tea without being asked and said, "You'll want the room at the back. Walls are thick."

"We want information," Wren said.

"And I want my husband back from the sea, and neither of us is getting what we want tonight." But the woman leaned on the bar, lowered her voice, and gave them the shape of it anyway, in the clipped cadence of someone reporting weather. "Fleet sailed from Corvane three days ago. Four ships. Magistrate's warrant says survey and recovery of derelict property in the Meridian Fjord. Everyone knows what 'derelict property' means when Hale signs it. It means everything, and it means nothing gets shared."

"The Meridian," Zephyrine said. "The wreck in the fjord. When did word of it reach Corvane?"

"Word reached everyone. A trawler brought up a piece of her rail two months back, gilt-edged, and by the time it got to market half the coast knew there was something worth diving for." The innkeeper shrugged. "Hale moves fast when there's salvage. Faster than the law requires. Faster than the law allows, if anyone were fool enough to say so."

"When does he dive?" Wren's voice had gone very quiet, which Zephyrine was learning meant the opposite of calm.

"Tide tables say slack water comes six times before the dry season locks the channel. His divers won't work rough water — his men are harbor divers, soft ones, they need it glassy. First good tide is day after tomorrow." The innkeeper looked at them both, and something moved behind her tiredness, some old sympathy or older resentment. "You're racing him. I can see it. Don't race him here. People who race Corvane Hale in his own waters wash up on the flats with their pockets turned out, and the magistrate writes it down as misadventure."

Zephyrine carried the tea to a corner table and sat with her back to the wall, where she could watch the door and the window both. Wren dropped across from her, and for a long moment neither spoke. The fire cracked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard groaned under someone's restless weight.

"Six tides," Wren said finally. "He dives on the second. We have until the sixth."

"If we're lucky. If the weather holds. If his divers are as soft as she says." Zephyrine turned the cup slowly on the table, watching the tea shiver. "Luck isn't a plan."

"Then what's the plan?"

"The plan is the thing your father drew on that map." She nodded toward Wren's coat, where the grease-stained paper rode against her ribs. "Voss marked approaches to the wreck that aren't on any chart. Underwater ledges, a back channel through the reef — he spent years finding ways in that nobody else knows. That's our margin. Not speed. Knowledge."

"And if his knowledge is wrong? He was dying when he drew it. Maybe his memory was dying too."

"Then we drown," Zephyrine said simply, "and it won't matter what Hale finds."

Wren stared at her. "You say things like that like they don't cost anything."

"They cost everything. That's why I say them plainly." Zephyrine set the cup down. "Listen to me, Wren. I've guided people over mountains for eleven years, and the ones who die are never the ones who understand the danger. They're the ones who dress it up. Who call the avalanche 'a chance of snow.' Your father understood that. It's why he hired me — a disgraced guide costs less and lies less. We have nothing left to protect, so we have nothing left to hide behind."

Something flickered across the girl's face — not agreement, not yet, but the loosening of resistance, the first hairline crack in a wall built to keep guides out.

* * *

She went up to the back room after midnight and stood at the narrow window with the compass open in her palm. The needle swung, steadied, pointed north-northwest — toward the fjord, toward the fleet, toward the man whose signature sat at the bottom of a forged survey fourteen years ago.

She had been nine years old when her mother came home from that expedition. Zephyrine remembered the door opening, remembered the silence that followed it, the particular silence of a woman trying to decide which truth to tell a child. Her mother, Maren Eriksen, had been the finest surveyor on the coast — the woman whose charts every guide in Greyhollow carried, whose name was spoken like a blessing before hard crossings. And then the Kestrel route survey came in, and Corvane Hale — young, ambitious, already wearing authority like a borrowed coat — certified a competing chart that contradicted hers on every critical bearing. A pass marked safe that wasn't. A party went in on Hale's chart. Two guides died. And when the inquiry came, it was Maren's figures that were called into question, Maren's instruments that were impounded, Maren's name that was struck from the rolls while Hale rose on the wreckage of hers.

Her mother never guided again. She drank instead, quietly, methodically, the way she'd once plotted bearings, and she died four years ago with the compass in her hand and the injustice unfinished, and Zephyrine had inherited both — the brass and the grudge — and worn them together ever since.

Now the same man was sailing toward the same waters, chasing the same wreck where her father — where *Wren's* father — had hidden proof of what Hale was.

*Proof.* Voss hadn't been after gold. Zephyrine had known it since the ridge, since the map's margins, since the dying man's grip on her wrist. He'd been after a ledger, and a ledger meant names, and names meant the kind of ruin that couldn't be bought back.

She closed the compass. Outside, the fleet's lanterns burned in their patient rows, and between her and them lay sixty miles of coastline, a storm building somewhere over the open sea, and five tides' worth of time that was already draining away like water through a cracked hull.

Cold dread had settled in her chest on the headland, and she waited for it to paralyze her the way fear had paralyzed other clients, other moments of her life. It didn't. It did something else instead. It hardened, the way wet sand hardens, the way resolve hardens — layer on layer, until it was something she could stand on.

Six tides. Hale had ships, divers, warrants, and the law bent around his finger like wire. She had a stolen map, a hostile mechanic's daughter, a blacklisted name, and her mother's compass pointing true.

It would have to be enough.

A knock at the door, soft and double-rapped — Wren's knock, she was already learning its rhythm. Zephyrine opened it. The girl stood in the corridor with the lamp behind her and her father's map already unrolled in her hands, her eyes bright with something that had no business being hope.

"There's a boatman downstairs," Wren said. "And he says he knows a way into the fjord that even Hale's charts don't show."
