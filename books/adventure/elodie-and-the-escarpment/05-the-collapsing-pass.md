## 5. The Collapsing Pass

The defile had a name on no map Wren had ever seen, but her father would have known it. He'd have called it something plain and honest — the Throat, maybe, or the Gullet — because that was what it was: a crack in the escarpment's shoulder where the mountain leaned in from both sides until the sky narrowed to a ribbon of bruised grey. Water ran somewhere below them, unseen, sounding like breath drawn through teeth.

Wren walked behind Zephyrine and hated how much she was learning to read the woman's back. The set of her shoulders when the footing turned bad. The half-second pause before each step, testing stone the way other people tested ice. Three days of this, and Wren could tell you without looking whether the ground ahead was rotten just by watching where Zephyrine put her weight.

She hated that too. Hated that she was learning anything at all from a guide.

"Loose scree past this point," Zephyrine said, not turning around. Her voice carried flat along the walls of the defile, stripped of echo. "Stay in my line. Don't pick your own path."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking about it."

Wren bit down on the retort. It was true, which made it worse. She'd been eyeing a shelf of stone to the left that looked faster, drier, better than the wet chute Zephyrine was angling toward. Everything about the woman's route looked slower than necessary, more careful than the situation demanded. That was guides for you. Her father used to say it with a laugh and a shake of his head — *guides get paid by the hour, love, not by the mile* — and then he'd go off over some pass with one anyway, gone whole seasons, coming home thinner and quieter and smelling of rope tar and someone else's campfire smoke.

Guides took him away. Guides kept him away. And now one of them had brought her father back to her in pieces: a map folded into eighths, a compass worn to brass at the edges, and a promise Wren hadn't asked for and couldn't put down.

The chute was worse than it looked. Of course it was. Scree to the knee, then a slab of tilted granite slick with seep-water, then more scree, all of it funneling downward into the dark where the hidden water talked to itself. Wren's boots found no purchase she trusted. She kept her eyes on Zephyrine's heels and counted steps the way she counted threads on a seized bolt when she was working — one, two, three — because counting kept the fear in a small room where it belonged.

She was on step forty-something when the mountain coughed.

It started as a sound she felt rather than heard, a deep grumble underfoot like a millstone turning, and then the slope above them let go. Not all of it. Just enough. A tongue of grey rock and old snow-melt slurry came hissing down the far wall of the defile, and Wren watched it with the detached clarity of shock — watched it cross the chute they'd been climbing not thirty seconds earlier, watched it slam against the near wall and pile up, watched the whole throat of the pass rearrange itself around them with a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

Then the dust hit, and there was nothing but white.

* * *

When it settled, the world had changed shape.

The way ahead was buried. The way behind was buried. They stood on a shelf of surviving stone barely wide enough for the two of them and their packs, wedged between the defile's walls like a coin in a slot. Below, where the chute had been, was a fresh scarred fan of rubble running down thirty feet to the rubble-choked floor. Above, the broken slope still ticked and whispered, shedding little promises of more to come.

Wren's legs told her to sit. She sat. It wasn't a decision; her knees simply went, and suddenly she was down with her back against warm rock and her hands flat on stone that seemed, even now, to be trembling faintly, though she knew it wasn't. That was her. Trembling.

"Don't move," Zephyrine said.

"I'm not—" Wren's voice came out thin. She tried again. "I'm not moving."

Zephyrine had braced herself against the opposite wall, one boot on a nub of rock, reading the slide the way Wren read engine rooms: top to bottom, system by system. Dust still ghosted off her shoulders. A cut above her eyebrow bled in a slow thread she hadn't noticed yet.

"The shelf holds," she said finally. "This section didn't move because it's keyed into the wall. But we can't stay. There'll be a second release — there always is, after a first. The slope's been loading all spring." She turned, and her eyes found Wren's with an exactness that made Wren want to look away. "We go down. Now, while it's quiet."

Wren looked down. Thirty feet of loose rock fanned out beneath her, ending in darkness and the sound of water. Her hands were shaking so hard she could see it, and she thought, absurdly, of her workbench at the dockyard, of the day a steam line burst two feet from her face and she hadn't flinched, hadn't even blinked, because machines were honest. Machines failed for reasons you could learn. Mountains just *decided*.

"I can't," she said.

"You can."

"I can't." It tore out of her louder than she meant, bouncing off both walls, and she flinched at her own voice as if it might bring the rest of the slope down. "I don't— I'm not built for this. I fix things. Bolts and bearings and boilers. Things that hold still while you work on them. This—" She gestured at everything, the whole vertical world of it. "This doesn't hold still."

Zephyrine crouched at the edge of the shelf, unhurried, and began uncoiling a length of rope from her pack. "My mother said almost exactly that once," she said. "Word for word, nearly. *Things that hold still.*"

Wren stared at her. In three days Zephyrine had given her weather reports, route instructions, and corrections of her knots, and not one single fact about herself. Not her mother, certainly. Everyone in Greyhollow knew the name Eriksen the way you knew the name of a shipwreck — *the guide who falsified the survey, the one whose charts killed the Meridian's shore party* — except that everyone was wrong, supposedly, if you believed the daughter, and nobody did.

"She was a guide," Zephyrine said. She fed the rope through a flake of rock, testing it, her hands moving with a calm that Wren recognized now as practiced, not natural. "A better one than I'll ever be. And the year I was fourteen, she took a contract through country like this with a party of six, using a survey she didn't draw. The survey was wrong. The pass collapsed. Two men died." The rope hissed through her grip. "Her name went on the report. Not the surveyor's. Hers. Because the surveyor was Corvane Hale, and Hale was already a magistrate's son with a magistrate's friends, and my mother was a guide with a dead client list and no one to speak for her."

The name landed in Wren's chest like a dropped wrench. *Hale.*

"Hale," she repeated. "The magistrate sailing for the Meridian."

"The same." Zephyrine didn't look up from the rope. "He forged a depth survey twenty years ago to bury a salvage claim, and he forged mine to bury my mother. Same hand. Same lazy arrogance. My mother spent the rest of her life blacklisted, guiding illegal routes for smugglers' money, trying to buy back the proof that would clear her." At last she raised her head, and there was something raw under the calm, something Wren suspected very few people were ever shown. "She died before she found it. So when your father told me what was in the Meridian's strongroom, I didn't take the contract for the pay, Wren. I'd have done it free."

The confession hung in the cold air between them. Wren found that her hands had stopped shaking — not entirely, but enough. It was a strange medicine, another person's grief. It made hers feel less like a wound and more like a load-bearing wall: something that hurt, but held something up.

"He never told me any of it," she said quietly. "The ledger. Hale. Any of it. He just kept leaving." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "I spent six years hating him for walking out. Turns out he was walking *toward* something."

"Toward you, eventually. That map was addressed to you before it was ever a map to me." Zephyrine rose and anchored the rope, then held out the free end. "So. Down. Thirty feet. You've rebuilt engines blind in the dark of a hull, I'd wager. This is the same work. You test every piece before you trust it, you keep three points of contact, and you never, ever rush a fitting."

"That's not the same at all."

"It's exactly the same. The only difference is the mountain doesn't care if you're afraid, and neither does the work." Zephyrine's voice gentled by a degree, so slight Wren might have imagined it. "Fear's just information. Read it. Don't obey it."

Wren took the rope. The hemp was cold and damp and real, and holding it gave her hands a job, which helped more than any words could have. She moved to the edge of the shelf and looked down into the dark, and this time she made herself look like a mechanic looks at a job: breaking it into parts. Footing here. Handhold there. Traverse left. Drop. Again.

"Talk me through it," she said. "All the way down. Don't stop talking."

"I can do that."

"And if I freeze again—"

"I'll be right above you, talking anyway." A pause. "My mother used to sing. Terribly. If it comes to that, I will sing, and you will descend out of pure self-defense."

Despite everything — the dust in her throat, the dark below, the ticking slope above — Wren laughed. It startled her, the sound of it, bouncing small and alive off the stone.

She went over the edge.

* * *

Thirty feet took an hour. Zephyrine talked the whole way — foot placements, weight shifts, the location of every sound-looking hold — and twice, when Wren's grip locked and her breath went shallow and the whole grey world narrowed to a pulse in her ears, Zephyrine's voice came down steady as a lamp: *left hand, waist height, that flake, yes, now breathe.* Once, true to her word, she sang four bars of something old and mournful and spectacularly off-key, and Wren descended the next ten feet out of pure spite, and they both pretended not to notice that it worked.

When her boots finally met the defile floor, solid and level and blessedly horizontal, Wren stood there a moment with her palms pressed flat against the living rock, feeling it hold her, feeling it simply *be* stone, indifferent and immovable and, for this moment, kind.

Above, Zephyrine rappelled down the last stretch and landed light as ash.

They looked at each other across three days of flint and silence, and something had shifted in the geometry between them — Wren could feel it sitting there, fragile as new-welded seam, untested but real.

"Your mother," Wren said. "What was her name?"

"Solveig." Zephyrine said it carefully, like setting down something breakable. "Solveig Eriksen."

"I'll remember it."

Something flickered across the guide's face — surprise, then something warmer that she banked quickly, the way you cover a flame against wind.

They started down the defile toward the river country, and it was Wren, scanning the valley mouth below, who saw it first: smoke. Not one column but many, rising off the fjord's far shore, thin and orderly and wrong.

"Camps," she said. "That many fires isn't trappers."

Zephyrine shaded her eyes, and Wren watched the blood leave her face.

"No," she said softly. "That's a fleet making landfall. We're out of time."
