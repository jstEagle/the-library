## 6. Smugglers' Toll

The river came out of the mountains like something fleeing them — grey-green, fast, loud with meltwater and old grudges. We heard it a full hour before we saw it, a low grinding roar beneath the pines, and when the trees finally broke open around us, there it was: the Serpent's Throat, the only waterway that cut through the escarpment's southern wall to the fjord below.

And on its bank, as I'd known they would be, the Tollmen.

There were nine of them camped among the boulders where the current slowed enough for boats. Their skiffs were lashed to iron rings driven into the rock generations ago, their tarps strung between alder trunks, and their cookfire sent up a column of greasy smoke that could be read from a mile off by anyone who knew what to look for. *Here. Passage. For a price.*

I knew what to look for. I'd been reading this country since before I could write my own name.

"Stay behind me," I said to Wren, low, without turning my head. "Let me do the talking."

She made a sound in her throat that wasn't agreement but wasn't refusal either. It was the sound she'd been making for three days now, ever since the pass, ever since I'd talked her down that crumbling face of stone with her hands shaking so badly I'd had to count her breaths for her. Something between us had cracked open there, and neither of us had decided yet whether to let light in or keep it boarded shut.

The man who rose to meet us was called Brannock, if the stories held true, and the stories usually did with men who wanted you to know their names. He was big the way river stones are big — worn smooth, dense, immovable — with a beard gone to rust at the edges and a hatchet riding his belt like an afterthought. His eyes went over me the way a butcher reads a carcass.

"Zephyrine Ravensworth." He said it slowly, tasting it. "They said you were done. Blacklisted."

"They say a lot of things in Greyhollow."

"They do." He smiled without warmth. "And yet here you are, walking out of the high country with a stranger and no pack worth robbing. Which means either you're desperate or you're clever, and in my experience the two wear the same boots."

Behind him, his people had drifted closer — not obviously, not in a way that would be rude, but close enough. A woman with a crossbow leaned against an alder. Two younger men stood up from the fire. The arithmetic of it was plain: nine against two, and the river on our side of the ledger.

"We want passage down the Throat," I said. "Two seats to the fjord mouth. We pay in coin."

"Coin's heavy to carry and easy to spend," Brannock said. "What else have you?"

"Nothing else."

His eyes flicked past my shoulder, to Wren, to the oilskin roll strapped across her back where I'd transferred the map two nights ago — because I'd begun to suspect that anything I carried would be taken from me first, and I wanted one more layer between Voss's promise and the world. Brannock saw the roll. Of course he saw it. He'd probably seen it before we'd cleared the treeline.

"That," he said pleasantly, "looks like something else."

* * *

We sat at their fire because refusing would have been worse than sitting. They gave us fish stew that tasted of pine smoke and iron, and Wren ate hers like a woman who'd grown up hungry and never forgotten how, while I turned the situation over the way you turn a stone to see what's living underneath.

Brannock laid it out plainly, because men like him respect plainness. "The map comes with us. You ride free. That's the toll."

"The map isn't mine to give."

"Then whose is it?" His gaze slid to Wren. "Hers? She looks like a dock rat, not a cartographer."

"It belongs to a dead man," I said, "and to his daughter, and it's going where he meant it to go. That's the whole of it."

"A dead man's wishes." Brannock laughed, and the laugh had gravel in it. "I've buried a hundred dead men's wishes in this river. Here's what I know about maps, guide. Every map on this escarpment is a lie someone drew well enough to sell. So here's my offer, since I'm feeling generous. Show me what's so precious. If it's worthless, you pay coin after all. If it's worth something—" he spread his hands, "—then we're all richer for knowing."

He wanted to see it. And once he saw it, he'd want to keep it, and once he wanted to keep it, nine armed people on a riverbank would make wanting into having.

Unless what he saw wasn't worth keeping.

I looked at the fire. I looked at the river, running silver and violent toward the sea. And I thought about my mother, who taught me to read contour lines before I could read letters, who told me once — drunk on plum brandy, laughing, the last good year before Hale's forged survey gutted her name — *"A map is just a story with north at the top, Zeph. And any fool can tell a story."*

I'd spent six years telling myself I wasn't the kind of person who lied. Blacklisted guides can't afford the luxury of scruples, but I'd kept a few anyway, kept them like river stones in my pockets, small and hard and proof of something.

I reached into my coat and drew out the grease-stained paper I'd worked on by firelight until the third watch of the night before — Voss's coastlines traced from memory onto scavenged chart-paper, his marginal notes copied in a hand I'd practiced until it ached, the ink aged with wet ash and dried tea. A forgery. A good one. Good enough to die for, if it came to that, which was rather the point.

"This," I said, unrolling it across our knees, "is what's precious."

* * *

Brannock read it the way his kind read everything — fast, greedy, missing the fine print. His finger traced the fjord, the marked shoal, the little cross where the Meridian slept under thirty feet of cold green water. His breath changed. I watched it change, and I hated myself a little, and I kept my face still as driftwood.

"Well," he said softly. "Well, well."

"Toll accepted, then?"

"Toll accepted." He took the paper with both hands, careful as a priest handling relics, and I felt something twist in my chest watching him hold it — because even false, it was Voss's handwriting, Voss's dying wish given shape, and handing it over felt like handing over a piece of the dead man himself. "You two ride in the second boat. Kess rows. Touch nothing, tell no one what you've seen, and when we put ashore at the fjord mouth, we part ways and none of this ever happened."

"None of this ever happened," I agreed.

Wren said nothing at all. I noticed that. I noticed everything about her silence in those minutes — the way her eyes stayed on the fire, the way her hands rested loose and empty on her knees, the way she breathed slow and even, like a woman counting something. I'd counted her breaths on the cliff face. I knew what her calm looked like when it was real, and this wasn't it.

This was the other thing. This was the thing I'd seen once before, years ago, in a magistrate's clerk who smiled too easily and signed papers that ruined my mother.

I didn't know yet what she'd done. But I knew the shape of it.

* * *

The boats went down the Throat in the gold hour before dusk, when the light came sideways through the gorge and set the water burning. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful — the walls rising sheer and russet on either side, the spray hanging in the air like suspended glass, the river roaring through channels barely wider than the skiffs themselves. Kess rowed with the economy of long practice, and I sat in the stern with my heart hammering against the lie folded into my coat pocket — because I hadn't given Brannock the original. I'd given him the forgery, yes, but I'd kept a second copy, cruder, hidden in my boot, because a guide who surrenders her only navigation is a guide asking to drown.

And then I looked at Wren, sitting across from me with the wind pulling strands of hair across her face, and I saw the oilskin roll.

Still strapped across her back. Still bulging with the true map, the real one, the one Brannock believed he owned.

My stomach dropped through the bottom of the boat.

"When," I said, barely above the river's voice.

"Last night. While you were copying." Her eyes didn't leave the water ahead. "You talk in your sleep. Well — you mutter. Contour numbers. I swapped the rolls while you were working and put the forgery where yours had been. Then tonight, when you showed him yours—" she shrugged, one shoulder, infuriatingly casual, "—you showed him the copy of the copy. The original never left my back."

"You let me sit at that fire and bargain with a fake while you already had the real one hidden."

"You bargained better when you thought you were losing something." Finally she looked at me, and there was no apology in her face, only a fierce, bright, terrible pride. "That's what Da used to say about salvage. Never bid on a wreck you can afford to lose."

I stared at her. The gorge walls slid past, orange and black, and the spray struck my face, and somewhere behind us Brannock was unrolling his prize at the front of the lead boat, gloating over lines I'd drawn by candlelight, believing he'd taken the dearest thing we carried.

I should have been angry. Some part of me was — the part that had spent six years clinging to a clean name in a dirty trade, the part that believed there was still a difference between surviving and stealing. But another part, a part I didn't have a name for yet, felt something lift in me like a bird off cold water. Because she'd done it cleanly. No blood, no blades, no one hurt. Just patience and nerve and a thief's timing, learned in three days from a woman she claimed to despise.

Justice outside the law. Learned fast.

"He'll find out," I said. "Sooner or later he'll look close and see the paper's wrong, or the ink will run, or he'll meet someone who knows Voss's hand. Men like Brannock don't forgive being played."

"I know."

"And when he finds out, he'll come looking. Downriver, along the coast, wherever the trail leads. Nine boats' worth of looking."

"I know that too." Wren pulled the oilskin roll from her back and held it in both hands, and the last light caught the grease stains on the wrapping, Voss's fingerprints ghosted into the fabric. "But he'll be looking for two women running scared. So maybe we shouldn't run scared." Her jaw set, and I saw her father in it suddenly, plain as a portrait — the same stubborn architecture, the same refusal to be small. "Maybe we should get there first."

Ahead, the gorge opened its throat and spat us toward the sea. Far out on the darkening water, past the headland, I could just make out lights — more lights than a fishing fleet has any business carrying, strung along anchored hulls like a carnival come to town.

Tide four was nearly spent. And Magistrate Corvane Hale's fleet was already home.
