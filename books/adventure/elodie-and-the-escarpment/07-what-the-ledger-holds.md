## 7. What the Ledger Holds

The watchtower stood on the shoulder of the headland like a tooth left in an old jaw — grey stone, windowless on its seaward face, its iron door long since gone to rust and theft. Wren had passed it a hundred times as a girl, when her father still took her out in the skiff and pointed at the coast and told her stories that were half true. She had never once thought to go inside.

Now it was the only roof between them and the wind coming off the water like a blade being drawn.

"Mind the stairs," Zephyrine said, and went up first anyway, which Wren was learning was the guide's way of saying *I don't trust these steps with your weight until I've tested them with mine.*

Wren followed, one hand on the cold spiral of stone, the other pressed flat against the wall where the mortar had crumbled to grit. The stairwell smelled of gull droppings and old salt and something green — moss or rot or both — and by the third turn her lungs were burning from the climb. She hated that. She hated that the guide climbed it without breathing hard, that her boots made no sound on the stone while Wren's scraped and rang. She hated most of all that she was glad, absurdly, stupidly glad, to be under a roof at all.

The top room was round and open to the sky through a broken lantern housing, half of it collapsed inward so that a wedge of evening light fell across the floor like something spilled. A hearth in the far wall, black with decades of dead fires. A wooden table bolted down by some long-forgotten keeper, scarred and warped but standing. Shelves with nothing on them but a gull's nest and two eggs gone chalky with age.

Zephyrine set her pack down, shrugged off her oilskin, and stood a moment at the broken seaward gap, looking out. Below them the land fell away in terraces of gorse and scree to a grey ribbon of shore, and beyond that the fjord opened — wide, flat, the color of hammered pewter, running away north into haze.

Wren didn't look. She couldn't yet. Every time she looked at that water she saw the fleet's sails on the horizon this morning, small as paper cutouts, and her chest did something complicated and painful.

"He knew this tower," she said instead. It came out before she'd decided to say it. "My father. He used to talk about the keepers here. Said they could see a ship's flag three leagues out."

"They could." Zephyrine turned. In the slanted light her face was all planes and shadow, and there was ash-grey dust on her sleeves from the pass they'd crawled out of that morning. "Sit. Eat something. Then we read the map properly."

"I've read it."

"You've looked at it. There's a difference." She pulled the folded square of grease-stained paper from the inner pocket of her shirt — she'd been carrying it against her skin since the ridge, Wren had noticed, the way you carry something living — and laid it flat on the bolted table, weighting the corners with four stones from the windowsill. "Your father drew this for someone who already knew the fjord. Look at how much he *didn't* draw. No soundings. No anchorages. Just the approach channel and the wreck's position. That's not a treasure map, Wren. Treasure maps are for strangers. This is a letter."

Wren sat. Her knees ached; everything ached; the rockslide seemed to live in her body still, the memory of the whole cliffside coming down around them, of Zephyrine's voice cutting through the roar — *left foot, no, your other left, girl, now* — steady as a bell in a storm. She pulled her father's coat tighter around her shoulders. It still smelled of him, tar and pipe smoke and the particular machine-oil sweetness of his workbench, though every day the smell was fainter, and every day she caught herself breathing it in like a thief.

Zephyrine struck a light from her kit — flint and steel, no matches, because matches were for people who trusted the weather — and lit a stub of candle she'd wedged into a bottle. Warmth gathered. Shadows moved along the curved walls like slow animals.

"Now," Zephyrine said, and bent over the map. "Watch the margins."

* * *

It was true what people said about guides: they read country the way clerks read ledgers. But Wren hadn't understood until tonight that they read *paper* the same way. Zephyrine tilted the map against the candlelight, turned it, held it nearly flat to her eye, and the grease stains that had meant nothing — smears, blots, the careless marks of a man eating sausage over his own handiwork — began to resolve.

"There." Her finger stopped on a stain near the map's eastern edge, below the little inked hulk of the *Meridian*, below even the depth-marks in Voss's cramped hand. "That's not grease. Look at the edge of it. Ink, washed with oil so it would only show at a raking angle. He wrote it backwards, too — mirror script. Sailors' trick, for anything you didn't want read over your shoulder."

"How do you know it's not just—"

"Because I've seen it before." Something crossed Zephyrine's face, quick as a fish under ice. "My mother used it. For her survey notes, near the end, when she started to think people were reading her mail." She said it flatly, the way you set down something heavy so your hands are free. Then she took a scrap of charcoal from her kit, turned the map toward herself, and began to write on the back of a strip of sailcloth, transcribing each mirrored character as it came.

Wren watched the letters appear. Watched them become words.

*Not gold. Not cargo. Captain's strongbox, below the waterline, aft hold, third frame from the sternpost. Inside: ledger, oilskin-wrapped, waxed. Hale's own hand. Survey of the Kestrel approaches, dated two years before the wreck. Compare it to the survey he filed. The difference is the whole crime.*

She read it twice. Three times. The candle flame swayed and the words stayed the same.

"Hale," she said. "Corvane Hale. The magistrate."

"The magistrate," Zephyrine said quietly, "who is sailing right now for that wreck with a fleet behind him."

"But why would my father—" Wren stopped. Started again. "He was a prospector. He dug channels and surveyed shoals for salvage rights. He wasn't — he fished, he fixed engines, he was never—" She heard her own voice climbing and couldn't stop it. "He was never a man who went after magistrates."

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "He was a man who went after truth, apparently, and dressed it up as prospecting so nobody would ask questions." She tapped the margin note. "This is dated. Two years before the *Meridian* went down. Which means your father found out about the forged survey years ago, and spent years getting himself into a position to reach the proof — and then got sick, and ran out of time, and hired the first disgraced guide desperate enough to take a dying man over the escarpment."

The words landed one after another like stones dropped down a well. Wren sat with her hands flat on the table, on either side of the map, as if the paper might move.

"The forged survey," she said slowly. "That's yours. Your mother's."

"My mother surveyed the Kestrel approaches eleven years ago. Official chart, commissioned work, the best of her career." Zephyrine's voice was very even, which Wren was beginning to understand was how she sounded when things were worst. "Hale filed a competing survey. Different soundings, different channel marks. When the discrepancy was investigated, evidence surfaced — manufactured evidence — that my mother had falsified her figures. Her license was revoked. Her charts were withdrawn. She died four years later with the word *forger* carved on her name, and I have spent every year since guiding under a name nobody trusts, because hers wasn't worth anything anymore." She looked up, and the candlelight caught her eyes and made them bright and hard. "Except it turns out the forgery was Hale's. And your father knew. And he hid the proof of it aboard a wreck at the bottom of a lawless fjord rather than bring it to any court Hale controlled."

The tower creaked around them. Wind worried at the broken lantern housing with small persistent fingers. Somewhere below, a gull cried once and was answered.

Wren thought about her father. Not the myth of him — not the absent man who was always away, always chasing some lead, always promising next season, next haul, next time — but the specific man. The man who checked her knots twice. Who kept his tools wrapped in oiled cloth and sharpened them on Sundays whether they needed it or not. Who had taught her that if a thing was worth doing it was worth doing *properly*, and who had said it with such ferocity that young Wren had rolled her eyes and thought, *all right, Da, it's a bracket, not a cathedral.*

He hadn't been chasing fortune. He'd been doing a thing properly. All those absences, all those seasons — he'd been carrying a secret the size of a magistrate's career, alone, because he couldn't trust anyone with it, and he'd hidden the proof in the one place he was certain no honest person and no dishonest one would ever think to look, and he'd gotten sick, and he'd died on a ridge with a stranger holding his hand.

Her eyes were burning. She let them. She was done, she decided, done with hiding her face over grief the way he'd hidden ink under grease.

"He named me Wren," she said, when she could speak. "After the bird. He said wrens sing loudest in winter." A wet, cracked laugh escaped her. "I always thought it was just — a small thing. A plain thing. He was a plain man."

"He was a careful man," Zephyrine said. "There's nothing plain about careful. Careful is what keeps you alive long enough to matter." She rolled the sailcloth transcription and held it out. "Here. It's your father's hand, even mirrored. It should be in your pocket, not mine."

Wren took it. The charcoal letters were already smudging at the edges; she folded the cloth with the care she'd use on a live charge, and put it inside her father's coat, over her heart.

"So we're not racing the fleet for salvage," she said. "We're racing them for evidence."

"We're racing them for a ledger that can pull a magistrate down and raise two dead people's names out of the mud." Zephyrine blew out the candle — daylight was failing anyway, and flame showed through cracks like a beacon. In the sudden blue dusk her voice came low and level. "Understand what that means, Wren. Hale isn't sailing to loot a wreck. If he suspected the ledger survived, he's sailing to *burn* it. And everyone who's seen it. We won't be thieves to him. We'll be witnesses. Witnesses get handled differently than thieves."

"Handled."

"Drowned, usually. But yes."

Wren looked past her, through the broken housing, at the fjord going dark. Somewhere out there, under forty feet of cold green water, her father's last proper piece of work waited in a captain's strongbox, wrapped in oilskin, patient as a seed. Somewhere closer, sails were furled and anchors ready and men who answered to Corvane Hale were counting tides.

Four were spent. She'd counted them herself from the clifftop, the way her father had taught her — the great breath of the sea, in and out, in and out.

Two left.

"Then we'd better be better thieves than they are witnesses," Wren said, and heard, to her surprise, that her voice had stopped shaking. "Tell me the plan. And don't leave out the part where it's impossible. I want to know exactly how impossible."

For the first time since the ridge — since the dying man, since the promise, since the whole cursed journey began — Zephyrine Ravensworth smiled. It was a thin thing, sharp at the corners, but it reached her eyes.

"Impossible," she said, "is just a sounding nobody's taken yet. Get your kit. We dive at slack water tomorrow, and the sixth tide won't wait."
