## 8. The Fleet at Anchor

The cliff above Meridian Fjord had a name the old charts no longer bothered to print — Skald's Tooth, her mother had called it, back when Zephyrine was small enough to ride in a pack-frame and the world was nothing but granite and weather and a woman's voice reading the wind like scripture. *You can tell what a storm intends by how it combs the water,* Maren Ravensworth used to say, one gloved hand shading her eyes, the other holding that brass compass flat as an altar. *Wind lies. Water only tells you what it's already decided.*

Zephyrine lay on her belly at the Tooth's crown now, twenty years older and twice as heavy with knowing, and watched the water decide.

Below, the fjord opened like a grey throat. Four tides had come and gone since Greyhollow — four slow breaths of the sea, each one stealing an hour she could not buy back — and on the fifth tide's ebb, the salvage fleet had finally shown itself. Three hulls rounding the headland under working sail, riding low and arrogant, their black paint fresh enough to gleam even under a bruised sky. The lead ship flew no colors worth respecting: just the magistrate's seal stitched into a pennant, a coiled serpent over crossed surveyor's chains, snapping in the wind like something alive and hungry.

*The Corvane's Pride.* She knew the name from harbor gossip all down the coast, from dockhands who spat when they said it. Hale had named his flagship after himself. Of course he had. Men who forged their legacies liked to sign them.

Beside her, Wren Voss went very still in the way she'd learned to go still — the mechanic's stillness, total and economical, the stillness of someone whose hands wanted to be doing six things at once and had been ordered to do none of them.

"That's him," Wren said. Not a question. She'd heard Zephyrine say the name too many times across too many cold camps to mistake it. "Hale."

"Him." Zephyrine put the spyglass to her eye. Through the lens the flagship resolved into detail: divers' platforms rigged off both quarters, a derrick amidships with its cable already paid out into the green water, lanterns being hung along the rails though it was barely midday. Preparing to work through dusk. Preparing to work through anything. "And that's the wreck site. See where the gulls won't land? The Meridian sits under that slick. Forty fathoms if she's a foot, right where Voss's map says she rolled."

Through the glass she watched a boat lower from the flagship's side, six men at the oars, a weighted line paying out behind them. Sounding. They were sounding the grave before they opened it.

Something in her chest turned over slowly, like a stone shifting in scree.

Twenty years ago, another man had sounded those same waters for Corvane Hale — or waters very like them, up the coast, where the Kestrel mining surveys lived or died on depth readings and tide tables. Her mother had taken the original soundings herself. Maren Ravensworth had spent three seasons on that coast with frostbitten fingers and a notebook full of numbers so honest they were almost holy, and then Hale's report had come out citing figures that could not have been taken by any living hand, and the commission had needed someone to blame, and a woman guide with no family name but her own had been convenient. *Falsified data,* they'd called it. *Negligent practice.* They'd struck her from the rolls in a room Zephyrine hadn't been allowed to enter, and within two years her mother had gone north on a contract no reputable guide would take, and within three there had been nothing left to strike but a cairn on a ridge no one visited.

The compass sat against Zephyrine's ribs even now, under her coat, warm from her own body. It had never once pointed anywhere but north. Whatever else the world said about Maren Ravensworth, the needle had kept her mother's faith better than the commission ever had.

"Say it," Wren said beside her. "Whatever you're chewing on. Say it out loud, because I can hear you thinking from here and it sounds expensive."

Zephyrine lowered the glass. Below, the second sounding boat was in the water now, and the third, and the flagship's derrick cable trembled with the first diver going down.

"They'll have the ledger by tomorrow's high water," she said. "Maybe sooner. Hale knows exactly what's aboard — he's known for twenty years, which is why he let the Meridian sit on the bottom like a held breath until the last man who could testify about her cargo was dying in the mountains. He didn't come here for salvage, Wren. Salvage is what you call theft when you've bought the magistrate." She tapped the cliff stone with one knuckle. "He came to burn the evidence. Or sink it deeper. Either way, once his divers find the strongroom, we're finished, your father's promise is finished, and my mother stays a liar forever."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then: "So we take it tonight."

"We can't take it tonight."

"Why not?"

"Because of that." Zephyrine pointed north, past the fleet, past the headland, to where the horizon had stopped being a horizon.

It had been building all day — she'd felt it in her teeth before she saw it, the way you feel a rockslide start through the soles of your boots. A front stacked up over the open sea like slate shelving fallen from the sky, blue-black at its base, boiling white at its crown, and beneath it the sea had gone that particular wrong color, a green-grey like old pewter, flat and oily between gusts. The wind had been backing all afternoon, swinging from northwest around toward northeast in slow reluctant degrees, and every degree it swung it had dropped colder.

"Storm?" Wren asked.

"A gale by midnight. A full blow by morning." Zephyrine read it the way her mother had taught her, the combing of the water, the color of the light, the behavior of the gulls — the birds had all gone inland hours ago, streaming over the escarpment in ragged skeins, and that alone told her more than any barometer. "It'll hit the fjord sometime in the small hours and sit on it for the better part of a day. Nobody dives in that. Nobody *sails* in that, not even fools with a magistrate's pennant."

"So it buys us time." Wren's voice lifted with hope, and Zephyrine hated, briefly, to flatten it.

"It buys us time and it takes away everything else." She sat up, knees drawn in, and made herself say the whole ugly arithmetic of it. "Look at the shape of it. The storm kills the dive for everyone — Hale's divers, us, anyone on the water. So we wait it out huddled somewhere while Hale waits it out aboard a hundred-ton ship built to laugh at weather, and when it blows through, his divers go down first because they're already anchored on the site and we're whatever distance away we managed to get. We lose the race we're already losing. Unless—"

She stopped. Because the unless had been forming in the back of her skull since she'd seen the derrick cable trembling, and it was a bad thought, a cliff-edge thought, the kind of plan her mother would have called *a way to die with extra steps.*

"Unless," Wren prompted, in exactly the tone of someone who has already guessed.

"Unless we don't wait it out." Zephyrine looked at the girl — this sharp-boned, stubborn, grieving girl who had started the journey hating her and had somehow, somewhere in the collapsing pass or the smugglers' river, stopped — and gave her the truth straight, because Wren Voss had earned truth the hard way. "Slack water comes twice a day. Tomorrow's morning slack falls maybe an hour before the front makes landfall. That's our window. One hour of dead-calm water between the tail of today's wind and the teeth of tomorrow's — long enough to dive the Meridian, not one minute longer. And Hale won't expect it, because no sane guide puts divers in the water with a gale bearing down."

"And is it sane?"

"No."

"Good," Wren said. "I've had enough of sane. Sane is what people are right before they give up." But her hands had gone still in her lap, and Zephyrine saw her looking down at the fleet, at the little boats, at the forty fathoms of green dark, doing her own arithmetic. "One hour. How long does the dive itself take?"

"Down and through the hold to the strongroom, twenty minutes if the map's true and the wreck hasn't shifted. Back up, slower, thirty. Call it an hour with no mistakes." Zephyrine let that hang. "Which means we get zero mistakes."

"And the ascent." Wren's jaw worked. "My father told me things, when he was drunk enough to talk about the water. He said the deep doesn't care how fast you're in a hurry. You come up too quick and the air in your blood turns to foam and—" She stopped. Swallowed. "He said it like he'd seen it."

"He had. So have I." Zephyrine pulled the map from its oilskin sleeve and spread it on the stone, weighting the corners with pebbles, and the wind immediately tried to take it, worrying at the grease-stained paper like a gull at a fish. Voss's handwriting crawled across it — depths, bearings, a sketch of the Meridian lying on her beam ends with the strongroom marked aft of the mainmast stump in ink that had gone brown with age. In the margin, in smaller script, almost hidden: *tide tables, sixth day, dry season — slack 0640, do not trust the evening slack, current runs contrary.* Even dying, the man had been thorough. Even dying, he'd been leaving instructions for a daughter he couldn't face.

"Here." She touched the mark. "We dive the morning slack. Six-forty. The front hits land around eight, if the wind keeps its schedule, and wind is the one thing in this world that never keeps its schedule — which means we might get less. We surface by seven-thirty at the absolute latest, gale or no gale, ledger found or not, and we run for the leeward shore before the sea stands up. If we're late—" She met Wren's eyes. "If we're late, the fjord decides, and the fjord has never once been merciful."

Wren studied the map. Then the fleet. Then the storm stacking itself against the sky like the end of the world taking its time.

"The boats," she said. "We don't have one."

"I know."

"And the nearest fishing village is half a day south, and every boat there will be hauled out and chained down ahead of a blow, because fishermen aren't insane."

"I know."

"So we need to steal a boat," Wren said, "from a fleet of armed salvagers, anchored in a fjord that's about to become a killing jar, tonight, without being seen — and then spend the night somewhere close enough to dive at dawn, which means hiding on this coast in a storm, in the open, with no shelter I've seen since the watchtower." She paused. "And then we dive a forty-fathom wreck with a gale coming, and come up inside a window narrower than a coffin, and outrun the weather to shore carrying a ledger that a magistrate will kill us both for."

"Yes," Zephyrine said.

A long silence. The wind worried the map's corners. Far below, the flagship's lanterns were being lit one by one, little orange eyes opening along the black hull, and the derrick cable went slack as the first diver came up empty-handed — too deep, too dark, too soon; they'd need the full day and the next, and the storm would eat the next.

Then Wren Voss grinned. It was her father's grin, Zephyrine realized — the same crooked, reckless architecture of it, the grin of a man who had bet his whole life on long shots and lost them all except the one that mattered.

"You're right," Wren said. "That's impossible. Every single part of it." She began rolling up the map with quick, sure hands, a mechanic handling something she intended to use rather than admire. "So which impossible part do we do first?"

Zephyrine looked north again, at the storm, and felt the old thing rise in her — the thing that had carried her up the Kestrel Escarpment with a dying man on her back, the thing her mother had died with and been called a liar for, the thing that had no better name than *yes anyway.*

"The boat," she said. "We steal the boat tonight. And Wren—" She waited until the girl looked at her. "When we take the Corvane's Pride's tender out from under that flagship's nose, we're not just thieves anymore. After tomorrow, Hale won't stop at ruin. Whatever happens on that wreck — we come back with the ledger, or we don't come back."

Wren slung the oilskin over her shoulder and stood, small and straight against the boiling sky.

"My father sent you to find me," she said, "and sent me a map instead of a letter, and made both of us carry it. I think he knew exactly what kind of impossible this was going to be." She turned down off the crown of Skald's Tooth, toward the darkening water and the lantern-lit hull below. "Come on. Tide's turning."

And far below them, unhearing, the fleet rode at anchor on a sea the color of forged iron, while the storm came on like a debt whose interest had finally come due.
