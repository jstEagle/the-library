## 9. The Sixth Tide

**ZEPHYRINE**

The storm came down the fjord like a debt collector, and Zephyrine counted its footsteps in thunder.

They had watched from the cliff since before dawn, two small figures pressed against wet granite while below them the Meridian lay on her side in thirty feet of green-black water, her masts snapped, her hull furred with weed and barnacle, a drowned cathedral no one had mourned in twenty years. Hale's flagship rode at anchor half a mile off, lanterns strung along her rail like a carnival that had come to the wrong town. Divers' platforms. Winches. A crane arm swinging slow over the swell.

Tide four had turned at midday. Tide five was turning now, and the barometer in Zephyrine's pack — her mother's barometer, brass-cased, needle trembling — told her what the sky already shouted: the front would hit within the hour and would not lift until well past dark.

"Slack water," she said, "is in forty minutes. It lasts maybe twenty. After that the tide turns hard against us and the storm turns harder."

Wren didn't look away from the flagship. Wind had whipped her hair into a black tangle and salt crusted her lips white. "His divers will wait out the weather. He's got a fleet to lose; we've only got ourselves."

"That's exactly why we go now." Zephyrine checked the knots on the dive line one more time, then again, because her hands wanted something to do besides shake. "He'll pull his men up when the squall hits. Nobody dives into a storm for paper. But I'm not diving for paper either."

She wasn't sure what she meant by that yet. Maybe she'd know when she found it.

The cove where they'd hidden the skiff was a quarter mile of scree and gorse above the waterline, and they went down it in the first spit of rain, sliding on their heels, catching at rock, Wren swearing steadily under her breath in the rhythm of someone who used profanity the way other people used prayer. The skiff bobbed at its mooring line, taking spray. Zephyrine looked at the open water between them and the wreck — gray now, going darker, the wind dragging white veins across it — and felt the old fear rise in her throat, the one that lived there since she was nine years old and learned what the sea does to people who are loved by it.

Her mother had drowned guiding a party across the Meridian Strait. That was the official story. The unofficial story, the forged survey, the magistrate who signed it — that story had eaten eleven years of Zephyrine's life.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said. "Where you go somewhere else."

"I'm checking the current," Zephyrine said, which was true, and also not the thing.

They rowed into the wind. Every stroke cost double; every wave slapped the bow sideways and made them pay it back. Rain came in earnest then, fat cold drops that stung like flung gravel, and the flagship's lanterns smeared into long yellow ghosts through the murk. Zephyrine rowed until her shoulders burned and her breath tore, and when the wreck's black shoulder rose out of the water ahead of them, listing, weed-hung, she felt something she hadn't felt in years: not hope exactly. Aim.

The slack came as the old divers say it comes — all at once, the sea forgetting itself for a few borrowed minutes. The swell flattened. The current died. The water around the Meridian went still as poured glass, and through it Zephyrine could see the wreck's ribs descending into green gloom, an entire ship holding its breath.

"Tide six," she said. "Last one. Go."

* * *

**WREN**

The water took her like a fist closing.

Cold first — a cold that squeezed the air out of her chest and made her want to scream into her regulator just to feel something move. Then the dark, broken only by the lantern glow of Zephyrine's lamp ahead, a soft gold coin swimming through murk. Wren kicked after it and hated every second, hated the weight on her back, the taste of rubber, the way her father's map — wrapped in oilskin, strapped inside her jacket — pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

She had never been underwater in her life. She'd grown up in engine rooms and dry docks, among things that were solid and could be fixed with a wrench. This was nothing solid. This was everything trying to get inside her.

*Follow the lamp,* Zephyrine had said on the surface. *Hand signals only. If anything goes wrong, we go up together, no matter what we've found or haven't.*

The wreck grew around them. First the hull, a wall of weed-draped planking taller than any building Wren had ever climbed. Then a breach in the starboard side, torn open long ago, edges softened by decades of sea. Zephyrine slipped through it and Wren followed, and suddenly she was inside the Meridian — inside the drowned belly of the ship her father had spent three years and every coin he ever earned chasing.

It should have felt like an answer. It felt like a throat.

Lantern light swept across corridors gone soft with silt. A door hung off one hinge, swaying gently in some current too deep for Wren to feel. A boot — leather, intact, empty — rested on a stair tread. She looked away from it fast.

Zephyrine stopped at a junction, consulted something in her glove — a copy of the map, grease-stained, Voss's cramped handwriting ghosting under the lamplight — and pointed down the port corridor. *Cargo hold. The ledger's in the strongroom behind it.* Her father's margin notes had been specific, obsessive, the handwriting of a man who knew he might not live to explain himself: *third rib past the mast step, hatch with the brass ring, false floor.*

They swam. The corridor narrowed. Silt rose around their fins in slow brown clouds, and Wren thought, absurdly, of flour dust in her father's kitchen, the morning he'd left for the last time, pancakes he'd burned because he was already reading the map instead of watching the pan. She'd screamed at him. She'd said she hoped the mountain kept him.

The mountain had. Just not the one she'd meant.

Ahead, Zephyrine's lamp flared on a hatch — round, iron-ringed, crusted but whole. Third rib past the mast step. Wren's chest went tight around her regulator. Her father had drawn this from memory, from rumor, from twenty-year-old salvage charts, and it was *true*, every line of it true, and he had died on a ridge a thousand miles from here believing in it.

Zephyrine gripped the ring and pulled. Rust held. She braced both boots against the frame and hauled again, and this time the hatch groaned open with a sound Wren felt in her teeth, exhaling a bloom of silt that swallowed the lamplight whole.

Into the cloud, out of the dark beyond it, came another light.

Wren saw it before Zephyrine did — a pale green gleam low in the flooded corridor behind them, then a second, then the shapes attached to them: divers, big-shouldered, moving fast, commercial rigs with hard-hat lamps and knives at their belts. Hale's men. They hadn't waited out the storm after all. Or they'd read the same tide tables and gambled the same gamble.

Zephyrine spun, lamp swinging, and in its beam Wren saw her face through the mask — and saw her make the calculation, quick and cold as arithmetic. Fight four men underwater, with knives, in zero visibility, in a collapsing wreck. Or—

Zephyrine pointed: *through the hatch*. Then she did something Wren would think about for the rest of her life. She unclipped the lamp from her harness, set it on the sill of the open hatch facing outward, and pulled Wren into the dark beyond it, into the strongroom, leaving their own light burning as bait in the doorway.

The divers reached the hatch seconds later. Wren heard them — a clank of tank on iron, the burble of regulators — and then the lead man's light swept past the opening, caught the abandoned lamp, and kept moving, chasing the glow deeper down the corridor while the two of them hung in absolute blackness behind a rusted hatch, breathing as shallowly as staying alive allowed.

In the dark, Wren found Zephyrine's wrist and squeezed it once. She felt the answer come back through the grip: *not yet. Not yet. Now.*

They went through the strongroom by touch. False floor — her father's words were a litany in Wren's head — third plank, brass pin, and her fingers found it, found the pin, found the gap beneath where twenty years of seawater had somehow spared an iron box lashed down with tarred canvas. She hauled it into her arms. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

Above them, faint through the hull, the storm arrived — a sound like the whole sky being dragged across the sea.

And the water began to move. The slack was ending. The sixth tide, turning.

* * *

**ZEPHYRINE**

The current found them in the corridor like a hand shoving from behind.

Zephyrine had dived wrecks for fifteen years and she had never felt a slack die this fast — the storm's pressure breathing down the fjord, sucking the tide out early, turning the Meridian's flooded interior into a river running the wrong direction. Their exit route, the breached hull amidships, was now upstream. Swimming it would be like swimming up a waterfall in the dark with a stranger's knife at your back.

So she chose the other way. Down and aft, through the hold, following the flood path toward the stern where the old escape hatches opened near the rudder stock — a route from the map's margins, one of Voss's annotations Zephyrine had memorized without knowing why: *if the sea turns, follow it out the stern. The ship will show you.*

The ship showed them. The current carried them through the hold's cathedral dark, past collapsed cargo netting and the pale drift of dead fish, past a fallen mast that loomed like a fallen tree, and the whole time the green lights of Hale's divers thrashed somewhere behind and above, fighting the same river they were riding. Zephyrine heard one shout into his regulator — a distorted, animal sound of panic — and did not look back.

The stern hatch was jammed half-open, wedged by a spar, and the current squeezed them through it like pulp through a press. Then open water, black and boiling, rain hammering the surface overhead in a million silver fists, and Zephyrine pulling Wren up the shot line they'd anchored at the wreck's shoulder, hand over hand, lungs screaming, until the night broke over them like a bucket.

The skiff was still tied. Barely. It bucked at its mooring like a hooked fish, and getting into it was less boarding than drowning in installments. Zephyrine cut the line, and the sea took them — hurled them seaward, away from the wreck, away from the flagship whose lanterns had gone dark one by one as the storm closed its hand.

Behind them, faint through the roar, a whistle blew aboard the flagship. Three notes. A recall, or an alarm. It didn't matter which. Nothing Hale owned was putting boats into that water.

Nothing except the shore.

* * *

**WREN**

Hale's men were waiting on the rocks.

Not many — the storm had stranded most of them aboard — but enough. Four figures in oilskins at the mouth of the cove where the skiff scraped ashore, lanterns guttering, rifles slung muzzle-down out of respect for the wind rather than mercy. And in front of them, tall and dry under a wide hat that should have been impossible in this weather, stood Magistrate Corvane Hale himself, as if he had simply willed the storm to part around him.

Wren climbed out of the surf with the iron box lashed to her back under her jacket, seawater streaming off her, and felt something she had never felt before in her life: completely out of fear. There was a kind of calm on the far side of terror, flat and clear, and she stood on it.

"The box," Hale said. His voice carried easily, pitched for command. "Give it to me, and walk away. Both of you. I'll even let you keep the skiff."

"You don't even know what's in it," Wren said.

"I know what my surveyor put in it." Something moved behind his composure, brief as a fish under ice. "Your father was a thorough thief, Miss Voss. I'll grant him that."

"He wasn't a thief." Wren stepped forward, and felt Zephyrine shift beside her, not to stop her — to stand with her. "He was a salvager who found your forgery. The Kestrel survey. You redrew the depth charts, you falsified the passage reports, and when my mother—" she faltered, one heartbeat, "—when *her* mother's expedition followed your charts, you needed someone to blame. So you blamed the guide. You built your career on a dead woman's name."

Rain ran down Hale's face and he let it, unmoved, unhurried, a man who had never once been cold or wet in his life. "That is a very dramatic story," he said. "Drowned women and dying men. Juries prefer ledgers. Fortunately for me, the only ledger in this fjord is about to be at the bottom of it." He lifted two fingers.

The men raised their rifles.

And Zephyrine laughed — short, wild, soaked to the bone, alive. "Magistrate," she called over the wind, "how many witnesses do you think a storm like this leaves?"

Hale's fingers paused.

"Four riflemen, one magistrate, one wrecked fleet at anchor, and a box that two women have already described — in writing, sealed, and witnessed — to a harbor clerk in Greyhollow, to be opened if we don't come back for it by tomorrow's dawn bell." Zephyrine walked forward, into the lantern light, rain streaming off her chin, her mother's compass glinting at her throat. "You can shoot us. And by spring you'll be the one on trial, with your own surveyor's testimony and your own signature in evidence. Or you can let us walk, and spend the rest of your life wondering which is worse."

The rifles stayed up. The rain fell. Somewhere out in the black, thunder rolled along the escarpment like a drum being carried toward them.

Hale looked at the box on Wren's back. He looked at Zephyrine's face, and whatever he found there made his jaw tighten — recognition, maybe, of a name he had buried so thoroughly he'd forgotten it could climb out of the ground.

Then lightning split the sky above the escarpment, white and total, and in its light Wren saw the truth arrive on Hale's face a full second before he spoke it:

"You're Eriksen's daughter," he said quietly. "Of course you are."

The rifles lowered — slowly, uncertainly, their owners looking to a magistrate who had suddenly stopped looking like one. And down the beach, out of the storm, came the sound of oars and voices: Greyhollow's harbor watch, late, wet, and very much awake, drawn by the flare Wren had fired from the headland an hour ago — the flare Zephyrine had told her was a waste of powder.

The sixth tide was turning. And it was turning for them.
