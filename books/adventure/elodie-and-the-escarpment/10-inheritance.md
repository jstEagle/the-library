## 10. Inheritance

The ledger went public the way a landslide goes public — all at once, and with no regard for who stood beneath it.

Zephyrine watched it happen from the gallery of Greyhollow's customs house, pressed against cold oak among three hundred salt-stiffened bodies. Magistrate Corvane Hale stood on the dais in his grey wool coat, delivering his quarterly address on harbor tariffs, his voice smooth as ballast stone. He had not changed in seven years. The same silver at his temples, the same habit of resting one hand on the rail as though the room were a ship he alone could keep steady.

Then Wren Voss walked up the center aisle carrying the ledger wrapped in oilcloth, and the room's weather turned.

She set it on the dais without asking permission. She unwrapped it the way you unwrap something that has been underwater for twenty years — carefully, but without reverence, because reverence was for things that hadn't lied to you. Zephyrine heard her voice carry, clear and dockyard-flat: *"Read page forty. Read the survey dated the ninth of Frostmonth. Read the signature that isn't my father's."*

Hale's hand left the rail.

The clerk who opened the ledger was a young man with ink-stained cuffs, and Zephyrine would remember forever how his face changed — the small, involuntary flinch of a person reading something true in a room built on lies. He read the figures aloud. The forged depth soundings. The altered channel markers. The survey of the Kestrel approach that had condemned a wreck — and condemned the woman who'd charted it honestly, the surveyor Maren Eriksen, whose name had been struck from the guild rolls while her daughter stood in this same gallery, eleven years old and holding her mother's compass like it might answer questions.

The sound the crowd made was not a gasp. It was lower than that, older — the sound of a town recalculating everything it believed about its own magistrate.

Hale spoke. Something about forgery, about desperate salvagers, about a dead prospector's daughter manipulated by — and here his eyes found Zephyrine in the gallery, and for one moment the smoothness cracked and she saw underneath it, the same hunger she'd seen on the escarpment rocks when his divers closed in and lost — *a disgraced guide*.

But the room had already stopped listening. That was the thing no one told you about ruin: it wasn't dramatic. It was arithmetic. One clerk, one ledger, forty witnesses doing sums they couldn't undo. By nightfall Hale was under arrest in his own customs house, and by week's end the guild rolls were being reprinted, and Zephyrine stood in the archive office while an old man with trembling hands drew a single line through the word *stricken* beside her mother's name and wrote above it, in careful copperplate: *exonerated, posthumous.*

"Your mother," the archivist said, "charted that channel true. Every sounding. She was right about all of it."

"I know," Zephyrine said. Her voice came out strange, thin, like air through a crack in ice. "I always knew."

Knowing had never been the hard part. The hard part was the eleven years between knowing and proof, and now that the distance was closed she found she didn't feel triumphant. She felt the way she felt at the top of a long climb — emptied out, wind-scoured, unsure what she was supposed to do with her hands.

She walked down to the harbor that evening. The tide was going out over the flats where the Meridian's masts still broke the surface at low water, black ribs in amber light. Somewhere under that water was a hold she'd swum through with her lungs burning, a flooded corridor where Wren's grip on her wrist had been the only fixed point in the dark. Somewhere under it was Callum Voss's map, gone soft as skin in the brine, its work finished.

She took out her mother's compass. The needle swung, settled, pointed north toward the escarpment, which even from here she could see — that long pale scar across the horizon where a dying man had made her swear an oath she hadn't wanted.

"You'd have liked her," she told the compass, meaning Wren, meaning maybe both of them. "Stubborn. Terrible at following."

The needle trembled and held.

* * *

Wren claimed the estate on a morning of high thin cloud, in a lawyer's office that smelled of lamp oil and old paper, and discovered that her father had been richer in debts than coin — except for one debt he'd paid in full, in advance, in the only currency he'd ever trusted.

"He prepaid your apprenticeship," the lawyer said, sliding the paper across. "Guild of Marine Engineers. Paid when you were nine years old. He never told you?"

Wren looked at the document. Her father's handwriting — the same slanted, grease-smudged hand that had drawn the map, that had written *bring this to my girl before the sixth tide* on the back of a promise he must have known he might not live to keep. Nine years old. He'd been planning her escape from the docks since before she could remember wanting one.

"No," she said. "He never told me anything. That was rather his whole problem."

But her throat was tight, and the lawyer pretended not to notice, and afterward she sat on the office steps for a long time with the paper in her hands, watching gulls fight over fish guts in the gutter, thinking about a man who had loved her in the language of prepaid fees and unmailed letters and a map folded eight times against his heart.

The estate itself amounted to a salvage claim on the Meridian — hers now, legally, every rivet and rotted plank — plus a chest of charts, a rusted diving bell, and enough outstanding obligations to keep the lawyers fed for a year. Wren sold the claim to a cooperative of Greyhollow fishermen who'd hated Hale longer than she'd been alive. They'd raise what was worth raising and let the rest sleep. It seemed right. Some wrecks should stay wrecks.

What she kept was the diving bell, because her father had built it, and the ledger, because the world needed to remember what it cost, and one page torn from the back of a chart table — a sketch of a girl of nine, standing on a dock, done from memory by a man who'd been at sea when he drew it.

She kept that in her breast pocket, over her heart, where maps belonged.

* * *

They met at the escarpment's edge in the last week of the dry season, though *met* was perhaps too deliberate a word for two women who had each, independently, ridden three days into the mountains because neither could think of anywhere else to go.

The Kestrel Escarpment in autumn was a different country than it had been in their crossing. The passes stood open and gold-lit, the scree slopes quiet, the defile where Wren had frozen and Zephyrine had talked her down handhold by handhold now just another stretch of stone wearing its afternoon light like an old coat. They camped on the ridge where Callum Voss had died, because it seemed important to be there once with the sun up and the wind gentle and no one dying.

Zephyrine brewed tea on a spirit stove. Wren cleaned a fishing reel she'd brought for no reason she could articulate. Below them the fjord country fell away in blue distances, and beyond that the sea, and beyond that whatever the sea decided.

"They're offering me contracts again," Zephyrine said at last. "Guiding. Real ones. Guild-sponsored. There's talk of a survey commission — remapping the Kestrel approach properly, with my mother's original soundings as the baseline." She turned her cup in her hands. "Eleven years I defined myself by what I'd lost. I don't know how to do it the other way."

"You'll figure it out," Wren said. "You talked me down a cliff face while a rockslide ate the route behind us. You'll manage a job offer."

"It's not the same."

"No," Wren agreed. "It's worse. Cliffs are honest."

They sat with that until the laughter came, unexpected and a little ragged, two women on a ridge laughing at nothing and everything, the sound running off down the scree like water.

When it faded, Wren said, quietly, "I'm taking the engineers' apprenticeship. In the spring. My father paid for it when I was nine. Seems rude to keep him waiting longer."

"Good," Zephyrine said. And meant it, and found that meaning it didn't hurt the way she'd expected.

They broke camp at dawn. At the trail fork — one path down to Greyhollow and the harbor, one along the escarpment's spine toward the high country and the survey work waiting there — they stopped, and stood, and neither of them said anything clever, because there wasn't anything clever left to say. Everything essential had already been said on a rock face, in a flooded hold, in the white roar of the sixth tide.

Wren stuck out her hand. Zephyrine took it. Then, breaking some rule neither of them had written down, Wren pulled her into a brief fierce hug, thumped her twice on the back like sealing a bulkhead, and stepped away before either of them could make it strange.

"Sixth tide next year," Wren said. "There's a cove on the north shore where the fishing's supposed to be absurd. If a certain surveyor happened to be passing through—"

"I'll bring the tea," Zephyrine said.

Wren grinned, shouldered her pack, and took the harbor road, and Zephyrine watched her go until the path bent and took her — a small straight-backed figure walking into a future her father had paid for and she had earned twice over.

Then Zephyrine turned north.

The escarpment ran ahead of her along the roof of the world, ridge after ridge dissolving into haze, unmapped country, her mother's soundings in her pack and her mother's compass in her hand. The needle held steady. The wind came up off the drop and smelled of stone and snowmelt and distance.

She checked her bearing, adjusted her straps, and started walking — not away from anything, for the first time in eleven years, but toward.
