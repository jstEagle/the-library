## 1. The River Keeps What It Is Owed

The Monsoon River in the hour before dawn was the color of a bruise healing, and Kestrel Nakamura loved it best that way—when it was too dark to see what the water carried and honest enough not to pretend it was empty.

They stood at the wheel of the *Tern* with one boot hooked on the rail, drinking tea that Sefa had made too strong because Sefa believed weakness of any kind was a personal insult. The boat moved under them with the easy rolling gait of a fat old dog trotting home, engines ticking over at half-throttle, dredge lines trailing behind in the brown water like fishing lines cast by someone with more patience than skill.

Routine run. That was all this was supposed to be. Upriver to Halfway Lock, a hold full of salvage scrap to sell, a hold full of rice and lamp oil to buy on the way back down. Two weeks, maybe three if the lock-keepers were feeling bureaucratic. In and out before anyone whose attention Kestrel didn't want had time to notice them.

Kestrel had three rules, and they kept them the way other people kept religion.

Never stop for strangers. Never take passengers. And never—*never*—stop the boat on open water for anything less than fire.

The rules had kept the *Tern* alive through eleven years of river that ate boats for breakfast. The rules had kept the crew alive, which mattered more, though Kestrel would have chewed off their own arm before saying so out loud where Brannick could hear it. Brannick collected sentimental statements the way driftwood collects barnacles, and he would carry that one around forever, polishing it at odd moments, bringing it out at dinner like a relic.

"Captain." Odile's voice came soft from the chart table, where she sat with her coffee and her charts and her stillness, marking their position with those small precise X's she made as though each one were a signature on something binding. "We're coming up on the Silt Reach. Current's running lazy. Good morning for it."

"Good morning for anything," Wren said from somewhere near the bow, where he had wedged himself against the winch housing with a battered primer open on his knees. He was seventeen and read navigation manuals the way other boys read adventure serials, mouthing the words. "Good morning for practicing dead reckoning. I did the whole night's position without asking Odile once."

"And how far off were you?" Odile asked, without looking up.

Wren went quiet in the particular way of a boy doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Four miles," he admitted.

"Four miles is the difference between the channel and the sandbar that ate the *Merry Adela*," Odile said. "With everyone aboard."

"But I *did* it," Wren said, with the stubborn brightness that Kestrel privately found exhausting and publicly encouraged, because a pilot who wanted to learn was worth ten who didn't. "Someday I'm going to run this river without charts at all. Just memory. Like the old pilots."

"The old pilots are all dead," Kestrel said.

"That's what makes it impressive," Wren said.

Kestrel turned back to the water and hid the smile in the tea. This was the thing about the *Tern*'s crew that no one outside it ever understood: they bickered like cats in a sack, and every single argument was a love letter. Sefa complained about the debts she owed in three river towns and cooked portions big enough to sink a barge. Brannick talked to the engine like a nervous horse and slept in the engine room when the boat was lonely, which was his word for it, never theirs. Odile said perhaps forty words a day and somehow knew everything. Wren burned to be somebody, and hadn't yet learned that the rest of them already thought he was.

It was a good crew. It was, Kestrel thought, watching the bruise-colored water slide past, the only family they'd ever trusted, which was why the rules existed, and why the rules would hold.

Which was, of course, exactly when the river decided to test them.

* * *

It happened an hour after sunrise, when the mist had burned off and the Silt Reach lay open ahead, wide and shallow and treacherous as a liar's smile. Kestrel felt it first through the deck—a shudder in the hull, deep and wrong, like the boat had stepped on something that stepped back.

Then the stern swung. Not much. A hand's width. But the *Tern* didn't swing on her own; she was forty years old and set in her ways, and Kestrel knew her moods the way they knew their own pulse.

"Brannick," Kestrel called down the engine hatch. "What's our drag?"

There was a pause. Then Brannick's voice came up, puzzled, unhurried: "Reading heavy. Like we're towing something."

"We're not towing anything."

"Well." Another pause. "We are now."

Kestrel cut the throttle and stood very still, listening. Behind the boat, the dredge lines—the long chains and drags they trailed through likely bottom on the chance of picking up scrap iron—had gone taut. Not snagged-on-a-log taut. Taut like harp strings. Taut like the river itself had caught its breath.

Kestrel walked aft. Wren fell into step beside them, vibrating with interest. Odile appeared at the rail with the silent efficiency of a woman who had heard everything and missed nothing.

Below the surface, maybe six fathoms down in the murk, something was holding the lines. Something heavy enough that the *Tern*, riding light with a half-empty hold, listed slightly toward her stern, dragged by her own gear.

"Snagged log," Wren said hopefully.

"Logs don't pull," Kestrel said.

"Big log?"

"Wren."

"A wreck," Odile said quietly. She was looking at the water with an expression Kestrel couldn't read, which was unusual enough to register. "Something with mass. Iron, probably. Or lead."

Kestrel looked at the straining lines, and then at the depth of the water, and then at the sky, doing the arithmetic they'd done a hundred times. Every minute spent stopped here was a minute visible on open water. Every minute was risk. The rule existed for exactly this moment, and the rule said: *cut loose.* Lines and drags were replaceable. A season's profit lost to a snapped cable was cheaper than a boat lost to whatever came looking at a boat sitting dead in the current like a duck on a pond.

"Cut it loose," Kestrel said. "We'll lose the drag chain. Annoying, not fatal. Brannick, get the—

"No."

Everyone turned. It took Kestrel a moment to identify the voice, because it belonged to Sefa, and Sefa did not say no to Kestrel. Sefa said *yes captain* and *whatever you say captain* and *I've put extra pepper in yours, captain,* and then she came up from the galley wiping her hands on her apron with the expression of a woman arriving at a meeting she had been preparing for all morning.

"It could be a windfall," she said.

"It could be a propeller shaft. It could be nothing."

"It could be a *windfall*," Sefa repeated, with the patience of someone explaining rain to a fish. "Kestrel. I've been doing the books. We have fuel for six weeks and coin for four. Wren needs new charts, the starboard dredge winch needs a new bearing, and I owe Master Ollivan in Halfway Lock more than I'd like to discuss in front of the boy. One good haul—one *single* good haul—and we don't work again until the rains end. A whole season. Do you know what I'd do with a whole season?"

"Cook," Brannick offered.

"I'd cook *voluntarily*," Sefa said. "There's a difference."

Kestrel looked at the crew. All four of them, lined along the rail now, and all four wearing variations of the same expression—that hungry, hopeful, dangerous look that Kestrel had seen on salvagers' faces a hundred times and hated every time, because it was the look that preceded bad decisions.

"Rule three," Kestrel said.

"Rule three says no stopping for anything less than fire," Wren said. "This is basically fire. Fire of opportunity."

"That's not—"

"You wrote the rules," Brannick said mildly. He had come up from below, grease to the elbows, and stood with his arms crossed in a way that suggested the engine had opinions and he was merely relaying them. "You never let us vote on them. You always say the rules aren't a democracy. Fine. But you also always say the *boat's* a democracy when it matters, and you're outvoted, Captain. Four to one."

"That's not how—"

"Five to one," Sefa said. "I'm counting me twice, for the debts."

For a long moment Kestrel said nothing. The lines hummed behind them. The river slid past, brown and patient, keeping whatever counsel rivers keep.

Here was the truth Kestrel didn't say: the rules weren't caution. Caution was a habit; the rules were a wall, and walls get built around things. Eleven years of never stopping meant eleven years of never being caught standing still, never being boarded, never being asked questions by people in uniforms with clipboards. The rules were how a person with a bounty on their old name stayed a person with only a present tense.

But Kestrel looked at Sefa's tired eyes and Wren's burning ones and Brannick's steady, patient face, and understood that the wall had a cost too, and the cost was paid out of these four people, a little at a time, every season they ran lean and moored cold and told themselves next year.

"One hour," Kestrel said. "Not a minute more. If it's not free in an hour, we cut and go. And if it turns out to be trouble—" they pointed at each of them in turn, "—it's your trouble. I'm just the one who steers us away from it."

"Fair," said Brannick.

"Liar," said Sefa fondly, and went to get the grapnel hooks.

* * *

It took fifty minutes to bring it up, and Kestrel spent all fifty of them at the rail with their jaw tight, counting the minutes aloud at intervals until Wren begged them to stop.

The thing on the lines was a chest.

Not a crate, not a drum, not the tangled mass of a collapsed barge. A chest—iron-banded, lead-sheathed, sealed at every seam with solder gone green with age, the size of a sea chest and heavier than sin. It came up out of the brown water streaming, shedding silt, and landed on the deck with a boom that Kestrel felt through their boots, and the whole crew stood around it dripping and triumphant like hunters over a kill.

"Lead-lined," Brannick said, thumping it with a knuckle. The sound came back dull and dense. "Somebody wanted what's inside kept dry. And private."

"Paper, then," Odile said softly. "Or something that rots."

"Could be coin," Wren said. "Lead lining keeps out water. Coin keeps out poverty. Everybody wins."

Kestrel crouched by the chest and brushed the silt from the lid with two fingers, and that was when they saw the stamp.

It was pressed into the lead above the hasp, deep enough to survive twenty years underwater: a circle broken by a wavy line, and beneath it, a crown with no head under it.

Kestrel knew that stamp. Kestrel had known it since childhood, back when it flew on flags, back before the war, back before the flood, back before an entire nation went under the water in a single terrible week while the rest of the river world agreed, quickly and thoroughly, to stop talking about it.

Vessia.

The cold went through Kestrel like river water through a hull seam—instant, total, finding every low place. They kept their face still, because Kestrel's face had been trained to stay still by professionals, and said, in the flat voice of pure pragmatism:

"Sealed cargo from a drowned country. You know what that means? It means nobody's alive to claim it, and every magistrate from here to the delta will call it Compact salvage property the second they hear the word Vessia. We don't open it. We don't sell it. We put it in the hold, we deliver our scrap, and we decide later whether it's worth anything or worth throwing back. Sealed. Understood?"

"Understood," Brannick said slowly, watching Kestrel with the careful look of a man reading weather.

"Sealed," Odile echoed—but she was looking at the stamp, and her face had gone the color of the mist that morning, and her hands, when she folded them, folded very carefully indeed.

"Can we at least weigh it?" Wren asked.

"You can look at it," Kestrel said. "Reverently."

They got the chest into the hold, into the corner behind the spare sail canvas, and Kestrel rigged a tarp over it and told themselves the cold feeling in their stomach was just indigestion from Sefa's tea.

But that night, standing alone at the wheel with the *Tern* running easy again and the Silt Reach falling away astern, Kestrel found themselves doing the one thing the rules forbade most of all.

Looking back.

Behind them the river lay flat and silvered under the rising moon, wide as a road, patient as debt. Somewhere under all that calm water lay a drowned country, and somewhere in the *Tern*'s hold sat a piece of it, stamped with a seal that should have stayed lost, and Kestrel—eleven years careful, eleven years invisible—felt the first cold certainty settle over them like spray:

The river keeps what it is owed.

And sooner or later, it always comes to collect.
