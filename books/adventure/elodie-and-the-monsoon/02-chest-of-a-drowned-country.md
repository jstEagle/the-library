## 2. Chest of a Drowned Country

The winch groaned like an old man being asked to dance, and Kestrel Nakamura stood at the rail with their arms crossed, watching the river give up something it had held for a long time.

The Monsoon was brown-green and patient, the way it always was in the low-water season, and it did not like being asked to surrender its property. The dredge lines ran taut off the stern, humming, and every few seconds the whole boat shuddered as whatever hung beneath the surface shifted its weight against them.

"Captain," Brannick called from the engine housing, one hand on the winch brake, his forearms slick to the elbow with river water and grease. "She's heavy. Heavier than she should be."

"Heavy things are usually not worth having," Kestrel said.

"That's what you said about me, and look how that turned out."

Kestrel didn't smile. They were watching the water where the lines entered it, reading the way it moved around the strain—slow boils, a lazy eddy spinning off to port. Something down there was big. Bigger than a snagged log. Bigger than the engine block they'd pulled out of the shallows two seasons back, which had paid for Sefa's debts twice over before anyone asked too many questions about where it came from.

"We cut loose," Kestrel said. "That's my call. That's the rule."

"The rule," Wren repeated, from up in the rigging, where he spent most of his life these days, halfway between boy and bird. "You have a rule for everything, Captain."

"I have rules so I don't need luck."

"And we're rich," Sefa said. She had come up from the galley wiping her hands on her apron, which was how she arrived everywhere, as though the kitchen followed her like a loyal dog. She squinted at the strained lines, then at Kestrel, and her broad face took on the expression she used when negotiating with fishmongers, moneylenders, and occasionally gods. "Captain. Whatever's down there is lead-lined. I can smell it. Lead means somebody wanted it protected. Somebody wanted it *kept*. Things people want kept are worth more than things people throw away."

"Things people sink are usually sunk on purpose," Kestrel said.

"Then they'll pay us to forget we found it." Sefa shrugged, magnificent and unbothered. "Either way, we eat for a year."

Kestrel looked at Odile, because Odile was the tiebreaker without ever seeming to vote. The navigator stood at the chart table under the awning, her hands flat on the old river charts, her face doing what it always did: nothing at all. But Kestrel had learned, over four years, to read the small weather of her. And right now there was a storm behind her eyes that hadn't been there an hour ago.

"You've got nothing to add?" Kestrel asked her.

Odile looked at the water. Then at Kestrel. "It won't come up clean," she said quietly. "Whatever it is. Nothing comes up clean from this stretch."

"That's three for cutting loose," Kestrel said, turning back to the crew. "Brannick, slack the—"

"It's two," Wren called down. "Sefa hasn't voted."

"Sefa doesn't get a vote when she's already decided and is just waiting for the rest of us to catch up."

"I'm voting," Sefa said serenely, "for raising it."

Wren whooped. Brannick sighed the sigh of a man whose curiosity had gotten him into trouble in four different countries and would likely finish him in a fifth. And Odile said nothing, which Kestrel noted, and filed away, and would think about later, at night, when thinking was all there was to do.

So it was three to two, or two and a half to two depending on how you counted silences, and Kestrel had lost votes before and knew better than to die on this particular sandbar. A captain who overrode her crew became a jailer. A captain who never lost became a tyrant. Kestrel had been both, once, in another life, under another name, and had promised the river never again.

"Fine," Kestrel said. "But we do it my way. Slow. Brannick, ease the winch. If anything shifts wrong, if those lines sing, we drop it and run. Nobody goes in the water. Nobody touches it bare-handed until it's drained and dry. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said the crew, in the tone of people agreeing to rules they fully intended to break.

* * *

It took two hours.

Two hours of easing line, of the Tern listing gently to starboard as the weight came up, of Brannick coaxing the old winch with words that were half prayer and half insult. Two hours of Wren hanging upside down from the ratlines calling out depth marks, his voice cracking with excitement in a way that made him sound twelve instead of seventeen. Two hours of Kestrel standing at the rail doing arithmetic they didn't like: fuel burned, time lost, the patrol skiff they'd passed at dawn still somewhere on the river behind them.

And then, in the middle of the afternoon, with the sun high enough to burn the mist off, the chest broke the surface.

It came up streaming, brown water sheeting off it, and even coated in twenty years of silt it was beautiful in the way that dangerous things are beautiful. Iron-banded. Lead-sheathed along every edge—the seams sealed with poured lead, not soldered but *poured*, done by someone with time and resources and a reason to be thorough. It was the size of a sea chest, the kind an officer would own, and it hung dripping above the deck while everyone stared at it and nobody breathed.

"Lower it slow," Kestrel said. Their voice came out flatter than they intended. "Center of the deck. Not the hold."

"Why not the hold?" Wren asked.

"Because if it's trapped, I'd rather it blow a hole in the sky than in our keel."

"That's very reassuring, Captain."

"I'm not here to reassure you. I'm here to keep you alive. There's a difference."

They set it down on deck planks laid over the main hatch, and the Tern rocked once, heavily, as if adjusting to the new weight of its passenger. Brannick circled it, whistling low. Sefa touched it with one finger, then snatched the finger back as though the chest had opinions about being touched.

And Kestrel stepped forward to look at the stamp.

They knew before they saw it. Some part of them had known since the lines first went taut, some animal instinct in the gut that had been screaming since dawn, quiet and cold, the way it screamed only a few times in a life. Kestrel had learned young to listen to that voice. It had kept them alive through things the crew would never hear about. It was screaming now.

The silt came away under Brannick's rag, and there it was.

A seal, pressed deep into the lead near the lock plate: a circle broken by three wavy lines, and above them, a tower.

Vessia.

The drowned nation. The word every child on the river learned the way children learn the names of storms—not as a place, but as a warning. Twenty years ago Vessia had been a country of locks and levees and terraced river-cities, proud and careful, a nation that had built its whole life around managing the water. And then the monsoon season had come, and the floodgates had failed—or been made to fail—and the river had taken everything. Three hundred thousand people, the stories said. Whole cities gone under in a night and a day. The Compact had been formed afterward, the salvage nations dividing up the drowned territories, and the world had agreed, in the way the world agrees to things that are convenient, to stop asking questions.

Nobody salvaged Vessian wrecks. That wasn't law exactly, but it was close enough. The Compact licensed every wreck claim on the river, and no license had ever been issued for Vessian waters, and any salvage that turned up bearing the Vessian seal was supposed to be reported, surrendered, and forgotten. Kestrel knew this because Kestrel had once been on the other end of such knowledge, in a life with a different name, wearing a uniform with a crane-and-chain on the shoulder, learning exactly what happened to people who didn't report.

The cold went through Kestrel like river water through a cracked hull.

"Captain?" Brannick was looking at them. "You know this mark?"

"Everyone knows this mark," Kestrel said. "It's Vessian."

"Vessia," Wren breathed, dropping down from the rigging in a rush, landing beside the chest with the fearlessness of youth and the total absence of sense. "Actual Vessia? From the Flood?"

"Don't touch it."

"I'm not touching it, I'm looking at it. There's a difference—" he caught Kestrel's look, and grinned, and stepped back anyway. "Sorry. Habit."

"It's worth a fortune," Sefa said softly. She had gone still, which for Sefa was the truest sign of awe. "Or it's worth nothing. Or it's worth a hanging. With Vessia, it's always one of the three."

"It's worth nothing," Kestrel said. "Because we're going to put it back."

The deck went quiet. Even the river seemed to hush, the way it does sometimes, listening.

"Put it *back*," Brannick repeated.

"We found nothing. We snagged a log, we cut loose, we move on. That's the story and we tell it to no one, because telling it to anyone is worse than telling it to everyone. We re-sink it, weighted, somewhere the dredge lines won't find it again, and by tomorrow it's a bad memory."

"Captain." Sefa planted herself in front of the chest like a mother hen with a very expensive egg. "That is a season of freedom down there. That's my debts cleared. That's Wren's pilot papers. That's a new engine for Brannick so he stops talking to that one like it's a misbehaving child—"

"She IS a misbehaving child," Brannick said.

"—and you'd throw it back in the river because of a *stamp*?"

"Because of the stamp, yes." Kestrel heard their own voice go hard and practical, and leaned into it, because the alternative was letting anyone see what was actually underneath, which was not pragmatism at all. "Here's what happens to boats that pull Vessian salvage. First, someone talks. Someone always talks—a dockhand, a fence, a drunk crewmate, doesn't matter. Second, the Compact hears. Third, the Compact sends people. Not patrol skiffs—*people*. Quiet ones. And those people don't arrest you, because arrests make records and records make questions. Boats that pull Vessian salvage just... stop arriving. You understand? They become stories. 'Remember the *Marrow Bell*? Nice boat. Wonder what happened to her.' That's the whole fate. One sentence, told in a tavern, ten years from now."

"You can't know that," Wren said.

"I know it better than you'd believe."

It came out with more weight than Kestrel meant it to. Wren blinked. Sefa's eyes narrowed, just slightly, the way they did when a fishmonger quoted a price too smoothly. And Odile, standing at the edge of the group, looked at Kestrel for a long moment with an expression that Kestrel couldn't read at all—which frightened Kestrel more than the seal did.

"Rules are rules," Kestrel said, softer now, pulling it back. "We don't open it. We don't sell it. We don't even look at it too long. Tomorrow, before dawn, it goes back in the river, and the river keeps what it keeps. That's final."

"You said the crew votes," Brannick said carefully.

"On course and cargo, yes. On this, no. This isn't cargo. This is a corpse, and you don't loot corpses, and you especially don't loot *this* corpse." Kestrel turned away before anyone could see their hands. They were steady—they'd trained them to be steady, years ago, in that other life—but inside, everything was moving like floodwater. Because Kestrel knew what lead-lined chests with Vessian seals were *for*. They'd seen the inventory lists, in that other life. Records. Archives. Evidence. Things the Compact had spent twenty years making sure stayed sunk.

And now one was sitting on the deck of the Tern, dripping on the planks, while four people Kestrel loved more than their own name looked at it like a lottery ticket.

* * *

Evening came down gold and slow, and the Tern lay at anchor in a bend thick with willows, and Kestrel stood their usual watch alone at the stern.

Below, the deck lamp burned. Sefa was cooking—rice and river-fish and the pickled things she made that Brannick claimed to hate and always finished. Wren was supposed to be studying his current charts and was instead lying on the hatch cover next to the chest, talking to it, apparently, in a low murmur Kestrel could half-hear. Asking it questions. Boys and their ghosts.

And Odile was sitting beside the chest with her sewing, which she did every evening, except that tonight her needle wasn't moving, and she was looking at the seal the way people look at graves.

Kestrel watched her from the dark of the stern and felt the cold come back.

There were a hundred reasons to order that chest over the side tonight and be done with it. Every one of them was sensible. Every one of them was survivable. And every single one of them was a lie, and Kestrel knew it, because the truth was simpler and much worse: Kestrel didn't want it back in the river. Kestrel wanted it *never found*. There was a difference, and the difference had a name, and the name was twenty years old, and Kestrel carried it the way the chest carried its lead—as ballast, as armor, as proof that some cargoes never stop weighing.

Tomorrow, Kestrel told themselves. Before dawn. Weighted and down. The river keeps what it is owed, and it has been owed this for twenty years, and I am not the one who pays that debt.

Below, Odile finally set down her sewing. She glanced up—toward the stern, toward the dark where Kestrel stood—and for a moment their eyes met across the lamplit deck, navigator and captain, each holding a silence the other could almost hear.

Then Odile reached out and rested her hand, very lightly, on the chest's lock plate.

And left it there.
