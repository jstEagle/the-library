## 3. Crew of the Tern

Brannick Oduya had a theory about engines and families, though he'd only ever said it out loud once, drunk on Sefa's plum brandy, and Wren had laughed so hard he fell off the cargo hatch.

The theory was this: an engine didn't care who you used to be. It cared whether you showed up with clean hands and paid attention. A family, if you were lucky enough to build one out of strangers on a hundred-foot riverboat, worked the same way. Nobody aboard the *Tern* asked anybody where they came from. They just asked whether you'd greased the bearings, and whether you were hungry, and — this was the important part — whether you'd be there tomorrow.

Brannick had been there eleven years. Longest of anyone except Kestrel. He knew every rattle in her hull the way you know the coughs of someone you love.

He was down in the engine room that evening, sleeves rolled to the shoulder, elbow-deep in the port engine's guts, when he heard the footsteps on the ladder — light ones, quick ones, Wren's.

"Brannick. Brannick. Are you dead? Blink twice if you're dead."

"I'm dead," Brannick said, not looking up. "Go tell the captain."

"Can't. She's up top being grim at the river." Wren dropped down onto the grating beside him, sixteen years old and all elbows, his pilot's certification hanging around his neck on a cord like it was a medal from a war nobody had won yet. "I need to ask you something and you have to answer honestly."

"That's a bad way to start a question."

"Did you ever want to be anything else? Besides an engineer?"

Brannick sat back on his heels and considered him. The boy's face was earnest as a sunrise. Somewhere above them, the deck plates ticked as the day's heat left the steel.

"Sure," Brannick said. "When I was your age I wanted to be a surgeon. Cut people open, sew them shut, get thanked for it."

Wren stared. "Really?"

"No. I wanted to be a race-boat pilot. Fastest thing on the Monsoon." He wiped his hands on a rag that was more rag than cloth now. "But my hands are built for torque, not throttle. You get what you're built for, eventually. Sometimes it takes a while to stop arguing about it."

"But you *did* argue about it."

"For years." Brannick pointed the wrench at him. "And then one day I noticed I hadn't thought about racing in a month, because I was too busy keeping a boat alive, and the boat had people on it, and the people were—" He stopped. Hunted for the word. "Mine," he finished. "That's how it gets you. Not with a bang. With a Tuesday."

Wren nodded slowly, absorbing this like scripture. Then: "Kestrel says if I log another two hundred hours on the wheel she'll put me in for my deep-water endorsement. Deep water, Brannick. Past the delta. Ocean charts."

"Ocean charts," Brannick agreed gravely. "Very dangerous. Full of water."

"You know what I mean."

"I know exactly what you mean." And he did. He remembered being young and certain the world past the horizon was where life actually happened, and everything behind you was just the waiting room. He also knew — though he'd never say it to the boy — that the horizon had a way of following you around no matter how far you sailed toward it. "Two hundred hours is nothing. You'll have it by the end of the season."

"By the end of the season we'll be rich," Wren said, with the absolute conviction of youth. "That chest we pulled up. Kestrel won't say what it's worth but Odile looked at it and went quiet, and Odile never goes quiet, she's already quiet—"

"Boy." Brannick kept his voice easy, but he caught the boy's eye. "We don't count money that isn't spent yet. That's rule six."

"There aren't numbered rules."

"Then how would you know I broke one?" He grinned until Wren grinned back, and the moment passed the way moments did on the *Tern* — quickly, warmly, without anyone examining it too closely. That was the trick of the whole operation, Brannick thought. Don't examine things too closely. Keep the bearings greased. Show up tomorrow.

Though lately — since the chest came up out of the mud — showing up tomorrow felt like it had a small weight attached to it. Nothing he could name. Just a change in the air pressure below decks.

* * *

Sefa was cooking, which meant the galley smelled like a promise and the whole aft section of the boat had drifted into a state of involuntary good humor. Brannick climbed up from the engine room with grease to his elbows and followed his nose.

She had the little stove going full talk — three pots, two pans, and a flatbread griddle she'd welded herself out of scrap plate, because Sefa did not believe in buying what you could make and did not believe in making anything ugly. She was a broad, loud woman from the terraced country upriver, with flour on her forearms and a scar through one eyebrow she claimed came from a duel over a recipe.

"Out of my galley," she said, without turning around.

"It's the crew's galley."

"It's *my* galley that the crew is allowed to use. Sit. You look like a man who hasn't eaten since noon."

"I haven't eaten since noon."

"Tragedy. Sit."

He sat at the bolted-down table while she dealt flatbread onto his plate with the efficiency of a card sharp. Up close he could see the ledger open on the shelf by the spice tins — her ledger, the one she thought nobody knew about, the one with columns of numbers in her cramped handwriting. Debts. Money sent upriver to a brother-in-law who bet on dredge races and lost, mostly; a mother's roof that needed replacing before the wet season; a niece's school fees in a town whose name Brannick had heard Sefa say exactly once, softly, like a prayer she didn't believe in anymore.

Eleven years, and Sefa still cooked like a woman paying something off. Maybe that was what debt really was, Brannick thought. Not the numbers. The needing to be useful, fast, before anyone looked too hard at why.

"How's the port engine?" she asked, which was how she said *how are you*.

"She's singing. Slight knock on the third cylinder when she's cold. I'll shim it at Low Water."

"At Low Water we're stopping? Actually stopping?" Sefa turned, wooden spoon raised like a scepter. "Kestrel agreed to stop?"

"Repairs need doing, and the floating market at Corrin's Reach has the shims we need. Captain's being practical about it."

"Practical." Sefa snorted. "Our captain being practical. Next you'll tell me she smiled."

"She smiled once. In '19. There were witnesses."

"Wren claims it was gas."

"Wren wasn't even aboard in '19."

"He has theories." Sefa laughed, big and unguarded, and for a second the ledger on the shelf might as well not have existed. Then her eyes flicked to it — just a flicker, half a heartbeat — and Brannick watched her turn back to the stove and become busy again. The small lie, performed nightly: *everything is fine, I owe nothing to anyone, feed them and they'll never ask.*

He didn't ask. Rule six, or whatever number it was. But he slid his plate back for more bread, because the best way to love Sefa was to let her overfeed you, and the best way to protect her was to eat like her cooking was working.

* * *

Odile was at the chart table in the pilothouse when Brannick brought her supper, because somebody always brought Odile her supper, because if you didn't she'd forget to eat and navigate on an empty stomach and get strange. She had the river charts spread out under the lamp, correcting them — actually correcting them, in fine pencil, shifting a sandbar mark by half a cable with the confidence of a woman who had personally argued with that sandbar and won.

"Port side channel's moved again," she said, by way of hello. "After the rains. Tell the captain."

"Tell her yourself."

"She's being grim at the river."

"So I hear." Brannick set the plate down clear of the charts. Odile murmured thanks without looking up. She was maybe fifty, gray coming into her black hair, hands steady as a spirit level. She'd been aboard four years — walked on at a lock with one bag and a fee paid in old-mint coin, and Kestrel had taken one look at her and hired her before she'd finished asking. Brannick had never understood that. Still didn't. Kestrel trusted nobody that fast. Except apparently the quiet navigator with the accent.

Because that was the thing about Odile. Four years, and she'd never once said where she was from, but she couldn't hide it in her vowels. Soft edges where the river speech had hard ones. A lilt that rose at the ends of sentences like a question she refused to ask out loud. Vessian. Anybody who'd traded downriver knew a Vessian accent the way you knew weather — twenty years after the flood, the accent was everywhere and nowhere, worn by refugees and their children scattered across five nations, and most people had learned, politely, brutally, not to mention it.

Nobody aboard mentioned it. That was the deal none of them had ever spoken aloud.

"Odile," Brannick said, settling against the rail of the chart table. "Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask. I may not answer. Those are the traditional terms."

"Fair terms." He scratched his jaw. "The chest. When we pulled it up, you looked at the seal and you went white as sailcloth. And now you've been down to the hold twice today, alone, just to stand near it. I'm not asking what's in it. I'm asking if we should be worried."

Odile's pencil stopped moving.

For a long moment the only sound was the river against the hull and the lamp's faint buzz. Then she set the pencil down, aligned it precisely parallel to the chart edge, and looked at him with those level dark eyes.

"You're a good man, Brannick Oduya," she said, the accent thickening slightly the way it did when she was tired or careful. "You bring me food. You fix what's broken. You don't ask questions you don't want the answers to." She picked the pencil back up. "Keep being that man a little longer. That's my answer."

"And if the answers come find us anyway?"

"Then," Odile said quietly, returning to her sandbar, "we will finally stop lying to each other, and it will be a great relief to us all."

It was the closest thing to a confession Brannick had gotten out of her in four years, and it scared him more than any silence had.

* * *

He found Kestrel at the stern rail after supper, as expected, being grim at the river. The Monsoon ran wide and brown-gold in the last light, carrying its usual cargo of silt, secrets, and other people's mistakes. Kestrel stood with their forearms on the rail, cap pulled low, watching the water like it owed them money.

Brannick leaned on the rail beside them. Neither spoke for a while. That was the luxury of eleven years — silence you didn't have to fill.

"Sefa fed me twice," Brannick said eventually. "Wren wants ocean charts. Odile corrected the charts and told me to keep not asking questions. Standard day."

"Mm."

"The chest, Kess."

"What about it."

"It's got the whole boat walking on eggshells and pretending we're not. Sefa's counting money she doesn't have. Wren's planning retirements. Odile's visiting the hold like it's a shrine. And you've ordered it sealed and chained, which means you know something, which means you've known something since the day we pulled it up." He turned to look at them. "I'm not asking what. I'm asking: are we okay? Boat-wise. Family-wise."

Kestrel was quiet long enough that a kingfisher went past low over the water, blue and gone.

"We're okay," they said at last. "Boat-wise."

"Family-wise?"

Kestrel's jaw worked. Whatever lived behind it had teeth. "Family-wise," they said, "everyone aboard is exactly who I think they are. That's more than most boats can say."

Which was, Brannick noted, not an answer at all — it was a deflection wearing an answer's clothes, and he'd known Kestrel long enough to recognize the stitching. Everyone aboard was exactly who Kestrel thought they were. Well. He thought about Sefa's ledger. About Wren's horizon-hunger. About Odile's pencil, aligned so carefully, and the white look when the seal came up dripping out of the mud.

Everyone aboard was exactly who they seemed, right up until they weren't. Every one of them carried a locked room somewhere behind the ribs. His own included. That was the price of admission to the *Tern*: you got a family, and you got to keep your secrets, and everyone agreed — without ever saying it — not to test the locks.

"Captain!" Wren's voice cracked down from the crow's nest, high and bright and wrong. "Smoke! Two points off the bow — steam smoke, funnel smoke, not cooking smoke!"

Kestrel straightened like a wire snapping taut. Brannick watched them lift the glass, watch their shoulders change.

"Compact colors," Kestrel said quietly. "Patrol skiff. Running a sweep line." They lowered the glass, and for a heartbeat Brannick saw something cross their face that he'd never seen there in eleven years — not fear exactly, but its older, harder cousin.

"Get below and keep our girl singing quiet," Kestrel said. "And Brannick — whatever happens next, remember rule six."

"We don't have numbered rules."

"Then how would you know I'm breaking one?" And Kestrel turned for the pilothouse, calling for Odile, as the first smudge of Compact smoke crawled up over the bend of the river.
