## 4. Six Tides

The morning the Compact found them, Kestrel was teaching Wren how to cheat at cards.

"Not cheating," Kestrel corrected, for the third time, sliding a card out of their sleeve with the smoothness of long practice. "Improvising. Cheating is when you get caught."

"You're getting caught right now," Wren said, pointing at the card.

"That's called confidence, boy. The mark sees the card and thinks, *surely no one would be so bold.*"

"Surely someone would," said Odile, without looking up from her charts. She sat at the corner table as she always did, a cup of tea going cold beside her, her pencil moving in small precise ticks across the river survey. "You're being so bold at this very moment."

Kestrel tucked the card away and dealt properly, which was its own kind of concession. The *Tern* ran quiet that morning, riding the slow brown current two days out of Sefa's floating market, the mist still burning off the water in long silver rags. It was good running weather. Good weather on the Monsoon meant everyone else was also on the water, which was the price of good weather, but Kestrel had learned to take what the river gave and not itemize the cost until later.

Sefa came up from the galley with a tray of fried dough and the expression of a man who had counted his coins twice and disliked both totals.

"Debt collectors," he announced, setting the tray down like a verdict. "Two letters waiting at the last lock. Both of them polite. That's how you know they're serious."

"Polite debt collectors," Brannick rumbled from the engine hatch, where he was elbow-deep in the port pump with grease to his shoulders. "River's full of miracles this season."

"They quoted scripture, Brannick. About debts."

"Which testament?"

"The one about interest."

Wren laughed, then caught himself, because laughing at Sefa's debts had once gotten him a ladle across the knuckles, and some lessons stay learned. But Sefa only sighed and took a piece of his own fried dough, which was how everyone knew it was bad news. When the cooking was good, Sefa didn't eat standing up. He ate standing up when the world required fortifying.

"We'll make the grain run," Kestrel said. "Two more hauls like this one and your creditors can quote whatever they like to an empty dock."

It was the kind of thing captains said. Kestrel said it with enough weight that it almost felt true, and the crew, who were kind in the specific ways that mattered, let it feel true for the length of the breakfast.

That was when Wren went stiff at the rail.

"Sail," he said. Then, correcting himself the way he always did, because the *Tern* was a steamboat and dignity mattered: "Smoke. Two points off the stern quarter. Coming fast."

Kestrel set down the cards.

They all did — Odile's pencil stopped mid-tick, Sefa's hand paused over the dough, even Brannick's clanking from below went briefly silent, the way a ship holds its breath when its people do. Two days of peace had made them soft. Kestrel felt the old coldness come up through their boots, the captain's coldness, the one that lived somewhere below the warmth and only climbed when it was needed.

"Glass," they said.

Wren already had it. He was fifteen and quick as a fish and he'd been up the mast before Kestrel finished standing. "Skiff. Grey hull. Flying—" A pause. "Crane-and-chain."

Compact.

* * *

The patrol skiff was a sleek, mean little vessel, iron-bowed, built by people who had never once needed to love what they floated on. It came alongside at a respectful distance and a voice crackled across the water through a speaking-trumpet, official and bored in equal measure.

"Steamer *Tern*, out of the lower locks. This is the Salvage Compact patrol vessel *Diligent*. Heave to for inspection."

Kestrel stood at the rail with their hands visible and their face arranged into the particular blankness of a working captain mildly inconvenienced. Behind them, in the mirror of the cabin glass, they could see the crew doing exactly what they'd drilled: Sefa vanishing below with the strongbox, Odile closing her charts, Brannick killing the engine to a lazy idle, Wren stationed at the rail looking sixteen parts innocent and four parts awed, because Wren still hadn't learned to look at the Compact without something like reverence, and Kestrel hadn't yet found a way to burn that out of him gently.

"Morning, officer," Kestrel called back. "What's the occasion? We're a grain boat, not a treasure galleon."

The skiff drifted closer. There were four Compact officers aboard, grey uniforms, grey faces, one of them holding a ledger open against the wind. The one with the trumpet was young and had the look of a man who enjoyed the trumpet more than the work.

"Salvage recovery operation," he said. "A registered wreck site downstream has reported missing property. All vessels on this reach are subject to inspection."

"A wreck site." Kestrel scratched their jaw, buying time, thinking fast and showing none of it. "Which site? There's more wrecks on this stretch than fish."

The young officer consulted his ledger with the solemnity of a priest. "Site designation V-9."

Something went very still in Kestrel's chest, behind the ribs, where they kept the things they never looked at directly.

V-9. They knew the designation system. Everyone on the river knew it, vaguely, the way you know the name of a disease you hope never visits. V for the old drowned country. The Compact had spent twenty years picking over Vessia's bones — dredging the flooded towns, hauling up doorframes and church bells and the small drowned possessions of a nation, stamping each piece crane-and-chain and selling grief by the ton. Officially it was *recovery of unclaimed assets*. Unofficially, everyone called it grave-robbing with paperwork.

"V-9," Kestrel repeated, letting a little boredom bleed into it. "The old ferry terminal? We passed that yesterday. Nothing there but mud and eels."

"There was considerably more than that," the officer said, and now the boredom in his voice had thinned. "A sealed cargo manifest was recovered from the site records. Substantial contents. When our recovery team arrived, the primary vault had been disturbed. Dredge lines don't lie, Captain. Someone's been dragging that site within the week, and the drag marks lead upstream."

He let that sit on the water between them.

"So," Kestrel said slowly, "you think somebody pulled something out of a flooded building, and you're stopping every boat on the river to ask if we've seen it."

"I'm asking to inspect your hold."

"And I'm telling you my hold is full of millet, salt pork, and Sefa's regrettable attempt at pickled cabbage, which I'd classify as contraband on any day ending in Y." Kestrel spread their hands. "But sure. Come aboard. Waste your morning. Though I'll note for the record that we're a day and a half upstream of your site, and whatever your divers lost, it isn't swimming."

The officer studied them. Kestrel held the study, easy as breathing, the way you hold a heavy load on your shoulder — perfectly still, perfectly casual, all the strain hidden in places nobody checks.

It was Odile who saved them, though neither the officer nor, at first, Kestrel realized it.

"Captain," she said from behind, in her careful, faintly accented voice, "the tide table. If we're delayed past the turn, we lose the window at the shallows." She stepped up beside Kestrel holding her chart roll, and she held it slightly open, and Kestrel saw — only saw, because they were watching for it — that the top chart was not the shallows at all but a survey of a completely different reach, one they weren't remotely bound for.

The officer glanced at it. Charts were boring. Boring was good.

"Shallows," he said, and something in his manner shifted, the way it does when a man realizes he might be talking to professionals rather than smugglers. "You run the sandbars?"

"When the river lets us," Kestrel said modestly.

"No Compact pilot will take that reach." He said it with distaste, as though the sandbars had failed some professional obligation. "If you're bound through the Narrows, you're either fools or the best hull on this water."

"Both, usually."

The officer almost smiled. Then the ledger-man behind him murmured something, and the smile died, and the officer straightened. "We'll log your passage. But understand the situation, Captain, since you seem a reasonable person." He tapped the ledger. "The fleet is running a search grid over this whole reach. Six tides remaining in the sweep pattern. After that, the grid moves upstream." He looked at Kestrel, and for just a moment the boredom fell away entirely, and underneath it was something harder and older and much less comfortable. "Upstream includes the Narrows. Whatever was taken from V-9 is worth enough that the Commodore himself has taken personal interest. Six tides, Captain. If it surfaces inside that grid, we find it. And whoever's carrying it."

"Six tides," Kestrel repeated.

"Then you're free to run. Mind the second bar past the burnt mill — she's shifted since the spring flood." The trumpet lowered. The skiff peeled away, iron bow throwing a small insolent wake, and in ten minutes it was a smudge of smoke to the south, and then nothing.

Kestrel watched it go for longer than was strictly necessary.

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse, which was where the *Tern* held its parliaments — cramped, smelling of engine oil and tea, with the wheel ticking gently under Kestrel's hand and the whole crew crowded in like sardines who'd elected a spokesperson.

"Six tides," Brannick said. He'd come up with the grease still on him, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up its job years ago. "That's three days, near enough, if the tide runs honest."

"Tide doesn't run honest in the Narrows," Odile said quietly. "Four days. Maybe five, if the fleet is cautious."

"Fleets aren't cautious," Kestrel said. "Fleets are confident. That's worse."

"So they're looking for whatever we've got lashed under the tarp in the forward hold," Sefa said. He said it flatly, without accusation, which was somehow heavier than accusation. "The chest we agreed — *Kestrel agreed* — to leave sealed and unopened and sell to the first fence with deep pockets and shallow curiosity."

"I remember the agreement," Kestrel said. "I wrote it."

"Then you'll recall the part where we assumed nobody would miss it."

"Nobody misses a chest, Sefa. Nations miss chests." Kestrel turned the wheel a spoke, unnecessary, steadying. "There was a manifest. Sealed cargo, registered site. Which means somebody upstairs knew what was in that vault before it ever went into the water, and somebody downstairs wanted it badly enough to report it missing, and both somebodies wear grey."

Wren had gone pale in the corner. "The commodore. The officer said the commodore took personal interest. Commodore Ashvael?" His voice did the thing it did around the Compact's great names, the little lift of awe that Kestrel kept meaning to correct and never found a gentle enough way to.

"The same," Kestrel said. "Which tells you the chest isn't cargo. Cargo gets a lieutenant. Whatever's in that box, Ashvael wants it found before anyone else reads it."

Silence, except the wheel ticking and the river slapping the hull in its patient, ancient way.

Odile stood by the chart table with her hands folded, and Kestrel watched her, because Kestrel had learned to watch Odile the way you watch a barometer — not for what she said, but for the pressure behind it. Her face was composed. Her hands were white at the knuckles.

"Vessia," Wren said, hushed. "The chest has the Vessian seal. Is that why—"

"It's why the Compact cares," Kestrel cut in, smoothly, before the sentence could go anywhere else. "Twenty years of dredging that country and they've never once cared about a single box. Now they care. So the box matters, and we are currently the only people on the Monsoon who know where it sleeps." They looked around the wheelhouse, at each of them in turn — Brannick solid as a bollard, Sefa worried and brave about it, Wren trying to understand a game whose rules kept changing, Odile unreadable as deep water. "Here's the arithmetic. Six tides until the grid reaches the Narrows. Our route runs through the Narrows. We cannot outrun a fleet, we cannot outfight a fleet, and we cannot give back what we've already pulled out of the mud, because the mud doesn't do refunds."

"So we sell it fast," Sefa said. "Before the grid closes. A fence, a fortune, and we scatter like the plan always said."

"Or," Brannick said, and stopped, because Brannick started sentences like that and rarely finished them, and everyone waited anyway, because when Brannick finished one it mattered. "Or we finally open the damn thing and find out what's worth a commodore's signature."

"No," Kestrel said.

It came out faster and harder than intended. The wheelhouse went quiet in a new way.

"Sealed stays sealed," Kestrel said, gentler now, the captain smoothing the water behind the boat. "Not because I don't trust this crew. Because everything that's gone wrong since we hooked that chest has come from knowing too much, and the safest place for all of us is ignorance with a price tag. We sell it. We vanish. That's the whole plan, and it's a good plan, and plans die when people get curious."

"Curiosity keeps boats afloat," Odile said softly. "You've said so yourself, Captain. You read the river because you can't help it."

"I read the river because it tries to kill me weekly and I prefer to see it coming."

"Same instinct," she said, and there was something in her eyes then, brief and submerged, that Kestrel couldn't name and didn't try to. She rolled her charts closed. "I'll rework the route tonight. If we make the Narrows on the third tide instead of the fourth, we're through the grid before it forms."

"Do it," Kestrel said. "And Odile—" She paused at the door. "Good work with the chart. Quick thinking."

"Yes," Odile said. "Quick thinking."

She left. Sefa followed, muttering about pickled cabbage and creditors, and Brannick squeezed Kestrel's shoulder once on the way out, which from Brannick was a paragraph.

Wren lingered.

"Captain," he said, when they were alone with the wheel and the water. "Six tides. Do you think they'll really find it?"

Kestrel looked out at the brown river, at the mist burning off toward noon, at the wide patient water that had already swallowed one entire country and kept its mouth shut about it.

"Wren," they said, "the Compact always finds what it's looking for. The question that should keep you up nights is what happens after." They ruffled the boy's hair, rough and affectionate. "Go on. Up the mast. Eyes open, every smoke on the horizon. From today, we count tides."

Wren scrambled up the rigging, and Kestrel stood alone at the wheel, and beneath the deck plates, lashed under tarp and chain in the forward hold, the lead-lined chest sat in the dark with its drowned country's seal, keeping its own counsel.

One tide gone. Five left.

And in the chart room behind them, Odile unrolled her charts again, and did not work on any of them.
