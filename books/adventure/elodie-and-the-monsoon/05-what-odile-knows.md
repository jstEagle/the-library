## 5. What Odile Knows

Odile had never been able to sleep on a boat that sat still.

It was an old habit from the war years, though she never called them the war years aloud—on the *Tern*, nobody did. The boat at anchor creaked differently than a boat under way. Under way she was a living thing, complaining and conversational, all her joints talking to each other. At anchor she was quiet in the wrong way, the way a house is quiet when someone has just left it.

So Odile climbed out of her bunk at the hour when even Wren stopped dreaming about steamships, took the lantern down from its hook by the galley, and went to sit with the chest.

She told herself it was insomnia. She was very good at telling herself things. Twenty years of practice.

The chest sat amidships under an oilcloth, where Kestrel had ordered it stowed and *sealed*—Kestrel's word, delivered in that flat captain's voice that meant the discussion was over before it started. *We don't open other people's graves.* Which was a strange thing to say about salvage, Odile had thought at the time. Salvage was exactly that: other people's graves, picked clean and sold by the pound. Everyone on the river lived off drowned things. They simply didn't usually say so.

But Kestrel had said it with a face like wet slate, and Brannick—who could read Kestrel's moods the way Wren read water—had backed the order without a word. So the chest sat, lead-lined and patient, wearing Vessia's seal like a wound that hadn't decided whether to bleed.

Odile knelt beside it.

The seal was a bridge over three waves, struck into lead with a stamp that had been old when her grandmother was young. She had seen it last on the door of the tribunal hall in Corvel-sur-Marne, on the day the water came up the steps two at a time and the clerks were still carrying ledgers to higher floors because nobody in authority would say the word *evacuate*. Bureaucracies drown politely. Her mother had gone back for the deed to the house. The house was worth less than the walk.

"You're not supposed to be open," she told the chest, in Vessian. It was a soft language, full of shushing sounds, a language built for libraries and lullabies. "Neither am I."

Her hands were already working the hasps.

* * *

The lock was Compact-issue, which meant it was built to keep honest scavengers out and let anyone with a government education in. Odile had a government education. Three years at the hydrographic academy in Corvel before the academy became a reef. She picked it in ninety seconds and felt, absurdly, a flicker of professional pride, as if somewhere her old instructors were nodding.

Inside, under a skin of river silt and one very indignant crayfish—which she relocated to the scuppers with an apology—the chest held paper.

Not coin. Not plate. Not the brass instruments and silverware that made salvage runs worth the dredge lines. Paper, wrapped in waxed canvas, dozens of packets of it, and paper was the one cargo no salvager wanted because paper rotted, burned, and testified.

She unwrapped the first packet with hands that had begun, without her permission, to shake.

Manifests. Shipping manifests, Vessian customs stamps, dates in the last season before the flood. Grain shipments moving *out* of the lowland provinces in quantities that made her frown—out, when everyone knew the granaries had flooded full. Machinery manifests. Cement, by the barge-load, consigned to the upper dam works. And fuel oil, hundreds of tons of it, delivered to floodgate stations that ran on water pressure and needed fuel for nothing except—

Except pumps. Emergency pumps. If you had pumps, you had something you expected to need pumping.

Or something you expected to need flooding.

"No," she said quietly, in the language with the shushing sounds, as if the word worked better there. As if saying it softly kept it small.

The letters came next. She read them by lantern light while the *Tern* rocked gently on her anchor chain and the river breathed against the hull, and the letters were worse than the manifests, because manifests were arithmetic and letters were people. A district engineer writing to his ministry, polite as a knife wrapped in linen, asking—in the third letter, the politeness gone—to know why the seasonal gate schedule for the Marne basin had been altered without hydrographic review. A reply from the ministry, stamped and unsigned, informing him that the alteration fell under Salvage Compact jurisdiction per the Reconstitution Accords and that further inquiry would be noted in his file.

*Noted in his file.* Odile almost laughed. The Compact's whole grammar of menace fit in four words. They'd threatened her with her file once, in another life, through a man with a clipboard and kind eyes.

The last packet was thin. A single folded sheet, and when she opened it she saw it was a roster—a list of names, titles, postings. *Hydraulic Engineering Corps, Gate Works Division.* Fourteen names. The men and women who had designed, built, and operated the floodgates of the Marne basin.

She read down the list the way you read a passenger manifest after a wreck, hoping and dreading in equal measure.

Halfway down, in the column of senior gate engineers, second from the bottom:

**VAILLANT, ÉTIENNE — Chief Engineer, North Gates, Corvel Station.**

The lantern flame stood perfectly still. The river seemed to hold its breath around the hull. Odile sat back on her heels and looked at her brother's name for a long time.

Étienne. Who had taught her to pick locks, actually—she'd forgotten that, or filed it away so deep she'd mistaken it for instinct. Who used to bring home misprinted charts and fold them into paper boats for her, sailing them down the rain gutter, narrating their voyages in a captain's voice until their mother shouted at them both to come inside. Who had written her one letter from the north station, six weeks before the flood, that she had never understood until this exact moment:

*I may have to make a stand about something, little fish. If I do, remember that gates are only ever as honest as the men who schedule them.*

She had thought he meant corruption. Skimmed budgets, falsified inspections, the ordinary rot. Everyone knew the Gate Works skimmed; it was practically a municipal service.

He hadn't meant rot. He'd meant murder, dressed in schedules.

And then Étienne Vaillant had died in the flood along with forty thousand others, and the record showed him as a hero of the disaster response, posthumously commended by the very ministry whose unsigned stamp sat in her hand, and Odile had carried his commendation certificate out of Corvel in a waterproof sleeve like everyone else carried photographs, and for twenty years it had been the one clean thing she owned.

They had given him a medal for drowning.

The sound she made wasn't quite a laugh and wasn't anything else, and she clapped a hand over her mouth because Sefa slept ten feet aft and snored like a friendly landslide, and if Sefa woke, Sefa would ask, and Sefa's kindness was a tide you could drown in.

* * *

She didn't know how long she sat there. Long enough that the lantern needed trimming. Long enough that her knees ached and she realized she'd been rocking slightly, the way she had as a child, a self-soothing motion her mother had called *the little tide.*

Footsteps on the deck above—Brannick, doing his midnight round of the engine room, humming. That was the thing about Brannick: he hummed when he was content, and he was nearly always content, and his contentment was load-bearing. The whole crew rested on it. Odile included herself in that thought carefully, precisely, the way you test ice.

Because here was the crack, and she could feel it running:

If the Compact had altered the gate schedules—if the flood was engineering, if Étienne had tried to stop it and been silenced—then the papers in her lap were not salvage. They were not cargo. They were the only surviving testimony of a murdered country, and she was sitting on them in the belly of a boat captained by a person who had taken one look at the Vessian seal and gone the color of old ice.

Kestrel knew something. Kestrel had known it the moment the crane brought the chest dripping over the rail, and whatever Kestrel knew had made them order it sealed, unopened, unsold—which was either conscience or fear, and Odile had spent twenty years learning that the two wore the same coat.

And Odile herself—what was she going to do? Hand them over? *Here, Captain, the proof that my country was drowned on purpose, and oh, incidentally, my brother's name is on the engineers' roster, and also I've been lying about where my accent comes from since the day I signed aboard?*

She had told the crew she was from upriver. Some vague delta province, accents blur together, don't they, doesn't yours? Wren had believed it because Wren believed everything said confidently. Sefa had believed it because Sefa believed everything said sadly. Brannick had believed it because Brannick believed the best of people as a matter of engineering principle. And Kestrel—

Kestrel had looked at her for one long moment on the day she signed aboard, that assessing river-gray look, and said, "Delta, is it," in a tone that had neither question mark nor period, and Odile had understood that Kestrel knew, and that knowing was a service Kestrel provided silently, like ballast, and asking was a debt Kestrel never collected.

That was the loyalty. That was what she was cracking: not a rule, not a contract. An arrangement of mercies, unspoken, holding a found family level in the water.

And now the chest sat between her and that arrangement like a reef between a pilot and her channel.

Because if she gave the papers to Kestrel, Kestrel would do the pragmatic thing. Kestrel always did the pragmatic thing—that was the whole architecture of the woman, pragmatism laid over something else like armor plate over a boiler. The pragmatic thing was to sell the chest sealed to some fence who didn't read Vessian, take the fortune, scatter the crew to safe harbors, and let forty thousand dead stay officially accidental forever.

Or Kestrel would do the other thing. The thing with the bounty on their old name. The thing that ended with iron-hulled ships and crane-and-chain flags and a short drop at a Compact dock.

Either way, the *Tern* as Odile knew her—Sefa's kitchen, Wren's chart-dreams, Brannick's humming, the small lies stacked like sandbags—either way, that boat sank.

Unless.

Unless the papers simply never surfaced. Unless Odile, who had a government education and a lockpick habit and the night watch nobody questioned, kept them. Read them. Learned what Étienne had learned, finished what Étienne had started—but quietly, safely, in ways that touched no one aboard. She could copy pages. She could leave the originals hidden and pass information piecemeal, someday, somehow, to someone. There were still Vessians on the river. Ferrywomen. Lock-keepers. People who lit candles on the anniversary and called it a custom, not a mourning, because mourning attracted attention.

She could carry it alone. She'd been carrying it alone for twenty years. One more weight, properly stowed, wouldn't change the trim.

"That's a lie," she informed the chest, in Vessian, softly. "You know that's a lie."

The chest, being lead-lined, declined to answer.

* * *

She worked fast after that, the way grief makes you efficient. She sorted the packets by lamplight: manifests, correspondence, the roster. She found, near the bottom, a slim ledger bound in gray board that made her breath catch—gate maintenance logs, Corvel Station, final season, and in the margin of the October entries, in a handwriting she would have known blindfolded, upside down, underwater: *E.V.*, noting pressure anomalies, noting schedule deviations, noting—and here her brother's pen pressed hard enough to emboss the next page—*gates will not hold scheduled cycle. Requesting emergency review. This is not weather.*

*This is not weather.* Four words in her brother's tidy engineer's hand, and Odile pressed the ledger to her sternum and let herself, finally, briefly, silently, come apart—shoulders shaking, tears cutting channels through river grime, the little tide rocking her—because Étienne had known, had *documented*, had begged the machinery of the state to look at what it was doing, and the machinery had noted it in his file, and then the water had come up the steps two at a time.

When it passed, she wiped her face on her sleeve, blew her nose with a boatswain's lack of dignity, and got practical.

She took the ledger, the roster, and the three most damning letters, and wrapped them in the waxed canvas. The rest—the bulk of it—she rewrapped and repacked exactly as found, right down to resettling the silt and leaving the lock turned to the position she'd found it. Then she went forward to the chain locker, moved two spare hawsers, and set the wrapped bundle into the hollow beneath the anchor chain bed, where the smell of hemp and rust would cover paper and no one but a navigator ever went looking.

She was rebanking the hawser when the deck above her creaked.

Not Brannick's tread—Brannick walked like a man apologizing to the planks. This was the other tread, the one she knew best of all: light, deliberate, placed like a pilothouse course. Kestrel kept night watches nobody assigned, prowling the deck the way other people paced sickrooms.

Odile went still among the hawsers, heart tapping against her ribs like a signal lamp.

Above her, footsteps crossed to midships. Paused at the chest. A long silence—the particular silence of someone checking a seal, or thinking about checking a seal. Then the footsteps resumed, unhurried, toward the stern, and a match flared faintly through the deck seams as Kestrel settled into the dark by the wheel to smoke and keep whatever counsel captains keep at three in the morning.

Odile let out her breath by degrees. The bundle lay under the anchor chain, warm from her hands, patient as sediment.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would find a way to look at Kestrel across Sefa's breakfast and lie with her whole calm face, the way she'd done for twenty years, the way the whole crew did, kindly, daily, in the name of belonging. Tomorrow she would begin carrying the heaviest cargo the *Tern* had ever shipped, and no one would weigh it, and no one would know.

She was halfway up the locker ladder when she heard Kestrel speak from the darkness by the wheel—not loudly, not accusingly, just a voice pitched across a quiet deck like a stone skipped to land at your feet:

"Couldn't sleep either?"

Odile closed her eyes. In the chain locker below, the anchor chain hung directly over the place where her brother's last words slept under waxed canvas, and the tide of everything unsaid rose in her throat.

"No," she called back, keeping her voice easy, keeping her accent delta-flat. "Bad anchorage. Boat talks too much."

A pause. The ember of Kestrel's cigarette glowed, dimmed.

"Mm," said Kestrel. "Funny. She's been quiet as a grave all night."
