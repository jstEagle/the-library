## 6. The Salvage Fleet

Wren had the dawn watch, which was the best watch and everyone knew it, even if Sefa claimed the mid-morning watch was best because that was when breakfast happened. Wren disagreed. Dawn was when the river did its tricks.

The Monsoon at first light turned everything to copper and rumor. Mist sat on the water in long gray ribbons, and the sandbars moved overnight the way they always did—slyly, without apology—and if you watched carefully from the crow's nest you could see where the current lied about itself. A smooth stretch of river that looked deep enough to float a cathedral might be six inches of water over a bar that would peel the *Tern*'s hull open like an orange. Wren had learned to read the lies. He wasn't as good at it as Kestrel, who could apparently smell a sandbar through solid fog, but he was better than he'd been a year ago, and every morning he climbed the ratlines two rungs faster than the morning before.

Today he reached the nest in record time, hauled himself over the rail, and immediately saw the thing that would ruin his whole week.

Three ships on the horizon. Iron-hulled. Black smoke standing up from their stacks in straight, disciplined columns, like soldiers at attention.

Wren's heart did something complicated.

He knew those silhouettes. Every kid who'd grown up on the river knew them, the way farm kids knew the shapes of the tax wagons. Salvage cruisers. Compact fleet. And not the little patrol skiffs that buzzed around like angry wasps checking manifests—these were the big ones, the dredge-and-crane ships, the ones with the crane-and-chain painted on their bows in white paint so thick it probably cost more than the *Tern* entire.

"Captain," Wren called down, and his voice came out steadier than he felt, which he counted as a professional victory. "Traffic. Three points off the port bow. Heavy traffic."

There was a pause below him, the particular quality of silence that meant Kestrel had stopped whatever they were doing.

"Heavy how?"

"Iron." Wren put the glass to his eye. "Three of them. Cruisers. Crane-and-chain on the bows."

Another silence. This one had a temperature to it.

"Wren," Kestrel said, in the voice they used for things like *we are about to have a problem*, "come down here a moment."

Wren came down the ratlines in half the time it took to go up, because fear is excellent for speed, and found the whole crew already gathered in the pilothouse like a jury. Brannick had oil on his hands and a wrench tucked in his belt out of pure habit. Sefa stood with her arms crossed, still wearing her apron, which meant she'd abandoned eggs mid-cook, which meant this was serious, because Sefa did not abandon eggs. Odile stood by the chart table, very still, the way she got sometimes, like a heron deciding whether the pond was safe.

Kestrel took the glass without a word and studied the horizon for a long time.

"Sweep grid," they said finally. "They're running a search pattern. Look at their spacing—even, methodical. They're not chasing anything. They're *combing*."

"Combing for what?" Sefa asked.

Kestrel lowered the glass. Their eyes went, just for a heartbeat, to the hold. To the chest. Then away again, quick as a card sharp's glance.

"Missing salvage," they said. "Reported from a wreck site. That's all we know, and it's all we need to know. We stay on our route, we fly no colors worth noticing, and we mind our own business until their business goes somewhere else."

"Those are cruisers," Brannick said. "I've only ever seen one from a dock. It had a crane on it that could lift our whole boat like a duckling."

"Yes," said Kestrel. "So let's be something they don't feel like lifting."

---

Here was the thing about Wren, the thing he'd never quite managed to say out loud to any of them: he *loved* the Compact.

Not in a casual way. Not the way you loved a good harvest or a lucky find. He loved it the way other kids loved saints. When he was small, living on his aunt's leaky mooring barge outside Corrin's Landing, the Compact salvage fleet had come through twice a year, huge and gleaming, and the whole town had turned out on the banks to watch. The cruisers kept the shipping lanes open. They ran the locks on schedule—*on schedule*, a phrase that had sounded like magic to a boy whose whole life ran on maybe. They posted the river charts, free, on every customs house wall, corrected monthly. They pulled drowned boats off the bars and returned bodies to their families wrapped in clean canvas, and there was a ceremony, an actual ceremony, with a bell rung nine times.

Order. That was the word Wren had carried around inside him since he was eight years old. The river was chaos—it ate boats, it moved its own floor, it drowned your father and didn't even leave you anything to bury—but the Compact was the answer to the river. Rules. Charts. Bells rung nine times. Wren had told anyone who'd listen that one day he'd wear the chain-and-crane himself, pilot one of those iron beauties, keep the lanes open for honest folk.

His crewmates had mostly learned to be kind about it. Sefa called it "the boy's religion" and left it alone, which from Sefa was a form of tenderness. Brannick once spent an entire evening patiently explaining that institutions were made of people and people were made of flaws, and Wren had nodded along and believed approximately none of it. Even Kestrel, who visibly flinched whenever the Compact came up, had never mocked him directly. They'd just said, once, quietly, *keep your eyes open, kid,* in a tone Wren hadn't understood yet.

He understood the Compact was hunting them now. Fine. He could hold two ideas at once—he was a pilot, that was basically the job. The Compact was good, and the Compact was looking for them, and both things were true, and the second one was temporary while the first one was permanent. That was just sense.

So when Kestrel ordered him back up the mast to keep eyes on the fleet, Wren went up feeling something he was almost embarrassed to name, even alone in the crow's nest with nobody to hear his thoughts.

He felt glad to see them.

---

The fleet grew all morning. By noon Wren could make out details without the glass: the lead cruiser's name plate, *CS Meridian*, letters tall as sails; the working cranes folded flat along the decks; the ranks of crewmen at the rails in gray uniforms, moving with that crisp economy Wren had dreamed about. Behind the three cruisers came a string of barges, low in the water, loaded with salvage—chain, plating, the ribs of some poor dead vessel stacked like firewood.

And behind the barges, smaller, trailing like a dog that hadn't been invited: a scavenger raft.

Wren had grown up around scavenger rafts. Half the river lived on them—families lashed together on pontoon decks, drifting the margins, diving wrecks the Compact hadn't gotten to yet or didn't bother with. His aunt had called them "the river's crumbs," not unkindly. They were raggedy, sure. They fished the same waters as everybody. But they were *river people*, and river people looked out for each other, mostly, when it was convenient, and often when it wasn't.

The raft was flying a signal flag. Wren squinted. Distress colors—or something close to it. Hailing colors, anyway. The raft was coming alongside the last barge, and figures on it were waving, holding up baskets, holding up—

Holding up salvage. Small stuff. Brass fittings, a coil of wire, things glinting. They were showing it to the Compact sailors. Trading, maybe. Or surrendering it. Either way, ordinary. River business.

Wren watched, and the morning went strange.

A launch dropped from the *Meridian*'s side. Just one boat, six oars, a man in the stern with something in his hands. It cut across toward the raft with terrible efficiency, no wasted motion, and Wren felt a flicker of pride even then—look how well they rowed, look how *orderly*—before the flicker curdled into confusion, because the launch wasn't slowing down.

It didn't come alongside. It came *past*. Close enough that Wren could see the faces on the raft turning, see a woman lift a child up to see better, see the man in the stern stand and swing his arm in a long arc.

Something arced out of his hand, glittering, and broke against the raft's deck.

Flame went up like a flower opening.

Wren's mouth was open. He couldn't remember opening it. The fire caught fast—the raft was wood and rope and tar and old cloth, everything a raft is, everything a raft has to be—and the wind off the cruisers' wake pushed it along the deck, and the people on the raft were screaming now, high thin sounds that crossed the water the way sound does when everything else has gone quiet, and the launch turned and rowed back to the *Meridian* at the same crisp, economical pace, six oars dipping together like nothing had happened, because as far as the six oars were concerned, nothing had.

"They're burning," Wren heard himself say. Nobody was near enough to hear him. He said it anyway. "They're—they're just—"

Figures leapt off the burning raft into the river. The woman with the child—Wren lost sight of her, then found her again, swimming, the child held above the water in both arms, swimming for the bank, a quarter mile of open river between her and anything green. Other swimmers strung out behind her. The raft burned down to the waterline with impressive thoroughness. Compact sailors lined the *Meridian*'s rail and watched, and some of them, Wren could see even at this distance, even through salt-stung eyes, some of them were laughing.

The barges moved on. The sweep grid continued. Somewhere in the *Tern*'s hold, twenty feet below Wren's boots, a sealed chest stamped with a drowned nation's seal sat in the dark, and Wren thought about it for the first time not as a windfall or a problem but as a question he'd never asked: *where did it come from? Who put it in the river? What else burns?*

He stayed in the crow's nest until his legs shook. Partly from duty. Mostly because he didn't trust them.

---

Sefa climbed up at dusk with a thermos of tea and a plate of the eggs she'd rescued from abandonment, because Sefa's answer to the end of the world was that you should face it fed.

"You've been up there ten hours," she said, wedging herself onto the nest rail. She was a wide, comfortable presence, built like a stove, and she radiated heat and cumin and disapproval in roughly equal measure. "Brannick says you haven't come down. Kestrel says leave him. I say nobody skips my cooking twice."

"I ate," Wren said.

"You ate hardtack. That's not eating, that's chewing as a hobby." She poured tea into the thermos cup and pushed it into his hands. Her knuckles were scarred from thirty years of galley work, and her eyes, when he finally made himself meet them, were older than the rest of her. "You saw it too, then."

"The raft." His voice cracked on the word and he hated it. "They just—there was a kid, Sefa. There was a kid on it and they *laughed*."

"Mm." Sefa drank her own tea and looked out at the darkening water, where the fleet's lights had arranged themselves into a tidy constellation, beautiful from this distance, the way most cruel things are. "You know what my mother used to say? Back home, before the debts chased us onto the water. She said, *when the powerful are polite, count your spoons.*"

"That's not—" Wren's throat worked. "The Compact keeps the lanes open. They fixed the Corrin lock after the flood year. They gave out charts free. I *saw*—"

"You saw the front of the house." Sefa shrugged, gentle and merciless. "Everyone sees the front of the house. My mother saw the back of it, once, in a port town, and we left that country, and here we are, and I cook eggs." She bumped her shoulder against his. "Boy. You're allowed to be sad about it. Being sad about it means you're not broken. Just don't be sad *quiet* forever. Sad quiet turns into something with teeth."

Below them, the pilothouse door opened. Kestrel stepped out onto the deck, glass raised, studying the fleet's lights. After a moment Odile joined them, silent as falling snow, and the two of them stood shoulder to shoulder looking at the distant constellation, and Wren saw Kestrel say something short, and Odile nod, and neither of them looked surprised. Neither of them looked shocked at all.

Like they'd known. Like they'd *always* known.

"Sefa," Wren said slowly. "What aren't they telling us?"

Sefa refilled his cup instead of answering, which was its own kind of answer, and drank her tea, and watched the lights of the Compact fleet burn orderly and bright across the black water.

Downriver, unseen in the dark, the lead cruiser changed course. Its running lights swung in a slow, deliberate arc—not continuing the sweep grid, not combing anymore, but pointing. Pointing straight upriver.

Straight down the *Tern*'s route.

Wren felt the tea go cold in his hands.

"Somebody get the captain," he said.
