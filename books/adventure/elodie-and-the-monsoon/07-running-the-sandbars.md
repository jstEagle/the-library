## 7. Running the Sandbars

The fleet came out of the morning haze like a debt collector at a funeral: inevitable, unwelcome, and dressed better than anyone had a right to be.

Kestrel saw them first from the wheelhouse—three smudges of iron on the southern horizon, hulls riding high and mean, the crane-and-chain of the Salvage Compact snapping in the wind. They'd been shadows for two days. Now they were shapes. Now they were a schedule.

"Wren," they said, without raising their voice. The boy was already halfway up the mast ladder, because Wren had the instincts of a squirrel and the self-preservation instincts of a moth.

"Three ships," Wren called down, delighted, which was exactly the wrong emotion. "Iron-hulled! The lead one's got a ram bow—I've only ever seen pictures of those!"

"Isn't that wonderful," Kestrel said dryly. "Brannick! Fuel?"

Brannick's head appeared from the engine hatch, gray-streaked hair plastered to his forehead, a wrench in one hand like a scepter. "Main tank's at half. Reserve's full."

"Good. We're going to spend all of it."

Brannick looked at them for a long moment, the way he always did when Kestrel announced something catastrophic in the tone of someone ordering lunch. "All of it."

"All of it."

"The reserve we keep for emergencies."

"This is one."

"The reserve we keep for *actual* emergencies, not for—"

"Brannick." Kestrel pointed south with their chin. "That's not a patrol. That's a sweep pattern. They're not looking for smugglers, they're looking for *us*, and they've got three ships to our one and iron to our wood. We can't outrun them on open water. So we don't run on open water."

"Then where?"

Kestrel smiled, and it was not a nice smile, and Brannick—who had known them eleven years and loved them anyway—sighed like a man watching his friend pick up a live snake.

"Wherever you're smiling like that," he said, "somebody dies."

"Nobody dies today. Hold that thought. It's going to be load-bearing."

* * *

Here was the thing about the Monsoon River that no chart admitted, because charts were written by people who wanted the river to behave: the sandbars moved. Not slowly, not seasonally—the way a polite river might rearrange its furniture between visits—but constantly, greedily, with the sullen energy of a child told to clean its room. A channel that held four fathoms on Tuesday could hold eighteen inches by Friday. The Compact's hydrographers mapped the river every year and were wrong every year, and every year some salvage barge sat down hard on a bar that hadn't existed when the survey ink dried, and every year the river people collected salvage fees off the wreck and said nothing to anybody.

Kestrel had spent twenty years learning those bars the way other people learned languages—not from books but from argument, from failure, from standing hip-deep in cold water at dawn cursing at a sounding pole. They knew the river the way Odile knew her silences: intimately, and with a certain amount of grief.

Which meant Kestrel knew something the fleet didn't. Between the Tern and the open stretch below Halloway Bend lay the Weeping Sands—forty miles of shifting shallows that the Compact's own charts marked with a single dismissive line: *UNRELIABLE — AVOID.*

Avoid. As if the river were a neighborhood you could simply decline to visit.

"Odile," Kestrel called. "Chart table. I need your memory."

She came up the companionway with a mug of Sefa's terrible chicory tea and an expression that suggested she already knew this was going to cost her something. Odile always knew. It was her gift and her burden, knowing things, and she carried both quietly.

"The Weeping Sands," Kestrel said. "Twenty years ago. What did they look like?"

"Why do you want twenty years ago? The bars have moved since."

"I want the shape of it. Bars move, but rivers have habits. Where the current wants to go, it keeps trying to go. You grew up on this stretch."

Something flickered across Odile's face—not quite pain, more like a door opening onto a room she kept locked—and then it closed again, smooth as water. "The main channel used to thread north of the Widow's Tooth," she said. "But the flood two springs back pushed the delta east. If I were guessing, I'd say the deep water runs close under the bluffs now, past the drowned orchard, and cuts hard south at the old ferry stake."

"That's what my gut says too." Kestrel spread the chart, which was five years old and therefore a work of historical fiction. "Wren! Get down here. You're going to learn something."

"Is it how to outrun a ram bow?" Wren asked, sliding down the ladder with more enthusiasm than grace.

"No. It's how to make a ram bow irrelevant."

* * *

The trick, Kestrel explained, drawing with a grease pencil on the chart's plastic cover, was simple to say and murderous to do. The fleet's ships drew nine feet loaded. The Tern drew five and a half, light. Every inch of draft was a weapon. In the Weeping Sands, the deep channels were narrow, winding, and—this was the important part—moving. The Compact's pilots would be flying their soundings from last season's surveys, calling depths that no longer existed, and every time one of those iron hulls kissed a sandbar at speed, the Compact would lose a little more of its appetite for the chase.

"You're going to drive us through moving sandbars at full throttle based on a five-year-old map and a woman's memory," Sefa said from the galley doorway, arms crossed, wooden spoon in hand like a marshal's baton. "And you're smiling."

"I'm smiling because it's going to work."

"People smile when it works. Right now you look like Brannick describing an engine fire."

"It's going to work *beautifully*," Kestrel said, and Sefa threw up her hands and went back to her galley, muttering in three languages, at least two of which she'd invented specifically for occasions like this.

They came about and ran for the bluffs, and behind them the lead ship answered with a whistle blast that rolled across the water like the sky clearing its throat. Then a second blast. Then a voice, thin with distance, crackling over a speaking-trumpet: orders to heave to, in the flat bureaucratic cadence of people who had never once been ignored successfully.

Kestrel ignored them successfully.

* * *

The first hour was almost fun, in the way that falling is almost flying.

Kestrel took the Tern off the marked channel and into the gray-green shallows where the water went from river-colored to tea-colored to the pale yellow-green of sand showing through, and the crew learned very quickly why Kestrel never let anyone else take the wheel in skinny water. There were no markers. There were no numbers. There was the color of the water, the texture of the ripples, the way the current wrinkled where it poured over a bar's shoulder, and there was Kestrel's hands on the wheel making small constant corrections, a conversation conducted entirely in pressure and angle.

"Talk to me," Kestrel said, and Wren, perched on the bow rail with a sounding pole like a young god of some extremely minor religion, began calling depths.

"Six feet! Five and a half! Five—" the pole scraped, a sound like a fingernail on the world's largest chalkboard, "—four and a half, coming left it deepens—"

"Left, then. Odile, does that match?"

"Orchard should be off the starboard bow," Odile said, peering at the water. And there it was: the ghostly crowns of drowned apple trees, twenty years dead, standing in six feet of water like a congregation waiting for a sermon that never came. She went quiet. Kestrel noticed, and didn't ask, because there wasn't time, and because some doors you don't knock on while running from an armada.

Behind them, the lead ship followed—and the river introduced itself.

It happened the way it always happened, with a shudder they felt through the hull before they heard it: a long grinding boom, iron on sand, the sound of a million-ton institution meeting a geography lesson. The ship's bow swung wide, plowing a brown furrow, and her whistle blew three short angry blasts that meant, roughly, *this is not our fault.*

"Ha!" Wren shouted, punching the air with the sounding pole.

"Don't celebrate yet," Kestrel said. "That was the easy one. That bar's been there a month; they'll have it on their new soundings inside the hour. The ones ahead—" Kestrel squinted at the water ahead, reading it like a letter from an old enemy, "—the ones ahead are fresh. Born last spring. Nobody's sounded them. Not even me."

"Then how do you know where they are?"

"I don't. That's the job."

Wren looked at Odile. Odile looked at her tea. Somewhere below, Brannick's muffled voice rose through the deck plates, asking the engine politely for everything it had ever had.

* * *

The second hour was not fun at all.

The channel narrowed under the bluffs until the Tern's wake slapped back at them off stone walls, and the water did the thing shallow water does when it lies: it went glassy and innocent and deep-looking, all the way up to the moment it wasn't. Kestrel read it by feel now, half instinct, half prayer, the wheel talking to their palms in a language older than charts. Twice they swung hard around a bar that Wren found with the pole a half-second before the hull would have found it harder. Once they went through water so thin that the propeller chewed gravel and the whole boat shuddered like a dog shaking off rain, and Kestrel heard Brannick below make a noise that was not a word in any language.

And the fleet kept coming. That was the thing about the Compact—they were slow learners, but they were learners. The second ship hung back and sent boats ahead with sounding crews, buying depth the honest way, foot by foot. They were paying for the river in patience, and patience was the one currency the Compact printed endlessly.

"They're mapping as they come," Odile observed quietly.

"They're mapping *behind* us. They can map all they like; the bars will have moved by tomorrow. But today—" Kestrel's jaw tightened. Today the river was doing them a favor, and favors from the Monsoon River always arrived with an invoice attached.

The invoice arrived at the Narrows.

It was a place Kestrel knew—or had known—a slot between two bars where the current ran hard and the water ran deep, the kind of shortcut that separated professionals from corpses. But the spring flood had done its work, and as they came into it at full throttle, Kestrel felt the wheel go strange in their hands, the helm answering a half-beat late, the boat drifting left when it should have held true.

"Silt," Kestrel breathed. "The bar's migrated. It's filled the slot—"

"Depth!" Wren shouted, pole down, and the number he called was a number that ended conversations.

Kestrel did the arithmetic of desperation. Hard right meant the bluff shoal. Hard left meant the fresh bar. Straight ahead meant the bottom, at speed, with three warships behind them. There was a fourth option, visible only if you'd spent twenty years making mistakes on this river and memorizing each one like a lover's face: a flooded timber channel on the right, an old log-drive cut from before the Compact, choked with deadfall, barely wider than the Tern herself, unmarked on any chart made in the last century.

"Hold on," Kestrel said. "Everyone hold on. Brannick, I need everything and then I need a miracle."

"We're fresh out of miracles! I checked the stores this morning!"

"Then give me everything else."

Kestrel put the Tern into the cut.

For ninety seconds the world became wood and water and noise. Deadfalls clawed at the rails; Sefa's voice rose from the galley in fluent invented profanity as her pots achieved orbit; Odile stood at the chart table with both hands flat on it, steady as a compass needle, feeding Kestrel angles from memory of a river that no longer existed. The hull struck something—once, twice, a glancing blow that sang up through the keel and into everyone's teeth—and then the cut spat them out into open green water beyond the sands, and the fleet was behind them, and the second ship was sitting on the bar with her stern cocked up like a duck taking a bath, and Wren was laughing so hard he fell off the bow rail.

* * *

Kestrel allowed themselves exactly one minute of triumph, standing at the wheel with the wind in their face and the Weeping Sands spreading out astern like a moat well defended. One minute. Then they did the accounting.

The reserve tank: empty. Burned to the dregs getting through the cut, because Brannick, bless him, had given them everything and then somehow found more. The hull: holed in two places above the waterline where the deadfall had punched through, plus whatever the glancing blows had done to the planking below, which Kestrel wouldn't know until the next calm water and a long look with a lantern. The engine: running hot and complaining in a register Brannick described as "pre-funeral." And somewhere in the last hour, Kestrel had noticed, the bilge pump had started cycling more often than it should.

Running, Kestrel thought, watching the fuel gauge sit at zero like a small round accusation. Running always looks free until you count what it costs. Every mile bought with the reserve was a mile they couldn't buy later. Every plank cracked today was a plank that wouldn't hold tomorrow. The debt compounded, quiet as interest, patient as the river itself. You could outrun a fleet. You couldn't outrun the bill.

"How bad?" Sefa asked, appearing beside them with a bowl of something stew-like, because Sefa's answer to mortal peril was lunch.

"Bad enough that we stop soon. Good enough that we choose where."

"There's a floating market at Gullwing Reach," Odile said. "Two days, if we nurse it. They'll have planking, pitch, and diesel that's only mostly cut with lamp oil."

"And people," Kestrel said.

Odile paused. "There are always people."

"There are always people who know things." Kestrel looked south, where the haze had swallowed the fleet but not, somehow, the feeling of it. Three ships. A sweep grid. Six tides, the patrol skiff had said, before the search reached everywhere the Tern could hide. They'd spent two tides running. The clock didn't care that they'd won the day.

Sefa handed them the bowl. "Eat. Brooding burns calories; I read that somewhere."

"I'm not brooding. I'm accounting."

"You're brooding *with numbers*. Eat."

Kestrel ate. It was, annoyingly, excellent.

* * *

They made Gullwing Reach by dusk of the second day, the Tern limping along on fumes and stubbornness, listing slightly to port like a person favoring a sprained ankle. The floating market strung itself across the anchorage in a ragged constellation of barges and pontoon platforms, lanterns coming alight one by one, the smell of frying fish and tar and cut lumber drifting out over the water. It smelled like safety. Kestrel had been in this business long enough to know that smell was a liar.

"Rules," Kestrel said, gathering the crew on deck as the market lights brightened. "We go in quiet. No chest talk. No fleet talk. Wren—"

"I know, I know. No telling strangers exciting facts."

"You once told a customs officer the exact tonnage of our cargo *and* our route home. With visual aids."

"He seemed interested!"

"Brannick, you're with me for the planking and the fuel. Sefa, provisions, and buy friends, not just food—you know how. Odile..."

Odile waited. The lantern light caught her face, and for a moment Kestrel thought again of that flicker on the water, the locked room behind her eyes.

"Stay aboard," Kestrel said gently. "Rest. You earned it."

Something in her shoulders eased, and something else tightened, and Kestrel—who had built a life on noticing what people hid—filed both away and said nothing, because the bilge was still pumping, the fuel was still gone, and the fleet was still out there, patient as interest.

They rowed in as the last light died. The market swallowed them in noise and lantern-glow, and Kestrel found the chandler's barge by following the sound of an argument about the price of oakum, and it was there, haggling over planking with a broad man in a salt-crusted coat, that Kestrel felt the prickle rise on the back of their neck—that old animal sense, older than any sandbar, that the water ahead was not as deep as it looked.

The chandler's partner stepped out of the shadow of the mast, wiping his hands on a rag, and stopped.

He stared. His eyes went wide, then narrow, then wide again, traveling from Kestrel's face down to their boots and back up with the slow delight of a man finding money on the ground.

"Well," he said softly, tasting it. "Well, well. I know that face. Twenty years, and I still know that face. Captain *Vane*, isn't it? Or should I say—"

"Friend," Kestrel said pleasantly, stepping closer, smiling the smile that Brannick had once described as *the reason I sleep with a wrench*, "you must be thinking of someone else."

But the man was already grinning, and behind him the market lights burned on, and somewhere out in the dark the river kept its ledger, adding another line.
