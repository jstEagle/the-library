## 8. The Price of Stopping

Sefa had a theory about Kestrel Nakamura, and the theory was this: the captain was allergic to stopping.

Not in the ordinary way. Plenty of river folk hated to sit still. But Kestrel treated a moored boat the way other people treated an open wound — something to be patched fast and never, ever looked at directly. In the four years Sefa had cooked aboard the *Tern*, he could count their stops on one hand: fuel, always; repairs, only when Brannick threatened mutiny with a wrench; and once, memorably, for Wren's birthday, which Kestrel had endured like a man sitting through his own funeral.

So when the hull plate below the waterline started weeping — not gushing, just weeping, a slow silver thread of Monsoon finding its way into the bilge — and Kestrel actually said the words *"we'll put in at Lantern Reach,"* Sefa knew two things. One: the damage was worse than anyone was saying. Two: whatever it cost them in coin, it would cost Kestrel something worse in skin.

"Floating market's the cheapest yard on this stretch," Brannick said, already elbow-deep in the engine room's guts, talking to the machinery the way he talked to frightened animals. "Old Ferro can cold-patch us in a day if we catch him before he drinks his profits."

"And if we catch him after?" Wren asked from the doorway, all elbows and optimism.

"Then we pay double and listen to his poetry."

Sefa stirred the pot and said nothing, because saying nothing was his whole profession. A cook hears everything on a boat. He heard Odile correct Wren's chart-reading with a patience she showed no one else, and he heard the Vessian lilt underneath her river-accented words that she probably thought she'd buried. He heard Kestrel lie smoothly to the patrol skiff in chapter — no, in that long gray morning on the water — and he heard the exact moment the captain's voice went flat and formal, like a door closing. Cooks hear everything. The trick is deciding what you've heard.

Lantern Reach appeared at dusk the way it always did: a hundred barges lashed together in a loose knot, strung with paper lanterns that made the whole town look like a constellation that had gotten drunk and lain down on the water. Smoke from a hundred cookfires. Music from three directions, none of it agreeing. Somewhere a woman was selling eels and arguing about it.

The *Tern* slid into a berth between a tar-barge and a floating chapel, and Sefa felt the crew relax by degrees — all except Kestrel, who stood at the rail with their arms crossed, scanning the lantern-lit crowd the way other people read weather.

"I'll handle the yard," Kestrel said. "Sefa, you're with me. Brannick, nobody boards. Wren—"

"I know, I know. Stay on the boat."

"You're learning." Something almost warm crossed the captain's face, there and gone. "Odile, keep the ledger. We're not rich, but we're going to look like it."

They went down the gangplank together, and Sefa watched Kestrel pull their collar up and their shoulders in, becoming smaller, more forgettable, a trick Sefa had seen a hundred times and never understood how it worked. Big people made themselves small. Small people made themselves furniture. It was a river skill, and Kestrel had it like a second skin.

* * *

Ferro's yard was the third barge past the chapel, and Ferro himself was exactly where Brannick predicted: halfway through drinking his profits, a squat man with burn-scarred forearms and a voice like a rusted hinge. He took one look at the *Tern*'s papers and one longer look at Kestrel, and Sefa saw the exact moment the evening curdled.

"Well," Ferro said, and smiled with too many teeth. "Well, well. Cold-water run, is it? Long way from the upper river, Captain... what do they call you these days?"

"Nakamura."

"Mm." Ferro turned the papers over slowly, savoring them. "Nakamura. Funny thing about names on this river. They wear out. Get traded. Like hull plates." He tapped the manifest. "I knew a captain once, years back. Compact service. Enforcer division, if you can believe it. Young one. Sharp as a filleting knife. Had a name that started the same way yours does."

The lantern above them hissed. Somewhere behind Sefa, a fishmonger was still arguing about eels.

"Ferro," Kestrel said, level as a spirit gauge. "The hull plate. Below the waterline, port side. Can you patch it or not?"

"Oh, I can patch it." Ferro set the papers down with great care, like a man laying out cards. "Question is what I charge. See, my memory's expensive. Old men like me, all we've got left is memory. And mine says I stood on a dock in Vessia's trade quarter, twenty years back, and watched archives burn. Watched enforcers carry ledgers out of the tribunal hall and feed them to a fire barge, page by page, while the floodwater came up the streets. And mine says the youngest of those enforcers had your jaw, Captain. Your hands. Your way of standing like the ground owes you money."

Sefa felt the words land in his own chest before he understood them. He looked at Kestrel — really looked — and saw something he had never seen on that unshakeable face. Not guilt, exactly. Guilt would have been easier. This was older and more tired than guilt. This was a person standing very still inside a burning building.

"The price," Kestrel said quietly. "For the patch."

"Double. Plus a little insurance." Ferro leaned in, and his breath was rice wine and old smoke. "You keep sailing through my reach, Nakamura, or Vessia, or whatever you call yourself, you buy your fuel here. Every time. That's the toll. That's what my silence costs. Cheap, really, considering what some folks would pay to know where you are."

A long pause. The eel argument had ended. The music had changed key.

"Done," Kestrel said.

And that was it. That was the whole negotiation. Sefa had watched Kestrel talk down patrol captains and talk up rusted engines and talk their way past six tides of Compact search grid, and here the captain folded in four sentences, paid a blackmailer's toll without blinking, and walked away from the table like a person leaving a grave.

* * *

They didn't speak on the walk back. That was the strange part. Sefa had braced for questions — his own, mostly, tumbling out sideways the way his questions always did — but Kestrel moved through the lantern-crowd in silence, buying nothing, greeting no one, and Sefa found himself carrying the silence like a full pot he was afraid to spill.

It wasn't until they were back aboard, until Brannick reported the patch scheduled for dawn and Wren had been sent to bed with the wounded dignity of a boy told to sleep, that Sefa cornered the captain in the galley. Not cornered, exactly. Cooks don't corner. Cooks simply happen to be standing where you need to stand, holding food you need to eat.

"Sit," Sefa said. "You haven't eaten since noon. I counted."

"Sefa—"

"There's pepper fish and there's the truth, and I'm only licensed to serve one of them."

Kestrel sat. That alone told Sefa how bad the day had been. They ate in silence for a while, the good kind of silence, the kind a galley is built to hold, and then Sefa wiped his hands on his apron and asked the question gently, the way you approach a dog that's been kicked.

"Is it true? What Ferro said."

Kestrel set down the fork. Looked at it for a long moment as though it were a chart to somewhere far away.

"Yes."

Just that. No speech. No defense. Sefa had prepared for speeches. He'd spent the walk back composing rebuttals, cataloguing betrayals, rehearsing the righteous anger of an honest man. And now the honest anger sat in his chest with nowhere to go, because the captain wouldn't even give it a fight.

"I burned the Vessian archives," Kestrel said. "Twenty years ago. I was nineteen and I believed the Compact when it told me the documents were sedition. Rebel propaganda. Things that would hurt people if they got out." A breath. "They were right that the documents would have hurt people. Wrong about which people. There were engineers' names in those ledgers. Letters. Manifests. Proof of things I didn't understand until—" They stopped. Started again. "Until recently. Until now, maybe. I don't know yet."

"The chest," Sefa said. It wasn't a question. The whole boat had felt the change in Kestrel since the dredge lines came up heavy; the whole boat had pretended not to notice the captain ordering it sealed.

"The chest," Kestrel agreed.

Sefa thought about his debts. That was where his mind went, absurdly, traitorously — back home, before the river, before the *Tern*, when he'd signed papers he couldn't read in a language he barely spoke, and a lender had smiled at him with Ferro's exact smile. He knew what it was to be owned by a piece of paper. He knew what it was to discover, too late, what you'd actually signed.

"So Ferro owns a piece of you now," Sefa said. "Every time we fuel up."

"For now."

"And the Compact's fleet is sweeping the river, six tides from our route, hunting whoever pulled that chest out of the water. And the man who could tell them exactly where we are is drinking rice wine on barge three." Sefa rubbed his face. "Kestrel. You understand I have to think about the crew. About Wren. That boy thinks the Compact is the good guys. He salutes their ships in his head, I've seen him do it."

"I know."

"And you — you *were* them. You wore the crane-and-chain. You carried the torch." The anger finally found its feet, and Sefa let it stand up, but he kept his voice low, because walls on boats are thin and families thinner. "Do you know what I did when I figured out Odile was Vessian? Nothing. I cook louder. That's my contribution. I make her favorite soup and I never ask. Because everyone on this boat is running from something, and the running is the one thing we all agreed not to ask about. But you—" His voice caught. "You weren't running *from* them, Kestrel. You were running *for* them. For years. And we've been your hiding place."

The galley lamp swung gently with the water. Kestrel hadn't touched the fish in minutes.

"Yes," they said. "We have."

Sefa waited for more. It didn't come. And that, in the end, was what decided him — not forgiveness, because he wasn't there yet, maybe wouldn't be for a long time, but the sheer *emptiness* across the table. A liar would have filled the silence. A manipulator would have managed him, soothed him, given him a version shaped to keep him loyal. Kestrel just sat there and took it, like a hull taking water, and let him decide what to do with the leak.

"If Wren asks me," Sefa said at last, "I won't lie to him."

"I wouldn't ask you to."

"If Odile asks me—"

"She has more right than anyone to ask." Something moved behind Kestrel's eyes, quick and dark, gone before Sefa could name it. "If she asks, send her to me."

"And the fleet? If Ferro sells you out anyway, toll or no toll?"

Kestrel finally picked up the fork again. Ate. A small, stubborn act of continuing.

"Then we run the sandbars," they said. "And I get us out. That part, Sefa — that part you never have to wonder about. Whatever I was, I am very, very good at getting this crew out."

Sefa looked at the captain for a long moment — at the tired eyes and the steady hands and the nineteen-year-old ghost sitting at that table like a third passenger — and made his choice, the only choice a cook can make.

He got up, ladled a second helping onto the plate without being asked, and said:

"Eat. Then sleep. Tomorrow we patch the boat, and nobody on this barge hears anything from me."

Kestrel nodded. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me." Sefa turned back to his stove, because turning away was easier than letting the captain see his face. "Thank me by telling Odile before she hears it from someone worse than Ferro."

Behind him, the silence stretched long enough that he thought the conversation was over. Then Kestrel's voice came, quiet, almost lost under the lap of water against the hull:

"That's the plan. It's just — I keep waiting for a tide when the plan doesn't cost more than the secret."

Sefa stirred the pot and didn't answer, because he was a cook, and cooks know that some hungers you feed and some you just sit beside.

But later, banked fire, dark galley, he lay in his bunk doing arithmetic he didn't want to do: a fence with a memory, a fleet with a grid, a sealed chest, and a navigator whose accent slipped when she was tired. Four weights on one small boat, and the water rising.

And near dawn, half-asleep, he heard footsteps on the deck above — soft ones, bare ones, going aft. Going toward the cargo hold.

Going toward the chest.

Sefa lay still in the dark, heart thumping against his ribs like a fist on a locked door, and made himself a promise with the taste of pepper fish and secrets still in his mouth: *one night*. One night of the silence he'd chosen. After that, whatever he'd heard walking across that deck — he was going to have to decide whose trust was worth more, and the terrible thing, the thing that kept him staring at the ceiling until the lanterns of Lantern Reach burned down to embers, was that he already suspected he knew exactly whose bare feet those were.
