## 9. Auctions and Ghosts

The floating market of Corvo's Rest smelled of frying fish, tar, and the particular desperation of people selling things they'd rather keep. Kestrel had been coming here for eleven years, back when it was called something else and the piers were half as long, and in all that time they had learned one rule above all others: never stop longer than you must.

They had now been stopped for two days.

"Captain." Brannick appeared at their elbow on the Tern's aft deck, wiping engine grease onto a rag that was itself mostly grease. "Fence is here."

Kestrel's stomach did the thing it did, a slow drop like stepping off a pier in the dark. "Already? I told him tomorrow."

"He said tomorrow was a day too long for what he's carrying in his case." Brannick shrugged. "Also he brought pastries. Sefa let him aboard before I could stop her. She says anyone who brings almond cakes gets the benefit of the doubt."

"Sefa would sell the anchor for almond cakes."

"She nearly did once. In Vellport. Don't ask her about it."

Kestrel looked out over the water. The market sprawled across forty lashed barges, awnings strung between masts like laundry, lanterns already lit against the early dusk. Somewhere a fiddle was being played badly by someone who loved it very much. It should have been pleasant. It was the kind of evening Kestrel had built this whole drifting life to have.

But the chest sat below decks under three tarps and a false floor, lead-lined and silent as a held breath, and every hour they lingered, the Compact's six-tide search grid crept closer down the river.

"All right," Kestrel said. "Show him up. And Brannick—nobody leaves us alone with him, and he doesn't go near the hold."

"You think he'd try?"

"I think a man who shows up a day early with pastries wants something badly enough to be clever about it."

* * *

The fence's name was Merro Hask, and he dressed like a man who wanted you to believe he was poor: patched coat, scuffed boots, a single modest ring. Everything about him was chosen. Kestrel had known him since the Tern ran mail runs upriver, and had never once seen him wear the same face twice.

He sat at the galley table with his hands folded around a cup of Sefa's chicory tea like it was the finest thing he'd ever been offered, which was also part of the costume.

"Captain Nakamura," he said warmly.

"That's not my name."

"It isn't, no." He smiled, unbothered. "Forgive me. Old habits. Captain, then. May I say, this is a fine boat. Well-kept. Loved, even. You don't see that often in salvage work."

"You didn't come to compliment my deck caulking."

"No." He set down the cup. From his coat he drew a slim ledger, bound in grey leather, and laid it flat but did not open it. "I came because certain parties have heard a rumor. A rumor about a wreck site upriver, and a dredge crew that pulled something heavy out of the silt, and a rumor that whatever it was, it never made it to any salvage manifest."

Kestrel kept their face still. Behind them, at the stove, Sefa stirred a pot with elaborate unconcern. Wren sat on the bench by the door, ostensibly mending a net, actually listening so hard Kestrel could feel it.

"Rumors are cheap on this river," Kestrel said.

"This one has a price attached." Merro opened the ledger. Inside were columns of figures, written small and neat. "I'm authorized to offer four hundred crowns. Cash, tonight, no questions asked, no manifest, no record. The item stays exactly where it is; my principals handle collection themselves. You sail away rich. Your cook pays off her debts in Vellport—" Sefa's spoon went still, "—your pilot buys himself a berth on a proper packet line, your engineer retires to somewhere with trees. Everyone eats."

Silence. The fiddle outside scraped through its tune. Four hundred crowns. Kestrel did the arithmetic without wanting to: the Tern's hull damage from the sandbars, the fuel reserve gone, Sefa's creditors who sent letters with red wax seals, Wren's eyes when he talked about the packet boats with their brass fittings and their schedules. Four hundred crowns was five years of freedom bought in an afternoon.

It was also, Kestrel noticed, not a number a fence threw around for an unknown chest. That number meant the buyer knew what was inside.

"And if I say no?" Kestrel asked.

Merro closed the ledger gently. "Then I report that the item does not exist, and my principals continue looking elsewhere, and everyone goes on with their lives. I'm an auctioneer, Captain, not a threat. Threats are bad for trade." He smiled again. "Though I will note, professionally, that the search grid moving downriver has four tides left on it, and the men running it are less polite than I am."

"You know about the grid."

"Everyone knows about the grid. It's in the shipping notices." He spread his hands. "Look. I understand caution. But consider: whoever sealed that chest expected it to stay drowned. It didn't. That's nobody's fault—that's the river doing what rivers do. All you've done is fish something out. What it means, whose grief it belongs to, whether it changes anything after twenty years—" he waved a hand, airy, "—that's above your station and mine. Take the money. Buy your people a season. Let the dead keep their own paperwork."

And God help them, Kestrel almost did.

Not because of the money—not mostly—but because of the faces around the galley. Sefa, who hadn't slept properly since Vellport, pretending she wasn't listening while her whole body leaned toward the word *debts*. Wren, whose net-mending had entirely stopped. Even Odile, standing in the companionway shadow where she thought no one could see her, watching Kestrel with an expression Kestrel couldn't read and didn't want to.

Four hundred crowns and the chest gone by morning. No fleet. No grid. No more waking at night cataloguing everything that could be taken from them.

"One condition," Kestrel heard themself say. "We're paid before your principals touch anything. And we're gone before they arrive."

"Of course." Merro began writing in the ledger. "I'll need only a description of the item for authentication. Lead-lined, yes? Sealed? And the stamp—"

"The stamp stays covered."

"Naturally, naturally. Though my principals have already described it to me." He glanced up, mild as milk. "Vessia's seal, isn't it? The double tide and the tower. Lovely work, Vessian engravers. Such a shame about the country."

Something in Kestrel's chest tightened. "Twenty years ago. Yes. A shame."

"Mm." Merro blotted his pen. "You know, I handled Vessian goods back before the flood. Porcelain, mostly. There was a workshop in the lower city—the good stuff, blue glaze, you could see your face in it. I sometimes wonder what happened to all those kilns." He shook his head with genuine, practiced regret. "Still. Necessary engineering, the flood. Terrible, but necessary. My principals were quite clear on that point. Sometimes a river has to be managed, and management requires sacrifices, and twenty years on, look how prosperous the trade lanes are. Everyone downstream eats because of what was done upstream. That's not villainy, Captain. That's hydrology."

The words landed in the galley and just—sat there. Sefa's spoon had stopped again. Wren was staring at Merro with the open, unguarded face of a boy hearing a teacher say something wrong and not yet knowing why it was wrong.

Kestrel found they couldn't speak for a moment. Not because the words surprised them—they'd suspected, since the chest came up dripping, that the flood had hands behind it. But suspicion was a cold thing you could carry. Hearing it said aloud, casually, over chicory tea, by a man eating almond cakes—*necessary engineering*, in the same tone he'd use for dredging permits—

Twenty years ago Kestrel had stood in a Vessian archive with a torch in their hand and been told the same thing in different words. Necessary. Sedition. Cleanup. They had believed it, because believing was easier than asking what was in the ledgers they were burning.

They weren't going to believe it twice.

"Get off my boat," Kestrel said quietly.

Merro blinked. "Captain—"

"The offer's declined. Take your cakes—you brought them, they're yours—and get off my boat."

"Now hold on. Four hundred crowns is not nothing. If the figure's the problem, I can wire my principals, perhaps five—"

"*Off.*"

Kestrel hadn't raised their voice. That was somehow worse. Merro studied them for a long moment, and the affable mask slipped just far enough to show the appraiser underneath, recalculating, filing something away. Then he rose, tucked the ledger into his coat, bowed slightly to the room at large, and took his pastry box from the table.

"A pleasure, Captain," he said at the door. "Truly. I do hope we meet again under happier circumstances." His eyes flicked, briefly, to the companionway where Odile stood—and Kestrel saw it, saw him see her, saw something register behind his face like a coin dropping into a slot.

Then he was down the gangplank and into the market crowd, and the Tern felt larger and emptier at once.

* * *

Sefa slammed the pot onto the stove hard enough to rattle the shelf. "Four hundred crowns." Her voice cracked on the second word. "Kestrel. Four. Hundred. Crowns. Do you know what the Vellport lenders charge in interest? Do you know what they take when you can't pay? I've got a sister who—"

"I know."

"You *don't* know! You've never owed anybody anything, you sleep on your own boat, you—" She caught herself, jaw working. "You don't know what it's like to have someone else's name on your neck."

"Sefa." Brannick, low, from the doorway.

"No, it's fine. It's fine." She turned back to the stove and attacked the pot. "I'm not saying take it. I'm saying I get to be angry about it for one night, and then tomorrow I'll agree with whatever you decided, because I always do, because that's what crew does. One night."

"Take the night," Kestrel said. "Take two."

Wren spoke up from the bench, hesitant. "Was he right? About the flood? I mean—the trade lanes *are* better. Everyone says the Compact opened up the whole river after Vessia. That's what they teach in the shore schools. Managed waters, safe passage, fair salvage law." He looked around at their faces and faltered. "That's what they teach."

Odile moved out of the companionway shadow. She crossed to the table, picked up one of the abandoned almond cakes, and set it in front of Wren without a word. Then she looked at Kestrel, and her voice, when it came, was quiet and accented and steady.

"In Vessia," she said, "we taught our children that the river was a neighbor. You do not drown a neighbor to improve your road." She paused. "Whatever the chest holds, Captain—it was worth killing to bury. Ask yourself why a man who calls murder *hydrology* is afraid of paper."

She went up on deck. The hatch thumped shut behind her.

Nobody spoke. Outside, the fiddle had given up; the market lanterns bobbed on black water.

Kestrel stood at the galley table where Merro had sat, looking down at the ring-mark his teacup had left in the wood, and understood that something had finished turning over inside them. An hour ago they'd been ready to deal. Ready to let the dead keep their own paperwork. Now there was a phrase stuck in their skull like a splinter—*necessary engineering*—and next to it, older and sharper, the memory of smoke and burning shelves and their own young hands holding the torch.

Some things you sold. Some things you carried until you could put them down in the right place.

"We don't sell it," Kestrel said. "Not to Merro, not to anyone wearing a Compact crane-and-chain. We run the grid, we make port at Greywater, and then—" they stopped, because the *then* had no shape yet, only a direction, downriver, toward a drowned city none of them had ever spoken aloud.

Brannick raised an eyebrow. "Then?"

"Then I'll tell you all something I should have told you years ago."

Sefa turned from the stove, flour on her knuckles, anger and worry fighting openly on her face. "Kestrel—"

A light winked at the rail. Out past the market's edge, far down the dark reach of the river, a signal lamp was flashing in short, disciplined bursts—Compact pattern, crisp as a clerk's handwriting. Three ships' worth of answering lights woke along the horizon, one after another, moving with the current.

Moving downriver. Toward them. Ahead of schedule.

Wren was already scrambling for the crow's nest, and Kestrel was already reaching for the engine telegraph, and the night that had begun with almond cakes ended with the Tern slipping her moorings into the dark water, quiet as a secret, while somewhere behind them a fence in a patched coat watched them go and smiled.
