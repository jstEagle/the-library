## 10. Wren's Bargain

Wren had learned the Compact's signal code the way other boys learned prayers: by heart, in secret, with a kind of trembling devotion.

He'd found the manual three years ago, wedged under a loose floorboard in the pilot house where some previous owner of the *Tern* had hidden it—probably to keep it from a crew exactly like this one. *Compact Maritime Signaling, Third Revision.* The pages were soft as cloth from handling, and Wren had handled them plenty, tracing the flag positions and lamp patterns by candlelight while the boat rocked him like a mother who couldn't quite commit.

That was the thing nobody on the crew understood about him. They saw a sixteen-year-old who could read water like scripture and thread the Sandbars blindfolded, and they assumed that was the whole of his ambition. But Wren had grown up on stories of the Salvage Compact the way other kids grew up on ghosts: the great iron ships, the crane-and-chain flying clean above everything, the order of it all. A river ruled by *rules*. Channels dredged and marked. Wrecks catalogued instead of squabbled over. A boy with nothing—not a family name, not a coin, not even a last name anyone was sure of—could sign on with the Compact and become somebody with a uniform and a berth and a place at the table.

So when he climbed into the crow's nest that evening and saw the fleet's lead ship sitting out past the far bend like a black cathedral that had decided to float, his heart did something complicated. Part terror. Part longing. Part—though he'd have bitten through his own tongue before admitting it—*hope*.

They were still hunting them. Three days since the sandbar run, three days since the *Tern* had limped into the floating market town of Corvo's Rest with her hull singing a new note whenever the current pushed wrong. Brannick had patched what he could patch, but everyone knew the truth: they were slow now, and the fleet was patient, and patience always won on open water.

Wren stood his watch. He checked the lamps. He drank Sefa's terrible chicory substitute and pretended it was coffee because correcting Sefa led to lectures, and lectures led to chores.

And all the while, the signal manual sat folded inside his coat, against his chest, warm as a second heartbeat.

* * *

It was Odile who almost caught him first.

She came up to the nest near midnight, two days later, carrying a tin cup of something hot and a look that said she'd been standing below deck listening to the hull for a long time. She did that lately. Ever since the chest came aboard, Odile had developed a habit of touching things—the rail, the mast, the doorframe—as if confirming the boat was still real.

"You're watching the fleet," she said. Not a question.

"Standard watch," Wren said. "You're supposed to be sleeping."

"I sleep when the river lets me." She handed him the cup. Their fingers brushed and he felt how cold hers were. "What do you see?"

He looked out at the distant lights of the lead ship, strung along her superstructure like a festival that had forgotten to be festive. "Iron-hull. Crane-and-chain. That's the commodore's pennant on the mainmast—I think. I think that's Ashvael."

Odile went very still beside him. Stillness, on Odile, was loud.

"You know the Compact's signals," she said quietly.

"Everyone knows the crane-and-chain."

"Not the rest. Not the way you look at their lamps. You read them, Wren. Like Kestrel reads water."

The cup trembled slightly in his hands. He covered it by drinking. The chicory was terrible. He loved it anyway, the way you love anything that belongs to a home.

"They're signaling," he admitted, because lying to Odile felt like kicking a heron. "Position checks. Grid sweeps. They're working the search pattern methodically. Six tides, Kestrel said, and we're—" He did the math he'd been doing for two days. "We're inside four."

Odile looked out at the lights for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had an accent in it that only came out late at night, vowels rounded soft as river stones. "When I was small, there were Compact stations on every lock in Vessia. Before." She stopped. Started again. "The officers wore white gloves. My mother said it was so they never had to touch anything real."

Then she climbed down and left him alone with the cup and the lights and the manual against his heart, and Wren told himself that what he was planning wasn't betrayal. It was *navigation*. You didn't fight a flood; you read it and moved with it. The fleet was a flood. Surrender was high ground.

Surely mercy was standard procedure. Surely a boy who'd done nothing but steer well would be treated fairly. Surely institutions built on rules ran on fairness the way engines ran on fuel.

He was fifteen when he'd first believed that. He was sixteen now, and still believing it, which shows you how long a lie can stay warm if you feed it often enough.

* * *

He did it on the third night, during the dead watch, when the boat was quiet except for Brannick's snoring, which carried through the deck planks like a friendly earthquake.

The signal lamp was kept in the chart room locker—a Compact-issue Aldis pattern, because Kestrel bought salvage wherever salvage was cheapest, irony being free of charge. Wren let himself into the chart room with the particular silence of someone who has rehearsed this walk forty times in his head and gotten lost twice.

The chart room smelled of pencil shavings and old varnish. Charts lay rolled in their cubbies. The chest—the chest—sat in the corner under a canvas tarp, lead-lined and sullen, radiating the specific gravity of trouble.

And here is where Wren's plan, such as it was, began to come apart. Because the lamp was in the locker, and the locker was behind the chart table, and the chart table was where Kestrel worked at night, and spread across it right now, weighted down with a brass protractor and Sefa's spare salt dish, was paper.

Manifests. Wren knew manifests; he'd been copying them since he was twelve, Kestrel's neat block letters beside his own careful hand. These were older. Water-stained at the edges but professionally preserved, the kind of preservation that cost money, the kind of money that meant somebody official had once cared very much about these papers surviving.

He should have taken the lamp and gone. That was the plan. Take the lamp, go up top, flash the surrender code—*TN-4, TN-4, crew requests parley, no resistance offered*—and let the grown-up machinery of the Compact handle everything.

But the topmost page had a list on it. And lists were Wren's whole language.

*NAMES OF PRINCIPAL ENGINEERING PERSONNEL — VESSIA HYDROGRAPHIC WORKS — RETAINED FOR COMPACT RECORD.*

Below it, a column of names with ranks and assignments. Wren's eyes went down it the way they went down channel markers, quick and hungry, and then stopped, snagged, snagged hard, on the fourth entry from the bottom.

*ASHVAEL, M. — Deputy Director, Gate Systems — Clearance: Full.*

Ashvael. Commodore Ashvael. The name from the pennant ship, the name the patrol skiff officer had spoken with something close to reverence back before the sandbars, the name that made even Kestrel go quiet in a way Kestrel never went quiet.

Commodore Ashvael had been a *gate engineer*.

Wren frowned. That wasn't sinister, exactly. Lots of engineers ended up in command; the Compact loved promoting its builders. But the document wasn't just a roster. There were annotations in the margin, in a different hand, red ink gone brown with age:

*Gate 7 — opened ahead of schedule per directive. Ashvael executed personally. Commended.*

Opened ahead of schedule. Gates opened ahead of schedule. Gates were for floods, floods were for—

For Vessia. For the drowned country. For the nation that wasn't a nation anymore because twenty years ago the Monsoon had risen in a single terrible week and swallowed it whole, and everyone agreed it was a tragedy, an act of God, bad weather meeting bad luck, and the Compact had been formed afterward to salvage what remained, and everyone agreed *that* was a kindness.

Wren stood in the chart room with the lamp under his arm and read the page again, and then again, and the third time he noticed the letterhead at the top, the embossed seal half worn away, and recognized it because he'd seen it burned into the chest lid a hundred times without ever really looking.

Vessia's own seal. On Vessia's own engineering records. In the Compact's own archive stamps.

The Compact hadn't just salvaged Vessia. The Compact had *kept the paperwork*. Kept it sealed, kept it stamped, kept it in a lead-lined box at the bottom of the river—which meant somebody had sunk it there on purpose, which meant somebody had needed it to drown too.

"Institutions lie," Odile had told him once, apropos of nothing, mending a net. "But they lie in their own handwriting, child. That's how you catch them."

He'd laughed at the time. Sixteen-year-olds laugh at old women's wisdom; it's practically a law of nature.

His hands were shaking now. He set the lamp down on the chart table, next to the salt dish, next to the proof, and pressed his palms flat against the wood until the shaking slowed.

Out beyond the far bend, the commodore's ship hung her lights over the water. Somewhere on that ship was a man whose signature was on a commendation for opening a floodgate early. Ahead of schedule. *Executed personally.* Those words had been written by someone proud.

And Wren had been about to signal that ship. Had rehearsed the surrender code forty times. Had held the manual against his heart like a prayer book.

Mercy, he'd thought. Standard procedure. Rules.

But rules were written by the same hand that wrote *commended*, weren't they? The same ink. The same institution. If the Compact had drowned a country and filed the murder under *engineering*, what did Wren imagine happened to one stowaway-hearted pilot boy who flashed a lamp and asked nicely?

He thought of Sefa's debts, and how Sefa joked about them so nobody worried. He thought of Brannick's snoring, steady as a heartbeat through the floor. He thought of Odile's cold fingers and her mother's line about white gloves. He thought of Kestrel, who never explained anything, ordering the chest sealed and unopened, dread dressed up as pragmatism—and suddenly, horribly, Wren understood why. Kestrel had known. Or suspected. Or feared. Kestrel had looked at that seal and seen the same handwriting Wren was seeing now.

The lamp sat on the table. The fleet sat on the horizon. Between them was a boy with a choice, and the choice had already changed shape while he wasn't looking.

Because here was the worst part, the part that made his stomach fold over on itself: he had *almost done it*. Whatever happened next—whatever the crew decided, whatever the chest contained, whatever justice or ruin came up the river—he had spent three nights aiming a signal lamp at his own family and calling it navigation.

Guilt is a strange cargo. It doesn't weigh anything until you try to put it down.

Wren took the manifest page—just that one, folding it small, hiding it in his coat where the manual used to live—and slid the lamp back into the locker, and stood a moment in the dark chart room listening to Brannick snore and the river breathe and his own heart doing arithmetic it didn't want to do.

He had to tell someone. That was the thought that finally landed, solid as a mooring cleat. Not Kestrel—not yet, not alone, not with whatever history lived behind Kestrel's eyes—but someone. Odile, maybe. Odile, who touched the boat like it might vanish, who had an accent that came out after midnight, who had looked at that chest on the day they raised it like a woman looking at a grave she'd been told was empty.

He turned for the door.

And stopped.

Because through the chart room's rear porthole, out past the rail, out past the dark water, the commodore's ship had lit a new signal. Wren read it without meaning to, the code living in his bones whether he wanted it there or not.

*Grid sweep resumes at dawn. All vessels in sector will be boarded and inspected.*

Dawn. Four hours.

And beneath it, in the smaller repeating lamp, flashing patient as a pulse, a single code Wren had never seen used outside the manual's appendix—the one reserved for vessels harboring persons of interest.

They weren't hunting the chest anymore.

They were hunting someone aboard.

* * *

Wren climbed the ladder to the crow's nest on legs that felt borrowed, and looked down through the deck hatch at the dim shapes of his sleeping family—Sefa curled around a cookpot like it owed her money, Brannick sprawled honest and enormous, Odile's hammock swaying empty because of course it was empty, she was somewhere below touching the hull again, keeping the boat real.

Four hours. One folded page against his ribs. One confession, due immediately, to a woman whose brother's name might be on the list he hadn't finished reading.

He took a breath that tasted of river and rust and the end of believing in easy things, and started down.
