## 11. The Brother's Ledger

Odile had been carrying the chest's secrets for nine days, and they weighed more than the lead lining ever had.

She told herself it was caution. Caution was a respectable word for cowardice, and she had built a whole life out of respectable words—*navigator* instead of *runaway*, *accent* instead of *home*, *I'd rather not say* instead of every true thing she'd swallowed since she was nineteen years old. Nine days of swallowing. The documents sat in her sea chest beneath a false bottom she'd made from an old chart case, and every time Sefa banged around the galley or Wren laughed too loudly at his own jokes, Odile felt the weight of them pressing up through the deck planks like water finding a seam.

Tonight the Tern lay moored in a backwater cut, tied to a dead cottonwood that leaned over the river like an old man telling a secret. Kestrel had taken the middle watch alone, which meant the deck was theirs and the cabin was hers, and Brannick was snoring in the engine room with the particular thunderous contentment of a man who had fixed something difficult that afternoon.

It was time.

Odile lit the small lamp, turned its wick low, and lifted the false bottom.

The documents came out in the order she'd hidden them, because she was a navigator and order was how she loved things: the manifests first, then the letters, then the ledger. She had read them all eleven times. She knew them the way she knew the river's sandbars—not as information but as geography, a country she could walk through with her eyes shut.

Her brother's handwriting was in three places. She didn't need to look. She looked anyway.

*Tomas Vey.* Floodgate engineer, second class, Vessian Bureau of Waterworks. A man who wrote his sevens with a little crossbar because their mother had drilled it into him. A man who had once spent an entire summer teaching Odile to skip stones across the millpond and had cried, quietly, at her wedding, and pretended he hadn't.

The last entry in his hand was dated eleven days before the flood.

*They are going to close the gates during the spring tide and call it a failure of the levees. I have written to the Tribunal. I have written to the Compact liaison. If you receive this, I was wrong about one of them.*

He had been wrong about both.

Odile folded the ledger closed with hands that did not shake, because twenty years of practice will teach even a grieving woman steady hands, and climbed the ladder to the deck.

Kestrel stood at the rail with a mug of Sefa's terrible chicory, watching the black water slide past. They didn't turn when Odile's boots hit the deck—they never did; hearing her come was one of the hundred small competencies Kestrel carried like coins in a pocket—but their shoulders shifted a fraction, making room. It was, Odile thought not for the first time, the most affectionate thing about them.

"You're off-watch," Kestrel said.

"I couldn't sleep."

"Nobody on this boat can sleep. We're a crew of insomniacs held together by Sefa's cooking." A pause. "Which is also why nobody sleeps well."

Despite everything—the ledger against her ribs, the words she'd rehearsed forty times burning her throat—Odile smiled. That was the trap of this crew, the beautiful trap: they made you laugh at exactly the moments you'd decided to be grave.

"Kestrel." She set the lamp on the rail between them. "I have to show you something. And then I have to ask you for something. And you're going to want to throw me off this boat, so I'd like it noted that I'm doing this anyway."

That got their attention. Kestrel turned, and in the lamplight their face did the thing it always did when the world presented a bill—went very still, very flat, the face of someone counting.

"Show me."

Odile untied the oilcloth bundle from inside her shirt and laid it on the rail. She opened it the way you open a wound: quickly, all at once, before you could talk yourself out of it.

"I opened the chest," she said. "Nine days ago. The night we pulled it up. While everyone slept."

Kestrel said nothing. That was worse than shouting. Odile kept going before the silence could become a wall.

"The manifests list the salvage the Compact pulled from Vessia in the first year after the flood. Archive materials. Tribunal records. Things that were supposed to have burned." She tapped the top sheet. "These letters are from engineers at the Bureau of Waterworks. And this—" her hand hovered over the ledger, and for a moment she could not make herself touch it, "—this is my brother's."

"Your brother's," Kestrel repeated.

"Tomas Vey. He designed floodgates. Third gate on the eastern barrage had his name stamped inside the housing—I know because he showed me, once, when I was small, the way other men show off a boat or a medal." Her voice was doing something strange, level and distant, as if it belonged to someone else standing on the deck. "He figured out what they were going to do. He tried to warn people. Eleven days before the water came, he wrote that entry, and then the water came, and Tomas Vey has been listed among the dead for twenty years, except he isn't dead, Kestrel. He was silenced. There's a difference, and the difference is a crime, and the men who committed it—" she turned the ledger to the final page, where the names were listed in a clerk's neat hand, receipted and signed, "—are on this list. And some of them sit on the Salvage Compact right now."

The river made its long dark noise beneath them. Somewhere aft, Brannick rolled over in his bunk and the engine room floorboards complained.

Kestrel picked up the ledger. Odile watched them read—watched the lamplight move across their face as the pages turned—and understood, with the clarity that comes only at the worst times, that Kestrel had known something since the moment the seal came out of the water. Had gone cold on that first day and ordered the chest sealed, and Odile had thought: *pragmatism*. She had thought: *our captain is careful*. She had not let herself think the truer thing, which was that Kestrel Nakamura recognized that seal the way a man recognizes a scar in a mirror.

"You knew what Vessia was," Odile said quietly. "Before tonight."

"I knew what the seal was." Kestrel set the ledger down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might go off. "Everyone old enough to drink knows what the seal was. It's on half the salvage on this river."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Kestrel said. "It isn't."

They looked at each other across the lamp. Odile had navigated by harder stars than this one, but not many.

"Here is what I want," she said. "I want us to take these documents to the river tribunals. The floating courts—the ones at Lockmast and Ternwater, the ones that still hear cases under old river law. This is evidence. Real evidence, sealed, signed, in the Compact's own clerks' hands. They can't bury it if it's already in front of a tribunal. They can't drown it twice."

Kestrel laughed. It was a short, awful sound, with no humor in it anywhere, and Odile realized she had been on this boat two years and had never once heard Kestrel laugh like that, and hoped never to again.

"The tribunals," Kestrel said.

"Yes."

"The river tribunals that operate under Compact charter. The tribunals whose clerks are paid from the Compact's salvage revenues. The tribunals that would take one look at this ledger and one look at the names on it and hand it straight upstairs with a bow." Kestrel shook their head slowly. "Odile. You can't deliver a knife to a man by handing it to his hand."

"Some of the magistrates are honest."

"Some of anything is anything. One honest magistrate out of twelve means your brother's ledger spends the rest of its life in an evidence locker in Lockmast until a fire takes it, or a clerk misfiles it, or a magistrate who *wasn't* honest gets promoted." Kestrel's voice stayed level, but Odile could hear the current underneath it, fast and deep. "You don't put truth in front of power and ask it to behave. Power reads truth the way a dredge reads a riverbed. It takes what's valuable and piles the rest on the spoil bank."

"Then what?" Odile heard her own voice rise and didn't stop it. "What do we do? Sell it? Is that the plan? Because I've watched you circle that chest for nine days like a man pricing a coffin, and I'll tell you now—if you sell my brother's last words to some fence in a floating market, I will—" She stopped. What? What would she do? Leave the only family she had? The threat collapsed under its own weight, and Kestrel saw it collapse, and had the grace not to smile.

"You'll what?" Kestrel asked gently.

"Nothing. I'll do nothing. That's the worst part." Odile pressed her palms flat against the rail. "I've been doing nothing for twenty years. I changed my vowels and my name and I let a whole country stay drowned and quiet, because quiet was safer, because I was nineteen and alone and the Compact had lists and I might be on one. And then we hauled a piece of my home out of this river with a rope, Kestrel, and I held my brother's handwriting in my hands, and I am done being safe. Somebody has to carry this to a court. Even a bad court. Especially a bad court—it's the only kind left."

For a long moment Kestrel didn't answer. They picked up the chicory, found it cold, and drank it anyway, which Odile had privately always considered the most honest thing about them.

"Let me tell you what happens," Kestrel said at last, and their voice had changed—lower, wearier, the voice of someone reciting rather than arguing. "We dock at Lockmast. You walk into the tribunal hall with your bundle. The magistrate is honest—you get lucky, the way you never have before. He opens the ledger. He sees the name Ashvael on the signature page. And before the ink is dry on the intake record, there's a Compact skiff at the dock, and a warrant, and a very polite officer explaining that the documents are stolen salvage property and you're a thief, and possibly a Vessian asset, which is a phrase with a cell attached to it. And then the Tern has a choice. Sail away without you, or sail after you into a Compact port with a patrol fleet already looking for a reason." They set down the mug. "I know how it goes because I've been the polite officer, Odile. I've been the skiff at the dock."

There it was. Not a confession—Kestrel would sooner scuttle than confess—but a door left open a crack, warm light spilling through, enough to see the shape of whatever room they kept locked.

"I know," Odile said softly. "I've known for two years. You hold your cup in your left hand and pour with your right, and enforcers are trained to pour with their right. Your river-reading is military gridwork underneath the folk craft. And once, in Ternwater, a lock-keeper called you 'sir' before he caught himself, and you gave him a month's wages to forget it." She shrugged. "I navigate. I notice things."

Something moved behind Kestrel's eyes—alarm, maybe, or relief, or the strange grief of a person discovering their prison has had windows all along.

"So you understand," Kestrel said. "When I say the tribunals will eat this ledger, I'm not being careful. I'm being *accurate*. There's a difference, and the difference is I've watched it happen."

"And when I say we try anyway," Odile answered, "I'm not being naive. I'm being Vessian. We were a nation of canal-builders, Kestrel. Do you know what my people believed? That water finds its level. That you cannot dam a river forever—you can only decide whether it rises gently or breaks. Twenty years ago the wrong people decided for us." She laid her hand flat on the ledger, on her brother's crossed sevens. "This time I'd like to decide."

Kestrel looked at her for a long time. The lamp hissed. Downriver, faint as a memory, a bell buoy clanked its slow count.

"If we go to a tribunal," Kestrel said finally, "we go armed with more than paper. We go knowing which magistrates can't be bought, which clerks will run, which dock we can leave from in ninety seconds. And we go *after* the six tides run out—because right now that fleet is sweeping grids toward our route, and I will not hand my crew to Ashvael's cranes to make a point." They rubbed their jaw. "And Odile—we don't go because you beg me. We go because the crew votes. All of them. Wren, Sefa, Brannick, everyone. If we're carrying your brother's ghost into a Compact port, every soul aboard carries him too, and every soul aboard deserves a say."

Relief hit Odile so hard she nearly sat down on the deck. She converted it, with twenty years of practice, into a nod.

"A vote," she agreed. "Fair. Though I warn you, I intend to campaign shamelessly. Sefa owes me for the card games. Brannick can't resist a lost cause. And Wren—"

"Wren thinks the Compact hangs the moon," Kestrel said dryly. "Good luck with that."

"He thinks the Compact *used* to hang the moon. He's young. Young people update their maps." Odile gathered the documents, rewrapped them in the oilcloth with hands that were, at last, allowed to shake a little. "Thank you. For not throwing me overboard."

"Don't thank me yet." Kestrel turned back to the rail, to the black water sliding past, and their voice dropped to something almost private. "Because here's the part I haven't said. Part of me wants you to be right. Part of me has wanted it for twenty years, since the night I stood in a Vessian archive with a torch in my hand and told myself it was sedition I was burning." The words came out flat and fast, like cargo shoved through a hatch. "If you're right, then there's a version of me that could have stopped it and didn't. So understand what you're asking. You're not asking me to deliver a ledger. You're asking me to find out what I am."

Odile stood very still. The bell buoy counted on, patient as tide.

"Then come find out," she said.

Kestrel didn't answer. But they didn't say no either, and on the Tern, Odile had learned, that was practically a signature.

She went below with the ledger against her heart—and stopped halfway down the ladder, because the lamp in the chart room was burning, and Wren was sitting at the table with the signal lamp's shutter key in his hand, and the look on his face was the look of a boy who had just done something he could not undo.
