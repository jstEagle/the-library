## 12. Mutiny at Low Water

Brannick had spent thirty years learning that an engine tells you everything before it breaks. A rattle in the fuel line. A shiver in the mounts. A smell like burnt sugar when the governor's getting tired. Boats were honest that way. People were not, and the *Tern* was currently carrying six of the second kind and one of the first, and only the first one was behaving itself.

He was elbow-deep in the coolant manifold when Sefa came down the ladder, which was itself a warning. Sefa did not visit the engine room. Sefa considered the engine room a place where joy went to be compressed into a dense, greasy brick. She stood at the bottom of the ladder with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and Brannick knew, the way he knew a bearing was about to go, that something had already gone.

"You look like a woman with a confession," he said, not looking up.

"I look like a woman with a headache."

"Same thing on this boat." He wiped his hands on a rag that had been black since the previous owner. "Out with it."

She didn't sit. She paced the narrow aisle between the boiler and the bulkhead, three steps each way, which was all the pacing the engine room allowed. "At the market town. The fence. Vasko."

"I remember Vasko. Man with the laugh like a door hinge."

"He knew Kestrel. Not as Kestrel." She stopped pacing. "He called them by another name. An old one. And he said—" Her voice did something Brannick had never heard it do, which was falter. Sefa, who could haggle down a fishmonger twice her size, who had once talked a Compact customs officer out of inspecting a hold full of untaxed pepper by complimenting his hat for twenty minutes. "He said they used to burn things. For the Compact. Archives. He said Kestrel was an enforcer, Brannick. A *burner*."

The manifold wrench went very still in Brannick's hand.

He thought about all the things he had never asked Kestrel Nakamura. Where the scar on their left forearm came from. Why they flinched, just barely, at the word *Vessia*, the way a man flinches at a sound he's been pretending not to hear for years. Why a captain with their skill ran a boat this small, this far downriver, this carefully invisible.

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure Vasko believed it. I'm sure Kestrel didn't say no." Sefa's laugh came out wrong, more like a cough. "I didn't say anything. I paid him, we loaded the parts, I came home. And I've been carrying it around in my chest for two weeks like a stone in my apron pocket."

Brannick set the wrench down. "Why tell me and not them?"

"Because I don't know what to do with it yet, and you're the one who always knows what to do with things."

"That's a terrible reason to trust me."

"It's the only reason anyone trusts you."

He couldn't argue with that. It was, in fact, the entire architecture of his position aboard the *Tern*: he fixed things, and people mistook that for wisdom.

"Here's what we do with it," he said. "Nothing. For now. Kestrel gets to be the one who tells us, when they're ready. That's the deal we all made when we signed on, even if nobody said it out loud. Everybody on this boat has a locked room. We don't pick each other's locks."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment, and then nodded, and went back up the ladder, and Brannick returned to the manifold, and the stone in his own chest sat there, heavier than hers, because he had known. Not the specifics. But he had known there was a room, and he had spent years walking past its door, and now somebody had told him what was behind it, and he found he missed the not-knowing.

* * *

The signal was discovered by accident, which was how most disasters aboard the *Tern* got their start.

It was Odile who found it, two nights later, and Brannick only learned the details afterward, pieced together from the wreckage of the conversation in the chart room. She had gone in for the tide tables and found the signal lamp's shutter key out of its drawer, and the lamp itself angled toward the southern horizon, and a page torn from Wren's logbook with a grid reference written on it in his careful, schoolroom hand. A grid reference that matched, almost exactly, the last known position of the Compact's lead ship.

Wren didn't even lie well. He sat on the chart bench with his knees together and his ears going red and said, "I was practicing," which was the kind of sentence that had never once been true in the history of sentences.

"Practicing," Odile repeated. Her voice was very quiet, which anyone who knew her understood to be the loudest thing she could do.

"Signaling. For when I get my own boat. You have to practice."

"Three nights running," Odile said. "Toward the Compact fleet."

The chart room went so quiet Brannick, standing in the doorway with a cup of chicory he had no intention of drinking, could hear the river slapping the hull. Kestrel was there. Sefa was there. Wren looked at each of them in turn, hunting for an ally, and finding none, and Brannick watched something in the boy's face go from cornered to defiant, which was worse.

"Fine," Wren said. "Yes. I signaled them. Once. Maybe twice. I told them where we'd be and I asked if a surrendered crew gets treated fair, and the answer was *yes*, it was in the Compact's own maritime code, article eleven, I looked it up—"

"You looked up the code," Kestrel said. Their voice was flat and strange. "You signaled the fleet hunting us, and you comforted yourself with their code."

"They have *rules*—"

"They have a crane-and-chain flag and three iron ships, Wren. Their rule is that they win."

"They burned a raft," Wren said, and his voice cracked straight down the middle. "I know. I *saw*. But that was one ship, that was one bad captain, the Compact is bigger than one bad captain, it's *order*, it's the only reason there's law on this river at all, and if we just tell them the truth—"

"The truth," Odile said, "is in that chest, and the chest says the Compact's commodore helped drown a country, and you signaled that commodore our position." She said it without heat, which somehow made it land like a hammer. "Article eleven of what, Wren? What code covers that?"

Brannick watched the boy's face and saw the exact moment the ideal died — not with a shout but with a kind of quiet collapse, like a mast going. Wren looked down at his own hands as if they belonged to someone who had disappointed him.

"I didn't know," he said. "I swear on the river, I didn't know what was in the chest."

"You didn't want to know," Sefa said. "There's a difference, love. I'd know. I've done it myself."

And then, because the night apparently had decided to be thorough, Sefa looked at Kestrel and said, "And since we're doing locked rooms tonight — Vasko knew you. Your old name. He said you used to burn archives for them."

Brannick closed his eyes.

* * *

They ended up on the aft deck because the chart room had become too small to hold what was in it. Low water, the *Tern* riding high and light on the mud-flats off a reed island, the moon doing its best and failing. Kestrel stood at the rail. The others arranged themselves in the loose, defensive semicircle of a crew that has stopped being a crew for the moment and become a jury.

Kestrel didn't deny it. Brannick had known they wouldn't. Kestrel Nakamura lied beautifully about small things — cargo weights, customs forms, the quality of Sefa's stew — and had never once, in all the years Brannick had sailed with them, lied about a large one.

"It's true," Kestrel said. "I was an enforcer. Twenty-two years old and certain the Compact was civilization and everything outside it was rot. They gave me a torch and a list and told me the Vessian archives were sedition. I burned them. I was good at it. I got a commendation." A pause. The river filled it. "The list was evidence. I just didn't know that yet."

"You could have told us," Sefa said.

"Could I have?" Kestrel turned, and Brannick saw the exhaustion on their face, the kind that doesn't come from lack of sleep. "When? Day one, when you were all deciding whether to sign on with a stranger? Year two, when Wren used to draw the Compact flag on his charts like it was a saint's icon? When, Sefa? Name the moment it would have been safe."

Sefa opened her mouth. Closed it. Brannick understood. There wasn't a moment. That was the trap of a found family — you built it out of the people who didn't ask, and then one day the not-asking became the thing between you.

"And you," Kestrel said to Odile, gently. "Your accent. Your brother. The documents you've been reading at night and putting back."

Odile's chin came up. "I was going to tell you."

"You were going to tell me when it suited you. Same as all of us." Kestrel laughed, and there was no humor in it, just a kind of terrible clarity. "Look at us. One chest, and every secret on this boat has come up like bodies after a flood. Wren signaled the fleet. Sefa's been carrying Vasko's words for two weeks. Odile's been reading her dead brother's name by lamplight and saying nothing. And I—" They spread their hands. "I'm the reason there was ever anything to find."

"The vote," Brannick said.

Everyone looked at him.

He hadn't planned to say it. It had simply come up out of him the way the truth comes up out of an engine — a pressure that has to go somewhere. But now that it was said, he found he meant it.

"We're past talking," Brannick said. "Talking's how we got here, everyone talking around the things that matter. So we vote. That's the one thing we've never done — we've always just *agreed*, because agreeing was easy. Well. Sell the chest to the auctioneer, take the money, scatter to four corners of the river and never sail together again. Or keep it, and fight, and see what the truth costs us. Everyone gets one vote. Everyone's vote weighs the same, including the boy who signaled the fleet and the captain who burned the archives. That's the whole point of us, isn't it? That nobody's past buys them a bigger voice."

"It's not that simple—" Kestrel started.

"It's exactly that simple. That's what makes it terrible."

* * *

They voted by lamplight in the chart room, because Brannick insisted it be done properly, at the chart table, where all their courses had been laid. He went last, on purpose.

Kestrel voted to fight. Brannick had expected that — had understood, watching them at the rail, that something had already turned over in them, some engine part that could not be un-turned. "I've spent twenty years running from what I did," they said simply. "I'm tired of the running being the punishment. Let the truth be the punishment. It's heavier, but at least it goes somewhere."

Odile voted to fight. She didn't explain. She didn't have to. Brannick thought of her brother's name on a list of engineers, and understood that for her this had stopped being a decision somewhere around chapter five of her own story, as it were.

Sefa voted to sell. "I love every one of you," she said, and her voice wobbled and she hated it, Brannick could see her hating it. "I love you enough to say the truth: we're six people on a wooden boat, and they're three iron ships. I've got debts. Wren's got a whole life ahead of him. You don't spend children on principles."

Wren voted to sell, and his voice was small when he did it. "I'm sorry," he said, to all of them, to the room, to the river. "I'm sorry about the signal. I just — I wanted the world to be the thing I thought it was."

"It isn't," Odile said, and put her hand over his, briefly. "But you can still be the thing *you* thought you were. That part's up to you."

Two to two. Brannick felt every eye come around to him and wished, not for the first time, that he had been born a man who could fix engines and nothing else.

He thought about the *Tern* — not the boat, the thing the boat had become. Six people who should never have found each other, stitched together out of debts and accents and locked rooms. He thought about how a family wasn't the absence of secrets. It was what you did the day the secrets came up.

He thought about Sefa's stone in her apron pocket, and how she'd carried it two weeks rather than set it down, and how the carrying had hurt her worse than any setting-down would.

"We scatter," Brannick said, "and we're rich, and we're strangers, and in twenty years some other boat pulls some other chest out of some other river, and there's nobody left who remembers why it mattered. We fight, and maybe we die, and maybe worse — maybe we win and have to live with what winning costs." He looked at each of them. "But we'd be *us* when we did it. Whatever we are. Whatever we've been hiding. I'd rather be that, together, than anything apart."

He put his hand flat on the chart table.

"Fight," he said.

Three to two.

Nobody cheered. That wasn't the kind of moment it was. Sefa cried a little, quietly, and pretended she wasn't. Wren stared at the deck. Kestrel exhaled like a diver surfacing.

It was Odile who broke the silence, standing at the chart room's little window, looking south toward the dark water.

"Brannick," she said. "The tide tables. Come look at this."

He came. She was pointing at the column for the southern cut — the narrow water between the reed islands and the mainland shallows, the only route the fleet could take to corner them if they ran south.

The low water was wrong. Not by much. By a hand's width. But wrong, and Brannick, who had spent thirty years learning that everything tells you before it breaks, felt the cold walk up his spine.

"That's not the river," he said.

"No," Odile said. "That's the floodgates. Someone's holding the water back."

And far off to the south, past the reeds, past the dark, a light blinked once. Twice. Three times.

Deliberate. Patient. Like something that already knew where they were.
