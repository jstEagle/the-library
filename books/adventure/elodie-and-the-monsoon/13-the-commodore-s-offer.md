## 13. The Commodore's Offer

The cut at Sallow Bend was the kind of place Kestrel loved and feared in equal measure. The river narrowed there to barely two boat-lengths wide, squeezed between bluffs furred with dead reeds, and the current ran fast and clean through the middle like a held breath. On a good day it was the fastest stretch of water between the floating market and the marsh hamlets—a shortcut only the Tern could take without scraping paint.

On a bad day, it was a trap.

Kestrel stood at the wheel and did the arithmetic they'd been doing all morning, the same arithmetic that had kept them awake since the sandbars. Fuel: enough for six hours of running, four if they pushed. Damage: patched, but patching was a promise, not a fact. And behind them, somewhere past the last bend, three iron-hulled ships flying the crane-and-chain.

"They'll catch us in open water," Odile said quietly. She'd come up beside them without making a sound, the way she always did, as if the deck were reluctant to announce her. "You know that."

"I know we're not in open water yet."

"That's not an answer, Captain."

"It's the only answer I have left." Kestrel eased the wheel a hair to starboard, feeling the Tern answer like a tired animal that still trusted its handler. "Sallow Bend's ahead. If we can get through before they close the mouth—"

"Then what?"

"Then I'll invent a then."

Odile almost smiled. It was a thin thing, her smile these days, worn down by ledgers and brothers and twenty years of careful silence. But it was there, and Kestrel took it the way you take warmth from a small fire when the wood is running out.

Below, Brannick had the engine singing as sweetly as she could sing, which for a salvaged riverboat engine meant a tune with a limp in it. Sefa was in the galley pretending to cook, which mostly meant standing where he could hear everything and comment on none of it. Wren was aloft in the crow's nest, and Kestrel hadn't missed the way the boy's eyes went to the horizon every thirty seconds, or the way his hands wouldn't stay still around the rail. Guilt had a posture. Wren was wearing it.

The bluffs closed in. The light went green-gold through the reeds. For eleven minutes, the world was just water and speed and the good honest racket of Brannick's engine, and Kestrel let themselves believe, briefly, that rivers still belonged to the people who knew them.

Then the sound came.

It rolled up the cut like a struck bell—the deep, carrying note of a ship's horn, amplified by the narrow stone until it wasn't a sound so much as a hand pressed flat against the chest. Ahead of them, at the mouth of the bend, a gray shape slid out of the haze and settled across the channel like a door closing.

The *Vigilant*. Commodore Ashvael's flagship. Iron hull, crane-and-chain snapping at the masthead, and a bow gun that didn't need to be run out to make its argument.

Behind them, faint but unmistakable, a second horn answered. The other two ships had reached the tail of the cut.

Kestrel brought the Tern around in a slow, unwilling arc and killed the engine. There was no version of this where they ran. There was only the version where they chose how to stop.

* * *

"Captain." Brannick climbed halfway up the companionway ladder, oil to his elbows, face carefully blank in the way of a man who has already done his panicking privately. "I heard it. We all heard it."

"I know."

"What do you want me to do?"

"Stay by the engine. If I raise my right hand, you bring her back to life and you take us at the *Vigilant*'s stern like your life depends on it. Because it will."

Brannick looked at them for a long moment. Whatever he saw in Kestrel's face made him nod once and go below without another word. That was the thing about Brannick: he understood that some orders weren't really about the order. They were about being ready to be brave on someone else's signal.

Sefa appeared next, wiping his hands on his apron, which was how he appeared everywhere. "I've got a pot of pepper stew going," he said. "If we're going to die, we're going to die smelling wonderful."

"Not dying today, Sefa."

"Mm." He squinted at the gray shape blocking the river. "That's a very large ship to say 'not dying' at, Captain."

"Go stand near Odile."

"She's already standing near me. She does that when she's worried. She thinks nobody notices."

"I notice," Kestrel said, and Sefa's eyebrows went up, and then he went and stood near Odile, and Odile pretended she hadn't been waiting for him.

Wren came down from the crow's nest last, moving slowly, and stopped at the base of the mast as if his legs had opinions his head hadn't approved. His face was the color of old candle wax.

"Wren," Kestrel said.

"Yes, Captain."

"You have something to tell me?"

A pause. A long one. The river ticked against the hull. Somewhere in the reeds a bird complained about all of it.

"No, Captain," Wren said, and Kestrel looked at him, and Wren looked at the deck, and neither of them said anything else, and the lie sat there between them like a third crew member nobody had hired.

Later, Kestrel thought. Later, when we're not all about to be shot.

* * *

The hail came over the water in a voice made of brass and authority, from a speaking-trumpet wielded by someone who clearly enjoyed the acoustics of narrow cuts.

"*Vessel Tern. This is Commodore Ashvael of the Salvage Compact fleet. You are ordered to heave to and prepare for boarding. Resistance will be logged.*"

*Logged*, Kestrel thought. As if the Compact's cruelty were a ledger entry. As if anything they did ever went unwritten down.

They stood at the rail, hands visible, and watched a launch drop from the *Vigilant*'s side. Six marines in gray, oars stroking in perfect unison, and standing in the bow—not sitting, standing, because of course standing—with one hand on the gunwale and the other holding a folded document, a man whose uniform fit him the way a reputation fits a name: too well, and slightly smug about it.

Commodore Ashvael came aboard the Tern himself.

Kestrel had imagined this moment, in the way you imagine disasters—vaguely, superstitiously, hoping the imagining would act as insurance. In the imaginings, Ashvael had been a monster. What stepped onto their deck was worse: a tidy man of perhaps fifty-five with silver at his temples and a face built for official portraits, who looked around the Tern's deck with the frank, cataloguing interest of someone appraising furniture.

"Captain Nakamura," he said.

The name went through Kestrel like cold water through a cracked hull.

Not Kestrel. Not *Captain*. Nakamura. The name that had a bounty on it in three jurisdictions. The name they had buried under a river of aliases and a decade of careful, exhausting anonymity. Nobody alive should have known it. Nobody except—

Beside Sefa, Odile had gone very still. Stillness was her native language, but this was different. This was the stillness of a woman watching a door she thought was locked swing open on its own.

"Commodore," Kestrel said, keeping their voice level, because the first rule of drowning is don't splash. "You have me at a disadvantage. You know my name; I only know your rank."

"Ashvael. Mireille Ashvael." She said it as though it should mean something, and perhaps it did, in the offices where such things mattered. She unfolded her document with two crisp movements. "I'm authorized to make you an offer, Captain. I'd ask for privacy, but I suspect your crew hears everything whether I whisper or not, so we'll do this in the open, like professionals."

"Generous."

"I'm told I have a reputation for it." She smiled. It was a good smile. It had been practiced on many people who are now less wealthy than they expected to be. "Here is the offer. You surrender the chest—unopened, seals intact—and the Compact pays you a sum that would buy you a shipyard, not just a ship. Full pardon for the bounty on your former identity. Your record expunged. Your crew walks away untouched, free to work the river under whatever names they like. And—" her gaze moved, deliberate as a searchlight, and settled on Odile, "—full pardon and Compact protection for your navigator. Miss Odile Vessarin, isn't it? Or shall we use whatever name she's been sailing under? Either way. The Compact takes care of its assets."

The word landed on the deck between them all like something dropped from a great height.

*Asset.*

Kestrel felt it click into place, piece by piece, the way Brannick's engine clicked into gear—the patrol skiff in chapter six, no, the patrol skiff weeks ago, asking questions that were a little too specific; the sweep grid that seemed to know the Tern's route; the fence at the market town who'd looked at Odile a half-second too long. They hadn't been hunting the chest. Or rather—they hadn't only been hunting the chest. Six tides of search grid, three iron ships, a commodore personally commanding the operation, all for a sealed box pulled off a river bottom?

No. You didn't send a fleet for paper. You sent a fleet for people.

They'd been hunting Odile all along. The chest was just the bait that had finally dragged her to the surface.

And beside Sefa, Odile lifted her chin by exactly one degree, which from Odile was a shout.

* * *

"That's a very generous offer," Kestrel said, and found, to their own surprise, that they meant the observation even as they despised it. "Pardon, payment, protection. You must want that chest very badly."

"I want what's in it very badly, yes."

"The manifests? The letters? The list of engineers?"

Ashvael's smile didn't move, but something behind it did—a flicker, quick as a fish turning under the surface. "You've opened it."

"One of us did."

"Then the offer changes." She said it smoothly, without heat, the way a person updates a price. "The pardon stands. The payment becomes negotiable. But the documents come to me, and Miss Vessarin comes with my fleet, and in exchange, Captain Nakamura, you and your crew sail out of this story entirely. No tribunal. No salvage court. No questions about how a former Compact enforcer came to be fishing drowned nations out of my river."

"My river," Kestrel repeated.

"All of it is mine. That's what the Compact means." She glanced around the deck—at Sefa, arms crossed over his apron; at Brannick's head poking up from the engine hatch; at Wren, who couldn't meet anyone's eyes; at Odile, straight-backed and silent and burning. "You've sailed together a long time, I imagine. Found family. Very touching. Very common, among people with things to hide." Her attention came back to Kestrel, and softened, horribly, into something almost kind. "I read your file, Nakamura. All of it. The archives at Vessia. The destruction order. You were young. You were following instructions. Do you know how many people on this river have a file like yours? The Compact forgives useful people. That's not cynicism—it's policy. Mercy is cheaper than vengeance, and it buys better."

"You're offering me mercy."

"I'm offering you a future. There's a difference, but it rarely matters to anyone who accepts."

Kestrel looked at her for a long moment. And here was the terrible thing, the thing they would carry afterward like ballast: for three full seconds, they wanted it. Three seconds of imagining the bounty gone, the name buried for good, the crew paid and safe and scattered to lives that didn't involve iron ships and burned rafts and commodores who said *asset* the way other people said *cargo*. Three seconds in which selling the chest and vanishing sounded not like betrayal but like wisdom.

Three seconds. Then their eyes found Odile.

She was looking at Ashvael with an expression Kestrel had never seen on her face—not fear, not grief, but recognition. Cold, complete recognition. She knew this woman. Or she knew what this woman was. Twenty years ago, in a country that was still above water, Odile had looked at faces like this one while they signed things.

"Your brother," Ashvael said to Odile, gently, terribly, "was a gifted engineer. The Compact remembers its people, Miss Vessarin. Even the difficult ones."

Something in Kestrel's chest went hard and final, like a lock throwing shut.

"No," they said.

Ashvael raised an eyebrow. "You haven't heard the number yet."

"It's not about the number." Kestrel stepped forward, putting themselves—broadly, uselessly, symbolically—between the commodore and their navigator. "Twenty years ago I followed instructions. I burned a nation's memory because men in clean uniforms told me it was sedition. I spent a decade learning what I actually burned. So let me save us both some time, Commodore: I am done being forgiven by the people who gave the orders. Keep your pardon. Keep your number. And keep your hands off my crew—including the ones with files, including the ones with secrets, including the ones who've lied to me, because apparently that's just what family is."

Ashvael studied them the way she'd studied the deck—as inventory. Then she sighed, folded her document along its original creases, and became, visibly, a different person: the smile banked, the warmth shuttered, the portrait-face replaced by the face that signed things.

"Heave to and prepare for boarding," she said to her marines, conversationally. "Log any resistance."

"Commodore." Kestrel's voice dropped low. "One more thing. You said the chest comes to you unopened, seals intact. It's already open. Which means your offer was never real—you knew that when you made it. Which means this whole speech was theater, staged for an audience." They tilted their head toward the crow's nest, toward the rail, toward the boy who couldn't look at anyone. "You wanted my crew to hear how cheap mercy is. You wanted one of them to take it."

For the first time, Mireille Ashvael looked genuinely pleased.

"Captains who survive as long as you have," she said, stepping back to the launch, "are usually undone by their crews, not their enemies. I do hope yours proves the exception, Nakamura. I truly do. I've grown rather fond of you in the last ten minutes."

She went over the side. The launch pulled away. The *Vigilant*'s boarding lines began to swing out across the narrowing gap between the ships, grappling hooks glinting, and behind them, at the tail of the cut, the second horn sounded again—closer now, much closer.

* * *

"Captain." Brannick's voice came up from the hatch, tight. "Engine's warm. Say the word."

Kestrel's right hand hung at their side. The signal. Bring her to life and take us at the *Vigilant*'s stern. It had seemed like a plan, hours ago, in the green-gold light of the reeds. Now it looked like what it was: a coin flip with iron on both sides.

The grapples arced. The first one hit the rail with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

And then Wren moved.

He was up the rigging before anyone could shout—up the ratlines two rungs at a time, the way he'd climbed since he was thirteen, toward the tow line the *Vigilant* had thrown to pin the Tern's bow, and Kestrel saw the knife in his hand and understood, all at once, everything: the sleepless nights, the signal, the guilt wearing him like a coat, and now this—whatever this was going to be.

"WREN!" Kestrel roared, and raised their right hand high, and below them Brannick threw the engine into life with a sound like a heart starting, and the Tern lurched forward into the gap between the grapples as above them, at the top of the rigging, the boy swung out over open water with a blade against the Compact's line—

—and the *Vigilant*'s deck guns, all along her gray flank, began to run out.
