## 14. Fire on the Water

Wren had always imagined that courage would feel like something. A warmth in the chest, maybe. A voice in the ear saying *now*. What it actually felt like, crouched behind a coil of tow cable on the deck of an iron Compact ship while his family's boat burned its last fuel to get away from him, was mostly nausea.

He'd done this. The signal. Three flashes on the lamp, the pattern every pilot child learned before they learned their letters: *vessel in distress, requesting assistance.* He'd told himself it wasn't betrayal. It was arithmetic. One boy's surrender against five lives saved by mercy. The Compact was order. Order was safety. He'd grown up on stories of the crane-and-chain pulling drowning towns out of the floodwater, and he'd believed them the way you believe in the tide — completely, without ever once asking who owned the moon.

Then he'd seen the manifest list in the chart room. Commodore Ashvael's name, third from the top, right there in the Compact's own tidy handwriting. *Engineer of record, Vessia floodgate project.* And below it, in smaller type, a column of numbers Wren understood all too well as a pilot: tonnage displaced. Population not evacuated. He'd stood there until the lantern oil burned low, and the arithmetic he'd been doing his whole life came apart in his hands like wet paper.

Now the fleet had answered his signal after all, just later than anyone wanted, and the Tern was cornered in the narrows with three iron hulls closing like a hand.

* * *

The boarding attempt began, as most terrible things on the river do, with paperwork.

A launch put off from the lead ship, six marines in oilskin and a compact officer — Wren noted the irony distantly, hysterically — holding a clipboard under a storm lantern. Behind the launch, two of the iron ships were positioning themselves fore and aft of the Tern, and Wren saw the tow lines come out. Not grappling hooks. Tow lines. They weren't going to board so much as *collect*, the way you collect driftwood or a derelict. The Tern would be hauled to a Compact anchorage, impounded, inventoried. Crew processed. Cargo seized.

Cargo. Odile's brother's ledger. Twenty years of drowned truth, weighed and stamped and filed into a hole no one would ever open again.

Wren was on the Tern still — they hadn't locked him up, which somehow made it worse; Kestrel had only looked at him once, a look with no heat in it at all, which is the coldest kind — but when the fleet closed in, Kestrel had ordered everyone aft to defend the stern boarding point, and Wren had been left at the bow rail with a boat hook and his own conscience, both equally useless.

The first tow line arced across the gap and hit the Tern's forward bollard with a sound like a bell dying. A second followed. On the lead ship, a capstan began to turn, and Wren felt the Tern shudder as the slack came out of the lines.

That was when he saw the fuel.

They'd been burning reserve for two days running the sandbars, and Brannick had decanted the dregs into deck drums because the good engineer never leaves fuel where a shell burst can find it. Two drums sat lashed at the bow, half-full, sloshing quietly. And between the Tern and the lead ship, the water was slicked with it — a rainbow skin of spilled diesel trailing from a bad gasket, pooling in the calm of the narrows, a hundred yards of river wearing a coat of fire just waiting for a match.

Wren looked at the tow lines. Both of them ran from the capstans on the lead ship, across that slicked water, to the Tern's bollards. The lines themselves were hemp over wire, tarred, and tarred hemp burns the way ambition burns: fast, and all the way down.

He thought about the manifest. He thought about Sefa teaching him to fold dumplings, laughing at how he pleated them like envelopes. He thought about Kestrel's cold look, which he deserved, and Odile's face when she'd said her brother's name, which he hadn't earned the right to think about yet but did anyway.

Then he took out his knife and started cutting, and when the cut wouldn't go through the wire core, he stopped cutting and started thinking, because courage without craft is just noise, and if there was one thing a pilot knew, it was that everything on a river is connected to everything else by exactly one rope, one valve, or one bad habit.

* * *

Here is what Wren Nakamura-no-longer-quite-sure-of-his-own-name knew about tow lines, having spent three years listening to Brannick complain about them:

They were designed to part cleanly under strain, with a weak link spliced in near the ship end, so that if a tow snagged, the line broke at a predictable point instead of tearing a capstan out of the deck. The weak link was a short section of thinner wire, wrapped in serving twine, painted yellow so some idiot wouldn't splice over it.

Wren was, historically, exactly such an idiot. But tonight the idiot knew where the yellow paint was.

If the lines parted at the weak links, nothing much happened. The fleet would just re-rig. But if the lines parted anywhere else — if they parted *late*, under enormous strain, with the capstans still turning and forty fathoms of tarred hemp whipping back across a slicked river —

Well. Brannick had a saying about that. *The river forgives nothing, but it forgets even less.*

Wren couldn't reach the weak links. They were on the fleet's side. What he could reach was the tension itself. The capstans were hauling the Tern toward the lead ship, and the Tern's engine was dead — no, not dead, Kestrel had banked it, saving the last fuel for one final run. Which meant the whole strain of the tow sat on those lines like a held breath.

All he had to do was make sure that when the breath let out, it let out *fire*.

There was a signal locker at the bow. In it: flares. Six of them, the old kind, the kind that burned magnesium-hot and stuck to whatever they touched. Compact issue, traded for at a market town two seasons ago, and Sefa had grumbled about the price for a week.

Wren took two. Then he did the thing that scared him more than anything else that night, more than the fleet, more than Kestrel's eyes: he went aft, past the fighting at the stern rail where Brannick was swinging a spanner with the serenity of a man pruning roses, and he found Kestrel.

"I need the engine," Wren said. "One minute of it. Full astern."

Kestrel turned. The look was back, but different now — measuring, the way Kestrel measured weather. "Why."

"Because the tow lines are rigged wrong and I can break them, but I need the strain to spike first." His voice cracked on *spike*, which was fine, honest even. "Trust me or don't. But decide fast."

Something moved behind Kestrel's face — Wren would spend a long time deciding it was hope — and then Kestrel said, "Brannick! Give the boy his minute," and went back to the rail, and that was that. No speech. No forgiveness. Just a helm order, which on the Tern was the same thing.

* * *

The Tern surged backward. The capstans on the lead ship, set for a dead weight, suddenly hauled against an engine's full fury, and somewhere in the dark the weak links sang a high thin note — and held, because of course they held, because the universe had a sense of humor — but the *bollards* didn't hold, because Wren had already, in the ten minutes prior, quietly unscrewed the retaining pins on both of them, a trick he'd learned from watching Brannick curse at a bollard in the rain for an entire afternoon.

Both lines ripped free of the Tern and snapped back across the water like the tails of angry gods. And here was the part Wren had calculated and the part he hadn't:

The calculation: the snapping lines would drag through the diesel slick, throwing spray, churning the fuel into the water column, spreading it wide.

The part he hadn't calculated: the lines, whipping free, would wrap around the lead ship's own capstan housing, and the friction of forty fathoms of tarred hemp paying out at speed against iron would do what friction does.

The first glow was small. An orange seam along the lead ship's fo'c'sle, like someone lighting a cigarette in the dark. Then the slick caught.

Fire on water doesn't behave like fire on land. It *runs*. It ran down the rainbow skin of the narrows in both directions at once, a road of flame laying itself between the ships, and the lead ship's deck crew started shouting in the flat panicked voices of people whose training never covered their own boat being on fire.

Wren stood at the bow rail with two flares in his hands and realized he was now, technically, the most dangerous person on the river, and that what he felt wasn't courage after all. It was something louder and stupider and better.

He fired the first flare low, across the water, into the thickest of the slick between the Tern and the second iron ship, closing the southern gap with a wall of flame. The second flare he threw — actually threw, arm screaming, a throw Wren would describe modestly for the rest of his life and Brannick would describe as "adequate" — into the tow lines still smoldering on the lead ship's deck, finishing what friction had started.

"Kestrel!" he screamed. "NOW!"

And the Tern, faithful rotten beloved Tern, came about through the fire-road with her last gallon of fuel singing in her throat, and ran the burning narrows like a fox through a wheat field at harvest.

* * *

Wren remembered the escape in pieces afterward, the way you remember any night that rearranges you.

Sefa at the galley hatch, passing up wet blankets because Sefa passed food and comfort the same way, automatically, as breathing. Brannick on the engine, coaxing the last of the reserve with the tenderness of someone milking a dying cow. Odile at the chart table calling depths in her soft accent, *starboard, starboard, hard*, steering them through flame by memory of water she'd never seen clear.

And then the shell.

He never found out which ship fired it. Later, nobody could say; the fleet was in chaos, burning, their formation a rumor. But a shell came in low and flat and hit the Tern's port quarter, not enough to kill her, never enough to kill that stubborn scow, but enough to split one of the reserve drums that Brannick hadn't gotten to yet.

Sefa was closest. Sefa was always closest to wherever the work was.

Wren heard the whump and turned in time to see the flash catch Sefa's apron, then sleeve, then the whole left side of him blooming orange, and Sefa did the worst possible thing, the human thing, which was to run. Brannick tackled him mid-stride — full engineer's tackle, shoulders low — and rolled him against the deck, beating the flames out with bare hands and a blanket, and the smell of it, burnt cloth and burnt hair and underneath it something worse, that smell got into Wren and never entirely left.

They got the fire out. That was the horror of it, how quickly it went out, how ordinary the deck looked after, just scorch marks and a wet blanket, as if the river had merely coughed. Sefa lay on his back breathing in short whistles, the left side of his face tight and shining, his hand curled in Brannick's grip, and he said — Wren leaned close to hear it, and would carry the words like a stone in his pocket forever —

"Sefa's fine," Sefa wheezed. "Dumplings Thursday. Don't let Wren fold them. He does them like envelopes."

Then his eyes rolled back and Brannick shouted for the kit and the Tern ran on out of the fire-light into honest darkness, and Wren stood at the rail with empty hands and soot to the elbows, understanding at last the thing he'd signaled the fleet to teach him:

Mercy was real. It just didn't live on iron ships. It lived on a rotten salvage boat, in a cook's burned hands, in a captain who gave a coward his minute of engine without asking why twice.

* * *

They anchored at dawn in a blind channel behind a reed island, engine cold, fuel gone, Sefa laid out in the galley on the table where he'd fed them for six years. Brannick worked on him with the burn kit and the quiet competence of a man who has fixed worse things than people, though not many. Odile sat by Sefa's head and read to him from a Vessian prayer book she pretended she didn't have, in a language Sefa drifted in and out of, and whether he understood it didn't matter, because it was the sound of someone staying.

Kestrel found Wren at the stern rail.

For a long time neither spoke. The reeds rustled. Somewhere far off, a bird complained about the morning on general principle.

"The pins on the bollards," Kestrel said finally. "You loosened them before you asked me for the engine. You'd already decided."

"Yes, Captain."

"You could have told me. I'd have said yes."

Wren looked at the water. "I needed to not know if you'd say yes. If it went wrong, I wanted it to be mine."

Kestrel nodded slowly, the way Kestrel nodded at charts, at weather, at things that were true whether or not they were convenient. "It went wrong anyway. Sefa."

The name landed like the shell had. Wren's throat closed.

"He'll hate me," Wren managed.

"No," Kestrel said. "He'll feed you. That's worse. You'll have to sit there and eat it." A pause, and then, quieter, the closest thing to absolution the Tern carried: "You bought us the narrows, Wren. All of us. Even him. Courage isn't not being afraid. It's being afraid in a useful direction."

Wren laughed, or sobbed, or produced some sound that could be filed under either, and Kestrel clapped him once on the shoulder — the whole ceremony, the entire pardon, one clap — and went forward to stare at the fuel gauge as if grief could be measured in gallons.

* * *

By noon the picture was grim in the specific way river pictures are grim: no fuel, a wounded cook, a holed quarter that Brannick had patched with canvas and prayer, and a fleet behind them that, burned or not, would regroup. Wren took the crow's nest out of habit, scanning the reed maze, and it was Odile who climbed halfway up the ladder and handed him a folded scrap of paper, her eyes red but steady.

"A hamlet," she said. "Marsh people. Ferrywomen, lock-keepers. My mother's people kept it hidden through the whole occupation." She looked at the horizon the way other people looked at photographs. "They can help Sefa. And they can help more than Sefa."

"What do you mean, more?"

Odile smiled, and it was the first time Wren had seen her smile since the chest came aboard, and it was not entirely a comforting sight. It was the smile of someone holding a lit match of her own.

"I mean," she said, "that we're done running. Tell Kestrel: north channel, past the drowned church spire. And tell them—" she paused, choosing, "—tell them my brother's ledger is about to find its readers."

Below, in the galley, Sefa stirred and asked, faintly, whether anyone had thought about lunch, and the whole boat exhaled, and Wren climbed the rest of the way to the nest and turned the Tern's nose toward the marshes and the thing waiting there that none of them — not even Odile — fully understood yet.

Behind them, far down the narrows, the smoke of the Compact fleet still rose straight into the windless sky, like three black fingers counting the days remaining.
