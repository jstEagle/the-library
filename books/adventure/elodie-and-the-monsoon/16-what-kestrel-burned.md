## 16. What Kestrel Burned

The marsh hamlet had a name that changed depending on who you asked—Halfway Light to the ferrywomen, Tidewrack to the scavengers, and nothing at all to the Compact's charts, which was precisely why they loved it. The *Tern* sat moored in the reeds with her engine cold and her hull patched in three places, looking less like a ship than like something the river had coughed up and decided, on reflection, to keep.

Kestrel stood at the rail of Sefa's cookhouse deck and watched the crew gather without being asked. That was the thing about family, Kestrel had learned. You didn't have to call them. They could smell it on you when something needed saying.

Brannick came first, wiping grease from his hands onto a rag that had been mostly grease for six years. Wren scrambled down from the crow's nest ladder even though there was no reason to be up there except that being up there made him feel useful. Odile came last, quiet as always, and took the seat farthest from Kestrel—the seat she'd been taking since the vault schematics, since the brother's ledger, since the word *engineer* had started hanging between them like smoke.

Sefa set out tea nobody had asked for. Seven cups, though they were only five. "Habit," she said, when Wren raised an eyebrow. "My mother did it. One cup for whoever's coming late." She glanced at Kestrel. "Somebody's coming late tonight."

"I'm not sure anyone's coming at all," Kestrel said.

"Then we'll drink their share." Sefa sat down heavily, her debts and her bad knee and her enormous heart all settling into the chair at once. "Talk, Captain. You've got the face you get before you run aground on purpose."

Kestrel almost laughed. Almost.

They looked at each of them in turn. Brannick, who'd held the vote together when the crew split three-two and would have split worse if he hadn't stood in the middle of the chart room and refused to let anyone leave until everyone had said their whole piece. Wren, who had signaled the fleet and then cut its tow lines, whose guilt still lived behind his eyes like a fish under ice. Sefa, who had learned what Kestrel was at the floating market and had carried it in silence for days, and whose silence had been louder than any accusation. And Odile—

Odile, who had every right to hate them, and who hadn't yet decided whether she did.

"You know I was Compact," Kestrel began. "Sefa knows more than the rest of you. It's time the rest of you knew everything, because after Vessia—after what we're about to do—you should decide whether you want me aboard knowing all of it. Not some of it. All of it."

"Captain," Brannick started, gentle, "you don't owe us—"

"I do, actually. That's rather the point." Kestrel pulled out a chair but didn't sit. Standing felt more honest. Sitting felt like asking for comfort, and comfort wasn't what this was going to be. "You know I ran salvage enforcement for the Compact. What you don't know is what that meant in the years after the flood. There was a division called Records Compliance. We were told Vessian archives had survived the water—cellars, vaults, private collections, things sealed against just such an eventuality. Seditionists, we were told, were preserving them to rebuild Vessia. To raise the old flags. To destabilize the peace."

Wren frowned. "Rebuild it? The city was underwater."

"Yes," Kestrel said. "It was. That never struck me as strange either, Wren. A drowned nation rising again—like weeds through a deck. I was nineteen. I'd been recruited off a river hulk where I was eating one meal a day if the weather cooperated. The Compact gave me boots, a wage, a rank, and a story about myself: that I was keeping the river peaceful. Keeping people safe. You'd be amazed what a hungry person will believe if you hand it to them with a uniform."

The reeds rustled. Somewhere across the marsh a night bird called, three notes, patient.

"The archives weren't sedition," Kestrel went on. "I know that now. I've known it since the chest came up on my lines, and I knew it in my gut long before that, and I chose not to look at the knowing, which is its own kind of crime. Those documents were manifests. Letters. Engineering records. Proof of who built the floodgates and who ordered them opened. The flood wasn't an act of war the way the histories say—a tragedy both sides mourned, a nation lost to the fog of conflict. It was engineering. Deliberate. Scheduled. Signed." Kestrel's voice caught on the word and they pushed through it. "And I burned the proof. Not once. For four years. Vault after vault. I was good at it. I was promoted twice."

Nobody moved. Sefa's hands were folded around her teacup like she was holding something small and alive.

"There was one archive," Kestrel said, quieter now, "in a lock-keeper's house outside the drowned capital. An old woman kept it. She fed us first—insisted, said no one burns papers on an empty stomach, it's bad manners. She talked the whole time about her son, who'd been an engineer on the gates. She said he'd tried to warn people. She said the papers would prove him right someday." Kestrel's jaw tightened. "I burned them while her tea was still warm in my stomach. I remember thinking her son sounded like a troublemaker. I remember being proud of how thoroughly the fire took."

Odile had gone very still. Stillness was her language; Kestrel had learned to read it over years of shared watches. This stillness had a tremor under it, like ice before it goes.

"Her name was Maren Oduya," Kestrel said. "Her son was Tomas Oduya."

The sound Odile made was not a word. It was smaller than a word, older. Her hand went to her collarbone, pressing down over her sternum as if holding a door shut.

"He was my brother," she said.

"I know," Kestrel said. "I've known since the ledger. Since your accent finally made sense to me—which it should have years ago, and didn't, because I didn't want it to. I burned your brother's warning, Odile. I burned his mother's proof of it. And then I sat across a chart table from you for six years and let you pour my tea."

* * *

For a long moment the only sound was the river moving through the reeds, that endless patient conversation water has with itself.

Wren broke it, because Wren always broke silences, usually badly. "You didn't know," he said. "You said—they told you it was sedition. You were nineteen. That's barely older than me."

"That's an explanation, Wren. It isn't an absolution. Don't spend yours on me—I haven't earned it, and you'll need it for yourself someday, everyone does." Kestrel finally sat, because their legs had opinions. "Here's what I'm asking. Not forgiveness. I'm asking whether you can sail with someone who did this, knowing that the man who signed the destruction orders—" they drew the seal from inside their coat, the enforcer's stamp, brass and worn walnut, and set it on the table with a click that sounded enormous, "—is sitting at your table. Because when we rig that buoy in the vault, my seal goes on the broadcast with the evidence. My name, my order number, my signature. Every settlement on the river is going to hear that Kestrel Nakamura burned Vessia's truth along with the Compact's men. If you're tied to me, you're tied to that."

Sefa spoke first. Of course she did. "I knew already," she said. "At the market, when the fence said your old name. I've been carrying it around like a stone in my apron pocket for two weeks." She turned the teacup in her hands. "I hated you a little, Captain. Not for what you did. For making me carry it alone. For deciding for me what I could handle."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize yet. Let me finish, I rehearsed this." She fixed Kestrel with the look she used on Wren when he stole from the biscuit tin. "Then I thought about my debts. You know why I'm on this boat. Six creditors, and only one of them money. When my sister died, I owed people grief I couldn't pay, and I lied about how I was doing for a year, little lies, kind lies, the lies you tell so people can keep loving you without drowning in you. Everybody on this boat tells those lies. Wren told the biggest one—he signaled the fleet—and he's still here. Brannick pretends his knees are fine. Odile pretended for six years she was from nowhere." Sefa shrugged. "I pretended your past was clean because I wanted my captain to be simple. You were never simple. None of us are. So no, I don't forgive you. Forgiveness is cheap and I'm expensive. But you're not leaving this boat, because leaving would be one more decision you make for other people, and I am done letting you do that."

Kestrel blinked. Something hot pressed behind their eyes and they let it stay there, unshed, as a kind of payment.

Brannick leaned forward, elbows on knees, the engineer's posture, the fix-it posture. "Captain, I've rebuilt engines that were seized solid with salt. You know what I've learned? You can't fix a machine by throwing it away. You fix it by understanding exactly what broke, and putting it back to work where the break happened." He tapped the table. "You burned archives. Fine. Then you're exactly the person who belongs in the archive vault, signing the broadcast. Not despite it. Because of it. The river doesn't need another clean conscience telling it the truth. It needs yours. Dirty ones carry further."

"That's not justice, Brannick. That's arithmetic."

"It's both. Most true things are."

Wren was staring at the brass seal. He reached out, hesitated, and touched it with one finger, the way you'd touch a snake to learn it wasn't a stick. "When I signaled the fleet," he said, "I thought the Compact was the grown-up thing. Order. Cranes and chains and grids and rules. Then they burned that raft, and then Ashvael offered to buy us, and I understood the rules were just a story too. Your story was wrong, Captain. Mine was wrong. But you're the only one telling a new one." He swallowed. "If you leave, who steers us through the sandbars? Nobody else can read them. It's selfish, maybe. I don't care. Stay because we'll die on a sandbar without you."

A wet laugh escaped Kestrel before they could stop it. "That's the worst argument anyone's ever made to me."

"It's the best one. Fear's honest. Everything else is decoration."

Which left Odile.

She had not touched her tea. She rose, slowly, and crossed the deck, and stood over the seal the way one stands over a grave marker—reading a name cut in brass. When she spoke, her Vessian accent was fully present for the first time in six years, soft vowels rolling like water over lock-gates, and none of the crew flinched, because they had all known, in the way families know things without knowing they know.

"My brother wrote letters," she said. "From the gate works. I have one left—it's sewn into my coat lining, I've carried it since the water came. He wrote: *If anything happens to me, the numbers will speak. Numbers don't drown.*" She looked up. "He was wrong. The numbers drowned, Captain. They drowned in furnaces lit by people like you. For twenty years my country hasn't even been allowed to be dead properly. No mourning. No grave. Just a hole in the map that everyone agreed not to look at."

"Odile—"

"No. You'll listen now, because I listened for six years." Her voice didn't rise; it deepened, like the river finding its channel. "I should hate you. Part of me does, and that part is entitled to its seat at this table forever, and you'll pour its tea and you won't complain. But the part that knows my brother—" her breath shuddered, held, steadied, "—the part that knows Tomas would say the numbers matter more than the avenging. He didn't want vengeance. He wanted to be heard. And you—" she pointed at the seal, at Kestrel, at the whole sorry architecture of them, "—you are the only living person who can testify to what was destroyed. Victim and instrument, in one body. The tribunals of the river will need both."

"So you want me aboard as evidence."

"I want you aboard as *accountable*. Do you understand the difference? Forgiveness says it's over. Accountability says it continues, every day, in everything you do, and you do it anyway, and you do it *with us*, where we can see you." She sat back down, finally, and picked up her untouched tea, and drank it like a woman closing a very long meeting. "Also, you're the only one who can read the sandbars. Wren is right. It's a terrible argument and it's completely decisive."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone below and the lamp had burned low, Sefa came back out and collected the cups. She paused at the seventh, the empty one poured for whoever was coming late.

"Who was that for?" Kestrel asked.

Sefa considered. "The nineteen-year-old in the uniform," she said. "They showed up eventually. Late." She stacked the cup with the others. "But they showed up. That's more than most manage." At the hatch she stopped, half-turned. "First watch is yours, Captain. Tide turns at moonset. And Kestrel—" her voice gentled, "—the tea was terrible tonight. Tomorrow I'll make it proper. We've got a drowned city to sail into, and nobody sails anywhere on my terrible tea."

She went below. Kestrel stood alone at the rail with the brass seal in their pocket, heavier than lead, lighter than it had been at sundown, and watched the moon come up over the reeds on a line that would carry them, at last, into Vessia.
