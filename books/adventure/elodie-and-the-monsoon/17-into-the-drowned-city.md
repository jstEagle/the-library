## 17. Into the Drowned City

Brannick had spent thirty years listening to engines, and he had learned that every machine told you the truth if you put your palm flat against it. The Tern's hull was singing a note he didn't like — a low, worried hum in the portside plating where the sandbar chase had bruised her — but she was holding, and holding was what the Tern did best. He kept his hand there anyway, the way you'd keep a hand on a frightened animal's flank.

Ahead of them, Vessia rose out of the water like a rumor finally admitting it was true.

"Saints above," Sefa said quietly. She had come up from the galley without being called, which meant even her debts couldn't keep her below decks today. Her ladle was still in her hand. Brannick decided not to mention it. Some things a person needed to hold onto.

The drowned capital spread before them for miles: rooftops breaking the surface like the backs of sleeping animals, bell towers leaning at angles that made Wren's pilot instincts visibly itch, whole avenues visible beneath the green-brown water, cobbled and patient. Twenty years of river silt had softened everything, draped it in weed, given the windows a milky glassy look — but the city was *there*. Streets. Stairs leading down into nothing. A child's swing still hanging from a balcony, rusted to lace.

"It's bigger than I imagined," Kestrel said. They stood at the wheel with their arms folded, wearing the face they wore when they were working very hard not to feel something. "I imagined smaller."

"You imagined a town," Odile said. "This was a city of two hundred thousand."

Nobody answered that. There wasn't an answer that fit in a mouth.

Brannick took the engine room watch as they crossed the outer levee — what had been the outer levee; now it was just a submerged ridge the Tern cleared by less than an arm's length, and he felt her belly kiss mud once, gently, like a warning tap on the shoulder. He eased the throttle back and listened. The hum in the port plating deepened, thought about becoming a rattle, then thought better of it. Good girl.

* * *

Odile navigated.

That was the strange thing, Brannick thought, watching her from the engine room hatch. For six years she had been the quiet one, the one who marked charts in a small precise hand and spoke maybe forty words a day, half of them corrections to Wren's headings. Now she stood at the bow like a woman reading a letter from home, calling depths and turns off memory instead of soundings.

"Port, past the spire. The canal under us is the Marrow Street run — it's deep, it was a canal before the flood, the water just rose to meet it." She paused. "There was a bakery on the left corner. Marta's. She gave me honey bread when I was small enough to be given things for free."

"Marta's," Sefa repeated softly, tasting the name. Cooks did that. Brannick had seen her do it with dead mothers' recipes.

"The chimney should be—" Odile stopped. Below them, unmistakable through ten feet of green water, stood a brick chimney crowned with weed, tilted but standing. A shoal of silver fish poured out of a second-floor window where an oven door used to be.

Sefa put her ladle down on the rail very carefully, the way you set down something you might otherwise throw.

Wren, at least, had found his voice again, though it came out hushed, reverent, the tone other boys used for cathedrals. "The whole street's down there. You could walk it. If you could breathe."

"People walked it," Odile said. "Once."

The gentle humor that usually held the Tern together — Sefa's grumbling, Wren's schemes, Brannick's own endless negotiations with recalcitrant machinery — had gone thin as morning tea. Even Kestrel's orders came out quieter than usual. *Slow ahead. Mind the cable line.* As if the city were sleeping and none of them wished to be the one who woke it.

Because that was the truth none of them said aloud, though Brannick felt it settling into his chest like damp into wood: this wasn't a ruin. Ruins were empty. This was a grave, and they were sailing across the top of it, and somewhere below were two hundred thousand people who had been told, by everyone including themselves after enough years passed, to stop being mourned.

* * *

They anchored at midday in what Odile said had been a public garden, the plane trees now dead black fingers poking from the water, their canopy long since rotted away. It gave them cover from the open channel and, more importantly, depth enough that the Tern sat quiet while Brannick went hunting for the source of that hull-hum.

He found it in the forward bilge: a seam working loose along a strake, weeping slow and steady. Not fatal. Not today. But it wanted copper sheeting and rivets and about four hours of labor, and four hours was time they might not have.

"We could patch it proper at the hamlet on the way back," Wren offered, crouched beside him with the lamp, handing him tools before he asked, which was why Brannick tolerated the boy in his engine room despite the boy's habit of asking questions.

"If there's a way back," Brannick said, and immediately regretted it, because Wren's face did the thing it did — the flicker toward the horizon, checking for sails.

"There's always a way back," Wren said, too firmly. "You taught me that. 'A good engineer never builds a boat she can't get out of.'"

"I said that about engine rooms, boy, not about drowned nations." But he squeezed the boy's shoulder, because the truth was the sentence had helped him too, and lying to each other kindly was half of what a crew was.

Up on deck, voices. He climbed the ladder in time to hear Odile say, "—the tribunal hall is past the old exchange. We take the Grand Processional until the third bridge, then down. The vault entrance is behind the records annex. There's a stair."

"And the fleet?" Kestrel asked. No softness in that question. That was Kestrel all over: grief got filed, but threats got counted.

Odile was quiet a moment. "The Processional is wide water. If they come, we'll see them coming."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Odile said. "It isn't." And she turned back to the chart, and Kestrel let it lie, and Brannick understood that both of them knew exactly what the other was doing, and that this was how those two loved people — sideways, in silences, in questions deliberately not pressed.

* * *

They ran the Processional at dusk, engine at a whisper, and Brannick swore afterward — privately, in the engine room, where he did his swearing — that he would never forget it as long as he lived, which given recent events he was no longer prepared to assume would be a long time.

The Grand Processional had been Vessia's pride, Odile said. A ceremonial avenue of water running through the heart of the city, lined with statues of the river saints. The statues were still there. They rose out of the dark water on either side of the Tern, furred with weed, faces smoothed nearly featureless by twenty years of current, arms raised in blessing or warning or simple greeting — it was impossible anymore to tell which. Fish scattered from the Tern's bow wake in silver sheets. Somewhere far off, water dripped inside a hollow tower with the regularity of a clock.

Wren stood at the rail with his cap in his hands. Sefa had come up again, ladle abandoned entirely, which Brannick suspected was the truest measure of awe the Tern possessed.

"They're all facing the water," Wren whispered. "Why are they all facing the water?"

"Because the water came from everywhere," Odile said.

Kestrel cut the engine to a drift for a hundred yards — against every rule Brannick had ever heard them state, *never stop, never drift, keep way on* — and for that hundred yards the only sounds were the lap of water and the drip of that distant clock. Then Kestrel rang for slow ahead, and the moment passed, and nobody mentioned it, and everybody remembered it.

It was Sefa, naturally, who broke the spell in the way cooks break spells, which is to say by feeding people. She brought up a pot of rice and beans and they ate on deck in the last grey light, passing bowls, and Brannick caught himself thinking that if he had to eat a last meal anywhere, he'd have chosen worse places than a drowned saint's avenue with his family around him. Then he told himself sternly not to think in last meals. Engineers did not think in last meals. Engineers thought in load tolerances and escape routes.

Speaking of which. He went below and checked the skiff's stores again. Water, flares, the patched sail. Fine. He checked the reserve fuel cans lashed aft. Thin. Very thin. He did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the sandbars and arrived at the same answer: enough to run, not enough to hide. He wrote the number on the inside of his wrist with a grease pencil, an old habit, and went back up wearing his best face, the one that said *all machinery nominal*, which was technically true and spiritually a lie.

* * *

The third bridge announced itself by nearly killing them.

It had half-fallen, its span collapsed into the water at an angle, leaving a gap barely wider than the Tern, choked with the skeletons of barges that had tried to shelter beneath it twenty years ago and never left. Odile talked them through it in a voice gone flat and careful, the voice of someone walking through their own childhood with a lamp: "Hard to starboard. The barge rib will clear your hull by a hand's width if you trust me. Now stop. Stop. *Stop.*"

Kestrel stopped. The Tern hung in the gap, close enough on either side that Brannick, hanging over the rail, could have touched the drowned barges' rails. Something shifted in the dark water below — big, slow, unbothered. A fish, probably. Probably.

Then through, and the channel opened, and there it was.

The tribunal hall did not rise out of the water so much as *preside* over it. Even drowned to its second story, even wearing two decades of silt and ivy-of-the-river, it kept its shape: a broad stepped platform, columns thick as trees, a pediment whose carved figures had been defaced — not by water. By chisels. Brannick had seen that kind of damage before, in towns after the Compact's reclamation crews passed through, and his stomach did a slow roll. Somebody had come here specifically to erase the faces.

"The records annex," Odile said, pointing to a lower building hunched against the hall's flank, most of it below the waterline. "The stair goes down from the archive floor. The vault was built to survive anything. My brother used to joke that the only thing the architects trusted was the basement." Her voice caught on *brother*, just slightly, and steadied.

"When?" Kestrel asked. Meaning: when do we go in.

"Now," Odile said. "While there's light to rig lamps. While—"

And that was when Wren, up in the crow's nest they'd rigged for the journey, made a sound Brannick had only heard from him once before, the night the fleet burned the scavenger raft.

"Sail," Wren said. "Two sails. No — three. Coming up the Processional behind us. Iron hulls. Crane-and-chain at the mastheads."

Everyone looked at Kestrel. Kestrel looked at the water between the Tern and the tribunal hall, measuring, the way they measured everything, and Brannick watched them arrive at the arithmetic: three iron-hulled Compact ships in a channel with no exits, and a vault that required hours underwater, and a fence in some floating market town who had sold them for the price of a pardon and a purse.

"The lantern traffic," Kestrel said quietly. "Of course. He watched our heading. He didn't need to follow us. He just needed to know where we were going, and tell the commodore the only address in Vessia worth guarding."

"Kestrel," Sefa started.

"Rig the dive lamps," Kestrel said. "Odile, you and I go down. Brannick—" and here their voice did something it almost never did, which was hesitate, "—if we're not back up before they're alongside, you take the Tern out. That's not a request."

Brannick looked at his captain, at the woman or man or whatever Kestrel was these days — the person who had pulled him out of a debtors' lockup nine years ago and never once asked what he'd done to get there — and he shook his head slowly.

"You know I don't take orders about abandoning family," he said. "Never have. Ask my first wife. Well — don't. But the principle stands." He rolled up his sleeves. "Now somebody hand me a lamp before I start patching hulls out of pure nerves."

Below them, faint but unmistakable, the fleet's engines changed pitch — from cruising to pursuit, a sound Brannick knew in his bones the way he knew the Tern's own heartbeat.

The city that was a grave was about to have visitors, and the visitors were not mourners.

"Down the stair," Odile said. "Quickly. My brother waited twenty years. He can't wait much longer."
