## 18. The Vault Beneath the Tribunal

The drowned city did not look like a grave from the water. It looked like a city that had simply sat down to rest.

Kestrel brought the *Tern* in slow over the rooftops of Vessia, the hull skimming so close to the tiled slopes that Wren could have reached down and touched them. The tiles were green now, furred with twenty years of river weed, and here and there a chimney stood above the surface like a man wading out to meet them, patient and unsurprised.

"Mind the spire," Odile said quietly. She stood at Kestrel's shoulder, one hand resting on the wheel housing—not steering, just touching, the way you'd touch a friend's arm in a crowd. "There's a bell tower ahead. Saint Meren's. My mother rang there on feast days."

"I see it."

"You're going to hit it."

"I am not going to hit it."

Kestrel took the *Tern* through the gap between the bell tower and what had once been a customs house, with perhaps a boat's width to spare on either side, and Brannick, who had come up from the engine room specifically to say nothing, said nothing very loudly until they were through.

"Show-off," Sefa called from the galley hatch, where she was pretending not to be terrified and mostly succeeding.

"Professional pride," Kestrel said. But their hands were not entirely steady, and it had nothing to do with the spire. Every street below them was a street Odile had named as they approached—*the weavers' quarter, the tanners' lane, the long market*—and every name landed in Kestrel's chest like a stone dropped down a well. Because Kestrel had read those names before, in another life, in dispatches and demolition orders, and had thought of them then as grid references.

"Left at the tribunal," Odile said. "It's the big building with the columns. You can't miss it. Nobody could ever miss it. I got lost in it for three years as a clerk's assistant and I still can't miss it."

The tribunal hall rose ahead of them, its colonnade standing in six feet of green water, the pediment above bearing a carved inscription that the river had tried to eat and only half managed: *THE LAW IS A LAMP LIT FOR EVERY* — and then nothing, the rest of the sentence drowned along with everything else.

"The lamp lit for every what?" Wren asked, squinting up at it from the bow.

"Citizen," Odile said. "Every citizen." She paused. "The stone kept its promise longer than the people did."

Nobody had anything to say to that, which was its own kind of agreement.

* * *

They moored inside the colonnade itself, running lines around two marble columns that had held up a nation's justice and now held up a riverboat, which Brannick observed was probably the most honest work the columns had done in decades. The water inside the hall was calm, dark, and strangely clear—the roof overhead had collapsed in one great section, letting down a column of grey daylight that fell all the way to the flooded floor and turned the submerged atrium into something like a cathedral aquarium. Fish moved through the corridors where petitioners had once queued. A school of silver darters poured down the grand staircase, which still descended, step by drowned step, into the dark.

"The vault is beneath the record rooms," Odile said. "West wing. There are stairs behind the registrar's office. It was built to survive fire, flood, and riot." Her mouth twisted. "It survived all three. Just not in the order anyone planned."

"How deep?" Brannick asked, already doing engineer's arithmetic with his eyes.

"Two levels down. Maybe forty feet of water in the stairwell. The vault door itself should be above the flood line—it was rated for the cellar flooding, not the whole city." She looked at Kestrel. "I used to fetch files from it. I know the way with my eyes shut."

"That's reassuring," Sefa said, "and also exactly the kind of thing people say right before we find out the way has a kraken in it."

"There's no kraken, Sefa."

"There's always a kraken."

There was no kraken. There was, however, a collapsed stairwell, a jammed pressure door, and a family of very territorial catfish occupying the registrar's office, and by the time the four of them—Kestrel, Odile, Brannick, and Wren, with Sefa holding the boat and loudly narrating her own vigilance to no one—had fought through all three, the grey light above had begun to lean toward evening.

The last barrier was the vault door itself: a disc of black iron taller than Wren, banded in bronze, with a wheel-lock mechanism that had waited twenty years for someone to turn it.

Odile put her hand flat against the metal. For a moment she didn't move, and Kestrel watched her breathe, once, twice, the way you breathe before opening a door you know will change the room you're standing in.

"It's smaller than I remember," she said finally.

"Everything is," Brannick said gently. "That's just being grown. Come on, then."

She turned the wheel. Somewhere inside the door, ancient springs groaned, complained, and surrendered. The seal broke with a sound like a long-held note ending, and cold dry air breathed out of the dark—a pocket of the old world, preserved, smelling faintly of paper and lamp oil and time.

Kestrel lit the lanterns. They went in.

* * *

The vault was not large. That was the first surprise. Kestrel had expected, from the weight of the thing in their imagination, a cavern; instead it was a room hardly bigger than the *Tern*'s hold, lined floor to ceiling with steel drawers, each one labeled in neat Vessian script. The second surprise was the state of it: orderly. Untouched. Twenty years underwater and the room above it had drowned, but this room had been built like a ship's heart, sealed and proud, and everything in it was exactly where the last clerk had left it.

Odile walked the rows slowly, trailing her fingers across the labels, reading names under her breath. Kestrel recognized the cadence. It was how you spoke at a graveside.

"Here," she said at last. She stopped before a drawer marked with a symbol Kestrel knew better than their own heartbeat: the hydrographic crest, the gate-and-wave. "Master engineering. Flood control." Her voice was steady in the way that cost something. "This is it."

Brannick drew the drawer. Inside lay a bundle of schematics, rolled and sleeved in oilcloth, and beneath them a ledger bound in grey leather. Odile lifted the ledger and opened it on the vault's central table, and the lantern light fell across the first page, and nobody spoke for a long moment.

It was the master plan. All of it. The floodgate network on the upper tributaries, the reservoir schedules, the release calculations—and in the margins, in a cramped hand, annotations. Dates. Objections. *Volume exceeds design tolerance by factor of three.* *Downstream warning impossible at this discharge rate.* *Who authorized this?*

"That's my brother's writing," Odile said. Her finger rested beside the last annotation without quite touching it. "He wrote to the tribunal eleven times. These are his copies. He kept proof that they knew." She turned pages. The objections grew more desperate, then more careful, then stopped altogether. On the final annotated page, a single line in different ink, official ink: *Engineer T. Oduya reassigned. File closed.*

"They didn't even spell it right," Odile whispered. "His name. They couldn't be bothered."

Wren, who had been uncharacteristically silent, leaned over the table. "So this proves it. This proves they did it on purpose. Anyone could read this and—" He stopped. Kestrel watched him arrive at the same conclusion the whole crew had been circling for weeks. "Anyone *with power* could read this."

"Now you understand why it's been sitting in a box," Kestrel said.

But Kestrel wasn't looking at the ledger anymore. Kestrel was looking at the bottom drawer of the hydrographic cabinet, the one labeled in the enforcers' cipher—the one Kestrel had been trying not to look at since they entered, the way you try not to look at a loose plank you already know will give way.

"Open it," Odile said softly. She had followed Kestrel's eyes. Of course she had.

"Odile—"

"Open it, Kestrel. Whatever it is. We didn't come all this way to flinch in the last room."

Kestrel opened it.

Inside lay the enforcement records. Destruction orders. And on the topmost page, under the Compact's crane-and-chain, under the heading *SEDITIOUS MATERIALS — DISPOSAL AUTHORIZED*, was a signature Kestrel knew intimately, because Kestrel had practiced it for years in a training academy and had spent ten years since trying to forget it.

Their own name. Their true name. Their own seal pressed in red wax beside it, crisp and perfect, preserved in the dark like a flower.

The order authorized the burning of the Vessian national archive. Dated three weeks after the flood. Signed by Enforcer K. Nakamura, aged twenty-three, who had believed the reports, who had stood in a courtyard while a hundred years of a nation's memory went up in smoke, and who had told themselves it was sedition, it was dangerous paper, it was necessary—

*Necessary.* The auctioneer's word. It tasted the same coming back up.

"Kestrel." Odile's voice came from very far away, or maybe Kestrel had gone somewhere else and her voice had traveled to reach them. "Look at me."

Kestrel looked at her. Waiting for it. The hatred, or worse, the pity.

Instead Odile picked up the destruction order, read it carefully, all of it, and set it down beside her brother's ledger so the two documents touched.

"Do you know what this is?" she said.

"My confession."

"No." She tapped the ledger. "This is *their* crime, written in their hand, sealed in their vault. And this—" she tapped the destruction order— "is your crime, same thing. They go together. They've always gone together. The people who drowned my country and the people who burned its evidence were the same machine, Kestrel, and you were a gear in it, and gears don't get to decide the machine's crimes, but they do get to decide what they do next." Her eyes were wet and absolutely fierce. "You don't get to drown in that drawer. I won't allow it. I lost a brother to these people. I'm not losing you to them too."

Sefa would have made a joke. Brannick would have put a hand on someone's shoulder. Wren would have said something earnest and slightly wrong. But it was just the two of them in the lamplight, the drowned nation's whole testimony spread on the table between them, and Odile simply stood there, waiting, until Kestrel nodded—which was not forgiveness, and was not meant to be, but was, unmistakably, permission to keep going.

"All right," Kestrel said. Voice rougher than intended. "All right. Then let's finish it."

* * *

The broadcast buoy sat in the vault's corner like a fat brass beetle, and Brannick fell upon it with the joy of an engineer meeting a well-made problem. It was Compact surplus, ironically—installed in every tribunal vault in the years after the war, designed to transmit vault inventories to regional headquarters at each tide. Designed, in other words, to tell the Compact what the Compact owned.

"Well," Brannick said, elbow-deep in its guts, "it still wants to talk to everyone at the next tide. Only question is what we make it say, and who counts as 'everyone.'"

"Can you widen the audience?" Wren asked.

"Kid, I can make this thing sing opera to every receiver from the Headwaters to the Salt if I retune the resonator. The hard part was never the talking." He glanced up. "It's that once it talks, it can't untalk. One transmission. No take-backs."

"Good," Odile said.

Kestrel laid the documents out on the table one final time. The schematics. The ledger. Eleven letters of objection from a dead engineer who had tried to save a country with arithmetic. And, on top, the destruction order with Kestrel's name and seal, because Odile had insisted, and because she was right, and because a truth with the inconvenient parts removed was just another kind of lie—the kind Kestrel had been telling themselves for ten years.

"You understand what happens when this goes out," Kestrel said to the room generally. "Every lock-keeper, every ferrywoman, every scavenger camp from here to the delta hears it. And every one of them also hears my name on the disposal order. There'll be people who want to strangle me with my own mooring lines. Reasonable people, some of them."

"Then you'll be moored near reasonable people," Odile said, "and you'll answer questions like a grown-up. Revolutionary concept."

"She's been hanging around Sefa too long," Brannick murmured, soldering.

Kestrel picked up the wax stick and the enforcer seal that lay in the drawer—the actual seal, brass and bone handle, the instrument of that signature—and stood there holding it. Twenty years of running, and it had all come down to this small absurd object in a dry room under a drowned city.

"What are you doing?" Wren asked.

"Finishing the paperwork." Kestrel pressed the seal into fresh wax on the buoy's authentication plate—the transmission required an authority stamp, and the only authority that mattered now was the one attached to these crimes. Then, beneath the impression, in plain characters where anyone could read it, Kestrel wrote:

*I, Kestrel Nakamura, formerly Enforcer of the Salvage Compact, certify these records true. I burned the archive they describe. I sign this as my indictment. Let the river judge us both.*

"There," Kestrel said quietly. "Now it's honest."

Odile read it twice. When she looked up, she didn't say thank you, and Kestrel was grateful, because thank you would have been unbearable. She just said, "My brother would have liked you. He was stubborn about the truth too. Infuriatingly so."

"Runs in the river, apparently."

Brannick closed the buoy's casing with a click that sounded terribly final. "Armed and scheduled. Next tide, this little beetle tells the whole river what happened to Vessia." He wiped his hands. "Which means, captain, that we have approximately one tide to not be standing in a drowned city when the Compact comes asking why their vault buoy is transmitting sedition."

As if the river had been listening—as it generally had opinions—the water in the stairwell beyond the vault door gave a long, low shudder. Not the tide. Something heavier. Something with an iron hull and a crane-and-chain on its flag, moving up the flooded boulevard with the patience of a thing that had been tipped off, and had all the time in the world.

From far above, muffled by forty feet of water and a collapsed roof, came the sound Kestrel had been hearing in nightmares for weeks: the fleet's signal horn, one long blast, echoing off the drowned towers of Vessia.

"They found the boat," Wren whispered.

Kestrel looked at the armed buoy, at the documents sleeping inside it, at the tide mark on the wall that said dawn was hours away and the transmission was hours further—and drew the lantern closer, and started counting doors.

"Then we hold this room," Kestrel said, "until the river is ready to listen."
