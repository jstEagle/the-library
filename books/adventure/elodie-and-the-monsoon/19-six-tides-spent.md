## 19. Six Tides, Spent

Wren had counted the tides of the search grid like a boy counting coins, and now, standing in the crow's nest above a drowned city, he understood that he had been counting down to this his whole life: the moment the Compact's iron came for them.

The fleet breached Vessia at dawn.

Three ships, then four, their hulls grinding through the shallow water where rooftops used to be. The crane-and-chain flew from every mast, black on grey, and the sound they made was not like boats at all. It was like a door being opened in a house that had been sealed for twenty years — a long, groaning complaint from the drowned streets as wakes rolled through them and knocked tiles loose and sent silt boiling up in brown ghosts.

Wren gripped the rail of the nest and felt something in his chest do the same thing it had done the day he watched them burn the scavenger raft: that slow, sick tilting, like a floor going out from under him.

He had idolized these ships. He could admit that now, privately, in the privacy of his own skull where nobody could make fun of him for it. He had drawn them in the margins of his navigation charts when he was eleven. He had believed, with the whole stupid burning sincerity of a boy raised on stories about order, that the crane-and-chain meant *the river is taken care of*. That somebody was minding things.

And here they were, minding things, come to drown a second city — this one made of paper and memory — because the truth inside it was inconvenient.

"Anything?" Kestrel's voice floated up from the deck, deceptively calm, the way it always went right before everything went wrong.

"Four hulls," Wren called back. "Two holding off the north breach. Two coming up the Processional." He swallowed. "They're not searching. They're coming straight for us."

A pause below. Then, dry as driftwood: "Well. Someone tipped them off."

Wren didn't say the fence's name. Nobody did. It hung there anyway, sour, between all of them.

* * *

The Tern sat low in the water over what had once been the Tribunal Square, her keel a hand's breadth above the flooded archive roof. Getting the buoy armed had taken most of the night — Odile and Kestrel below in the vault, splicing cable by lantern-light, arguing in two languages about whether the tide-trigger would fire true. Brannick had spent the night in the engine room with the last of their fuel, coaxing it into the lines like a man feeding the very last of the bread to guests he loved.

Now the fuel gauge read a quarter. Maybe less. Wren had learned to read Brannick's gauges the way you learn to read a person who lies politely: subtract a little from whatever they tell you.

"We can't outrun four ships on a quarter tank," Sefa said. She stood at the galley hatch with a cleaver in one hand and a bandaged arm held carefully against her chest — the burns from the fuel slick had scabbed ugly, and she kept touching them like she was checking they were still hers.

"We're not going to outrun them," Kestrel said. "We're going to be somewhere else when they arrive."

"That's the same thing."

"It's never the same thing," Kestrel said. "Outrunning is fast. Being somewhere else is *smart*."

But Wren, watching from above, saw the flaw in it even before Kestrel did, or maybe just saw it more honestly, because honesty was cheaper when you were nineteen and everyone expected less of you. The Processional was a straight drowned avenue, wide as a market street, and the two lead hulls were already in it. There was nowhere else to be. The archive vault had one way out and the fleet knew it, because someone had told them, and the arithmetic of the morning was simple and terrible:

Somebody had to stay behind and hold the door.

Nobody said it. That was the thing Wren would remember afterward, the thing that would sit in his stomach for years like a swallowed stone. Nobody said it. Kestrel started giving orders about cable tension and Odile started checking the buoy's seals and Sefa started wrapping the cleaver handle in cloth so it wouldn't slip if her burned hand failed her, and all of them were doing arithmetic, silently, together, the way a family does when the money won't cover the winter.

It was Brannick who solved it, the way Brannick solved everything: by making it sound like an engineering problem instead of a sacrifice.

"The vault door," he said, coming up from below with grease to his elbows and that unhurried look he wore like a coat. "Hydraulic counterweights. Old Vessian work — beautiful stuff, better than anything the Compact builds, no offense to anyone present." He nodded at the fleet on the horizon, which took no offense. "I can jam it half-closed. It'll take them hours to cut through, if they're careful, and they'll have to be careful, because the doorframe is load-bearing for the whole tribunal hall and if they rush it they bring the ceiling down on their own divers."

"And you'll need time to rig it," Kestrel said.

"I'll need time to rig it," Brannick agreed pleasantly.

"How much time?"

"However much time you need to get clear on a quarter tank."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats, and Wren counted every one of them from the crow's nest, hating each one individually.

Kestrel looked at Brannick for a long moment. Whatever passed between them wasn't said aloud and Wren couldn't hear it and wouldn't have understood it if he had. It was captain-things, old-crew-things, the private language of people who had pulled each other out of rivers before.

"No," Odile said quietly. "No, we draw lots—"

"We don't draw lots," Brannick said. "Lots are for people who haven't thought it through. I've thought it through. I'm the only one who knows the counterweight mechanism, the buoy needs another hour of splicing that only I can supervise without frying the relay, and—" he paused, and smiled, and it was the worst smile Wren had ever seen, warm and certain and completely closed to argument, "—and I'm the slowest swimmer aboard. If it goes wrong down there, I'm the one who should be nearest the door."

"You're also the biggest," Sefa said furiously. "You're the one they'll see first."

"Yes," Brannick said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Wren came down from the nest when the first hull was close enough that he could count the rivets, which was a thing he wished he couldn't do. The deck of the Tern had gone quiet in that specific way decks go quiet before violence — everyone moving precisely, speaking softly, touching things gently, as if loudness might attract the iron the way blood attracts eels.

He found Brannick in the engine room, kneeling at the fuel line, doing something unnecessary to a coupling that was already perfect.

"You could come with us," Wren said. "If we run now. Right now. Before they're in range."

Brannick didn't look up. "On a quarter tank, against two hulls in a straight channel? You'd be boarding salvage by noon, and so would the chest, and so would Odile." He tightened something that didn't need tightening. "No, boy. Somebody holds the door. That's not tragedy, that's plumbing."

"It's not plumbing."

Brannick looked up then, and his face did the thing it always did when Wren got earnest, which was soften around the eyes while staying completely level everywhere else. "No," he admitted. "It's not plumbing." He wiped his hands on a rag that was more grease than rag. "Listen to me. Twenty-two years I've been turning wrenches on other people's boats. Best crew I ever had is on this deck right now, and that includes the cook who steals from the sugar tin—"

"I do not steal from the sugar tin," Sefa's voice said, from the top of the ladder, with tremendous dignity.

"—and the pilot who drew our ship on his charts when he was eleven, next to drawings of the Compact fleet, which I always thought was funny and now think is instructive." He stood, joints cracking like kindling. "You hold the family together from up there, Wren. Eyes high, voice steady. That's your job today. Mine's the door."

Wren wanted to argue. He had a whole speech prepared; he'd been composing it since dawn, all the reasons Brannick mattered, all the things the boat needed him for, the way he hummed when he worked and the way he'd taught Wren to listen to engines like they were telling you about their childhood. But Brannick was already climbing the ladder, and there is a kind of decision that closes behind a person like water, and Wren understood, with the terrible clarity of the young, that this was one.

So instead of the speech, he said the only true thing left: "Come find us after."

Brannick stopped on the ladder. Looked back down at him. And said, gently, "If I can."

Which was not yes. Which was both of them knowing it was not yes. Which was, somehow, still a kindness.

* * *

The running battle, if you could call it that, lasted forty minutes and consisted mostly of the Tern fleeing through her own reflection.

Kestrel took her down the flooded boulevards with the skill that had made Wren worship them in the first place — threading between chimney-pots, cutting corners where the water over a submerged wall ran thin enough to skim, using the drowned city itself as armor. Twice a Compact skiff tried to cut them off at a plaza and twice Kestrel lured it over a buried colonnade whose columns kissed the hull underneath with a sound like knuckles rapping a coffin lid, and both times the skiff sheared off, its pilot unwilling to gamble their keel on architecture they couldn't see.

Wren called the water from the nest. Shallows, snags, the places where a rooftop reached up hungry. His voice stayed steady because Brannick had told him to keep it steady, and because keeping it steady was the only contribution he had left that was worth anything.

Behind them, astern, the tribunal hall sank slowly into the brown water as its ceiling came down in stages — deliberate, controlled, Brannick's work, bringing the weight of twenty years of stone down across the vault approach. The Compact's divers would be digging for days.

Then the third hull came around the headland of the customs house, and it hadn't been in the Processional at all, and Wren's voice cracked on the warning.

"They flanked us," he shouted. "They were waiting — they knew the route —"

Of course they knew the route. Someone had told them. The same someone. The name sat in Wren's mouth like a fishbone.

The hull closed. Grapples arced across the gap between the ships, trailing chain, and the first one bit into the Tern's stern rail with a shriek that Wren felt in his teeth.

What happened next happened fast, and Wren saw almost none of it clearly, the way you never see the important moments clearly, only in pieces afterward, forever.

He saw Sefa's cleaver come down on the grapple line and bounce, and come down again, her burned arm hanging wrong. He saw Odile at the stern with a boathook, methodical, terrifying, unhooking chain like she was doing laundry. He heard Kestrel's voice, level as glass, saying *hard to port, Wren, hard to port NOW*, and he hauled the wheel and the Tern swung and the second grapple splashed short.

And he saw Brannick.

Brannick stood on the sinking steps of the tribunal hall, waist-deep, one hand raised — not waving goodbye, Wren realized later. Signaling. Three fingers, then two, then one. *Fuel remaining. Minutes remaining. Go.*

The last grapple caught. The hull began to win the tug-of-war, because iron always wins tug-of-war eventually. And Brannick waded toward it, calm as Sunday, carrying a spanner the size of a small dog, and climbed aboard the Compact ship's bow like a man boarding a ferry, and the last thing Wren saw of him was the Compact sailors surging around him and Brannick's arms going up — surrendering, or maybe just stretching, or maybe opening wide enough to hold the attention of twelve men at once, which was its own kind of engineering.

The grapple line went slack. Someone aboard the hull had cut it — or Brannick had, with the spanner, in the confusion, in the first three seconds, before they took the wrench and took him.

The Tern lurched free.

* * *

They ran on fumes. Actual fumes — Brannick had blended the dregs of the reserve with lamp oil and something Sefa cooked with, and the engine complained about it in a language of knocks and coughs that Wren translated for Kestrel from the engine room ladder, lying a little, the way Brannick had taught him, subtracting from the truth so the captain wouldn't slow down.

The buoy was armed. Odile confirmed it three times, then a fourth, hands shaking, voice not. At the next tide, it would wake up under the water of the drowned capital and speak to every settlement on the river — every lock-keeper's shack, every ferry landing, every scavenger raft with a salvaged receiver humming in its cabin. The names. The schematics. The order with Kestrel's seal on it. All of it, everywhere, at once.

They cleared the north breach as the sun came fully up, and behind them the drowned city filled with iron, and somewhere in it, in a hold or a cell or a flooded corridor, was the man who had held the door.

Sefa sat on the deck cradling her arm and not crying, which was louder than crying. Odile stood at the stern rail facing back the way they'd come, absolutely still, the wind pulling at her grey braid. Kestrel stood at the wheel with the expression of a person carrying something breakable across ice.

Wren stood in the middle of the deck and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding all morning, the terrible kind, the kind that doesn't balance. One engineer, traded for a truth. One door, held. And the question that would not stop asking itself inside him, small and cold:

*Would it have been me, if I'd been bigger? Would it have been me, if I'd been slower?*

He didn't have an answer. He suspected nobody did. He suspected that was what made it sacrifice instead of arithmetic after all — that it couldn't be made to balance, that Brannick had known it couldn't be made to balance, and had walked down those steps anyway, humming.

"He was humming," Wren said aloud, to no one. "When they took him. He was humming."

Sefa looked over at him. Her face was wet and furious and proud, all at once, the way only Sefa's face could manage.

"Of course he was," she said. "Man finally got a door worth holding."

Ahead, open water. Behind, iron. In the tank, nothing but fumes and prayer. And Kestrel turned from the wheel and looked at each of them in turn — Sefa, Odile, Wren — with a look Wren had never seen before, a look like a person reading the last page of a map.

"They know this stretch of river," Kestrel said quietly. "There's no shallows ahead, no ruins, no cover. Nowhere to be smart." A pause. The engine knocked, coughed, knocked again, counting down. "So we're going to need to die."

Wren blinked. "...I'm sorry?"

"Not permanently," Kestrel said, and there — there it was, the ghost of the crooked smile, aimed at all of them like a lantern. "But the Tern has about ten minutes of life left in her, and I intend to spend every one of them teaching the Compact exactly what they'll believe."

The wheel turned. The bow came around toward the open, empty, trapless water.

And Wren, who had once drawn this boat in the margins of his charts beside the ships of the Compact, picked up the sounding line and went to help his captain plan a funeral.
