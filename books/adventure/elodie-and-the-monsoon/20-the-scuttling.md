## 20. The Scuttling

There is an old river saying that Sefa likes to deploy whenever I am being difficult, which is to say whenever I am being myself: *The Monsoon forgives everything except a captain who lies to her own boat.*

I have thought about that saying for twenty years without understanding it. Boats don't hear. Boats don't forgive. A hull is planking and pitch and the stubborn arithmetic of displacement, and if you treat it like a friend you will die of sentiment somewhere north of the shallows.

But standing at the Tern's rail with a hand axe in my grip and the Compact fleet coming up the channel behind us like a fist closing, I finally understood what she meant.

You can lie to a patrol. You can lie to a commodore, to a fence, to a tribunal, to yourself — I had made a career of the last one. But you cannot lie to your own boat. She knows your weight on her deck. She knows where you sleep and where you bleed and how many times you've patched her seams with promises you didn't keep. And when you open her veins in shallow water, she knows exactly what you're doing, and she does it anyway, because that is the bargain boats make with the people who love them badly.

"Kestrel." Odile's voice, low, from the companionway. "The buoy?"

"Armed," I said. "Set for the next tide."

"And the tide?"

I looked east, where the water had gone the color of hammered tin. "Two hours. Maybe less."

Behind us, three iron hulls. Ahead of us, open water and no cover worth the name. The Sandbars were two days astern, burned out of our fuel reserves along with any hope of outrunning Ashvael again. We had come out of the drowned city with the vault's originals sealed in the broadcast buoy lashed to our aft deck, Brannick's blood still drying on the vault door we'd been too slow to hold, and Wren at the wheel flying the Tern like she was made of grief instead of wood.

The commodore's lead ship was gaining. Of course it was. Iron doesn't tire.

* * *

We gathered in the chart room because that is where this crew has always made its terrible decisions — crammed around a table built for four, six of us now with Brannick gone, which left a gap in the room shaped exactly like him. Nobody sat in his spot. Nobody said his name either, but we were all sitting around it.

"The way I see it," I said, "we have two options, and both of them are bad, so let's pick the interesting one."

Sefa snorted. Even now, even with her arms bandaged to the elbow and the burn salve making her smell like a pharmacy, she found energy for contempt. It was, I think, how she prayed.

"Ashvael wants the chest," I went on. "Wants Odile. Wants me, though she'll dress that up as a pardon until the moment it's irons. What she doesn't know — what nobody knows yet — is that the chest stopped mattering an hour after we left the vault. The buoy's rigged. At next tide it fires everything. Schematics, manifests, names, my own seal on the destruction order, all of it, to every settlement on the river that keeps a receiver running. Which, thanks to the whisper-network, is most of them."

"So the truth gets out regardless," Wren said slowly. He'd barely slept since the archive. There was a new geometry to his face, something older settling into the places where the Compact's order used to live. "Then why are they still chasing us?"

"Because they don't know about the buoy. They think we're running with the evidence. As long as they believe that, they chase *us* and not the hamlets." I laid my palm flat on the chart. "Which means the only thing left to protect isn't the documents. It's the people carrying them."

Odile understood first. She always did; it was the navigator in her, reading currents before they showed on the surface. Her eyes went wide, then narrow.

"You're going to sink her."

"I'm going to *park* her," I said. "Permanently. In the shallows off Serpent's Reach, where the mud holds a hull upright and the reeds hide a mast. Scuttle her, stage it right — debris, oil slick, maybe Wren's spare coat, sorry Wren—"

"It's fine. That coat was aspirational anyway."

"—and by the time Ashvael's divers get down there, the Tern will look like what she's supposed to look like: a salvage tub that ran out of luck, crew lost with all hands. Dead captains don't have bounties. Dead crews don't get hunted. Dead people—" I heard my own voice do something complicated on the last word, and stopped talking for a second.

"Dead people can walk into a hamlet," Odile finished quietly, "and be nobody."

"That's the idea."

Sefa was staring at me with an expression I couldn't read, which from Sefa usually meant she'd already read it and was deciding whether to say it out loud. She decided.

"You hate this plan."

"I *love* this plan," I said. "It's flawless. It's also the worst thing I've ever done to anyone, including the time I lied to all of you about who I was, and I'm aware of the competition."

"No." She shook her head, careful of the bandages. "You're doing the math wrong. You keep saying *you're* sinking her. Like it's yours alone." She looked around the table. "She carried all of us. Wren learned to fly her. I fed her. Odile navigated her through water that shouldn't hold a channel. If she goes down, she goes down as ours. All of us put a hand on the seacocks. That's not sentiment, Kestrel. That's just accurate bookkeeping."

And that — that was the moment I nearly lost it in front of my crew, which would have ruined the whole dignified-captain performance I've maintained for years. I looked at the table, at the gap shaped like Brannick, at these five people who had voted to stop for a chest in the river and never once stopped choosing each other since, and I understood that the Tern wasn't the only thing I was about to lose. I was losing the version of myself that had a boat, a name, a bounty, a history. Everything findable. Everything that could be tracked, priced, pardoned, or hanged.

What was left over, apparently, was family.

"All hands on the seacocks," I said, when I could trust my voice. "All of us. Bookkeeping."

* * *

Serpent's Reach at dusk looks like the river gave up halfway through becoming land. Reeds to the horizon, channels that wander like drunkards, water the depth of a rumor. I have run this stretch since I was fifteen and knew it blind, which is precisely why I chose it: Ashvael's iron hulls draw too much to follow us in here. She'd have to stand off in the deep channel and send boats, and boats take time, and time was the one cargo we had in abundance.

We came in slow, engine at a whisper, Wren calling depths from the bow in a voice gone very steady. Twelve feet. Nine. Seven and a half. The Tern's keel kissed mud with a sound like a long exhale.

"There," I said. "That's home now."

Nobody laughed. It wasn't a joke. Or it was entirely a joke, which on this boat amounts to the same thing.

We worked fast and quiet, the way grief makes people efficient. Debris over the side — a hatch cover, a coil of line, the galley's dented kettle that Sefa insisted on sacrificing with full military honors ("She served eleven thousand meals. She deserves a burial."). Oil poured on the water in a spreading rainbow. Wren's coat hung from a broken spar. Odile took the skiff's painter and rigged it to look torn away rather than launched, because Ashvael was thorough and thorough people notice tidy staging.

The buoy we moved to the skiff, wrapped in sacking, lashed down amidships like the precious, dangerous cargo it was. Two hours to the tide. Somewhere out in the dark, every lock-keeper and ferrywoman and scavenger with a receiver was waiting, the way the river people had waited twenty years for someone to hand them back their dead.

Then it was time for the seacocks.

Brannick had shown me where they were my first month aboard, back when I still pretended I might someday sell this boat and retire. Four of them, down in the bilge, brass wheels gone green with age. One by one we went down the ladder into the dark and wet-smelling heart of her — Sefa first, one-armed, turning hers with her teeth gritted; then Wren; then Odile; then me.

The water came in the way river water always comes in, patient and total. Not a rush. A decision.

I stayed below longest. I'll admit that. I stood in the rising black water with my hand flat against her frames and told her things I never told the crew — that she'd been the first place I ever felt safe, that I was sorry, that I hoped wherever hulls go when they stop being boats, the water there was kind. The old saying came back to me one more time, and I finally answered it out loud, to nobody, in an emptying bilge:

"I'm not lying to you. I never did. Everyone else, yes. Never you."

Then I climbed up into the air, and we pulled away, and I did not look back, because I am a professional, and professionals do not look back, and if you believe that I have a bridge in Vessia to sell you.

* * *

We rowed.

Five of us in a skiff built for three, low in the reeds, oars muffled with rags, while behind us the Tern settled by degrees onto the mud that would hold her forever. The fleet's lamps burned out in the deep channel, patient as circling birds. At some point a searchlight swept the reed line and passed over us, and I watched Wren not breathe for eleven seconds, which I counted, because counting kept me from thinking about the sound the Tern made as she went down — a groan, then a sigh, then nothing, the way a house sounds when the last person leaves.

"They'll buy it," Odile murmured. "Debris pattern's right. Oil's right. They'll dive the wreck and find a sealed chest-shaped box in the hold."

"We left them one," I said. "Full of ballast stones and Sefa's oldest recipe cards."

"My *recipe cards*—"

"Your most classified assets. Let Ashvael decode *those*."

That got a laugh out of them, thin and shocked, the laugh of people surfacing from deep water. Even Sefa, who then winced and blamed me for it, correctly.

We lay on the oars in the reed-shadow and waited for the tide.

I want to tell you I felt triumphant. Here is what I actually felt: light. Unbearably, terrifyingly light. Twenty years I had carried a name like ballast — Kestrel Nakamura, enforcer, arsonist, ghost — and a boat like a reputation, and a route like a cage, and I had called all of it freedom because moving fast feels like being free if you never stop long enough to check. Now the name was dead in the water. The boat was mud-fast under three feet of river. The Compact believed us drowned. Every handle they'd ever had on me had sunk with the seacocks, and what remained was five people in a borrowed skiff with a wrapped bundle between their knees and no possessions whatsoever.

I have never in my life been so completely unfindable. I have never in my life felt so much like I existed.

"Kestrel." Wren, at the tiller, watching the eastern sky. "Tide's turning."

On cue — because the river keeps its appointments even when governments don't — the buoy woke.

I felt it more than heard it: a hum through the hull of the skiff, through the water itself, a vibration like the river clearing its throat. Then the transmitter's pulse went out across the dark, riding the tide, and somewhere ahead of us a lock-keeper's receiver chattered awake, and a ferrywoman's, and a scavenger's, and a hundred others strung along the banks like lanterns. The schematics. The manifests. The engineers' names. Commodore Ashvael's signature. My seal on the destruction order, signed in a younger hand that belonged to a person who had died tonight along with a boat.

Every settlement on the Monsoon was going to wake up knowing the truth about Vessia. Knowing it in the Compact's own handwriting.

Odile had her hand pressed flat against the wrapped buoy, the way you'd touch a sleeping child or a lit fuse. She was crying without any noise at all, which is the Vessian way, I was learning — grief conducted inward, stored against winters.

"It's done," she whispered. "Twenty years, and it's done."

"Not done," I said. "Started. Truth's a current, Odile. It's only useful while it's moving."

Out in the channel, the fleet's searchlights suddenly went frantic — sweeping, crossing, converging on our staged grave. Ashvael had figured out something was wrong, or was about to, or had received the transmission herself and was learning, right now, in front of her officers, what the river thought of her engineering. Either way, the hunt for the Tern was over. The hunt for everyone responsible was just beginning, and it wouldn't be run by iron ships. It would be run by lock-keepers closing gates, by ferrywomen refusing crossings, by every bank of the river at once.

But that was tomorrow's weather.

Tonight we rowed south, toward the marsh hamlet, toward the whisper-network's safehouses, toward the one piece of unfinished business that none of us had said aloud since the vault door closed with Brannick on the wrong side of it.

Sefa said it first, into the dark, in the tone she used for announcing dinner.

"They have our engineer."

"Yes," I said.

"And the tribunals start forming within days, once the towns digest what that buoy told them. Locks close. Fleet movements stall. Ashvael's going to have her hands full holding her own crews together." Odile again, navigating, plotting a course out of pure fury and hope.

"Meaning there will be exactly one window," I said, "when the place they're holding him is understaffed."

Wren turned the tiller a degree. "How understaffed?"

"Understaffed enough," I said, "for five dead people in a stolen skiff."

The river carried us south, and ahead of us, faint on the night wind, we could already hear the first lock bell of the uprising ringing.
