## 21. The Tide That Answers

The buoy spoke at dawn.

Odile heard it before she understood it—a thin, singing tone rising out of the marsh reeds, the kind of sound a drowned bell makes when the current finally finds its clapper. She was already awake. She had been awake for three days, mostly, sleeping in snatches on a pallet in Ferrywoman Ilsa's back room while the marsh hamlet of Tidewait held its breath around her.

She went outside barefoot into the grey light and stood among the ferrywomen and lock-keepers and scavenger children who had gathered at the water's edge, all of them looking up at nothing, listening to the sky.

Every settlement on the Monsoon had a receiver. It was an old law, older than the Compact—*the river speaks to those who live on it*—and the Compact had never bothered to tear down the receivers because they had never believed anyone would dare use them for anything but tide tables and fish prices.

Now the voice coming through was Vessian.

Not the language—though Odile's chest tightened to hear the old cadences woven through it—but the *voice*. Her brother's voice, reconstructed from his letters, read aloud by the buoy's patient machinery in the accent of a nation that had been dead for twenty years. The manifests. The floodgate schematics. The names of the engineers who had built the gates, and the names of the people who had ordered them built, and the name of the young Compact enforcer who had burned the archives that would have told everyone sooner.

And then, after all of it, one sentence repeated in every tongue the river spoke:

*Vessia did not drown. Vessia was drowned.*

Nobody in Tidewait said anything for a long moment. Then old Ilsa, who had ferried refugees off the Vessian rooftops as a girl and never once spoken of it, took off her hat and held it against her chest, and every other person on that bank did the same, and the marsh went quiet in a way Odile had not heard since she was nine years old.

"They're going to come for us," said one of the scavenger boys, who was perhaps twelve and had the practical look of someone already calculating escape routes.

"No," said Ilsa, putting her hat back on. "They're going to come for *them*. And this time, boy, we're going to be standing between."

* * *

It happened faster than Odile could have imagined, and she had spent twenty years imagining things happening fast.

By midmorning, the lock-keepers had closed the locks. All of them. It was, Odile learned later, almost choreographed—the whisper-network doing what it had always done, passing word hand to hand, skiff to skiff, from the marsh hamlets up through the middle river and into the delta towns, except this time the word was not *hide* but *now*. Lock-keepers who had spent two decades saluting the crane-and-chain simply reported mechanical failure. Gates jammed. Chains rusted through. Terrible shame, gentlemen, might take weeks.

The Compact's salvage fleet, scattered across six tides of search grid hunting a boat full of ghosts, found itself unable to move. Iron-hulled ships need fuel, and fuel moves by barge, and barges need locks, and the locks were closed. Commodore Ashvael's great machine ground to a halt not because anyone attacked it but because every single person whose labor it depended on looked at the water one morning and quietly declined.

"It's like watching a man discover he was only ever standing on other people's shoulders," Kestrel said. They had appeared in Tidewait two days after the scuttling, alive and sunburned and wearing a stranger's coat, and Odile had hugged them hard enough to crack a rib before remembering she was angry at them for faking their own death without telling her. She was still angry. She intended to stay angry for approximately the rest of her life, or at least until supper.

"The tribunals are starting," Wren said, sliding down the ladder from the watch platform Ilsa kept atop her boathouse. He was different now—harder to startle, slower to believe anything printed on official paper—but his eyes still went bright when he had news. "Three of them. One at the delta, one at the middle locks, one here. River law. Not Compact law." He grinned. "Scavengers, ferrywomen, lock-keepers. My aunt used to say the river keeps its own court. I thought she meant the weather."

"Your aunt," said Sefa, stirring the enormous pot of rice and smoked eel she had commandeered Ilsa's kitchen to make, "sounds like she'd have gotten along with mine. Sit down, all of you, before it gets cold. Brannick is going to walk into this camp any hour now, and he will want seconds, and if there aren't any I will never forgive any of us."

"Any news?" Odile asked, and hated how her voice caught on it.

Wren's grin softened. "He was seen. Two days ago, being moved downriver on Ashvael's flagship, toward the delta tribunal. Under guard." He paused. "Under *arrest*, Odile. The fleet's own marines arrested him. Apparently holding a vault door against a boarding party reads differently now that everyone knows what was in the vault."

Odile sat down at the long table and let Sefa put a bowl in front of her and did not taste a single bite of it.

* * *

The tribunal at Tidewait convened on a barge.

That was the first thing that struck Odile about it—that they hadn't borrowed or stolen a courtroom but simply lashed four flatboats together and laid planking across them, so that the whole court floated, rocking gently, and the judges sat with their feet literally in the jurisdiction they claimed. There were seven of them: Ilsa; a lock-keeper named Tomas with forearms like dock rope; a scavenger matriarch called Grandmother Six-Fingers, which turned out to be literal and, Odile gathered, lucky; two dredge captains; a boy of maybe nineteen who represented the raft communities; and, to everyone's astonishment, a former Compact tide-clerk named Merrin who had walked into the marsh three days ago carrying a satchel of records and a confession.

Ashvael was brought in on the second day.

Odile had prepared herself for a monster. What arrived instead was a tall, immaculate woman in a spotless uniform, hands bound with river-cord, escorted by six of her own former marines—who had, Odile learned, voted on it, formally, the way the Compact taught them to vote on everything, and then done the one thing the Compact never imagined its procedures would permit: voted wrong.

The commodore looked out over the assembled crowd of mud-splattered river people and said, with genuine puzzlement, "You have no authority here."

"Ma'am," said Grandmother Six-Fingers, settling onto her bench with the creak of a woman who had pulled a thousand bodies out of this river, "we have the only authority there is. We live here."

They read the charges aloud. Odile listened to her brother's name spoken in open air, in daylight, by strangers—as evidence, as grief, as a thing that mattered—and had to grip the rail of the barge until her knuckles went white. Kestrel stood beside her, close enough that their shoulder pressed hers, and said nothing, which was its own kind of fluency now.

Ashvael's defense was everything Odile expected and somehow worse for it. Necessary engineering. Regional stability. The flood had prevented a wider war. The numbers had been run. Sacrifices had been calculated. She said *the numbers* the way other people said *the weather*, and Odile watched Wren's face across the crowd and saw the last of his childhood idealism finish dying there, quietly, without drama.

Then Merrin, the tide-clerk, stood up with her satchel, and produced the requisition orders, and read them aloud—the actual handwriting, the actual signatures, the actual bureaucratic banality of mass murder—and something in the crowd shifted. Odile felt it pass through the assembled hundreds like a current under a hull. Not rage exactly. Something more dangerous. Recognition.

"You signed these," said Tomas the lock-keeper, not unkindly, holding up a page.

"I executed policy," said Ashvael.

"Someone had to open the gates," said Ilsa.

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and draft and direction.

"I gave the order," Ashvael said finally, and Odile thought: twenty years. Twenty years of *necessary*, and it took one lock-keeper asking a simple question to make her say it out loud.

The sentence, when Grandmother Six-Fingers pronounced it, was not what any of them expected. No gallows. The river people didn't build gallows; they had learned long ago that hanging one officer just made room for another. Instead: Ashvael would spend the rest of her life working the locks. Opening and closing them, day after day, tide after tide, for the people she had tried to rule—under supervision, in plain clothes, answering to lock-keepers. "You wanted to control the river," said Six-Fingers. "Congratulations. You've got the worst job on it."

The crowd laughed—not cruelly, but with the deep relief of people who had braced for blood and gotten poetry instead. Even Ashvael's escort laughed. Odile found herself laughing too, wet-eyed, holding Kestrel's arm, and thought: *Elias, did you hear? They didn't even kill her. They made her useful.*

* * *

Brannick came home on the third evening, walking up the marsh path behind two marine escorts who had, somewhere along the way, stopped being escorts and started being luggage-carriers for a man who had clearly charmed them thoroughly over several days of shared rations.

He was thinner. He had a new scar along his jaw. He was, Odile decided, the most beautiful sight the Monsoon had ever produced.

Sefa hit him at a dead run, which would have toppled a smaller man, and Brannick absorbed it the way he absorbed everything—arms folding around her, chin resting on her head, murmuring something into her hair that made her laugh and sob at once. Wren circled them both shouting questions. Kestrel hung back, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a captain trying very hard to look like this was a normal Tuesday.

"Captain," Brannick said over Sefa's head, grinning. "I hear you sank my engine room."

"She was your engine room for eleven years," Kestrel said. "You don't get to keep her."

"Eleven years and one flood," Brannick agreed, and crossed the distance, and embraced Kestrel with the particular fierceness of a man who had held a door against soldiers partly because he knew, absolutely, that the people behind it would come back for him. Odile joined the pile. It was undignified. It was, she thought, the entire point.

Later, when the fires were lit and Sefa was feeding half the hamlet and the marine escorts had defected permanently into the rice line, Brannick settled beside Odile on the dock, legs dangling over black water.

"Tell me about him," he said. "Your brother. You never did."

So she did. About Elias, who fixed clocks and floodgates and once their mother's heart, in that order of difficulty. Who wrote letters in beautiful careful script because he believed precision was a form of love. Who saw the numbers run and understood what they meant and tried, with nothing but paper, to stop a nation from drowning. Who was silenced so efficiently that for twenty years even his sister had believed the official story.

"He'd have liked this," Odile said, nodding at the fires, the noise, the marsh full of people who remembered. "All of it except the singing. He hated when I sang."

"You sing beautifully."

"He said the same thing. He was lying to be kind. It was a whole thing."

Brannick laughed, low and warm, and bumped her shoulder with his. "You know you don't have to sail with us anymore. If you wanted to stay. Rebuild. There'd be a place for you here—there's a place for you anywhere on this river now."

Odile looked out at the water. Somewhere beneath that black surface lay streets she had walked as a child, a tribunal hall, a vault where her brother's words now lived in a broadcast buoy rigged to speak again every year on the anniversary, forever, so no one would ever again agree to forget. Somewhere south, a patched-up salvage hull waited in a hidden slough, renamed, repainted, ready.

"I spent twenty years running from being Vessian," she said. "Turns out the whole river was Vessian the whole time. Or close enough." She smiled. "No. I'm sailing. But I'll come back. Every year. For the mourning and the singing."

"Even though Elias hated the singing?"

"*Especially* because."

* * *

On the last morning, the tide brought gifts.

That was how Ilsa described it, standing on her dock with her hat in her hands as skiffs converged on Tidewait from every direction—delta boats and middle-river boats, rafts and dredges and one improbable paddle-steamer flying a bedsheet flag embroidered with a crude but recognizable Vessian seal. Word had gone out through the whisper-network that the crew of the sunken Tern was here, alive, and the river had responded the way rivers do: by delivering things.

A new anchor chain from the delta smiths. Jars of pickled eel from a raft community Wren had once, apparently, saved from a patrol skiff and never mentioned. A coil of rope from the lock-keepers' guild, tied with a note reading *for the next time you need to hold a door*. And from a delegation of dredge captains, wrapped in oilcloth: a ship's bell, salvaged from the mud of the drowned city, cleaned and polished, engraved with a name.

Not *Tern*. That boat was gone, and everyone agreed she deserved to rest. The new name gleamed in careful letters, and Odile read it aloud because Kestrel had gone suddenly, suspiciously hoarse:

*Elodie*.

"That's—" Kestrel started.

"We know what it means," said Ilsa. "The ferrywomen talk. A story about a girl and a monsoon. Seems fitting, for a boat that outran a fleet." She set the bell in Kestrel's arms like a christening. "Besides, the river likes a good name. And yours needed a fresh one anyway."

Kestrel held the bell and looked, for a long moment, like a person trying to remember how to cry properly, and then cleared their throat and said, in full captain's voice, "Somebody find me a crew."

"You're looking at it," said five voices at once.

Odile leaned on the rail and watched her family argue cheerfully about rigging schedules in the morning light, and below the hull the tide came in strong and clean, carrying the river's answer to every bank at once: *We remember. We remember. We remember.*
