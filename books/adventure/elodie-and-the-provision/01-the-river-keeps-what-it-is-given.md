## 1. The River Keeps What It Is Given

The flood went out the way a drunk leaves a bar — loudly, and taking things that didn't belong to it.

Jude Blackwood stood at the rail of the *Magpie* and watched the Kestrel River run brown and swollen past Graywater Bend, carrying fence posts, half a chicken coop, a drowned orchard's worth of apples bobbing like drowned eyes. Three days ago the water had been over the town dock by four feet. Now it was dropping fast, and when the Kestrel dropped it left gifts. That was the arrangement nobody signed: the river gave, the river took, and every spring Jude made her living sorting one from the other.

"Sandbar's moved again," called Tomas from the bow, where he stood with the sounding pole planted like a man staking a claim. "It's walked fifty yards downstream. Maybe more."

"Then we walk after it." Jude swung down from the wheelhouse, boots ringing on the deck plates. "Get the dredge rigged. If the channel's shifted, the mail boat grounds herself tomorrow morning and I'll never hear the end of it."

The *Magpie* was forty years old and looked every day of it, a flat-bottomed workhorse of riveted steel with an A-frame crane bolted amidships and a hold that smelled of diesel, bilge water, and the particular sourness of rope that had been wet too many times. She was not beautiful. She was not fast. She was, in the way of certain unlovely things, exactly what she claimed to be, and Jude had built her whole life around the idea that this was enough for anyone.

Her crew numbered three. Tomas Okonkwo at the bow, twenty-three, all elbows and opinions. Marta Reyes on the winch, who'd worked the Kestrel longer than Jude had and said less than anyone Jude had ever employed. And down in the engine room, old Ferro, who communicated chiefly through the language of wrenches thrown against bulkheads in ascending order of displeasure.

They were, all of them, people the river had agreed to keep. That was the only hiring criterion Jude applied, though she'd never said so aloud.

"Drop the hook," she said, and the anchor went down with a splash that swallowed itself in the current.

The sandbar had spent the winter where charts said it should, a long pale shoulder of silt breaking the current two hundred yards off the east bank. The flood had picked it up like a child dragging a blanket and dumped it somewhere new, and now the water over its spine ran shallow enough to boil white in the morning light. Jude could read the bar the way other people read weather — the color of the water telling her depth, the texture of the boil telling her what lay underneath. Silt, mostly. Silt and whatever the river had decided to bury there this time.

Marta paid out the dredge cable and the bucket went down, bit into the bar, came up dripping. First load: gray mud, a tangle of weed, a beer bottle older than Tomas. Second load: more of the same, plus a bicycle frame fused into a single rusted sculpture. Third load hit something that made the whole cable shudder and the winch groan like a struck animal.

"Hold," Marta said, which was the most words she'd used before noon in a month.

Jude crossed to the winch. The cable stood taut as a harp string, humming faintly, and the drum had stopped paying out with the bucket barely ten feet down. Whatever was down there wasn't silt. Silt let go. This held on.

"Snagged on a log," Tomas offered.

"Logs roll." Jude leaned over the rail, squinting down into the brown water. The current here broke oddly, she saw now — a seam in the boil, a place where the flow split around something with edges. Something square. Her stomach did a small, professional drop. Square things under rivers were almost always bad news: bridge pilings, sunken barges, cars.

"Take us up two feet and swing left," she called to the wheelhouse. Ferro answered with a blast of reverse and the *Magpie* walked sideways against her anchor, cable creaking, until the bucket hung over the edge of whatever it had bitten.

"Again."

The bucket went down. Came up slower than it should have, the winch laboring, and when it broke the surface it was cradling a shape that made even Marta take a half step back.

It was a chest. Not a sea chest, not a steamer trunk — nothing so honest. It was a strongbox, maybe four feet long, built of black iron banded with lead, and the lead had kept it clean. Twenty years or two hundred, the river hadn't touched it. Water sheeted off its flat lid in sheets, and set into that lid, centered like a signature, was a stamp pressed deep into the metal: a mountain above three wavy lines, ringed by lettering worn nearly smooth but still legible if you knew to look.

Tomas knew to look. Jude watched him go still in a way he never went still, watched his face do something complicated and then shut like a door.

"That's—" he started.

"I know what it is," Jude said.

Vaskaria. The word sat in her mouth like a stone she'd swallowed years ago and never quite passed. Every map she owned showed the Kestrel rising clean out of the northern mountains, unhindered, unclaimed. But the maps were twenty years younger than the truth. There had been a country up there once, a thin green wedge of valleys between the range and the border, with towns and a capital and a postal service and a seal — the mountain, the three rivers — stamped on everything its government touched. Then there had been a war nobody called a war, and an annexation nobody voted on, and one spring the maps simply came back from the printers without it, the way a family photo comes back without the uncle nobody mentions anymore.

The river had risen out of Vaskaria. Of course it had. Rivers didn't sign treaties.

"It's probably nothing," Jude said. "Some bureaucrat's filing cabinet. Some customs house safe that went off a bridge during the fighting." She heard her own voice doing what it always did in moments like this — flattening, smoothing, talking the world back down to a size she could carry. "Lead-lined. Heavy as sin. We don't have the gear to haul it properly and no buyer within a hundred miles wants government scrap."

"So we throw it back?" Tomas asked.

"We put it on the bank and forget it."

But even as she said it, Jude was looking at the thing. There is a kind of object that seems to look back — not supernaturally, nothing so easy as that, just with the plain weight of having survived something you can't imagine. The chest sat in its sling of cable, streaming water, and it had the patience of all buried things. It had waited twenty years under a sandbar. It could wait considerably longer than her discomfort.

"Rig the boom," she said finally. "We'll set it on the gravel bar. Let it dry out. Somebody official can come collect it, and if nobody does inside a week, it's salvage and I'll sell it for lead weight and stop thinking about it."

She told herself she believed that. She was, by trade and temperament, a good judge of currents, and she registered — distantly, professionally — that she was lying against one.

* * *

Getting the chest aboard took an hour and cost them a chain. The boom groaned, Ferro swore in three languages, and when the strongbox finally thumped down onto the aft deck, the whole boat rolled to starboard as if flinching. Up close it was worse than it had been at a distance. The iron was scarred — not rust, but gouges, deliberate ones, as if someone had once tried to open it with tools and given up. One corner bore a dent shaped exactly like a boot heel. And along the lower edge, half obscured by a band of lead, someone had scratched letters into the metal with something harder than patience:

**DO NOT OPEN — BY ORDER OF —**

The rest was gone, ground away or corroded, and the sentence hung unfinished the way certain sentences do, more troubling for their amputation.

"By order of who?" Tomas asked.

"Doesn't matter. Order expired twenty years ago with everything else." Jude crouched beside the chest and ran her thumb along the seal. Mountain, three rivers. She'd seen it before, once, years ago — on a coin an old ferryman carried, rubbed smooth as a pebble, kept in a pouch he wouldn't explain. He'd gone overboard one night in high water and they'd never found him either. The river keeps what it is given. She'd thought it a comfort once. Standing over the chest, it sounded like a threat.

"Lock's a lever tumbler, old pattern," Marta said behind her. Jude hadn't heard her approach; Marta moved like ballast shifting. "I could have it open in ten minutes."

"No."

Marta raised one eyebrow, which from her constituted a full argument.

"It's not ours," Jude said. "Whatever's in it, it belongs to somebody's paperwork, some ministry that may or may not still exist, and I have learned —" she stopped, recalibrated, started again — "I have learned that the safest thing to be on this river is uninvolved. We found scrap. We'll report scrap. End of story."

Marta looked at her for a long moment with those flat river-colored eyes, then turned away, which meant the argument was lost, or postponed, or simply beneath her. Hard to say with Marta. That was rather the point of Marta.

They spent the rest of the day clearing the channel, and the work was good the way routine work is good — it filled the hands and emptied the head. Bucket after bucket of silt came up and went onto the bar. By late afternoon the boil over the sandbar had smoothed to a clean riffle, the mail boat's draft would clear with a foot to spare, and Jude allowed herself the small satisfaction of a job done invisibly well. Nobody would ever know the channel had been fixed. That was the nature of the work. You kept the river open and stayed out of the ledger yourself.

It was near dusk, light going amber on the water, when she noticed Tomas at the stern with his phone out, crouched over the chest.

"What are you doing?"

He straightened too fast. "Checking signal. My sister was supposed to—"

"Tomas."

He held her gaze the way young men do when they've already decided the price is worth it. He was a good deckhand, quick and strong and cheerful, and he had come to the *Magpie* two seasons ago from somewhere upriver he never described, with an accent that softened whenever he passed the drowned valley country, and Jude had hired him precisely because she recognized the shape of his silence because it matched her own.

"I photographed it," he said quietly. "The seal. The writing on the side. Before you tell me to delete it — I'm not going to."

"You don't know what that is."

"I know what it isn't." His voice stayed low, steady, and that steadiness frightened her more than shouting would have. "It isn't scrap. Scrap doesn't get sealed in lead. Scrap doesn't say *do not open*. My grandmother came down out of those valleys in '09 with nothing but a spoon and her sister's name written on her arm, and the name wasn't real because the country wasn't real anymore, and nobody — *nobody*, Captain — has ever said one true sentence about what happened up there." He looked down at the chest, and the last light caught the seal, mountain and rivers, patient as a held breath. "That box is a sentence. Maybe the only one left. And you want to leave it on a gravel bar for the next flood to take back."

"The next flood will do what floods do," Jude said. "It's not our business."

"Whose business is it, then?"

And there it was — the question she'd spent ten years building her life to avoid, sitting on her deck in a lead-lined box, asking itself in a deckhand's voice. Jude looked upriver, toward the mountains going blue and then black as the sun dropped behind them, toward the valleys that weren't on any map she owned, toward whatever the chest had come down from, riding a flood the way all the dead of that country must have ridden theirs.

"Secure the deck," she said. "We make Graywater Bend by dark. Tie it down below — forward hold, under the tarpaulin."

Tomas blinked. "You're keeping it?"

"I'm *not throwing it back*," Jude said, and heard the difference, and hated that she heard it. "There's a difference. Get the tarp."

He got the tarp. They wrestled the chest down the hatch and into the forward hold, and when Jude slid the cover closed over it, the sound of the latch was small and final, like a door in a dream.

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep. The *Magpie* lay tied at Graywater Bend, riding gentle against the pilings, and the river talked to itself outside her cabin window the way it always did, and Jude lay in her bunk staring at the overhead and listening to the one extra sound aboard: nothing. The silence of a locked hold. It was remarkable how loud it was.

Ten years, she thought. Ten years since she'd been anyone worth finding. Ten years of measuring her life in miles instead of years — six hundred miles of Kestrel, two hundred of the Vane, every reach and riffle catalogued in her head like prayers — and in all that time she had built herself a religion of invisibility with three commandments: keep moving, keep working, keep out of the ledger. No registry under her true name. No photographs. No history heavier than a boat's papers. Other people collected lives; Jude collected distances. It had worked. It had worked so well that sometimes she forgot it was a wall and not a home.

And now the river — her river, her one faithful thing — had reached down into its own drowned memory and laid a piece of somebody else's buried past on her deck, stamped and sealed and scratched with a warning, and the wall felt thinner tonight than it had in a decade.

Because she knew — she'd known it on the sandbar, watching the current split around those square edges — that things like this chest did not surface by accident. Floods brought up bottles and bicycles and bones. They did not bring up lead-lined strongboxes bearing the seals of erased nations, unless the erasure was starting to leak. Unless somewhere upstream, in valleys that officially contained nothing but scenery, the past had gotten heavy enough to float.

In the morning, she told herself, she'd find a surveyor's office, a customs man, anybody with a stamp of their own, and hand the problem off. In the morning it would be somebody else's business.

Outside, the river kept talking. Down in the hold, the chest kept its counsel. And at the stern, unbeknownst to her, Tomas Okonkwo lay awake too, reviewing the photographs on his cracked screen — the seal sharp in the amber light, the broken warning legible, and in the corner of the final frame, accidental, half-blurred: the face of the woman who wanted to throw it back, looking at the camera like it was a mirror.

He was not sure yet who he would send them to. But he was sure, with the certainty of a man whose grandmother had owned one spoon, that he would send them to someone.
