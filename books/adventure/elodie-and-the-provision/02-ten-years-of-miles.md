## 2. Ten Years of Miles

The chest sat in the hold like a passenger who wouldn't pay his fare.

Jude Blackwood had hauled a lot of things up and down this river in ten years — diesel drums, salt pork, roofing tin, coffins, once an entire disassembled chapel that took three days to unload at a mission landing nobody else would touch. She knew how freight behaved. Good freight rode quiet. Bad freight shifted, groaned, complained against its lashings like it had opinions. The chest did neither. It just sat there, lead-lined and patient, wearing the seal of a country that no longer existed, and somehow that was worse than any amount of shifting. Freight with opinions you could argue with. This thing just waited, the way debts wait.

She'd covered it with a tarpaulin and a stack of netting, which was a stupid gesture because she was the only one who went below anymore, but she did it anyway, the way you throw a sheet over a mirror in a house where someone died. Not for the room's sake. For yours.

* * *

Ten years ago, Jude Blackwood had been a different kind of captain on a different kind of water, and her name had opened doors instead of closing them.

She'd grown up in the survey offices' shadow — daughter of a hydrographer, raised on charts the way other children were raised on scripture. Her father had drawn the river for the government, line by sounding by contour, and he'd taught her early that a map was never neutral. *Every map is an argument*, he used to say, tapping his pen against the paper. *Somebody decided what goes on it and what stays off. Your job is to know whose argument you're drawing.* She'd thought it was just something old men said over brandy. She'd spent a decade learning otherwise.

By twenty-eight she held a commission in the River Survey Corps. By thirty-one she was running her own detail — six men, two launches, a mandate to chart the middle reaches after the war's end, when the government decided the flooded territories needed remapping for "administrative clarity." That was the phrase. Administrative clarity. She could still hear it in the voice of the man who'd briefed them, a civilian in a gray suit whose name she'd never learned and whose face she had never once managed to forget.

The Vaskarian reaches were a hundred miles of drowned villages and dead tributaries. The war hadn't just broken the nation; the peace had erased it. New borders drawn through the floodline, new names stenciled on the towns, and orders — sealed orders, opened only past the last checkpoint — instructing the survey teams to log depths and hazards only. No cultural features. No church spires, no graveyards, no ruins marked. Nothing that would suggest anyone had ever lived there.

Jude had followed those orders for eleven days.

On the twelfth day, off what the charts called Marker 44 but what her soundings kept insisting had been a town called Velika Reka, her lead line came up tangled in something that wasn't weed. A child's shoe. Leather, small, the stitching still intact, preserved by cold water. One of her crew — a boy named Anselm, who'd signed on for the wages and the adventure — fished it out and turned it over in his palm and asked, in the easy voice of a man asking about weather, whether they should log it as a hazard.

Nobody laughed. Nobody said anything. The shoe sat in the bottom of the launch, filling slowly with river, and Jude looked at it for a long time before she gave the order to move on.

They logged it as a hazard.

That night she couldn't sleep in the survey cabin, so she sat in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low and read the sealed annex she'd been given and told herself she hadn't read. Most of it was bathymetry. But folded into the back, in a hand different from the rest, was a page of annotations — not measurements but tallies. Villages. Dates. Numbers beside each one, and beside some of the numbers, a single word: *complete*. And at the bottom, initialed, a routing stamp from an office that still existed, in a building she'd walked past a thousand times, staffed by men who still held their posts.

She understood then what administrative clarity meant. The flood hadn't killed those people. The flood had been the *alibi*.

She reported nothing. That was the first cost. She told herself there was nothing she could do — a junior officer, six men under her, a state behind the orders with all its weight. She told herself the truth would come out on its own, that such things always did. She burned the annotation page in the galley stove and watched the ink curl and thought: *now I'm the same as everyone else.* The smoke smelled like every compromise she would ever make, arriving all at once, ten years early.

But the river kept giving her shoes. Lead lines came up with rosaries, door hinges, a wedding band fused to a scrap of curtain. The drowned nation spoke to her nightly in fragments, and each fragment made the silence she'd agreed to heavier, until carrying it felt like ballast she couldn't pump out.

The end came the way ends usually come for people like her — not with a decision but with a slip. At a debriefing in the capital, a senior cartographer joked about the blank spaces on the new charts. *Cleanest water we've ever surveyed,* he said. *Not a soul on it.* And Jude, exhausted and half-drunk on bad wine, heard herself say: *There were souls. We just weren't allowed to draw them.*

The room didn't go silent. That was the terrible part. The conversation simply flowed around her words and closed over them, smooth as water over a sunk stone, and she understood that everyone in that room already knew, and that her saying it aloud was not a scandal but a bookkeeping error — a small item to be corrected. Three weeks later her commission was not renewed. No charges. No explanation. A letter thanking her for her service, signed by an office, not a man.

She might have fought it. Some part of her wanted to, wanted it badly enough that she scared herself. But fighting meant becoming visible, and visible people got audited, investigated, found useful or dangerous, and either way they got *used*. She had seen what the state did with people who knew things. It didn't destroy them. It filed them.

So she did the arithmetic of disappearance. She had savings, a boat, and a trade that traveled. She moved downriver, then further downriver, then onto a different river entirely, buying her current vessel — the *Maren*, forty feet of honest, ugly workboat — with cash and a name borrowed from a woman who owed her money and died owing more. She became Captain Blackwood, freight hauler, unremarkable, forgettable. She learned to let her hair go iron-gray early because gray hair makes men guess your age wrong and wrong guesses make them underestimate you. She learned to talk about weather and fuel prices and nothing else. She learned that the safest lie is a boring one.

And somewhere in those first years, invisibility stopped being a tactic and became a discipline, and then stopped being a discipline and became a religion. She observed its rites faithfully. She kept no photographs. She wrote no letters worth keeping. She measured her life in river miles because miles don't accumulate into anything — a mile doesn't ask you what you've done with your year. Years accuse. Miles just pass. Ten thousand miles a season, and none of them leading anywhere, which was the point. A woman going nowhere has no destination to be ambushed at.

It worked. For ten years it worked so well she almost believed it was living.

* * *

Now she stood in the hold of the *Maren* with a lantern in one hand and looked at the shape under the tarpaulin, and the old religion offered no comfort.

The dredging had been routine — post-flood sandbar work, the kind of job that pays badly and reliably. Snagging the chest had been luck of the worst kind. She'd wanted to throw it back. She'd stood at the rail with the winch humming and genuinely considered letting it drop, letting the river keep what the river had taken, and she would have — except her deckhand, Tomas, had been standing right there, and a captain who throws away salvage in front of her crew raises questions, and questions are visibility, and visibility is death. So she'd kept it. Hidden from the world by accident, hidden from her crew by habit, and now it sat below decks accruing interest.

Because here was the thing she couldn't stop calculating, alone in the hold with the lantern hissing: a chest does not get lead-lined for storage. Lead is for protection — against water, against decay, and against detection. Somebody had lined this box so its contents would survive drowning *and* so they couldn't be found by someone scanning for metal. That was not a strongbox. That was an archive built to outlast a purge. Which meant somebody, twenty years ago, had known documents were coming that would need to survive both the river and the state — and had failed to get it out in time.

Which meant the chest was evidence. Of what, exactly, she didn't know. But she knew the shape of it the way she knew the shape of a storm front, and the shape matched the page she'd burned in a galley stove ten years ago, tallies and dates and the word *complete*.

Her father's voice, unhelpfully: *Your job is to know whose argument you're drawing.*

"I'm not drawing anything," she said aloud, to the lantern, to the chest, to nobody. "I'm hauling freight."

The chest said nothing. Debts never do. They just sit in the dark of the hold and compound.

She climbed out and dogged the hatch and stood in the evening air watching the river run brown and heavy with the flood's leavings, and she made herself a promise in the old grammar of her religion: sell it fast, sell it blind, take the money, tell no one, stay nothing. A buyer would turn up — salvage always found buyers, especially salvage with seals on it. She'd ask no provenance questions and answer none, and whatever was inside would become someone else's problem, the way the shoe at Marker 44 had become someone else's problem, the way everything hard in her life got handed downstream to a future self who was, by now, completely maxed out.

Down at the dock, Tomas was coiling line. He glanced up as she passed, and there was something in the glance — quick, assessing, gone too fast — that snagged on her like a hook on sunken fence wire. He'd been odd since the dredging. Quiet in a new way. Twice she'd caught him below near the hold when he had no business there.

Probably nothing. Deckhands were always odd. Probably he was just young, and young men were curious about locked boxes the way rivers were curious about weak banks.

Probably.

She walked up the plank toward Graywater Bend, telling her miles to count themselves, and behind her the *Maren* rocked gently at her moorings, carrying her debt into the dark.
