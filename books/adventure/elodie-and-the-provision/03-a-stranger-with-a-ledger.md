## 3. A Stranger with a Ledger

The rain had followed Elodie Vasseur for eleven days, and she had begun to think of it as a companion — an unpleasant one, always at her shoulder, always talking. It came down on Graywater Bend in gray sheets that turned the trading post's veranda into a drum and the river into something brown and muscular, hauling itself north with the whole sky's weight behind it.

She stood under the eaves with her ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest and watched the boats come in.

There were six of them moored along the rotting pilings: a fuel barge flying the colors of a company that had gone bankrupt two years ago, a pair of fishing skiffs, a government packet boat too clean to be anything but official, and — third from the end, riding low and sitting wrong in the water — a working dredge boat with a patched wheelhouse and no name painted anywhere she could find.

No name. She had been watching it since morning. Every other vessel on this river wore its name like a tag on a corpse's toe; this one had been scraped or sanded or simply never marked, its hull showing only a paler rectangle where letters might once have been. The captain had come ashore twice — a woman, broad-shouldered, sunburned to the color of old rope, moving with the economy of someone who had spent decades making herself small in open spaces. She bought diesel filters and rope and a sack of rice, paid in worn notes counted out exactly, spoke to no one, looked at no one, and went back aboard.

Elodie had spent four years learning to read people who did not want to be read. The archivists of the diaspora learned early that the people worth finding were the ones arranged to be unfindable.

She opened her ledger to the page she had turned down weeks ago, in a border town whose name she'd already let go of. The photograph there was grainy, printed from a negative that had crossed three borders sewn into a coat lining: a lead-lined chest, half-buried in dredged sand, stamped on its lid with a crowned double-river — two streams braiding through a coronet.

The seal of Vaskaria.

A nation that had appeared on maps for three hundred years and then, twenty years ago, appeared nowhere at all. A nation whose archives — forty tons of them, by the last honest count — had been loaded onto barges one autumn and taken downstream, and had never arrived anywhere that admitted receiving them.

Elodie closed the ledger. Her hands were steady. She had practiced this.

* * *

The trading post sold everything from outboard pistons to tinned peaches, and at midday it was crowded enough that a conversation could hide inside the noise of commerce. Elodie waited until the captain came ashore again, waited until she was standing at the counter arguing mildly about the price of caulking cotton, and then Elodie stepped up beside her and set her ledger on the counter between them, open to the photograph.

"Captain," she said. "I'd like to buy passage."

The woman didn't look at the photograph. That was the first thing Elodie noted, and it told her more than a glance would have. An innocent person looks. An innocent person says *what's that?* This one kept her eyes on the shopkeeper with the deliberate patience of someone counting seconds.

"You've got the wrong boat," the captain said. "I haul freight. No passengers."

"I don't want to be a passenger." Elodie kept her voice level, pitched under the ceiling fan's clatter. "I want to hire you. Upriver. As far as you'll take me."

"Upriver's closing. Monsoon'll have the high water another month, and after that the pass is shut till spring." The captain finally turned, and her eyes were pale gray and very still, the eyes of someone who had learned that attention is a debt and refused to owe it. "Find a packet boat."

"The packet boats are government boats."

"Then walk."

The shopkeeper, wisely, became fascinated by his inventory.

Elodie turned the ledger toward the captain fully now, and watched — carefully, hungrily — for the flicker. It came. Not in the face; the face was a locked door. But the woman's thumb moved against her forefinger, once, a small unconscious motion, the way a man touches a wound he's decided not to mention.

"You recognize the seal," Elodie said quietly.

"I recognize a picture of mud."

"It's Vaskarian. The crowned double-river. It hasn't been struck anywhere legally in twenty years, because striking it became a crime under Article Nine of the Unification Accords, which means whoever owns that chest either doesn't know what they own—" she paused, "—or knows exactly, and wishes they didn't."

Something shifted behind the gray eyes. Not guilt. Calculation. Elodie had seen guilt before; it leaked. This was tighter than that. This was a woman doing arithmetic.

"Look," the captain said, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, which frightened Elodie more than anger would have. "Whatever story you've got — and I can see it's a real one, I'm not saying it isn't — it's not mine. I pulled a chest out of a sandbar. Salvage law says it's mine. I'm selling it to a buyer in the capital, and when I sell it, it stops being my problem. That's all it ever was. That's all it's going to be."

*Salvage law.* Elodie nearly laughed. Twenty years of exile law, of tribunals held in rented halls in cities that had never heard of Vaskaria, of old men weeping over certified photocopies — and here it was reduced to salvage law, as if a nation could be drowned and its body claimed by whoever's hook snagged it first.

"That chest," Elodie said, "contains the confiscated state archive of Vaskaria. The manifests. The census rolls. The photographs from the eastern districts in the winter of the relocation. The orders." She made herself say it plainly. "The orders with signatures on them. My grandfather was the Keeper of the Provisional Archive. When the barges went downstream, he put himself on the last one, and neither he nor the archive was ever seen again. I have spent four years following rumors of lead-lined chests down every river in this country. You are looking at the only person on this river who knows what you're carrying, and I am telling you it isn't cargo. It's evidence. And it's mine by bloodright."

The fan clattered. Somewhere outside, rain hammered the tin roof of the fuel shed.

The captain looked at her for a long moment — really looked, for the first time, and Elodie felt it like a searchlight — and then said, flatly:

"No."

"You haven't even heard my offer."

"I heard enough. Whatever you're paying, the other buyer's paying more, and the other buyer doesn't stand on my deck reading manifestos at me." She picked up her caulking cotton and her change. "I'm sorry about your grandfather. Genuinely. But I'm not in the business of nations. I'm in the business of miles."

"Miles to where?" Elodie asked, and hated how it sounded — how desperate, how young. "Captain, do you know what happens to that chest after you sell it? Do you know who buys things like that? Because I do. I've spent four years watching documents surface and vanish. A photograph appears in an estate sale and is gone by morning. A manifest is offered to a university and withdrawn in a week. Someone is buying the war back, piece by piece, and burning it. Your buyer in the capital isn't a collector, Captain. Collectors keep things. Whoever's paying you is going to make sure no one ever sees what's in that box again, and you'll have helped, and you won't even know what you helped do—"

"Stop." 

It wasn't loud. But it landed like a hand flat on a table. The shopkeeper found somewhere else to be. The captain stepped close, close enough that Elodie could smell diesel and river water on her jacket, and her voice was low and hard.

"I don't know what you think I am. I'm nobody. I dredge sandbars and I haul scrap and I keep my head down and my name off things, and that has kept me alive on this river for ten years while smarter people ended up in ditches. You come at me with a dead country and a dead grandfather and a speech, and you want me to what? Turn around? Run your errand upriver into monsoon season with whatever's chasing that chest chasing me too?" She shook her head slowly. "I've got a crew. I've got obligations. I've got a boat that barely floats. I'm not a hero, and I'm not a martyr, and I'm sure as hell not a museum."

"No," Elodie said. "You're a witness. You just don't know it yet."

"And you're a stranger with a ledger." The captain's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "On my river, that makes you the dangerous one."

She walked out into the rain without hurrying, and Elodie stood at the counter with her heart going like a paddle wheel and understood, with cold clarity, that she had failed — and also that she hadn't. Because the woman hadn't denied the chest. Hadn't denied recognizing the seal. Hadn't called her a liar or a lunatic. She had said *no* the way people say *no* to a thing they are afraid of wanting.

Elodie gathered her ledger. Outside, through the silver curtain of rain, she watched the nameless boat at its mooring, riding low, keeping its secret, and she revised her plan the way she revised everything: patiently, completely, without mercy.

Passage money wouldn't work. Fine.

Then she wouldn't ask for passage.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what archivists do: writing everything down. In the back room the postmaster rented her for a scandalously small fee, she recorded the conversation verbatim while it was fresh — the captain's exact words, the thumb against the forefinger, the rectangle of pale wood where a name should have been. She wrote down the boat's description, its draft marks, the way it sat listing slightly to port as if carrying something heavy forward of the mastless middle. She wrote down the time the captain had gone aboard and the light going out in the wheelhouse.

Then she sat by the window and watched the rain and thought about her grandfather, who had taught her that an archive is not a building. *An archive,* he used to say, tapping her sternum with one dry finger, *is a promise made by the living to the dead: you will be remembered correctly.* He had boarded that barge holding that promise the way other men board lifeboats holding children.

And somewhere down the river, in a hold that smelled of bilge and diesel, his promise sat in a lead-lined box, and a woman who wanted only to be nobody was planning to sell it to somebody.

Elodie opened her ledger to a clean page and began, in small precise handwriting, to make a list. Not of arguments — she'd tried arguments. A list of facts. What the survey office would pay. What the government feared. What routes ran upriver. Where the patrols stopped. How many days until the monsoon closed the pass.

Because if the captain would not carry the truth willingly, then the truth would have to arrange its own transport, and Elodie Vasseur had four years of practice arranging things that powerful people wanted forgotten.

She was still writing when the postmaster knocked, apologetic, rain dripping off his hat.

"Beg pardon, miss. There's a fellow downstairs asking after you. Says he's from the survey office." He frowned. "Odd thing, though. He didn't ask after you by name. Asked after any travelers come through recently asking about *cargo*."

Elodie's pen stopped.

Outside, down at the moorings, half-hidden by rain, a man stood looking at the nameless dredge boat for a long, long moment — and then turned away, unhurried, and disappeared into the weather.

The captain thought she was invisible. Elodie had thought she was careful.

They were both wrong, and the river knew it first.
