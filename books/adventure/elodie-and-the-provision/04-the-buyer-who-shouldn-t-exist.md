## 4. The Buyer Who Shouldn't Exist

The surveyor arrived on a Tuesday, which was the first wrong thing about him.

Graywater Bend kept its own calendar — market days, fuel barge days, the day the mission bell cracked and was never replaced — and Tuesdays meant nothing to anyone except the woman who ran the dry goods store and counted her tins on a schedule no one else understood. Nobody came upriver on a Tuesday. Nobody came upriver at all in the week after a flood, when the channel was still rearranging itself like a man turning over in his sleep and every pilot with sense stayed tied off, drinking coffee, waiting for the river to finish its argument with itself.

Jude was on her foredeck re-packing a winch bearing when the skiff came around the bend below the trading post. It was government issue — she knew that before she could read the lettering, from the way it sat in the water, heavy in the stern where the radio gear lived, running a little too fast for the debris still riding the current. Gray hull, no name painted on the bow, just a registration number small enough that you'd have to want to read it. She'd seen boats like it in another life, another river, another name.

She set down the bearing and wiped her hands slowly on her rag, giving herself time.

The skiff eased alongside her mooring with a competence that annoyed her more than anything else about it. Whoever was driving had done this a thousand times, come alongside boats that didn't necessarily want company, and he made it look casual, which took skill. He killed his engine and let the current swing his bow in, and he looked up at her with a smile that had been assembled that morning from parts.

"Captain Blackwood."

The name went through her like cold water through a wetsuit.

Not *ma'am*. Not *captain of the dredge boat*, not the half-dozen careful non-names she'd been called for ten years by people who understood that some people on this river didn't have names, or traded them like currency, or had left theirs somewhere downstream. *Blackwood.* Her father's name. Her real one. A name she hadn't answered to since a courthouse in the capital, ten years and four thousand river miles ago, when she'd signed papers that turned her into nobody.

She kept her face doing nothing at all. That was the first discipline she'd ever learned, before engines, before rivers: a face that does nothing tells nothing.

"You've got me mixed up," she said. "Name's on the transom. Go read it."

"I did." He gestured at her hull with an open hand, friendly as a neighbor. "*Provision.* Nice old girl. Pre-war yard, am I right? They don't lay a keel like that anymore." He said it the way a man says a thing he's memorized, not a thing he knows. "But I'm not here about your boat. I'm here about what's in it."

* * *

He introduced himself as Mr. Corliss, Survey Office, Hydrographic Division, and he climbed aboard without being asked because she didn't stop him and they both understood what that meant. He was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, dressed the way government men dress when they want to look like they aren't government men — canvas jacket, scuffed boots, a hat that had been aged artificially by somebody who understood aging. Everything about him was calibrated. His friendliness was calibrated. His boots were probably calibrated.

He admired her deck. He admired her winch. He asked after her engine hours and her crew and whether the flood had cost her much work, and he listened to her answers with the focused attention of a man who wasn't listening at all, who was letting her talk while he watched how she talked. Jude gave him nothing but weather and diesel prices. She'd been giving people nothing but weather and diesel prices for a decade. She was good at it.

Then he stopped walking around her deck, planted himself, and got to it.

"There's a chest in your hold," he said. "Lead-lined. Salvaged off the sandbar below the old Vaskari channel, day after the flood crested. Your deckhand photographed it before you brought it aboard."

The river made its noise under the hull. Somewhere behind her, in the town, a dog started up and quit again.

"I don't discuss cargo," Jude said.

"No," Corliss agreed pleasantly. "You don't. That's one of the things I know about you."

* * *

Here is what Jude Blackwood knew about being invisible, learned over ten years of practice: invisibility is not something you achieve. It's something you're permitted. You can burn your papers, change your hair, take a boat so far up a forgotten river that the maps themselves give up, and you will be invisible exactly as long as nobody with resources decides otherwise. Anonymity isn't a wall you build. It's a door someone else has chosen, for now, to leave shut.

She had told herself a different story for ten years. She'd told it so long it had worn smooth as river stone: *I disappeared myself. I did it. Me.* The truth, standing on her deck in scuffed government boots, was that she had never once tested the arrangement. She had never sent so much as a postcard east, never filed a claim, never crossed a checkpoint where a camera might compare her face to a list. She had hidden inside the hiding, and somewhere, all that time, there had apparently been a file with her name on it, and someone keeping it current, and now that someone had sent Mr. Corliss up a flooded river on a Tuesday to stand on her deck and say *Blackwood* like it was nothing.

"How much do you want for it?" Corliss said.

"For what?"

"The chest. Contents intact, seals unbroken, no inventory taken, no receipts issued. Triple salvage value. I'm authorized to go higher if you find that insulting." He smiled his assembled smile. "No questions. No paperwork. You keep dredging sandbars, I keep drawing charts, and neither of us ever has to think about Vaskaria again."

Triple value. She did the arithmetic without wanting to — new planking, a rebuilt injector pump, two seasons free of the banks, the debt on the *Provision* paid down to nothing. It was a lot of money. It was also, and this was the part that made her hands want to close into fists, money offered by a man who had walked onto her boat carrying her dead name in his pocket like a knife he hadn't needed to draw yet.

"Why does the Hydrographic Division care about a chest?" she said.

Corliss looked out at the river, unhurried, a man enjoying the view. "Sediment displacement," he said. "Flood scouring. Very interesting season hydrologically."

They stood there while the lie finished settling between them like silt.

* * *

She thought about the night they'd pulled it up. The chain snagging, old Tam leaning over the rail with the hook, the whole crew grumbling because a snag meant lost hours and lost hours meant lost pay. Then the chest coming up through the brown water, streaming, and the light changing on deck — she remembered that specifically, the way everyone went quiet at once, the way even the river seemed to lower its voice. Lead-lined, iron-banded, and stamped into the lid a seal: the double mountain and the falling star, the arms of a country that had been on maps when she was young and was on none now. Vaskaria. Erased twenty years ago in a war whose official history occupied less than a page and mentioned no towns by name.

Her deckhand, Sef, had crouched over it with his phone before she could say anything, taking pictures — the seal, the bands, the mud still crusted in the letters. She'd told him to put it away. He had, mostly. She'd wanted to open it right there and he'd said, quietly, in that way he had of saying things that sounded like agreement and weren't, "Better we don't know what we're carrying, Captain. Better for everybody." And she'd heard the fear in it and mistaken the fear for wisdom, or told herself she had, and the chest had gone into the hold and sat there through three weeks of dredging like a debt she couldn't remember contracting.

Now a man from the government was offering triple value for a box he officially didn't know existed, from a division that officially drew charts, and mentioning a name that officially belonged to no one on this river.

"You know what's in it," Jude said.

"I know what the Office wants done with it," Corliss said. "That's a different question, and it's not one you need answered. That's rather the beauty of the offer. No questions. You sell a sealed box. What's inside stays a matter between the lead lining and God." He spread his hands. "Frankly, Captain, it's the cleanest transaction you'll ever make."

"And if I don't sell?"

The smile stayed exactly where it was. That was the answer. The smile staying put while everything behind it recalibrated — that was the whole answer, delivered in perfect government grammar.

"Then I'll report that the item in question doesn't exist," Corliss said, "and my superiors will continue to believe that, and things will remain simple. I'd very much like things to remain simple. Simple is why I'm here instead of someone else. Someone else would have led with the name." He tipped his head slightly. "I led with the money. I'm the polite version, Captain. Do consider that."

* * *

She walked him back to his skiff because there was nothing else to do, and because she wanted him off her deck, and because some animal part of her brain insisted that as long as she controlled the boarding ladder she controlled something. He shook her hand. His grip was warm and dry and entirely without meaning.

"Three days," he said. "I'll be down at the post until Thursday. After that I go back and write my report, and reports are like rivers, Captain — they only run one direction." He stepped down into the skiff, steadied himself against the gunwale, and looked up at her one last time with genuine, professional regret. "It was a good name, Blackwood. Whatever you did with it. Somebody spent real effort making sure it stayed quiet all these years. That kind of effort doesn't get spent forever."

The skiff pulled away, running too fast for the debris, and rounded the bend, and the river closed over the place where it had been as though it had never been there at all. That was the thing about this river. It kept what it was given. It gave back almost nothing.

Jude stood on her foredeck for a long time.

* * *

Below, in the hold, the chest sat in the shadow of the spare drum and the coiled anchor line, exactly where it had sat for three weeks, minding its own business, being a box. She went down and stood over it. She didn't touch it. Touching it felt like answering a question in front of witnesses, and even alone in her own hold she caught herself glancing at the hatch.

Triple value. New planking. The debt gone. Ten more years of miles, invisible, unlisted, unmapped — the life she'd built, purchased back at retail, with the understanding that the door stayed shut because she'd agreed to keep it shut. Except now she knew the door had never been hers. Now she knew that every quiet year had been a lease, not a life, and the landlord had finally come round, and the rent he wanted was a sealed box and her continued silence, payable in full, no questions.

And underneath the fear — because the fear was real, she could feel it sitting in her chest like ballast, cold and permanent — underneath it something else was shifting, something with more weight than fear.

Anger. Not the hot kind. The river kind. The kind that moves boulders by deciding, patiently, over years, that they are going to be somewhere else.

They had erased a country. She'd known that the way everyone knew it — a paragraph in a schoolbook, a blank space on a map, a war with no named towns. But she had hauled a piece of that blank space up out of the river with her own winch, and it was heavy, and it was lead-lined, and somebody powerful enough to send Corliss up a flooded river on a Tuesday wanted it to stay sealed and silent and nonexistent. Which meant, by the simplest arithmetic she knew, that whatever was inside it was true, and dangerous, and alive.

A box that heavy doesn't get triple value for being empty.

She climbed out of the hold, dropped the hatch, and sat down on it. Just sat, in the afternoon light, with the river talking its endless talk against her hull.

Three days. He'd given her three days, which meant he expected her to spend two of them convincing herself and one of them finding him. Government men always thought in threes; they thought everyone thought in threes.

Jude looked downriver, toward the bend and the trading post and the gray skiff tied at the post's rickety dock, and then upriver, toward the high country where the water came from, where the maps went thin and then blank, where a nation used to be.

There was a woman at the trading post. She'd come asking questions two days ago — sharp-eyed, ink-stained fingers, a foreigner's vowels, asking after a salvaged chest with a seal on it, offering passage money with the desperate precision of someone spending money she couldn't afford. Jude had refused her flat and sent her off, and had told herself the relief she felt watching her go was just the ordinary relief of a private person dodging trouble.

She hadn't believed it even then. She believed it even less now.

Trouble was already aboard. Trouble was lead-lined and triple-value and carried her father's name in a stranger's mouth. The only question left was which direction it floated.

Jude stood up, went aft, and began checking her fuel lines — not because they needed checking, but because her hands wanted work, and because a captain who might need to cast off in bad weather starts by knowing her boat is ready.

Down at the trading post, she could see the archivist's figure moving along the dock, patient as tide, coming back around again.
