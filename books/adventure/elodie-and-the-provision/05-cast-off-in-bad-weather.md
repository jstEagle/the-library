## 5. Cast Off in Bad Weather

The rain started an hour before midnight, and Jude took it as a kindness.

It came down in sheets off the ridge above Graywater Bend, hammering the tin roofs of the trading post, drowning the lamplight in the windows, and turning the whole river into a hissing gray confusion where no watchman would want to stand. She stood in it anyway, on her own deck, for a long time before she moved — because the last time she'd run from anything she'd been twenty-six years old and certain of herself, and she wanted to remember what that felt like before she did it again.

The *Maren B* sat low at her moorings, riding the brown water with the patience of a thing that had never once been asked to hurry. Forty years old if she was a day, patched twice below the waterline, honest as a mule. Jude had bought her under a name that wasn't hers and registered her in a county that barely kept records, and for ten years the boat had been the only thing in the world that knew exactly who she was and never said it out loud.

Now there was a chest in her hold that weighed like a confession, and a woman asleep in her wheelhouse who claimed it by blood.

Jude looked upriver, into the dark. Somewhere past the drowned town, past the fence lines and the checkpoints that didn't officially exist, the mountains held the pass. And somewhere past the pass, if half the rumors were true, there was still a press running — ink and paper and people stubborn enough to print what the government had spent twenty years drowning. Elodie believed it the way believers believe: completely, without evidence, with her whole thin body.

Jude believed in current, in tonnage, in the load-bearing capacity of old steel. But she'd read those manifests twice by lamplight — no, she hadn't, not yet, the chest was still locked — but she'd seen the surveyor's face when he said her real name, and that had been document enough. *Blackwood.* He'd said it soft, almost gentle, the way you say a word to prove you can afford it.

She untied the bow line first.

---

Getting Elodie aboard had been the easy part, which should have warned her. The archivist had taken one look at Jude's face when she came back from the survey office and said, "We're leaving tonight," and simply nodded, as though she'd been packed for days. Maybe she had. There was something about the woman — a stillness, a patience with waiting — that suggested a life spent sitting outside closed doors until they opened.

"Where do you want me?" Elodie had asked, standing on the dock with her satchel and her coat already dark with rain.

"Away from the windows. If anyone hails us, you're cargo."

"I've been cargo my whole life," Elodie said, and went down the hatch without another word.

Jude took the wheel herself. Of course she did. Ten years of running this river alone had made her crew of one, and the habits of solitude sat on her like ballast: check the bilge, mind the draft, trust nothing floating. She eased the throttle forward and felt the old engine answer with its familiar diesel cough, and the *Maren B* slid away from Graywater Bend without lights, without horn, without so much as a wake worth noticing.

Behind them the trading post shrank to a smear of yellow. Ahead the river unrolled black and blind, and the rain came down like the sky meant to finish what the war had started.

---

They made good time for three hours, which Jude spent learning to hate.

Not the river — the river she understood. It rose and fell and lied about its depth, but it lied honestly, the way weather lies. What she hated was the arithmetic. The pass through the high country opened once a year, when the glacier calved and shed its winter face, and the window was measured in days, not weeks. Miss it and the monsoon sealed the mountains until spring, and spring was a long time to sit in a flooded town with a chest full of dead men's signatures and a surveyor who knew her name.

She ran the numbers against the current, against the fuel, against the damage she couldn't see yet but could feel coming the way you feel a tooth go bad. Behind her, faint through the deck, she heard Elodie moving — the small sounds of someone making themselves useful in a space too small for usefulness. Once the hatch cracked and a thermos appeared at her elbow, coffee black enough to strip paint.

"You should sleep," Elodie said.

"I'll sleep when we're through the fence line."

"What fence line?"

"The one that isn't there." Jude took the coffee anyway. "Government says there's no checkpoint between here and the pass. Government also says Vaskaria never existed. You learn to navigate by what isn't there."

Elodie was quiet a moment. Rain drummed the wheelhouse roof like fingers on a desk. "My grandmother used to say that," she said finally. "That the truest maps are the ones with the blank spaces. Everything important is written in what they left out."

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have made a fine smuggler."

"She was a schoolteacher." A pause. "They shot her in the second winter."

Jude said nothing. There was nothing to say that the rain wasn't already saying, hammering down on both of them, on the boat, on the whole drowned country. She drank the coffee and watched the black water and drove north.

---

The fence line found them at 3:40 in the morning.

Later, Jude would reconstruct it precisely: the bend above the old ferry crossing, where the channel narrowed and the current should have pushed them toward the cut bank on the east side. She'd run this stretch a dozen times in flood season. She knew the water here the way she knew her own hands.

But floods move things. Floods are how the river keeps what it is given.

She felt it before she understood it — a shudder through the hull, low and wrong, like the boat clearing its throat. Then a grinding, a long tearing scrape along the starboard side, and the wheel went heavy in her hands as the *Maren B* slewed sideways into the current.

"Fence," she said aloud, to nobody, to herself. "Damn it all — fence."

War surplus chain-link, most likely, strung across the channel years ago to catch contraband or refugees or both, and forgotten when the money dried up. The flood had lifted it off the bottom and hung it just beneath the surface, invisible, patient, a net for anything that came through in the dark. The posts had gone downstream years ago; the wire itself stayed, anchored in the mud on both banks, humming under the hull like a struck string.

Jude killed the engine. Not because she wanted to — because the propeller had fouled, and pushing it would only wrap the wire tighter around the shaft. The *Maren B* swung broadside and began drifting, slow and terrible, down the channel with the rain coming down and the current taking its toll.

Below decks, something shifted. "What happened?" Elodie's voice, tight, awake now.

"We hit a fence that doesn't exist." Jude was already moving, pulling the lantern, grabbing the boathook. "Stay below. Don't come up unless I call."

"The chest—"

"Is bolted down and staying that way. Stay *below*, Vasseur."

She got the hatch open and the rain came in sideways, cold as a slap. The river was high and fast and full of debris — branches, planks, a dead sheep turning slowly in an eddy like a thought nobody wanted. She played the lantern along the starboard rail and saw the wire: caught under the rub rail, singing taut from the bow cleat all the way aft, silver-bright in the light, strong as the day it was strung.

Twenty minutes. That's how long it took her, waist-deep in the well with the rain trying to drown her where she knelt, working the boathook under the wire and levering it clear an inch at a time while the current fought her for every inch. Her hands went numb inside ten minutes. She kept going. This was the work she understood — weight and leverage and the stubbornness of metal — and for twenty minutes it was a mercy to understand nothing else.

The wire came free with a twang she felt in her teeth. The *Maren B* righted herself, wallowed, steadied.

And then Jude did the thing she'd been avoiding since the bend: she went below and looked at the bilge.

---

The lantern showed her everything she needed and none of what she wanted.

Water in the bilge — not much, not yet, maybe two inches standing over the limbers. But it was coming in. She traced it aft along the frames, hand over hand in the dark, until she found it: a seam on the starboard side, just above the turn of the bilge, where the wire had worried the caulking loose or the fence post — God knew where the posts were — had kissed the hull as they dragged over. A seam maybe eighteen inches long, weeping. Not gushing. Weeping.

A boat can weep for a long time. A boat can also stop weeping very suddenly, and start screaming instead.

"How bad?" Elodie stood at the foot of the ladder, hair plastered flat, holding the second lantern high. She hadn't stayed below. Jude decided not to waste breath being angry about it.

"Bad enough that I need to beach her tomorrow and recaulk. Good enough that we don't sink tonight." Jude wrung out her sleeve. "There's a drowned town up ahead. Meridian Flats. High ground on the north bank, old foundations. I can put us aground there in daylight and patch the seam."

"Meridian Flats." Elodie said the name the way other people said the names of churches.

"You know it?"

"My family lived forty miles from it. When the valley was flooded — during the relocation — some of the towns upstream were evacuated. Some weren't." Elodie's voice had gone very even, the way voices do when they're carrying more than they can hold. "Meridian Flats wasn't."

Jude looked at the weeping seam, then at the archivist's face in the lantern light, and understood that she was about to beach her boat — her home, her anonymity, her whole careful architecture of nowhere — on the doorstep of a massacre, in monsoon season, with a government surveyor somewhere behind them tracking a lead-lined chest.

The sensible thing was to turn around. Run downstream, sell the chest to whoever paid, take a new name, start over. She'd done it before. It was the one skill she'd perfected.

Instead she climbed back up to the wheelhouse, restarted the engine — the shaft ran true, thank God, the wire hadn't wrapped — and turned the *Maren B*'s nose upriver into the rain.

"Pump's under the aft bench," she called down. "Every hour, pump the bilge. I'll take first watch."

Above them the clouds had swallowed the moon entirely, and the river ran black and rising, and somewhere ahead lay a town that existed only in blank spaces on official maps. The rain kept falling like it had orders. Jude drove north through it, one hand on the wheel and one eye on the gauge that told her how fast her boat was dying, and behind her in the hold the chest rode its bolts in the dark, keeping its own counsel, weighing exactly what it had always weighed.

At dawn, gray and dripping, the drowned rooftops of Meridian Flats began to rise out of the floodwater ahead — chimneys first, like raised hands.

And behind them, far downstream, so faint it might have been thunder, an engine that wasn't theirs.
