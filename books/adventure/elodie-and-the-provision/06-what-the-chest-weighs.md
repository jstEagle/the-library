## 6. What the Chest Weighs

The rain came down like a verdict.

Elodie had been aboard the *Maggie June* for four days now, and in that time she had learned three things about Jude Blackwood: she drank her coffee black enough to stain a ledger, she slept with one boot on, and she did not talk about the chest. Not once. Not even obliquely. It sat in the hold beneath a tarp and two coils of spare line, lead-lined and padlocked, stamped with the seal of a country that no longer appeared on any map sold south of Graywater Bend, and Jude steered around the subject of it the way she steered around snags — without comment, without looking back, as if momentum alone could carry them past it forever.

But the monsoon had other plans.

They had tied up at a fueling dock outside Corben's Landing when the sky simply gave up pretending to be weather and became a wall. The rain hit the river so hard it bounced, a gray shroud thrown over everything past ten meters, and the pilot of a downstream barge shouted through cupped hands that the channel above was closed, washed out, no traffic until it lifted. So they waited. And waiting, Elodie had learned, was where Jude Blackwood's silence lived most comfortably.

She found Jude in the wheelhouse at midmorning, hands wrapped around a tin mug, watching water hammer the windows as though it might confess something if observed long enough.

"The hold's taking damp," Elodie said. "I went down to check my case."

"Your case is fine."

"It's fine because I moved it off the deck plates. Which are weeping at the forward seam, by the way. You'd know that if you went below."

Jude's jaw tightened. "I'll look at it when the rain lets up."

"You said that yesterday."

"I say a lot of things." She didn't smile when she said it, which somehow made it worse — or better; Elodie hadn't decided yet which way she wanted it to be.

Elodie stood in the doorway a moment longer, rain streaming off the eave behind her, and then she said, quietly, "You're going to have to open it eventually."

"No," Jude said, "I'm really not."

And that was all. That was the whole conversation. Elodie climbed down into the rain and stood on the dock with the water running off her hood and understood, with the particular clarity of someone who had spent eleven years among paper rather than people, that she was going to have to do this the hard way.

* * *

The lock was not difficult. That was the first insult of the day.

Elodie had expected — had perhaps hoped for — something worthy of a state secret: a boron-alloy hasp, a government pattern lock, something that would justify the story she told herself about why she needed two hours and a bent hairpin from her repair kit. Instead it was a commercial padlock, river-grade, the kind sold in trading posts to fishermen who wanted to keep their outboard fuel honest. Her grandfather had kept an identical one on his tool shed in Vasseur Hollow. She had picked it at nine years old to get at his good fishing knife, and he had caught her, and instead of scolding her he had shown her how to feel the tumblers with her fingertips, saying, *A lock only keeps out people who believe in locks.*

She thought about him the whole time she worked. The rain drummed the deck above like fingers on a desk, impatient, insistent. The hold smelled of bilgewater and diesel and old rope, and the single porthole let in light the color of dishwater. The chest sat where it had sat since Graywater Bend, patient as furniture, and Elodie knelt in front of it with her sleeves pushed up and her heart going like a trapped bird.

The third tumbler gave at 11:40 by her watch. The shackle popped with a sound no louder than a knuckle cracking, and then she was sitting in the dim with an open lock in her hand and absolutely no idea what to do next.

Because here was the thing she had not put in any of her reports to the network, the thing she had barely admitted to herself: opening the chest would change nothing about whether the documents were real. They were real. She had seen the photograph. She had spent half her life assembling fragments of Vaskaria from other people's attics — a ration card here, a school register there, a wedding portrait with the faces scratched out by some clerk's careful pen. She knew what the archive was. What she didn't know was what Jude Blackwood was, and whether the woman upstairs would help carry it or burn it or hand it over to the first man with a government badge and a fair offer.

So Elodie did the thing that felt least like a choice and most like gravity. She lifted the lid.

* * *

The lead lining made the chest heavier than its size suggested, and colder, and when she folded back the oilcloth inside, the smell rose up to meet her: paper, old ink, camphor, and underneath it something faintly chemical — fixative, she realized, the preservative solution archivists used, meaning someone had packed these documents intending them to survive. Intending them to last until they were needed.

Manifests. Dozens of them, typed on the heavy stock the Provisional Authority reserved for official transport, each one listing names — columns and columns of names — with destinations. Elodie knew those destination codes. Every descendant of Vaskaria knew them, the way Jewish families once knew the numbers of trains. *Transit Point 7. Transit Point 3. Processing — East.* And beside each name, in a second column, a disposition stamp. Most of them read the same word.

*Resolved.*

Two thousand, four hundred and eleven names across the manifests she counted before her hands started shaking too badly to count further. Resolved. Resolved. Resolved.

Beneath the manifests, photographs. She made herself look. A courtyard, wet cobblestones, men kneeling with their hands laced behind their heads. Soldiers standing over them with the relaxed posture of men waiting for a bus. Another: a building she recognized — the records annex in Vaskaria's capital, the place where her mother had worked, where the national archive had been housed — photographed mid-burn, flames coming out of the second-floor windows while a queue of trucks waited in the street to carry away whatever the fire missed. A third photograph showed a row of officers on a reviewing stand, and Elodie's breath stopped in her throat, because she knew those faces. Not from history books. From newspapers. From the current edition. Three of those men held office now. One of them had cut a ribbon at a reservoir dedication two years ago, smiling, and the photograph of his smile had run in every paper from the coast to the border, and here he was twenty years younger with his hand on the shoulder of a subordinate and a burning archive reflected in the glass of the window behind him.

And beneath the photographs, orders. Signed orders. Counter-signed, initialed, stamped with the dry authority of a state doing its bookkeeping. *Authorize relocation of archival personnel per Directive 44. Disposition of non-compliant elements at discretion of regional commander.* Signatures she had spent years trying to prove belonged to the men who now governed the provinces where Vaskaria used to be. Here they were, in their own hands, in their own ink, dated within days of each other, telling the whole story in the flat bureaucratic language of atrocity: who gave the order, who received it, who confirmed completion.

*Completion confirmed. Annex site cleared. No further action required.*

No further action required. Twenty years later, Elodie was the further action. She was what the paperwork had never accounted for — the daughter of a burned country, grown now, trained now, sitting in the belly of a stranger's boat with the proof of everything in her lap.

She sat there a long time. The rain kept on. At some point she realized she was crying, silently, the way you learn to cry in archives, and she wiped her face on her sleeve and began, methodically, to rewrap the oilcloth, because whatever happened next, the documents had survived fire and flood and twenty years of deliberate forgetting, and they were not going to suffer damp on her watch.

Then she heard boots on the ladder, and Jude Blackwood dropped into the hold.

* * *

Later, Elodie would try to reconstruct exactly how it happened — who spoke first, whose fault it technically was — and she would fail, because the truth was that it happened the way weather happens. Jude came down the ladder fast, saying something about the seam, and stopped dead at the sight of the open chest, and for three full seconds neither of them breathed.

"You picked my lock," Jude said.

"Yes."

"That's my boat."

"Yes," Elodie said again, and did not apologize, because she found, examining herself honestly, that she wasn't sorry. "And that is my family in your hold. My mother's colleagues. My grandmother's neighbors. Two thousand four hundred and eleven people I've counted so far, and there are more boxes under the manifests." She lifted a photograph — the burning annex — and held it up into the porthole light. "Do you know this building?"

Jude looked. Elodie watched her look, and saw the exact moment the image landed — a small contraction around the eyes, a stillness that wasn't calm.

"No," Jude said.

"It's the National Records Annex. Burned in the third week of the occupation. Everything your government's maps say never existed was in that building, and this is what happened to it, and the man who ordered it—" she tapped the reviewing-stand photograph, the young officer with his hand on the subordinate's shoulder, "—is currently Deputy Minister of Interior. He signs flood-relief appropriations now. Your taxes may have paid his travel expenses."

"Stop," Jude said.

"Or what? You'll throw me off in monsoon season?"

For a moment Elodie genuinely did not know what Jude would do. The captain stood with the ladder at her back and the whole weight of the boat between her and the river, and her face did something complicated — anger and fear and something else moving underneath both, something that looked almost like recognition, like a woman reading a document she'd been shown once before and had chosen to forget.

Then, instead of ordering Elodie out, Jude crouched. She took the top manifest off the stack — Elodie let her, watching her hands — and read it. All of it. The names, the codes, the stamps. Then she set it down, took the next one, and read that too.

She read for forty minutes. Elodie timed her, because timing things was what she did when she was afraid. Forty minutes, kneeling on a wet deck plate in a leaking hold, while the rain hammered the hull like a crowd demanding entry. Jude read the way Elodie imagined she did everything — completely, without performance, without a single wasted motion — and when she reached the orders, the signed ones, she read each signature twice. Elodie saw her lips move on the second pass. Sounding them out. Making sure.

Then Jude put everything back in the chest exactly as she'd found it, closed the lid without locking it, and sat down on the deck plate across from Elodie with her forearms on her knees, and said nothing.

That was the worst part. Elodie had prepared for shouting. She had prepared for accusations, for being put ashore at Corben's Landing the moment the rain lifted, for the cold arithmetic of a captain protecting her license and her neck. She had not prepared for silence — for this woman who talked like every word cost money sitting there spending nothing at all, looking at the middle distance where the chest was, breathing.

"Say something," Elodie finally said.

"Why?" Jude said. "You already know what's in it. You've known for years. I'm the one finding out."

"And?"

"And nothing." Jude rubbed a hand over her face. When it came down, she looked older. "My father ran cargo on this river during the war. Before they erased it. He used to take loads up to the transit points — sealed crates, armed escort, no questions written into the contract itself. He never told me what was in them. He died before the erasure finished, so maybe he never knew either, or maybe—" She stopped. Started again. "Maybe he knew, and that's why he drank, and that's why I don't."

The rain filled the pause like a third person in the room.

"I signed on with the river because it doesn't ask," Jude said. "Miles don't ask questions. Cargo doesn't ask questions. You haul what's written on the manifest and you deliver it and nobody ever has to know anything, including you. That was the arrangement. That was the whole religion." She looked at the chest. "Twenty years I've been invisible on this water, and it turns out the invisibility was issued to me. Somebody arranged it. Somebody decided the captain who asks no questions gets left alone, and I built my entire life inside that decision without ever once wondering who made it or what it was covering for." Her laugh had no humor in it at all. "I'm not a free woman, Elodie. I'm a well-maintained blind spot."

Elodie said, carefully, "Now you know what the blind spot was hiding."

"Yeah." Jude nodded slowly. "Now I know."

She stood, then, and Elodie braced for the order to leave, for the lock to be replaced, for the lid to close on the twentieth year of the forgetting the way it had closed on all the others. Instead Jude crossed to the forward bulkhead, pulled a canvas tool roll from its clips, and knelt at the weeping seam.

"This'll take till dark," she said. "Rain's supposed to hold another day at least. Maybe two." She unrolled the tools without looking back. "If you're going to sit down here cataloguing your dead, do it quiet. I can't hear myself think over the crying."

It was, Elodie understood, the closest thing to permission that Jude Blackwood's religion allowed. She wiped her face again, opened her field notebook to a fresh page, and began to number the manifests.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon in a silence that gradually changed texture — from hostile to merely shared, and then, somewhere near dusk, to something Elodie had no clean word for. Jude caulked the seam. Elodie documented. Twice, without discussion, Jude held the lantern closer so Elodie could read a faded stamp, and once Elodie passed up a photograph without being asked, and Jude took it and looked at it longer than she needed to and handed it back without comment, and that was its own kind of conversation.

Near evening, Jude broke the silence exactly once.

"The signatures," she said, not turning from the seam. "The ones still in office. How many sets of eyes have seen these?"

"In the original? These are copies — certified copies, the network made them before the originals went into deep storage. Four people alive know where the originals are. Fewer than thirty have seen even these copies. After tonight, thirty-one."

Jude was quiet a moment. "And the plan is what? You carry them over the pass to the presses?"

"If the pass opens. If we're not caught. If the boat holds together." Elodie closed her notebook. "The plan is a series of miracles, Captain. It usually is."

Jude set down her caulking iron and finally turned around. In the lantern light her face was unreadable, but her voice, when it came, was stripped of everything except the question itself:

"And if I put you and the chest ashore tomorrow and pretend none of this ever touched me — what happens to me?"

Elodie met her eyes. "Nothing, probably. For a while. You go back to hauling cargo no one asks about, and someday a surveyor shows up asking what happened to the chest you dredged up, and you discover your invisibility was always a loan, not a gift. Or—" she shrugged, "—maybe they let you keep hiding. Blind spots are useful. But you'll know. Every mile, every sealed crate, every quiet contract — you'll know what the quiet is for. That's the price of having read it twice. There isn't a river far enough."

Above them, the rain intensified, a sudden furious barrage, and the hull groaned, and somewhere aft the lamp swung on its hook and threw their shadows huge against the cargo netting — two women and a chest, adrift inside a drowned world.

Jude looked at her for a long moment. Then she picked up the caulking iron and went back to work, and Elodie understood that the answer would not come tonight, and possibly not tomorrow, and that it would come, eventually, the way rivers arrive at the sea — not by decision but by slope.

She was wrong about the timeline by less than a day.

Because near midnight, with the rain still falling and the lamp trimmed low, the engine-room telegraph clanged — Jude, awake in her bunk, signaling from habit — and Elodie heard, over the water, thin and wrong and getting less thin, the sound the hull makes when a bad seam stops being a nuisance.

The *Maggie June* was dying beneath them, and the nearest bank was the drowned town of Meridian Flats.
