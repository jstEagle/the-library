## 7. The Leaking Hold

The bilge pump woke Jude at half past three in the morning, running longer than it should.

She knew her boat the way other people knew their own breathing — every rattle, every rhythm, every honest complaint. The pump was supposed to cycle: thirty seconds of labor, a pause, silence while the water found its level again. Instead it had been grinding for two full minutes when she surfaced from sleep in the wheelhouse bunk, and by the time she swung her legs down onto the deck plates she could feel it through her bare feet — a wrongness in the way the hull sat, a list to port that hadn't been there when she'd anchored above the fence line at dusk.

Rain hammered the wheelhouse glass. The river beyond was black on black, no shore visible, no bank, just the sound of water moving with intent.

"Elodie." She didn't raise her voice; there was no need. "Get up."

A rustle from below, then Elodie's head appeared through the companionway, hair loose, eyes already sharp. She'd learned fast how a boat spoke. "How bad?"

"Pump's losing." Jude was already moving, dropping into the engine bay, throwing the switch on the second pump. It coughed, caught, added its voice to the first. She knelt and pulled the inspection plate over the forward bilge, and the smell that came up told her everything before the light did — river water, cold and mineral, and under it the faint sweet rot of wood that had been underwater for twenty years and remembered how to drink.

The seam had opened along the port chine, aft of where they'd hit the submerged fence line six days ago. She'd known it was bad. She'd caulked it twice, driven oakum into the gap with a mallet by lantern light while Elodie held the lamp steady and asked questions Jude hadn't wanted to answer. But the monsoon river wasn't like the low-season water she'd run for a decade. It carried whole trees. It carried fences. It carried, apparently, the patience of something that had been waiting for her since Graywater Bend.

Water was coming through in a thin, steady sheet, not a gush — a seam weeping rather than split — but the pumps were falling behind by inches per hour, and the rain outside showed no intention of stopping, and upstream the pass would close in nine days whether or not her hull agreed to carry them.

"We beach her," Jude said. "Now."

"In this?"

"In anything. A boat that sinks in deep water takes everything with it." She looked up, met Elodie's eyes, and didn't say *including the chest*, because they both knew it, the way they now knew most things without saying them. Six days below decks with a lock pick and nothing but time had changed the shape of what lay between them. The chest sat in the hold like a third passenger. They did not discuss it anymore. They simply arranged their lives around it, the way you arrange furniture in a house you both intend to keep.

Jude went forward into the rain.

* * *

Beaching a loaded riverboat alone is a controlled disaster. Beaching one with an archivist who has never handled a line in her life is a negotiation with chaos, and Jude ran it the only way she knew: by shouting numbers and trusting the other person to be worth the trust.

"Forward line — take the coil, don't wrap it around your hand, never wrap it—" Elodie had the coil, white-knuckled, rain streaming off her chin. "When I say, throw it as far toward the trees as you can. Not at me. At the trees."

The drowned town announced itself before Jude chose it. That was the truth of it — she'd been aiming for the high gravel bar marked on her chart four miles back, but the river had opinions, and the current took the stern and swung them sideways across a flooded field, and out of the rain came shapes that stopped her breath for a moment even now, even after everything.

Rooftops. Just the ridgelines of rooftops breaking the brown water, a dozen of them, maybe more, shingled and sagging, some with chimneys still standing like sentries over houses that were entirely beneath the surface. And above the waterline, where the ground rose toward what had once been a street, the upper stories of buildings stood open-eyed and empty, windows gone dark or reflecting the lightning in long silver flashes.

Meridian Flats.

Every river pilot knew the name the way sailors know a reef. During the war the government had ordered the levee cut — officially a flood-control measure, unofficially reprisal, though no document had ever said so, though no document needed to. Four hundred people had been given forty-eight hours to leave. Some hadn't believed it. Some had nowhere to go. The river had come through in a night, and afterward the maps had been redrawn and the town removed from them, and the government had discovered that drowning a place also drowns the argument about it.

Jude had passed Meridian Flats a hundred times in ten years. She'd never once stopped. You didn't stop at places like this; stopping meant looking, and looking meant knowing, and knowing was the thing she'd built her whole life to avoid.

But the river was making the decision tonight. The stern swung, the bow answered, and she drove the boat up onto the old main street — because it *was* a street, she realized as the keel kissed bottom with a shudder that traveled the whole hull, it was the paved high street of Meridian Flats, and her boat was aground in front of what had been a general store, its false front still standing, its painted letters ghosted but legible under the wash of her spotlight:

**V. OKONKWO & SONS — DRY GOODS**

"Line!" she shouted, and Elodie threw it true, first try, into the drowned orchard beyond the store, and Jude leapt into waist-deep water with the end of it and waded ashore and made it fast around the trunk of a fig tree that had spent twenty years learning to live with wet feet.

Then she stood in the rain, chest heaving, and looked at what she'd done. Her boat — her home, her hiding place, her ten years of miles made solid — sat tilted on the bed of a murdered town, lit up like an accusation.

Behind her, Elodie said nothing at all. When Jude turned, she saw the archivist standing shin-deep in the flooded street, staring at the store front, and her face had gone somewhere else entirely.

"I know this name," Elodie said.

* * *

They got the chest up on deck before they did anything else — hauled it out of the rising water in the hold, wrestled it onto the cabin roof, lashed it down under a tarp. Jude worked in silence, efficient, grim, and watched Elodie work beside her with the same grim economy, and understood that neither of them was going to say anything about the rooftops until the essential things were safe. That was another thing they'd learned about each other: grief could wait its turn, but it did not forget its place in line.

By gray dawn the rain had thinned to a steady drizzle. The pumps, running on the last of the battery, were holding their own against the leak in the beached hull — barely. The boat wasn't going anywhere. Neither, Jude thought, looking at the waterlogged street receding into morning mist, was anything else.

Elodie stood at the rail with her hands wrapped around it, looking down the drowned avenue. In the better light Jude could see more of it now: the upper floors of a row of houses along the north side, doors and window frames intact above the waterline, paint peeling in long curls. A church steeple, leaning. A water tower with the town's name still stenciled on it, the letters bleeding rust down the steel like old wounds reopened.

"The Vasseur archive wasn't all in the capital," Elodie said quietly. She wasn't talking to Jude, exactly. She was talking to the town. "My grandmother wrote about this place. There was a schoolteacher here who kept copies of everything — parish records, land titles, photographs. When the evacuation order came, she packed them in oilcloth and put them in the attic of the schoolhouse. She thought the water wouldn't reach the attics." A pause. "It reached the attics."

"You've read her letters."

"I've read everything my family kept. Everything the network recovered." Elodie turned, and her eyes were red-rimmed but dry, which somehow was worse than tears. "I grew up on the names of towns like this one. I could draw you the map of Vaskaria from memory — every village, every ferry crossing — and I have never stood in a single one until now. Do you understand? Twenty years of names, and this is the first one that's *real*."

Jude said nothing. What was there to say that the street itself wasn't already saying?

Elodie climbed down over the rail, waded to the nearest house, and stood before its door. The door was swollen shut, blue paint blistered, but above it, screwed into the frame, a small enamel plate had survived the decades: **HALLORAN**, in flaking white letters.

"Halloran," Elodie repeated. She touched the plate with two fingers, gently, the way you touch a sleeping child. "There were Hallorans in the manifests. In the chest. The deportation lists — the ones dated after the flood, after everyone was supposed to be gone." Her voice stayed level. Her hands didn't. "Three Hallorans on a transport manifest dated eleven months after the levee was cut. Eleven months, Jude. Someone was still living here. Hiding here. In the attics, maybe. And the state found them, and put them on a barge, and the barge's destination is stamped *resettlement east*, and we both know what resettlement east means, because I've read the follow-up telegrams."

Jude stood in the doorway of the next house over. Its plate read **MERCER**. Beyond it, **ADEYEMI**. Beyond that, a corner house with a plate so weathered she had to lean close: **VASSEUR**.

She went very still.

"Elodie."

"I see it."

"No. *Elodie.*"

The archivist crossed the flooded street in four strides and stopped beside her, and together they looked at the name on the doorpost of the drowned house, and the rain came down soft on both of them, and neither moved for a long time.

"My grandmother's sister," Elodie said finally, barely above the rain. "Anselm Vasseur. He refused the evacuation order. He said the town was his parish and he would not abandon his records, and my grandmother never forgave him for dying for paper, and she spent her whole life collecting paper anyway, so I suppose neither of them ever really won that argument." She laughed — a small, broken sound with no humor in it. "He lived here. This was his door. And I'm standing in front of it because your boat sank on his street."

"The river decides where things stop," Jude said. It came out rougher than she intended. "That's the first thing you learn piloting. You don't choose your groundings. You just choose what you do after."

"And what do you do after, Captain Blackwood?" Elodie's voice cracked on the name — her real name, the one the surveyor had used like a knife in Graywater Bend, the one Elodie had only spoken since with care, almost tenderness, as if returning something stolen. "Ten years of passing this town. Ten years of not stopping. And now the river has stopped you here, in front of these doors, and you can't pretend you don't know what happened, because I can *show* you what happened, it's sitting on your cabin roof under a tarp—"

"I know what happened," Jude said.

The words came out of her flat and final, and they surprised them both.

She looked down the drowned avenue — at the rooftops, at the leaning steeple, at the water tower bleeding its name into the rain — and something in her chest that had been sealed as long as the chest itself gave a quarter-turn, like a lock picking itself.

"My father crewed the relief barges," she said. "After the flood. Officially relief. He told me once — once, when he was drunk, when I was nineteen — that the relief barges ran light. That they went downriver full of people and came back empty, and the paperwork said supplies. He never said it again. I never asked again. I took this river instead, because on the river nobody asks anything, and I told myself that was freedom." She shook her head slowly. "It wasn't freedom. It was just a longer way of not arriving."

Elodie reached out and took her hand. Her fingers were cold from the rain and they held on hard.

"He tried to tell you," she said. "And you heard him. It just took you twenty years to answer."

* * *

They spent the day doing the work that keeps grief at arm's length: pumping, caulking, hauling gear to the high ground of the old churchyard, where the ground rose enough to make a dry camp among leaning headstones whose inscriptions the rain had long since taken. Jude drove oakum into the opened seam by feel, lying on her side in three inches of bilgewater, and Elodie kept the lamp and handed tools and talked — steadily, endlessly, telling Jude the history of Meridian Flats from the archive's pages, the schoolteacher's name, the ferryman who'd run the last crossings, the boy who'd reportedly known every side channel of the swamp the town became.

Toward evening the rain stopped altogether, and the sky over the drowned valley broke into bands of copper and violet, and the river lay smooth as poured metal between the rooftops.

That was when they saw the skiff.

It came down the flooded avenue from the north, poling quietly through the shallows where the street dipped — a flat-bottomed local craft, patched and ancient, poled by a figure too small to be an adult. Behind him, in the bow, sat a woman in a nun's habit with a lantern unlit in her lap, and the boy brought the skiff alongside Jude's grounded hull with the ease of someone who had been navigating these drowned streets since he could walk.

He couldn't have been more than fourteen. He looked up at Jude on her tilting deck, then at Elodie, then at the tarp-covered shape of the chest, and his face held none of a child's blankness. It held arithmetic.

"You're aground on the Okonkwo store," he said. "Grandmother Okonkwo says nobody grounds there unless the river wants them. So." He shrugged, matter-of-fact. "What does the river want?"

The nun laid her hand on the boy's shoulder and looked up at Jude with an expression of terrible gentleness, and said, "We saw your lights. We've been waiting twenty years for lights on this street."

Jude looked at Elodie. Elodie looked back, and in her face was the question that had been building since the Halloran door, since the Vasseur door, since the chest had come up dripping out of the sandbar — the question that was no longer *what do we do* but *who are we going to be*.

Downstream, faint under the evening calm, came the sound Jude had been dreading all day and hoping she'd imagined: engines. More than one. Running against the current, slow and searching, sweeping the banks with searchlights that strobed through the dusk like lightning that had learned to lie.

The boy heard it too. His head came up like a bird dog's.

"That'll be the patrol," he said. "You'd best come with us. They don't know the channels." A pause, and then, with the flat certainty of someone quoting scripture: "We do."
