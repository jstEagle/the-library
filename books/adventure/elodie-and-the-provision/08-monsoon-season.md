## 8. Monsoon Season

**JUDE**

The rain came on the third day and did not stop.

It arrived first as a rumor in the hills — a gray smudge where the ridgelines should have been — then as a sound like a freight train that never passed. By dusk the river had risen two feet over the sandbar where they'd beached the *Maggie June*, and by midnight it was lapping at the pilings of Meridian Flats' drowned main street, touching the doorposts of houses that had been underwater for twenty years.

Jude stood under the lean-to they'd rigged from a tarpaulin and two oars and watched the water climb. Beside her, Elodie held a lantern against her chest like something she was protecting.

"We're not going anywhere for a while," Jude said.

"How long?"

"Monsoon runs six weeks if it's mean. Eight if it's spiteful."

Elodie looked out at the drowned town, at the rooftops breaking the current downstream like the backs of sleeping animals. "And we just stay here."

"We repair the hull. We wait it out." Jude turned back toward the boat, which sat heeled over on the bar with its port seam open like a wound. "There's nothing upriver but high water and worse. Nothing downriver but the people looking for us."

The lantern light caught Elodie's face, and Jude saw something she hadn't expected: not fear. Relief. As if being pinned in place was the first honest thing the world had offered either of them in weeks.

* * *

**ELODIE**

They repaired the hull badly.

That was the phrase Jude used, every evening, standing back from the day's work with her arms crossed and her jaw set, surveying the caulking like a judge reviewing a bad verdict. *Badly.* The oakum went in wet because there was no dry place to keep it. The pitch wouldn't set in the humidity. They hammered seams in the rain with the tarp pulled down over their heads until the space beneath smelled of tar and two bodies who had stopped pretending they didn't notice each other.

"You're driving it too shallow," Jude said on the ninth day, taking the mallet from Elodie's hand without asking.

"I'm driving it exactly how you showed me."

"I showed you wrong, then. Deeper. You want the fiber to bite, not sit there like a guest."

"Everything with you is biting," Elodie said, and took the mallet back.

Jude stared at her. Rain drummed the tarp. Somewhere out in the flooded street, a roof timber groaned and settled, the town rearranging itself around their presence like a body turning in sleep.

"That's fair," Jude said finally, and it was such an unexpected concession that Elodie laughed — a short, startled sound she hadn't made since Graywater Bend. Jude looked away quickly, but not before Elodie saw the corner of her mouth move.

They argued about everything after that. About whether to caulk the garboard seam fore-to-aft or aft-to-fore. About whether the patrol skiff they'd heard on the second night had been searching or merely passing. About whether Vaskaria had been betrayed from outside or sold from within — this last one Elodie raised deliberately, watching Jude's hands go still on the caulking iron, watching her choose not to answer, and filing the silence away the way she filed everything: carefully, in order, against a future need.

But between the arguments, in the long wet hours when there was nothing left to hammer, they began telling each other true things.

Not all at once. In pieces, the way cargo comes off a boat — one crate at a time, each heavier than it looked.

* * *

**JUDE**

"My father was a lockkeeper," Jude said, on a night so dark the lantern seemed to make a hole in the world rather than light in it. "Third generation. He kept the gates at Corben's Reach, forty years. I was supposed to take them after him."

"What happened?"

"The war happened. The Provision happened." She said the word flatly, without ceremony, though she knew what it meant to Elodie — the emergency act, the legal instrument that had dissolved a nation into an administrative district and its people into paperwork. "I was nineteen. The army requisitioned the locks to move munitions upriver. My father refused to open the gates for them. Not politics — he didn't have politics. He just said the reach was his, and the army could build its own river."

Elodie had gone very still. "What did they do?"

"They made an example. That was the whole doctrine of those years — examples. It's cheaper than justice and travels further." Jude turned her cup in her hands. "I changed my name the following spring. Took my mother's mother's name. Blackwood. Nobody on this river ever asked me about the one before it, because nobody on this river asks anybody anything. That's why I came here. A river full of people who don't ask."

"And now someone's asking."

"Now someone's asking," Jude agreed. She looked at Elodie across the lantern. "Your turn. You've had three weeks of my silences. Pay some of yours."

* * *

**ELODIE**

So Elodie told her about the archive — not the chest, not yet, but the archive itself. How her grandmother had been the Keeper of Records for the Vaskarian provincial library, and how in the last week before the occupation she'd sealed the collection into lead-lined cases and shipped them downriver on a grain barge, scattered among eleven hundred sacks of wheat, addressed to no one.

"She knew the wheat would be confiscated. That was the point. Confiscated goods go into government warehouses, and government warehouses don't burn things they've already counted. She was betting on bureaucracy to preserve what soldiers would have burned."

"It worked for twenty years."

"It worked until the flood." Elodie wrapped her arms around her knees. "The warehouse at Kessel's Landing went under in the spring floods. Everything loose washed out. Most of it's gone — rotted, sunk, scattered along sixty miles of riverbed. And then your dredge pulls up the one case that matters most, and I find you at Graywater Bend by pure luck, and I've been telling myself for weeks that it *was* luck."

Jude's eyes narrowed slightly. "What else would it be?"

"Nothing," Elodie said. "Luck. It was luck."

She felt the lie go out of her mouth and settle into the space between them like silt. Jude studied her a moment longer, then nodded and let it lie — and that, more than anything, made Elodie ashamed. Trust given freely was harder to carry than trust demanded.

Outside, the rain kept falling. It had become the whole world: a sound without edges, a gray country with no borders, and inside it a boat, a tarp, a lantern, and two women learning the shape of each other's silences.

* * *

**JUDE**

On the nineteenth day, the rain thinned to a drizzle and Jude climbed the bluff behind the town to look downstream.

Meridian Flats revealed itself in the clearing light the way a body reveals itself in stages of undressing — reluctantly, and more damaged than remembered. From above she could see the whole grid of streets under the brown water, the church steeple leaning, the ferry landing with its pilings like the ribs of something that had once breathed. Elodie had spent an afternoon wading the flooded lane with a notebook, copying names off the doorposts of houses that stood chest-deep in the river. *Vasseur. Ostrander. Mireles. Brandt.* Half the names of a nation, nailed to wood, drowning annually.

Jude understood then, in a way she hadn't at Graywater Bend, that Elodie wasn't salvaging documents. She was salvaging an address. A place where her people had lived, that the maps had been ordered to forget.

She was still standing there when she saw it: a patrol skiff, coming upriver slow, its engine pitched low against the current. Military gray, canvas canopy, a searchlight rig even in daylight. It was a mile off and closing, and there was nowhere for Jude to go except down the bluff and into the mud, and nowhere for the *Maggie June* to hide except behind a drowned church.

She got down the hill fast enough to tear her sleeve and skin her palm, burst through the tarp flap, and found Elodie asleep in the bow with the ledger open on her chest.

"Skiff," Jude said. "Downstream. Coming up."

Elodie was awake instantly, the way soldiers wake — no transition, just present. "Here?"

"Ten minutes. Maybe less. They'll see the boat."

"Can we push off? Get into the side channel—"

"With a half-caulked seam and the river at flood stage?" Jude was already moving, throwing the tarp over the deck cargo, kicking mud over the fresh pitch work to dull its shine. "We'd open her like a tin of sardines. We stay. We're salvagers — a captain and her hired archivist pulling goods out of a drowned town. It's even mostly true."

"Mostly."

"Best kind of lie. The kind with a spine of truth in it." She paused, then said it, because it needed saying before the skiff arrived and it became a performance instead of a choice: "If they board, give them nothing. Not your name, not your business, not your face. Look bored. Boredom is invisible."

"And if they ask for you?"

Jude's hands went still on the tarp line. "Then we find out what name they ask for."

* * *

**ELODIE**

The skiff came up the drowned main street at half throttle, gunwales awash with spray, and Elodie stood on the sagging porch of the old customs house with her notebook in her hand and mud to her knees, and performed boredom like her life depended on it, because it did.

Two men in the boat. One young, at the wheel, chewing something with the aggressive indifference of the very bored himself. One older, in a civilian coat over a military collar, with the particular posture of a man who had been sent rather than assigned — a courier of other people's questions.

The skiff swung alongside the *Maggie June*. The older man looked at the boat for a long moment, at the mud-smeared seams, at the tarp, at the dredging winch with its cable still trailing into the floodwater.

"Salvage work?" he called up.

"Drowned town's full of brass fittings," Jude answered from the deck, wiping her hands on her thighs. "River gives, we take. You want something?"

"Looking for a boat went up past Graywater Bend three weeks back. River steamer, working hull." He consulted a folded paper without much interest. "Captain named Blackwood."

Elodie kept her eyes on her notebook. She wrote a name off the nearest doorpost — *Ostrander* — and did not look up, and her heart was a fist inside her ribs, because she had never heard that surname spoken aloud before, and she understood in one cold instant that Jude had given her a false name at Graywater Bend, or a true one, and she did not know which, and that either way the woman ten feet from her had layers she had not begun to map.

"Blackwood," Jude repeated, tasting it. "No. Nobody by that on this stretch. Few dredgers below the bend, couple of log rafts. What'd this Blackwood do?"

"Nothing." The man folded the paper away. "Just a question we're asking everyone."

"Everyone asks nothing," Jude said, "and finds nothing. Good luck upstream. Water's high and getting higher."

The man looked at her a moment too long — the way a man looks when he is memorizing rather than seeing — then touched his cap brim and the skiff pulled away, up the drowned street, around the leaning steeple, gone into the rain-haze.

Elodie waited until the engine note faded entirely before she spoke.

"Blackwood," she said. "Is that your name?"

Jude crouched at the deck edge, watching the wake dissolve against the pilings. For a while she said nothing. Then:

"It's the name I gave the world twenty years ago. Whether it's mine—" She shrugged. "Depends who's asking. Today somebody was asking. Which means somewhere there's a list with it written down, and lists travel faster than boats."

"You told me at Graywater Bend you were nobody."

"I told you I was a river captain. Both things were true." She looked up at Elodie then, and her face in the gray light was stripped of everything performative, down to something raw and level. "You've got silences of your own, Vasseur. I've watched you carry them for three weeks like ballast. So we'll call it even — for now. But when this monsoon breaks, and we run for the pass, I need to know what's in your hold as well as mine. Because that man memorized my face today, and next time he won't send a skiff."

Elodie hugged the notebook to her chest, feeling the weight of everything she hadn't said pressing outward against her ribs.

"Ask me," she said quietly. "Anything. Tonight. I'll answer."

Jude held her gaze a long moment, rain dripping off the tarp edge between them, marking time.

"Not tonight," Jude said. "Tonight we finish the starboard seam. Tomorrow, if we're still alive, you can start telling me true things."

She turned back to the hull. And Elodie stood on the drowned town's porch, in the smell of tar and rain, understanding that the clock had started — not the monsoon's clock, not the patrol's, but a smaller, quieter one, ticking inside her own chest, counting down the hours until she either gave Jude Blackwood the truth or lost the only person on this river who had ever stopped asking questions long enough to listen.
