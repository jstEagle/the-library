## 9. The Man from the Survey Office

He came up the levee path at mid-morning, when the rain had thinned to a gray drizzle and the river ran the color of tarnished pewter. Jude saw him first from the porch of the drowned church they'd been using as a workshop — a figure in a dry, expensive coat, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man who had never once had to wonder whether he'd be welcome where he was going. Everyone else in Meridian Flats moved like the water might come back for them. This man moved like the water worked for him.

She knew the walk before she knew the face.

Ten years was a long time, but some things the body keeps the way a river keeps its channels. She had last seen that walk crossing the tarmac of a government airfield, a clipboard under one arm, and she had been a different woman then — a woman with a name that fit her, a commission, a future measured in promotions instead of river miles. She had watched that walk recede from the window of a transport plane and told herself she'd never see it again.

The river, she was learning, kept what it was given. Apparently it gave things back, too.

"Jude." He stopped at the bottom of the church steps, rain beading on the shoulders of his coat, and smiled the way a man smiles at a ledger that has finally balanced. "Or should I say — no. Jude is fine. Jude suits you."

Behind her, inside the church, she heard Elodie stop moving. The scratch of the caulking iron went silent. Rain dripped through the hole in the roof where the steeple had been, ticking against the upturned hull of the *Lark* like a clock counting down something.

"You've got the wrong porch," Jude said.

"I don't think I've ever had a wrong porch in my life." He climbed two steps, no further, and stood with his hands clasped behind his back — a posture she remembered, a posture from briefing rooms, from the survey office with its maps of places that were about to stop existing. "Captain Blackwood. Formerly Lieutenant Blackwood, Hydrographic Corps. Formerly the youngest woman ever seconded to the Provisional Boundary Commission. Formerly a great many things." He tilted his head. "You look well. The river's been good to you."

"The river doesn't know me."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "That was the point."

* * *

His name was Aldous Vane. She had never known his first name back then — he had been *the surveyor*, *the man from the office*, a voice on a radio frequency that only existed for eleven minutes a day. Now he said it himself, offering it like a card, and she understood that the giving of the name was the opening move of whatever game this was. *I'll show you mine.*

They sat in the church because he suggested it and because refusing would have told him more than accepting. Elodie stayed. Jude didn't ask her to and Vane didn't object, and something in the way he glanced at Elodie — brief, cataloguing, the way he'd once glanced at coastline — told Jude that he had known about her for some time. Probably before Elodie had known about herself.

He produced a thermos of coffee, actual coffee, and three tin cups, and poured with the ceremony of a man performing an old habit. The smell filled the drowned church like an intruder.

"Do you know how we found the chest?" he asked.

"I assume you dredge too."

"We do. We dredge everything. Every sandbar from the delta to the high water, twice a year, on contract. You'd be astonished what a nation leaves behind when it's erased properly. Buttons. Dentures. A piano, once, forty miles from any house." He sipped. "But we didn't find your chest. *You* found your chest. We found the photograph."

Jude kept her face still. Somewhere behind her eyes, she saw it: Tam, her deckhand, crouched in the mud with his phone raised, that quick furtive flash. She had wanted to throw the chest back. Tam had wanted to keep it. She'd told herself the boy was just greedy.

"Deckhands talk," Vane said, reading her silence correctly, as he had always read her silence correctly. "Deckhands drink. Deckhands sell photographs to a man in Graywater Bend who sells them to three other men, one of whom works, in a very small way, for us." He set down his cup. "The seal was the problem, of course. Vaskaria. We spent twenty years making sure that word appeared nowhere, and then a riverboat captain fishes it out of a sandbar like a coin from a fountain, and there it is again, stamped in lead, patient as a seed. Do you know what I felt, Captain, when the report crossed my desk? Relief. Genuine relief. Because for twenty years I have been waiting for that chest to surface, and I would rather it surfaced into my hands than anyone else's."

"It surfaced into mine."

"Yes. That was the mistake. Ours, I mean." He smiled again, and for the first time the smile had effort in it. "You were supposed to sell it in Graywater Bend to a buyer we'd arranged. A dealer. He'd have paid you fairly, moved it downriver, and it would have entered a warehouse and then a furnace, and you'd have gone on being no one, which is what you paid so dearly to become. But you didn't sell. You took on a passenger. You went upriver. You started repairs in a town that officially has no residents." His eyes moved, gently, to Elodie. "You started behaving like a woman with a purpose. And a woman with a purpose is a woman who can be read."

* * *

The rain picked up again while he talked, drumming on the hull of the inverted *Lark*, and Jude found herself looking at the boat instead of the man. Three weeks of work in that hull. Elodie's hands gray with oakum. Her own hands, for the first time in ten years, working toward a destination instead of away from one. She thought about the provision — the promise they'd made in the rain, half-joking, the night the patrol skiff went by asking after a captain named Blackwood: *whatever happens, we finish it.* She had said it first. She kept turning that fact over like a stone with something underneath it.

"Here is what I can offer," Vane said. "And I want you to understand that I am authorized. This is not a bluff, Captain; I don't bluff. It's inefficient." He drew a folder from his coat, dry under oilcloth, and laid it on the upturned keel between them without opening it. "Amnesty. Full and formal. The Boundary Commission files on you are thorough — I should know, I helped write the summary. Desertion in the face of a lawful order. The order being: seal the survey, burn the manifests, report the Vaskarian archive destroyed. Which you did not do. Which is why there is a version of you living under a dead woman's trade license on a river that doesn't keep records, and why there has been a standing flag on your name for a decade that you have very carefully never tripped."

Jude's mouth was dry. "You let me run."

"We let you run. There's a difference, and I think you've always known it. An officer who disobeys one order is a problem. An officer who disappears is a liability we can schedule. We knew where you were every year of your exile, Captain. The riverboat license renewals. The mooring fees at Graywater Bend. You were never hiding. You were being *stored*." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Invisibility was never your religion. It was your sentence, and you made your peace with the cell because the cell was quiet. I understand that. I respect it. I'm here to offer you the door."

"And the price."

"The chest." He opened the folder at last and turned it so she could see: a single typed page, a form, blanks waiting for her name — her real name, printed in a government font, the letters small and exact. "You surrender the archive to the Survey Office. You sign a statement that you recovered nothing but ballast and river silt. You forget the archivist — not harm her, Captain, we're not monsters, she'll simply be escorted to the border and released, free to grieve her paper nation in whatever country will have her. And you go back to being—" he tapped the form, "—this. Lieutenant Blackwood. Pension. Clearance. A name that opens doors instead of hiding from them. Ten years of miles, erased in an afternoon. You could measure days in years again."

The rain filled the silence. Down at the landing, the river slid past the drowned doorposts, and Jude thought of Elodie's finger on the name VASSEUR, and the way she had said, *they were home when it happened. The river didn't kill them. The river just kept what it was given.*

"And if I refuse?"

Vane closed the folder. He looked, for a moment, genuinely tired — a man who had been carrying a nation's erasure for twenty years and had hoped, foolishly, to set it down here in a ruined church in the rain.

"Then I report that the chest was recovered by persons unknown and lost in the flood season, and the Office sends people who are not authorized to offer anything. I've delayed that report three times already. I can delay it once more — a week, perhaps. Long enough for you to do something sensible." He stood, buttoned his coat, and left the folder on the keel. "Keep the form. Burn it, if it helps. But Captain — Jude — think about what the chest actually is. It's paper. It's dead men's signatures and dead men's photographs. You've read it. You know it changes nothing for them. The only thing it can change is the lives of the living, and the living have a way of surviving anything, including the truth." He paused on the top step, rain running off his brim. "You were the best hydrographer I ever trained. You read water like scripture. So read this: the river is going to close. The pass is going to close. And everything you're carrying is going to be worth exactly what the next government says it's worth, which is nothing. Amnesty doesn't come upriver twice."

He came down the steps and walked away along the levee, that unhurried walk, and did not look back, because men like Aldous Vane had never once needed to.

* * *

Jude sat for a long time with the folder on her knees.

Elodie didn't speak. She had heard all of it — of course she had; the church was the size of a chapel and the rain made whispering pointless — and she sat across the hull with her caulking iron cold in her hands, watching Jude the way you watch water rise around a mooring line, wanting to act and knowing the line isn't yours to cut.

It was Jude who finally spoke, and her voice came out lower than she intended.

"Blackwood." She said the name like testing a rotten plank. "I haven't heard it out loud in ten years. I used to practice forgetting it. I'd write the wrong name on freight manifests on purpose. I'd introduce myself wrong in mirrors, almost." She laughed, and it wasn't a good laugh. "He's right. That's the filthy thing. He's right about all of it. I wasn't hiding. I was *stored*. Somebody put me on this river like a bottle with a message that never gets read, and I called it freedom because the cell was quiet."

"Jude—"

"He knew your name before I did." Jude looked up. "He looked at you like a coastline. Like something already charted." She stood, and the folder slid from her knees to the floor, and she left it there, face-down, the form inside with her real name printed on it in a government font, waiting. "He gave us a week."

Elodie rose too. "And what are we going to do with it?"

Jude looked at the *Lark*, half-caulked, half-hers, half-something-else-now. She looked at the folder. She thought about amnesty — the word itself, from the Greek, she remembered someone telling her once: *not-remembering*. A whole life built on not-remembering, and a man from the Survey Office offering to make it official.

"We're going to need more oakum," she said. "And we're going to need the boy with the side channels. Because if that man tracked a photograph from a deckhand's phone to this porch in three weeks, then the people he reports to are already closer than he is — and he wasn't the one who mentioned the pass." She picked up the folder after all, and held it out to Elodie. "You should burn it. It's your name they want forgotten too."

Elodie took it, but didn't move toward the stove. "And yours?"

Jude looked out through the broken steeple at the river, high and brown and patient, carrying everything it was given toward the sea.

"Mine," she said, "I'm going to keep."
