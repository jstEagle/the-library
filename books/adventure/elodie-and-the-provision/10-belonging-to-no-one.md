## 10. Belonging to No One

The rain had been falling for nineteen days, and Elodie had learned to measure time by the drips.

There was the drip from the roof seam that struck the oilcloth over the galley table — one every four seconds, steady as a metronome. There was the slower one that found the tin cup she'd wedged under the companionway, filling with a sound like a coin dropped into a well. And there was the one she hated most: the thin whistle of water finding the patched seam in the hull, the sound of their repair failing by inches a day, a sound Jude heard even in her sleep and flinched at.

Nineteen days pinned inside a dying boat in a drowned town, and Elodie knew things about Jude Blackwood now that no one on this river knew. She knew the captain took her coffee bitter enough to strip varnish and pretended not to like it sweet when Elodie made it that way anyway. She knew the scar on her left forearm came from a winch cable, not a knife, though she'd let the crew believe the knife story for years. She knew that when Jude read, her lips moved slightly, forming the words, as if she didn't trust ink unless she could taste it.

And Jude knew things about her. That was the problem. That was the whole catastrophe of it.

Elodie sat on the settee berth with the archive open across her knees — not the chest itself now but the contents, spread out in careful stacks she'd been cataloguing by lamplight for two weeks. Manifests. Photographs. The transport orders with their signatures. She'd been raised among these documents the way other children were raised among saints' cards; she knew them the way you know your own face, which is to say imperfectly, and with a horror that never quite dulled.

Jude was up top, under the tarp they'd rigged between the wheelhouse and the rail, watching the river. She spent hours there. Elodie used to think it was vigilance — the patrol skiff, the surveyor, all the reasons they'd be fools to relax. But lately she'd begun to understand it was something else. Jude watched the water the way penitents watch altars. It was where she went to not be seen, even by herself.

*You have to tell her,* Elodie thought. *Before she finds out some other way.*

Because there was another way. There was always another way. The network had taught her that much: secrets don't stay buried, they just change hands. Her grandmother's whole archive had changed hands three times before the chest sank, and every hand that touched it had left fingerprints, and every fingerprint had cost someone everything.

She climbed the ladder into the rain-smell of the wheelhouse. Jude stood at the window with her arms folded, watching Meridian Flats drown again in miniature — the rooftops of the old town breaking the floodwater downstream like the backs of sleeping animals, the church spire leaning at its permanent angle of surrender.

"Captain."

"You only call me that when you want something." Jude didn't turn around. "Or when you're about to say something you've rehearsed."

Elodie's mouth went dry. Nineteen days, and the woman could read her like a manifest.

"I need to tell you about Graywater Bend," she said. "About how I really found you."

* * *

It had rained the day she first saw the photograph too. Elodie remembered that — the way she remembered everything, uselessly, completely, her memory a warehouse with no door. She'd been in the back room of a bookbinder's shop in Corvane, three hundred miles and a different lifetime downriver, when Tomasek laid the print on the workbench in front of her.

Tomasek was the network. Not all of it — the network had no center, that was the point of it, cells strung along the diaspora like beads on a broken necklace — but he was her bead. A former customs clerk with ruined lungs and a face like a closed ledger, he ran the Corvane cell out of the bookbindery because no government agent on earth would search a shop that smelled that strongly of glue.

The photograph showed a dredge deck. Mud-caked men squinting into the sun. And in the foreground, half-buried, a corner of lead sheeting stamped with the double-headed seal of Vaskaria.

"A deckhand sells his pictures to anyone who'll pay," Tomasek said. "He doesn't know what he has. But we know. And so does the Survey Office, which means we have perhaps days." He tapped the image. "The chest is real, Elodie. If this is what I think it is — if it's the Vasseur consignment — then it's the archive. Your family's archive. The manifests, the orders, the photographs. Everything they confiscated when they emptied the valley."

"And everything they burned everywhere else," Elodie said quietly.

"Everything they burned everywhere else." He slid an envelope across the bench. Inside was money, a route map, and a name. "The captain who pulled it up is called Blackwood. Jude Blackwood. Runs cargo out of Graywater Bend. No history, no record, no debts. A ghost with a boat." His eyes, pale and tired, held hers. "We can't approach a ghost directly. Ghosts run. You're the heir. You have bloodright. Make her give it to you."

"And if she won't?"

"Then buy it. Then steal it. Then do whatever you have to." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Twenty years, Elodie. Twenty years they've told the world Vaskaria never happened. You are the last person alive who can prove otherwise, and you're going to feel bad about inconveniencing one river captain?"

She had said yes. Of course she had said yes. She'd rehearsed the confrontation at the trading post on the walk in from the ferry landing — the seal, the claim, the offer of passage money, all of it staged, all of it scripted, and Jude Blackwood refusing with those flat grey eyes and saying *it's not mine to sell*, and Elodie thinking: *good, she's honest, honest people are predictable.*

That was the first mistake. Honest people are not predictable. Honest people are worse. Honest people will surprise you by standing in front of a gunboat for something they don't even believe in yet.

The second mistake was the monsoon. The third was the leak. The fourth, fifth, sixth, and seventh mistakes were all the same mistake: nineteen days of coffee made sweet, of arguments about caulking that turned into arguments about nothing, of telling each other true things in the dark while the river ate the town below them. Somewhere between the fence line and the flooded churchyard, Elodie had stopped being an operative and started being a person, and persons were useless. Persons fell in love with the people they were using.

"Say it plainly," Jude said now. She still hadn't turned around. Rain hammered the tarp overhead like applause from an enormous indifferent crowd. "Whatever it is. Plainly."

"The network sent me." The words came out of Elodie like teeth. "The survivor cells, downriver. They tracked the chest from the moment your deckhand's photographs surfaced. They couldn't approach you — you had no history, no leverage, nothing to hold. So they sent me. The heir. Bloodright. I was supposed to make you surrender it, or buy it, or take it." She made herself keep going. "Using you was part of the plan, Jude. From the beginning. The trading post wasn't chance. I'd been looking for you for six days before you docked."

Silence. The drip in the tin cup below decks kept its slow time, four seconds, five, six.

Jude turned around then, and her face was worse than anger. Anger would have been fine. Anger was weather; Elodie could have sailed through anger. This was something being dismantled behind Jude's eyes, plank by plank, and Elodie recognized the expression because she'd seen it in the archive photographs — the faces of people in the valley the week before the confiscations, still smiling for the camera, not yet knowing.

"The name," Jude said finally. Her voice was very level, the way it got when she was most afraid. "At Graywater Bend. When you offered me the money. You already knew I'd refuse. That was the script."

"Yes."

"And the night the surveyor's man came asking after me — you told me to cast off that same night. Was that the plan too? Keep the ghost moving, keep her dependent, keep her running upriver with nowhere to go but where you pointed?"

"It was also the truth," Elodie said. "They were coming for you. That part I'd have said regardless—"

"But you didn't say it regardless. You said it as part of it." Jude laughed once, a short ugly sound with nothing in it. "God. Every true thing you've told me. The names on the doorposts. Your grandmother. What the documents weigh. I stood in that hold and read those orders twice and told you things I've never told another living soul, and the whole time—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Was any of it yours? Any of it at all? Or were you just keeping the asset calm?"

"That's not fair."

"No," Jude agreed. "It isn't. Funny how that works."

"Jude—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and Elodie saw it wasn't quite steady. "Don't say my name like you have a right to it. You know what the worst part is?" She stepped closer, and up close her eyes were wet, which she would hate later, and Elodie knew that too, knew her that well, God help them both. "The worst part is I'd already decided. Down at the chest, reading those signatures — I decided I'd stand witness. For the dead, for you, for whatever you are to me. I gave up my name for this. My whole life of not-being-found. And it turns out I wasn't even giving it to a person. I was giving it to a plan."

"You were giving it to me," Elodie said. "The plan was paper. I'm the one who's been bailing out your hull for nineteen days."

"And whose hull is it?" Jude asked softly. "Whose route? Whose river? You've known since Corvane exactly where we'd end up, haven't you. The pass. The press beyond it. I was the boat, Elodie. That's all I ever was to them. To you." She shook her head slowly. "I spent ten years making myself belong to no one so no one could sell me. And the first person I let in came with a ledger."

"Then arrest me," Elodie said, and her voice cracked clean through. "Condemn me. Do it properly. But don't stand there and tell me it was all a script, because the script ended at Graywater Bend, Jude. The script didn't include the monsoon. The script didn't include Meridian Flats, or your scar, or the way you say my name when you think I'm asleep. None of that was theirs. All of that was ours. And if you throw that away because of how it started, then you weren't invisible after all — you were just hiding, and hiding is what cowards call freedom."

The words landed. Elodie watched them land, watched them cut, and knew even as she said them that some truths are weapons whether or not you mean to fire them.

Jude went very still.

Then she reached for the oilskin hanging by the ladder, shrugged into it, and went down the steps into the rain without another word.

* * *

From the wheelhouse window, Elodie watched her go.

Jude didn't take the gangplank toward the nun's house or the ferry shed where the boy kept his skiff hidden under nets. She walked the drowned street instead — the one where the water ran shin-deep between doorposts, past the houses with their names still legible above the doors, names Elodie had wept over weeks ago while Jude stood beside her in silence and, Elodie had believed, grieved with her.

Past the Kovač house. Past the bakery with its ovens full of river silt. Past the schoolhouse where the bell rope still hung, swinging in the wind off the water, tolling against nothing.

Jude walked until the rain took her outline, and then it took the rest of her, and Elodie stood at the glass with her palm flat against it, doing the arithmetic of loss. Nineteen days. Four-second drips. One confession, delivered too late by exactly nineteen days.

Below decks, the archive lay open on the settee, the dead waiting patiently under lamplight. The chest sat locked at the foot of the berth, lead-lined, patient, holding everything except the one thing Elodie actually wanted.

She thought about Tomasek's voice: *do whatever you have to.* She thought about the network's cold arithmetic, twenty years of it, the beads on the broken necklace, and how she had believed she was the necklace's finest bead, and how tonight she understood she'd been its most expendable. They had sent her because she was expendable. An heir was useful; a lost heir was better; a lost heir who might not come back was best of all, because grief makes excellent cover and martyrs make excellent symbols.

They had planned for her to fail here. Some cell, somewhere, had probably already drafted the elegy.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the river rose another finger's width against the pilings, patient as everything else in this country.

Elodie looked at the chest, and at the door Jude had left swinging on its hinge, and understood that she had exactly one night to decide what she was: the network's instrument, her grandmother's heir, or the woman who had meant every true thing she'd ever said on this boat and intended to go on meaning them, alone if she had to.

Downstream, faint beneath the rain, she heard it — an engine note too deep and too steady for any ferry, running against the current in this weather, when no sane pilot moved.

Someone was coming upriver. And Elodie had a terrible, certain feeling she knew exactly who.
