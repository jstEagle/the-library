## 11. Cold Camp

The rain had thinned to a fine gray needle by the time I made the old ferry landing two miles downstream of Meridian Flats, and by dark it stopped altogether, which was worse. Rain gave me an excuse to be miserable. Clear skies just meant I could see exactly how far I'd walked and how little I'd brought.

A tarp. A bedroll. Half a tin of coffee. The matches in my breast pocket, dry because I'd thought about them for a full minute before leaving, which told me something about myself I didn't want to hear — that even walking out on Elodie Vasseur into the monsoon, some part of me was planning ahead.

I strung the tarp between two drowned pilings that still stood above the waterline like the ribs of something that had died standing up. Meridian Flats was behind me, upstream, its rooftops under six feet of brown water, its doorposts holding names Elodie had read aloud like a litany until her voice went rough. And Elodie was there too, in the boathouse loft where we'd been camped for three weeks, and she was not there anymore either, not really, not since she'd sat across from me with rain running off the tin roof and said, *I was sent. Using you was part of it.*

Cold camp. That's what my father used to call it, back when he still spoke to me — a night out with no fire, no food cooked, nothing given away by smoke or light. You did it when you didn't want to be found. I hadn't done one in ten years. I'd gotten good at them once. Apparently the skill keeps, like scar tissue.

I sat under the tarp with my back against a piling and watched the river go by, doing what it always did, taking what it was given and keeping it.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Around midnight I dug the stub of pencil out of my jacket pocket and a sheet of paper I'd torn from the back of Elodie's ledger — stolen, I supposed, now, everything I had of hers was stolen — and I flattened both against my knee.

Here is what I wrote first:

*To the Office of Surveys and Inland Navigation.*

Because that was who the letter was for. That was who it had always been going to be for, if I was honest, from the moment the surveyor put his card on my chart table in Graywater Bend and said my name — my real name, the one nobody on this river knew, the one I'd buried so deep I sometimes forgot it myself. *Jude Blackwood.* He'd said it the way a man sets a key down on a bar: here, this is yours, come collect it whenever you're ready.

*To the Office of Surveys and Inland Navigation. I have in my possession a sealed chest recovered from the wreck identified as the Vaskarian packet steamer* Loyal Daughter. *I am prepared to surrender it intact, contents unexamined, in exchange for—*

And there I stopped, because the next word was the whole letter, wasn't it. In exchange for what? He'd offered it plain enough. Amnesty. My name back. A clean registration, a license, the right to walk into a government office without my neck prickling. Ten years of miles instead of years, and all I had to do was hand over a box I couldn't open and forget a woman with ink-stained fingers who picked locks in the rain.

I looked at the sentence a long time. Then I crossed out *in exchange for* and wrote underneath it, smaller:

*I am prepared to surrender it intact. No conditions. Please send someone.*

That was better. That was cleaner. That was the version of me that had kept her head down through two changes of administration and a war and the slow drowning of every town between Graywater Bend and the pass. That woman had survived. That woman had a philosophy, and the philosophy was simple: the river keeps what it is given, and so do governments, and the only way to keep anything of yourself is to give them nothing at all.

I read it again. *No conditions. Please send someone.*

Please. I'd written *please* to the office that erased a nation off the maps. Somewhere upstream, asleep in a boathouse loft with her boots still on because she never took them off, was a woman whose entire family archive was locked in my hold — manifests, photographs, orders bearing signatures of men who still cut ribbons and gave speeches — and I was writing *please* to the men whose signatures those were.

But that wasn't quite true either, and lying to yourself on paper is harder than lying out loud, because paper holds still. The chest wasn't in my hold anymore. It was in the boathouse, under the floorboards Elodie had pried up herself, wrapped in her own oilskin, guarded by the nun and the boy and old Sava the ferryman, who had lost his brother to whatever was in those photographs and hadn't asked a single question when we asked him to hide it. The chest was theirs now. It had been theirs since the night I read the documents twice and said nothing, and then read them a third time by lamplight while Elodie pretended to sleep and let me.

So the letter was a lie twice over. It promised a thing I no longer possessed, and it implied a man I no longer was.

I wrote a third line anyway. Habit. When you can't steer you can still log.

*I found the chest. I want that understood. Nobody sent me. Nobody used me. I pulled it off a sandbar with my own hands and I carried it as far as I've carried it, and if it goes back to you it will be because I decided, not because anyone made me.*

There. That was the truth at the bottom of the letter, the way silt is at the bottom of the river. I'd walked out into the rain because she'd lied to me, yes. Because three weeks of telling each other true things had turned out to be half a ledger on her side, entries withheld, and I knew what it was to keep books like that. But underneath the hurt, colder and quieter, was the thing I hadn't said to her and wasn't saying to the surveyor either: that for one moment, reading those orders in the lamplight, I had felt *found*. Not caught. Found. Like a boat that's been aground so long it forgot it was built for water.

And I hated her for that. Or I hated myself. On a cold camp, at midnight, with no fire, the two feel about the same.

* * *

Toward morning the fog came up off the water the way it does after heavy rain, thick as wool, and in the fog I heard an engine.

Not close. Upstream, toward the Flats, low and steady, the kind of idle a patrol skiff holds when it's being careful. I'd spent ten years learning engines the way other people learn faces, and that one had the flat note of a government two-stroke, the kind they issue by the hundred, the kind that sounds like nobody in particular.

I sat very still under the tarp and listened to it move along the drowned shoreline, pause somewhere near the church steeple that still broke the surface, and hold there. A long hold. Long enough for a man with a loudhailer to ask his questions. Long enough for a boy who knew every side channel to slip a skiff through the reeds and carry a warning to a boathouse loft.

Then the engine moved on, downstream, toward me, and I did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. If they were working the banks, they'd work them all. If the surveyor had found us once at Meridian Flats — and he had, he'd stood in our boathouse with amnesty in his mouth and my old name in his pocket — then he knew the ground, and a man who knows ground comes back to it. The fog would lift by midmorning. My tarp would be a gray flag against the green. Two miles of open water between me and the Flats, and me without a boat, without a channel map, without the boy.

I thought about staying put. I thought about how easy it would be. Let them come. Hand over the location of the chest — except I didn't know where the chest was, not precisely; Elodie had moved it again after our fight, or the nun had, or the boy, and that was its own kind of message, wasn't it. They'd hidden it deeper *because* I'd walked out. They'd assumed I might sell them. I had written a letter proving them right.

The engine faded into the fog and the fog closed over it, and I was alone with the paper on my knee and the pencil stub and the matches in my breast pocket, and I understood that I had come downriver not to get away from Elodie Vasseur but to get away from the decision, and the decision had followed me anyway, the way decisions do, because they live in your pockets.

* * *

I wrote four more drafts before the light came.

The second draft was angrier. *Take your amnesty and your clean registration and file them where you file the people you drown.* I liked that one. I read it twice. It was also useless, because anger is fuel and fuel is for engines, and I was sitting on a mudbank with no engine at all.

The third draft was honest, which is the most dangerous kind. *I didn't know what was in the chest when I dredged it up. I know now. I have read the orders. I have seen the photographs of the column of people on the quay at Vaskar City, and I have counted the coats, the way you count coats when you are trying to make yourself believe a number. I cannot uncount them. Whatever arrangement kept me invisible on this river for ten years, I understand now that it was not mine. It was yours. You hid me the way you hid them. I am done being part of your filing system.*

I read that one three times. My hands were shaking, and not from the cold.

Because that was it, laid out flat. That was the thing the surveyor had shown me without meaning to, the thing Elodie's betrayal had only covered over like fog covers water. My invisibility had never been my religion. It had been someone else's arrangement. A captain named Blackwood who lost her commission and her name in the same week twenty years ago — someone had decided that woman should vanish, and had made it easy, and I had called the ease *freedom* for ten years. The same hands that smoothed a nation off the maps had smoothed me off the muster rolls. I'd been grateful. Grateful. To the same office.

Elodie had used me. True. She'd admitted it with rain in her hair and shame in her voice, and it had cut deep because the trust had gone real somewhere in those three weeks of rain and bad caulking and arguments about rope knots. But she had used me the way the river uses current — openly, according to her nature, and she had wept doing it. The surveyor had used me the way a hook uses bait, and smiled, and called it an offer.

I folded the third draft along the lines of my knee and held it a long time.

* * *

The fourth draft I wrote slowly, because it was short.

*I am not surrendering the chest. I am not surrendering the woman. Come and take us both if you can find us, and when you do, remember that everything in that box has already been copied, read aloud, and memorized by more people than you can drown.*

It wasn't entirely true — I had no idea whether copies existed, though knowing Elodie, knowing archivists, knowing the network that had sent her, I'd have bet my remaining engine parts on it — and it was vain, and it was a declaration of war from a woman sitting under a tarp with a pencil stub and eleven matches. It was also, for the first time in ten years, a sentence with my name fit to stand at the bottom of it.

I signed it. *Jude Blackwood.* Not the river name, not the trading-post alias, not *Captain* anything. The whole name, letters tall, pressed hard enough to score the paper beneath.

Then I sat with all four drafts in my lap and the fog lifting off the water in rags, and I burned them.

All four. Even the fourth. Especially the fourth — because a letter is a surrender of a different kind. Once it's sent, the choice belongs to the reader. And I was done letting other people read me and decide. The surveyor could have my name when I handed it to him face to face, if I ever chose to, and the world could have the truth when I carried it to the press beyond the pass myself, and Elodie — Elodie could have whatever I decided to say to her, said out loud, looking at her, no paper between us.

The matches were damp and it took three of them. The drafts caught reluctantly, the way wet things burn, smoking and flaring and going up in black flakes that the river took the moment they cleared the tarp's edge. The river keeps what it is given. I gave it my surrender, all four versions of it, and watched it keep them.

What was left was the pencil stub, the ledger page's blank twin, and a fact I could no longer get around: the truth in that chest belonged to me now too. Not by bloodright, not by assignment, not by any arrangement anyone else had made. By labor. I'd hauled it off a sandbar. I'd read it twice and a third time. I'd lain awake counting coats. You don't get to hand that back and stay clean, and you don't get to hand it back and stay *yourself*, either. There are cargoes that change the boat that carries them. Every riverman knows it and pretends otherwise.

I struck the last match I intended to waste, lit the corner of the blank page, and let that go too, a small white ghost of flame, a signal fire with nobody to signal.

Then I packed the tarp, shouldered the bedroll, and started the two miles back upstream along the drowned shoreline, past the church steeple, past the doorposts with their names, toward the boathouse where a woman was probably awake and probably furious and definitely guarding a floorboard I was no longer permitted to know the location of.

I didn't have a plan for what I'd say. Cold camps don't produce plans; they produce clarity, and clarity is harder. Something like: *You used me. I'd have done the same. Both of those are true and I'm not choosing between them yet.* Something like: *I'm not back for you. I'm back because the truth is mine now too.* All of it true, none of it sufficient, all of it hers to accept or throw back in the river.

The fog was burning off. Behind me, far downstream, faint as a memory, I heard the flat note of a government two-stroke turn around and start back upriver — patient, thorough, the sound of a man who always comes back to known ground.

They were coming to the Flats again. And this time I intended to be there when they arrived.
