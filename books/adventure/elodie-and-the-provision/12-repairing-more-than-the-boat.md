## 12. Repairing More Than the Boat

**JUDE**

She was still there.

That was the first thing Jude registered when she came down the bank in the gray of morning, three days after walking out into the rain — Elodie Vasseur, sleeves rolled to the elbow, kneeling in the mud beside the *Maren Lee* with a mallet in one hand and a fistful of oakum in the other, working the seam above the waterline like she'd been born to it. Her hair was tied back with what looked like a strip of bedsheet. Her hands were filthy past the wrist. There was caulking compound smeared along her jaw where she'd pushed her hair out of her face, and she hadn't noticed, and something in Jude's chest turned over hard enough to hurt.

Jude stopped at the edge of the willows and stood there like an idiot, half-hidden, watching.

The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist. The river ran high and brown past the drowned doorposts of Meridian Flats, and the boat sat on the shingle where they'd beached her, canted slightly, looking exactly like what she was: a vessel that had taken a beating for someone else's war. The seam below the portside chine had opened two fingers' width. Elodie had driven a plank patch over it with nails pulled from a drowned house, and the patch was ugly and crooked and would probably hold until the next set of rapids, which was more than Jude could say for most of her own decisions lately.

Elodie drove another wedge of oakum into the seam and struck it home, and the mallet's knock carried across the flat like a heartbeat.

Jude had rehearsed this. On the walk back from the burned letter, she'd rehearsed it the whole way — the words she'd say, the order she'd say them in, the way she'd keep her voice level so it wouldn't sound like forgiveness before she'd decided whether it was. She had a whole speech. It was a good speech. It began with *I'm not here for you* and ended somewhere cold and dignified.

She watched Elodie work for another minute, and then the speech died in her throat, because Elodie shifted her weight wrong and hissed — the mallet had glanced, or her hand had slipped, and there was blood welling at the heel of her palm, and she didn't stop. She just wrapped her rag around it and kept driving oakum into a seam on a boat that wasn't hers, on a river that had swallowed her family, in a town of ghosts, alone, because the person she'd been counting on had walked out into the rain and not come back.

Jude came down the bank.

Elodie heard her boots on the shingle and went still. She didn't turn around right away. When she did, her face did several things in quick succession — relief, then anger, then the careful blankness of someone deciding not to show either — and settled on nothing at all.

"You're back," she said.

"I'm back."

"Your letter burn well?"

"It burned."

"Good." She turned back to the hull. "Hold this plank. I can't reach the far nail."

It wasn't absolution. It wasn't even conversation. But Jude waded into the shallows and put her palm flat against the wet cedar while Elodie hammered, and the vibration of each strike ran up through the wood into her arm, and neither of them said anything else for a long time, and it was somehow easier than everything either of them had said last week.

* * *

**ELODIE**

He'd left without taking anything. That was the detail she kept turning over during the nights he was gone, the one that wouldn't sit still. His coat was still on its hook. The tin of coffee, half full, still sat by the stove. He'd walked out into a monsoon with nothing but the clothes he wore, which meant he hadn't gone anywhere. Which meant he'd gone nowhere on purpose, the way people do when they need to be empty for a while.

She understood that better than she wanted to.

So when he appeared at the edge of the willows, thin and soaked and looking like a man who'd been arguing with himself for three days and losing, she didn't give him the satisfaction of a scene. Scenes were what the network trained you for — righteous anger, the moral high ground, the speech about trust. She'd given him all of it already, standing in the doorway with the rain coming down behind her, telling him he'd been a means to an end, watching his face close like a shutter. And it had felt true and it had felt necessary and it had also felt, in some part of her she didn't examine, like throwing away the only real thing she'd found in ten years of exile.

The work was better than talking. Work was honest. You couldn't lie to a seam; it held or it didn't. So she put the mallet in his reach and made him hold planks and pass tools, and they rebuilt the *Maren Lee*'s belly plank by plank in the dripping mist, and every hour that passed without either of them saying anything cruel was its own small treaty.

By the third day the silence had worn thin enough to see through, and it was Jude who broke it, which surprised her.

"The surveyor," Jude said. They were turning the hull's lowest strake, both of them under it, shoulder to shoulder in the dark smelling of tar and river. "When he found us here. He said your name before I gave it to him. Vasseur. He knew what the chest was before we opened it." A pause. "You'd already been asking after it. At Graywater Bend, before me. Someone told him."

Elodie's hands stilled on the caulking iron. "Yes."

"How long have they been watching the chest?"

"Since it surfaced. Maybe since before. The network has people in the dredging offices, the salvage registries. Anything stamped with the Vaskarian seal gets flagged now. It took twenty years, but the world finally learned to look for us — not to find us, just to make sure whatever's left stays buried." She drove the iron home. "I wasn't sent to find you specifically, Jude. I was sent to find the chest. You were the current it happened to be riding on."

"And using me was in the plan."

"Using anyone was in the plan. I'm good at it. I've been doing it since I was nineteen — charming harbormasters, forging transit letters, making men feel important so they'd carry me and my papers one more mile upriver." She looked at him sideways in the dark under the hull. "What I withheld wasn't the mission. The mission you already guessed. What I withheld was that I stopped following the plan somewhere around Meridian Flats, and I didn't tell my handlers, and I don't intend to. They think I'm still executing. As far as the network knows, you're a hired engine and I'm a loyal courier. If they ever learn otherwise, I lose them the way you lost your name. I decided that trade was worth it. I should have told you I was making it."

Jude was quiet for long enough that she heard the river twice.

"I'd have done the same," he said finally. "If someone handed me my old life in a chest, I'd have lied to the whole river to carry it home. I know I would. Because I did something close to it once, going the other way." He wiped tar off his hands onto his knees. "That's not forgiveness. That's just recognition."

"It'll do," Elodie said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she pretended it hadn't, and he pretended with her, and under the hull of the wounded boat that was as close to a vow as either of them could manage.

* * *

**JUDE**

They worked through the afternoon, and somewhere between the garboard strake and the stuffing box the silence changed species — from the kind you keep out of pride to the kind you keep because talking would interrupt something better. Jude found himself explaining the engine to her, the old Lister single-cylinder he'd rebuilt four times with wire and stubbornness, and she asked questions that were actually good questions, and when he let her hold the feeler gauge she handled it like a relic, and he caught himself smiling at the top of her head bent over the fuel line and didn't mind being caught.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm supervising."

"You're staring."

"Fine. I'm staring." He handed her the wrench. "You've got ink on your jaw. Has had for three days."

She scrubbed at it with her wrist and made it worse, and he laughed — really laughed, the first time since Graywater Bend — and the sound startled a heron off the pilings across the channel, and Elodie looked at him with caulking on her nose and said, "You're impossible," in the tone that meant something entirely different, and he let it mean that.

But the tide of easy feeling had to go out eventually, and when it did, the hard question was waiting on the exposed flats, the same question that had been sitting between them since the night he walked out: what happens now. Not between them — between them was, improbably, repairing itself. Between them and the river ahead.

They sat on the shingle as the light went copper, sharing the last of the coffee, the patched hull gleaming wet behind them.

"The pass opens soon," Elodie said. "The glacier. Nunatak Station broadcasts the calving window on the shortwave — seven days, maybe eight, when the ice sheds its winter face and the high channel runs. After that it closes until spring."

"I know the window. I ran cargo over it twice, years ago, before—" He stopped. Before the name, before the erasure, before all of it. "It's not a route. It's a rumor the river believes for one week."

"Then we believe it too. For one week." She turned to face him fully, cross-legged, hands wrapped around the tin cup. "I want to lay it out honestly this time. All of it. No omissions, no strategy. Can you bear that?"

"Probably not. Do it anyway."

"The chest goes to the press at Kestrel Crossing, over the pass. Twenty years they've kept a printing house alive there on smuggled type and donated paper, waiting for the archive. My handlers want it delivered quietly, through their channels, so the network controls the release. I'm not giving it to them that way. I'm giving it to the printers directly, with a full accounting of provenance — how it was found, who carried it, what was done to get it here. Witnessed, not managed." She paused. "Which means my handlers will consider me compromised. Possibly dangerous. Possibly worth retrieving. I won't pretend that risk stops at the pass."

Jude looked at the river. The gunboats were downstream somewhere, sealing the lower water. The patrol skiff had come through asking his name. The surveyor knew both their faces now. Every mile upstream was a mile the net couldn't reach quite as easily, and the pass was the one door out of the country that had no lock on it yet.

"If we run it together," he said slowly, "it can't be your mission anymore. Or mine. It has to be ours — jointly, stupidly, with both of us knowing the whole shape of it. No handlers, no networks, no one steering from behind. Just the two of us and a badly caulked hull and a week of open ice." He met her eyes. "Those are my terms. Full honesty, shared risk, and if either of us wants out at any bend in the river, we say so out loud and nobody follows. I spent ten years invisible because staying silent felt safe. I'm done mistaking silence for safety. I'd rather be seen and sunk than hidden and afloat."

Elodie was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "There's a word for that arrangement in the old registry law. From before. Two names entered together on a vessel's papers, sharing equal claim and equal liability." Her mouth curved. "My grandmother called it *the provision*. It's how her family ran their boats out of Vaskaria — never master and crew, always partners, always both names on the line. It's why the archive matters to me the way it does. It's the last country I have, and it fits in a sentence."

"Then write us into it," Jude said. "Both names. Equal claim, equal liability, full honesty, free exit." He held out his hand, palm up, tar-blackened, ridiculous and solemn. "Provision?"

She took it. Her grip was strong and her palm was rough from the caulking and she held on one beat longer than a handshake requires.

"Provision," she said.

* * *

**ELODIE**

They spent the evening planning like engineers instead of fugitives, which was its own kind of intimacy — charts spread on the cabin floor weighted with rivets, her archival memory supplying what his charts lacked. The high channel entered above the old fence line and threaded the calving corridor for eleven miles before the takeout below the pass. Fuel was the problem: their cache had gone down with the decoy raft, and Meridian Flats had perhaps forty liters hoarded among its nine remaining souls. The nun had kerosene. The boy knew a fuel barge wrecked in the side sloughs, tanks possibly intact. The ferrymen knew the ice schedule better than the station broadcast did.

"We'll need all of them," Jude said, tapping the chart. "And they'll need a reason."

"They have one," Elodie said. "They lost their town to the same signatures. We're not asking for charity. We're asking them to finish something."

Jude nodded slowly, and she watched him arrive at it — the thing she'd known since the photograph at Graywater Bend, the thing the whole journey had been bending toward: that the chest was never cargo. It was a passenger. It had kin all along this river, in every drowned doorpost and burned registry, and carrying it home was going to take everyone it belonged to.

Outside, the rain had stopped altogether for the first time in weeks. Through the cabin's salt-fogged window the sky over the northern ranges had cleared to a hard, clean violet, and above the glacier's white shoulder a light was rising that neither of them had names for — pale green, wavering, like breath on glass.

Jude saw it and went very still.

"That's early," he said. "That's weeks early."

"What is?"

"The calving light. The glacier glows like that the night before it starts to shed." He was already moving, pulling his boots back on, grabbing the shortwave headset. "The window isn't next week, Elodie. It's starting *now*."

The radio crackled and spat through the static, and then, threading through it, faint and repeating, came the Nunatak Station broadcast — the one they'd thought they had days to prepare for.

The pass was opening tonight.
