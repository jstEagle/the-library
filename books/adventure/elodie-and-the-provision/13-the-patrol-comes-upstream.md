## 13. The Patrol Comes Upstream

The news came up the river the way bad news always did — on the water first, then in the mouths of men.

Jude heard it at dawn, standing on the sagging porch of the ferry office at Meridian Flats, watching mist peel off the current. Old Serafim's grandson came poling a flat-bottomed skiff through the drowned street with the kind of haste that made a boy his age conspicuous, and he didn't even tie off before he started shouting.

"Two boats," he said. "Gunboats. Gray hulls, twin screws, mounted at the bow. They're sitting at the mouth of the Bend, one above the falls and one below, and they're boarding everything that floats."

Jude's hands went still on the railing. Below her, in the flooded lane where Main Street used to run, her boat — *her* boat, the one Elodie had spent three weeks helping her recaulk, the one with a patched seam held together by prayer and pine pitch — sat low in the water with the chest lashed under the tarp in her hold.

"How far below the falls?"

"Half a mile, maybe. They've got a chain across the channel at night. Lanterns on it." The boy — Tam, they called him, though Serafim called him *menace* with something close to affection — wiped rain off his face with the back of his wrist. "They're asking after a captain. A woman. Blackwood."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped anchor.

Not *the woman with the boat*. Not *the salvager*. Not any of the soft shapes her anonymity had worn for ten years. *Blackwood.* Her name, the one she'd buried in a filing cabinet in a government office a decade ago, spoken now by a fourteen-year-old on a drowned street two hundred miles from anywhere.

"They describe her?"

"Dark hair going gray at the temples. River tan. A scar—" Tam touched his own forearm, aping the gesture someone had made to him. "Left arm. They're paying for it, too. Serafim says the reward's more than the ferry makes in a year."

Jude looked out at the river. The mist was burning off, and the water lay brown and swollen with the last of the monsoon, moving with the patient, indifferent strength of something that had already swallowed a town and would not hesitate to swallow her.

"Go home," she said. "Don't pole past the Bend for a while. Whatever you saw, you saw nothing."

Tam grinned — fourteen and incorrigible — and pushed off. But halfway across the flooded street he turned and called back, "Sister Anouk says to tell you: the high channel opens when the glacier does. She says you'll know what that means."

Jude stood on the porch a long time after he'd gone, listening to the rain begin again.

* * *

Elodie was under the floorboards when Jude told her.

Not literally — though by now Jude wouldn't have put it past her. Elodie was at the table in the back room of the ferry office, the chest open in front of her, cataloguing. Three weeks of monsoon rain had turned her into a creature of lamplight and paper. She'd developed a system: manifests sorted by date, photographs interleaved with acid-free tissue Sister Anouk had produced from a trunk that had survived both the war and the flood, orders signed in a hand Jude now knew better than her own. Colonel R. Vasht. Director of Interior Security M. Ostrand. Names that still appeared in the gazette, still signed budgets, still shook hands on platforms with bunting behind them.

Jude told her about the gunboats standing in the doorway with rain streaming off her oilskin, and watched Elodie's hands stop moving.

"Both banks of the Bend?" Elodie said.

"Both banks. A chain across the channel at night. They're boarding traffic." Jude pulled off her oilskin and hung it, water drumming on the floorboards beneath it. "They know my name, Elodie. My real one. Which means the surveyor didn't just guess it back at Graywater Bend. He's had it since the beginning. Someone gave it to him."

Elodie sat back. In the lamplight her face was pale and sharp, the face of someone who had spent twenty years learning to read documents the way other people read weather. "The harbor registry," she said quietly. "When you salvaged the chest, you'd have logged the find. Salvage law requires it. A sealed chest, lead-lined, stamped with a national seal — that's not a log entry, Jude, that's a report. It would have gone up the chain within the week."

Jude had known that. She had known it since the surveyor said her name in the trading post and the floor of her certainty had tilted. But knowing a thing and hearing it said aloud in a warm room while rain nailed the windows were different animals.

"So it was never a question of whether," Jude said. "Only when."

"Only when." Elodie closed the folder in front of her with both hands, the way you'd close the eyes of the dead. "And now the when is here."

* * *

They spent the day doing the arithmetic of escape, and the arithmetic was ugly.

Jude laid it out on the table with the ferry office's old charts — Serafim's charts, water-stained and annotated in three generations of different ink. The river ran south to the Bend, and the Bend was sealed. North, the river climbed into the high country, narrowing, quickening, until it reached the wall of ice the maps called the Corry Glacier and the boatmen of three generations had called, simply, the Wall.

"There's a channel through it," Jude said, tapping the chart where the blue line of the river vanished into the hatched white of the icefield. "Or there was, when my father ran this stretch. The glacier calves every spring — sheds its winter face, and for a week, maybe ten days, there's open water where there's usually a wall of ice two hundred feet high. The high channel. It comes out above the pass, on the far side, where the water runs down toward the border country."

"And if you miss the week?"

"Then you wait a year. And the channel doesn't just open, it *moves*. The ice doesn't retreat politely. It calves in slabs the size of churches, and the wave from a big calving will swamp anything smaller than a gunboat." Jude looked at her. "My father lost two deckhands to it. He never ran it again. Said the river up there wasn't a river, it was a rumor the ice told about itself."

Elodie studied the chart. Her finger traced the blue line to where it died in the white, and stayed there.

"When does it open?"

"Any day now. The ice booms at night — you can hear it from the Flats when the wind's right, like artillery. Serafim says it's early this year. Warm winter." Jude straightened. "Which means we have maybe a week of open water, and two gunboats between us and every other route out, and a boat with a patched hull that I would not bet against calm water, let alone a calving field."

"Then we don't take it through the Bend," Elodie said. "We don't take it south at all."

"No. We take it north. Through the Wall."

The rain filled the silence between them. Down in the flooded street, the river muttered against the doorposts of drowned houses, against the names Elodie had spent an afternoon touching with two fingers, one by one, like a woman counting a congregation.

"They'll expect us to run south," Elodie said slowly, thinking aloud. "South is the press, the border, the pass road — the obvious direction. They've sealed the obvious direction. They'll have watchers on the north channels too, but thin ones. Nobody runs north. Nobody runs the Wall. It's suicide with scenery."

"It's suicide with scenery," Jude agreed. "And it's the only door they haven't locked, because they don't believe anyone's fool enough to use it."

"And are we?"

Jude looked at her. Three weeks ago the answer would have been simple — *I'm a salvager, not a martyr, I haul cargo and keep my head down* — but three weeks of lamplight and paper and Elodie's voice reading dead men's orders aloud in the dark had rearranged something in her that she didn't have a name for yet. The chest sat under the floorboards, lead-lined, patient, heavier than lead. She'd read its contents twice. She'd read them a third time alone, at night, when she thought Elodie was asleep. The signatures were still in office. The photographs were still true.

"They're going to bury it again," Jude said. "That's what the gunboats are for. Not us — *it*. We're a detail. The chest is the story. And if it goes back into a government hold, it goes into a drawer, and the drawer goes into a room, and in twenty years there'll be another Vaskaria and another chest and another river full of drowned towns, and no one will ever have seen the signatures." She flattened her hand on the chart, on the white of the icefield. "I spent ten years being no one because being no one was safe. It turns out it was never safe. It was just dark. I'm done with dark."

Elodie was quiet a moment. Then she said, in the tone of someone setting down a weight she'd carried alone: "My father ran the Wall once. Before. He said the ice sang." Her eyes were bright and wet and utterly steady. "Let's go listen to it."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon and into the dark, and the town worked with them.

That was the thing about Meridian Flats that Jude still hadn't gotten used to: there were perhaps thirty people left in it, living in the upper stories of drowned houses and the dry rooms of the ferry office and the convent on the hill, and every one of them had lost something to the same hands that had signed the orders in the chest. They didn't ask questions. Sister Anouk appeared at the kitchen door with a crate of salt pork, hard bread, and a jar of lamp oil, and said only, "The high channel. My brother ran it in the year of the big calving. He said the trick is to be boring — no sudden moves, no engine screaming, let the river do the driving." Then she crossed herself, which Jude understood to be the local equivalent of *good luck, and God help you*.

Serafim came at dusk with his grandson and a hand-crank pump and helped Jude strip the engine's intake and clear the silt of three weeks' monsoon. Tam — sent, clearly, as a spy and a messenger both — reported that the gunboats had taken a timber barge that morning and held it four hours, and had paid a boy from the Bend to carry a description of Jude up and down the bank. "They say you're dangerous," Tam said, with naked admiration.

"I'm a woman with a boat," Jude said.

"That's what dangerous is," said Tam, with the certainty of fourteen, and went back to cranking the pump.

By midnight the boat was as ready as she would ever be: fuel topped off from the cache Serafim had kept hidden through the war and never needed, the chest lashed amidships under the tarp and then lashed again with a second line because Jude had dreamed, twice now, of it going into the river, the hull patched and patched again, the lamp trimmed low. Elodie sat in the wheelhouse with the archive's most essential folder in a waterproof oilskin bag strapped across her body — *if the chest goes down, this goes on* — and studied the chart of the high channel by lamplight until Jude made her stop.

"You'll wear a hole in the ice with your eyes," Jude said.

"I'd rather wear the hole now than meet it unprepared." But Elodie rolled the chart and set it aside, and looked out the wheelhouse window at the black water and the blacker hills, and after a while she said, "Jude. If it goes wrong up there—"

"It won't."

"If it goes wrong," Elodie repeated, with the patience of an archivist who had learned that documents, like people, lied most fluently in the places where the truth was unbearable, "I want you to take the bag and go on without me. The chest is the story. I'm only the—"

"You're the reason it's a story and not just paper," Jude said, more roughly than she'd meant to. "Don't finish that sentence. I've heard it before, from a man who ran the Wall, about his deckhands. He finished it. It didn't help him."

Elodie looked at her for a long moment, and something in her face shifted — the archivist's composure cracking just enough to show the woman underneath, the one who had touched the drowned doorposts with two fingers.

"All right," she said softly. "Then we both come through. That's the provision."

"That's the provision," Jude said, and did not examine why the word, which they had never defined, fit the shape of the promise exactly.

* * *

They cast off an hour before dawn, on the last black of the night, with the rain gone soft and the wind gone still — the held-breath quiet that Serafim said meant the glacier was getting ready to speak.

Jude took her up the drowned main street at dead slow, the engine ticking over like a clock in an empty house, past the doorposts and the names, past the convent hill where a single candle burned in a window — Sister Anouk, awake, watching, praying or simply witnessing, which Jude had learned was often the same thing. Elodie stood beside her in the wheelhouse with the chart and the lamp, and neither of them spoke. There was nothing left to say that the river hadn't already heard.

The channel narrowed. The banks rose. The water changed — browner to greener, slower to quicker, the monsoon's last gift running downhill toward the sea they were leaving behind. And then, far ahead, where the stars should have been, there was a paleness in the sky, a white absence, and beneath it a sound.

Not the ice booming. Not yet. This was lower, older, continuous — a groan that came up through the hull and into the soles of Jude's feet and into her teeth, the sound of something immense shifting its weight after a long sleep.

"There it is," Elodie whispered.

The Wall.

Even in the dark it was there — a blackness blacker than the night, a cliff of cold standing across the whole width of the valley, and along its base, where the winter face had begun to let go, a seam of open water glinting faintly, running north into the ice.

Jude eased the throttle forward. Behind them, downstream, invisible but no less real for it, two gray gunboats sat on a sealed river, waiting for a woman named Blackwood to come to them.

She had other plans.

"High channel," she said, and turned her bow north, into the sound of the singing ice.
