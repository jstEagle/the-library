## 14. Smugglers of Meridian Flats

The town had a bell once. Elodie could see where it used to hang — a rusted bracket bolted to the eave of the ferry house, and beneath it, a pale circle on the timber where the rope had worn the wood smooth over decades of hands. The bell itself was gone, taken for scrap or for war or simply carried off by someone who thought a drowned town wouldn't miss it.

She stood in the rain under that empty bracket and thought: *they took everything, even the sound of calling people together.*

Jude was beside her, arms crossed against the wet, saying nothing in the way she'd perfected — a silence with weight to it, like ballast. Behind them, up the slope, the *Marta June* sat beached on the mud shelf with her stern half-swallowed by willow scrub, patched seams weeping slow threads of river into the bilge. The patrol skiff's wake from three days ago had long since flattened. But the asking hadn't stopped. A man in a gray coat had come through on the mail run Tuesday, showing a photograph of Jude to the ferrymen, and the ferrymen had lied to his face with such ease that Elodie understood immediately: this town had been lying to officials for twenty years, and they were very good at it now.

"They'll help," Jude said. It wasn't hope in her voice. It was more like a weather report.

"You've asked them already?"

"I asked Old Sable for a towline last month when I ran aground here the first time. He charged me nothing and told me about every government boat that passed for six weeks after." She shrugged. "People who hate the same people you hate are almost family."

"That's not how I'd put it."

"How would you put it?"

Elodie watched the rain stitch silver holes in the brown water of the oxbow. "I'd say people who have lost the same things recognize each other before they trust each other. Recognition first. Trust is what you build on top, if you're lucky."

Jude looked at her sideways, and there it was again — that flicker, quickly banked, of everything that had broken between them at the cold camp and everything that had been mended since, badly, honestly, with caulking compound and shared watches and words neither of them dressed up anymore. "Then let's go get recognized," she said.

* * *

The ferry house smelled of tar, wet dog, and lamp oil. Inside, four people waited as though they'd been summoned, though no one had summoned them — they had simply heard the two strangers were coming down the hill and arrived the way water finds low ground.

Old Sable sat nearest the stove. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a mooring post, with hands so scarred and knotted they looked less like hands than like old rope that had learned to grip. He'd ferried the Flats for fifty years, back when the Flats had been above water and the river had kept to its channel like something civilized. His grandson Denny stood behind him — sixteen, all elbows and watchfulness, with a boy's haircut grown out into a young man's shag and eyes that catalogued everything. Sister Bernadette occupied the one chair with a back, her habit gray with age and river damp, her face lined but level, the kind of face that had heard confessions from smugglers and soldiers alike and found both equally human. And leaning in the doorway, arms folded, was Marta Okonkwo — no relation to the boat, though Jude swore the name was a good omen — who ran the fuel cache and the rumor trade and possibly half the economy of the upper river, depending on whom you believed.

"Sit," said Sable, indicating a bench that had clearly been a pew once. "You're dripping on my floor, but the floor's seen worse."

They sat. The stove ticked. Rain worked at the roof in long patient fingers.

"I'll say it plain," Sable began, "because I'm too old for the other way. You've got a chest full of paper that says what happened here wasn't an act of God." He didn't look at Elodie when he said it. He looked at the stove, at the fire behind its grate, as if addressing the fire. "The winter the river came through the levee, they told us it was flood season. Bad luck. Bad engineering, maybe, if you wanted to be angry about it. Forty-one families. My wife's whole church congregation. The school with sixty children in it, because it was a Friday and Fridays they stayed late for music."

Nobody moved. Elodie found she was holding her breath and made herself breathe.

"Two years ago," Sable went on, "a survey crew came through taking core samples of the silt. One of them drank too much at Marta's table and said a thing he shouldn't have. Said the levee charges had been pulled, not blown. Pulled — by hand, by men with orders, because the valley needed flooding to cover what the army left upstream at the works." He finally turned his head, slowly, like a gun traversing. "So when your chest surfaced out of that wreck this spring, girl, and word traveled downriver the way word does — I knew what was in it before you ever opened it. We all did."

"We didn't know for certain," Sister Bernadette said mildly. "We hoped."

"Hoped," Sable repeated, with contempt for the softness of the word. Then, to Elodie: "Show me."

Elodie looked at Jude. Jude gave the smallest nod — *your chest, your call* — and Elodie rose, went out into the rain, and came back with the ledger book she'd carried from the hold wrapped in oilcloth. She laid it on the table between the stove and the pew and unwrapped it, and the room went quiet in a way that felt like the whole town holding still.

She turned pages. Manifests. Requisition orders. The photographs — she'd learned which ones to turn past quickly, even now, even for people who had earned the right to look. And then the orders themselves, the ones bearing signatures that still held office, signatures printed in newspapers across three provinces every morning, and she laid the book open flat and stepped back from it, because it wasn't hers alone anymore. That was the whole point. It had never been hers alone; it had been everyone's, stolen from everyone, and now it was going home.

Sable read for a long time. He read the way old men read — lips moving slightly, one thick finger tracking each line as though the words might try to escape him. When he reached the page with the levee order, his finger stopped. He did not touch the signature. He hovered above it, the way you hover a hand over a flame to learn how hot it is.

"Pulled by hand," he said quietly. "By men with orders."

"Yes," Elodie said.

"And this goes to the press. Over the pass."

"If we can get it there. The lower river's sealed. Two gunboats at the narrows, checking everything that floats."

Sable closed the book gently, the way you close a coffin lid, and pushed it back toward her. Then he looked around the room — at the nun, at the woman from the doorway, at his grandson — and whatever passed among them was older than language, some signal system built out of twenty years of surviving the same lie.

"Well," he said. "Then we'd best talk about channels."

* * *

Denny talked about channels the way other boys talked about football — with diagrams drawn in spilled tea on the tabletop, with a vocabulary entirely his own. The Dead Slough. The Widow's Cut. The Needle's Eye, which wasn't a channel at all until the spring melt made it one, and wouldn't be one much longer unless they moved within the week.

"The gunboats sit at the narrows because the narrows are the only deep water," he said, jabbing a finger at a puddle of tea shaped vaguely like the river bend. "Everything else up there is braided — gravel bars, dead arms, fence lines under maybe a foot of water. A loaded boat draws too much. They know that. That's why two boats can seal forty miles."

"But?" Jude prompted.

"But a shallow-draft skiff with the engine trimmed up, running light at night, no lights—" He glanced at Sable, caught himself, plowed on. "There's a chain of them. Dead Slough to Widow's Cut to the Needle's Eye. It adds a day and a half. It scrapes paint off anything you love. And halfway along there's a fence line — the old stock fence from before the flood, wire and posts, just under the surface. I've marked it. Three gaps. I can take you through gap two blindfolded."

"You won't be blindfolded," Jude said. "You'll be aboard."

The boy's whole body changed. Elodie watched it happen — the shoulders squaring, the chin lifting, sixteen years of being the kid who knew things suddenly converting into being the person who mattered. "Aboard," he repeated, testing the weight of it.

"He's been running that slough alone since he was thirteen," Sable said, and there was pride in it and grief in equal measure, the particular mixture of a man watching a child grow up in a place with no future. "He knows the water better than the water does."

"It's not enough," Marta Okonkwo said from the doorway. She hadn't sat. She never sat, Elodie suspected; sitting would mean staying, and Marta's genius was motion. "Channels get you past the boats. Channels don't get you fuel, and I hear you lost your cache running the cordon downstream." She looked at Jude without apology. "Word travels. Don't glare. What I don't know isn't worth knowing, and what I do know keeps this town alive."

"What are you offering?"

"Forty gallons, cached at the head of the Widow's Cut, paid for in—"

"In nothing," Elodie said. "In nothing except seeing it delivered."

Marta smiled then, and it transformed her face from a trader's mask into something fierce and young. "Oh, I like her. No. Not for nothing, archivist. For a receipt." She tapped the table. "When your press puts it out — the names, the signatures, all of it — I want copies. Twenty. Fifty. Whatever they'll give me. Because there are towns up this river full of people who lost somebody at Meridian Flats and were told it was the weather, and those people deserve to read their own history in print instead of hearing it secondhand from a fuel trader with a reputation for embellishment."

"You want to distribute it," Elodie said.

"I want to *smuggle* it," Marta corrected happily. "There's a difference. Distribution is legal. This won't be."

* * *

It was Sister Bernadette who surprised Elodie most, though afterward she couldn't have said why. Perhaps because she'd filed the nun away as gentleness, and gentleness turned out to be only the packaging.

They walked together after the meeting, the two of them, up the muddy lane past the drowned doorposts — Elodie had shown her the names days ago, and the nun had touched each one like a reliquary — and Sister Bernadette spoke without preamble, as women do who have spent decades cutting to the marrow.

"You're wondering what use a nun is to a smuggling run."

"I wasn't wondering. I was grateful you came."

"Mm. Gratitude is cheap. Curiosity is honest." The nun's boots sucked at the mud, unhurried. "I was at the mission upstream at the works. Before. I signed the burial registers for eleven men the army listed as deserters, and none of them were deserters, and I wrote to the diocese, and the diocese wrote to the ministry, and the ministry wrote back thanking me for my service and suggesting I pray for the souls of the departed rather than trouble myself with the paperwork of the living." She stopped at the top of the lane and looked down at the oxbow, at the water standing over streets. "So I prayed. For twenty years I prayed, and I am here to tell you, Miss Vasseur, that prayer is a fine thing and it has never once corrected a single line of a government record."

"And you think paper will."

"I think paper is the only confession the powerful understand. They can ignore a nun. They cannot ignore their own signatures." She turned, and her eyes were dry and bright and absolutely without self-pity. "I'm coming over the pass with you. My knees are bad and my usefulness is debatable, but I buried those eleven men, and I remember every name, and when your printers set type, there will be a witness who can swear to faces, not just documents. Documents burn. Memory with a name attached is harder to kill."

Elodie looked at her — this small, soaked, stubborn woman standing over a drowned town — and felt something crack open in her chest that had been sealed since Graywater Bend, since before that, since the network first put a photograph of a lead-lined chest in her hands and told her what she was worth to them as an heir.

"Sister," she said, "why? Truly. You owe these papers nothing."

Bernadette considered the question with genuine care, the way she seemed to consider everything.

"Because I spent twenty years believing I was the last one who remembered," she said at last. "And then you came walking down the hill with a book under your arm, and I discovered I was only ever the last one who remembered *alone*." She resumed walking. "That's the whole answer. Come along. Sable will want to argue about the tide tables, and he argues better when someone contradicts him."

* * *

They argued until dark, and after dark, by lamplight — Sable and Jude over draft and timing, Marta and Denny over fuel loads and the ethics of letting a sixteen-year-old run the Needle's Eye at night (the ethics lost), Elodie and everyone over contingency plans, because planning for failure was apparently a Vasseur inheritance. The chest sat in the corner of the ferry house under oilcloth, and people touched it as they passed the way you touch a church rail — not casually, not idly. With intent.

Near midnight, when the others had gone and only the stove's dying heat remained, Elodie sat on the old pew and listened to the rain and felt the strangest sensation settle over her: not safety, exactly. Safety was a country she'd renounced. It was more like — placement. Like a document returned to its correct folder after twenty years in a stranger's drawer.

Jude came in from checking the boat, shaking rain off her oilskin, and dropped onto the bench beside her without asking, which was itself a kind of document.

"They're going to pay for this," Jude said quietly. "All of them. If it goes wrong, the survey office doesn't forgive towns. It erases them. We've seen the proof."

"Yes."

"So why are they smiling?"

Elodie thought about it. Denny's grin as he'd traced the channels. Marta laughing over her smuggling arithmetic. Sable's finger hovering over the signature like a man warming his hands at a fire twenty years cold. The nun's dry bright certainty.

"Because they've been discarded," she said. "By the maps, by the ministries, by the river. Discarded people aren't afraid of losing what they were never given credit for holding." She leaned her shoulder against Jude's, a small deliberate weight. "My network sent me to retrieve an archive. Nobody planned for this part. Nobody planned that the archive would turn out to be a town."

Jude was quiet a long moment. Outside, far down the oxbow, a night bird called across the water, and somewhere beyond the hills, faint as a headache, the glacier groaned — the sound they'd been hearing for two days now, the mountain clearing its throat, the high route opening whether they were ready or not.

"Glacier's getting louder," Jude said.

"I know."

"Three days. Maybe four, if the melt holds."

"Then tomorrow we load," Elodie said, "and the day after, we run the Fence Line."

Jude nodded, and reached over, and blew out the lamp — and in the sudden dark, both of them listening to the rain and the distant grinding ice, neither of them moved to go to bed, because they both understood that the easy nights were finished, and this one was worth keeping.
