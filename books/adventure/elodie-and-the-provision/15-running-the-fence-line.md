## 15. Running the Fence Line

The boy's map was drawn in pencil on the back of a hymnal page, and it was better than any chart I'd ever bought.

Tam had inked the channels in a child's careful hand — not the child he was anymore, but the child who'd learned them, poling a skiff through the drowned streets of Meridian Flats before his voice broke. *Old fence line here,* he'd written, tapping a place where the river split around a submerged stockade from the war years. *Gunboats can't follow past the second cut. Draft too deep. They know it. They'll wait for you at the mouth instead.*

"They'll have lights at the mouth," I said.

"They'll have lights everywhere they think a boat can go." He grinned, gap-toothed and certain. "So don't go where a boat can go."

Elodie stood over his shoulder with the archive chest between her feet like a dog that wouldn't leave its post, and I watched her trace the pencil lines with one finger, memorizing them the way she memorized everything — manifests, signatures, the names on drowned doorposts. She looked up at me across the lamplight of the nun's kitchen and said nothing, which was its own kind of agreement. We'd made our terms in the rain. Tonight we'd find out if they held under fire.

* * *

We cast off at half past two, no running lights, engine idling so low it was more felt than heard. The moon was a thin nail-paring behind cloud, which suited us. The ferrymen had gone out an hour earlier with the decoy — a raft lashed from three skiffs and every spare plank in town, rigged with lanterns at mast height and a tarpaulin humped over the middle to suggest cargo, or a chest, or a person crouched low. Old Seraf piloted it himself. He'd refused to let anyone younger take the risk, and when the nun argued with him in their rapid river dialect, he said something that made her stop mid-sentence.

Elodie translated for me later, quietly. *"I am already a ghost,"* he'd said. *"They cannot drown what the river has already taken."*

The Flats fell away behind us, dark except for one candle in the nun's window, and I put my hand on the throttle and thought about the first time I'd ever run dark — twenty years ago, another river, another life, another name. Some skills you keep like scars. They ache before weather changes.

"Fuel gauge," Elodie said.

"I see it."

Three-quarters. It would be enough if everything went right. Everything never went right; that was practically the definition of tonight. But we had no choice about going, only about how, and Tam's channels were the how.

The river bent north, then east, and the current quickened as the valley narrowed. Somewhere below and behind us, out of sight, the gunboats sat on the lower water like closed fists. I'd seen them that morning from the church tower — gray hulls, searchlight rigs, the flat authority of government steel on government water. Two of them. Enough to seal forty miles of river against anything bigger than a canoe.

Unless the canoe knew the way through.

* * *

The first cut came up faster than I liked. Tam had drawn it as a slot between two drowned houses — rooftops just breaking the surface on either side, black and slick with moss, like the backs of sleeping animals. The channel between them was maybe four meters wide, and the current ran through it sideways, pushing toward the left-hand roof.

"Slow," Elodie said.

"If I slow, the current takes the stern."

"If you don't slow—"

"I know." I eased back anyway, splitting the difference, and we slid into the slot with the roofline close enough on the starboard side that I could have touched it. Shingles scraped along the hull with a sound like a long intake of breath. Below us, invisible, the house still held its rooms. Elodie kept her eyes forward. I kept mine there too. There are things you don't look at when you're running dark, and the drowned dead are all of them.

Through the cut and out into open flooded timber — orchard trees standing to their shoulders in black water, apples hanging pale as knuckles. Then the fence line.

It rose out of the river without warning: a stockade of iron posts, war-era, driven into the bed when this whole valley was a checkpoint and the people of Vaskaria were being moved through it in barges. Most of it had rusted off below the waterline, but the tops stood above the surface in a broken row, and the gap Tam had marked was real — a section torn out by some flood, wide enough for us if I threaded it exactly.

"Port ten degrees," I said, mostly to myself. "Hold."

Elodie held the wheel while I went forward with the boathook to fend off the nearest post. The iron was furred with rust and zebra mussels and something soft that I chose not to identify. The current shouldered the bow toward the gap and I let it, riding the push, and for three seconds we were inside the fence itself, moving through the skeleton of a border that had been built to cage people, and I understood why Tam had drawn it in pencil rather than ink. Some lines you don't want to make permanent.

Then we were through, and the river opened, and I allowed myself one breath that went all the way down.

* * *

The radio — the shortwave we'd borrowed from the nun — crackled once at four in the morning. A voice, bored and official, checking a patrol schedule. Then, fainter, two voices on the water band talking to each other, and the word that made my stomach drop: *contact.*

The decoy had been seen.

I couldn't hear what happened next. The signal dissolved into static and rain-static, and Elodie turned the dial through the bands twice and found nothing but the long hiss of empty frequencies. We looked at each other across the little wheelhouse and neither of us said the thing we were both thinking, which was that old Seraf was out there on a raft of planks and lanterns with a searchlight coming up on him, and that he had known that might happen, and had gone anyway.

"He'll run the shallows," Elodie said finally, as much to convince herself as me. "He knows the flats better than any gunboat crew. They can't follow him in."

"No," I agreed. "They can't."

Neither of us believed it entirely. We put it away in the same place we'd been putting everything since Meridian Flats — the box of things too heavy to carry and too important to drop — and I brought the engine up to cruising speed and pointed us north.

* * *

The second cut was where it nearly ended.

Tam had warned me: *the second cut bends. Everyone thinks it goes straight because the water looks straight. It doesn't.* And he was right, because the water looked straight, a clean lane between two levees grown over with willow, and my eye wanted to trust it, and only the memory of a twelve-year-old's pencil line kept me from holding course.

I turned early. Hard early, harder than felt sane, and the boat swung wide, and that was the only reason we lived through the next minute — because the channel bent hard to starboard behind a screen of willow, and if I'd held the line the bow would have gone up the levee bank at speed.

But turning wide in a narrow channel has its own arithmetic. The stern swung toward the far levee, toward a snagged tree trunk lying just at the waterline where the flood had left it, and I saw it a half-second too late to do anything but shout and throw the engine into reverse.

The reversal saved the propeller. It did not save the port side. The hull met the trunk with a grinding crunch that traveled up through the deck plates and into my teeth, and we slewed, and for one long moment the boat lay against the snag like a animal against a fence, taking its measure.

"Leak check!" I was already moving, pulling the inspection hatch in the wheelhouse floor. Elodie dropped down into the hold with the flashlight, and I heard her boots on the bilge boards, and then silence, which is the worst sound a hold can make.

"Jude."

"How bad?"

"She's holding. Barely. The seam above the garboard — it's weeping, not gushing. The old damage from the fence line, the night we left Graywater." Her voice came up steady, which cost her something; I could hear the cost. "The patch is seeping at the edges. If we hit anything else, even glancing—"

"We won't hit anything else."

"You can't promise that."

"I know." I took the wheel back. "Get the pump ready anyway."

Behind us, downstream, a searchlight swept the sky once, twice — a pale blade swinging over the drowned orchards, too far off to reach us, close enough to read by. Then it steadied, fixed on something, and began to move. Toward the decoy. Toward whatever was happening on the lower water that we would never see and would carry with us forever.

I turned north and gave the engine everything she had left.

* * *

The mouth of the third channel was where the gunboat found us.

Not the way Tam predicted — not waiting at the choke point with lights blazing — but worse, quieter. One boat, running without its own lights, positioned in the open water beyond the cut like a spider at the edge of its web. I saw it only because the pilot made a mistake: a cigarette ember, high on the wheelhouse wing, a single red point hanging in the darkness fifty meters off our bow.

I killed the engine. We drifted, silent, momentum bleeding away, and the current carried us sideways toward the bank while the cigarette glowed and dimmed and glowed again, and somewhere on that gray deck a man was smoking and watching the black water and seeing nothing.

Fifty meters. In daylight, nothing. On a moonless night with both boats dark, it might as well have been a mile — unless he had night glasses, unless he'd heard our engine before I cut it, unless the river carried him the sound of our wake lapping the levee.

Elodie's hand found my arm in the dark. Not gripping. Just resting there, the weight of it, warm through my sleeve. Neither of us breathed loud. The chest sat between our feet, and I swear I could feel it breathing too, all those dead voices pressed together under the lead lining, waiting.

The cigarette dropped. A boot sole scraped. And then the gunboat's engine turned over — but heading south, back downriver, toward the noise and light of the decoy operation, called away by whatever was happening on the lower water. The ember shrank to a spark, to nothing, and the gray shape slid away into the dark it had come from, and the river belonged to us again.

I didn't start the engine for another full minute. When I did, I did it gently, and we crept the last mile of the channels like thieves in a house where everyone was asleep.

* * *

Dawn caught us in open water above the cordon, and it came up the color of pewter, rain fine as sifted flour, the mountains ahead showing nothing but a suggestion — a darker gray where the sky ended, and above that, invisible, the glacier we still had to pass.

We'd done it. That was the fact of the morning, plain as the wet deck under my boots. Two gunboats, a sealed river, a week-long window, and we had slipped the whole arrangement in one night on a twelve-year-old's pencil map, with a raft of lanterns burning downstream as our alibi. Elodie stood at the bow with the rain running off her chin, and when she turned to me her face was doing several things at once, and the one that won was a smile.

"We're through," she said.

"We're through."

And then I checked the fuel gauge, because checking gauges is the religion of people who run rivers, and the needle told me the rest of the truth.

Three-quarters had become less than a quarter. The night's work — the full-throttle runs, the reversals, the idling in the dark, and somewhere, probably, a hairline crack in the feed line from the scrape at the second cut — had drunk us down to fumes. I did the arithmetic twice and got the same answer both times. We had perhaps sixty kilometers of motoring in us. The glacier corridor alone was eighty, and it asked for throttle constantly, fighting the meltwater current, dodging ice.

I said it out loud, because Elodie had taught me that much — that true things get said, even the ones with teeth.

"We've lost the reserve cache. Or we never really had it. Either way—" I tapped the gauge. "This is what's left."

She looked at the needle for a long moment. Rain dripped off the end of her nose. Behind her the mountains waited, and behind the mountains, over the pass, the printing house that had waited twenty years.

"So we sail it," she said. "Where there's wind. Where there's current. And where there isn't—" She shrugged, and it was the bravest shrug I've ever seen. "Then we pole. We row. We tow each other. The archive gets over that pass, Jude. The only question is what shape we're in when it does."

I looked at the fuel gauge, and at the gray wall of the range, and at her, standing in the rain on the bow of a dying boat with a nation's evidence at her feet, and I turned us north into the current, because she was right, and because the alternative — stopping, hiding, going back — had stopped being a thing either of us knew how to do.

Ahead, faint through the rain-smoke, the glacier showed itself for the first time: a white shoulder above the valley, and at its face, so distant it was almost imagined, a crack of thunder that wasn't weather.

The ice was already calving. The road was already opening.

And we were coming to it on fumes.
