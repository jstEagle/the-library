## 16. The Glacier Road

**JUDE**

The river changed its voice a mile before they saw the ice.

Jude had run water her whole life — brown water, green water, flood water thick as porridge — and every river spoke. This one had gone quiet in a way that wasn't quiet at all. Beneath the engine's labor she could hear it: a deep, tectonic mutter, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. The boy Tomas had told them what it was, drawing lines in the mud of the ferry dock with a stick. *The glacier talks all night. You'll hear it before you see it. When it stops talking, that's when you pray.*

She heard it now through the soles of her boots, up through the hull, into her teeth.

"Throttle's steady," Elodie called from beside her, one hand on the rail, the other flat against the chest between them like a woman keeping a hand on a sleeping animal. They'd lashed it to the deck plates with everything Meridian Flats had given them — cargo straps, inner-tube rubber, Sister Anselm's belt, though Jude had drawn the line at the belt and used rope instead.

"Keep it there." Jude squinted upstream. The channel narrowed ahead into a corridor of gray stone walls, and rising above them, filling half the sky, was the glacier's face.

She'd seen photographs. Photographs were lies. The thing was two hundred feet of blue-white ice, streaked with black moraine like veins, cracked into towers and seracs that leaned over the water at angles no architect would sign off on. Meltwater poured from its base in a hundred silver threads. And the whole face — the whole impossible mile of it — was *moving*, so slowly the eye couldn't catch it, but the ear could. That groan. That long complaint of stone-heavy ice being dragged downhill by gravity and time.

"That's our road?" Elodie said.

"For six days, give or take." Jude checked her watch out of habit, then put it away. Out here, hours didn't matter. The glacier kept its own calendar. "When it calves enough to open the channel, you get maybe a week before the ice floes jam it shut again. Tomas says day three is usually best."

"And today is—"

"Day one."

Elodie looked at her. Rain beaded on her hood; beneath it her face was pale but set, the way it got when she'd already decided and was only waiting for the world to catch up. "Then we go today."

"We go today." Jude eased the throttle forward and aimed the bow at the mouth of the corridor, where the current ran fast and cold and the first drifting chunks of ice turned slowly, like sleepers.

Behind them, downstream, somewhere past the bend, two gunboats sat on the lower river waiting for a captain named Blackwood. Ahead of them lay a route that existed for days instead of seasons, guarded by nothing but physics and luck.

Jude found, to her surprise, that she preferred the glacier's honesty to the state's.

* * *

**ELODIE**

The cold came off the ice in waves, rolling down the corridor like breath from an open freezer door. Elodie pulled the archive box onto her lap, unstrapped it, and lifted the lid.

They had agreed on this the night before, huddled over Sister Anselm's stove while Tomas's mother fed the fire with fence posts salvaged from drowned fields. If they were going to carry the truth through calving country, they weren't going to carry it silently. The dead had ridden in lead-lined boxes for twenty years. It was time somebody read them their names out loud.

She opened the first folder. Her hands shook — from cold, she told herself, though the shaking had started before the cold did.

"First manifest," she said, pitching her voice over the engine and the groan of the ice. "Vaskaria State Archive, Eastern Repository. Evacuation transport *Meridian Star*, eleventh of the month, spring crossing." She paused. The date meant nothing to the world anymore. It meant everything to her. "Passenger count, four hundred and six."

Jude glanced back from the wheel, said nothing, held the boat true.

"Names follow." Elodie smoothed the page against her knee. The paper was water-stained at the edges, the ink faded to the color of weak tea, but the clerk's hand was precise, bureaucratic, damning in its neatness. "Ardent, Mira. Ardent, Pavel. Ardent, Ilya, age four months."

The corridor walls rose around them. Spray came off the ice towers in fine cold mist, and where it struck the rocks it had frozen into sheets that caught the gray light and threw it back silver.

"Aubert, Celestine. Aubert, Henri. Aubert, Marguerite, age nine." Elodie's grandmother's name. She had known it was here — had spent ten years knowing it was here, in a box, in a flooded hold, in a river — but knowing and reading aloud were different countries. Saying it made Marguerite nine years old again, made her real, made her a girl who had probably been afraid of the water and gone aboard anyway because the men with rifles told her family to.

Her voice held. She was proud of that later, that it held.

Above them, with a sound like a rifle shot, a piece of the glacier's shoulder let go. It fell perhaps eighty feet into the channel a quarter mile ahead, and the wave it raised came downstream toward them, swelling as it came.

"Hold on," Jude said, very calm, and turned the bow into it.

* * *

**JUDE**

The wave hit like a fist wrapped in a wet blanket. The bow rose, hung, and slammed down; spray went over the wheelhouse in a sheet; the chest slid two inches against its lashings and stopped. Jude rode it out the way she'd ridden a thousand floods — knees soft, hands light, eyes on the water and not on the wall of ice leaning over her.

When the chop settled she looked back. Elodie had thrown herself over the archive box, arms around it, hood blown back, hair plastered to her face. She looked up, dripping, and — Jude would remember this for the rest of her life — she was *laughing*. A short, wild, terrified laugh, the kind that comes out of people when awe and dread get tangled together and neither will let go.

"You all right?" Jude shouted.

"It said my grandmother's name!" Elodie shouted back, which was not an answer, and was the only answer there was.

Jude turned forward again. The channel opened ahead of them, wide as a street, walled in ice on both sides. In the gaps between the seracs she could see deep into the glacier's body — blue light, ancient and sourceless, the color of the inside of winter. Chunks of drift ice knocked against the hull, soft as knuckles. Somewhere in that blue dark, water was running. She could hear it, a hundred hidden streams under the ice, all going the same direction they were.

Twenty years ago, she thought, this whole valley had belonged to a country. There had been villages up here. Ferry landings. A customs post at the pass. She'd never seen any of it — she'd been a child in another province when the maps were redrawn — but the archive in the box behind her knew. The archive remembered every name the new government had spent ink and blood trying to forget.

And now she was the one carrying it. Not selling it. Carrying it.

The realization sat in her chest, unfamiliar as a key found for a lock she'd forgotten she owned.

"Keep reading," she called back.

* * *

**ELODIE**

She read for two hours.

She read the manifests, all eleven pages, all four hundred and six names, giving each one its full weight — first name, family name, age if the clerk had bothered to record it. She read the transport orders, signed by a Colonel whose signature she had memorized from other documents, a man who now held a ministry. She read the requisition for the lead lining of the chests — *for preservation of records pending review* — and understood, finally, the full obscenity of it. They had sealed the evidence in lead to protect it from water and fire. They had planned, even then, to keep it. To bury it deeper later, or destroy it properly, when the season allowed.

The river had gotten there first. The river had taken the whole archive, the whole town, the whole country, and held it all these years the way it holds everything — patiently, without judgment, waiting to be asked.

"The last page," she said, when her throat had gone raw. She almost didn't read it. It was a letter, handwritten, folded into the manifests, unsigned. Someone in the archive had slipped it in — a clerk, a guard, she would never know who. The handwriting slanted left, hurried, the ink blotted in places as if written by candlelight.

"*To whoever finds this,*" she read. "*We were told the transports were going south to safety. Then we were told to seal the records. Then we were not told anything at all. I have put the true lists in with the false ones. God forgive me for the years it took. God keep whoever is brave enough to carry them.*"

The engine droned. The ice groaned overhead, a long descending note like a cathedral organ dying.

Elodie lowered the letter to her lap. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the silence was full — full of water sounds and ice sounds and the names still hanging in the cold air, four hundred and six of them, riding up the corridor with the boat like passengers who had finally, after twenty years, been allowed aboard.

"They're here," she said quietly. Not to Jude. To the ice, to the river, to the blue dark in the gaps. "All of you. We're taking you through."

Ahead, the glacier's face curved away around a bend of black rock, and beyond it, faint as a rumor, the water ran faster.

* * *

**JUDE**

Day two nearly killed them.

The channel choked with brash ice at noon — a thousand floating shards packed tight by the wind, grinding against the hull in a sound like teeth. Jude backed them out of the worst of it and threaded a narrower side channel Tomas had described, close under the eastern wall, where the meltwater ran cleaner. Twice the propeller hit something soft and yielding that was worse than hitting rock. Both times she held her breath until the engine note steadied.

Elodie stood at the bow through all of it, breaking trail with her eyes, calling depths and openings. She'd learned the river's grammar fast — faster than any passenger Jude had ever carried. By afternoon she was reading the water the way she read documents: closely, hungrily, looking for what was hidden underneath.

They camped that night on a gravel bar in the shadow of the ice, engine cold, stove lit, the chest lashed between them like a third traveler. The glacier talked all night, exactly as Tomas had promised — cracks and booms and long sighing groans, the language of something too large to lie.

"Do you think it knows?" Elodie asked in the dark. Her voice came from the direction of the stern, where she'd rolled into her bedroll. "The river. That it's been keeping them. Keeping all of it."

Jude stared up at the stars, sharp and cold above the ice towers. "Rivers don't know anything," she said. "That's why I always liked them."

"And now?"

Jude was quiet a long time. The ice groaned. Somewhere far off, a serac fell, and the sound rolled down the valley like distant thunder.

"Now I think," she said, "it keeps things until somebody comes asking. And I think we're late. But we're here."

In the dark she heard Elodie exhale, slow, and settle. Neither of them slept much. Neither of them minded.

* * *

**ELODIE**

On the morning of the third day, the glacier opened its throat.

Tomas had promised it: *day three, the channel's widest, the old channel shows itself.* He'd been right. Overnight the melt had swollen, and the river had chewed a passage straight through the ice field that had blocked the main stem — a lane of brown-green water forty yards wide, walled in blue, running fast and clean toward the pass.

Jude took them into it at full throttle.

Elodie sat amidships with the chest open on her lap and the wind tearing at her hood, and she read the names again — not all of them this time, but the ones that mattered most, the ones she'd saved like coins in a pocket. Marguerite Aubert, nine years old. The Ardents, all three, the baby listed as *Ilya, 4 months, nursing.* The ferryman Doss, who had run this same stretch of water in summer, whose granddaughter was the nun at Meridian Flats who had given them her belt and her blessing in the same breath.

The spray came off the bow like gunfire, exactly as the plan had warned, stinging her cheeks, soaking the pages until she had to shield them with her body. She didn't stop. The dead deserved to arrive with the wind in their faces.

Halfway through the corridor, the ice on both sides began to glow.

It was the sun, finally clearing the western ridge, striking the glacier's face at the one angle that lit it from within. The whole wall turned to fire and jewel-light at once — amber, rose, a blue so deep it looked like looking down into the sky from underneath. Elodie stopped reading. She couldn't help it. Four hundred and six names hung unfinished in the air, and the world had turned into something no archive had ever managed to describe.

Beside her, Jude had gone still at the wheel, her scarred hands light on the spokes, her face lit gold and blue, and she was crying — not sobbing, just crying, the way rivers run, steadily, without drama, as if it were simply what she was made to do and she'd been dammed up for twenty years.

Elodie reached over and put her hand on Jude's arm, and kept reading.

"Bellamy, Tomas. Bellamy, Anna. Bellamy, Josef, age six."

The boat ran on through the burning ice, carrying its cargo of names toward the pass, and behind them, with a crack that echoed off the mountains like a starting gun, the glacier began, very softly, to close the road behind them.
