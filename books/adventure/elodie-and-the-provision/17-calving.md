## 17. Calving

The glacier announced itself long before we saw it.

We heard it first as a sound I mistook for thunder — a low, rolling complaint that came down the corridor of the high channel and pressed against the hull like a hand. Then the air changed. The monsoon had broken two days back, and the morning was clean and cold in a way the low river never was, and the cold carried a smell I had no name for: stone and old water and something mineral, like the inside of a bell.

Elodie stood at the rail with the wind pulling her hair sideways, one hand flat on the chest where it sat lashed between us, and she said, quietly, the way people speak in churches, "There it is."

I had run ice before. Nothing like this.

The glacier came down off the mountain on our left like a frozen river caught mid-fall, a wall of blue-white maybe forty meters high where it met the water, streaked with gray moraine, cracked everywhere with seams so deep they looked black. It had shed its winter face, the boy at Meridian Flats had told us — seven days, he'd said, maybe eight, while the calving opened the channel through country that was sealed all the rest of the year. We were on day five. The water ahead of us was littered with brash ice, plates and slabs of it riding the current, grinding against each other with a sound like teeth.

"Keep to the right," I said. "The meltwater runs fastest along the face. Ice collects on the slack side."

"You've done this."

"I've done rivers." I throttled down to half. "This isn't a river. This is a river being born, and nobody's taught it manners yet."

She laughed — a short, nervous sound, gone almost before it left her — and then went back to watching the wall. I watched it too. You watch a glacier the way you watch a sleeping animal. Not because you expect it to move. Because if it moves, you want to be the first to know.

* * *

The corridor narrowed for the second kilometer, the glacier's toe on the left and a scree shore on the right, leaving us perhaps sixty meters of open water threaded with ice. I took us through at a crawl, nudging plates aside with the bow, the engine laboring in the thick cold water — meltwater is heavier, Elodie told me, and denser, and it holds an engine differently, and she was right, though I'd have resented hearing it from anyone else. The boat handled like a drunk animal. Every slab we pushed apart slapped back against the hull behind us.

The chest sat lashed amidships under the tarp. I'd re-tied the knots twice that morning without deciding to. My hands kept going to it the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

"Jude." Elodie's voice had changed. Flat. Careful. "Look at the face. Above the waterline. Do you see how the color's different there?"

I saw it. A band across the ice wall, maybe ten meters up, where the blue went pale and chalky — the old winter face, the part that hadn't calved yet, held up by nothing but habit.

"That whole section is hanging," she said.

"Everything on a glacier is hanging. That's what a glacier is."

"I'm serious."

"So am I." I eased the throttle forward. "Which is why we're not stopping to admire it."

For a while that was all. Kilometers of it. The corridor widened, narrowed, widened again. Sun came off the ice so hard it made my eyes water even through the glare of the overcast. Elodie read aloud for a stretch — she'd been doing that since we entered the corridor, names and manifests and fragments from the archive, giving the dead passengers of this story their voices back, and I let her, because the alternative was listening to the ice, and the ice talked constantly. Groans. Cracks like rifle shots. Long sighing exhalations from deep inside the wall, the sound of a thing breathing in a language older than any of the signatures in that chest.

Then, at the widest point of the passage, where the water opened out to nearly three hundred meters and I allowed myself, briefly, to breathe —

The glacier spoke a word I understood.

It started as a crack, but not like the others. The others had been snaps, reports, the small talk of settling ice. This was structural. This was the sound of a decision being made somewhere far above us, traveling down through two hundred meters of compressed snow, and when it reached the waterline the whole face of the glacier seemed to lean.

"Jude—"

"I see it."

I didn't see all of it. Nobody sees all of it; that's the trick of a calving, the scale lies to you. What looked like a slab coming loose was a building. What looked like a building was a hillside. A section of the winter face — the chalky band Elodie had marked, and more below it, far more — let go all at once, silently at first, the way big things move, and then the sound arrived, a roar with weight behind it, and the mass of ice went into the water like a mountain lying down.

I did the arithmetic in under a second, the way you do when the river has been doing your thinking for twenty years. Distance: maybe four hundred meters. Wave height: everything. Time: not enough.

"Off the wheel," I said. "Hold onto something that doesn't float away."

"What—"

"*Now.*"

She dropped flat behind the engine housing and got both arms around the chest's lashings, which was wrong, that was exactly wrong, but there was no time to argue about right. I killed the engine — a running engine in a wave that size is a snapped propeller shaft and a boat that can't be steered at all — and turned us bow-on, because that was the only prayer the river had ever taught me that worked everywhere: meet it straight, don't let it take you sideways, don't let it roll you.

The wave came.

I want to say I saw it coming, and I did, the way you see weather coming, the way you see your own history coming — a white wall rising off the base of the glacier, folding forward, gathering the brash ice into itself like a fist closing. It crossed the channel in seconds. The last thing I registered clearly was the sound of Elodie saying my name, just once, not a scream, more like a signature on a document.

Then the world was water.

* * *

A wave that size doesn't hit a boat. It picks a boat up and introduces it to a new set of rules.

We went up the face of it at an angle I will feel in my back for the rest of my life, bow skyward, stern dropping, and for one suspended moment the *Maren B* — thirty years old, patched twice, mine in every way a thing can be mine — stood on her tail like a rearing horse, and I could see the top of the wave curling over us, green and lit from within, full of turning ice.

Then it broke, and I stopped being a captain and became cargo.

The water took me off the wheel and rolled me. Cold like a struck match to every nerve. I tumbled with the boat somewhere nearby — I felt her, a great dark shape moving beside me in the churn, and that was worse than anything, feeling my own vessel tumbling like a leaf — and then I surfaced, gasping, into a world of spray and noise and floating ice.

The boat was still there. On her beam-ends, half-swamped, but there. The engine housing stood above water like a drowning man's raised arm. And Elodie —

I couldn't see Elodie.

There was debris everywhere — the tarp gone, a fuel can spinning in an eddy, oar, cushion, the tin cup I'd drunk coffee from that morning performing a slow, obscene pirouette on a raft of brash. The chest's lashings — I could see one of them, a single line, still fastened, trailing in the water. Where the chest was, I couldn't say. Where Elodie was, I couldn't say.

I swam.

Twenty years of keeping myself alive by staying unattached, by measuring days in miles instead of years, by belonging to no place and no person and no truth larger than the next sandbar — and in the water, in the killing cold, with my boat dying beside me, every one of those arrangements dissolved at once. There was no hierarchy anymore. There was only reach, and what was in reach.

The chest surfaced fifteen meters downstream, rolling end over end, lead-lined and stubborn, riding low. Elodie surfaced two meters beyond it, on her back, coughing, one hand raised — not waving, just raised, the way you raise a hand to prove you exist.

And here is the thing I will answer for, to her, to myself, to whoever eventually asks: I went for the chest first.

Not because I loved it. Because I'd done the arithmetic again, the same arithmetic, the only arithmetic the river permits: the chest was sinking. She was swimming. Lead-lined, air-trapped, already tilting nose-down, it would be gone in thirty seconds, and gone meant gone — twenty years of murdered witnesses, a nation erased twice, Elodie's family, the whole reason either of us was in this freezing water, dropping into a trench no dredge would ever reach. She could tread water for thirty seconds. She'd grown up on this river's delta. She could tread water for a minute.

I knew all that. I swam anyway, and I hated myself in every stroke of it, and I would do it again.

My hands found the rope. The chest tried to take me with it — of course it did, it weighed as much as a coffin, which is what it was — and for a few seconds I was in a tug-of-war with the drowned nation of Vaskaria, and losing. I got a turn of line around my forearm, got my boots braced on a passing slab of brash ice, and hauled. The chest came up grudgingly, streaming, and I dragged it onto the largest floe within reach and collapsed on top of it, lungs burning, and looked up for Elodie.

She wasn't where she'd been.

The current in the corridor ran hard after a calving — the displaced water has to go somewhere, and it goes everywhere, all at once — and the chop had scattered the ice, and I spun in a full circle and saw nothing but white water and white ice and the glacier standing there, innocent, enormous, already beginning to forget what it had done.

"Elodie!"

Nothing. Spray. The groan of brash grinding.

"*Elodie!*"

Then — a splash, off to the right, near the scree shore. A hand. Then her head, hair plastered black across her face, and her voice, furious and alive: "*I'm here — Jude — I'm here —*" and I have never in my life heard two words carry so much freight. I'm here. Not help me. Not goodbye. *I'm here.*

I shoved the chest along the floe to keep it from rolling off, then slid back into the water and swam for her, and the cold had gotten into my arms by then, into the joints, the way cold gets into a hull through a bad seam. She grabbed me and I grabbed her and for a moment we just held on, two people treading water in the wreckage of a plan, and she was shaking so hard her teeth clicked, and she said, "You went for the chest."

"I know."

"You went for the *chest* first."

"I know." I got an arm across her chest, under her arms. "Yell at me when we're not drowning."

"Oh, I'll yell at you for *years*—" and then a wave slopped over us and ended the conversation.

* * *

Getting aboard a half-swamped boat from the water, towing another person, with hands that no longer fully belonged to me, took longer than I can cleanly remember. I recall the ladder rung bending. I recall Elodie refusing to go up first until the chest was secured, and me telling her with a fluency I didn't know I possessed exactly what she could do with the chest, and her climbing anyway, and hauling me up after by the collar like a landed fish.

The *Maren B* lay awash to her gunwales, listing hard to port, the engine compartment full. I got the bilge pump going — the little hand pump, because the electric one was underwater, because everything electrical was underwater — and Elodie bailed with the tin cup, the same cup, retrieved from the flood like a small absurd miracle, and we worked in silence except for the sounds of water leaving and breath arriving.

An hour. Maybe two. The current carried us, boat and all, out of the corridor's throat and into the widening shallows below the glacier's reach, where the water went braided and gray-green and mercifully slow. I grounded us on a gravel bar in six inches of water, on purpose, before the river could ground us anywhere else.

The engine was dead. Not damaged — drowned. Whether it would ever turn over again was a question for a dry afternoon, tools, and luck, and I had no strong faith in any of the three.

We sat on the gravel bar with our backs against the hull, wrapped in the emergency blanket from the locker, sharing it like a poncho because neither of us wanted to let go of it long enough to divide it. The chest sat between us, streaming, dented, intact. Downriver, the glacier filled the horizon, white against white sky, quiet now, its work done.

"We lost the fuel cache at the fence line," Elodie said finally. Her voice was scraped raw from river water and shouting. "Now we've lost the engine."

"Now we've lost the engine."

"And the pass closes in two days."

I looked at the mountain ahead of us, at the notch of the pass, close enough now to see individual scree slopes, close enough to touch, almost, and impossibly far for anyone on foot carrying a chest that weighed as much as a body.

"The boat was never going to make it anyway," I said. "I've known since the fence line. I just didn't want to say it out loud, because saying it makes it true, and as long as it's unsaid, she's still my boat."

Elodie turned to look at me. Water dripped off her chin. She was shivering and gray-lipped and there was a cut over her eyebrow I hadn't noticed, and she was looking at me the way she'd looked at the glacier — like something vast that might move at any moment.

"Jude," she said. "What are you telling me?"

I put my hand flat on the hull beside me. The *Maren B* lay against the gravel with her seams open, taking water even now, settling by degrees, dying beneath us in the shallows of a river she'd owned for thirty years, and I found — this surprised me — that I could look at it. That some door in me had come open in the flood and wouldn't close again.

"I'm telling you we walk from here," I said. "Both of us. And the chest. Tonight, before the light goes."

I stood up, and my legs nearly refused, and I made them work, and I started unbuckling the lashings to see what we could rig into a sling.
