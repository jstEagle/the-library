## 18. What Was Worth Saving

I woke to the sound of water where water should not have been — not the river's long gray murmur but a thin, insistent dripping, close as a whisper, somewhere above my head.

For a moment I did not know where I was. The ground beneath me was stone, not deck. The air smelled of pine and cold and the mineral breath of ice. My hands, when I raised them into the gray half-light of dawn, were a ruin — split at the knuckles, the nails torn, one thumbnail black beneath. I lay on a shelf of gravel at the foot of a scree slope, under an overhang of rock, and above the overhang the sky was the color of unpolished pewter, and beyond the sky, impossibly high, the glacier stood in the morning like a wall of old glass.

Then I remembered the wave.

I sat up too fast and the world tilted, and I said her name before I was fully awake — "Jude" — the way you say a word you've been holding in your mouth in your sleep.

"Here."

She was ten feet away, sitting with her back against the rock, her legs stretched out in front of her, and she was looking at the river. Or what had been the river. The *Marguerite* — I had learned the name only two nights before, in the last calm hour before the corridor, when she'd told me her boat carried her mother's name and had never once said so aloud on any other occasion — the *Marguerite* lay on her side in the shallows forty yards out, half-submerged, her wheelhouse stove in, her stern awash. The current combed through the broken windows and out again, patient as a loom.

"She didn't drown," Jude said, without turning. "She grounded. There's a difference. Small one, but it's there."

I crawled the ten feet because standing seemed ambitious, and sat beside her, and we looked at the wreck together the way you look at a grave you're not yet ready to dig.

The chest sat between us. Lead-lined, seal-stamped, heavier than sin and twice as dense. It had come through the calving the way it had come through the flood that drowned Vaskaria, the way it had come through twenty years of river silt — indifferent, sealed, intact. Jude had saved it before the engine. I had seen her do it, seen her turn from the flooding engine room with the water at her waist and choose the chest, and I had thought, in the white roar of that moment, *she chose the truth over the boat*, and then she had turned around and come back for me, and I had stopped thinking anything at all.

"How long was I out?"

"Since yesterday afternoon. You slept like the dead. I checked your breathing twice." She said it flatly, but her hand, resting on the gravel between us, had moved half an inch closer to mine, and I understood that the flatness was the cost of the checking.

"My head—"

"There's a lump the size of a goose egg behind your ear. You'll live. You complained about my coffee in your sleep, which I took as a good sign."

"I don't complain about your coffee. I describe it accurately."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile had been planning to build. Then it faded, and she looked back at the river, and I looked with her.

The corridor was behind us now. I could still hear the glacier, faintly, a sound like a door being dragged across the floor of the world, every few minutes, all night, all morning. The calving had not finished. It would not finish for days. We had come through the week the mountain allows, and we had come through it by the width of a hull's plating and the stubbornness of one woman's hands on a wheel, and the boat had paid the toll.

"We can't take her through the pass," I said. It wasn't a question. The pass was a footpath and a mule track and, in the best years, a jeep road washed to gravel. The *Marguerite* was done.

"No," Jude said.

"Jude—"

"Don't." She said it quietly, but it landed like a hand on the chest. "Don't do the thing where you're sorry. I can't carry sorry on top of everything else today. She ran the corridor. That's more than most boats get to say. She ran the corridor and she grounded instead of sank, and there's a difference, and I'm allowed to say there's a difference."

I said nothing. I put my hand over hers on the gravel. She let it stay. After a while, her fingers turned and laced through mine, and we sat like that, two wrecked people on a gravel shelf, watching the river dismantle a boat that had carried one woman's whole hidden life and, in the last three weeks, mine too.

* * *

We ate what was left of the provisions: hard bread gone soft in its oilcloth, a tin of fish, the last of the coffee, which I described accurately and Jude pretended not to hear. Then we went down to the wreck.

The water was knee-deep around her hull, snowmelt-cold, the kind of cold that takes the argument out of your body in thirty seconds. We worked in shifts of breath and numbness, passing things ashore in the fading light: the tool roll. The tarp. The coil of rope, which had saved us twice in the corridor and now coiled up like a sleeping animal, saltless and river-smelling. The engine had drowned in its own compartment; Jude looked at it once, for a long moment, and then closed the hatch with a gentleness I had never seen her use on anything mechanical.

"You could salvage her," I said. "Come back after the pass opens. Haulers come down this way. Someone with a winch—"

"She's past hauling." Jude stood in the shallows, water to her thighs, her hair plastered to her skull, and she was not looking at the boat anymore but at the far bank, at the wall of the glacier catching the last red light like a coal in ash. "I've known it since the wave. I just hadn't said it out loud yet. Things aren't real for me until I say them out loud. You've noticed that, I think."

"I've noticed you'd rather carry a chest up a mountain than say a true thing at normal volume."

That earned me the smile — the real one, brief and crooked and gone. She waded ashore, and we stood dripping in the cold, and the light went out of the ice, and we built a fire with wet wood and stubbornness, because there was no dry wood this high and this late, and we sat close enough to it to steam.

It was there, in the hiss and spitting of the fire, that she said it.

"The boat was never the point."

I looked up. She was staring into the flames, and her face had the expression I had learned to recognize over the monsoon weeks at Meridian Flats — the expression of a woman opening a door she had nailed shut from the inside.

"Say more," I said, because she had taught me that too: don't fill the silence, don't rescue people from their own sentences.

"Ten years," she said. "Ten years of miles. You know how many miles the *Marguerite* ran under my hands? I do. I kept a log. Not the official one — my own. Every sandbar, every fuel stop, every mile of every season. Fourteen thousand, six hundred and some. I could tell you the number to the mile, but the mile keeps changing on me, because I stopped logging it the night we left Graywater Bend, and I haven't picked it up since." She turned her hands toward the fire, and I saw the burns and the splits on them, the rope scars, the engine grease ground so deep into the whorls of her fingers it would come out with her skin. "I told myself I was a river woman. That the river was my country and the miles were my years and as long as I kept moving, nothing could find me. No name, no address, no history. Invisibility as a religion. I preached it to myself every morning like a psalm."

"And it worked," I said. "For a while."

"It worked like a dam works." Her voice stayed level, but her jaw was tight. "It holds, and holds, and holds, and the whole time the water behind it is getting deeper. You know what the miles were, Elodie? They were a way of never arriving anywhere. A day measured in miles has no anniversary in it. No one's birthday. No grave. You can't be haunted by a place you never sleep in twice. I chose the river because the river forgets. Every flood takes the banks apart and puts them back different, and nobody on the water asks you who you were before. The river keeps what it's given and asks nothing back. I thought that was freedom."

"And it wasn't."

"It was hiding with better scenery." She laughed, short and without much humor in it, and fed a branch to the fire. "And then a dredge line snags a chest out of a sandbar, and a woman with a ledger walks up my gangplank and says *that's mine, by blood*, and I should have thrown her off the boat. Any sensible smuggler would have. Instead I let her stow away, and then I read what was in the chest, and then I sat in the rain in a drowned town with her and told her true things, and somewhere in there—" She stopped. Started again. "Somewhere in there I arrived. First time in ten years. I arrived at a person. And it turns out arriving is the thing I was running from, and it turns out it doesn't kill you. It just makes the hiding impossible. You can't go back to miles after that. The miles don't work anymore. They're just distance now. They used to be a place."

The fire cracked. Up the valley, the glacier spoke — that long dragging door — and neither of us flinched. We were learning its grammar.

"What was her name?" I asked quietly. "Your mother. The one the boat was named for."

"Marguerite Blackwood." She said it the way you set down something heavy you've carried a long way — with relief and with the ache of the emptied hands. "She taught school. In a town that isn't on the maps anymore, as a matter of fact. Different war, same hands. She used to say a person can live a whole life without ever once being seen, and call it safety, and die of it anyway." She looked at me across the fire, and the light moved in her eyes. "You're looking at me right now like you see me. I keep waiting for it to be unbearable. It isn't. That's the strange part. Ten years of thinking it would be unbearable, and it isn't."

"It's not," I agreed. "It's the opposite of unbearable. It's the only bearable thing I've found."

* * *

We slept in shifts, and in the morning we took stock, and the arithmetic was simple and ugly.

The pass was two days' walk — three, with the chest. The chest weighed what it weighed. The provisions would not cover three days at climbing pace for two people who had been half-drowned two days ago. Behind us, somewhere down the corridor and the river beyond it, the surveyor's people would be working their way up the wreckage of our wake, and the gunboats would be reporting a hull lost in calving country, and every hour we stood still was an hour they gained.

So we built a travois.

It was Jude's idea, of course — the river woman's answer to everything: if you can't float it, drag it. We stripped what we could from the wreck — the last mercy of the *Marguerite*, giving up her parts the way she'd given up everything else — two lengths of planking from the cabin roof, the long steering oar, a section of the rail. We lashed them into a triangle with the rope, padded the crossing with the tarp, and rigged a harness from the webbing of the life vests, cut and resewn with Jude's sailmaker's stitches, which I watched her set with the same unhurried precision she'd used at the wheel in the corridor, as if her hands had their own calm that the rest of her had never been invited to.

We loaded the chest at dawn on the second day. It took both of us to lift it onto the travois, and when it settled, the planks groaned, and Jude stood with her hands flat on the lead-lined lid for a moment, the way you'd rest a hand on the flank of an animal you were about to ask a hard thing of.

"Last ride," she said to it. "You'd better be worth it."

"It is," I said. "I've read it. I know what's in it. It's worth every mile."

"That's the thing." She straightened up and looked at me, and the morning light came down the glacier behind her like a held breath. "I haven't read it the way you have. I read it once, in the hold, in the rain, and I understood it, and I said nothing, and I've been saying nothing ever since, because saying it out loud makes it mine. Makes me a witness instead of a hauler. And I've spent ten years being a hauler, Elodie. Hauling cargo, hauling myself, hauling my one secret from sandbar to sandbar." She picked up her end of the harness. "So today I stop hauling and start carrying. There's a difference. Small one. But it's there."

We took up the harness together, one on each side, the chest between us like a third passenger, and we began to climb.

* * *

The pass road, when we found it, was not a road so much as an argument the mountain had once had with engineers and won. Switchbacks stitched into the scree, washed to bedrock in the spring melt. A tunnel through a shoulder of granite, its portal half-choked with old slide, cold air breathing out of it like a held note. We walked in the tunnel because the alternative was a ledge with no railing and a drop into white water, and in that cold dark, with our boots echoing, Jude talked.

She talked the way people talk in the dark — more easily, the words unwatched. About her father, who had signed things. About the year she'd understood what he'd signed. About the night she'd changed her name on a ferry manifest with a borrowed pen and felt, instead of shame, the clean cold relief of erasure, and how she had mistaken that relief for peace for ten years.

"I thought my name was the problem," she said, her voice bouncing off the wet stone. "Turns out the problem was what I'd let my name be attached to. You can't fix that by hiding it. You fix it by—" She stopped walking. In the black, I heard her set her feet. "You fix it by standing next to it in daylight and saying: this is mine, and this is what I did about it."

"And what did you do about it?" I asked, though I knew. I wanted to hear her say it.

"I'm carrying it up a mountain," Jude Blackwood said, "to a printing press, with my real name, to be printed next to it. That's what I did about it."

The tunnel let us out into light so sudden and so wide that we both stopped, harnesses creaking, and stood blinking on a shelf of rock with the whole upper valley spread below us — the river braided and silver, the forest dark and endless, and far off, past the last ridgeline, a smudge of smoke rising thin and straight into the wind.

Smoke meant a stove. A stove meant the settlement below the pass — the border town of exiles, the town with the press.

Jude looked at it for a long time. Then she looked at me, and there was something in her face I had never seen there in all the weeks since Graywater Bend — not the river woman's watchfulness, not the smuggler's arithmetic, but something younger, something that had been waiting under the miles all along like a seed under snow.

"That's the last mile," she said. "You know what's strange? I don't want it to be over. Ten years of nothing but the next mile, and now that there's only one left, I find I want a few more." She shifted the harness on her shoulder. "Come on, Elodie. Let's go be seen."

We took up the weight between us and started down toward the smoke — and that was when we both heard it, faint but unmistakable, carried on the wind off the pass: the sound of a press already running, and beneath it, rising up the switchbacks, voices. More voices than two.
