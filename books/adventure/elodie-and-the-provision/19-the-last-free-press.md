## 19. The Last Free Press

**JUDE**

The pass let them through the way a door lets through a draft — grudgingly, briefly, already swinging shut behind them.

They came down off the glacier road on the fourth day, Elodie walking ahead of Jude on the switchbacks, the chest strapped between them on a litter made from the boat's broken wheel-mount and two lengths of gunwale. Jude's shoulders had stopped registering pain somewhere above the tree line. Her hands were a different matter. They had been raw since Meridian Flats, and now they were something worse than raw, and she had stopped looking at them.

Below them, the valley opened like a held breath finally released.

The town sat where three rivers braided together — a border town, a town of afterthoughts and arrivals. From this height it looked unremarkable: tin roofs, smoke, a bridge too wide for the traffic it carried. But Jude could see the flags. Not one nation's flag. Dozens. Hanging from balconies and doorposts and the eaves of the customs house, faded and patched, the colors of places that no longer existed on any map she'd ever been required to know.

"Vaskaria's is there," Elodie said quietly. She pointed to a blue-and-silver rag flying crooked over a building near the river's edge. "Third balcony. It's been up for twenty years."

"You've been here before."

"No." Elodie adjusted her grip on the litter pole. "But everyone who left has. You don't raise a flag in a place you've never been."

The building under the third balcony was long and low, built of river stone, its windows glowing amber against the dusk. A sign above the door had been repainted so many times the letters stood out in relief, layers beneath layers: **THE PROVISION PRESS — EST. THE YEAR THE MAPS LIED.**

Jude stood in the street a moment longer than she needed to, letting the cold work its way through her coat. Behind her, somewhere down the pass they'd just survived, the surveyor was still coming. He would come unarmed — he'd have no choice; this valley belonged to no jurisdiction that answered to his office — but he would come. Men like him always arrived at the end, when there was nothing left to stop.

She shouldered her side of the litter. "Let's not give him time to catch up."

* * *

**ELODIE**

The woman who opened the press door was seventy if she was a day, small as a wren, with ink stains to both elbows and eyes that went from Elodie's face to the chest and back again with an expression Elodie had no name for.

"We heard the glacier calve," the woman said. "Three days ago. Old Tomas said it meant someone was coming through." She stepped back from the doorway. "I am Marta Ilves. I set type for Vaskaria's state archive for eleven years before they burned it. Come in before the whole valley sees what you're carrying."

Inside smelled of ink and oil and woodsmoke, and underneath it all, faintly, of paper — old paper, patient paper. The press itself dominated the main room, a flatbed machine older than Elodie, polished by decades of hands. Along the walls ran shelves of type cases, and above them, pinned like specimens, hundreds of clippings. Elodie read them as she walked: obituaries. Twenty years of obituaries, cut from exile papers across three borders, every Vaskarian death recorded in this room because nowhere else would record it.

Marta saw her looking. "We kept the list," she said simply. "A people is a list until it can be a country again."

Elodie set down her end of the litter. Her hands were shaking — she'd been telling herself it was the cold, the altitude, the weight, and now, standing in front of the press, she stopped lying to herself. "It's all here," she said. "The manifests. The orders. My grandfather's correspondence. The photographs from—" Her voice caught on the name of the place, the camp whose name she had grown up hearing only in whispers, and she started again. "Everything they took when they took the archive. Everything my family died carrying toward the river."

Marta knelt beside the chest. She did not touch it. She laid one palm flat on the lead lid, the way you'd touch a coffin or a cradle, and stayed there a long moment.

"Twenty years I've set type for other people's truths," she said. "Tomorrow we set ours." She looked up at Elodie, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "You'll want to be here for the first pull. Everyone whose blood is in these pages gets to stand at the press. That's the rule. We wrote it the night we founded this place, drunk and crying, and we've never once broken it."

"I'm here," Elodie said. "I'm not going anywhere."

Behind her, she heard Jude shift her weight by the door — still half in the doorway, never fully inside any room if she could help it — and Elodie turned and held out her hand without deciding to. Jude crossed the floor and took it. Her palm was wrecked, bandaged in strips of shirt, and she let Elodie hold it anyway.

"This is Jude Blackwood," Elodie said. "She hauled your archive out of a sandbar and up a glacier because I asked her to. Before I asked her to, she had every reason to throw it back in the river."

Marta rose, joints cracking, and studied Jude the way she'd probably studied proofs — looking for errors, finding none worth mentioning. "Captain," she said. "There's a bed upstairs and a stove that works. In this house, that's the highest honor we confer."

* * *

**JUDE**

Jude slept four hours and woke before dawn to the sound of the press being prepared.

She came downstairs into lamplight. The chest stood open on the composing table, and around it worked six people who must have come in the night — an old man sorting photographs into rows, a young woman with a magnifying loupe reading microfilm-fine handwriting, two typesetters already pulling letters from their cases. Nobody spoke much. The room had the focused silence of an engine room, and Jude recognized it immediately, felt it in her chest: the sound of people doing the one thing they were built to do.

Elodie stood at the table with a manifest in both hands. She'd been crying, recently or not at all — her face had gone past crying into something flatter and more permanent. When she saw Jude, she held up the page.

"This is the order for the second convoy," she said. "Dated nine days before the town was emptied. It lists the number of trucks, the number of guards, and the number of— " She stopped. Started again, quieter. "It lists how many people the trucks were rated to carry. And then it lists, separately, how many people were actually loaded. There's a column for the difference."

Jude took the page. Read it. Read it twice, the way she'd read everything in that chest below decks during the monsoon, because some things don't go in on the first pass.

The signature at the bottom belonged to a man who currently held a ministry.

"It'll hold up?" she asked. "In court, in print, wherever it goes?"

"It's original. It's sealed. It's countersigned twice." Elodie tapped the signature. "And there are forty-one more like it. They didn't just do it, Jude. They filed it. Bureaucracies always file."

The typesetters began on the headline first. Jude watched the letters go into the frame backward, mirror-world words she couldn't read until Marta pulled the proof and pressed it damp into Elodie's hands. Elodie read it aloud, and her voice broke on the last word, and the old man sorting photographs put his hand briefly on her shoulder and went back to work.

**VASKARIA LIVED. HERE IS THE RECORD. HERE ARE THE NAMES.**

Outside, full daylight. And crossing the square toward the press, coat buttoned to the throat, moving with the unhurried gait of a man who had decided there was nothing left to hurry for — the surveyor.

* * *

**ELODIE**

He came to the open door and stopped at the threshold, and Elodie understood why he stopped there: the threshold was the line. Beyond it he was nobody, a private citizen in a foreign valley, and everyone in the room knew it, and he knew they knew it.

He looked older than he had at Graywater Bend. The journey had cost him something visible. His shoes were ruined. He carried no briefcase, no papers, no weapon — his empty hands hung at his sides like tools returned to a wall.

"Miss Vasseur," he said. "Captain Blackwood."

Nobody answered. Marta kept setting type with a rhythm that said, clearly, *you are furniture.*

"I'm not here to threaten anyone," the surveyor said. "I couldn't if I wanted to. My office ends at the pass, and the pass closed behind me yesterday afternoon." He looked at the chest, at the open lid, at the rows of photographs. Something moved behind his face — Elodie watched for the calculation she expected and found, instead, something tired. "I read the proofs drying on your line outside. All of them."

"Then you know what happens next," Elodie said.

"I've known what happens next for twenty years." He said it plainly, without drama, and somehow that was worse. "I was a junior clerk when the archive was seized. I processed the requisition order myself. I told myself it was administrative. For twenty years I told myself that." He turned his gaze to Jude. "That's why I offered you triple value. Not because the office wanted the chest destroyed. Because I did. Because if it disappeared one more time, I could keep being a man who processed paperwork."

"And now?" Jude asked. Her voice was level. Elodie heard what lived under the levelness and marveled at it.

"Now I'm a man standing in a printing house watching his own government's signature go onto a front page in twelve-point serif." He almost smiled. It wasn't a smile. "There will be inquiries. There will be resignations, maybe trials, maybe nothing — governments are good at surviving their own confessions. But the names will be out. The record will exist. You can't unprint a thing once the wire takes it." He looked at the press, gleaming under the lamps. "My office sent me to retrieve stolen property. What I'm actually doing is witnessing. Someone from that world should see this done properly. It might as well be me."

He reached into his coat, and the whole room tensed — Jude's hand went to her hip out of twenty years of habit — but he drew out only a folded sheet of paper and set it on the nearest table.

"The requisition order. Original. My copy. Countersigned." He straightened his ruined cuffs. "It adds a signature yours doesn't have. The deputy minister's. Consider it my testimony, in the only form I know how to give it."

Then he turned and walked out into the square, and sat down on the bench by the fountain, and waited — for the presses, for the wire, for whatever came over the pass next — like a man keeping vigil at a grave he had helped dig.

* * *

**JUDE**

They pulled the first edition at dusk.

Marta gave Elodie the honor of the lever, and Jude stood close enough to feel the machine shudder through the floorboards, through her boots, through the bones the glacier hadn't managed to break. The sheet came out damp and black and true. Elodie lifted it overhead with both arms like a woman raising a child, and the room — the whole room, strangers and exiles and one river captain who'd spent ten years making sure no one ever learned her name — cheered until the windows rattled.

By midnight the wire office had taken the text. By morning, Marta promised, it would be in eleven papers across three borders, and there was nothing on earth that could call it back.

Jude stepped outside into the cold. The surveyor was still on his bench. He nodded to her, and she nodded back, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing left between them that words could carry.

Elodie came out and stood beside her, the first copy still in her hands, ink smudged along her forearms like war paint.

"They're going to want statements," she said. "Inquiries. Witnesses. People who touched the record, who can swear to where it came from." She paused. "Your name will be in all of it. *Recovered by Captain Jude Blackwood.* It's already set in type. I checked."

Jude looked out at the square — at the flags of dead nations snapping in the wind, at the surveyor on his bench, at the lit window of the press where the type cases waited for tomorrow's edition.

For ten years she had measured her life in miles precisely so it would never add up to anything. Now her name was in print, attached to the truth, permanent as a watermark. She waited to feel the old panic rise, the animal need to run for the water.

It didn't come.

"There's a ferry office two doors down," she said. "Saw it when we came in. Runs the upper valley routes." She glanced sideways at Elodie. "Come look at it with me in the morning. After the second edition."

Elodie smiled — tired, ink-stained, real. "What are we looking at?"

"A boat," Jude said. "One with a name on it."

Down in the square, the fountain froze a little more, and the flags snapped, and somewhere beyond the pass the river they'd come up was already carrying the news downstream, mile by mile, to every town that had forgotten how to say the word *Vaskaria* — and would, by week's end, remember.
