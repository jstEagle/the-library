## 20. Witnesses

The printing house smelled of ink and hot metal and forty years of other people's patience.

Elodie stood in the composing room with her sleeves rolled and her hands still shaking, watching the linotype operator set the first paragraph of the Vaskaria Provision — *that* was what they were calling it here, the Provision, the way you'd name a storm or a war — in type small enough to deny and large enough to be read. The machine clattered like a river in flood. Behind her, three men she'd known for less than a day were arguing in two languages about the order of the photographs, and above them all, pinned to a corkboard yellowed with age, hung a page pulled from a press twenty years ago and never followed. The headline was a single word: *WHERE?*

Underneath it, in smaller type: *The nation of Vaskaria does not appear on any map published after the spring of the year in question. This newspaper intends to find out why.*

It had been the last thing the paper printed before the soldiers came for the presses. The editor had gone into exile with a single drawer of correspondence and a grudge he had fed like a stove fire every morning of every year since. His name was Anton Brek. He was seventy-one years old, he had hands like tree roots, and when Elodie had carried the chest down from the pass and set it on his floor, he had knelt beside it and put his palm flat on the lead lining the way you'd touch the flank of a horse you thought might be dying.

"Twenty years," he'd said. Not to her. To the chest. To the dead.

Now the chest stood open in the corner of the composing room, and the dead were being typeset.

Elodie had not slept. Nobody had. The documents had been photographed, catalogued, cross-referenced against Brek's drawer of correspondence until three in the morning, and at dawn the first dispatches had gone out over the wire — coded, fragmented, sent through three relay stations because no one trusted any single line. The manifest of the transport *Cormorant*. The requisition orders for the valley road that was never a road. The photographs, grain and silver and horror, of the column of people walking east out of the valley in the rain, and the second photograph, taken from a ridge, of the same valley the following week, empty, and the third — Elodie had only been able to look at the third once — of the lime kilns burning.

And the signatures. That was the part that would not stop moving in her chest. Four signatures. Two of the men who had signed the orders were dead, which was mercy of a kind. Two were not. One sat in a ministry in the capital. One chaired the commission that had spent two decades officially determining that no such massacre had ever occurred, that Vaskaria had been a cartographic error and its people a statistical rounding, dispersed voluntarily, absorbed without incident.

By noon, the wire services in three countries had the story. By evening, it was in the streets.

* * *

Elodie heard it before she saw it — the sound of a town reading aloud.

She had gone out to buy bread because Brek's housekeeper insisted that witnesses who fainted were no use to anyone, and she came out of the bakery into a street where men stood in doorways with newspapers held at arm's length, reading to each other in the low, carrying voices people used for scripture. A woman sat on a bench with the paper folded to the photographs, and she was not crying, she was simply very still, the way Elodie's mother had gone still, once, when a letter came. An old man in the doorway of the cobbler's shop read the list of names — Brek had insisted the list run in full, all four hundred and eleven, even though it ate an entire page of advertising revenue — and his voice cracked on the name Vasseur, and Elodie stopped walking, because it was her name, her family's name, spoken by a stranger in a street in a country she'd never lived in, and it sounded like a bell.

They knew. That was the thing she had not been prepared for. She had carried the truth up a river and over a glacier and she had imagined, in the way you imagine things at three in the morning in a leaking boat, the moment of delivery as a kind of ending — a chest set down, a debt discharged. She had not imagined that delivery was a beginning. That the truth, once loose, would behave like water: finding every channel, rising in every low place, impossible to hold and impossible to refuse.

By the second day, the ministry man had resigned — *cited health, sources say* — and the commission chairman had gone on the radio to call the documents forgeries, and by the third day two of his own commissioners had resigned rather than stand behind him, and the inquiry was announced. Not a whitewash, not yet — Brek had made sure the wire copy named the inquiry's mandate in full, so that any narrowing of it would itself be news. *The Commission of Inquiry into the Disposition of the Vaskarian Population, 19— to 19—.* The dates were the accusation. The dates could not be unwritten.

"They'll try to bury it," Brek said, on the fourth evening, in the composing room. He was cleaning his glasses with a cloth that had once been white. "Slow it down. Starve it. Appoint a chairman who needs a year just to review the paperwork. Inquiries are how governments apologize without saying sorry." He set the glasses back on his nose and looked at her over them. "Which is why the record matters more than the headline. Headlines fade. Records are what the next generation digs up when they want to know what their grandparents did. We are not building a newspaper article, Miss Vasseur. We are building the thing that outlives everyone in this room."

He wanted her for it. That was the offer, made without ceremony: a desk, a salary drawn from exile subscriptions and one suspiciously generous anonymous donor, and the work of a lifetime — verifying, annotating, contextualizing, building the archive that would make the massacre undeniable forever. The Vaskarian Collection. Her family's confiscated archive, returned to the light, and her hands on it.

"I'm not asking you to decide tonight," Brek said. "I've waited twenty years. I can wait a week."

* * *

She found Jude on the roof.

The printing house had a flat tar roof with a low parapet, and the border town fell away below it in tiers of red tile and washing lines, down to the river — a different river now, wide and slow and foreign, the far side of the pass. Jude sat against the parapet with her hat off, which still startled Elodie a little; Jude Blackwood without her hat was like a river without its current, something essential briefly set aside. Her face was turned toward the mountains, where the glacier was a white seam between dark shoulders, already closing, already forgetting them.

"You're famous," Elodie said, sitting down beside her.

Jude made a sound that was almost a laugh. "Am I."

"There's a photograph of you in this morning's edition. *The captain who ran the cordon.* Brek ran it under the story about the gunboats. You look like a criminal in it. It's wonderful."

"I look like a woman who lost her boat."

"You look like a woman who saved four hundred and eleven names." Elodie pulled the folded newspaper from under her arm and opened it across both their knees. The photograph was bad — taken from the far bank at Meridian Flats, apparently, during the patrol's visit, Jude mid-stride on her own deck, sharp-eyed and unsmiling. Beneath it, the caption named her. Not *a captain*. Not *an unidentified smuggler*. *Jude Blackwood, of Graywater Bend.*

Jude looked at it for a long time. Her jaw did the thing it did when she was keeping her voice level.

"Twenty-two years," she said. "I built twenty-two years on nobody knowing that name. I changed rivers twice. I paid a man in Corvess to misfile my license. I let my own mother die thinking I was dead rather than write to her, because a letter is a thread and a thread can be pulled." She said it flatly, without drama, the way she said everything that mattered. "And now some photographer on a hillside has undone it in one exposure."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. That's the strange part." She turned the paper over, studying the back of it as if the answer were printed there. "I keep waiting to feel the way I always thought I'd feel. Exposed. Hunted." She shook her head slowly. "I feel — lighter. Which frightens me more than being hunted would. Because I don't know yet what it costs."

The sun was going down behind the pass. The light came up the valley from below, which still disoriented Elodie, and it turned the glacier pink, and it lit the side of Jude's face, the scar at her hairline that she'd gotten on the fence line, the gray coming in at her temples that Elodie had been pretending not to notice for weeks and had stopped pretending about two nights ago, on this same roof, with rain coming — no, that had been Meridian Flats. Everything important seemed to have happened in rain.

"The inquiry opens in the spring," Elodie said. "Brek's sending a delegation to testify. And he's asked me—" She stopped. Started again, plainer. "He's asked me to build the archive. The permanent record. The Vaskarian Collection. It's a ten-year job if it's a hundred. It's the work I was born for, Jude. I mean that literally. I was raised on the inventory lists my grandmother memorized before the soldiers came. I know the collection numbers of documents I've never seen. And now I've seen them." Her voice caught and she let it. "I'm going to say yes."

"I know," Jude said. "You should."

Elodie looked at her. "I'm not asking your permission."

"I know that too."

"I'm asking you to stay."

Jude went very still. Out below them, a church bell rang the hour, and somewhere a dog answered it, and the washing lines snapped in the evening wind, and Elodie sat inside all of it watching the woman she — watching Jude, and waiting, because she had learned on this river that Jude Blackwood could not be hurried through a door. She could only be given the door and the time to walk through it.

"Stay," Elodie said again. "Not for me. Or — yes, for me, I'll say that plainly too, because we're done with the other way of doing things. But also for the work. You read those documents twice, Jude. You know them better than half the delegation will. You know the river, the crossings, the valley roads — you can read that country the way I can read a ledger. Brek needs a witness who was a stranger to all of it and believed it anyway. That's worth more than another grieving descendant. Grieving descendants can be dismissed as partial." She smiled, small and crooked. "Believe me. I've read the government's old statements. They were very thorough about my partiality."

"And me?" Jude said. "What am I, now that my face is in the paper?"

"Known," Elodie said. "That's all. Known isn't guilty and it isn't hunted. It's just — no longer invisible. And I know what invisibility was for you. I know it was a religion. But Jude—" She reached over and took the newspaper out of Jude's hands, folded it, set it aside, so that there was nothing between them but evening air. "You told me once, on the boat, that you measured your life in miles because miles don't ask anything of you. A mile doesn't want to know your name. But you're not on the river anymore. The boat is dead at the bottom of the shallows below the pass, and the miles are behind you, and for the first time in twenty-two years you've *arrived* somewhere. The only question left is whether you'll be here when you're here. Whether you'll let yourself be a person in a place, with a name in a registry and a face in a paper and a — with me. Or whether you'll find another river to disappear down, and spend the rest of your life being a rumor to the one person who knows all your true things."

Jude looked at her for a long moment. Then out at the glacier. Then at her own hands, which had hauled a chest of the dead out of a sandbar and up a mountain and had never once, in all that time, shaken.

"It's not that simple," she said, but her voice had gone rough, and they both knew the words were a stalling tide, not a refusal.

"Nothing on this river was simple either," Elodie said. "You ran it anyway."

* * *

Jude did not answer that night. Elodie had not expected her to.

She did not answer the next day, either, and Elodie kept to her side of the unspoken bargain — she went down to the composing room and said yes to Brek and began, with hands that trembled and then steadied, the work of the rest of her life. She built the first shelf of the Vaskarian Collection with her own screws and her own brackets, in a back room of the printing house with a window that faced the mountains. She numbered the first document herself: the manifest of the *Cormorant*, in her grandmother's hand, recovered from a sandbar by a woman who had wanted to throw it back.

On the third evening she came up to the roof with two cups of tea and found it empty, and something went cold in her that had nothing to do with the wind.

But the hat was there. Jude's hat, set on the parapet, weighted with a stone against the wind, which meant she had meant to come back, which meant she was somewhere below, in the town, in the open, among people who had read her name in the paper — walking around *known*, on purpose, or at least not on accident.

Elodie found her at the bottom of the hill, at the ferry landing.

The town's ferry ran the wide foreign river twice a day, a flat barge with a diesel engine and a rope ferryman named Oskar who had lost two sons to a war Elodie had never learned the borders of. Jude stood at the landing's edge talking to him, hatless, in full view of the evening crowd, and as Elodie came down the steps she heard the tail of it — Oskar saying something about the spring thaw, about the pass road reopening, about the ferry route that would run again for the first time in years — and Jude saying, in the voice she used for the true things:

"I'd want to see her papers first. Any boat I stand on, I see her papers."

Oskar laughed, a big broken sound, and clapped Jude on the shoulder like they'd been friends for twenty years instead of three minutes, and turned, and saw Elodie, and touched his cap and left them alone at the landing with the water sliding past, brown and patient, going somewhere neither of them had ever been.

"You're not leaving," Elodie said. It came out as a question anyway.

"No," Jude said. She picked up a stone from the landing, weighed it, skipped it — badly, three hops — out across the water. "I was asking about work. He says the pass road opens in spring. First ferry run in six years. He needs a licensed captain and he doesn't care that mine's misfiled in Corvess under a name I don't use anymore." She looked sideways at Elodie, and there was something in her face Elodie had never seen there before, or had seen only once, in the rain, at Meridian Flats, the night they'd made the provision. It looked like a woman standing in a doorway she'd spent two decades nailing shut, with the nails finally out and the door open and the wind coming through.

"I told him I'd think about it," Jude said. "But I'm not going to lie to you, Elodie. I stopped being able to do that somewhere on the glacier. I'm not thinking about it. I'm going to do it. A boat with a name on it. My name on a registry, where the government can read it, where anyone can read it." She turned to face the water. "Twenty-two years of being no one, and it turns out what I want is to be *someone*, somewhere specific, with a route and a schedule and a—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "With a reason to come home."

The word *home* sat in the air between them like a bird that had landed and might yet fly.

"And the archive?" Elodie asked. "Brek's work. The collection. The ten-year job."

"I'll carry boxes," Jude said. "I'll testify, if they'll have me. I'll read every page, because someone should read them who isn't grieving and isn't guilty — someone who just came to believe." She looked at Elodie then, fully, and the evening light came up the valley and caught her face and did not let it go. "But I'm a river woman, Elodie. I'm not going to be any good at a desk. I'm going to be good at the thing I'm for. And what I'm for, it turns out—" she gestured, at the water, at the town, at the whole visible length of her own life, "—is showing up. In daylight. Under my own name. On a route people can count on."

Elodie set the two cups of tea down on the landing's edge — she had been carrying them the whole way down without knowing why, without letting herself know why — and Jude took one, and their hands touched around the tin, and neither of them moved away.

"The provision holds," Elodie said. It wasn't a question either.

"The provision holds," Jude said. And then, because she was Jude, because she would be Jude until she died, she nodded at the far bank, at the water, at the whole unbuilt future. "But I want it in the registry. Both names. I'm done with arrangements that only exist because two people were afraid to write them down."

Elodie laughed — the first real laugh since the kiln photographs — and the sound went out over the water, and Oskar, up at the ferry house, heard it and smiled without knowing why.

They drank their tea at the landing while the light failed, two women with a dead boat behind them and a live river in front of them, and above the town the glacier went from pink to gray to dark, closing over the pass for the winter, keeping its own counsel until spring.

It was Oskar who came down the steps at full dark with a lamp and a newspaper and an expression Elodie would remember for the rest of her life.

"You'll want to see this," he said, and held the paper out to Jude, to *Jude Blackwood*, by name, in front of God and the river and anyone. "It's the capital edition. The inquiry — they've announced the chairman. And captain, they've announced the hearings." He tapped the page with a blunt finger. "They're going to hold the first session in the valley. *In the valley itself.* And the government — they've posted a general amnesty for all persons assisting in the recovery of evidence." He looked up at her, and the lamplight made his eyes very bright. "Which means, captain, that the men who wanted that chest at the bottom of the river are now legally required to let you bring it in."

Jude took the paper. Elodie watched her read it — watched her face do the thing it did, level and still, while everything underneath moved like current — and when she looked up, she wasn't looking at the newspaper at all.

She was looking upriver. Toward the pass. Toward the valley.

"They're not letting me bring it in," Jude said quietly. "They're summoning me. There's a difference." She folded the paper once, crisp, the way you fold a chart you're about to navigate by. "And there's someone on that list of witnesses I haven't seen in twenty-two years."

Elodie felt the cold come back, the river cold, the premonition cold.

"Who?" she asked.

Jude handed her the paper, and pointed, and said a name.
