## 21. A New Route

The registry office in Corren's Landing smelled of ink and river damp, the way all government buildings on this stretch of water smelled — like the river had gotten inside everything and refused to leave. Jude stood at the counter with a pen in her hand and a form in front of her, and for the first time in ten years, her hand was steady.

The clerk, a narrow man with spectacles fogged from the heat of the room, looked at the name she'd written and then looked at her.

"Blackwood," he said. "Like in the papers."

"Like in the papers."

"You understand that's a matter of public record now. The registry. Anyone can come in and read it."

Jude set the pen down. Outside the window, the pass road was visible where it came down off the mountain in a series of switchbacks, ending at the new dock the government had thrown up in six weeks of frantic construction — the same government that had spent twenty years pretending the pass didn't exist, that the road beyond it didn't exist, that the nation it had helped erase had never existed at all. The glacier had given them a corridor and the newspapers had given them a reckoning, and now there was a road, and a dock, and a line of surveyors' stakes marching along the bank like stitches in a wound.

"That's the idea," Jude said.

The clerk wrote something in the margin of her form. "There's a woman outside asking for you. Says she's your partner."

Jude's chest did something complicated. "Business partner?"

"She didn't specify."

* * *

Elodie was waiting under the awning of the chandlery next door, a ledger open on her knee, her hair tied back in the way she tied it when she was working — the way that meant she'd already been awake for hours and had opinions ready. She looked up when Jude came out, and there it was again, that look that had started somewhere in the rain at Meridian Flats and had never quite gone away: the look of a woman checking whether something she'd decided to trust was still there.

"Well?" she said.

"She took it. The share. Pending survey, pending registration, pending the usual theft." Jude sat down on the bench beside her. "The *Vasseur*."

Elodie closed her eyes briefly. "You let me name it."

"You earned it."

"I was going to say we should argue about it properly. It's tradition. We argue about everything else."

"We argued about it for three weeks."

"Three weeks is nothing. We argued about the engine for two months." Elodie opened the ledger again, and Jude saw her own handwriting in it, and Elodie's, side by side in the columns — fuel costs, cargo manifests, the ferry schedule they'd drafted and redrafted at the table in the exile boarding house while rain came down outside and the printing presses next door ran through the night. "The first run is in nine days. The road crew says the pass road is passable as far as the switchbacks, but the water above the calving corridor is still running high and brown. The pilot they've assigned to escort us has never run it."

"Then we run it first and he follows."

"He's government."

"He's government *now*," Jude said. "Six months ago he'd have been the man burning the archive. Things change. That's rather the point of everything we did."

Elodie looked at her sideways. "You've changed."

"I've been changed. There's a difference. The first one you choose."

* * *

The boat was not the *Marguerite*. Nothing would be the *Marguerite*, which lay in the far shallows below the pass with her engine drowned and her hull stove, a landmark now — the boy from Meridian Flats had told them that pilots used her to mark the channel, *bear left at the dead boat*. Jude had gone back once, in the autumn, with a salvage crew, and stood on the bank while they stripped what was worth taking, and had not cried until the engine came out on a barge and she saw the raw wound of the engine bed, the bolts she'd tightened with her own hands ten thousand times, and understood that she was watching a burial.

The ferry was a government surplus packet boat, forty feet, shallow draft, twin screws, built for patrol work and converted badly for passengers. She was ugly. She was sound. Her previous name had been painted over so many times it showed through in ghost letters on the transom — *VIGILANT*, maybe, or *VIGILANCE* — and Jude had stood on the dock the day they bought the share, looking at those ghost letters, thinking: that was the old life. The name under the name. The thing you were before you painted over it.

They'd sanded the transom down to bare wood and painted *VASSEUR* in white letters four inches high, and Elodie had wept at the sight of it, and Jude had pretended not to notice, and then had put her arm around her anyway, because pretending had stopped working between them somewhere in the monsoon and had never started again.

* * *

The trouble with being known, Jude discovered, was that people kept knowing you.

The chandler knew her. The harbormaster knew her. A woman selling oranges on the dock pressed a free one into her hand and said, "You're the one who ran the corridor," in the tone people used for saints and fools, and Jude took the orange and said, "I'm the one who had a good boat and a bad plan," and the woman laughed like that was the best thing she'd heard all year.

A newspaperman from the coast wanted an interview. A church group wanted her to speak. A man who claimed to represent the Vaskarian Restoration Committee wanted her to lend her name to a petition, and Elodie, reading his credentials over dinner, found three forgeries in his letters of introduction and had him escorted off the dock by the harbormaster, who was very pleased to be of service to Captain Blackwood, whom he had read all about.

"It doesn't stop," Jude said that night, on the ferry's afterdeck, watching the lights of Corren's Landing smear themselves in the water. "The knowing. I spent ten years making sure nobody knew my name and now I can't buy rope without someone telling me their cousin was in the resistance."

"Does it hurt?"

Jude considered it honestly, the way Elodie had taught her to consider things — all the way down, no shortcuts. "It's like a limb waking up. Pins and needles. Hurts and doesn't, both at once."

Elodie leaned into her shoulder. "My grandmother used to say that a name is just a debt you agree to answer to. She signed hers on everything. Letters, receipts, the inside of books. She said if they came for her, she wanted them to know exactly whose books they were burning."

"She sounds like she'd have liked you."

"She'd have hated you for six months and then never let you leave." Elodie tilted her head back. "Jude. The registry. You put your name on a government document. Your real name, your real address — the boarding house, my name as co-owner. Do you understand what you did?"

"I bought a boat."

"You testified. That's what a registry is. It's a document that says *I exist, I am responsible, come and find me.*" Her voice was quiet. "The last time you signed anything real, you were signing on with a river company under a name that wasn't yours."

"The last time I signed anything real," Jude said, "I was running from something. This time I'm running toward it. It's a different direction. It matters which way the boat points."

* * *

Three days before the first run, the surveyor came to the dock.

Jude saw him from fifty yards and her body knew him before her mind did — the grey coat, the government walk, the way he stood with his weight on his heels like a man who expected the ground to hold him without being asked. He'd lost weight since Meridian Flats. The inquiries had that effect on men like him; she'd read his testimony in the third week of the hearings, reprinted in full in the exile press, watched him dissemble and correct and finally, under the weight of his own signatures, confess to the arrangement that had bought her anonymity — the false papers, the quiet transfer upriver, the decade of *Captain Blackwood, no fixed address* that had been, all along, a leash with a very long rope.

"Captain." He stopped a respectful distance from the gangway. "I'm not here officially."

"You're never here officially. It's your best quality."

He almost smiled. "The ferry license. There was a question at the ministry about whether it should be granted, given your — history. I argued for it."

Jude waited. Men like him always had more.

"I argued for it," he said, "because you're going to run that corridor forty times a season, and every time you do, people are going to look at you and remember what the government did. You're more useful to the truth as a ferry captain than you ever were as a fugitive. I told them that. They didn't enjoy hearing it."

"And you came down here to tell me why?"

"I came down here," he said, "to tell you that I've been reassigned. To the pass road administration. I'll be processing cargo manifests at the switchback station." He looked at the ferry, at the fresh white letters on the transom, and something moved behind his face that might have been envy or might have been grief. "I spent twenty years making sure things stayed off the record. Now I'll spend the rest of my career making sure they stay on it. It seemed right that you should know. You of all people."

He put out his hand. Jude looked at it for a long moment — the hand that had offered her amnesty for the chest, that had spoken her true name on a dock in Graywater Bend like a man laying down a card — and then she took it, once, briefly, and let go.

"Manifests," she said. "Make sure the columns add up. That's all anyone ever had to do."

* * *

The night before the first run, they loaded the ferry by lantern light: mail sacks, medicine crates, a printing press's worth of the new Vaskarian reader for the school at the far settlement, and — lashed under tarpaulin in the forward hold, where the chest had ridden on the *Marguerite* — a steel document case, government-stamped, containing certified copies of the archive, going upriver to the new archive station at the pass. Elodie had argued for weeks about whether copies should travel by water at all. In the end she'd decided they should. "The river kept what it was given," she'd said. "It can keep this too, for a few hours."

They walked the deck together after the crew went ashore, checking lines by habit, and stopped at the transom, where the lantern light caught the letters.

"Say it," Elodie said.

"Say what?"

"The name. Out loud. You've been avoiding it."

Jude looked at the letters. *VASSEUR.* Her hand on the rail beside Elodie's hand on the rail. "Vasseur," she said, and it came out steady. "The *Vasseur*, out of Corren's Landing, bound for the pass, first run, master Jude Blackwood, mate Elodie Vasseur." She paused. "That's the first time I've said the whole thing."

"And?"

"And it sounds like a debt I'm glad to answer to."

Elodie laughed — the real laugh, the one from the rain — and turned to go below, and then stopped, because there was a boy standing at the foot of the gangway with a lantern and an envelope, dripping river water, having clearly run the whole length of the dock.

"Captain Blackwood?" he said. "Dispatch from the pass station. They said it was urgent. They said you'd want it tonight."

Jude took the envelope. The seal was the new one — the provisional administration's, ink still smearing on the cheap paper — and inside, in a clerk's hurried hand, was a single paragraph: the escort pilot assigned to their first run had been withdrawn; the pass road above the corridor had slipped in the night melt; and the ministry, citing safety, was closing the route until further survey.

Further survey. Jude had been on this river long enough to know what *further survey* meant, and who decided when it ended, and that somewhere in a ministry office a man who had lost the hearings was reading the same weather reports and finding, in a closed pass, a very convenient kind of silence.

Elodie read it over her shoulder. The lantern hissed. Downriver, the water ran high and brown, the way it had the week the glacier opened.

"Well," Elodie said. "How much survey do you need?"

"None," Jude said, and went to wake the engineer.
