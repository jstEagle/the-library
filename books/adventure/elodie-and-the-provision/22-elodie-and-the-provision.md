## 22. Elodie and the Provision

### Jude

The river had changed its mind about everything in a year, and Jude had learned to read it the way she'd once read her own silence — closely, and with respect.

The *Provision* sat low in the water that morning, riding heavy with newsprint and flour, lamp oil and school slates, and one sealed crate of books that Elodie guarded the way other people guarded children. The name on her stern was fresh white paint over the old hull — a boat with a name on it, and a registry with two names on it, and Jude had signed hers without flinching, which still felt like the bravest thing she'd done in a decade of brave things.

"You're staring at the water like it owes you money," Elodie said, coming up the companionway with two cups of coffee and her hair already losing its war against the humidity.

"It owes me a channel." Jude took the cup. "The sandbar at Kettle Reach moved again. The flood in March redrew the whole bend."

"You say that like it's a betrayal."

"Isn't it?"

Elodie laughed and leaned against the rail beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and the *Provision* carried them both upriver through the morning mist toward towns that were only now, a year after the wire services lit up with the Vaskaria archive, learning the shape of what had been done to the people who used to live along these banks.

Graywater Bend was first. It was always first — the trading post where a woman with a ledger had once boarded a stranger's boat and refused to get off. The post had grown since: a new roof on the warehouse, a schoolhouse going up on the rise, and nailed to the post office door, a printed broadsheet with the old seal of Vaskaria and beneath it, in three languages, the words *WE REMEMBER BY NAME.*

"They hung it crooked," Elodie said, but she was smiling, and her eyes were doing the thing they did when she was cataloguing grief and joy in the same breath.

"Leave it crooked. It's honest."

They unloaded flour and ink and four crates of the new printing — the full annotated archive, twenty years of confiscated manifests and photographs and orders, now with margins full of names supplied by survivors. The schoolteacher at Graywater Bend, a round-faced man who'd lost an uncle to the purges and hadn't known it until last spring, pressed a bag of dried apples on Elodie and wouldn't take no.

"Captain," he said to Jude, and the word still landed strangely, warmly, like a coat that fit after years of wearing rags. "You'll come back through in the autumn?"

"Every month the river lets us," Jude said.

It was the answer she'd have flinched from a year ago. A promise. A schedule. A route with her name attached to it, legible to anyone who cared to look. She had spent ten years measuring her life in miles precisely so that it would never add up to a place, a person, a debt. Now she measured it in deliveries, and the arithmetic came out different every time, and she had stopped being afraid of the total.

They cast off at noon. Behind them, the broadsheet flapped crooked on its nail, honest as a scar.

* * *

### Elodie

The boy from Meridian Flats was waiting at the third bend, as promised, poling a skiff out of the willows with that unhurried competence that had once, in a night of rain and searchlights, saved all their lives.

"Tomas." Elodie threw him the line before he asked. "You've grown."

"Everyone says that." He was fourteen now, and he said it the way he said everything, as if the world were a river he'd already memorized. "There's a new family at the Flats. Took the old ferryman's house. They want to know if you'll bring the schoolbooks next run. The ones about before."

"About before," Elodie repeated. That was what the children called it now — *before* — as if the erasure of a nation had been nothing more than a chapter break, and all they wanted was the pages that had been torn out. In a way, she supposed, that was exactly what it had been, and exactly what she carried.

She had spent the year learning that an archive was not a thing you delivered. It was a thing you *distributed*, the way a river distributes water — into every channel that would take it, into ground that would hold it, into roots. The printing house over the pass sent out crates and she sent them further, and in every town there was someone who took the books the way Tomas took the line: without asking, because they'd been waiting.

"How's your mother?" she asked.

"Better. She reads now. Out loud, at supper." Tomas said it simply, but Elodie heard the whole of it — a woman who had spent twenty years not speaking of Vaskaria, reading the names of the dead to her son over bread. "She says to tell you the provision still holds."

Elodie's hands went still on the line.

The provision. The promise they'd made each other in the rain at Meridian Flats, in the worst week of both their lives, when the hull was open and the patrol was downstream and Elodie had just confessed that she'd been sent, that the whole meeting at Graywater Bend had been an operation, that Jude Blackwood had been a means to an end from the first moment Elodie stepped aboard with her ledger and her lies.

*I will not lie to you again, even to protect you,* Elodie had said, rain hammering the tarp, the drowned town listening around them.

*And I will not leave you, even when I want to,* Jude had answered. *Even when it costs.*

That was the provision. Not grand. Not legal. Just two people agreeing, in the wet dark, to be exactly what they said they were.

"Tell her it holds," Elodie said.

Tomas poled away into the willows, and the *Provision* — the boat, named for the promise, because Jude had stood in the registry office and said *what do you call a thing that keeps you honest* and Elodie had said *I don't know, what* and Jude had said *apparently this* — carried on upriver toward the mountains.

* * *

### Jude

The high route was open again.

The glacier calved on schedule, the way it had for a thousand years and would for a thousand more, indifferent to governments and archives and the small stubborn boats of two women who had once run its corridor with a dying engine and a chest full of truth. The pass road had reopened in the spring — a real road now, graded and bridged, built by a ministry that had fallen and a ministry that had replaced it and neither of which could quite bring themselves to undo what the newsprint had done. The gunboats were gone from the lower river. The surveyor — Jude still thought of him that way, though he'd resigned in the scandal's first week — had been seen once in Graywater Bend, buying rope like an ordinary man, and had tipped his hat to her across the street, and she had nodded back, and that was all the war either of them could afford.

Jude stood at the wheel and watched the white wall of the glacier slide by on the starboard side, and she did the thing she still did, every time, without deciding to: she checked on Elodie.

Elodie was in the bow, reading aloud.

She did this on the runs through the corridor — had done it since that first terrified passage, when she'd stood in the spray and read the names of the dead into the wind as if the ice itself were the last congregation. Now she read from the new printing, the survivor testimony, the children's letters. Her voice carried over the engine. The glacier groaned its long tectonic syllables, and between the two of them — the woman reading and the mountain answering — the air felt like a church where everyone present had earned their grief.

"Listen," Elodie called back, and read: *'My grandmother kept a bakery on the Meridian road. We have found the foundation. We are going to bake bread there on the anniversary. Everyone is welcome.'*

Jude's throat did what it did. She kept her eyes on the channel.

That was the other thing the year had taught her: the archive hadn't ended anything. It had *started* things — inquiries, resignations, trials, yes, but also bread baked on old foundations, schoolbooks about before, a crooked broadsheet on a post office door. Truth, it turned out, wasn't a verdict. It was a seed, and it took seasons, and you had to keep hauling water.

The *Provision* cleared the corridor by late afternoon and dropped into the wide brown water beyond the pass, where the border town of exiles had become, in one year, something none of them had dared to call it: a town again. The printing house had a new press. The ferry Jude owned a share in was moored at the landing, and the ferryman — her partner, a taciturn man who'd lost his own history to the same hands — raised two fingers off his cap in salute.

"Late," he said.

"River moved the bar at Kettle Reach."

"River's always moving something." He looked past her to Elodie coming down the gangway with the book crate balanced on her hip. "Supper's at eight. Both of you. Non-negotiable."

"Yes, sir," they said together, and looked at each other, and laughed.

* * *

### Elodie

Later, after supper, after the town's lamps came on one by one along the shore like a constellation deciding to exist, Elodie found Jude at the landing, sitting on the mooring bollard with her boots off and her feet nearly in the water, watching the current.

"You're doing the thing," Elodie said.

"What thing."

"The staring-at-the-water thing."

"It's a good water." Jude shifted to make room. Elodie sat. "A year ago I'd have told you I'd die on some unnamed stretch of river with no name on my hull and no name in any registry, and I'd have called that a life."

"And now?"

Jude was quiet a while. The river talked to itself against the pilings. Up the hill, someone was playing a fiddle, badly, with great commitment — a Vaskarian tune, Elodie realized, one of the ones they'd recovered from a survivor's memory and set down in print last winter, so that it existed outside one old woman's head for the first time in twenty years.

"Now I keep a schedule," Jude said. "I have a partner who yells at me about the bar at Kettle Reach. I have my name on a boat, and the boat has a name, and the name is a promise I made you in the rain." She turned, and in the lamplight her face was open in a way that still, after everything, could undo Elodie completely. "I used to think invisibility was safe. It was just a slower way of drowning."

Elodie took her hand. The provision, she thought. Not the words — the *practice* of it. A year of telling each other true things, even the expensive ones. A year of Elodie admitting, over caulking and engine grease, that she'd have used anyone to get the archive home, and Jude admitting she'd have done the same, and both of them discovering that love built on terms was stronger than love built on need, because terms could be kept.

"Next month," Elodie said, "the school at the Flats wants us to stay two days. Teach the teachers. There's a woman there who remembers the old ferry routes — she wants them written down before she loses them."

"Then we'll stay two days."

"And the month after, the printing house wants to send crates all the way to the headwaters. Past the pass. There are towns up there that have never heard the word Vaskaria at all."

"Then we'll go past the pass." Jude squeezed her hand. "You're not even asking if I want to."

"I don't have to ask. It's in the registry."

Jude laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind Elodie had needed a year to earn — and pulled her up off the bollard, and they walked back up the landing together as the fiddle found its tune and the river carried on doing what rivers do, which is everything: taking what it's given, keeping what matters, and delivering, in its own good time, everything else.

Behind them, the *Provision* rocked gently at her mooring, name bright in the lamplight, holding.
