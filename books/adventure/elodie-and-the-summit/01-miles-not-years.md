## 1. Miles, Not Years

The salt flats did not forgive, and they did not forget, but they were honest in a way people never managed to be. Xanthe Crane had learned that her first week out here, fifteen years ago, when the crust had cracked under her boot heel and shown her a seam of brine glittering like a vein of broken glass. The land kept its accounts open. Every track, every dropped scrap of wire, every careless campfire ring stayed written on it for months, sometimes years, waiting for someone patient enough to read.

She was patient enough. That was the whole of her reputation, if you could call it that: Crane, the ranger who read the flats like other men read letters from home.

Three days now she had been reading Casimir Voss's handwriting in the earth, and it was elegant handwriting, which annoyed her more than anything else about him. Most thieves left chaos behind them—scuffed circles where they'd second-guessed themselves, double-backs cut sloppy through the alkali, water caches buried shallow and marked with cairns too proud to be mistaken for geology. Voss left almost nothing. His stride was long and even, his rest stops brief, his camps pitched in the lee of salt hummocks where the wind erased what little he disturbed. He walked like a man who had done this before, many times, for people who paid well and forgave nothing.

He had stolen, by the reckoning of the waystation ledger at Dry Fork, a brass sextant, forty feet of silk line, and a sealed oilcloth packet belonging to a surveyor named Pell who had died two weeks after depositing it and whose heirs wanted nothing but the packet back. The sextant and the line Xanthe understood. Thieves needed tools. It was the packet that made her saddle sore and her thoughts loud.

Nobody stole a dead surveyor's sealed papers unless they knew what was in them. And nobody knew what was in them unless they'd already seen something worth seeing.

She rode at first light and again at last light, walking her mule through the white heat of midday because the glare hid her silhouette better than shade ever could. The flats stretched north in sheets of light so hard they looked solid, and beyond them the mesa country rose in bruised purple terraces, and beyond *that*—she knew every mile of it, had measured it with her own boots, had refused, for fifteen years, to measure anything about herself with the same care.

That was the trick of a life like hers. You kept your distances exact and your names approximate. You learned the depth of every well and the height of every pass and you let the wells and passes learn nothing about you. *Crane*, the waystation men called her, tipping their hats with the particular respect owed to someone useful and unknowable. She signed the ledgers *X. Crane* in a hand deliberately plainer than her real one. She owned one photograph of herself, taken at nineteen, and she had burned it eleven years ago and remembered, with shame, how little she had felt while watching it curl.

Fifteen years of miles. Not years. Years invited arithmetic, and arithmetic invited memory, and memory was the one country she had renounced citizenship in.

Voss broke pattern on the third evening.

She found it where the flats gave way to a field of wind-carved yardangs, those leaning stone fins like the prows of drowned ships frozen mid-sail. His tracks went in among them and did not come out the far side, which meant he had stopped, or hidden, or been stopped. She dismounted and led the mule in on foot, one hand on the revolver at her hip out of habit rather than expectation, and followed the faint scuff of his passage between the stone fins until the light went amber and then copper and then the particular violet that meant she had perhaps twenty minutes of usable day.

His camp sat in a hollow at the base of the largest yardang, sheltered on three sides, and it was empty.

Not abandoned recently, either. The fire ring held ash gone gray and rained-on, though rain out here was a rumor more than an event. A tin plate sat rinsed and upturned on a flat stone. His bedroll was gone, his water skins were gone, but he had left—and this was the thing that made the hair rise along her forearms—a neat stack of split wood, enough for one fire, arranged as if for a guest.

Or as if to say: *I know you'll find this. Sit down.*

"Damn you," she said quietly, to the hollow, to the man, to the fifteen years of instinct now telling her three different things at once.

Protocol said burn what you find, log what you can't, and ride for Dry Fork to wire the district office. Protocol said a ranger did not sit in a fugitive's camp reading a fugitive's papers; a ranger secured evidence. Protocol had kept her alive and invisible for fifteen years and she had never once resented it until this exact moment, standing in violet light over a stranger's tidy fire ring, feeling curiosity move through her like water finding a crack in a dam.

She sat down anyway.

The charts were in the fire ring itself, half-burned, tucked beneath the ash where the last flames had licked them and died. Someone—Voss—had set them alight and then smothered the fire deliberately, as though he'd changed his mind halfway through destroying them. Or as though he'd destroyed exactly enough.

She lifted the top sheet with two fingers. Heavy paper, good stock, the kind surveyors used when a client paid for permanence. The burning had taken its western half entirely, but what remained was drawn in a fine confident hand: contour lines, soundings, a notation system she recognized from coastal surveys and had no business recognizing out here, four hundred miles from any water that could float a rowboat.

Because it was a coastline.

A harbor, rendered in careful ink. Headlands. A channel marked with depth figures—*four fathoms, six fathoms, shoaling*—and a small precise anchor symbol in the lee of the largest headland. And along the margin, in a different hand, older, the ink browned with age, a single word repeated like a refrain:

*Meridian. Meridian. Meridian.*

Xanthe sat very still. Somewhere above her a yardang caught the last light and threw it back in a long blade of copper, and the shadow of the stone fin crept across the hollow toward her boots like a tide coming in.

There was no sea here. There had been no sea here in a hundred years—not since before the railroads, not since the great drying, when the old-timers' grandmothers had supposedly watched the water walk away south and leave the basin to salt and silence. The charts in her hands described a harbor that existed only in the way legends exist: in the stories the drovers told at night, about a ship that never docked anywhere, about caves along the northern escarpment that breathed twice a day, in and out, like something sleeping. About how the cave country charged admission, and the price was never money.

She had heard those stories as a child, in another life, in a house with salt air in its timbers. She had heard them from a voice—

She put the thought down the way you put down something hot.

But her hands didn't obey. Her hands turned the chart over, and there on the reverse, in pencil, in Voss's neat and elegant hand, was a list. Coordinates, six of them, marching north along the escarpment. And beside the fourth coordinate, a single annotation, underlined twice:

*Tide-dependent. Six windows remain this season.*

Six windows. She looked up at the darkening sky as if it might explain itself. The dry season would end when the autumn equinox tides came, and everyone knew the equinox tides ran high—but high against *what*, out here? High against what sea?

Against none. Unless the sea had not entirely left. Unless somewhere under the salt, under a hundred years of dust, something still answered the moon.

Xanthe became aware that she was smiling, and that frightened her more than the charts did. Fifteen years without smiling at a piece of evidence. Fifteen years of keeping the two halves of herself sewn shut with routine, and here was a thief she'd never met holding the scissors.

She should burn the charts. Finish what Voss started. Log the camp, note the coordinates in her report in the flattest language available, and let the district office decide, and by the time anyone acted the season would have turned and whatever window Voss had found would close forever, and she would go back to being exactly as invisible as she had made herself.

Instead she folded the charts along their surviving creases and put them inside her coat, against her ribs, where her heartbeat could worry them.

Then she saw the last thing.

Wedged under the tin plate, weighted by a stone, was a slip of paper. Not burned. Deliberately left, the way you leave a calling card, or bait. On it, in that same elegant hand, five words:

*You already know the way.*

She read it three times. The violet light failed to blue-black around her while she read it, and the cold came up out of the salt, and the first stars pricked through over the escarpment, and all she could think was that Casimir Voss had never once looked back at his own tracks in three days of crossing open ground—because he hadn't been running from her at all.

He had been leading her.

And the worst part, the part that made her stand up too fast and stand there breathing hard in the dark among the stone ships, was that some locked room deep in her chest—the one she'd sealed at nineteen with a burned photograph and a changed name—had heard those five words and answered, quietly, treacherously, like a bell:

*I do.*
