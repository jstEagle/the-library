## 3. Six Tides

Xanthe Crane did her best thinking with numbers.

Names were dangerous things—soft, warm, full of history—but numbers were honest. A number told you exactly how much time you had left and never pretended to be your friend. So she sat on the ridge above the flooded basin with her field ledger open on her knee and let the arithmetic do what grief could not: make the shape of the problem small enough to hold.

The anomalous tide had come in at dusk two days past, a swell that should not have been possible this late in the season, driven by some pressure far out in the drowned country where the old sea still remembered itself. The hydrologists in Copperline would call it a freak event. The salt-rangers had another word for it, older and less printable. Whatever it was, it had opened the cave, and the cave held the Meridian Star, and the Meridian Star held everything Xanthe had spent fifteen years pretending she did not want.

She licked her pencil and wrote.

*Dry season ends in nine days.* That was fixed; the almanac was never wrong about the retreat of the water. *Tidal windows remaining: six.* She had counted them against the basin's rim marks herself, walking the shoreline in the blue hour with a measuring line and a heart that would not slow down. Six more tides before the water withdrew for good and the cave sealed itself behind a wall of collapsing silt, as it had sealed once before, a century ago, around a ship that had no business being in a desert.

*Salvage fleet arrival: eight days.* Quill's tenders were slow, coal-hungry things, but they traveled with the confidence of money, and money did not worry about tides. Eight days meant the fleet would arrive after the sixth tide. On paper, the mathematics favored her.

On paper.

Xanthe looked down at the basin, where the impossible water lay flat and pewter-colored under the rising moon, holding its secret the way a closed fist holds a coin. Two days ago she had stood inside the cave with her lantern raised and seen the hull of the Meridian Star emerging from the salt like a spine from an opened back—ribs of blackened oak, copper sheathing gone green, the name still legible in flaking gilt along the stern. She had read it twice because the first time she had not believed her own eyes.

She had also seen, wedged in the rocks near the entrance, the fresh chisel-marks of someone who had gotten there before her. Casimir Voss. He had found it first—or tried to—and failed to open whatever he'd come to open, and fled when the tide began to return. His camp on the flats had told her that much: half-burned charts of a coastline that existed only in legend, drawn in a hand too fine for a thief, annotated with depths and dates in ink that had faded the way century-old ink fades.

Which meant Voss had not drawn those charts. He had stolen them from someone who had. And whoever drew them had known the cave would open.

Xanthe turned the page. Her handwriting grew smaller, tighter—the handwriting of a woman building a wall out of figures.

*Assets:* one ranger's commission, valid within the Salt Warden district only. One rifle, one mule named Bittern, one reputation for incorruptibility that had taken fifteen years to build and would take one signature to spend. *Liabilities:* one wreck of international registry, one thief at large, one salvage fleet under charter from a broker whose name made harbor clerks lower their voices.

Adhara Quill. Xanthe had never met the woman, but she had read the contracts that passed through the warden's office in Copperline, and you could learn a person from their contracts the way you could learn them from their graves. Quill's charters were clean, airtight, and merciless. They claimed salvage rights on anything the receding season exposed, and they contained a clause—clause eleven, always clause eleven—granting the broker authority to "resolve competing claims by any means consistent with port law." Port law. As if the desert had ports. As if a hundred drowned sailors had signed away their bones.

If Quill reached the Meridian Star, the wreck would be stripped to its nails inside a week, the cargo sold in lots, the registry—if there was a registry—filed, stamped, and buried in a warehouse where no name written in it would ever surface again.

And if there was a registry—

Xanthe closed the ledger and pressed her palm flat against its cover, as if the numbers might escape.

Her grandfather had been a captain. That was the sentence she had not allowed herself to think in fifteen years, and now it arrived whole, uninvited, wearing its own weight. Captain Elias Vane-Marrow of the Meridian Star, lost with all hands in the Year of the Broken Coast, his granddaughter—a girl of six, sent inland to relatives a month before the final voyage, the last branch of the family left above water—presumed in the ledgers of three nations to have drowned with the rest, because no one had ever come forward to say otherwise.

No one had ever come forward. That had been the whole architecture of her life. Xanthe Crane was a careful woman with a careful job, and the girl who had been born with saltwater names had been folded up and put away so thoroughly that sometimes Xanthe almost believed she had never existed.

Almost. Until a lying tide opened a cave and put her grandfather's ship in front of her lantern like an accusation.

* * *

She went down to the basin at slack water, when the cave mouth stood clear of the sea and the air inside tasted of stone and old brine. She had work to do and only six tides to do it in, and fear was a luxury she scheduled the way other people scheduled meals.

Inside, the Meridian Star waited in the lantern light, patient as a saint. The preservation was unnatural—salt had packed every joint and seam, holding the wood against rot, holding even the rope coils on deck soft enough to bend. It was as though the desert had decided, a century ago, to keep something safe, and had simply never been asked what.

Xanthe worked methodically. She photographed the hull markings with her field camera, logged the cargo manifest stenciled inside the forward hatch coaming, measured and recorded. Evidence first. Always evidence first—that was the discipline that had kept her alive and employed and invisible. If she documented the find properly, reported it through channels, the wreck would pass into custodianship and the lawyers would argue for years and Quill's fleet would sit at anchor growing rust while the paperwork bred.

It was the correct procedure. It was also, she understood with cold clarity as she knelt in the dark writing measurements by lantern light, surrender. Custodianship meant disclosure. Disclosure meant her name—not Crane, the other one, the drowned one—entering the record. And a name in the record was a hand extended into the world, and hands could be grasped, and the people who depended on her silence—the salt-rangers she trained, the settlements she kept supplied through the dying season, Sef, who trusted her with his life on patrol and deserved better than a lie—would all of them stand a little closer to the fire that would follow.

Because there would be fire. She knew it the way she knew weather. A wreck with heirs was not a wreck; it was a claim, and claims in the salvage territories were settled with money or blood, and Adhara Quill had never once in her career paid with the former when the latter was cheaper.

So: move the evidence, or move herself. Those were the only doors, and both of them opened onto the same dark room.

There was a third thing in the cave besides the ship and the evidence. Xanthe had found it on her first visit and told no one, not even her ledger: a strongbox bolted beneath the captain's cabin floor, iron, salt-welded shut, its lock a mechanism of a make she recognized because she had grown up with its twin sitting on her grandfather's desk—a Vane-Marrow lock, keyed to the family, the kind that opened to blood-knowledge rather than force. Voss's chisel marks were on it, shallow and defeated. He had tried and failed. Which meant he knew it was there, which meant he knew what it might hold, which meant somewhere between the burned charts and this cave there was a story about her family that a stranger had pieced together before she had.

That thought frightened her more than the fleet did.

* * *

On the fourth day she rode Bittern east through the broken country to the relay station at Kestrel Gap, a shack and a signal mast staffed by a half-deaf old warden who asked no questions because he could rarely hear them. From Kestrel Gap, messages could go anywhere on the wire. Most messages went to Copperline. This one would not.

Xanthe wrote it in the code she had sworn never to use again—a trader's cipher from another life, built on shipping manifests and tide tables, the private language of a family that had done business on three coasts. Her hands remembered it perfectly. That was the cruelest part: fifteen years of burial, and the code came off her fingers like it had been waiting.

*TO: M. OKONKWO, LANTERN HOUSE, PORT SABLE.*

*SIX WINDOWS REMAIN. STAR VISIBLE. BOX SEALED, LOCK INTACT. VISITORS EXPECTED EIGHT DAYS. REQUIRE ADVICE AND, IF POSSIBLE, PROOF OF LINEAGE. THE TIDE THAT LIES HAS TOLD THE TRUTH. — C.*

She read it four times. Every phrase was load-bearing. *Star visible* told Okonkwo the wreck was found without naming it over an open wire. *Proof of lineage* told him she was ready, at last, to say who she was—to him, and maybe eventually to the record. *Visitors expected eight days* told him about Quill without saying the name, because some names, spoken into wires, traveled faster than telegraphy and arrived with teeth.

Milo Okonkwo had been her grandfather's purser's son, a boy of ten when the Star went down, now an old man keeping a lamp-lit archive of everything the salvage companies wanted forgotten. He was the one person alive who could confirm, with documents, that the drowned captain's granddaughter had not drowned. He was also the one person alive who could burn those same documents if she asked—which was, she admitted, staring at the finished message, probably why she had never contacted him before. As long as she never asked, she could pretend both futures were still open.

Sending the message chose one.

Her hand hovered over the transmit key. Outside, the wind was coming around to the northeast, the way it did before the season turned, carrying the smell of real water from the drowned country. Six tides. Somewhere beyond the horizon, eight days out but capable of arriving early, because brokers like Quill treated schedules as suggestions and laws as ballast, the fleet was burning coal toward her secret.

Fear sat in her chest like swallowed stone. She let it sit. Then she thought of the hull in the lantern light, her grandfather's ship holding its breath in the dark for a hundred years, waiting for someone to come who had a reason to come.

Xanthe Crane sent the wire.

The key clattered its small bright language into the dark, and when it was done she stood a long moment in the shack, listening to the wind, doing the only arithmetic that mattered now:

Six tides. One stranger who knew her name. One message already beyond recall.

And somewhere out on the flats, moving the way thieves move—patient, unhurried, certain—Casimir Voss was coming to see whether she had finally remembered who she was.
