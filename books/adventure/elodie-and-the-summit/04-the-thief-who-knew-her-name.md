## 4. The Thief Who Knew Her Name

The waystation at Cutter's Notch had been abandoned for thirty years, and Casimir Voss had made it look like it again—almost. Xanthe read the canyon from the ridge above, letting the last light of the dying day pool in the stone hollows below. A thread of smoke, thin as a hair, rose from the old chimney and vanished into the wind before it could betray itself. The water barrel by the door had been refilled; the dust around the hitching post was scuffed in patterns too deliberate to be careless.

He wanted her to see it. He wanted her to come down.

Fifteen years of tracking men across the salt flats had taught her that a visible camp was either a trap or a surrender, and Voss did not strike her as a man who surrendered. She sat her dune-schooner a while longer, watching the shadows lengthen between the canyon walls, and felt the strange new thing in her chest—the crack she'd first noticed at his abandoned camp, the one shaped like curiosity—widening into something less comfortable. Something like hunger.

She should have called it in. Deputy Sef Marrow was two days east with the patrol skiff, and protocol said anomalous finds went up the chain, not down personal roads. But protocol also said stolen property recovered by ranger action stayed in ranger custody pending review, and some stubborn, buried part of her wanted to look at Voss's face when he explained why a professional thief had carried half-burned charts of a coastline that existed nowhere on any map she'd ever surveyed—except one. Except the one the sea had drawn for her three nights ago, flooding a desert basin that hadn't held water in a century.

She rode down.

* * *

Casimir Voss heard the schooner's engine note long before he saw its running lights—a low thrum he recognized the way other men recognized voices. He'd been listening for it for six days, since the tide charts told him Crane would have found the cave by now. He set down the tin cup of tea he wasn't drinking, straightened the papers on the table so they lay in an inviting fan, and waited.

When the door opened without a knock, he didn't turn around. "You took the southern approach," he said to the wall. "I thought you'd come down the wash. You always favor high ground."

That got him what he wanted: silence with weight in it. Then her voice, level as a spirit gauge. "You always favor running."

"Only from people who don't matter." He turned. Ranger Xanthe Crane stood in the doorway with the dusk behind her, sidearm holstered but hand resting near it, sand-colored coat bleached to the exact color of the flats she'd haunted for fifteen years. She looked exactly as he remembered her mother looking, which was the whole point of everything, and he had to take a breath before he could continue. "Sit down, Captain's granddaughter. The tea's terrible but it's hot."

He watched the name land. Watched it the way you watch a depth charge sink—no explosion, just the long waiting for the shockwave to come back up from the deep.

Her hand did not go to her weapon. Her hand went flat against the doorframe, steadying herself, and something in Voss's chest twisted with an old guilt. He'd planned this confrontation for months. He had not planned to feel ashamed of it.

"My name is Crane," she said.

"Your name is whatever you buried it under." He gestured to the table, to the fanned papers—the salvaged half of his burned charts, plus a photograph gone soft and silver at the edges. "And I know, because I've spent four years finding out what you buried. Sit down, Elodie."

* * *

Xanthe sat because her legs decided the question without consulting her.

The name went through her like water through cracked ground—everywhere at once, filling spaces she'd sealed so long ago she'd forgotten they were hollow. *Elodie.* Nobody living had spoken it. She had made sure of that. Fifteen years of measured distances, of names given only in fragments, of a signature practiced until *Crane* flowed without hesitation. And this thief, this man she'd chased across four hundred miles of salt, said it the way you'd say a word in a language you'd learned from a dead person.

She looked at the photograph. Her hands were steady—that was the training, the fifteen years—but her vision swam, and she let it, because there was no one here to see.

A woman on the deck of a ship. Dark hair whipping, one hand raised—not waving, pointing, mid-order, caught in the act of command. Behind her, rigging and rail and a horizon that curved the wrong way for the desert. On the photo's lower edge, in faded ink: *Capt. Maren Vael — Meridian Star — final season.*

"My grandmother," Xanthe heard herself say. It came out flat, factual, a ranger's report delivered from somewhere very far away. "You have a photograph of my grandmother."

"I have more than a photograph." Voss turned the chart over. Burned edges, but the surviving lines were unmistakable now that she'd seen the cave with her own eyes: the headland, the notch, the depth soundings written in a hand she almost recognized. "I have her logbook's index. I have the salvage registry's cover sheet. And I have the reason the fleet is coming, which is the reason I stole all of it in the first place."

"You stole it to lure me out." The words arrived in her mouth already assembled, and she knew they were true even before she finished saying them. "Four hundred miles. Every camp you left where I'd find it. You weren't running from me. You were leading me."

"I needed the one person alive who could open what's down there." He leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for the first time she saw how tired he was—not the tiredness of flight but of carrying something heavy for years. "The captain's strongbox, Xanthe. Salt-sealed, keyed to bloodline or to a phrase only family would know. The registry inside names heirs. To cargo. To debts. To things Broker Adhara Quill has spent thirty years making sure stay drowned." He paused. "Presumed drowned. That's what the record says about you. Lost with the registry, age seven, no survivors of the Vael line. Quill built her empire on that presumption. You are the flaw in her foundation, and I am the man who dug until he found you."

The vertigo came then, properly, the way the tide had come into the basin—impossible, total, rearranging the shape of the world. She was sitting in a ruined waystation in a canyon whose name she'd never bothered to learn, and she was also seven years old on a deck that no longer existed above water, and she was also a ranger named Crane who had spent fifteen years being no one, and all three of these women were drowning in the same small room.

"Why?" she managed. "Why does a thief care?"

Voss smiled, and it was the saddest thing she'd seen in years. "Because my grandfather was her bosun. Because the registry names his family among those owed—and among those blamed. Because Quill's divers have been quietly stripping wrecks for decades and erasing the crews who objected, and my grandfather's objection is recorded somewhere in that box." He tapped the chart. "I can't open it. Quill can't open it. Only you can. So yes—I led you here. I'm sorry for the method. I'm not sorry for the need."

Outside, the wind changed direction, carrying the faintest mineral smell off the basin—the sea announcing itself, keeping its own schedule. Six tides, she thought. Maybe five. And eight days out, engines.

Xanthe looked at the photograph of her grandmother pointing orders into the wind, and felt the invisible life she'd built begin, slowly, to surface—like a hull rising out of salt.

"If I do this," she said, "you'll tell me everything. All of it. Starting with what Quill wants erased."

Voss reached for the teapot. "Then we'd better start talking," he said, "because the next tide comes early."
