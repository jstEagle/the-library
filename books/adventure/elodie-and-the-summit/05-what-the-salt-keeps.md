## 5. What the Salt Keeps

Casimir Voss had spent three years learning to read Xanthe Crane the way other men read weather, and what he saw now, in the amber light of the waystation lantern, was a storm deciding whether to break.

She stood with her back to the door she had locked behind her—his idea, not hers; he'd asked for the courtesy of walls before he gave her the courtesy of truth—and her hand had not left the grip of her rifle since she'd crossed the threshold. But she hadn't raised it. That was the thing about Crane that had kept him alive in canyons where lesser trackers would have shot first and inventoried later: she always wanted one more fact before she spent a bullet.

He intended to give her exactly enough facts to keep it that way.

"You said you knew my name," she said. "Not Crane."

"I said I knew *a* name. Yours." He lowered himself onto the bench across from her, slowly, deliberately, letting her watch every inch of the movement. A man moving fast around Xanthe Crane got fast results, and most of them were terminal. "The name on the registry."

"What registry."

"The one under the Meridian Star." He let the words sit in the dust between them. Outside, the wind worried at the waystation's tin roof, a sound like someone patiently unsewing the world. "You've seen the hull. You know she's real now. What you don't know is what's inside her, and why the salvage fleet wants her so badly they'll burn eight days of schedule and half their divers to get there first."

"Salvage rights are simple," Xanthe said. Her voice was flat as the flats themselves. "Derelict vessel, abandoned cargo, finder's claim. Quill's fleet files, Quill's fleet takes."

"Simple." Voss smiled without any humor in it. "That's the version they print for rangers. Here's the version they print for no one." He reached into his coat—her rifle came up an inch, and he froze, showing her two fingers, then drew out the oilcloth packet with the same exaggerated care a man might use defusing something—and unwrapped the last surviving chart. Not the burned one from his camp. This one he'd copied by hand, twice, because the original had been paid for with a man's life and he did not intend to lose it twice.

He spread it on the table between them and turned the lantern toward it.

"Meridian Star, out of Calloway Harbor, 1897. Passenger and freight, forty-one souls aboard when she went down." His finger traced the coastline that shouldn't exist—the drowned coast, the old coast, the one the desert had swallowed a century ago and salted over like a wound packed shut. "Except she didn't go down where the histories say. The histories were written by the insurance men. She went down here." His finger stopped on a bay that appeared on no modern map. "And she went down *empty* of the things that mattered, because the captain put them somewhere no insurance surveyor would ever look."

Xanthe hadn't moved. But Voss had learned to read the small weathers of her face: the fractional narrowing at *insurance men*, the stillness that wasn't calm but load-bearing, the way her thumb shifted on the rifle stock.

"The captain's strongbox," she said.

"The captain's strongbox. And the ship's registry." He tapped the chart. "Not the crew manifest—the *registry*. The legal document. Ownership of the cargo, debts of the house that chartered her, names of heirs. It went down with her because the captain was carrying it to safety, and the house that chartered the Meridian Star wanted it at the bottom of the sea far more than they wanted it delivered."

"Why?"

"Because of what it says." Voss leaned back. This was the part he'd rehearsed in a dozen empty camps, and every rehearsal had ended the same way: with him watching her face change. "The chartering house was Quill's house. Adhara Quill's grandfather built the fleet on the insurance payout from a ship he'd scuttled himself—overinsured, overloaded, sailed into a storm season he knew was coming. The registry proves it. Names, dates, signatures. And it doesn't just indict the dead. It lists the debts the house owed when she sank—debts that were never paid because the proof of them drowned. Wages. Shares. Compensation to families. Compounded over a century, that cargo isn't worth what Quill told her investors. It's worth a fraction of that, once the claims come due. The wreck isn't a treasure to Adhara Quill. It's a loaded gun pointed at her own family name, and she wants it melted down for scrap before anyone reads what's engraved on it."

The lantern flame leaned in the draft. Somewhere outside, a rock fox screamed once and went quiet.

"And the heirs," Xanthe said. Her voice had gone quieter, which with her was never a good sign. "You said heirs."

"I did."

"Say the rest, Voss. You didn't cross four hundred miles of flats and burn your own charts to stop at the interesting part."

No, he thought. She wouldn't. He'd counted on that from the beginning—it was the whole architecture of the theft, the map, the trail of breadcrumbs leading her out of her invisible life and into this room. He had stolen from her precisely because she was the only person alive who could do what needed doing, and she would never have agreed to do it if asked.

"The registry names the captain's line," he said. "Captain Elias Marren. One daughter survived him. One granddaughter." He looked up and met her eyes and did not look away, because looking away would have been worse than anything the rifle could do. "Presumed drowned with the ship's papers. Presumed dead for fifteen years, which is how long you've been presumed dead, Xanthe—or should I say, how long has it been since anyone called you by your mother's name?"

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed on the lantern glass, on the tin roof, on the space between the bench and the door. Voss watched her breathe—one breath, two—and watched the ranger go somewhere behind her eyes while whatever was underneath her surfaced, slow and enormous, like the hull rising through the tide.

"My mother's name," Xanthe repeated.

"Marren." He said it gently, which cost him; gentleness was not a tool he carried often, and it sat awkwardly in his hands. "Elena Marren, the captain's daughter. You were born Elodie Marren. The registry lists you. Lineal heir, sole surviving claimant to the strongbox and everything the registry establishes." He spread his hands. "I didn't make this true. I found it true, three years ago, in a broker's archive in Calloway, and I've been running from Quill's people ever since, because the moment I understood what the paper meant, I also understood that the only key to the strongbox is blood. The lock is a Marren lock. It opens to a Marren hand—to the signet pattern, the pressure sequence, the things the captain taught his daughter and his daughter taught her child before the child was old enough to remember learning them."

"That's not possible."

"It's entirely possible. You opened things as a girl you don't remember opening. Your hands remember. That's why I stole the map instead of selling it. That's why I led you here instead of leading Quill's divers. I needed the heir *alive*, Crane. Elodie. I needed you standing in front of that box, because there is no other way to open it, and if Quill reaches it first, she doesn't open it—she cracks it, takes the registry, and burns it, and a hundred years of theft gets buried under a hundred more."

Xanthe finally moved. She set the rifle against the wall—not surrendered, just set down, which from her was a kind of speech—and came to stand over the chart. In the lantern light Voss could see the resemblance he'd never seen in photographs, because there had been no photographs: the captain's jaw, the daughter's level brow. Fifteen years of salt and sun had written over it, but the writing was recent. The older text showed through.

"If I report this find," she said slowly, "as duty requires—"

"You surrender it."

"—the wreck becomes evidence. Evidence goes to the territorial office. The territorial office processes salvage claims through—" She stopped. He watched her arrive at it herself, watched her jaw tighten.

"Through brokers licensed by the Quill house," Voss finished. "Yes. Reporting it means handing the gun to the person it's aimed at. That's the beauty of it, from her side. The law is the lock on her cellar door. Every honest officer in the territory is a hinge on that door." He rose, carefully, and stood across the table from her. "So. Now you know what the salt keeps. The question—the only question—is what you keep. The badge, or the name. The post, or the truth. Because I will tell you plainly, Elodie Marren, what I have learned at great personal expense: you cannot carry both out of that cave."

"You don't get to call me that."

"No," he agreed. "But the registry does. And so does the lock."

Her hand came down flat on the chart, pinning the drowned coastline under her palm, and for a long moment she just breathed, and Voss recognized the expression from a hundred wanted posters' worth of faces he'd studied over the years: the look of a person standing at the exact point where the life they built meets the life that was taken from them, understanding that the ground between the two is narrower than either.

"Duty says report," she said at last, to the chart, or to herself. "Duty has kept me alive for fifteen years. Duty is the only thing I have never lied to."

"And duty delivers your inheritance to the woman who needs it destroyed," Voss said. "I know. I'm not asking you to like the arithmetic. I'm asking you to do it anyway."

She looked up at him then, and her eyes were dry and terrible and clear.

"How long," she said, "before Quill's fleet arrives?"

"Eight tides. If she keeps her schedule."

Something flickered across her face—too fast for him to name it, gone before it finished arriving—and she pushed back from the table and went to the window, looking north, toward the basin, toward the water that had no business existing in the middle of a desert.

"Voss," she said quietly. "When you found my camp trail three days ago. When you knew I was following you. Why didn't you run harder?"

"Because," he said, "I wasn't running from you. I was running *to* you. There's a difference, and it's measured in tides."

She turned from the window. Far off across the darkening flats, where the horizon met the last light, there was a smudge that might have been cloud and might have been smoke and might have been sails.

"Then you'd better hope," Xanthe Crane said, in a voice that had begun, very slightly, to sound like someone else—someone older, someone drowned, someone with a captain's jaw set against a storm—"that the difference is enough. Because I think she's early."

* * *

Voss slept badly, which was usual, and woke before dawn, which was survival. What was unusual was the sound from the yard: the soft, rhythmic strike of a whetstone, patient as a tide.

Xanthe was sitting on the waystation step, sharpening a diving knife he hadn't known she carried, her rifle across her knees, watching the east go grey. She didn't look at him when he came out. She spoke to the horizon.

"The strongbox opens to a Marren hand," she said. "Fine. Then a Marren hand opens it. But hear me, Voss, and hear me once: I am not doing this for the cargo, or the debts, or your century-old arithmetic. I'm doing it because the law I serve has been bent into a weapon, and the only way to straighten it is to put the truth in front of everyone at once—in daylight, in front of witnesses, where no broker can bury it. Whatever comes up out of that water goes on the record. All of it. Even the parts that drown me."

Voss stood in the cold morning air and felt, for the first time in three years, something dangerously adjacent to hope.

"That," he said, "is the first plan anyone's offered me that ends with me alive."

"Don't celebrate yet." She stood, slung the rifle, and nodded north, where the smudge on yesterday's horizon had resolved, unmistakably now, into masts. "Because unless I've forgotten how to count—and I haven't, counting distances is the one thing I've never forgotten—that fleet out there is two tides ahead of schedule. Which means somebody told Quill where we are."

She looked at him then, and the question in her eyes was a knife she hadn't bothered to draw.

"So before we take another step, Casimir—you're going to tell me who else knew."
