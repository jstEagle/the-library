## 6. Adhara Quill Arrives Early

The tide came in wrong.

Xanthe felt it before she saw it—a pressure change in the air, a hush along the basin rim where the wind should have been worrying the salt crust into white lace. She stood at the mouth of the cave with her hand flat against the hull of the Meridian Star, and the water rose around her boots like something patient finally keeping an appointment.

Two tides early. She had counted them the way she counted everything now: six remaining, then five, each one a door closing a little further. But this tide arrived swollen, greedy, climbing the cave walls past every mark she'd scratched, and she understood with a cold settling in her chest that the sea was not the only thing that had jumped its schedule.

She heard the engines at dusk.

Not the ragged putter of prospectors or the coughing sputter of salt-gatherers' skiffs—these were deep-throated, disciplined, many. She climbed to the rim and looked south across the darkening flats, and there they were: lights strung across the horizon like a carnival that had come to the wrong town. The salvage fleet. Eight days out by her arithmetic, arriving on the fourth day, riding barges through water that shouldn't exist in a desert that had forgotten its own name.

They'd been coming all along, then. Her calculations hadn't been wrong; her assumptions had. Adhara Quill didn't wait for tides. Quill made them.

Xanthe crouched in the dark and did what she had done for fifteen years: she measured. Distance to the fleet's lead barge, call it eleven miles. Time until they reached the basin, half a night if they pushed. Time until Sef returned from the eastern patrol—unknown, and that unknown sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

She went back down into the cave.

The Meridian Star lay on its side in the shallows, masts snapped but standing, rigging fused to coral-colored salt. In the lantern light the hull looked less like a wreck than a body preserved mid-breath, every plank swollen with a century of brine. And there, near the stern where Voss's charts said it would be, the captain's cabin door hung open on darkness—and inside it, somewhere below the waterline, the strongbox waited with its registry of names. Her grandfather's name. Her mother's name. The name Xanthe Crane had buried under fifteen years of measured miles.

She had sent the coded message two nights ago. She had refused Voss. She had told herself duty was simple: report the find, surrender the claim, go home to her post and her small invisible life. She had almost believed it.

Then the fleet arrived early, and simplicity drowned.

* * *

They came ashore at midnight with lamps and manners.

Xanthe met them at the basin rim because meeting them anywhere else meant looking like she was hiding. Six of them crossed the salt in formation, and at their center walked a woman in a coat the color of oxblood, cut sharp as a verdict. Adhara Quill was smaller than her reputation—compact, silver at the temples, hands bare of rings except one heavy signet she wore on her thumb like a weapon she'd grown tired of drawing.

"Ranger Crane." Quill's voice carried without effort, pitched for the space between them alone. "You found my ship."

"Your ship?" Xanthe kept her tone flat, ranger-flat, the voice she used on drunks and desperate men. "Salvage law says finds belong to the finder."

"Salvage law." Quill smiled, and it was a beautiful smile, the kind that had closed a hundred contracts and a few coffins. "Yes. Let's talk about law. You're a careful woman, Ranger. Fifteen years of careful reports, careful patrols, careful silence about your own history. I admire that. I've built a career on people who prefer not to be seen." She drew a folded document from her coat and held it out. "A finder's fee. Generous. Sign away any claim to the vessel and its contents, and you'll never see me or my fleet again. The basin seals in four tides. Everyone goes home rich or everyone goes home quiet. Your choice which."

Xanthe didn't take the paper. "Why bring six men to hand me a contract?"

"Because you haven't signed yet." Quill studied her the way a jeweler studies a stone with a flaw she intends to cut around. "And because I wanted to see your face when I said your real name."

The wind moved along the rim. Somewhere behind Xanthe, one of Quill's men shifted his weight, and the sound was very loud.

"Elenora Vashti Meridian," Quill said softly. "Granddaughter of Captain Elias Meridian. Listed drowned in the registry loss of '41—which was convenient, since a dead heir can't contest a salvage claim. Except heirs have a way of washing up in deserts and growing up to carry badges." She tilted her head. "You were seven years old when they pulled you off that rock. Do you remember the rock? The crew called it the Widow's Tooth. You clung to it for two days."

Xanthe's breath came slow and deliberate, the way she breathed when the ground was giving way. Fifteen years of burying that name, and Quill dug it up in a sentence, casually, like turning over a stone to see what fled.

"You've done your reading," Xanthe said.

"I've done my owning," Quill corrected. "There are debts in that strongbox, Ranger. Debts owed *to* certain houses and debts owed *by* them, written in a captain's hand a century old. My employers would very much like the second kind to stay dissolved in salt water. A living heir who opens that box could make trouble for respectable people. A living heir who signs—" she tapped the document against Xanthe's badge, once, lightly, "—is just a woman who found nothing."

"And if I refuse?"

Quill's smile didn't falter, which was somehow worse than if it had. "Then I have eight ships, forty divers, and lawyers in three territories, and you have a badge and a cave that will be underwater forever in four tides. War, if you want to call it that. Though wars need two armies, and yours is currently asleep in a waystation thirty miles east, unaware his partner has been lying to him since the day she found those charts."

The bottom dropped out of Xanthe's stomach. "You leave Deputy Sef out of this."

"There it is." Quill's eyes flickered with something almost like respect. "The one soft place. Every careful woman has one. It's why I always look." She pressed the contract into Xanthe's hand—not offering now, insisting—and stepped back. "You have until the next tide. That's six hours. Sign it and slide it under my barge door, or don't, and we proceed accordingly. Either way, Ranger—or should I say Miss Meridian—I'll know what kind of person you decided to be."

She turned and walked back toward the lights on the water, her men folding around her like a closing hand.

* * *

Xanthe sat on the rim with the contract unrolled across her knees until the sky went from black to bruise.

The terms were clean. That was the obscenity of it—the document was *clean*, fair even, a generous fee for a finder's waiver, the kind of paper a magistrate would skim and approve. No threats written down. No names. Just a signature line waiting for Elenora Vashti Meridian to erase herself one final time, permanently, legally, with witnesses.

Safety. That was what it bought. Safety for Sef, who didn't know yet that the woman he trusted had spent a week choosing a wreck over her oath. Safety for the salt-gatherers who depended on her patrols, her rations ledger, her silence about their small smugglings. Safety for everyone whose life she'd propped up with fifteen years of being no one in particular.

And it cost exactly one thing: the truth, surrendered before she ever got to hold it.

She thought of her grandfather's handwriting waiting in the dark below the waterline. She thought of Voss bleeding honesty at her in the canyon waystation—*you're the heir, open the box, the names will burn them*. She thought of the Widow's Tooth, which she did not remember, except sometimes in dreams, when the water was everywhere and something small and stubborn in her refused to let go.

Six hours. One tide.

Headlights swept the eastern track.

Xanthe stood so fast the contract slid from her knees and caught the wind, tumbling white across the salt, and she let it go. The vehicle bumped closer through the dark, engine laboring, dust plume lit gold behind it, and she knew the silhouette of that truck the way she knew her own heartbeat. Sef. Back early from patrol, back without warning, driving straight toward the one conversation she had prayed for six more hours to avoid.

He stopped twenty feet away and killed the lights. When he climbed out, he was still in his patrol coat, road-dusted, squinting against the glow of the fleet on the southern water.

"Xanthe." His voice was careful in a way she'd never heard from him. "I got your message. The coded one. And then I drove all night, and now I'm standing here looking at eight salvage barges in a dry lake bed, and a canyon full of questions you've been dodging since the charts." He took a step closer, and his eyes went from her face to the empty place where the contract had been, to the water lapping at the basin below. "So before I ask you anything else, I'm asking you this: whatever it is you've been carrying—whatever name it is—they're saying on the radio channels tonight—whoever you actually *are*—"

He stopped. Breathed. Held out his hand, palm up, the same gesture he used when taking evidence, when taking responsibility, when taking her weight on the rope line last winter in the whiteout.

"Tell me the whole thing," Sef said. "All of it. Or tell me to drive home. But don't you dare give me the version you think I can survive."

Below them, the tide began to rise again, and somewhere in the dark water the Meridian Star waited with a hundred years of names.
