## 7. Loyalty Weighs Less Than Water

Sef Marrow had learned to read Xanthe Crane the way he read weather: by what she withheld.

Fifteen years of ranger work had taught him that the desert told the truth twice—once in sand, once in silence—and that Crane was fluent in both. She was the best tracker on the flats, the steadiest hand in the post, and the only person he'd ever met who could stand three feet from you and be entirely elsewhere. He'd loved her for six of those fifteen years, and for six of those fifteen years he had made a discipline of not asking where *elsewhere* was.

Now the elsewhere had a name, and it was a cave full of drowned ship, and she hadn't told him.

He knew because ledgers never lie and rangers who file reports do. Three days ago, a salt-hauler out of the basin settlements had come through with a story: an anomalous tide, water standing in the dry sink below the old sea cliffs, a black hull gleaming under forty feet of brine like something asleep. The hauler's boy swore he'd seen a lantern moving inside it at night. Sef had pulled the patrol rosters to see who'd been sent to verify.

No one. No report. No entry. And Crane's requisition log showed her drawing dive line, lamp oil, and two extra tanks of compressed air—the kind of draw that required a signature, and hers was on it, dated the morning after the tide.

She hadn't filed. That was the part that sat in his chest like swallowed stone. Not that she'd gone. Rangers went places without asking all the time; the desert didn't wait for paperwork. But Crane *filed*. Crane logged every cairn and carcass and stolen water-cache with a clerk's precision, because records were how an officer stayed honest in a country that rewarded forgetting. Fifteen years of perfect entries, and now this one hole, clean-edged as a core sample.

He waited until dusk, when the post emptied and the heat came off the adobe walls, and found her at the drying racks behind the stable, folding dive line into coils with hands that moved too carefully, the way hands move when they're holding more than rope.

"You drew air," he said.

Her shoulders didn't stiffen. That was worse. She'd known he would come, and she'd decided how to meet him before he arrived, which meant whatever she said next had already been rehearsed against him.

"I did."

"Two tanks. Lamp oil. Line." He came around the rack so they were face to face. "And no report."

"No report," she agreed.

"Crane." He used the name deliberately, the way you'd tap a wall to find the hollow place. "I've stood watch beside you through sandstorms and smuggler fire and one genuinely memorable scorpion incident, and you have never once failed to file. So either you've changed in three days, or there's something in that cave you don't want written down. And I need you to tell me which, because I can't unknow what I know now, and neither can you."

The last light caught her face, and he watched her do it—the thing she always did, the going-elsewhere. Her eyes fixed somewhere past his shoulder, past the stable, past the whole visible world, into whatever country she kept behind them.

Then, for the first time in six years, she came back.

"There's a wreck in the basin," she said quietly. "A century old. A ship called the Meridian Star. It sank when this desert was still a sea, and the anomalous tide opened the cave it died in, and I've been diving it alone because in eight days—six now—a salvage fleet arrives to strip it, and everything in it, including its registry, will belong to Broker Adhara Quill."

"Why does that matter to you?" he asked. "Wrecks get salvaged. It's sad and it's legal and we both know it."

"Because the registry names heirs." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands had stopped moving on the rope. "And one of the names on it is mine."

* * *

They walked while she talked, out past the corral to the low ridge where the post kept its signal mirror, because she said she couldn't say it sitting down. Sef listened the way he'd been trained to listen—to structure, to gaps, to the load-bearing words.

She told him about the thief first. Casimir Voss, the man she'd been tracking across the flats for weeks, the man whose abandoned camp had held half-burned charts of a coastline that shouldn't exist. She told him about the tide, the cave, the hull preserved in salt like a held breath. She told him about Voss waiting for her in the canyon waystation instead of running—waiting because the map had been bait, because he'd needed to lure her out, because he knew.

"He knew my name," she said. "Not Crane. My name."

Sef stopped walking. The ridge wind moved between them, carrying the mineral smell of the distant water.

"Xanthe Crane is a name I built," she went on, and now she was looking at him straight, and it cost her—he could see what it cost, like watching someone carry a beam up a hill. "Fifteen years ago I buried another one. My grandfather captained the Meridian Star. When she went down, the registry went down with her, and everyone aboard was recorded as lost—including my family. All except me. I was four. I was carried inland by—" her jaw tightened "—by someone who didn't survive long enough to explain himself. I grew up in the basin settlements with no papers and no past, and when I took the ranger oath I gave them a name that belonged to no one, because a name that belongs to no one can't be taken away."

"What was it?" he asked. "Your name?"

She told him.

It was a small sound, two syllables, and it rearranged the entire landscape of their six years. He turned it over and felt it settle—not strange, exactly. More like a key finally fitted to a lock he'd been living inside without knowing it was a lock. *So that's the shape of you*, he thought. *That's what the elsewhere was. You've been standing guard over your own grave.*

"The salvage fleet," he said slowly, working it forward, "is coming to claim cargo whose legal heir is alive and standing in the desert wearing a badge."

"And if I come forward, I stop being invisible," she said. "Quill knows who I am—I'm sure of it now, the way she talked, the way she offered. Coming forward means becoming a person with a claim, which means becoming a person with enemies, which means everyone at this post, everyone in the settlements, everyone who depends on the rangers being boring and reliable, gets dragged into whatever war follows. Or I stay silent, sign nothing, let Quill take the wreck, and spend the rest of my life knowing I sold my grandfather's truth for my own comfort." She laughed, short and airless. "Voss called it a strongbox. He wasn't wrong. Everything worth having in this story is locked, and every key costs blood."

"Has Quill contacted you?"

"She'll contact me again." Something flickered across her face—fatigue, or fear, or the specific exhaustion of a woman doing arithmetic she hates. "She jumped her schedule, Sef. Two tides early. Whatever she's planning, it's happening faster than the math says it should."

He looked out toward the basin, where the last light was dying blue over the salt, and did the thing he'd spent six years not doing: he weighed her against his oath.

It should have been harder. He was a deputy ranger; she was his senior officer concealing a material discovery from the record; there was a version of this night where he wrote the report himself and hated himself cleanly forever. But oaths, he'd learned, were tools, not altars—you didn't worship them, you used them, and an oath used wrongly cut the hand that held it. The law existed to keep the powerful from eating the powerless. Quill's fleet was power arriving on schedule to eat a dead crew's legacy and a living woman's inheritance. Reporting the wreck *as things stood* wouldn't serve the law. It would serve Quill.

But he couldn't stand in the dark with her, either. Not half-in. Half-truths between officers got people killed; he'd seen it done at two posts before this one.

"Here's my price," he said.

She turned.

"I'm not filing, and I'm not reporting you, and I'm not pretending I don't know. But I don't stand beside people who keep secrets from me—I've buried friends who did that. So you give me the whole of it. Every dive, every conversation with Voss, every word Quill said to you, the registry, the debts Voss claims are written in it, all of it, starting tonight. If there's a plan, I'm in it, not adjacent to it. If there's a risk, I share it. You don't get to protect me by lying to me, Xanthe. Protection that runs on lies is just abandonment with better manners."

For a long moment she said nothing. The stars were coming out over the ridge, hard and white, and he watched her stand in them like a woman deciding whether to step off a ledge.

Then she exhaled, and it was the longest exhale he'd ever seen her take, fifteen years leaving her body in a single breath.

"Come inside," she said. "There's a chart under my floorboard and a name I haven't said aloud since I was four years old, and you're going to hear both."

* * *

Her quarters smelled of lamp oil and gun grease and, faintly, of salt—salt that had no business being there, brought back in her hair and clothes from water that shouldn't exist. She knelt and pried up the board beneath her cot and lifted out a packet wrapped in oilcloth: the recovered chart pages, Voss's half-burned originals, and a folded paper she handled differently from the rest, gentler, the way you handle a photograph of the dead.

"My grandfather's commission," she said. "Voss had it. He gave it back." Her thumb moved over the seal. "Elodie. My name was Elodie."

"Elodie," Sef said, and watched her close her eyes as if the word had weight enough to lean on.

They worked until well past midnight, her voice going hoarse, laying it all out on the floor: the tides, the timeline, the strongbox, the registry with its lists of heirs and debts, the wound Voss had taken covering their escape from the fifth-tide dive, the plan forming in her head for the summit at the cave mouth—reading the registry aloud to the assembled crews, turning truth into a weapon with a muzzle. He argued with her where the plan was thin. She let him, which he understood, finally, was itself a kind of intimacy: Xanthe Crane letting another person put hands on the architecture of her survival.

Near dawn, she fell asleep sitting up against the cot, mid-sentence, her head tipping onto his shoulder, and he sat very still under the weight of it and thought about loyalty. Everyone treated it like stone—solid, permanent, load-bearing. But sitting there in the lamplight with her breathing against him, he understood it was water. It weighed less than anything. It found every crack. It rose to its own level, and its level, for him, had always been her.

Outside, far off toward the basin, he heard it—or thought he heard it: engines, many engines, running ahead of any schedule, coming on through the dark like weather.

He didn't wake her. There'd be time enough for war in the morning, and only one more tide after that.
