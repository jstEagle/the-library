## 8. The Fifth Tide

The water came in like a debt being collected.

Xanthe stood at the cave mouth with the tide chart folded against her ribs and watched the basin fill—first a silver thread across the salt, then a broad dark tongue, then everything, the whole drowned floor of the world returning as if it had never left. A century ago this had been harbor. The sea had withdrawn out of spite or geology or some bargain no one remembered, but it kept its appointments. Twice a day it came back to check on what it had left behind.

The fifth tide. One more after this before the dry season sealed the cave for another year—or forever, depending on which surveyor she believed.

"You're sure about the timing." Sef crouched beside her at the lip of rock, checking the seals on his diving vest for the third time. He wasn't asking about the tide.

"I'm sure that Quill's divers will be here on the sixth," she said. "And I'm sure I'd rather not be in that water when they arrive."

Behind them, half in shadow where the canyon narrowed, Casimir Voss sat on his heels and said nothing, which from Voss was a kind of argument. He'd come because she'd made him—because the strongbox answered to blood, and his blood was worthless, and hers apparently was not—and he'd come hating every step of it, radiating reluctance like heat off stone.

"Last chance to tell me what's down there besides paper," Sef said quietly.

"Paper is enough." She looked at him. In the gray pre-dawn light his face was all angles and worry, and she loved him for the worry, and hated herself for what the worry would cost him. "Whatever's in that box, Sef—it doesn't stay in this canyon. Once we open it, everyone who ever lied about the Meridian Star has to answer for it. Including people who sign salvage contracts."

"Including you."

"Including me."

He nodded once, slow, and didn't say *then don't do it*, which was why she could love him. He adjusted her mask strap instead, fingers careful at her temple, an intimacy so small and ordinary it nearly undid her right there on the rocks.

* * *

The dive was worse than she'd planned for.

The cave swallowed them in stages: first the bright cold mouth, then a throat of green murk where the last of the night's darkness still clung, then the great hollow interior where their lamps found the Meridian Star waiting exactly as the salt had promised—hull intact, masts snapped but standing, rigging hung with mineral lace like a cathedral someone had abandoned mid-prayer.

Voss swam ahead, lamp beam sweeping the deck, and even underwater Xanthe could read his body language: the tight shoulders, the too-careful kicks. He knew this ship. Not the way she did—not by inheritance—but by obsession. He'd spent years reconstructing it from charts and rumor, and now the real thing dwarfed every guess he'd ever made.

She found the captain's cabin by feel more than sight. Her grandfather's name was still legible on the door plate, crusted white: **AURELIAN VOSS-CRANE**.

Her breath caught in her regulator. *Voss.* The thief had told her the truth about one thing, at least—the name connection was real, distant cousins twice removed, a family tree with one branch drowned and one branch thieving. It was how he'd known. It was how he'd always known.

The strongbox sat bolted beneath the captain's berth, iron gone soft with corrosion, its lock a flower of rust. But the mechanism inside was brass, and brass survives salt the way truth survives silence—battered, buried, intact. Voss produced the key he'd had cut from the wax impression on the stolen map's seal, and Xanthe pressed her palm flat against the lid, and the lock turned like a held breath finally released.

Inside: oilcloth. Under the oilcloth: paper. And under the paper—

The registry.

It was heavier than it should have been, dense with a century of secrets. She tucked it into the waterproof satchel strapped to her chest, felt its weight settle against her heart like a second heartbeat, and gave the signal. *Up. Now.*

They were ten meters from the mouth when the water changed.

Not the tide—the tide was still coming in, patient and enormous. This was something else: a churn, a disturbance upstream in the green, and through it, faint as a bad memory, the sound of engines. Not on the horizon anymore. *In the channel.*

Quill's divers. Early. Again.

* * *

They came out of the water into a world already ending.

The fifth tide was at full flood, waves slamming the cave mouth with a violence the charts hadn't predicted, and behind them in the green murk something moved—shapes, lights, the industrial patience of professional salvagers who had jumped their own schedule because Adhara Quill did not tolerate being anticipated.

"Go!" Xanthe shoved the satchel higher on her chest and grabbed for the rocks.

Sef went first, hauling himself onto the shelf, then reaching back for her. She got a hand up, got a knee up, got most of herself out of the water—

—and heard Voss shout.

She turned. He was still in the shallows, tangled in something—a line, a net fragment, a piece of the wreck's own rigging dragged loose by the flood—and beyond him, emerging from the murk with a diver's knife already open, came one of Quill's men.

There was no time to think. There was barely time to move. Xanthe threw herself back into the water.

What happened next happened in pieces: Voss shoving her toward the rocks instead of away from them. The flash of the knife. Voss's arm going out—not blocking, *offering*, putting his body between her and the blade the way a man only does when he's decided something final about his own worth. The hot bloom of red in the green water. Sef's hands on her collar, dragging both of them onto the shelf while the tide hammered the cave like a fist on a door.

Then they were out. All three of them. Wet, gasping, alive.

Voss was not entirely alive. He was bleeding from a long gash along his ribs, shallow but ugly, and the sand around him was drinking it fast.

"No," Xanthe said, and knelt, and pressed her hands against him hard enough to hurt. "No, you don't get to do this. You don't get to spend yourself buying my life and then leave before I've decided whether to forgive you."

His laugh was wet and thin. "Fifteen years... chasing me across the flats, Crane. And this is the thanks."

"This is the *down payment*." She tore strips from his shirt with her teeth, packed the wound, bound it, worked with the mechanical fury of a ranger who had set broken bones in the field and refused, absolutely refused, to consider the alternative. Sef was beside her with the flare kit and the emergency blanket, his hands steady, his face anything but.

"He took a knife meant for you," Sef said quietly.

"I know what he took."

"Do you understand what that *means*?"

She did. That was the terrible part. Fifteen years she'd built a wall between herself and Casimir Voss—thief, liar, the man who stole her past and led her a hundred miles of salt to find it again. And he had stepped in front of a blade for her without hesitation, without calculation, with the simple reflexive certainty of family. Whatever else he was, whatever debts he owed, he had paid this one in blood on a beach of a sea that shouldn't exist.

Voss's eyes found hers. "The registry," he rasped. "Tell me it's dry."

She pulled the satchel around, opened it. The oilcloth had held. The paper was damp at the edges but whole, and by the light of Sef's flare she fanned the pages open across her knees.

Names. Dozens of them. Dates. Signatures. The Meridian Star's true manifest—not cargo, at least not only cargo. Debts. Payments. A ledger of everything the wreck had been carrying when it went down: bribes, blackmail, silent partnerships, and at the center of it, written in a hand she recognized from Quill's own contract documents, the founding investment of the House of Quill itself.

Adhara Quill's grandmother had owned a third of the Meridian Star. Had ordered it overloaded. Had survived the sinking comfortably ashore, and built an empire on the insurance, and spent a century making sure no one ever read these pages.

"Oh," Sef breathed. "Oh, Xanthe."

"She didn't jump her schedule to beat us to the strongbox," Xanthe said. The words came out very calm, which surprised her, because nothing inside her was calm at all. "She jumped her schedule to beat us to the *burning*. If that registry surfaces tomorrow, her house dies with it. Everything. The fleet, the contracts, the name."

Voss laughed again, weaker, bloodier. "Told you... the heir was worth more than the map."

Below them, the fifth tide began its long withdrawal, and through the cave mouth they could hear it: engines in the channel, anchors dropping, the fleet arriving in force. Not two tides early now. *Now.*

Quill's voice would be on one of those decks. Quill's divers were already in the water, swimming toward a strongbox that was empty, a secret that was already ashore, a truth already climbing the canyon path in the arms of a woman who had spent fifteen years learning how to survive.

Xanthe closed the registry. Bound it tight. Looked at Sef, at Voss bleeding into the sand, at the black line of ships assembling on the horizon like a verdict.

"One tide left," she said. "Tomorrow, she comes for us. So tonight—we decide exactly what she finds when she gets here."
