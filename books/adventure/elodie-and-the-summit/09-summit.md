## 9. Summit

The fifth tide had given them the registry. The sixth intended to take everything else.

Xanthe stood at the cave mouth with the salt-stiffened ledger pressed against her ribs, watching the fleet come in across the drowned basin like a fist of iron closing over water. Nine hulls, black against the last light, their salvage lamps already burning—white, merciless, arranged in a crescent that left no gap wide enough for a skiff to slip through. The wind off the returning sea carried the smell of brine and engine oil and something older underneath, the mineral breath of the cave itself, as if the Meridian Star were exhaling after a hundred years of held silence.

Behind her, the tide roared into the basin in long gray ranks, climbing the walls she'd memorized in another life, under another name. The water knew the way in. It had always known.

"They're running lights out," Sef said at her shoulder, his voice low and steady the way it went when he was most afraid. "That's not a salvage formation. That's a blockade."

"It's an audience," Xanthe said.

She could feel Voss behind them, propped against the cave wall with a compress of kelp and ranger gauze doing poor work on the wound in his side. He'd insisted on coming up from the water on his own strength, one arm hooked over Sef's shoulder, and now he sat pale as driftwood, laughing softly at nothing.

"You look like a woman about to give a eulogy," he called.

"I am." Xanthe turned the registry over in her hands. Salt had fused its cover to a gray crust, but the pages inside had been kept dry by a century of sealed air, and the ink was still black, still legible, still damning. "Just not hers."

* * *

Adhara Quill came ashore in a launch that cut the swell without courtesy, and she stepped onto the wet rock of the basin floor as though the sea itself had signed her right-of-way. She wore no coat against the spray. Her hands were bare, and Xanthe remembered what those hands had done to the last person who'd stood between Quill and a claim—the deputy from Corvane Station, ten years back, whose disappearance had been filed as desertion. Xanthe had signed the file herself. She'd believed the file then.

"Crane." Quill stopped six paces out, close enough that the lamplight found every line of her face and gave none of it away. Behind her, crewmen fanned out along the shore—not soldiers exactly, but salvage men with salvage tools, hooks and winch-bars and the particular patience of people paid by the ton. "You've had a busy week."

"Two tides early," Xanthe said. "I'd heard you were punctual."

"I'm thorough." Quill's gaze went past her, to the cave mouth where the water surged and withdrew like a lung, then back. "The finder's fee is still on the table. Doubled, in fact. You sign the abandonment, you walk away with more money than a ranger sees in three lifetimes, and nobody has to remember any of this happened. Including who you are."

"There it is," Xanthe said quietly.

"There what is?"

"The name. You keep reaching for it like a knife you haven't decided to use." She let the registry shift under her arm, deliberately, and watched Quill's eyes track it. "You've known since Corvane. Maybe since before. So say it, Broker. Say it here, in front of your crews, and see what it buys you."

Something moved behind Quill's face—fast, quickly caged. Around the crescent of ships, heads were turning. Sound carries strangely over water; it carries even better over men who suspect they've been cheated.

"You're bleeding time," Quill said. "The tide won't wait for your theatrics, and neither will I. Last offer: sign, or my divers go in over your objection, and whatever happens in that cave happens without witnesses."

"That's the second time you've said *witnesses* like it's a threat," Xanthe said. "It's the only thing it's never been."

She opened the registry.

* * *

The first page was the ship's articles, dated one hundred years and eleven days ago, and her voice cracked once on the date because her grandfather's signature was there at the top—*Captain Emeric Vale, commanding*—in a hand she'd spent fifteen years forgetting and recognized in half a heartbeat anyway. She steadied. She read on.

She read the cargo manifest aloud: not just ore and salt-pearls, which was what the salvage contracts claimed, but bonded debts—notes of credit issued by the Meridian Star's owners against the wreck's recovery, sold onward, resold, until the paper trail ran through four houses and ended, generation later, in the house of Quill. She read the indenture clauses. She read the insurance sworn against loss of hull, collected twice—once by the original owners, once again, fraudulently, forty years ago, by a syndicate whose surviving signatory was listed in the margin in a clerk's careful hand: *A. Quill, executor.*

The crews had gone very quiet. Lamplight wavered on wet faces.

"She's reading salvage fiction," someone said near the front, but without conviction.

"I'm reading your pay history," Xanthe answered, and did not stop. She read the crew wage bonds—the money owed to the drowned sailors' descendants, owed still, compounding, a debt the salvage contract's fine print had quietly transferred to whoever recovered the wreck, which meant Quill meant to collect their grandfathers' wages as cargo and pay none of it forward. She read the name of the diver lost at Corvane. She read the name of the file she herself had signed.

At that, her voice did break, and she let it.

"I was a ranger for fifteen years under a name I stole to stay safe," she said, "and the safest thing I ever did was also the most cowardly, because I signed the lie they handed me about this man." She held the page up. "The registry doesn't just name heirs. It names debts. And every debt in this book ends in one house."

Quill moved then—fast, no speech, no theater, just a signal flicked to the shore party—and the line of salvage men started forward along the rocks.

Sef stepped into their path with his badge held high, and Xanthe saw him make the choice she'd made at the fifth tide: duty pointed both directions, and he chose the direction with the truth in it. "Ranger post!" he shouted. "This is a lawful claim adjudication! Any man who lays hands on it answers to the flats!"

For a moment, nothing. Spray. Engines. The tide slamming the cave mouth like a door testing its hinges.

Then a hook clattered down onto the rock—thrown down, not dropped—a winch-bar after it, a diver's knife. A grizzled foreman from the lead hull spat into the surf and said, loud enough to carry: "My grandfather crewed a coastal packet out of the old ports. If there's wage bonds in that book, I'll hear them read or know why not."

Another voice. Another. The crescent of ships began, boat by boat, to come apart.

Quill stood alone on the wet rock with her bare hands and her unsaid name, and Xanthe watched her do the arithmetic brokers do—crews against contract, mutiny against retreat, the price of pressing on weighed against the price of being pressed. It took perhaps ten seconds. Xanthe counted them like heartbeats.

"You'll regret this," Quill said. Only to her. Only loud enough for the two of them.

"No," Xanthe said. "I'll remember it. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

Quill looked at the registry, at the turning crews, at the cave exhaling its century of breath behind them all—and she stepped back into her launch. The fleet's lamps went dark one by one, hulls swinging seaward on the rising tide, until the basin held nothing but water and stars and the sound of men arguing cheerfully about wages.

* * *

Afterward, the three of them sat above the tideline while the sixth tide climbed to its height and began, at last, its long withdrawal toward the sealing of the season.

Voss lay back on the sand with his hand over his bandaged side and laughed until it hurt him, and kept laughing anyway. "A hundred years," he managed, "and the ship collects its own debts. I told you the cave charged admission."

"It charged you blood," Sef said.

"It charged all of us," Xanthe said. She looked at the registry in her lap—at her grandfather's name, at her own true name waiting two pages deeper in the heirship clause, written in ink that had outlasted every lie told over it. Tomorrow there would be filings, and claims, and a magistrate in Corvane who would want the whole story. Tonight there was only the water going out, and Sef's shoulder warm against hers, and the strange lightness of a woman who had finally set down fifteen years of miles.

But below the tideline, in the darkening cave, the sea was already drawing its shutters closed—and somewhere in the deepening water, the captain's strongbox still waited, holding the one page of the registry no one had yet read.
