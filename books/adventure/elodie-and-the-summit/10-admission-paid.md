## 10. Admission Paid

The sixth tide came at dawn, and Xanthe stood at the cave mouth to watch the water take back what it had lent her.

It rose the way it always rose now—wrong, wonderful, a green-gray tongue sliding up the salt basin that had been dry her entire life. The sea had no business being here. A hundred years ago it had drowned this valley whole, and when the land healed into desert the sea had simply waited, patient as a creditor, for the season when it could reach in again and collect. This was that season. The last of it. By tomorrow's dusk the basin would be salt again, the cave a sealed throat, and everything inside it—the Meridian Star, the strongbox, the registry with its ledger of debts and dead men's names—would belong to the dark until the sea chose otherwise.

Xanthe had chosen otherwise first. That was the difference.

She watched the water climb the hull's black ribs, visible now only as shadows beneath the surface, and she thought about the word *admission*. The old rangers had a saying about this coast: *the cave charges admission, and it doesn't take coin.* She had thought it superstition when she was twenty. She knew better now. The price had been witness. The price had been the name she buried, dug up like a bone and held to the light in front of forty salvage crews while Adhara Quill's contract burned in her fist. The price had been Voss, bleeding out on the sand with the registry clutched to his chest like a child.

But she had paid it. And the cave, in its way, had let her keep the change.

Behind her, boots on stone. She didn't turn. There were only two people in the world who walked that particular way—one deliberate, one pretending not to hurry.

"Quill's flagship weighed anchor an hour ago," Sef said, coming to stand beside her. His shoulder found hers, easy as breathing. "Last diver's skiff cleared the headland at first light. They're gone."

"They'll be back next wet season."

"They'll come back to a legal wall with your name on it." He said it without bitterness, and that was the miracle of Sef—she had shown him the ugliest room in her heart and he had simply hung a lamp in it. "The magistrate's writ holds. The wreck and everything in it are the property of the heir of record. And the heir of record is—" he paused, savoring it the way he'd savor it every time, she suspected, for years, "—Captain Elodie Vane-Meridian."

The name still landed strangely. Like a boot that fit but hadn't been worn in fifteen years.

"Say it again," she said.

"Elodie." He turned her gently by the shoulder, and the dawn caught the gray at his temple, the salt crust on his collar, the look in his eyes that had survived everything she'd thrown at it. "Elodie Vane-Meridian. Ranger, first class. Heir to a drowned ship. Terrible liar. The woman I—"

"Don't ruin it with flattery."

"I was going to say *the woman I'm having breakfast with*, but sure, we can go with terrible liar."

She laughed, and the sound went out over the water and came back off the cave walls, and for a moment she let herself believe the cave was laughing too.

* * *

Voss was gone by the third day.

She'd known he would be. Men like Casimir Voss didn't stay anywhere; staying was a debt and he'd spent his whole life dodging those. But he'd stayed long enough. The wound in his side had closed to an angry red seam under Sef's careful stitching and the waystation's bitter poultices, and he'd eaten her food, drunk her coffee, and sat through three evenings of silence broken only by the sea, and then on the third morning his bedroll was empty and his cup was washed and set upside down to dry, which was as close to a farewell as his kind ever came.

He'd left something on the table. The half-burned chart—the one that had started all of it, the coastline that shouldn't exist, drawn in a hand she now recognized as her grandfather's. The burned edge had been trimmed neat. Along the bottom, in Voss's quick slanted script: *Paid in full. Don't spend it all on honesty.*

She stood holding it for a long time.

Fifteen years she had chased that man across the salt, and in the end he had stolen a map to give her back herself. There was a shape to that she suspected she'd spend the rest of her life turning over. Thieves, her old instructor used to say, are just historians who work in reverse—they take the present to get at the past. Voss had taken her past and handed it back at gunpoint, more or less, and then bled for it on a beach, and then vanished before anyone could make him be grateful or be thanked.

"Forgiven?" Sef asked from the doorway. He'd read her stillness correctly, as he always did now.

"Forgiven," she said. "Not forgotten. I want him on a watchlist for at least a decade. It's the only way I'll know he's still alive."

Sef smiled and poured the coffee. Outside, the basin was already draining, the water retreating in long silver sheets, and the cave's mouth was closing like a patient eye.

* * *

The paperwork took a week, because the desert abhors a vacuum but it worships a form.

She signed her name eleven times in the magistrate's office at Cinders Landing, and the first six times her hand hesitated above the line, the old reflex firing—*Crane, just write Crane, Xanthe Crane, no one needs to know*—before she made herself write it. *Elodie Vane-Meridian.* The clerk looked at it, looked at her, and said, "That'll need verifying against the registry," and she said, "I *am* the registry," and the clerk, who had been on the receiving end of fifteen years of Ranger Crane's incident reports, decided not to argue.

The writ came back stamped and sealed. The Meridian Star, its cargo, its strongbox, its debts, and its cave were the legal property of the heir of record, in perpetuity, unclaimable by salvage, untransferable by contract, unassailable by any fleet that cared to test the matter. Adhara Quill's lawyers had sent one letter. It was very polite. It offered a great deal of money. Xanthe used the back of it to draft her report to the Ranger Corps, which was, in its entirety:

*Find: one wreck, one cave, one heir. The heir is me. The cave is closed. The contract is burned. Request: that I be allowed to keep my post, my name, and my silence, in that order.*

The reply came back from a director she'd never met, in a hand that suggested the director had read her file twice: *Granted. And Crane—or Meridian, or whatever you're calling yourself this season—the Corps has always known there was more to you than salt. We're glad you finally told us what.*

She read it four times. Then she folded it into the strongbox, which now sat in her quarters at the waystation, empty of everything except the things she chose to put in it. That was the whole point of a strongbox, she was learning. Not what it kept out. What it let you keep.

* * *

On the last evening of the wet season, the water left for good.

She and Sef walked out onto the basin as the tide withdrew, the mud firm under their boots, the air smelling of brine and hot stone. The cave mouth stood above the tideline, dark and dry and sealed—she had watched the last of the water slide out of it at noon, and now it was just rock again, unremarkable, a shadow in the cliff face that no one would ever look at twice. Somewhere behind that rock lay a hundred-year-old ship and a strongbox of her grandfather's and the bones, maybe, of the registry's other signatories, the ones whose names had indicted a salvage house and ended a fleet's ambitions with nothing but a reading aloud and the truth.

The crews had scattered after the summit. Some had signed on with coastal traders; a few had asked her, sheepishly, if the Rangers were hiring. Quill had retreated with her flagship and her iron hand and whatever was left of her reputation, and the desert had already begun sanding the whole affair smooth, the way the desert did with everything. In a year it would be a story told badly in waystation bars. In ten it would be a legend with the wrong hero. Xanthe found she didn't mind. Legends were for people who needed to be remembered. She had spent fifteen years learning how good it felt to be forgotten, and now she had learned the other thing too: that you could be known, truly known, by exactly one person, and it would weigh less than water and hold more than a fleet.

"Tell me what you see," Sef said. It was an old game between them, from the long patrols, before any of this. *Tell me what you see.* The answer was supposed to be tactical.

She looked out at the basin, drying into white. At the cliff, and the shadow in it. At the horizon where the sea had come from and would come from again, someday, for someone else.

"Home," she said.

Sef was quiet a moment. "That's not a tactical answer."

"Fifteen years of tactical answers. I'm retired from them."

He took her hand. They walked back toward the waystation as the sun went down behind the cliffs, and the light came off the salt in long rose-gold sheets, and the first stars of the dry season came out over the water that had found her.

Inside, she lit the lamp, and on the wall above the desk she hung the chart—Voss's chart, her grandfather's coastline, the burned edge trimmed neat. Under it, in her own hand, on a card from the magistrate's own stationery, she wrote the only inscription the wreck would ever get:

*The Meridian Star. Admission paid.*

Then she put the kettle on, because Sef was already asking, and outside the desert went on being the desert, vast and salt and silent, keeping its secrets, and keeping hers.
