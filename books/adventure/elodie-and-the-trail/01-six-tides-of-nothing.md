## 1. Six Tides of Nothing

The thief left boot prints like an apology — small, light, always half a step from where a sensible person would have stepped. Astrid Whitlock crouched over the latest of them at the edge of the salt pan and allowed herself the small luxury of being annoyed about it.

Three years. Three years of these prints. She could have drawn them from memory for a sketch artist: narrow heel, worn outside edge, the left one landing a fraction heavier than the right because Elodie Vane favored that side when she carried weight. Astrid knew her quarry's feet better than she knew most people's faces.

"That's insulting, you know," she told the print. "You're not even trying anymore."

The salt flats gave nothing back but glare. Noon out here was a white argument between sky and ground, no horizon to speak of, just heat shimmering up off the crust until the world looked like it had been left too long in an oven. Astrid straightened, worked the kink out of her lower back — three years of sleeping on bedrock will do that to a woman — and took a bearing off the sun.

Elodie was heading northeast. Toward the old riverbed, which was dry as a sermon and had been for forty years, and which meant she was either lost or after something. Elodie Vane was never lost. So: something.

Astrid followed.

She'd made a kind of peace with the chase by now, the way you make peace with a bad knee. It ached, but it was hers. There was a rhythm to it — read the sign, guess the mind, be wrong, be right, be wrong again, close the gap by inches. Other rangers talked about retirement like it was a place. Astrid privately suspected she'd retire the way a river retired: all at once, somewhere out of sight, and nobody would find the dry bed for years.

Her radio crackled at midday, right when she'd found a shade rock the size of a dinner table and was negotiating with it for full coverage.

"Whitlock. Base."

She unclipped it, held it near her mouth, and didn't bother hiding the sigh. "Go ahead, Base."

"You're ordered to break off the Vane pursuit. Effective now."

Astrid waited for the punchline. It didn't come. "Say again, Base. I think the heat got into your transmission."

"Break off the pursuit, Whitlock. We've got a situation upriver. The Calloway expedition has missed three scheduled check-ins. Three. Dispatch wants eyes on them today." A pause, and then, in the tone of a man who knew exactly what he was asking, "I know. I'm sorry. Orders are orders."

Astrid looked at the boot prints going northeast without her.

Three missed check-ins. Expeditions missed check-ins for boring reasons all the time — dead battery, buried antenna, someone deciding the schedule mattered less than a promising dig site. She'd been sent after silent expeditions twice before. Once it was a broken fuel line. Once it was a love affair nobody wanted to file paperwork about.

But three in a row, from a crew as disciplined as Calloway's? Calloway ran her expeditions like tide tables. She checked in the way other people breathed.

"Base," Astrid said slowly, "confirm the diversion is temporary. Because I've got fresh sign here, and if I lose her over a radio problem—"

"The diversion stands until Calloway's accounted for. That's not a radio problem, Whitlock. That's a missing eleven people." Base's voice softened by a degree, which for Base was practically a hug. "Look at it this way. She's headed northeast anyway. You'll be walking the same direction. Consider it a scenic route."

"She's not scenery, Base. She's mine."

"I heard you the first time. And the forty-first time. Get moving."

Astrid clipped the radio back on her belt and stood there a moment longer than was strictly professional, letting the resentment have its little parade. Then she shouldered her pack, tipped an imaginary hat to the boot prints, and turned northeast herself.

* * *

The riverbed announced itself an hour later as a change in the ground's mood — salt giving way to cracked clay, clay to silt gone hard as pottery, and then the broad dry channel itself, curving away toward the hills like a snake that had died mid-thought. Forty years dead, they said. The Meridian had been a real river once; there were towns along it with boat-shaped weathervanes that nobody under thirty could explain.

Astrid walked the channel floor because it was flat and fast and because anything traveling toward the wreck would travel through it. The wreck. Everyone called it that, though officially it was the Old Meridian Courthouse, drowned when the river changed its mind in the flood year and swallowed the county seat whole. Half the building still stood above the waterline at the far end of the basin, where a shallow lake had settled and refused to leave — a gray stone hulk with its courthouse dome poking up like a man refusing to admit he'd fallen through ice.

They said the records inside were still sealed. Flood-sealed, vault-sealed, sealed by mud and time and the particular stubbornness of government filing. Eleven people had come to argue with that seal, and now they weren't answering their radios.

By late afternoon the channel walls rose around her, baked clay streaked with old waterlines like tree rings, and Astrid's shadow stretched long ahead of her, pointing the way like a bad omen. She chewed the last of her jerky and let her mind do what it did best, which was worry a problem like a dog with a bone.

Here was the thing that bothered her. Not the silence — silence happened. What bothered her was the arithmetic. The salvage fleet was in port at Meridian Landing, six tides from sailing, and everyone knew it. Everyone knew it because the fleet made sure everyone knew it; Captain Brannock Voss treated publicity like ballast. Six tides, and then the fleet would be out on the water doing whatever it was salvage fleets did when nobody important was watching.

And eleven careful people had stopped answering their radios just now. Just in that window.

Coincidence, probably. Astrid had built a career on distrusting coincidences, but she was tired, and tired people were allowed to suspect things without acting on them.

She topped a bend in the channel as the sun went molten behind the western bluffs, and the smell reached her before anything else did — woodsmoke, and under it something sweeter and worse that she knew from field cremations and hoped she was misreading.

Then the vultures confirmed she wasn't.

There were six of them, sitting in the dead branches of a snag that leaned out of the channel wall, patient as clerks. Below them, tucked into a cutbank where the old ferry landing used to be, was the expedition camp — or the architecture of one. Two tents shredded. A generator sitting open like a gutted fish. Gear scattered with a thoroughness that wasn't looting so much as housekeeping, somebody making very sure nothing worked, nothing recorded, nothing remained.

Astrid drew her sidearm out of habit, though the camp was empty of everything except the wind and, near the fire ring, a single figure lying on his side with his knees drawn up.

He was alive. Barely. One of Calloway's surveyors — she recognized him distantly from a supply depot two seasons back, a thin man named Okafor who'd been famous for his coffee. He was famous for nothing now except breathing, each breath a small wet argument.

Astrid knelt and did what she could, which was water and shade and holding his hand while the vultures watched with professional interest.

"Calloway," he managed, when she asked. "They took... Voss's divers came up the channel at night. Took the records barge. Took—" A cough that went on too long. "Courthouse. They're stripping the vault. Everything sealed. Everything." His fingers tightened on hers with surprising strength. "Said the judge's files. Said burn what won't float."

"Who said? Voss?"

But Okafor's eyes had gone past her, fixed on something over her shoulder with the last of his attention, and his mouth shaped a word that might have been *run*.

Astrid turned, sidearm up.

Standing at the lip of the cutbank, silhouetted against the last red stripe of sunset, was a slight figure with a familiar narrow heel and a canvas satchel slung across her body — a figure Astrid had chased across three years and four hundred miles of nowhere, who looked down at the dying man, then at Astrid, and said, in a voice like a struck bell:

"Ranger. Of course it's you."
