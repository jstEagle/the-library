## 2. The Drowned Courthouse

The riverbed ran dry for eleven miles before it ran wet again, and Astrid Whitlock walked the whole of it with her collar up and her resentment simmering like a kettle left on a forgotten burner.

Three years. Three years of following Elodie's trail across the salt flats — a boot print here, a lifted canteen there, once an entire pack mule returned with a note pinned to its bridle that read *You need him more than I do. — E.* Three years of a chase so long it had stopped feeling like a job and started feeling like a marriage, and now Dispatch wanted her to drop it for a *missing persons welfare check* on a bunch of academics who couldn't be bothered to turn their radio dial.

"Ranger Whitlock, confirm diversion to Calloway expedition site." The radio crackled with the particular smugness of a dispatcher who had never been rained on.

"Confirmed," Astrid said, and did not add *under protest*, though she said it loudly in her head, where it counted.

The Calloway expedition had come upriver six weeks ago with tents, instruments, and the cheerful confidence of people who believed the desert owed them something. The riverbed itself was the mystery: the maps called it the Serpent, and the Serpent had drowned half a county forty years back — a courthouse, a records annex, a whole town square gone under when the flood year came — before the water sulked off into the earth again and left the ruins standing in a basin of cracked mud like dishes left in a sink.

Astrid smelled the camp before she saw it. Woodsmoke, yes, but under it something sweeter and worse.

The tents were slashed. Not searched — *slashed*, all of them, methodical, the work of people who wanted the camp dead, not robbed. Instruments lay shattered. A theodolite had been driven into the mud like a stake. And in the center of it, laid out on a tarp with a canteen and a pistol placed beside him like an offering, a man was dying.

He was maybe sixty, gray-bearded, with the sunburned forearms of a field man. He heard her boots and turned his head with the economy of someone conserving everything.

"Ranger," he said. "You're late."

"Dr. Calloway?" She knelt, checked the wound — a gut shot, days old, dressed badly and bravely. "Who did this?"

"Salvage fleet. Meridian flag." He smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, that smile. "Six ships came up the dry river on skids. Six. You could hear the winches for a mile. My students thought it was a rescue." His breath caught. "They wanted to know where we'd found the records annex. Sealed levels. Below the waterline. I told them nothing, and then I watched them ask the others less politely."

Astrid's hand had gone to her radio and stopped there. "How many of your people are left?"

"One. A grad student. She's in the rocks up-slope with the last radio that works. She's the one who finally raised Dispatch, after your office spent three check-ins deciding whether we were worth the fuel." His eyes were clear, which was worse than if they'd been clouded. "Ranger. Listen. The annex has sealed record cylinders. Forty years sealed. Court records. The fleet means to strip them and melt them down for the salvage value, and nobody will ever read them. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"No," Astrid said honestly.

"Neither did I, until the shooting started." He gripped her wrist with surprising strength. "There's a name that matters down there. I heard the fleet's divers talking. *Vasquez.* Judge Vasquez. Whatever's sealed in that annex, he signed it, and he wants it under forty feet of water and forty feet of silence forever."

The name landed in Astrid's chest like a dropped stone. Vasquez. The judge who had signed the warrant on the thief she'd chased for three years — the warrant her own mentor, Ranger Captain Ilsa Reyes, had co-signed. The case that had started the whole long marriage of pursuit.

"Dr. Calloway—"

"Go," he said. "Or don't. But if you don't, then when the fleet finishes, ride back here and tell my student the desert won. She'd want to know it wasn't personal."

* * *

She buried him at dusk, under a slab of flood-ripped masonry, and marked it with his theodolite, driven straight. Then she walked down into the drowned courthouse basin in the last blue light, because she was a ranger, and the records were jurisdiction, and jurisdiction was the one thing she had left that made sense.

The courthouse stood out of the mud like the ribcage of something enormous. Water still pooled in its basement levels, black and still, reflecting the first stars. Astrid came in through what had been a loading dock, pistol out, lamp low, and stood in the dark of the ground-floor archive listening to the building settle.

And then, from below — a sound. Small. Rhythmic. The unmistakable *scrape-scrape-clink* of a pry bar being worked by someone who was very good at it and in no hurry at all.

Astrid descended the flooded stair with the water at her knees and her lamp hooded, and came around a fallen shelving unit into a long basement room where the water lay mirror-still, and a figure in a wetsuit top stood waist-deep beside a ruptured cabinet, working a sealed record cylinder loose with a pry bar and a patience that looked almost devotional.

Three years. A hundred sightings, all of them wrong or too late. And here, at last, in the lamplight: a lean woman with wet hair plastered to her neck and the unbothered expression of someone interrupted mid-breakfast.

"Elodie," Astrid said.

The thief didn't startle. She looked up, took in the ranger, the badge, the pistol, and sighed the sigh of a woman greeting a relative she liked better at a distance.

"Whitlock." She said the name the way you'd name a weather pattern. "You know, most people would've taken the hint after the first mule."

"Step away from the cylinder."

"It's not mine. Technically it's the court's. Technically the court's been dead forty years, so it's *nobody's*, which in salvage law makes it mine." Elodie hefted it, considering. "Also I was here first, which in desert law makes it mine."

"Desert law isn't a thing."

"It's the only law with a hundred percent enforcement rate." Elodie waded a step closer, and the lamplight caught her face, and Astrid saw — for the first time in three years of photographs and secondhand descriptions — that the thief looked tired. Not hunted-tired. Something older. "You're not here for me, though. I watched you bury the doctor. I watched you sit by his grave a long time." Her eyes went to the cylinder. "You heard the name too. Vasquez."

Astrid's pistol did not waver, but something behind it did. "How do you know that name?"

"Because it's mine." Elodie said it plainly, without theater, which was somehow more shocking than theater would have been. "Not Elodie. That's the name I picked when the other one stopped working. My name is Mara Vasquez. The judge's daughter. And when I was nineteen I found out my father had been selling sealed verdicts out of this annex for years — erasing convictions, manufacturing others, whatever paid. So I stole the proof. And the proof of what I stole was that I stole it, so he signed a warrant, and a ranger captain named Reyes co-signed it, and the desert got a thief named Elodie, and my father got to keep his courthouse clean."

The water dripped somewhere far off. Astrid stood very still in it, knee-deep, three years deep.

"Reyes," she said. "Ilsa Reyes trained me."

"I know," Elodie said gently. "I've read your file. I steal things, remember. Including, lately, the habit of keeping up with the ranger who chases me. You're the only one who never gave up. I wanted to know why." She turned the cylinder in her hands. "This is one of his seal-rooms. There are more. The fleet's divers hit the north annex an hour ago — I heard the detonation charges. They've got six tides before the basin floods again, and they mean to take every cylinder and melt them, and my father's signature with them."

Above them, faint through forty feet of stone and mud, came a sound: the deep, patient grind of a fleet's winches, working in the dark.

Astrid holstered her pistol. It felt less like a decision than like a joint finally agreeing to bend.

"You're under arrest," she said.

Elodie raised an eyebrow.

"Eventually," Astrid said. "After. Six tides. You know this wreck better than the fleet does, and I know how rangers think, which is to say I know exactly how stupid they'll be. We do this together or we both lose."

For a long moment the thief looked at her, and something moved behind her face — surprise, maybe, or the beginning of a laugh, or the end of one.

"Ranger Whitlock," Elodie said at last, holding out the cylinder, "I have dreamed of hearing you say something foolish for three years. I just always imagined it would be *you're under arrest* with more conviction."

"That was my conviction."

"Then take it." Elodie pressed the cylinder into her hands and nodded upward, toward the grind of the winches, toward the fleet, toward the water that would come back in six tides and take everything. "Because here's the part you're not going to like, Astrid. The record that convicts my father? The one with your mentor's name on the warrant beside his?" She smiled, and it wasn't a happy smile. "It's on the lowest level. Below the waterline. Where the fleet's divers are already going."

The winches ground on above them, patient as tide.
