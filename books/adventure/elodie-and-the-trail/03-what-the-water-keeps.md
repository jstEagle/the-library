## 3. What the Water Keeps

**Elodie**

The courthouse had been drowning for forty years, and it had not gotten any better at it.

Elodie stood at the waterline where the great staircase vanished into green-black stillness, and she thought, not for the first time, that Judge Aldous Vane had built himself a monument to permanence and the river had disagreed. The columns leaned like tired men. The pediment that once read JUSTICE IS PATIENT was now missing half its letters, so it promised only ICE IS PA.

"Charming," Astrid said behind her.

"It grows on you."

"Everything out here grows on you. That's the problem with the desert." The ranger crouched at the water's edge and studied the flooded stairwell the way she'd study a trailhead — which, Elodie supposed, was exactly what it was. "How deep?"

"Forty feet to the archive doors. Fifty to the records vault." Elodie pulled the rebreather rig from her pack — two of them, salvaged from the Calloway camp before the fleet arrived, because she'd known even then that she wouldn't be doing this alone. She'd just assumed her partner would be unwilling rather than armed. "The air pockets in the upper floors buy us most of the way. There are three long swims. I've done them twice."

"And the third time is the charm, or the third time is when the building finishes falling on you?"

"That depends entirely on how fast we move." Elodie handed her a rig. "Which is why I'm going to say something I never thought I'd say to a law officer: try not to arrest anything down there. It'll slow us both."

Astrid took the rig without smiling, but something at the corner of her mouth twitched, and Elodie counted it as a victory. Three days ago this woman had put a rope snare around her ankle and dragged her out of a gully by her bootlaces. Now they were about to hold hands underwater, more or less. Life was strange. Life on the run was stranger.

"The divers," Astrid said. "How long do we have?"

Elodie looked upriver, toward the fleet anchored off the north bank — six hulls riding low with salvage cranes, their running lights burning through the dusk like a string of patient lanterns. "They'll wait for full dark. Meridian doesn't dive blind; too many bodies in there already." She said it flatly, because saying it any other way would mean thinking about the Calloway people, and she couldn't afford that yet. Grief was heavy, and she was about to swim with it. "So. One tide. Maybe less."

Astrid settled the mouthpiece around her neck. "Then let's go rob a courthouse."

"You know," Elodie said, wading in, "for a ranger, you're getting awfully comfortable with the vocabulary."

* * *

**Astrid**

The water was colder than the desert had any right to allow, and darker than either of them had promised.

Astrid followed the glow of Elodie's lamp through corridors that had been hallways once — past doorways with brass numbers still legible, past a drinking fountain growing a garden of white silt-plants, past a wall calendar frozen on a month neither of them could read. Fish scattered ahead of them like startled punctuation. Somewhere above, the fleet's engines idled, a sound she felt in her teeth more than heard.

*Three years,* she thought, kicking through a doorway marked CLERK OF RECORDS. *Three years chasing this woman across every flat and arroyo in the territory, and here I am following her into a drowned building because she knows the way better than I do.*

It should have stung. What actually stung was how natural it felt.

Elodie paused at a junction, checked a grease-pencil mark on the wall — her own, from a previous visit — and pointed left. Astrid nodded. They swam. The air pocket in the old hearing chamber came as a shock: a dome of trapped breath, silvered and trembling, big enough to stand chest-deep in while they caught their wind.

"Ten minutes," Elodie gasped, hauling herself onto a submerged bench. "Then the last swim. Vault's through the records office, down one level."

Astrid broke the surface beside her and pushed back her hood. In the lamplight the chamber looked almost holy — the judge's bench rising out of black water, the gallery benches floating loose like driftwood arranged by someone with opinions. "You've been here twice," she said. "Before the fleet ever came. Why? You didn't know what the Calloway expedition knew."

"I didn't need to." Elodie wrung out her braid. "I grew up in the territory below this river. When the dam failed and the valley flooded, my mother worked the salvage crews. She used to say the courthouse went under with all its paperwork still sealed, and whatever Vane buried stayed buried." Her voice did something complicated on the name *Vane*. "I came looking for my own file. Turns out I'm not in it. Turns out I'm not anywhere in it. Do you understand what that means, Ranger? A woman convicted of theft in Vane's court, sentenced, transported, escaped — and there's no record she ever existed. He didn't just frame me. He erased me."

Astrid was quiet. Then, because honesty seemed suddenly cheap compared to what they were about to risk: "What's your real name? Not Elodie-and-the-Trail. Not the name on three years of warrants."

The thief looked at her for a long moment. "Mariam Osei," she said. "My mother called me Mariam."

"Mariam." Astrid tried it out. It fit better than she expected. "Whose warrant carried your conviction? Somebody signed it. Even Vane needed a magistrate to countersign an erasure order — territory law, Article Nine."

Something flickered across Mariam's face. "You know the articles better than I do."

"I was raised on them." Astrid checked her depth gauge, buying a moment. "My mentor made me memorize them. Sergeant Ruth Calloway. Twenty-two years a ranger. She taught me that the badge was a promise, not a permission slip." She stopped. The water lapped. Somewhere far off, metal groaned against metal — the fleet, getting ready.

Mariam had gone very still. "Calloway," she repeated. "As in the *expedition* Calloway."

"As in—" Astrid's stomach dropped through the floor of the hearing chamber, through the flooded office below it, through forty years of river silt. "No."

"She countersigned it." Mariam's voice was gentle, which was somehow worse than if it had been triumphant. "I found the order two visits ago, in the clerk's duplicate safe. I memorized the seal number. I can recite it if you want. I've had three years to wonder who would sign away a nineteen-year-old girl's whole existence, and now I finally get to watch you find out."

*Ruth.* Astrid saw it whole in a single terrible instant: the quiet dinners, the corrections in the margins of her reports, the way Ruth had gone pale — *pale, she'd thought it was grief* — the day Astrid requested the Elodie case file. The way she'd said, *some chases aren't worth finishing, Astrid-girl*, and Astrid had taken it for wisdom instead of warning.

"She taught me everything," Astrid said. Her voice came out steady, which felt like a betrayal of its own.

"Then she taught you well enough to be down here," Mariam said. "That has to count for something. It's going to have to."

Astrid pulled her hood up. The lamp beam wavered once before she steadied it. "Vault," she said. "Now. Before I start feeling things I can't swim with."

* * *

**Elodie**

The vault door was bronze, and bronze, thank every patient god, does not rust shut.

Mariam worked the combination lock by feel and memory while Astrid held the light, and if the ranger's hands were shaking, she had the grace not to mention it. The wheel gave on the fourth try with a groan that echoed down the flooded corridor like a held breath finally released.

Inside: shelves, silt, and row upon row of sealed cylinders — forty years of verdicts, land grants, convictions, pardons, all the machinery of a court that believed itself permanent. Mariam went straight to the third shelf, second row, and lifted out a cylinder stamped with a seal number she could have recited in her sleep.

"This is it. My erasure order, the original warrant, and—" she turned it in the light, "—Vane's private ledger. Every case he sold. Every name he erased. Enough to convict him ten times over, if he were alive, and enough to ruin everyone he bought if he isn't."

Astrid took it with both hands, the way you take something breakable, or sacred, or both. For a moment her face did something young and lost, and Mariam understood that the woman was holding her whole career in a bronze tube and finding it lighter than expected.

Then the building spoke.

Not groaned — *spoke*, a deep concussion rolling up through the water, through the floor, through their boots. Dust bloomed off the shelves in slow gray ghosts. The lamplight swung wild.

"They're bringing the supports down," Astrid breathed. "They're not waiting for dark. They're collapsing the east wing — sealing the archive."

The second detonation came before Mariam could answer, closer, and the corridor outside the vault filled with a sound like the river clearing its throat. Through the doorway she saw the far junction cave inward, silt and masonry folding down into blackness, cutting off the route they'd come by.

Above them, faint through forty feet of water, the fleet's engines changed pitch. Rising. Leaving. Letting the river do what rivers do.

Astrid looked at the cylinder in her hands, then at the collapsing corridor, then at Mariam — and Mariam watched three years of pursuit end and something else begin, all in the space of one breath.

"There's a service shaft behind the evidence lockers," Mariam said. "It runs up through the rotunda. If the east wing goes, the west might hold long enough."

"*Might*," Astrid said.

"I've never once lied to you, Ranger. I've stolen from you, evaded you, and dragged you by your own snare, but I have never lied."

Another detonation. The vault doorframe cracked like knuckles.

"West wing," Astrid said, and shoved the cylinder into her coat, and ran.
