## 4. Ash on the Flats

The causeway was going under by inches.

Astrid felt it first through her boots — a softness in the old stone, a shiver running up through her ankles like the building itself was sighing. Then came the sound: water finding new paths through drowned masonry, patient and unhurried, the way it had been for forty years and would be for forty more.

She stood at the top of the courthouse steps with the record cylinder tucked against her ribs and Captain Vess Halloway of the Meridian salvage fleet standing six steps below her, flanked by four divers with harpoon guns that looked far too clean for men who'd spent the morning murdering archaeologists.

"Ranger." Vess said it like a title she was handing back, already gift-wrapped. "You've had a long walk. Let's not make it longer."

"You killed the Calloway expedition," Astrid said. "Seven people. Two of them were students."

"The river kills people all the time. Very convenient river." Vess smiled. She was a broad woman with sun-scoured cheeks and the calm of someone who had never once been interrupted mid-sentence. "Now. The cylinder."

Behind Astrid, somewhere down the flooded corridor of the lower archive, there was a splash — small, controlled, deliberate. Elodie, moving toward the east window they'd scouted an hour ago, before the supports went and the whole back half of the courthouse began its slow, dignified collapse into the channel. Elodie moved through water the way other people moved through gossip: quietly, thoroughly, leaving nothing behind but a changed subject.

Astrid needed to give her ninety seconds. Maybe less.

"You know what's funny," Astrid said, shifting her weight so the cylinder pressed flat against her side. "I chased this woman for three years. Three years of salt flats and bad coffee and sleeping in my truck like a coyote with a pension. And you know what I never asked myself?"

"I genuinely don't care," said Vess.

"Whether she did it." Astrid took one step down. The divers' guns tracked her. Good — eyes on her meant eyes off the east window. "That's the embarrassing part. I'm a ranger. I'm supposed to ask. But a warrant's a comfortable thing to carry. You just hold onto it and it does your thinking for you."

Vess sighed, the sigh of a woman negotiating with weather. "The cylinder, Ranger. Or I let Renn here demonstrate the harpoon gun, and then we take it off your body, which is heavier and slower for everyone involved."

"Ninety seconds," Astrid murmured, mostly to herself.

"Pardon?"

"I said—" and here Astrid did the thing she'd been turning over in her head since the supports blew, the thing that had felt insane right up until it felt inevitable, "—you can't have them."

She brought the cylinder up in both hands. It was a sealed legal archive, forty years of court records fused into a ceramic core, waterproof, fireproof, rated to survive anything a river or a desert could do to it.

Anything except what Astrid did next, which was crack the emergency flare off her belt, strike it against her thigh plate, and hold the white-hot end directly against the seal.

The divers shouted. Vess actually took a step forward, which Astrid noted with some satisfaction was the first undignified thing the captain had done all day.

"That's forty years of—"

"—of a judge named Aldous Crane framing a nineteen-year-old girl for thefts he committed himself, yes," Astrid said. The seal was glowing now, weeping smoke. It smelled like burnt sugar and old paper. "I read the index while you were busy being evil on a schedule. Very thorough man, Judge Crane. Very organized criminal. The records prove everything. Which is exactly why nobody gets them."

"You burn those, you burn the proof *against* him too," Vess said, and for the first time her voice had something real in it — alarm, maybe, or greed doing an impression of alarm.

"Yeah." Astrid watched the flame eat into the ceramic. "Took me a while to make peace with that part."

Here was the arithmetic she'd done, alone, in the dark of the archive while Elodie kept watch: the records could clear Elodie's name, yes. They could also be taken by Vess Halloway, who would sell them back to Judge Crane's people for more money than Astrid would earn in nine lifetimes, and the girl would stay guilty forever, and the judge would retire comfortably, and the river would keep its dead. Or the records could become smoke, and Elodie would stay guilty on paper but free in fact, and Crane would spend the rest of his life wondering which of his old crimes might surface someday from some mouth he couldn't buy.

It wasn't justice. It was the shape justice leaves when you press it into wet sand.

But it was the only version left, and the tide was coming in.

The seal split with a sound like an egg cracking. Smoke poured out between Astrid's fingers, acrid and gray, and inside the casing the pages curled and blackened and went to ash that the water rising through the causeway stones would carry out into the flats by morning.

"No," Vess said, quite simply, as though the universe had misspoken.

"Sorry," said Astrid. "You seem upset. Would you like to file a complaint? I know a department. Terrible response times."

Renn raised his harpoon gun, and Astrid raised hers — because she still had it, still wore the badge above it, and whatever else was true, she was not going to die unarmed on a sinking staircase defending a thief's good name. The two weapons held each other in a long, stupid stalemate while the courthouse groaned around them and the water climbed another inch.

And then, from the east window, a whistle. Two notes. Low, then high.

Elodie's voice floated across the flooded hall, bright and unbothered, the voice of a woman sitting on a windowsill with her boots already dry: *"Captain! Your skiffs are taking on water. All three. Funny thing about courthouses — they've got valves everywhere, and I've had a very educational afternoon."*

Astrid didn't look. Looking would have ruined it. But she heard the divers break formation, heard Vess swear with genuine feeling, heard boots splashing away down the corridor toward three sinking boats and a very long swim home.

Which left Astrid alone on the stairs with a burning cylinder, a rising tide, and a choice about which direction to walk.

* * *

She caught up with Elodie a mile out on the flats, where the salt crusted white and endless and the drowned courthouse sat behind them like a gray tooth in the earth, half-swallowed, keeping its last secrets.

Elodie was walking backward, facing her, hands in her pockets, grinning like the sunset had been arranged personally for her benefit.

"So," she said. "Three years."

"Three years."

"And you never once caught me."

"I caught you twice. You talked your way out both times. I've decided that counts as catching you."

"It absolutely doesn't." Elodie fell into step beside her — beside, not ahead, not running, which Astrid noticed and decided not to mention. "For the record, I had a speech prepared. For when you finally cornered me. About how I was innocent and the system failed me and you should feel bad. Very moving. Rehearsed it in mirrors."

"Why didn't you use it?"

"Because you burned forty years of evidence to cover my escape instead of trading it for your badge back." Elodie shrugged. "Felt rude to follow that with a speech."

They walked. The flats went violet, then ash-gray, then blue. Somewhere behind them the Meridian fleet was bailing out its skiffs and composing a report that would blame the river, the same convenient river. Somewhere ahead, a town with lights, and after that more towns, and none of them owed either of them anything.

At the truck — her truck, parked where she'd left it two days ago, sand in the engine and hope in the glovebox — Astrid keyed the radio one last time.

"Dispatch, this is Whitlock. Ranger unit seven." She turned the badge over in her palm, brass worn smooth at the edges from three years of nervous thumbing. "Recovering the Calloway investigation materials. Perpetrators fled by water. Evidence destroyed in the structural collapse. No further pursuit authorized." A pause. "Also, I quit. Effective immediately. Tell Commander Ruiz the coffee machine in my office is his problem now."

Static. Then Dispatch, confused: *"Whitlock, say again your last?"*

Astrid set the radio down, dropped the badge into the cup holder where it could rattle around for the rest of its natural life, and started the engine.

"She says again nothing," Elodie said from the passenger seat, feet on the dash, uninvited and entirely expected. "So. Where are we going?"

"We?" Astrid pulled onto the hardpan, heading nowhere in particular, which felt — for the first time in three years — like a destination. "There's no warrant anymore. No chase. You're free to go anywhere you want."

"I know." Elodie tipped her head back and closed her eyes, smiling at the ceiling of the cab like it was a sky. "That's why I'm asking where *we're* going. First place in six years I've gotten to pick."

Astrid drove west, into the last of the light, and found — to her considerable surprise — that she had an answer ready.
