## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Names

The river was running high that morning, the color of old pewter, and Kestrel Amberdale — who had not answered to that name in six years — stood at the edge of the Vess trail and listened to it the way other men listened for their own heartbeats.

"Emrys." The voice behind him was silk stretched over impatience. "You're certain this is safe?"

He turned. Master Corvane Aldous-Prell, third son of a shipping magnate, stood in boots that had never touched anything rougher than a ballroom floor, adjusting a hat with a feather on it as though the mountain might be impressed. Behind him trailed two porters Kestrel had hired out of the dockside taverns, both already sweating under loads they'd been paid too little to love.

"The trail is safe," Kestrel said. "The river isn't. Stay between me and the drop-off, and you'll live to bore your friends with the story."

Aldous-Prell's mouth thinned. He was perhaps thirty-five, soft in the way money makes men soft, but there was something underneath the softness — a quickness in the eyes when he looked at the water, at the scree slopes above, at Kestrel himself. A collector's eyes. Kestrel had guided enough wealthy men down enough trails to know the type: they came to the mountains to acquire them, the way they acquired everything else.

"Emrys," the man said again, tasting the name. "That's a border name, isn't it? Old one."

"It's mine," Kestrel said, which was true, and moved off up the trail before more questions could take root.

* * *

The first three miles were kind. The Vess river trail followed the water through a gorge of grey granite, the path cut into the cliffside by hands long dead — Callowmere hands, though no map said so anymore. Twenty years ago this had been a royal road, trodden by merchants and pilgrims heading toward a capital that glittered somewhere beyond the high passes. Now it was a smugglers' track and a guide's livelihood, and the stones remembered nothing, or pretended not to.

Kestrel walked it the way he walked everything: reading the ground. A guide who didn't read the ground was a corpse with a ledger. He noted the seep line on the north wall, two fingers higher than last month — wet rock meant loose rock above the second switchback. He noted the fresh talus fan below the old slide chute, pale stone scattered across the darker trail like spilled salt. Something had come down recently. Something could come down again.

"You walk like a soldier," Aldous-Prell observed from behind him. The man had shed his city pace within the first mile, which surprised Kestrel slightly; he'd expected to be carrying him by noon.

"I walk like a guide."

"No. Guides walk like men trying not to fall. You walk like a man who has decided falling is someone else's problem." Aldous-Prell smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which made it worse. "Where did you learn it?"

"Everywhere," Kestrel said. "The mountains teach everyone eventually. Some lessons are just more expensive than others."

"And where did you buy yours?"

Kestrel let the silence answer. Ahead, the gorge narrowed, and the river's voice rose from a mutter to a argument. He heard the porters shift their loads, heard Aldous-Prell's breathing settle into the rhythm of a man who had decided to enjoy being uncomfortable. Rich men did that. They bought hardship the way they bought wine, confident it would never actually hurt them.

By midday they had climbed above the gorge into the open bowl beneath Raven's Tooth, where the trail crossed a long slope of scree — ten thousand fragments of shattered slate, each one waiting its turn to move. Kestrel stopped the party at the edge and crouched, laying his palm flat against the surface.

"What are you doing?" Aldous-Prell asked.

"Listening."

"To rocks?"

"To what the rocks are doing." Kestrel rose. "Scree isn't ground. It's a crowd. Every stone wants to go downhill; most of them are just waiting for permission. We cross light, we cross spaced out, and nobody stops in the middle. If I say run, you don't ask where. Understood?"

Something flickered across the client's face — the collector's interest again, sharpening. "You talk about it like it's alive."

"Everything up here is alive. That's the part the maps leave out."

They started across. Kestrel went first, choosing each step with a deliberation that looked like superstition and wasn't: he was reading the angle of repose, testing the way the slope's skin shifted under his weight, listening for the deep grumble that meant the whole hillside was thinking about moving. The porters followed his line exactly. Aldous-Prell, to his credit, kept close and kept quiet.

They were two-thirds across when the mountain cleared its throat.

It began as a sound Kestrel felt through his boots before he heard it — a low shifting groan, deep in the slope, like a giant turning over in sleep. Then the world tilted. Not much. A hand's width. But on scree, a hand's width is a sentence being read aloud.

"Move!" Kestrel spun, grabbed the nearest porter by the pack strap, and hauled him downslope off the line. "Off the trail! Down and left, now!"

The porter stumbled, obeyed. The second porter was already scrambling. And Aldous-Prell — Aldous-Prell had frozen, standing dead center on the slope with his feathered hat askew, watching a hundred tons of slate begin to remember gravity.

Kestrel didn't think. Thinking was for afterward. He ran uphill — uphill, against every instinct screaming in his legs — closed the distance in six strides, and hit the man square in the chest with both hands, driving him sideways off the moving line just as the slide let go behind them.

The sound was enormous. The world became noise and dust and the particular terrible music of a mountainside rearranging itself. Kestrel wrapped his arms around the client and rolled them both downslope, riding the outer edge of the flow, feeling stones hammer past close enough to break bone, steering with his heels, his shoulders, the whole grammar of his body speaking a language he hadn't chosen to learn but had learned anyway, at a price he tried not to count.

Then it stopped. Dust hung in the air like a held breath. Somewhere below, the river kept talking, indifferent.

Kestrel lay on his back on solid ground, chest heaving, a fine grey powder settling over him like ash. Beside him, Aldous-Prell coughed, spat, and stared at the scar the slide had carved down the slope — a raw brown wound forty yards wide where the trail had been.

"That," the man said finally, "was not in your brochure."

"No."

"You pushed me."

"Yes."

"Uphill. You ran *uphill*." Aldous-Prell turned his head, and his eyes had changed. The collector's curiosity was gone. In its place was something colder and far more dangerous: recognition. The look of a man who had just realized the thing he'd purchased was worth considerably more than he'd paid. "Men don't do that. Men run away from slides. I've read the accounts. Even famous guides run. You ran *toward* it like you'd done the arithmetic beforehand and knew you'd win."

"Get up," Kestrel said. "We're losing light."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the treeline, and Kestrel felt the client's gaze on him across the fire like a hand resting on the back of his neck.

The porters slept. Aldous-Prell did not. He sat turning a tin cup in his fingers and watching Kestrel check the rope coils — checking them twice, three times, an old habit from a life where equipment failures had names attached.

"Emrys," he said at last. "Where were you trained?"

"There's no training for this work. There's only mistakes, and surviving them."

"See, that's interesting," Aldous-Prell said, "because I asked around in Vess before I hired you. I always ask around. And do you know what I found? Nothing. No record. No guild membership. No references older than four years. A man appears in a border town with skills that took someone twenty years to build, and no history at all." He smiled again, that not-unkind smile. "In my experience, Emrys, men without histories are men who've buried them."

Kestrel fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. Six years. Six years of being careful, of being forgettable, of answering to a name that fit him like a coat borrowed from a thinner man. And now some shipping heir with a feathered hat was picking at the seam because Kestrel had saved his life too well.

"The border eats records," Kestrel said. "Ask anyone."

"Oh, I have." Aldous-Prell sipped whatever was in the cup. "I also know what happened in the Serac Range seven years ago. Everyone in the trade knows. An expedition went up with a celebrated lead guide and came down four men short, and the guide was blamed, and the guide vanished, and the coalition struck his certification so thoroughly that even saying his name in certain rooms gets you looked at funny." He paused. "They say he was the best high-route man of his generation. Better than good. Uncanny, one account called it. Ran *toward* a collapsing icefall once to pull two clients clear, and won."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, a stone ticked loose and clicked down through the dark, and Kestrel's whole body went still without his permission.

"I don't know that story," he said.

"No," Aldous-Prell agreed pleasantly. "I imagine you wouldn't." He set down the cup, stretched, and settled back against his pack with the ease of a man who had gotten exactly what he came for. "Sleep well, Emrys. Tomorrow, the pass, yes? I'm told the view from the top is remarkable. All that country no one bothered to put on the maps."

Kestrel sat by the fire long after the client's breathing slowed into sleep, turning the conversation over like a stone he'd found in his boot. The man knew. Or suspected enough to be worse than knowing. Wealthy men talked, and wealthy men wrote letters, and somewhere in the machinery of the borderlands there were people paid to notice when a dead guide's ghost started walking trails under a borrowed name.

He looked east, where the mountains stacked themselves against the stars, range behind range behind range, and thought about how tired he was. Not body-tired — his body was the one honest thing he owned. Tired in the place where a name should sit. Six years of being Emrys, of flinching from his own reflection in shop windows, of guiding rich strangers through country he loved while pretending he had never been anyone at all.

Tomorrow he would take them over the pass, collect his fee, and disappear deeper into the borderlands. He'd done it a dozen times. He could do it again.

He was still telling himself that when he noticed, downstream along the river trail they'd climbed that morning, a single point of light moving through the dark. Too steady for a campfire. Too deliberate for a wanderer. Someone was coming up the Vess gorge at night, at pace, as if they knew exactly where they were going.

Kestrel banked the fire and woke the porters without a word.

They broke camp in the dark, and as they climbed away from the river, Kestrel looked back once. The light had stopped directly below their cold ashes, and it did not move again.

Someone, it seemed, had questions of their own.
