## 2. What the River Keeps

The Vess went mad in the night.

Kestrel woke to the sound of it — not the usual low conversation of water over stone but a grinding, bellowing roar, like some animal dragging itself through the town's throat. He was out of bed and at his window before his mind caught up with his body, an old reflex from years of sleeping in places where rivers decided things. The street below had become a brown tongue licking at the doorsteps of the chandlery across the lane. Debris rode the current: fence rails, a drowned sheep turning slowly, half a roof still wearing its shingles like a scalp of hair.

He watched it for an hour, coffee going cold in his hand, and felt something he couldn't immediately name. Not fear. Not quite. It was the feeling he got on a mountain when the weather turned — that sense of standing very still while something enormous rearranged the world around you, indifferent to whether you were part of what it moved or part of what it left behind.

By dawn the river had dropped as suddenly as it had risen, the way mountain-fed water does when whatever dam of rain or rockslide upstream finally gives out. Vess emerged blinking into gray light to survey its losses: two boats gone from the ferry moorings, the lower market stalls flattened into a raft of splintered melon crates, and along the banks a tideline of the river's leavings — mud, reeds, fish gasping in the shallows, and everything else the water had pried loose from the world above it.

Kestrel walked down to the bank because there was nothing else to do. His next client wasn't due until the following week, a wool merchant's son who wanted to be taken up to the tarns and photographed looking rugged. The morning belonged to no one. He told himself he was going to look for salvage — good rope washed downstream was rope you didn't have to buy, and Wren would pay him for anything worth reselling, which mattered when your debts had more digits than your savings.

That was the story he told himself. He'd gotten good at telling himself stories. It was practically the same trade as telling other people them.

* * *

The flood line was easy to read; it always was if you knew how. A band of darker stone two feet up the bank, silt settled in the crevices like frost. Below it, the ground was scoured clean and littered with the river's inventory. Kestrel worked the shallows methodically, the way he did everything, moving upstream against the current's last push, checking the eddies behind boulders where heavy things came to rest.

He found rope — forty feet of it, water-swollen but sound. He found a lantern with its glass intact. He found a child's boot, which he looked at longer than he wanted to before setting it back down in the mud.

It was the smell that stopped him. Underneath the wet-stone rot of the flood there was something else, older and stranger: lamp oil, tallow, paper gone soft. He followed it to a bend where the bank narrowed between two granite shoulders, a place he'd passed a hundred times without thinking about, and saw the corner of it wedged in the rocks like a tooth in a jaw.

A chest. Iron-banded, oak-planked, maybe three feet long, sitting in eighteen inches of water with the current combing through its seams. It should have been long gone — swept miles downstream, buried in silt, sunk past finding. Instead it sat there as if it had chosen to stop, jammed in the gap with a precision that made the hair on Kestrel's arms stand up. Rivers didn't set things down gently. Rivers threw things away.

He stood in the shallows for a full minute, water pushing at his calves, and let himself admit what he already knew: he didn't want to open it. Some part of him, the part that had kept him alive through twenty years of mountains and five years of borrowed names, was saying *walk away*. The river gave you things sometimes, and every one of them cost more than they looked.

But curiosity is a patient animal, and dread dresses itself in curiosity's clothes so well you can't tell them apart until it's too late.

He waded in. The chest weighed almost nothing in the water and everything once he dragged it onto the bank — he grunted, braced, hauled it up above the flood line onto dry gravel. Water poured from its seams in thin brown threads. The lock was a simple hasp, rusted through, and the lid came up with a groan of swollen wood and a breath of air that smelled like a tomb that had been flooded and then dried out and then flooded again: old paper, old metal, old time.

Inside, packed tight, were books.

Not books exactly. Journals — a dozen of them, bound in leather gone stiff and dark, their spines cracked, their pages furred with damp. Beneath them lay a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, and beneath that, flat and rectangular, something wrapped in waxed linen that clinked faintly when he shifted it.

Kestrel knelt in the gravel and did not touch any of it for a long moment. Then he picked up the topmost journal and opened it to the first page, because by now the thing in his chest that said *walk away* had lost to the thing that said *look*.

The handwriting was fine, slanted, confident. The ink had run in places where the water had reached, blooming into gray ghosts, but the first lines were legible:

*Field journal of Maren Vashti-Cole, Survey Corps of Callowmere, 14th year of the Restoration. We depart for the northern ranges tomorrow, and I record here my conviction that the commission we have been given is not the commission we have been told.*

Kestrel read the sentence twice.

*Callowmere.*

There was a word he hadn't heard spoken aloud in years, and never in Vess, and never anywhere within earshot of anyone who might repeat it. You learned early in the borderlands which words were stones you didn't turn over. Callowmere was the biggest of them. A kingdom that had been on maps when Kestrel was a boy — he could remember the shape of it, a thumbprint of territory pressed into the eastern ranges, its capital marked with a star — and then, twenty years ago, it simply stopped being there. Not conquered, officially. Not annexed. Erased. The maps were redrawn and the territory became "the Eastern Protectorates" and the people who had lived there became, depending on who was speaking, refugees, rebels, ghosts, or nobody at all. Children born after the erasure learned the name only as a warning, the way you learn the names of diseases.

And here it was, stamped in gold leaf on the inside cover of a journal the river had carried to his feet. Not just written — *stamped*. He turned the cover fully toward the light and saw the seal pressed into the leather: a circle bisected by a mountain peak, with a compass rose beneath it, eight points radiating outward.

Kestrel knew seals. Part of guiding wealthy clients was knowing whose crest was on whose luggage, which houses tipped well and which ones photographed themselves standing on summits they hadn't earned. He had never seen this seal. And yet some cold, deep part of him recognized it the way you recognize a face in a crowd from someone's description — secondhand, impossible, certain.

He unwrapped the oilcloth bundle. Inside were more journals, these older, water-damaged worse, their pages fused into bricks in places. And tucked among them, protected by the oilcloth better than the rest, a photograph.

It was a daguerreotype, the kind that cost real money, mounted in a folding case of velvet and tin. A woman held a child on her lap. The woman was thin and dark-haired and wore her exhaustion like a uniform; she looked at the camera the way soldiers look at it, as though it might be the last thing that remembers her. The child — a girl, he thought, though children that age are hard to read — had a shock of black curls and a solemnity far too old for her face, and on her left wrist, just visible below the sleeve of a white dress, a birthmark. Dark, irregular, shaped — he tilted the case toward the light — shaped like nothing much at all, or possibly like a bird in flight, or possibly like a flame.

On the back of the case, in the same fine slanted hand as the journal, someone had written four words:

*She is still hers.*

Kestrel sat back on his heels. The river talked behind him, settling back into its ordinary voice, and somewhere up the bank a cart rattled past on the high road, and none of it touched him. He was aware of the morning continuing around him the way you're aware of weather through a window.

*She is still hers.* Hers whose? Written by whom? A survey corps journal complaining of a false commission, sealed with the crest of a dead kingdom, wrapped in oilcloth — oilcloth, which is what you wrap things in when you intend them to survive water — and then committed to a river anyway. None of it fit together, and all of it fit together too well. This was not lost cargo. This was *sent* cargo. Someone had put this chest in the water on purpose, or someone had put it in the water hoping it would reach hands that understood, and either way the chest had traveled however many miles of current to stop, impossibly, at a bend of the Vess, in front of him.

He was a man who believed in reading terrain. Snowpack, cloud, rockfall scar, the lean of trees — the world wrote its intentions everywhere if you knew the script. And the script here said: *this was meant to be found.* What it refused to say was *by whom*, and that omission sat in his stomach like swallowed river water.

There was one thing left. The flat package in waxed linen, the one that clinked.

He unwrapped it with fingers that had gone careful and slow, the way they went on bad ice.

A compass.

Brass, heavy for its size, cased in a ring of the same metal with a hinged lid. It was beautiful work, older than anything in the chest, its casing worn smooth in the way of objects that have been handled for generations. Engraved around the bezel, in letters so small he had to hold it at an angle to catch them, ran a phrase in Old Callowmerean — a language he didn't speak, though he'd seen fragments of it carved on waystones in the high country, markers so old even the erasers hadn't bothered with them.

He flipped the lid open.

The needle swung, wobbled, steadied.

And pointed — not north.

Kestrel knew north the way he knew his own heartbeat. Twenty years of navigating by it, correcting for it, trusting it in whiteouts where it was the only honest thing left in the world. He knew where north was from this bend of the Vess within a degree or two, the way a musician knows middle C. The needle was pointing east-northeast, dead steady, unwavering, aimed like a drawn bow into the wall of the eastern ranges — into the mountains, into the high country, into the blank space on every map where Callowmere used to be.

He turned his body. The needle turned with him, holding its bearing, indifferent to his movement, indifferent to the earth's great magnetic argument. He turned again, slower, watching it the whole time. East-northeast. Always east-northeast. A compass that didn't point north wasn't broken. Broken needles wandered. This one had made up its mind.

It was pointing at something. Or someone.

Kestrel closed the lid. The click of it sounded very loud on the empty bank.

* * *

He carried the chest home through streets still slick with flood-mud, taking the back lanes without deciding to, an old habit wearing the disguise of a shortcut. In his room above the cooperage he set it under the bed, then took it out again because hiding it under the bed felt like hiding a body, then put it in the cedar trunk at the foot of his cot beneath his spare rope, which was somehow both hiding it and not hiding it, and finally sat on the edge of the cot and laughed once, short and humorless, at himself.

Look at you. Five years of being nobody, and one morning of river salvage and you're acting like a man with secrets.

But he was. That was the truth of it. He had secrets again, and they had weight, and weight was the one thing he had sworn off. He thought of the photograph — the tired mother, the solemn child, the birthmark like a bird or a flame. He thought of *she is still hers*, and wondered who *hers* was, and suspected, with a sinking certainty he couldn't justify, that the answer was a kingdom.

He spent the afternoon doing ordinary things with excessive care. He mended a strap. He priced the salvaged rope. He ate bread and hard cheese standing at the window, watching the street, and told himself he was watching for Wren, who came by most days, and not watching for anyone else.

Because here was the other thing the morning had taught him, the thing underneath the journals and the seal and the lying needle: chests like this did not travel alone. Things that mattered enough to seal and sink and send were things that other people wanted, and people who wanted things like this did not post bills asking politely. They came quietly, and they asked questions, and the last time men had come to Vess asking questions with that particular patience, Kestrel had been a different person with a different name, and he had learned how quickly a life could be taken apart by paperwork and testimony and the flat administrative language of men who destroyed things in triplicate.

Toward evening he opened the least damaged journal again — Maren Vashti-Cole's, the survey officer — and read further. The entries grew stranger as the water damage thinned. Talk of a commission to map passes that had already been mapped. Talk of supply caravans that never arrived. An entry, dated late in the book, written in a hand gone ragged:

*They are not surveying the ranges. They are learning them. There is a difference, and the difference is the end of us.*

Below that, the pages were fused solid.

Kestrel closed the book and sat in the failing light with his elbows on his knees, and the dread he had been carrying all day finally took off its disguise and showed him its face. It wasn't fear of what was in the chest. It was the knowledge, arriving with the quiet inevitability of weather, that the chest had not finished arriving. Rivers deliver things. They don't decide what happens next. Something in those pages pointed forward the way the needle pointed east-northeast, toward consequences he could feel massing beyond the horizon of the next few days.

A knock at the door.

His whole body went still — the stillness of prey, the stillness he hated in himself. Then the knock came again, twice, quick and impatient, and a voice he knew said, "Emrys. Open up. Half the town says you've been down at the banks hauling treasure out of my river, and I want to know why I'm hearing it from other people."

Wren.

Kestrel exhaled, looked once at the cedar trunk, and went to unbolt the door.
