## 3. The Debt Collector

The knock came at dusk, three measured raps, polite as a doctor's call.

Kestrel knew before he opened the door that it would be trouble. Nobody knocked like that in Vess unless they were paid by the hour. The fishermen banged. The drunks pounded. The women who sold fish stew on the corner simply shouted through the boards and let the smell do the persuading. But this — three soft knocks, evenly spaced, patient as a man who already knew how the evening would end — that was the knock of someone whose business was other people's ruin.

He set down the chest's lid, which he had been sanding smooth where the river water had warped it, and crossed the small room. Through the gap between shutter slats he saw a figure on his step: broad, gray-coated, hat brim low against the lamplight from the street. Behind him, at the mouth of the alley, two younger men leaned against a wall with the studied idleness of men being paid to look idle.

Kestrel considered the back window. It was a small room; the window was four steps away. He had gone out of windows before, in worse towns than Vess, for better reasons than this one.

But the chest sat behind him on the table, and something about leaving it alone with strangers on his doorstep made his stomach tighten in a way he couldn't name. So instead of the window, he opened the door.

"Emrys Vale," the man said, reading from a leather folio without looking up, as though the name were an ingredient list. "Guide. Licensed, provisional, border district. Debts outstanding to the house of Corvane and Partners: one hundred forty-two crowns, eleven shillings, plus interest accrued across nineteen months." He looked up then, and Kestrel saw a face built for patience — wide, pale, unremarkable, the kind of face you forgot while it was memorizing you. "My name is Halloway. I'm told you're a reasonable man."

"I'm told that too," Kestrel said. "By people who owe me money."

Halloway smiled without warmth, the way ice smiles at a lake. "May I come in? The street is public, and what I have to say is not."

Kestrel stepped aside. It was, he understood even as he did it, a mistake. You didn't invite the wolf in to discuss the sheep. But there was a part of him — the part that had spent five years eating other people's dust and calling it humility — that wanted to see the animal's teeth up close.

Halloway entered the way water enters a hull: quietly, everywhere at once. His eyes went over the room in a single pass — the bed, the stove, the guide's ropes coiled on their pegs, the sanded lid of the chest — and Kestrel watched them snag on the chest the way a fishhook snags on sunken timber. A fractional pause. Then onward, as if nothing had caught.

"Drink?" Kestrel asked, because his mother had raised him with manners that had outlived everything else she'd given him.

"No, thank you." Halloway took the room's only chair without being offered it, set the folio on his knee, and folded his hands atop it. "I'll be brief, since I know your time is valuable. One hundred forty-two crowns is not a fortune, Mr. Vale, but it is enough. Enough that Corvane's can take your license, your gear, and the roof over your head, and still leave a balance for the magistrates to squeeze out of your wages for the rest of your working life. You've seen it happen to others. Men walking the river road with their kit on their backs, going nowhere, because nowhere will have them."

"I've seen it," Kestrel said. He kept his voice flat. In the mountains you learned to read weather, and this was weather: pressure dropping, air gone still, the particular hush before something fell.

"Here's what I've come to propose." Halloway opened the folio. Inside lay a single sheet, written in a clerk's careful hand, and beneath it a line for a signature. "Corvane's is prepared to forgive the debt entire. Every crown. Every shilling. Interest included. Tonight, with a stamp and a signature, you walk out of this room owing nothing to anyone."

Kestrel waited. In his experience, no one forgave a debt. They transferred it, dressed it in kinder clothes, or sharpened it into something with a handle.

"In exchange," Halloway said, "you surrender the chest you pulled from the river nine days ago. Contents intact. Unopened, ideally, but we're realistic men. Intact is preferred. Opened is acceptable. Damaged is where negotiations become unpleasant."

* * *

For a moment the room was very quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere down the lane a dog was worrying at something, its small sounds rising and falling like a tide.

Nine days ago. Kestrel had told no one about the chest. He'd dragged it up the bank in the dark after the flood dropped it, hidden it under netting in his boat until midnight, carried it here wrapped in oilcloth like a body. He'd told the harbormaster he'd salvaged nothing but rope. He'd told Wren — the sharp-eyed water girl who noticed everything — that the river had cost him a week's work and gained him nothing.

And yet this stranger sat in his chair and named the day, the object, and the condition of its contents as easily as if he'd stood on the bank beside him.

"You have me confused with another salvage man," Kestrel said.

"I don't think I do." Halloway's tone didn't change at all. That was the frightening thing about it — the absolute absence of change, a voice planed smooth of every edge. "The river gives things up after a flood. Everyone knows that. Most of what it gives up is worth nothing. Sometimes it gives up something else." He turned the folio page, though there was nothing on the next page; Kestrel understood it as theater, a ritual gesture, a man showing off the machinery of his office. "A chest came down the Callowmere reach nine days ago. Stamped seal. Brass fittings. Heavy. The coalition's salvage office logged every reported find within a day of the flood — except yours, Mr. Vale, because you never reported yours. Which is itself a report, if you follow me."

Kestrel followed him perfectly. He also understood that following was now a luxury. "If such a chest existed," he said slowly, "and if a man had pulled it from the water — why would your office want it so badly? River salvage goes to auction. It's procedure. I know procedure; I've lived by it for five years."

"Procedure applies to driftwood and drowned livestock." Halloway closed the folio. "This chest bears the crest of a nation that does not exist. Under the Erasure Statutes, anything bearing that crest is contraband by definition. Not stolen property — contraband. Like plague linen. Like forged coin. Possession isn't a fine, Mr. Vale. Possession is a question, and questions of that kind are answered in rooms with no windows."

"And yet you're offering to forgive a debt for it. Generous, for contraband."

"We're generous with things we want." For the first time, something moved behind Halloway's face — not warmth, but the ghost of warmth, a clerk's pride in a well-drafted offer. "Consider my position. I could bring the salvage office here tomorrow with a warrant and six men, and they would find the chest, and you would go to a windowless room, and everyone would be unhappy and the paperwork would be extensive. Or you sign this paper tonight, the debt dies, the chest walks out the door under my arm, and tomorrow morning you are a free man with a clean slate and no history worth anyone's attention. I'm offering you the thing you've been buying piecemeal for five years, Mr. Vale. Anonymity. Wholesale."

There it was. Kestrel felt the offer settle over him like snowfall — soft, weightless, burying. A clean slate. No history. No name with edges on it. He had dreamed of exactly this in the bad years, in the rented bunks and the borrowed beds, the dream of being no one so thoroughly that even the past walked past him in the street.

And the price was a box of drowned paper.

It should have been the easiest trade of his life. He turned it over in his mind and found, to his genuine surprise, that he could not make himself do it.

"I'll need time," he heard himself say.

Halloway regarded him for a long moment, and Kestrel had the distinct sensation of being weighed by a man who had already finished the calculation. "Time is the one commodity I don't carry," Halloway said. "The offer stands until I stand up from this chair. After that, the salvage office handles it their way, and their way doesn't include forgiveness."

* * *

Later — much later, when he could think about it at all — Kestrel would try to trace the shape of the decision. He would lie awake in the dark with the chest's brass fittings cold under his hand and try to find the rational thread, the self-interested logic, the angle. There wasn't one. That was the truth of it, and it shamed him in a quiet way: he had thrown away a clean slate for a box of a dead country's secrets, and he could not say why.

But sitting there, in that ticking silence, some things were clear.

He thought about the journals inside the chest, the ones he'd told himself he hadn't read closely. Water-swollen pages, ink bloomed into gray ghosts, but legible where the wax had held. Expedition logs. Survey notes. And between the lines, in margins cramped with a different hand, arithmetic that had nothing to do with altitude — grain shipments that never arrived, granaries sealed by order, a famine described in the flat language of supply ledgers while the same ledger recorded troops moving in the opposite direction. He had read those pages twice by candlelight and told himself they were the obsessions of some dead clerk, that drowned paper proved nothing, that a man building a new life didn't dig up old graves.

He thought about the photograph. A child, perhaps four years old, standing on a terrace with mountains behind her, squinting into sunlight. On the back, in faded ink: *S., aged four. She will remember none of this, thank God.* He had looked at that small fierce face longer than he'd looked at anything in years, and he had told himself it meant nothing to him, that pity was a luxury, that his own dead were enough to carry.

He thought about the compass. He hadn't touched it since the first night. It sat in the bottom of the chest under the journals, brass-cased, heavy for its size, and when he'd opened it that once the needle had done something no needle should do — swung past north, steadied, and pointed off into the western hills with the confidence of a drawn line. He'd closed the lid on it as if shutting a door on a voice mid-sentence.

None of that was evidence. None of it was reason. Reasons could be spoken aloud to a creditor in a chair, and he had none.

What he had was simpler and stupider. He had spent five years being Emrys Vale, a man with no past, and he had learned the great secret of borrowed names: they fit like boots a size too small. You could walk in them. You could even climb in them, for a while. But every step reminded you, and at night your feet swelled with everything you were pretending not to be. And here, in this box, was the first thing in five years that had nothing to do with him — a dead kingdom's grief, a stranger child's face, a needle pointing somewhere true — and holding it, he had felt, for one absurd hour at a table covered in swollen paper, like a man wearing his own boots again.

Halloway was offering to take that away. To buy back the small boots and burn them.

"No," Kestrel said.

The word came out level, and it surprised them both. Halloway's eyebrows rose a fraction — the first genuine expression Kestrel had seen on him, and somehow the most chilling.

"No?" Halloway repeated.

"The chest isn't mine to sell. It came down the river to me, and—" Kestrel stopped, because the sentence was assembling itself out of materials he hadn't authorized. *It came to me.* As if the flood had been addressed. As if a chest could have a recipient. He heard himself finish anyway: "—and I haven't decided what it is yet. When I decide, I'll act accordingly."

"That," Halloway said softly, "is not a position. That is a delay wearing a position's coat."

"Then it's in good company. So is your offer."

Something shifted in the room then — not the light, not the air, but the temperature between the two men. Halloway rose, unhurried, and squared the folio on his knee. "Mr. Vale. Emrys. Whatever you are." He said it without malice, which was worse. "I collect debts. That's all I am, whatever you imagine — a man who moves obligations from one column to another. I've done it for thirty years and I'm good at it, and I'd genuinely rather do it kindly. But understand what you've just chosen. You've chosen the windowless room over the clean slate. Over a debt you cannot pay and a name that isn't yours. Men have made that trade for less than a box, and I have watched every one of them regret it."

"I'll add it to my regrets," Kestrel said. "They keep well."

Halloway paused at the door, folio under his arm, and looked back — not at Kestrel's face but at the chest, a long, cataloguing look, the look of a man measuring a crate for transport. "Nine days ago the river gave you something," he said. "Rivers give. Rivers also take back. Remember that I offered."

Then he was gone, and the two young men unfolded themselves from the alley wall and went with him, and the street was empty, and the dog down the lane had finally found whatever it was looking for.

* * *

Kestrel barred the door, which was a gesture — the bar was pine, the door older than the town — and stood with his forehead against the wood, listening to his own heart do its slow mountain work.

One hundred forty-two crowns, forgiven. Gone, because he'd said *no* to a patient man in a gray coat, for reasons he couldn't have written on that signature line if his life depended on it. Which it might.

He crossed to the table and lifted the chest's lid. The journals lay where he'd left them, swollen and gray-ghosted. The photograph of the child stared up at him from its envelope, squinting into a sun twenty years dead. And beneath them, the brass compass sat in its nest of rotted velvet, patient as a seed.

He should carry it to the river tonight and drop it in. Let the water take back what it gave. That was the sane move, the Emrys Vale move — drown the evidence, pay the debt in installments, stay small and alive and nobody.

His hand hovered over the compass and did not descend.

Instead he found himself thinking about the margin-arithmetic again, the granaries sealed by order, the ledger's flat cruelty. About the words he'd half-read and told himself he'd imagined: *the heir survived the crossing*, in a hand different from the log's, pressed hard enough to score the page beneath. About a needle that pointed west, into hills where maps went vague and place-names thinned out like breath in cold air.

Outside, footsteps passed his window and did not stop. But they were followed, a minute later, by footsteps that did — slow, unhurried, stopping just beyond the shutter's edge, where the lamplight ended. Someone was standing in the dark outside his door, doing nothing, saying nothing, being present the way Halloway had been present: patiently, professionally, all night if the wages required.

Kestrel blew out the candle and sat in the dark with his hand resting on the chest's lid, feeling through the wood the faint cold of the brass beneath.

In the morning, he decided, he would open the compass again. Properly this time. He would find out where that needle was pointing, and who *S., aged four* had grown into, and why a drowned kingdom's papers had floated nine days down a river to fetch up against the boat of a man with a false name and a debt he'd just refused to escape.

The footsteps outside shifted weight, and waited.
