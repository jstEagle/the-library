## 5. The Girl Who Sells Rain

Wren Halloway had three rules, and she'd learned them all the expensive way.

One: never sell water to a man who won't look you in the eye. Two: never carry anything you can't eat, burn, or deny. Three: never, ever fall in love with a payday.

The man who came up Mill Lane that morning, buying water in the wrong quarter of town, broke rule one inside a minute.

She watched him from the mouth of the tannery alley, where her cart sat in the shade and her two barrels sweated through their canvas covers. He was tall, gray at the temples, with a guide's shoulders and a walk that didn't belong in Vess — that long, rolling stride of a man used to picking his footing on scree, not cobblestones. He wore his poverty like a coat that had once been expensive. And he was buying water by the skinful, one skin, then another, then a third, which nobody did, because water in Vess was water, and you carried what you needed and no more.

"Emrys, isn't it?" she said, when he came near enough. "The mountain man. You took the Corven party up the river trail last week."

Something flickered behind his eyes — a shutter closing. "That's me. Emrys."

"Three skins today. Thirsty work, remembering?"

He paid without smiling. His coins were counted out in advance, exact to the copper, which told her he was either honest or terrified, and in her experience those were the same thing wearing different hats.

She watched him go, and she watched the way he walked — straight past the taverns, past the moneylenders, down toward the old customs house where the flood had left its brown tide-mark on the stones. Toward the river. Toward the wreckage.

Wren sold water because water was legal, and she smuggled because legal didn't pay for her mother's lungs. And rule two meant she knew every piece of salvage that came out of that river within a day of it coming out, because salvage was the one trade in Vess that had no rules at all.

The mountain man — Emrys, if that was even his name, and she'd have staked a week's earnings it wasn't — had pulled something out of the flood.

She knew it the way she knew weather in her knees.

* * *

By evening she'd confirmed it. Old Tam the netmender had seen him dragging a chest up from the shallows below the customs house, two nights after the river jumped its banks. "Heavy as sin," Tam said, splicing net without looking up. "And stamped. Fancy stamp, like on a church door."

"What kind of fancy?"

"Circle and a tower. Or a crown. Hard to say, all that mud."

Wren's hands went still on the cart rail. Circle and tower. She'd seen that mark exactly once, scratched into the lintel of a house her mother had refused to walk past, back when they still lived on the Callowmere side of the border, before the maps changed and the houses came down. Her mother had spat and said the word *Callowmere* like it was a bone caught in her throat.

That was six days ago, the flood. Six days, and the mountain man had been asking questions ever since. Questions at the chandler's about old river charts. Questions at the bookbinder about who bought journals and ledgers in Vess. Questions, this afternoon, at the ferry office about the passes — not the trade passes, everybody asked about those, but the high ones, the dead ones, the ones with no tolls because no one had walked them in twenty years.

Wren Halloway had grown up on the wrong side of a border that no longer officially existed, in a town that officially belonged to nobody, selling a product that officially fell from the sky. She knew what dangerous questions looked like. They looked like a man buying too much water and asking about dead passes.

So she made a decision, the way she made all her decisions: quickly, and with an eye on the profit margin.

He was either a mark or a meal ticket. Her job, over the next day or so, was to find out which.

* * *

She found him at dawn on the river flats, and she found him doing something strange.

He had a compass in his hand — a brass thing, old, the kind with a hinged lid — and he was standing in the mud with his back to the sunrise, turning in a slow circle, watching the needle. Then stopping. Turning again. Watching it again.

Anyone else would have looked like a fool. He looked like a man arguing with something.

"You're holding it wrong," Wren said, coming up the flat with her cart squeaking behind her. "Or it's broken. Either way, you look like a man losing an argument with a spoon."

He didn't startle, which she noted. Guides didn't startle. "It's not broken."

"No? Which way's north, then?"

He hesitated. That was the interesting part. A man with a working compass doesn't hesitate. "Northeast," he said finally, and it was a lie, and they both knew it was a lie, and the morning went very quiet between them.

"Northeast," Wren repeated. "Funny. Because I sell water to surveyors, and fishermen, and once to a coalition map-man who talked in his sleep, and northeast is that way." She pointed. "You're facing southeast. Toward the Serpent's Teeth. Toward passes that aren't on any chart I've ever sold a drink beside."

The mountain man looked at her for a long moment. Up close, in the flat early light, he had the kind of face that had been handsome before something had sanded it down. "What do you want?"

"Direct. I like that. Saves time." She leaned on the cart. "Here's what I want. I want to know what's in the chest you pulled out of the river, because I already know about the chest, so don't waste both our mornings pretending. And I want to know why a man named Emrys — which is a Callowmere name, by the way, and nobody this side of the border names their boys Emrys anymore, not since the maps changed — is asking about high passes that the coalition closed twenty years ago."

"You've been following me."

"I've been *noticing* you. Following costs more." She let that sit. "Here's what you want. You want someone who knows Vess. Who knows which guards take bribes and which take fingers. Who knows the smugglers' paths over the Teeth, the ones that don't appear on charts because charts get confiscated. You want a guide for the town, the way you were a guide for the mountain. And you want her to keep her mouth shut, which I will, because my mouth has a price and you look like a man who's about to be able to pay it."

"And what makes you think I'm going anywhere?"

Wren smiled. It was her best smile, the one that had talked a customs sergeant out of confiscating an entire barrel of Vess brandy. "Mountain man. You bought three skins of water yesterday and two more this morning. Nobody buys five skins of water to sit in Vess. You're provisioning. You're provisioning like a man who means to walk into the Teeth with no chart, no partner, and a compass that doesn't point north." She tapped the cart rail. "That's not a trip. That's a funeral with extra steps."

He almost smiled. Almost. It was like watching a door open a crack and then bar itself again. "And if I say no?"

"Then I go on selling water, and in about four days a coalition man in a good coat comes asking the same questions I just asked, except he won't be polite about it, and he'll find me anyway, because he'll find everybody. And I'll have to decide what to tell him about the quiet mountain man with the broken compass." She shrugged. "I'd rather not have to decide. Deciding under pressure is how mistakes happen."

That landed. She watched it land, watched something move behind his eyes — not fear exactly, but the recognition of it, the way a man flinches at a sound he's heard before.

"You're threatening me," he said.

"I'm *pricing* you. Threats are free. This costs you either coin or company, your choice." She held out her hand. "Wren. Wren Halloway. Water by the skin, secrets by the pound, and passage over the Teeth for a negotiated fee."

He didn't take the hand. He looked at it, then at her, and she saw him doing the arithmetic — the same arithmetic she'd done on him, mark or meal ticket, trust or cut losses — and she understood, suddenly and unpleasantly, that he was better at it than she was. Because he'd had more practice at being hunted, whatever he called himself. You could see the practice in him like grain in wood.

"Emrys," he said, and took her hand after all, and his grip was careful, the grip of a man who'd learned exactly how much strength was safe to show.

"Sure it is," said Wren.

* * *

She spent the day proving her worth, because a meal ticket has to be fed before it pays out.

She took him through the back half of Vess, the Vess that didn't appear in the harbormaster's ledgers: the chandler who sold lamp oil in unlabeled tins, the ferryman who ran cargo at night and asked no questions because questions slowed the loading, the widow Brann who kept a room above her dye-works that could hold four people and a chest, if the chest wasn't proud about its size. She showed him which streets the coalition patrols walked and which they only pretended to walk. She taught him the bell-code — two rings, market fire; three rings, patrol; one long ring, run.

He absorbed it all without comment, the way stone absorbs rain. But twice she caught him watching her with something that wasn't suspicion. The second time, she realized what it was: he was surprised. He hadn't expected competence. He'd expected a girl with a cart and a scheme, and he'd gotten a girl with a cart, a scheme, and a map of the town drawn in her head finer than anything Odell the cartographer sold for a crown a sheet.

At the dye-works, over the widow Brann's bitter tea, he finally talked.

Not much. A chest, salvaged from the flood. Papers in it. A name — he didn't say whose. A direction. He held up the brass compass and let the needle speak for itself, and Wren felt the hair rise on her arms, because she'd grown up on her mother's stories and her mother's stories had all ended the same way: *and they erased it, girl, they didn't just kill it, they erased it, and the first thing they erased was the roads.*

"Southeast," Wren said quietly. "Into the Teeth. Toward—" She stopped herself. Saying it aloud felt like whistling in a graveyard. "Toward the old kingdom."

"It's not a kingdom anymore. It's a hole in the map."

"Holes in maps don't stay empty forever." She turned her cup. "My mother came over the border before it closed. She used to say the coalition didn't conquer Callowmere, it *audited* it. And when the audit came up short, they burned the books." She looked at him. "Is that what's in your chest? Books that didn't burn?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. His silence had a shape, and the shape was yes.

And here was the thing — here was the moment Wren Halloway would think about later, on cold passes and worse nights, the moment she'd examine like a coin she couldn't decide was luck or a curse. She had him. She had the whole shape of it, and the whole shape of it was worth more than her cart, her barrels, her mother's medicine, and five years of skins and secrets put together. A man with proof that the coalition murdered a nation. A man the coalition would pay anything to bury. All she had to do was sell him.

One visit to the garrison. One conversation with the right sergeant. She'd be out of the water trade by winter. Her mother would have a real physician, real medicine, a room with a stove that didn't smoke.

She sat with that thought the way you sit with a hot coal, feeling its weight, feeling its warmth, feeling it eat through.

Then she thought of her mother's stories. *They erased it, girl.* And her mother, who had spat at the name Callowmere, had wept saying it — Wren remembered that now, remembered being small and confused by a woman who hated a country and grieved it in the same breath.

"You're not going to sell me," the mountain man said. It wasn't a question.

"No," Wren said, and was surprised to find she meant it. "But don't mistake that for virtue. It's just that I've done my arithmetic, and dead guides' secrets pay worse than live ones." She stood, brushing off her skirt. "Here's my price, and it's not coin. You're going into the Teeth with no chart and a compass that lies. You need someone who knows the smugglers' paths, and there are exactly two of us in Vess, and the other one is in prison. So: I come. I get a share of whatever this is worth — and it *is* worth something, don't insult us both by pretending otherwise — and when it's over, I get enough to buy my mother a winter without coughing."

"That's a lot of risk for a share of something you don't understand."

"I understand it fine. It's a hole in the map, and holes in maps are the only places left in this world where a girl selling rain can be worth more than a girl selling rain." She held out her hand again. This time he took it.

His palm was callused in the pattern of rope and rock, and his handshake was brief, and his eyes went past her shoulder to the window, to the street, and she saw him go very still.

"What?" she said.

"There's a man across the lane," he said softly. "Gray coat. He's been there since we sat down."

Wren didn't turn. Turning was for amateurs. "Gray coat, good boots, walks like he's counting his steps?"

"Yes."

"Then it's not a garrison man. Garrison men walk like they're owed money." She kept her voice light and her eyes on her tea. "Back door. Widow Brann keeps it oiled for exactly this. You're going to tell me who that is, mountain man, and you're going to tell me on the stairs, because if I'm selling my life to this venture, I want to know what's chasing it."

The mountain man rose, unhurried, a man folding away a table. But his face, when it turned to the light, had changed. The sanding-down she'd noticed that morning was gone. Underneath it was something older and harder and much more afraid.

"He's not chasing the chest," Kestrel — and she would learn the name later, much later — said quietly. "He's chasing me. And Wren? He knows my face better than I do."
