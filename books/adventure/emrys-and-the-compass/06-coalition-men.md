## 6. Coalition Men

The rain came to Vess the way debt came to Kestrel Amberdale — slowly at first, then all at once, and never alone.

He was in the taproom of the Drowned Bell, nursing a cup of barley tea he'd let go cold because cold tea lasted longer than hot, when the two men walked in out of the downpour. He noticed them the way he noticed everything now: sideways, without turning his head, the way a man reads a slope for the crack that will kill him.

They were wrong for Vess in a dozen small ways. Their boots were too clean — oiled leather, not the cracked river-mud kind every dockhand and fishwife wore this time of year. Their coats were cut for riding, not walking, and they carried themselves with the particular economy of men who had never once worried about being robbed. One went straight to the bar and ordered in a voice pitched to carry: two ales, and did the keeper know anything about salvage? River salvage?

Kestrel kept his eyes on his cup.

*Salvage.* The word landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He listened to it fall and fall and heard no bottom.

The barkeep, a broad woman named Marda who owed him nothing and liked him anyway, shrugged elaborately. "River's been throwing things up all week since the flood. Old fence posts. A dead cow. Some fool's trunk of ladies' hats, ruined. Why?"

"Anything else," the second man said. It wasn't quite a question. "Anything official-looking. Sealed containers. Documents."

Marda laughed. "You think the river delivers mail?"

The first man smiled too, but his eyes didn't join in. He set a coin on the bar — a full silver, more than the ale cost by a factor of ten — and said, "We're paying for information. Anything washed ashore from upstream. Anything with markings, seals, stamps. Anyone who's found such a thing and might be... holding onto it."

And there it was. The word *seals* hung in the smoky air of the Drowned Bell like a lantern nobody had lit yet.

Kestrel drank his cold tea and thought about the chest under the loose floorboard of his rented room, wrapped in oilcloth, three streets away. He thought about the brass compass with its needle that refused to point north. He thought about the photograph of the child with the dark curling hair and the birthmark like a falling star at her collarbone.

Then the first man turned toward the room, scanning it with the lazy thoroughness of a hawk over a field, and Kestrel stopped thinking entirely, because recognition hit him like a fall.

He knew that face.

Twenty years had worked on it — carved lines around the mouth, silvered the temples, put weight across the shoulders — but faces don't lie to a man who has spent his life reading them. Kestrel had last seen that face across a tribunal table in Corvane, lit by lamplight, while its owner read aloud from a signed statement that ended four careers and four lives' worth of reputations and sent Kestrel Amberdale into the wilderness with nothing but a borrowed name.

*Davor Reiss.*

The name rose in him whole, unbidden, tasting of pine smoke and high camps and a friendship that had once been the truest thing he owned.

Reiss's gaze swept past him, held for half a heartbeat — Kestrel felt it snag, felt the old instinct in the other man twitch like a dog scenting something familiar — and moved on. Half a heartbeat. Nothing more. Twenty years and a beard and a slouch and a different way of carrying his weight had bought Kestrel that much.

But his hands, flat on the table, had gone white-knuckled without his permission.

* * *

He left by the kitchen door, through the steam and the smell of boiled mutton, and walked the long way home through rain that had settled in for the night. His mind was doing what it always did under pressure: breaking the problem into terrain. Slope angle. Load bearing. Escape routes.

Davor Reiss was in Vess. Davor Reiss, who had been a junior liaison officer attached to the Amberdale expedition the year everything went wrong on the Greyspire massif. Who had sat beside Kestrel at a hundred campfires, argued maps with him until dawn, carried his spare rope when his shoulder was torn open above the tree line. Who had wept, actually wept, at the memorial service for the four dead climbers — and then, six weeks later, stood before the coalition tribunal and testified, calm as a man reading a shipping manifest, that Kestrel Amberdale had ignored weather warnings, overridden his own guides, and led four people onto a cornice he'd been told twice was unstable.

None of which was true. And Kestrel had never understood why Reiss said it. Not for money — Reiss wasn't poor afterward, but not rich either. Not for advancement — his career had climbed steadily but unspectacularly ever since. For years Kestrel had turned it over on bad nights like a stone with no answer underneath. *Why you, Davor? Of everyone, why you?*

Now Reiss was standing in a taproom in Vess asking about sealed chests pulled from a river, and the stone finally had an edge to it.

Because it wasn't coincidence. Kestrel had lived twenty years on the principle that coincidence was just a pattern you hadn't walked far enough along to see. The flood had brought the chest. The chest had brought questions. The questions had reached whoever needed them reached — fast, faster than a border town should have allowed — and the person they'd sent was a man whose specialty, apparently, included knowing exactly how Kestrel Amberdale thought.

Unless Reiss didn't know. Unless the assignment was blind, and the agent asking about river salvage had no idea his old friend was three streets away with the evidence under his floorboards.

Kestrel tested both possibilities as he walked, the way he'd test ice, putting weight on each in turn. If Reiss knew — if this was a net drawn tight around him — then the smart move was to burn the journals tonight, sink the compass in the deepest pool below the mill weir, and be Emrys the guide again by morning, poorer in truth but alive. If Reiss didn't know, then the danger was ordinary and survivable, and the chest could stay where it was.

But there was a third possibility, and it was the one that made his stomach tighten: that Reiss knew, and had known for days, and was not moving to take the chest because taking it would raise questions higher up. That Reiss wanted to see who else came looking. That the friend who had betrayed him once was now, for reasons of his own, watching the door instead of opening it.

Kestrel had spent twenty years learning not to believe in good versions of powerful men. But he had also spent those same twenty years unable to kill the memory of the man who used to be, and that memory kept whispering the third possibility to him in the dark, and he hated it for whispering.

* * *

His room above the cooperage was cold and smelled of oak shavings and river damp. He lit no lamp. He knelt, pried up the floorboard, and drew out the oilcloth bundle, and sat with it in his lap in the dark like a man holding something that might be a child or might be a bomb.

The journals inside were water-swollen but legible, written in the neat hand of a Callowmere survey officer named Aldous Vane. Kestrel had read them twice through in the five nights since the flood, and each reading had rearranged the world a little further from the shape he'd trusted. Grain shipments rerouted. Border tariffs engineered to strangle. A famine documented in advance, predicted in columns of figures, and then permitted — *managed*, Vane's word, with the terrible coolness of an accountant — while the coalition that governed the borderlands looked away with its hands cupped behind its back. And then the erasure: the decrees striking Callowmere from the maps, from the records, from the language of treaties, so thoroughly that children born ten years later would never learn the country had existed at all.

The compass lay on top of the journals. In the dark he couldn't see it, but he could feel it, that faint wrongness, like a tuning fork still humming after the music stopped. He'd checked it against the sun that morning, standing in the yard behind the cooperage with his back to the light. North was behind him. The needle pointed off his right shoulder, steady as a plumb line, toward the deep mountains southeast of the Greyspires — toward a fixed point that appeared on no map Kestrel had ever guided anyone across, and he had guided people across most of them.

Toward something. Or someone. The journals' final entries spoke of a survivor, a child smuggled out in the last weeks, an heir hidden somewhere the erasers had never thought to look because they'd erased the very ground she stood on.

A child in a photograph. A birthmark like a falling star.

Kestrel wrapped it all back up and slid it under the floorboard, and then he sat on the edge of his cot in the dark and let himself, for exactly sixty seconds by the church bell, feel what he felt.

Fear, yes. But underneath the fear, older and less manageable: grief. Because Davor Reiss in Vess meant the past hadn't stayed buried, and if the past was surfacing, it would surface all the way — the tribunal, the testimony, the four names he still said to himself every year on the anniversary, the way a man touches a scar to check it's still there. Marta Ellery. Tomas and Ivo Brandt — no relation to any priest, though he'd flinched at the name when he'd met the defrocked Father Ivo in Wren's company and hadn't explained why. And Penn Halloway's brother, of all the bitter jokes the mountains had ever played — no. That was another ledger. One wound at a time.

Sixty seconds. Then he stood, and rolled his bedding, and began, methodically, to pack.

Not to run. Not yet. Running told Reiss everything. But a man in Vess named Emrys had a client booked up the Vess river trail in nine days, and a reputation for reliability, and a life assembled plank by careful plank over twenty years. If the floodwaters were rising — and they were; he could hear them, the same way he could hear a slope beginning to move — then he needed to know which way the slide would come, and he couldn't do that hiding in a cooperage loft.

He needed to see Reiss again. Close. On his own terms.

* * *

He found him the next evening, or rather arranged to be found, which amounted to the same thing if you did it properly.

Marda mentioned — loudly, to the room at large, in the way Kestrel had paid her three coppers to mention — that Emrys the mountain guide knew the river trail better than any man alive, flood damage and all, and might be worth talking to about what the water had been chewing up and spitting out lately. She said it while the two strangers sat at their corner table for the third night running. She said it without looking at Kestrel, which was good craft, and Kestrel sat at his usual spot pretending to study a tattered trail gazetteer, which was better.

It took less than an hour. The second man — the quiet one, the one who watched doors — crossed the room and stood over Kestrel's table with the unhurried confidence of someone who had never been thrown out of anywhere.

"You're the guide."

"Emrys." Kestrel didn't rise. Rising was eager. "Depends who's asking, and about what."

"Dann." The man offered no hand. "My colleague and I are traveling on coalition business. We're told you know the river."

"I know the river the way it knows me, which is not warmly." Kestrel turned a page he wasn't reading. "What's your business with it?"

"Salvage. Something may have come downstream in the flood. Something that belongs to interested parties." Dann's eyes were pale and patient, like a fisherman's float. "If you found anything unusual — anything marked, stamped, sealed — there would be compensation. Considerable compensation."

"And if I hadn't?"

"Then we'd ask you to keep your ears open. Guides hear things." Dann produced a card, heavy stock, embossed with the interlocked-ring seal of the Borderlands Coalition — the seal Kestrel had seen on the tribunal summons twenty years ago, on the blacklist notice nailed to every guildhouse door from Corvane to the coast, on the orders that had erased a nation. "Where can we find you, if something turns up?"

"Nowhere, usually." Kestrel took the card, because refusing it would have been its own message, and turned it over once. "I'm up the trail most days. Leave word with Marda."

He felt rather than saw the second man, the one called Dann's colleague, shift at the corner table. Felt the attention settle on his back like weather.

He did not look up. Looking up was the hardest thing he had done in twenty years, harder than any pitch he'd ever climbed, because he knew what he'd find: Davor Reiss, watching him, and behind those older eyes perhaps nothing at all, or perhaps the one thing Kestrel could not afford — recognition.

He finished his tea. He gathered his gazetteer. He rose, nodded to the room, and walked to the door with the loose-shouldered amble of Emrys the guide, a man with nothing heavier on his mind than tomorrow's weather.

At the threshold, he broke his own rule.

He glanced back — one flick of the eyes, quick as a climber checking his anchor — and met Davor Reiss looking directly at him.

Not at Emrys. At him. With an expression Kestrel had seen only once before, across a lamplit tribunal table, in the half-second before Reiss began to read the statement that destroyed him: the look of a man carrying something heavy and deciding, again, not to set it down.

Reiss raised his ale slightly. A toast, or a greeting, or a warning — from Davor Reiss, the three had always been difficult to tell apart.

Kestrel walked out into the rain.

* * *

He didn't go home. Home was the first place they'd watch. Instead he climbed the fire stair of the granary across from the cooperage — a route he'd mapped months ago out of habit, the way other men whittled — and settled into the shadow of the grain chute with a view of his own window and the street below, and he waited, and he thought.

The rain thinned toward midnight. Lamps died one by one along Cooper's Row. And Kestrel turned the problem over one final time, testing the ice.

Reiss had looked at him and done nothing. Had raised a cup and let him walk out. Either Reiss didn't know — and the look was only the look of a bored agent cataloguing faces — or Reiss knew and was choosing, for whatever reason lived in that closed expression, to leave the door shut between them a little longer.

There was a third reading, and Kestrel made himself consider it honestly, because the mountains had taught him that the route you least want is often the true one: that the look had been a message. *I see you. I have always seen you. Move carefully.*

Below, a figure detached itself from the doorway of the chandler's shop across the street and crossed to the cooperage door. Not Dann. Not Reiss. A local — Kestrel knew the walk, the hunched, apologetic gait of a man named Sorrel who ran errands for anyone with coin and no questions about what the errands were. Sorrel tried the cooperage door, found it locked, and stood a moment in the wet street, looking up at Kestrel's dark window with the frank appraisal of a man measuring a house he intended to enter later.

Then he turned and walked away, and Kestrel counted his own heartbeats until he heard, faint under the rain's last whisper, the sound of the granary's street door easing open far beneath him.

Someone was coming up the stairs. Someone who had watched Sorrel, or sent him, or simply chosen the same hour — and in Vess, at midnight, after a day of coalition men asking about sealed chests, Kestrel Amberdale was done believing in coincidence.

He slipped out the loading window onto the granary roof, silent as snowfall, and dropped to the adjoining shed, and ran — low, fast, along routes only he knew — while behind him, in the dark building he'd just left, footsteps climbed steadily toward the empty shadow where a man had been sitting.

By the time they reached it, Kestrel was three streets away, breath smoking in the cold, pressed into the alcove of the tannery gate with the oilcloth bundle already under his arm. He'd gone back for it first. Whatever was happening, whatever game Davor Reiss was playing and whichever side of it Kestrel was standing on, the chest was no longer staying under any floorboard in this town.

Tomorrow, he'd need help — the kind that came cheap and asked no questions, the kind that came with quick fingers and quicker wits. There was a girl who sold water near the market steps who watched everything and owed nothing to anyone, and Kestrel had a feeling he was going to need both her eyes and her silence before the week was out.

Behind him, faint through the rain-washed air, a whistle sounded from the direction of the cooperage — two notes, then silence, then two more. The sound of men signaling to other men that the bird had flown.

Kestrel pulled his collar up and melted into the sleeping town, carrying a dead kingdom under his arm.
