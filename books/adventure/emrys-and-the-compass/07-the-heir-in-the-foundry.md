## 7. The Heir in the Foundry

Wren had sold water to better liars than Kestrel Amberdale, but never to a worse one.

He couldn't lie at all, that was the thing about him. He'd stand in the market with a brass compass cupped in his palm like a dying bird and ask his questions in a voice pitched too low, too careful — *do you know anyone from the old kingdoms, anyone with family from over the mountains* — and every word of it was true, which made it ten times more dangerous than a lie. Lies you could dodge. Truth was a cartwheel rolling downhill toward your ankles.

She'd been shadowing him for three days now, since she'd watched him pay a records clerk in Vess's dingy archive office double what the man asked and then walk out without the receipt. Nobody paid double without wanting something kept quiet. And nobody who wanted something kept quiet walked around afterward looking like a man carrying a lit lantern through a hay barn.

So Wren followed him. It was just good business.

* * *

The foundry district sat on the east bank of the Vess river, where the town's money ran out and its noise began. Six great sheds under roofs patched with tar and tin, chimneys bleeding orange smoke into a sky the color of old dishwater. The air tasted of hot iron and coal dust. Wren knew every alley in it, because the foundry district was where you went when you needed something done off the books — a lock picked, a crate moved without a manifest, a name forged on a work permit. She'd made her first real coin here at eleven years old, hauling water to the casting floors while the men sweated through their shirts and tipped her in coppers they'd swear they'd never seen.

Kestrel walked through it like a tourist, which was suicide. He stopped at corners. He looked up at rooftops. He asked a gate guard directions to the third shift foreman in an accent that said *I have never been here and I would like very much to leave*, and the guard looked him over the way a butcher looks over a lamb.

Wren drifted past on the other side of the lane, yoke across her shoulders, buckets swaying, invisible as furniture. That was the trick of the water-yoke: nobody looked twice at a girl selling rain. She'd been nobody for years and she'd gotten very good at it.

Kestrel reached the shed marked with three iron bars over the door — Ferro & Sons, though there were no sons anymore, only daughters, and only one of them still worked the floor.

Wren had heard about the girl before she'd ever seen her. The foundry workers called her *the ghost of Callowmere* behind her back, not unkindly, the way you'd call a tall man Stretch. Sable Voss. Orphaned twice over, once by fire and once by paperwork. Kept to herself. Worked the pour line because she could stand heat that sent grown men to the shade tents. Had hands like a woman twice her age and eyes that made foremen uncomfortable, dark and level and old.

Kestrel went in through the worker's entrance. Wren counted to forty, set down her yoke against the wall where the night watchman owed her a favor and would keep an eye on it, and followed.

* * *

The floor was hell with a roof on it. Heat rolled off the furnaces in visible waves, and the light was all orange and black, sparks drifting like malicious snow. Men moved through it half-naked, faces smeared with soot, shouting over the grind of machinery. Wren slipped along the wall into the shadow of a stacked pallet and found Kestrel standing at the edge of the pour line, hat in hand, talking to a girl who was pointedly not stopping work.

Sable Voss was maybe nineteen, lean as a whip handle, hair cropped short and singed at the ends, sleeves rolled to show forearms ridged with small burn scars. She was raking slag off a cooling channel with the bored precision of someone who'd done it ten thousand times, and she did not look up at Kestrel even once.

"—I'm telling you, I don't know anything about any of that," she was saying. Her voice carried flat and clear over the din. "My grandmother came from over the mountains. So did four hundred other people in this town. Go bother them."

"Your grandmother's name was Maren," Kestrel said. "Maren Voss. Before that, Maren Aldous, of the Callowmere civil registry."

The rake paused. Just for a heartbeat. Then it kept moving.

"I wouldn't know."

"You were born in this town, but you weren't registered until you were six. There's no record of you before then. No birth certificate, no baptism, nothing." Kestrel's voice stayed gentle, which Wren recognized as the voice he used on frightened clients on bad ledges. "Someone kept you off the books on purpose. Someone who knew what a name could cost."

"Maybe my grandmother didn't trust clerks." The rake scraped. "Smart woman."

"Maren Aldous was listed on the evacuation rolls at Corven's Ferry. Twenty-two years ago. She crossed the border with a child who wasn't hers."

The rake stopped again, and this time it stayed stopped. Sable turned and looked at Kestrel full in the face for the first time, and Wren saw it then, even across the hot orange distance of the floor — saw why the workers called her the ghost. High cheekbones, a jawline that belonged on old coins, coloring like nothing else in the foundry district, where everyone was some blend of borderland brown and river-pale. She looked like she'd been carved by people who'd never seen this valley. She looked like the portraits in the history books, the ones with the pages glued together by order of the Coalition Censorate.

"There's nothing over those mountains," Sable said. "That's what they taught us in school. There's nothing, there was nothing, and anybody who says different is a traitor or a fool. So which are you?"

"Neither, I hope."

"Fool, then." She turned back to her channel. "Get off my floor. You'll get us both reported."

"Sable—"

"*Voss.* My name is Voss." But her knuckles had gone white around the rake handle, and Wren, who had spent a lifetime reading people the way other people read weather, saw the storm behind the flat face and felt the first cold drop of something she refused to call conscience.

Kestrel reached inside his coat.

Wren tensed. Half the men who came into foundries asking questions came armed, and the other half were idiots. But what he drew out was neither knife nor paper. It was a photograph, soft-cornered and water-stained, and he held it out flat on his palm.

Sable looked at it the way you look at a snake. Then she looked, and everything in her face changed and locked shut all at once, like a door slamming in a house you thought was empty.

It was a child in the photograph. A toddler, maybe two years old, sitting on a stone step in front of a doorway carved with a crest — a mountain split by a sword. The child had a fistful of dark curls and a scowl far too serious for its face, and on the inside of its left wrist, plain as a brand, a birthmark shaped like a falling star.

"That's not me," Sable said.

"It's you," Kestrel said quietly. "It's you at two years old, in the courtyard of the palace at Corven, and the woman holding the camera was your mother, and the mark on your wrist hasn't changed because marks don't change. I've watched you work for twenty minutes, Sable. Every time you lift that rake, your sleeve pulls back."

The whole floor seemed to go quieter, though of course it didn't — the furnaces roared on, the hammers fell, the world continued being loud and indifferent. But in the little circle of space around the two of them, Wren could have heard a pin drop in a thunderstorm.

Sable pushed back her sleeve.

Wren craned. She couldn't see the wrist from her angle, but she could see Kestrel's face, and Kestrel's face told her everything. It went through recognition and grief and something almost like fear in the space of a breath, and then it settled into the careful blankness of a man standing on ice he knows is thin.

"My grandmother said this was a curse," Sable said. Her voice had gone strange — younger, and older, both at once. "She said it was a debt. She used to say — " She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, reciting, in the cadence of words said so many times they'd worn smooth as river stones: "*You carry it so they can't take it. Don't let them see it. Don't let them ask.*"

"She was right," Kestrel said. "And I'm sorry, because I'm asking."

"Then you're exactly what she told me to run from." Sable pulled the sleeve down hard and stepped back, and now her voice rose, cracking through the foundry din, turning heads. "There is no kingdom. There is no heir. There is a girl who pours iron twelve hours a day and sleeps in a room above a tannery, and if you think I'm going to throw that away for a dead country and a photograph, you're insane. Do you know what happens to people who talk about Callowmere? They disappear. My grandmother buried friends. Whole families, gone between one census and the next, and nobody asks, because asking is how you go next."

"I know what happens," Kestrel said. "It happened to me."

"Then you should be the last person—" She caught herself. Looked at him harder. "What happened to you?"

"That's a long story, and you shouldn't hear it here." He glanced around the floor, and Wren saw him clock the same things she'd already clocked: the foreman watching from the furnace platform, the runner boy who'd slipped out the side door the moment voices raised, the two men by the coal bins who weren't working and weren't talking and were wearing coats too good for a foundry. "Please. Ten minutes. Somewhere with fewer ears."

"No." Sable picked up her rake. "No. Whatever you think I am, I'm not. Whatever you think I owe, I don't. My grandmother paid everything. She paid with her whole life so I could have a boring one, and I intend to honor that." She drove the rake into the slag and didn't look at him again. "Get off my floor. Please."

For a moment Kestrel stood there, photograph still open on his palm, and Wren watched a man lose an argument he'd clearly been winning in his head for days. Then he closed his hand around the photo, bowed — actually bowed, like something out of an old book — and turned away.

He passed within arm's reach of Wren's hiding spot, and she saw that his face was gray with exhaustion and something worse than exhaustion.

She should have let him go. That was the smart play. Follow him home, learn where he slept, then decide whether to sell what she knew to the Coalition liaison office or squeeze him directly. Clean business. She'd done dirtier.

Instead she fell into step beside him at the door and said, "You're going to get that girl killed, walking in here like a tax collector."

Kestrel startled badly — jumped like a stung horse, hand going under his coat — and then stared at her. "Who—"

"Wren. I sell water. I also sell information, discretion, and the occasional alibi, rates negotiable." She jerked her chin back toward the floor. "You just announced yourself to a foreman, a censor's runner, and two field agents in foundrymen's coats. By tomorrow morning there will be a report in Vess saying a stranger came asking after Callowmere bloodlines. By the day after, someone will come asking the girl questions she won't enjoy. That's how it works. I've watched it work."

His face did the gray thing again, deeper. "How do you know all that?"

"Because I watch. It's my whole profession. Watch, remember, sell." She shrugged, buckets forgotten back at the wall, hands suddenly restless without them. "Here's what I know for free, since you look like a man who's about to do something stupid. Those two coats by the coal bins? They came in an hour before you did. They were waiting. Which means somebody talked, and it wasn't me, and it wasn't the girl, which means either you've been followed longer than you think, or somebody in this town already knew who you'd come to find."

Kestrel went very still. Wren had seen quarry animals do exactly that — the moment when running stops being an option and thinking has to take over.

"The chest," he said, mostly to himself. "Halloway. He offered to forgive everything I owe if I handed it over. I told him no. I thought — I thought he was just a creditor."

"And now?"

"Now I think I need to leave Vess tonight." He looked at her, really looked, and Wren felt the weight of it, the assessment, a mountain guide measuring a client. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're interesting," she said, which was true, and because the alternative answer — *because that girl's face when she looked at the photograph made my chest hurt and I hate it* — was not a thing she intended to say out loud in a foundry. "Interesting pays better than safe. Usually."

Behind them, back at the pour line, Sable Voss stood motionless among the moving workers, rake forgotten, staring at the doorway where Kestrel had been. And as Wren watched, the girl pulled back her sleeve one more time, looked at the mark on her own wrist in the furnace light, and covered it slowly, gently, the way you'd cover the face of someone sleeping.

Then the foreman shouted her name and she bent back to the slag, and the foundry swallowed her whole.

"Come on," Wren said. "Before those coats decide you're worth following. And Emrys — whatever your real name is — you're buying the drinks. Information this good doesn't come with the rain."

They went out into the coal-smutted evening together, and behind them the chimneys of Ferro & Sons went on bleeding their slow orange smoke into the dark, like a signal fire nobody had tended in twenty years.

Two streets later, Wren noticed the man in the good coat fall in behind them, and understood that the stupid thing had already started, whether she liked it or not.
