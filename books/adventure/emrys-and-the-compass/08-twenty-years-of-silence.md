## 8. Twenty Years of Silence

The foundry's back room smelled of coal smoke and old iron, and Sable had never once thought of it as a place where anything important could happen. It was a room for cooling off after a pour, for eating bread with ash still on your knuckles, for the ten minutes of dark you stole between shifts. Now it held a stranger with river water still drying in the seams of his coat, a sharp-eyed girl who kept touching a brass compass like it might bite, and a chest that had made her grandmother's voice rise up out of twenty years of silence.

She hadn't wanted to open it. That was the truth of it. When Kestrel—Emrys, he'd said, but she'd watched his face when he said it and knew it was a coat he wore, not skin—had laid the journals on the workbench and asked her, carefully, whether any of it meant something to her, her first instinct had been to say no. To say *wrong girl, wrong bloodline, wrong everything*, and go back to the crucible where the only thing anyone ever asked of her was that she not burn herself.

But then Wren had opened the chest fully, and the smell had come up out of it—paper and wax and something older, something like the inside of a church—and Sable had looked down at the crest stamped into the lid, the tower and the two rivers, and her hands had started shaking before her mind caught up to why.

Her grandmother used to draw that crest. In flour on the table. In spilled tea. In the condensation on the window when the room was cold enough to fog.

*Never tell anyone what this is,* Gran would say, and wipe it away with her sleeve. *Never tell anyone what I'm about to tell you.*

Sable sat down on an upturned crate because her legs had opinions of their own, and began, for the first time in her life, to tell someone.

* * *

"She told them as stories," Sable said. "That was the only way she could make me understand—that they were stories, bedtime things, so if I repeated them at school it wouldn't matter. Children mix up stories. Everyone knows children lie by accident." She turned her hands over, looked at the burn scars there, the calluses. "Except they weren't stories. And she knew one day I'd figure that out."

Kestrel had pulled one of the journals into his lap but wasn't reading it. He was watching her the way you watch a fuse burn—still, careful, not wanting to disturb the flame. Wren had gone uncharacteristically quiet, perched on the edge of the workbench with the compass flat in her palm.

"The first story was about the winter the markets closed," Sable said. "Not a famine—she never said famine, she said *the winter the markets closed*. The granaries were full, she said. She'd seen them. Her father worked the granary roads, hauling grain from the valley storehouses up to the capital, and one autumn the orders just stopped. No one bought. The roads were ordered empty. And the grain—" Sable swallowed. "The grain rotted in the storehouses while people starved in the city. She said soldiers guarded the doors. Not Callowmere soldiers. Soldiers with coalition armbands, though nobody called it a coalition yet, they called it the Provisional Authority, the friendly neighbors come to help with the harvest."

"Rotting grain under guard," Kestrel said quietly. He opened the journal in his lap, thumbed through water-swollen pages until he found what he was looking for, and read aloud: "*Third of Harvestmonth. The storehouses at Vael Cross stand full and sealed. The guards will not say whose seal is on the orders. We counted eleven wagons turned back at the ford. Eleven.*" He looked up. "There are entries like that for six weeks. Whoever wrote this was counting wagons."

"Gran counted too," Sable said. "In her way. She always said the number eleven like it was a prayer. I thought it was just a thing old people did, repeating numbers." Her laugh came out wrong, cracked in the middle. "Gods. She was there. This person writing this—she might have stood next to them at the ford."

"It's signed M. Vael," Kestrel said. "A surveyor, by the entries. Civilian. He was documenting, not fighting."

"Vael Cross," Sable repeated. "That's the name of the place in the second story. The bridge." She wrapped her arms around herself. "You have to understand how she told it. She'd get halfway and stop, and say *that part isn't for children*, and finish it weeks later like paying off a debt in installments. The bridge came last, when I was maybe twelve. The bridge at Marrow Ford."

Wren's head came up sharply. "Marrow Ford. That's on our route. Or it was—the bridge is down, has been down forever, everyone says it fell in the war."

"It didn't fall," Sable said. "That's the whole point. That's why it was forbidden." She made herself keep going, made herself stand in her grandmother's kitchen twenty years ago, watching the old woman's hands shake around a cup of barley tea. "The night the capital fell—or the night they said it fell, the night the Provisional Authority announced the royal family was dead and the government dissolved itself in disgrace—there were people crossing at Marrow Ford. Refugees, couriers, whatever was left of the court trying to get out with records, with children, with proof. And the bridge was brought down while they were on it. Not by artillery. By charges. Set by men who'd been working on the structure for three days dressed as repair crews. Gran's cousin was on that bridge. She saw the flash from the far bank. She was fourteen."

Nobody spoke. In the walls of the foundry, somewhere deep in the building, a furnace ticked as it cooled, a sound like slow counting.

Kestrel set the journal down and pulled out another. His hands weren't quite steady either, Sable noticed. He flipped pages with the care of a man handling something explosive, which, she supposed, he was.

"Marrow Ford," he said. "Here. *They have been 'repairing' the bridge for three days. There is nothing to repair. I walked it yesterday; the stonework is sounder than my own house. Tonight I am going to sit on the far bank and write down what happens, because no one else will.*" Kestrel stopped. Read the rest silently first, Sable could see him doing it, his eyes moving fast, his jaw tightening. Then he read aloud, and his voice had gone flat the way voices do when they're carrying more weight than they can shape:

"*Just past midnight. The charges went in sequence, upstream pier first. The span folded like a knee. There were lanterns on it. I counted the lanterns. I will count them every night for the rest of my life. Twenty-two lanterns, and the river took all of them.*"

"Twenty-two," Sable whispered. "Gran said twenty-two. She said her cousin carried a lantern because the night was black, and after, when she was old, Gran couldn't sleep without a light burning. She said it was for the ones who crossed dark." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I used to think that was just a quirk. Old women and their candles. It was a *census*. She was keeping a census of the dead in her own house, one flame at a time, and I never once asked her who the light was for because I was afraid of the answer, and now the answer is sitting on a workbench in front of me in a dead man's handwriting."

Wren slid off the bench. Sable half expected some flippant deflection—the girl dealt in jokes the way other people dealt in coin—but Wren just crouched down in front of her, awkward, and said, "Hey. You don't have to do all of it tonight."

"Yes, I do," Sable said. "Because if I stop now I'll never start again, and she waited twenty years for someone to ask. She died waiting. I'm not doing that twice."

* * *

The third story was about the heir.

Sable told it standing up, pacing, because sitting still had become impossible. "This is the one she made me swear on blood over. Actual blood, pricked thumb, smeared on the doorframe, the whole ritual. I thought she was being dramatic. Old country stuff." She laughed again, and it was worse than the crying. "She said the youngest princess's daughter survived the fall. Not the princess—the *granddaughter*. A child of three, smuggled out ahead of the collapse, before the bridges, before the borders closed. Sent south with a servant family who were told to forget their own names and take new ones. Told to raise the child as their own blood and never, ever let her learn what she was, because the moment the Provisional Authority heard a Callowmere heir breathed, they'd send men. Quiet men. Men who were good at finding children."

"And the compass?" Kestrel asked.

"I don't know. She never mentioned a compass. But she said—" Sable stopped pacing. "She said the loyalists built a way home. A thread through the mountains, a chain of safe houses, a sign only the true blood could follow. She said, *if the needle ever finds you, girl, you run the other way or you run all the way, but you don't stand still*. I thought she meant a sewing needle. Some proverb. Needle of fate, needle of the heart." She looked at the compass in Wren's palm, its brass case scarred, its glass clouded, its dark needle lying steady as a plumb line against the window's direction—not north, not anywhere north, but east and slightly south, toward the high country. Toward, Sable realized with a cold spreading feeling, the direction she had dreamed about her entire life without once knowing why.

"That's why you flinched when we showed you the photograph," Kestrel said gently.

"The birthmark." Sable pushed her sleeve up past her elbow. On the inside of her forearm, pale brown, shaped like nothing and everything—a river splitting around a stone. "My mother had one. My grandmother said hers was the mark of the river-wardens, old Callowmere service families, and that hers had been burned off her with lye when she was young so no one could read it. Burned off her own daughter's chance at a name before the girl could talk. But mine—" Her voice failed and restarted. "Mine she couldn't bring herself to touch. She cried when I was born. I always thought it was joy."

The room was very quiet. Outside, Vess went on with its evening—the mill wheels, the gulls arguing over fish guts, the ordinary machinery of a town that had never been anything but itself. Inside, four objects lay on a workbench: a stack of drowned journals, a photograph of a child, a brass compass pointing faithfully at a woman who sold iron heat for a living, and twenty years of forbidden stories that had just stopped being stories.

Kestrel picked up the photograph. Held it beside her forearm without touching her. The child in the picture had the same mark, same arm, same angle. Same dark hair, same wide-set eyes that Sable had spent her whole life being teased about—*foundling eyes, forge-girl eyes*—and that now resolved, horribly and perfectly, into a face she'd seen a hundred times in official portraits reproduced in histories she'd been required to study: the last royal family of Callowmere, lined up on a palace staircase, smiling for a camera eight months before the end.

"You're the granddaughter," Wren said. Not a question. Almost an apology.

"Don't say it like that," Sable snapped, and immediately regretted it, because Wren's face did something wounded and quick. "No—I'm sorry. It's not—it's not you. It's that saying it makes it real, and real means the quiet men exist, and real means Gran spent fifty years being afraid in a language I couldn't hear, and I ate dinner across from that fear every single night of my childhood and called it *gran being gran*." She sat back down on the crate, hard. "She gave me one other thing. At the end. I thought it was senility. She grabbed my wrist with everything she had left—she weighed nothing by then, a bird, a bundle of sticks—and she said, *When they come asking, and they will come asking, remember that the kingdom didn't die. It was murdered, and the murderers wrote the history books, and the books are the only place it's buried.*"

Kestrel had gone very still. Sable watched him look down at the journal in his lap—at the watermark-stained cover, the surveyor's neat hand, the record of rotted grain and counted lanterns and a bridge full of lights—and she watched him arrive somewhere she couldn't follow.

"What?" she asked. "What is it?"

"These journals were carried out of Callowmere," he said slowly. "Someone ran the gauntlet with them—across Marrow Ford or before it, south through the passes, keeping them dry, keeping them hidden, for twenty years. And then they ended up in a chest in a flooded river outside Vess." He looked up, and his eyes had a bleakness in them that she recognized suddenly, intimately, because she'd seen the same thing in her grandmother's face whenever strangers asked about the old country. "Which means whoever carried them was hunted. And whoever hunted them wanted them at the bottom of the river. And neither side is finished."

As if the words had summoned it, there came a knock at the foundry's street door—three raps, unhurried, official-sounding—and the furnace-tick of the cooling building seemed suddenly very loud.

Wren was on her feet with the compass palmed. Kestrel moved the journals off the workbench and into the chest in one smooth motion, and Sable understood that both of them had done this before, that hiding was a craft they'd practiced, and that she had just inherited a place in a story that had been running for twenty years without her.

Through the shuttered window, lamplight moved on the street. Two shadows, standing patient.

"They'll be asking about salvage," Kestrel murmured. "River salvage. Stay behind me, say nothing, and Sable—" he met her eyes, and there was something in his face that was almost kind and almost afraid, "—whatever happens, don't let them see your arm."
