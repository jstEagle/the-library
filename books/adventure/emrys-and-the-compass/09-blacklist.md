## 9. Blacklist

Kestrel did not sleep that night. He sat at the table in his rented room with the chest open before him and the lamp turned low, and he read until his eyes burned, and what he read pulled him backward through seven years like a hook through the gills.

The journals were written in a careful, clerkish hand — someone trained to record, trained to be believed. The ink had bled where the river had gotten in, but the words survived, and they were not the words of a dead kingdom mourning itself. They were the words of men who had watched the famine come and knew it had been sent.

*Grain shipments rerouted by coalition order, March. The eastern granaries sealed "for inspection" and never reopened. The passes closed to relief caravans on grounds of quarantine. A nation does not starve by accident.*

Kestrel set the page down because his hands were shaking, and when he looked up, the small brass compass sat on the table between him and the lamp, its needle trembling toward the wall, toward the mountains beyond the wall, patient as a debt collector. He had spent seven years telling himself a story about how his life had ended: that he had made a mistake, that mistakes have prices, that he was paying his fairly. It was a comfortable story. Comfortable stories are the most expensive kind.

Because he remembered everything now. He had spent seven years trying to remember less, and now the journals had cracked the seal on all of it, and it came back whole.

* * *

The expedition had been called the Meridian Ascent, and it had been, on paper, a survey climb — six clients, two guides, one route up the north face of Cairn Vael that no one had ever mapped properly. Kestrel had been twenty-six years old and already known in three valleys as the guide who could read weather in the color of the ice. Reiss had been his rope partner. They had climbed together for four years. Reiss had slept in Kestrel's tent through storms, had shared his last chocolate on the summit of something nameless, had once carried Kestrel's sprained ankle down four thousand feet of scree without complaint. When people asked how long they'd known each other, they answered in the same breath: *since we were boys.*

The morning of the third day, the sky over Cairn Vael was the color of pewter, and Kestrel did not like it. That was the first thing the tribunal never asked about, later — that he had said so, out loud, at dawn, standing at the mouth of the high camp with his face tipped into the wind.

"We go up or we don't," said Aldous Crane, the lead client, a shipping magnate with a mountaineer's vanity and none of a mountaineer's patience. "I've paid for the north face."

"The north face will still be there next season."

"I won't be." Crane smiled the smile of a man accustomed to being obeyed by geography. "You're the guide. Guide."

Kestrel had looked at Reiss then. Looking at Reiss was how he made decisions in those years; it was almost superstition, the way some climbers touch stone twice. And Reiss had shrugged, easy, unbothered, and said, "Sky's fine. I checked the barometer twice."

That was the second thing no one ever asked about. Because Reiss had not checked the barometer twice. Kestrel learned that afterward, from the equipment manifest, from the little lacquered instrument itself, which was recovered from the debris field with its case unopened and its needle frozen at a reading that would have told them both to turn around before breakfast.

They went up. Four clients, two junior guides, Kestrel leading, Reiss running sweep. By noon the pewter sky had gone the green-black of deep water, and the wind came off the col with a sound Kestrel had heard only twice in his life — a low, harmonic moan, like a bottle blown across — and both times before, he had turned his party around and been called cautious and been glad of it.

He gave the order to descend at half past twelve. He would swear to that until the day he died, and he did swear to it, later, under oath, in a room that smelled of floor wax, and it did not matter at all.

What mattered was the fixed line. On the descent, the party roped together along the traverse below the serac field, and the anchor — the anchor Kestrel had placed himself, that morning, hammering a piton into rock he had tested with his own hands — failed. Not cracked. Not pulled. *Failed*, cleanly, the way metal fails when it has been weakened deliberately, and Kestrel knew the difference even as he was falling, because every guide learns the sound a good piton makes coming out under load and the sound a bad one makes, and this was the bad sound, the wrong sound, the sound of something that had been broken before it was ever set.

Four hundred feet of slope took four climbers. Kestrel caught on the lower anchor — the backup, the one habit had made him place against every argument he'd ever had with himself about weight — and hung there in the white roar while the mountain ate the others. He could hear them. That was the part that lived behind his eyes for years afterward: not the fall, but the sounds after the fall, thinning out one by one, until there was only wind.

Aldous Crane died. Two of the junior guides died. A client named Marta Ilves died, a quiet woman who had given him her extra gloves on day one because his looked worn, and whose family would later be paid quietly and sign quietly and never speak of it again.

Reiss survived. Reiss had been thirty feet back on the rope when the anchor went — far enough to be spared, near enough to see everything — and Reiss got Kestrel down the mountain, half-carrying him through the storm, and held him in the base camp tent while Kestrel shook and shook and said *the piton, the piton was wrong* over and over like a man reciting a prayer he didn't believe in.

"You're in shock," Reiss said. "You placed it. You're the only one who touched it."

Kestrel had stared at him. Even hollowed out, even half-dead with cold and grief, some animal part of him had registered the sentence and found it strange — not cruel, exactly. *Finished.* Like a door closing on a room Reiss had already decided to leave.

* * *

The inquiry took eleven days. It felt, even then, less like an investigation than a ceremony — something performed rather than conducted. The coalition's Mountain Authority sent three assessors from the lowlands, men with soft hands and clipboards, and they interviewed the surviving clients' families and the porters and the base camp cook, and they examined the failed piton, which arrived at their laboratory in a padded box and came back, weeks later, with a finding.

*Metal fatigue consistent with improper placement technique.*

Improper placement technique. Kestrel had taught placement clinics. He had written the placement manual used by two guide associations. He had hammered that piton home with the same hands that had put ten thousand anchors into ten thousand walls without losing a climber to gear failure in eight years of work. But the finding was signed, and signatures are heavier than truth, and one of the signatures belonged to Davor Reiss, certified witness, rope partner, friend since boyhood.

Kestrel remembered the hearing. He remembered it in fragments, the way you remember a fever. The room with the tall windows and the light coming in gray. The assessor with the clipped mustache asking him to demonstrate his hammering technique on a practice block, watching him with an expression of polite, pre-written disappointment. Crane's brother-in-law, a coalition functionary, speaking about negligence in a voice like a man reading a menu. And Reiss, called last, sitting in the witness chair with his hands folded, looking at Kestrel only once — one glance, quick, almost apologetic — before he said, in a steady voice:

"He placed the anchor. I saw him place it. I have no reason to lie."

*I have no reason to lie.* Seven words. Kestrel had turned them over for years afterward, looking for the flaw in them, the way you worry a loose tooth. There was no flaw. That was the horror of it. Every word Reiss spoke at the inquiry was true, or true enough, or unfalsifiable, and the architecture of the lie was built entirely from load-bearing truths. Yes, Kestrel had placed the anchor. Yes, Reiss had seen him. Yes, Reiss had no reason to lie — except the one reason, the real one, the one that never appeared on any manifest.

What was the reason? For seven years Kestrel had assumed it was fear — that Reiss, facing the same tribunal, had chosen survival. Cowardice was almost comforting. Cowardice was human. You could hate a coward and still recognize him across a table.

But sitting now in his lamplit room in Vess with the Callowmere journals open under his hand, Kestrel felt the assumption shift beneath him like snowpack starting to move.

Because here was the thing he had never let himself line up beside the other thing. The Meridian Ascent had been funded, in part, through a coalition cultural grant — Crane had bragged about it at base camp, waving the letterhead like a trophy. The route they'd been surveying ran along the old Callowmere border corridor, the high passes that had once carried the kingdom's grain and refugees, passes the coalition had sealed after the annexation and wanted surveyed, quietly, for reasons never stated on paper. And the inquiry — a routine disaster inquiry, four dead climbers, regrettable — had been conducted not by the regional authority but by officials flown in from the capital, with a speed and a finality that Kestrel, in his grief, had taken for bureaucratic efficiency.

Now he read the journal entries and the pieces lay down next to each other like bones finding their joints.

*The coalition's survey teams work the border corridor under cover of sport expeditions,* the clerkish hand recorded, dated two years before the fall. *They map our passes and call it tourism. When we protest, the protests themselves become evidence of instability.*

Sport expeditions. Survey routes. A guide who knew those mountains better than any man alive — a guide with no political ties, no faction, no protection, a border-town boy whose testimony no one powerful would ever vouch for. If such a guide were to discover what the surveys were truly for, if he were to ask questions, if he were ever in a position to make trouble —

And if such a guide were destroyed, publicly, convincingly, blacklisted so thoroughly that no one would ever hire him, credit him, or believe him again —

Then whatever he said afterward would be the ravings of a disgraced man. The best lies are built on ruined witnesses.

Kestrel sat very still. The lamp hissed. Outside, Vess was settling into its evening noises — cart wheels, a dog, the river muttering in its stony bed — and inside, seven years of his life quietly rearranged themselves around a new center of gravity.

He had thought he fell because the mountain was cruel and the piton was bad and the world was unfair. Then he had thought he fell because Reiss was weak. Both stories had kept him upright the way a crutch keeps a man upright: functional, and a confession that he could not stand alone.

But the piton hadn't just failed. It had been *prepared*. He knew metallurgy the way Reiss knew paperwork, and fatigue fractures do not present the way that fracture presented, and he had known it, dimly, for seven years, and had buried the knowledge because the alternative was unbearable. If the piton was sabotaged, then someone had wanted the climb to fail. Someone had wanted *him* on that rope, leading, blamed, broken. And Reiss — Reiss who checked the barometer and lied about checking it, Reiss who stood thirty feet back on the rope, Reiss who testified with his hands folded and his voice steady — Reiss had not merely failed to stop it.

Reiss had been the instrument. Maybe witting. Maybe half-witting, a man fed a story he wanted to believe — that his brilliant friend had grown careless, that ambition had made Kestrel sloppy, that the tragedy was tragedy and not design. It would even have been kinder, in Reiss's arithmetic, to believe it. Believing it meant the world was still sane. Believing it meant he wasn't the kind of man who helped kill four people to bury a question.

Kestrel thought of Reiss's face at the hearing — that single glance, quick, almost apologetic — and understood it at last. It had not been guilt. It had been *recognition*: the look of a man confirming that the sacrifice was going according to plan, and hating the plan, and proceeding anyway.

Or perhaps that was vanity. Perhaps that was Kestrel, seven years downstream, building his own comfortable story — the story in which he had mattered enough to destroy. He turned the thought over and could not tell. That was the deepest injury of the thing, deeper than the blacklist, deeper than the hunger winters in Vess: he could no longer audit his own memory. Every recollection now came stamped *possible fabrication*, and he would carry that stamp until someone else's evidence confirmed his own.

The journals confirmed it. That was the terrible gift lying open on his table. Page after page of a bureaucracy documenting itself — requisitions, rerouted grain, sealed passes, and then, near the end, in a different, hurried hand:

*They have begun removing persons. Guides, clerks, teachers — anyone who knows the old routes, the old records, the old names. Not arrested. Discredited. A discredited man is cheaper than a dead one and speaks to no one.*

A discredited man is cheaper than a dead one.

Kestrel laughed — one dry note, startling himself in the empty room. Seven years he had crawled through the world under the name Emrys, taking rich fools up beginner trails, eating debt, sleeping in rooms with water stains shaped like countries, and he had believed the punishment fit the crime because the crime had been pronounced by men with seals. He had served his sentence faithfully for a verdict that had been manufactured the way the famine had been manufactured: deliberately, administratively, by people who filed the paperwork and went home to dinner.

The compass needle trembled against its glass, pointing at the wall, at the mountains, at whatever waited out there in the erased country. Pointing, he understood now, at the same machinery that had ground him under. His exile and Callowmere's erasure were not two tragedies. They were one tragedy, viewed from different altitudes.

He closed the journal. His hands were steady now, steadier than they had been in seven years, because rage, fully clarified, is a kind of calm.

Somewhere out in the dark of Vess, a knock sounded at the street door of the building — heavy, unhurried, official. Then again.

Kestrel blew out the lamp.
