## 10. Halloway's Price

The Vessel & Anchor was the kind of tavern that kept its lamps turned low so patrons could pretend they hadn't seen each other. Halloway liked it for exactly that reason. He took the corner table by the woodpile, ordered a beer he had no intention of drinking, and waited.

He was good at waiting. Twenty-two years as a debt collector taught you that money moved slow, like water finding its level, and men who chased it too fast spilled it. He sat with his hands flat on the table — a habit, keeping them visible, keeping them honest-looking — and watched the door through the smoky haze while he turned over in his mind what he'd learned that afternoon.

The guide called Emrys had a chest. A river-salvaged chest, hauled up from the shallows below the mill race three days after the flood, carried home under his coat like a stolen loaf. Halloway's boy on the docks — a limping lad named Petch who sold him gossip by the ounce — had seen it. Seen the man pry it open by candlelight in his rented room above the chandler's, seen him sit back on his heels and not move for a long time, just staring at whatever was inside.

And Halloway knew what chests pulled from rivers meant, because he'd been told to watch for them. He'd been told eleven years ago, in a room much like this one, by a man with a coalition pin hidden under his collar.

The door opened. Cold air came in with it, and a figure in a gray traveling coat that cost more than everything Halloway owned put together.

Davor Reiss crossed the room without looking left or right, which was its own kind of arrogance — a man so certain no one would dare mark him that he didn't bother checking. He sat down across from Halloway, uninvited, and signaled the barkeep with two fingers. Two more beers arrived. Neither man touched them.

"You look well," Reiss said. "Vess agrees with you."

"Vess is damp and full of liars," Halloway said. "It agrees with me fine."

Reiss smiled the way a ledger balances — precisely, without warmth. He was younger than Halloway remembered from their first meeting, or perhaps Halloway was simply older now, worn down to the useful parts. Gray at the temples. Eyes the color of a frozen puddle. He had the stillness of a man who'd never once needed to hurry.

"Report," Reiss said.

Halloway laid it out the way he laid out accounts: itemized, unsentimental. "Your salvage man exists. Calls himself Emrys Fen, works the low trails for coin when the tourists come through, does odd hauling work when they don't. Rents a room above Corrin's chandlery. Owes me four hundred and sixty marks across six creditors, most of it bought up cheap, all of it mine now." He paused. "He pulled a chest out of the river after the flood. Sealed. Iron-banded. He's had it three days."

Reiss's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a small shift, like ice creaking on a lake. "You're certain."

"Petch saw him carry it up. Saw the light burning past midnight. The man hasn't worked since. Hasn't spent, hasn't drunk, hasn't paid a mark against his debts. That's not a man who found scrap iron, Mr. Reiss. That's a man who found trouble and is deciding which way to run."

"And the chest itself?"

"I offered to buy it off him this morning. Told him I'd clear every debt he owed, wipe the slate, if he handed it over unopened." Halloway turned his beer glass a quarter turn on the table. "He refused."

Now Reiss did move. He leaned forward, elbows on the table, and studied Halloway with the particular attention of a man re-reading a line he'd skimmed the first time. "Refused. A drowning man refused a rope."

"He didn't know why I wanted it. That's the strange part." Halloway frowned. He'd turned the moment over all afternoon and it still didn't sit square. "I've bought things off desperate men for twenty years. They ask questions first — who wants it, why, what's in it — then they take the rope. This one looked at me like I'd asked for his hand instead of his chest. And then he said no. Flat. No counteroffer, no haggling. Just no." He shook his head slowly. "I've been doing this a long time, sir. Men don't refuse free forgiveness unless they've already decided the thing they're holding matters more than the life they're living."

Reiss sat back. For a long moment he said nothing, and the tavern noise filled the space between them — dice rattling somewhere, a woman laughing too loud, rain starting against the shutters.

"He doesn't know what he has," Reiss said at last. It wasn't a question either, but it wanted confirming.

"He can't. If he knew, he'd have run already, or he'd have sold it to me twice over. He's holding it because it feels important, not because he knows." Halloway hesitated, then added the last item, the one he'd saved. "There's more. He's been asking questions around town. Quiet ones. About Callowmere."

The word landed on the table between them like a dropped coin.

"About what?" Reiss said softly.

"Callowmere. Old stories. Who survived the fall, where families went. He asked a rag-and-bone woman, one of the water sellers, someone at the records house. Small questions, spread thin. But they add up." Halloway met the frozen eyes. "That's not curiosity, Mr. Reiss. That's direction."

* * *

Reiss stood and walked to the window, hands clasped behind his back, and looked out at nothing — the shuttered street, the rain silvering the cobbles, the black shoulder of the mountains beyond the rooftops. Halloway used the silence to drink half his beer. It was bad beer. It always was, in rooms where business happened.

Eleven years ago, the man who'd recruited him — Reiss's predecessor, a soft-spoken functionary named Aldous Vey — had explained the arrangement over a meal Halloway still remembered course by course, because it was the best food he'd ever eaten. *You collect debts,* Vey had said. *It's honest work. We're going to make it useful work. Certain names will cross your desk. You'll watch them. You'll report. In exchange, your own debts — and you have so many, Mr. Halloway, don't you — will be quietly managed.* Not forgiven. Managed. Kept at exactly the height that kept a man climbing.

Halloway had understood immediately what he was signing. He'd signed anyway. A man drowning doesn't inspect the rope.

For eleven years he'd fed them names. A smuggler in Harrow's Bend. A widow whose late husband had kept letters he shouldn't have. A schoolteacher who asked about old borders in front of the wrong students. None of them, as far as Halloway ever learned, had come to harm — or none he'd been shown. That was the trick of it, the thing he'd built his peace on brick by brick: he collected information, not heads. He told himself the machinery did the rest somewhere far away, in offices he'd never see, and that a man who only ever passed paper couldn't be held responsible for what the paper became.

Then Emrys Fen had pulled a chest out of the river, and the paper had acquired a name Halloway actually knew. A face. A man who'd stood in the rain outside the chandler's with mud to his knees, looking like any other broke guide in a border town, and who had refused — genuinely refused — the one thing Halloway had to offer.

"You understand what happens next," Reiss said. He still faced the window. His reflection in the dark glass was faint, ghostly, barely there at all. "The chest contains materials relating to a matter closed twenty years ago. Its recovery is a matter of state security. The individuals associated with its recovery—" he turned, and his voice stayed perfectly level, perfectly reasonable, the voice of a man reading a shipping manifest, "—will need to be managed."

"Managed," Halloway repeated.

"That's the term."

"I know the term." Halloway set down his glass. "I've heard the term for eleven years. I've never once asked what it means when it comes back around. I'm asking now."

Reiss regarded him with something that might, in a better man, have been respect. "It means the chest comes to me intact. It means the guide stops being a question anyone asks. It means the water seller, if she's involved, and anyone else he's spoken to, are accounted for." He said it the way a clerk reads an inventory. Three crates of nails. One chest. Several persons, managed. "You'll continue as you have been. Debt collection. You'll press him harder — I want him frightened, moving, predictable. Frightened men run toward the border or into the hills, and both directions are easier than a man standing still."

"And if he won't be frightened?"

"Then you'll tell us where he sleeps, and other people will handle the rest, and you will never learn what the rest entailed." Reiss picked up his beer, tasted it, and set it down with faint distaste, as though even the beer were beneath the occasion. "That has always been our arrangement, Halloway. You are the ledger. You are not the pen."

Halloway looked at his own hands on the table. Big hands, scarred knuckles, hands that had pried open strongboxes and dragged furniture into the street and once, early on, held a weeping woman's door shut while men searched her house. Honest-looking hands. He'd kept them visible for twenty years so people would trust him, and he'd used that trust the way a knife uses a whetstone.

"There's a girl," he said. "Water seller. Goes by Wren. Sharp as a fishhook. She's been circling the guide — I think she means to sell him something, or sell him, depending on the day. If he runs, she's likely to run with him. She knows the smuggler routes out of Vess better than anyone."

"Add her to the list."

"She's maybe nineteen."

Reiss finally looked at him directly, and there was nothing cruel in his face, which was somehow worse. There was nothing in it at all. "Halloway. Do you know how many lists I've added names to? Do you know what happened to the last man who asked me to shorten one?" He let the question sit. He didn't answer it. He never answered anything; that was the discipline of him. "The chest was sealed with the Callowmere crest. Whatever else it holds, it holds proof that a nation existed. Proof is a disease. We contain outbreaks. That is the whole of my work and, whether you enjoy hearing it or not, the whole of yours."

* * *

They settled the details the way they settled everything: quickly, quietly, in the language of commerce. Halloway would press the debts Monday. He'd offer again — higher stakes this time, forgiveness plus two hundred marks cash, enough to make refusal look like madness to any neighbors listening. If refusal came again, he'd note the guide's movements, his associates, his sleep. A report every second day, left in the hollow post at the tannery gate, as always. Payment as always: fifty marks against the principal of a debt Halloway had owed so long he'd stopped remembering what it was originally for.

Reiss rose to leave. At the edge of the table he paused, coat already buttoned, rain-light behind him.

"One more thing," he said. "The guide. Emrys Fen. I want everything you can find on where he came from. Five years back, ten. Where he learned the mountains. Why a man with his skills is hauling freight in Vess for coppers." Something flickered across the frozen-puddle eyes — the first human thing Halloway had seen in them all evening, and it looked almost like hunger. "Men like that don't fall out of the sky. Someone erased him before we ever heard his name. I want to know who held the eraser."

"He's hiding from something," Halloway said. "I've thought so since the day he walked into my office. Too careful. Flinches at official paper. Pays cash, keeps no friends, signs nothing."

"Everyone's hiding from something. Most somethings don't matter." Reiss pulled on his gloves, finger by finger. "Find out if his does."

Then he was gone, the door swinging shut behind him, taking the cold air out with him, and the tavern resumed its low murmur as though a hole in the world had closed up.

Halloway sat alone with the two untouched beers and the rain on the shutters.

He should feel nothing. That was the professional condition — the thing twenty-two years had built into him, callus over callus. A name goes on a list. A list goes in a post. A post goes to a man with a pin under his collar. Somewhere far downstream, consequences occurred, and none of it stuck to the ledger-keeper. He'd made his peace in installments, the way he made everyone else pay, and the balance had held for eleven years.

But he kept seeing the guide's face that morning in the rain. Not angry. Not frightened, even. Just tired — tired in a way Halloway recognized from mirrors, the exhaustion of a man carrying something heavy up a long hill who has just been told the hill goes on forever. *No,* he'd said. Just that. A man refusing the rope because the rope, he'd somehow known, was attached to something worse than drowning.

And the girl. Nineteen, sharp as a fishhook, selling water in a town that had plenty of rain.

Halloway finished his beer. It had gone completely warm and completely bitter, and he drank it anyway, because waste offended him more than most sins.

Monday, he'd press the debts. He'd offer the rope again, higher and brighter, and watch the man refuse it again, and write down where he slept, and post the paper in the hollow at the tannery gate. That was the work. That had always been the work. The machinery did the rest, in offices he'd never see, to people he'd never meet.

Except now he'd met them. Except now the list had a face that looked tired instead of afraid, and a girl of nineteen, and a chest that some part of Halloway — the part he kept locked in the deepest room of himself, the room where the weeping woman's door still echoed — very badly wanted opened before the men with pins got to it first.

He left coins on the table, exact to the copper, and stepped out into the rain.

Behind him, in the corner by the woodpile, the barkeep collected the glasses and thought nothing at all about either man. That was the way of it. The machinery ran on people who thought nothing at all.

Three streets away, at the chandler's, a light burned past midnight in a rented room, and a man sat on his heels beside an open chest, deciding which way to run.

Halloway had until Monday to make sure he chose wrong.
