## 11. The Expedition Forms

Wren had a rule about people: count what they're running from before you count what they're offering, and the second number will always be bigger.

She'd learned it young, hawking rainwater in the Vess market from a barrel she'd filled at the cistern for free. Everyone in the borderlands was running from something. The trick was figuring out whether they'd drag it behind them like an anchor or throw it at you the first time things went wrong.

Kestrel — or Emrys, or whatever his real name was, because she'd stopped believing that one around the same time she'd stopped believing in the honesty of butchers — had come to her stall three days after the night in the foundry, when Sable had finally agreed to hear them out and Wren had finally agreed, against every instinct that kept her alive, to stop thinking of the compass as merchandise.

"We need to go into the mountains," he'd said. "All the way. And we can't do it as three people."

"Three people is a crowd," Wren had said. "Three people is a target. Three people is two more than I'd ever travel with."

"Three people can't carry rope, food, and a chest of journals over the Low Pass in winter. And three people can't testify." He'd said *testify* the way other men said *confess*, like a word with weight in it, a word he was afraid of and drawn to at once. "A chest in a cellar proves nothing. People have to carry it out. People have to stand up and say what it says."

"And you need bodies who won't ask too many questions."

"I need bodies who'll ask exactly the right ones."

So now she stood in the back of Odell's map shop on Tanner's Row, arms crossed, watching Kestrel do the thing he apparently did — the thing that had gotten her, a professional skeptic, to sign on — which was make you feel like the dangerous choice was the sensible one.

Odell's shop smelled of ink and mildew. Maps covered every wall, but Wren had noticed what Sable noticed first: the walls were full of everywhere *except* the high country. Coastlines, river deltas, the coal provinces, the coalition heartlands rendered in loving detail. The mountains north of Vess appeared only as blank cream-colored space with a decorative mountain symbol or two, the kind cartographers stamped on places they'd never bothered to look at.

"You want a survey of the high route," Odell said. He was a small man, sixty or so, with ink-stained fingers and the careful, apologetic voice of someone who'd spent a lifetime being precise. "In winter. With no funding, no commission, and no official sanction."

"That's the shape of it."

"Then you want me for a different reason." Odell set down his pen. "You want me because I'm the man who drew the blanks."

Wren watched Kestrel not flinch. She was starting to catalog that too — the way he absorbed a blow without moving, like a man who'd practiced being hit.

"I read your name in the journals' margins," Kestrel said. "Odell. Surveyor-General's office, twenty-two years ago. The last man licensed to map Callowmere before the erasure."

"Before I helped erase it." Odell's voice didn't waver, but his hands did, just slightly, as he squared a stack of charts that were already square. "There was an order. Redact the interior. Remove the roads, the settlements, the passes. I told myself it was a legal formality, that the kingdom was dissolved and dead names clutter the record. I was very good at telling myself things." He looked up. "You want a guide through country that exists on no map. I'm the only man alive who knows what was taken off the paper, because I'm the man who took it off."

"You could refuse," Wren said, because someone had to say it. "You're old. You're comfortable. Why in the hells would you walk into the mountains?"

Odell smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin as a knife's edge. "Because I've spent twenty-two years drawing the world as the coalition wants it to be, and I would like, before I die, to draw it once as it is."

Wren counted what he was running from. Guilt, mostly. Guilt was heavy but it didn't throw itself at people. Guilt just sat there, and made a man reliable in a grim, joyless way.

"Fine," she said. "But he sleeps apart from the rest of us. I've seen what guilt does to a camp."

* * *

Father Ivo they found where Wren said they'd find him: drunk in the chapel of Saint Ferren, which had been deconsecrated nine years ago and now served as a flophouse for the town's unhoused, though Ivo still swept the floor every morning and still, when he thought no one watched, straightened the broken altar cloth.

He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a beard like an unweeded garden and hands that shook until you put a cup in them. Wren had bought water from him for years — he always overpaid, always said *bless you* like he meant it, and she'd always thought: *there's a man who lost something bigger than a church.*

"You're the one who's been asking about Callowmere," Ivo said, when Kestrel laid it out. He said it flat, no question in it. "The whole tavern's been asking who you are. Reiss's men have been asking louder."

"You know Reiss?"

"I buried two of his informants." Ivo drank. "They were informants first and parishioners second, and I buried them anyway, because that's the job. Was the job." He set the cup down. "They took my orders from me for refusing to read a government decree from the pulpit. The decree listed forbidden histories. Callowmere was at the top. I told them a priest doesn't choose which graves get mourned." He laughed, a dry sound with no joy in it. "They were quite right to defrock me. I would have done it again."

"Then you understand what we're carrying," Kestrel said.

"I understand what it will cost. That's different." Ivo looked at each of them in turn — Kestrel with his borrowed face, Wren with her barrel-seller's shrug, Sable half-hidden in the doorway with the compass in her pocket like it was burning her. "You want a witness who can't be bought and can't be scared. I'm expensive, friends. I come with a God who's been very quiet for twenty years, and I intend to make Him answer for it."

"Is that a yes?" Sable asked, from the doorway. Her voice had that flatness she got when she was deciding something she'd already decided.

Ivo looked at her a long moment. "It's a yes," he said. "Though I'll warn you — I'll ask questions the whole way. Whether exposing the truth heals anything or just digs up the dead to kill them twice. You'll want to strangle me by the second week."

"Second week," Wren said, "we'll all want to strangle everybody. You'll fit right in."

* * *

The tracker was the hard one, because the tracker didn't talk, and Wren had built her whole life on talking.

They found him — her? Wren genuinely couldn't tell and had decided it didn't matter — living in a lean-to above the tanneries, where the river smell gave way to the smell of hides and ash. The locals called him Ash, which wasn't a name so much as a description: gray-eyed, gray-skinned from years of smoke, somewhere past forty, with a face that looked like it had been carved by weather and never finished.

He didn't invite them in. He stood outside his lean-to with a skinning knife in his hand, not threatening, just holding it, the way a man holds a tool he's used to holding.

Kestrel did the talking, which was a mistake, so Wren took over.

"We're going up the high route," she said. "Past the Low Pass, into the erased country. We need someone who can read snow, read sign, and get six fools across a glacier without burying them."

Ash looked at her. Didn't nod, didn't shake his head.

"We can pay," Wren said. "Not much. But we can pay."

Nothing.

"We can promise you won't be followed, which is a lie, but it's an optimistic lie."

Nothing. But his eyes moved — to Sable, standing back with her foundry soot still in the creases of her hands. To the compass-shaped bulge in her coat.

And then Ash did something Wren had never seen him do in ten years of watching the tannery district from a hundred angles, because watching was her trade. He held up his hand, and with one finger, in the dust of the ground, he drew a shape.

Wren stared at it. A mountain, drawn in three strokes. And beneath the mountain, a second shape: a bird. Long-winged. A callow-hawk.

Sable made a small sound.

"You know it," Ash said — and his voice, when it came, was rust and river-stones, a voice that hadn't been used in years. "The hawk of Callowmere."

"You can talk," Wren said, stupidly.

"I can." He crouched, smoothed the dust flat with his palm. "I chose not to. Twenty years ago, men came to the settlement where my family hid. They asked questions in the market town. My mother answered one." He drew a line through the mountain, clean, decisive. "The soldiers came three days later. I have not spoken a word to a stranger since. Words are how they find you."

"Then why talk now?" Kestrel asked.

Ash looked up, and his gray eyes were the color of the country they were going into. "Because you are going *there*. And I have spent twenty years not going there." He stood, sheathed the knife. "I will guide you. I will not promise to speak. And if I say we turn back — we turn back. That is the price."

"That's the fairest price anyone's offered yet," Wren said.

* * *

They met that night in the back of the foundry, among the dead furnaces, because Sable said it was the only room in Vess she trusted, and Wren had noticed that Sable didn't trust rooms, only the memory of heat.

Six of them around a foundry bench: Kestrel with his maps and his borrowed name; Odell with his ink-stained fingers and a leather case of the old pre-erasure charts he'd hidden for two decades like a man hiding stolen relics; Ivo with a bottle he'd promised to stop drinking from by morning; Ash, silent again now that the strangers were plural, communicating in gestures that Kestrel, unnervingly, seemed to half-understand; Sable with the compass open in front of her, its needle trembling toward a wall and, beyond the wall, the mountains.

And Wren, doing the arithmetic.

She'd spent her life counting. Coins, cistern-loads, exits, the exact number of steps between a market stall and the alley behind it. She counted now.

A guide with a false name and a dead man's debt. A cartographer who'd erased a country and wanted to un-erase it. A drunk priest with a grievance against heaven. A mute who'd found his voice for a graveyard. And an heir who didn't want the crown, carrying a compass that didn't point north, toward a kingdom that existed in one chest of waterlogged paper and nowhere else on earth.

That was the expedition. That was what stood between a coalition agent named Davor Reiss and the truth in the chest.

It was, Wren thought, the worst arithmetic she'd ever done. It didn't add up to an army. It barely added up to a rumor.

But then she looked at Sable, who was staring at the needle the way you stare at a wound you've finally decided to look at, and at Kestrel, who was watching Sable with an expression Wren recognized because she'd worn it herself for years — the expression of a person calculating, always calculating, how much longer the hiding could last.

Trust, Wren knew, wasn't a thing you felt. It was a thing you spent, like coin, and you spent it on outcasts because outcasts were the only ones whose price you could read. Every one of them at this bench had a reason to vanish into the mountains that was stronger than any reason to stay. That was the whole ledger. That was the fragile, ridiculous sum of it: six people with nothing left to lose, which meant, for exactly as long as it held true, six people who couldn't be bought.

"We leave tomorrow night," Kestrel said. "Before the sweep. Halloway's men will be watching the river gates, so we go through the tannery district, up the goat paths—"

"The goat paths flood," Wren said.

"The goat paths flood in spring. It's autumn."

"The goat paths flood when Ash says they flood." She nodded at the tracker. "He's been reading this town's water longer than you've been reading its mountains, Emrys-or-whatever-your-name-is. We do this right or we don't do it."

For a moment she thought he'd bristle. She'd seen him bristle at the client on the river trail, a small flinch of the man he used to be. But he just looked at Ash, and Ash looked back, and something passed between them — the first coin of trust spent between the two people in the room who understood the mountains best and trusted everyone least.

Ash tapped the bench twice. *Agreed.*

"Tomorrow night, then," Kestrel said. "Through the tanneries, up the flood paths, and north."

"North," Odell murmured, almost to himself, unrolling one of his forbidden charts by candlelight — and there, faint beneath the redaction ink that had never quite soaked through, Wren could see the ghost of roads, the ghost of a valley, the ghost of a capital with a name. "I haven't drawn north in twenty-two years."

Sable closed the compass. The click of it was very loud in the dead foundry.

And it was Ivo — of all people, the drunk, the defrocked, the doubter — who said the thing that put the cold in Wren's spine and kept it there.

"You know they'll come after us," the priest said quietly. "Not soldiers first. Him. The one who knows you." He was looking at Kestrel. "They always send the one who knows you."

Kestrel didn't answer. But Wren saw his hand close, once, around nothing — around the shape of a name he hadn't said aloud in twenty years — and she thought: *well. There's the last item on the ledger. The one we haven't counted yet.*

Outside, the river rose in the dark, carrying the season's last flood down toward the sea, and somewhere along its banks, she had no doubt, a man in a coalition coat was asking the water where they'd gone.
