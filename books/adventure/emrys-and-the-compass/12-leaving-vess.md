## 12. Leaving Vess

The rain came at dusk, and with it the smell of coal smoke and wet stone that meant Vess was closing its shutters for the night. Kestrel stood in the doorway of Wren's cousin's boathouse and watched the lamplighter work his way down Rope Street, flame by flame, a man lighting the way for people who would all be asleep within the hour. It struck him, as it sometimes did, how much of his life had been spent watching towns from doorways — learning their rhythms so he could predict when they'd be emptyest.

Tonight he needed Vess empty by midnight.

"Stop brooding," Sable said behind him. She was wrapping her hands in strips of linen, foundry habit, though there was no forge here and no work for those hands tonight except holding steady. "You look like a man rehearsing his own funeral."

"I'm counting exits."

"You've been counting exits since noon."

"And I'll count them again at midnight," Kestrel said, "because the ones we have at noon are not always the ones we have at midnight." He turned back to the room. The boathouse smelled of tar and old rope, and it was crowded now with everything they intended to carry out of their old lives: the chest wrapped in oilcloth, Odell's map tubes, Father Ivo's single bag of books, the tracker Corren sitting cross-legged in the corner sharpening a knife with the patience of a man who had never once needed to hurry. Wren was on the floor with three of her smuggler's maps spread out around her like a card game she was losing.

She looked up. "Bad news."

"There's a surprise," Sable said.

"The ferry road's watched." Wren tapped a spot on the westernmost sheet. "Two men, maybe three, at the tollhouse. Not soldiers — soldiers stand straight. These are Halloway's kind. Debt-collector posture. Shoulders forward, like they're leaning into a wind nobody else can feel."

Kestrel crouched beside her. "Halloway himself?"

"Haven't seen him. Which worries me more than if I had. Halloway doesn't send men to watch a road unless he already knows which way you're going to run." She looked up at him, and in the lamplight her face was stripped of its usual market-day mischief. "So either you're predictable, guide, or somebody told him."

Nobody had told him. Kestrel was almost sure of that. Almost. He kept the doubt where he kept all his doubts, in the same pocket as the compass, and said nothing.

* * *

They ate a cold supper of bread and hard cheese and smoked fish Wren had bought from a woman who sold rainwater by the jug and asked no questions about anything. Father Ivo said grace over it, quietly, the way he did everything — as if volume might attract God's attention and God, like everyone else in Vess this week, was better off not involved.

"We leave at the change of watch," Kestrel said, when the food was gone. "Midnight, when the harbor patrol swaps crews. There's a gap of perhaps ten minutes when the quay is unwatched from the water side."

"Ten minutes," Odell said. He was the oldest of them, a cartographer with ink-stained fingers and a tremor in his left hand he tried to hide by keeping it in his pocket. "Is that enough?"

"It's enough if nothing goes wrong."

"Nothing ever goes wrong," Corren signed, with both hands, and then smiled for the first time all day. Even Sable laughed, briefly, and the laugh did something to the room — loosened it, made the fear bearable for a moment. Kestrel let them have the moment. They would need whatever laughter they could bank before morning.

Wren rolled up her maps. "There's another problem, and it's bigger than the quay."

"Tell me."

"The sweep." She said it flatly, the way you'd say the name of a storm. "My cousin heard it from a harbormaster's clerk who drinks too much and talks too much. Coalition riders come through tomorrow. Not a patrol — a sweep. Door to door. They're looking for river salvage. Anything pulled from the flood. Anyone who handled it." She looked at Kestrel. "That's us. That's exactly us. Somebody upstream has reported the chest, or somebody downstream has guessed at it, and tomorrow every house in Vess gets opened and every name gets checked against lists."

Lists. Kestrel felt the word settle on him like cold water. Somewhere in a coalition office there was a list with a name on it that hadn't been spoken aloud in six years, a name he'd buried under Emrys the way you bury a body — deep, and with stones on top. If a clerk compared faces against that list, borrowed names wouldn't matter. Faces were harder to forge than signatures.

"Then we don't just need to leave," Sable said slowly. "We need to be gone before dawn. Before anyone knows we were ever here in numbers."

"Yes," Kestrel said. "And we need to leave in a direction Halloway's men aren't watching."

"That's the part," Wren said, "where you stop being a mountain guide and start trusting a smuggler."

* * *

She laid it out for them on the floor of the boathouse, using buttons and a cork and a bent nail for markers. The ferry road west: watched. The north gate toward the high pastures: watched too, because it was the obvious route for anyone heading into the mountains, and Halloway was many things but never stupid about the obvious. The south road along the river: open, but it led down into coalition territory, toward the garrison town of Marrow Ford, and every mile south was a mile deeper into the machinery that wanted them.

"So we don't take any of them," Wren said.

"There's a fourth road?" Odell asked.

"There's no road at all." She moved the cork — herself, them — to the edge of the map, past the last drawn line, into the blank space where Odell's careful cartography gave up. "The tannery district floods every spring. Everybody knows it, so nobody builds there anymore except squatters, and the squatters are gone since the flood took two of them last month. Underneath the tannery district there's an old culvert. Brick. Pre-coalition, my grandfather helped build half the smuggling runs in this town and he drew me this one before he died. It comes out above the weir, on the river's north bank, past the tollhouse, past the gates, past everything. From there it's an hour of goat track to the old drover's path, and after that—" she looked at Kestrel— "after that it's your country."

"A flooded culvert," Father Ivo said. "In the dark. Carrying a chest full of paper that cannot get wet."

"Paper that survived a river," Sable said, "can survive a tunnel."

"The culvert isn't flooded," Wren said. "Not all of it. The flood crested four days ago. Water's dropped below the arch crown. Chest-high in the worst stretch, knee-high most of the rest, and there's a ledge along the north wall for most of it if you know where to feel for it." She met Kestrel's eyes. "You do know how to read a tunnel in the dark, yes? Or do mountain men only go up?"

"I've been in mines," Kestrel said. "Mines flood too."

"Good. Because I'm not going to lie to you — I've only run that culvert twice, both times dry, both times daylight. Tonight it's wet and black and if the water's come back up since my cousin checked yesterday, we swim or we turn around and get arrested. Those are the options."

No one spoke for a moment. Then Corren raised one hand, caught their attention, and signed something short and decisive. Wren translated, though Kestrel was beginning to follow the tracker's hands himself: *I have drowned twice already. A third time is arithmetic.*

"He says he's in," Wren said.

"He says more than that," Kestrel said, and Corren's mouth twitched.

* * *

They spent the hours before midnight preparing, and the preparation had the quality of ritual — each person doing small necessary things in silence, the way sailors ready a ship. Odell sealed his maps in waxed canvas. Father Ivo wrapped his books in his spare cassock, which struck Kestrel as either pragmatism or theology and probably both. Sable broke down her few tools and distributed the weight among packs that could carry it. Kestrel checked and rechecked the rope — sixty feet of it, coiled, tested at every hand-width for rot — and then sat down across from Wren while she dressed the blister on her palm.

"Why are you doing this?" he asked.

She didn't look up. "Because you're paying me."

"Wren."

"Because—" She tied off the bandage with her teeth, and when she spoke again her voice had lost its street polish. "Because I've lived in Vess my whole life, and Vess is a town that lives by pretending not to see things. Smugglers see everything and report nothing; that's the trade. And I've gotten good at it. Good at not seeing. And then you come along asking questions about a dead kingdom with a dead man's compass, and for the first time in years I actually *saw* something, and now I can't unsee it, and I hate you a little for that." She finally looked up. "Also you're paying me. Both things are true. Most true things come in pairs."

Kestrel thought about telling her the truth — that he wasn't paying her nearly what she was worth, that what she was worth couldn't be paid in coin — and decided she'd only make a joke of it. Some gratitude was better carried than spoken.

He went to the doorway instead and looked out at the town one more time. Rain still falling, fine and steady. Lamplight bleeding yellow onto wet cobbles. Somewhere a dog barking at nothing. Vess had been good to him, in its indifferent way. It had asked no questions, honored no histories, remembered no names. For six years it had been the shape of his safety: small, damp, forgettable. Tomorrow the sweep would come through it like a comb through hair, and whether or not it found anything, it would never be quite the same town to him again. You only got one last safe place, he thought. After that, every place was just terrain.

Behind him, Sable appeared at his shoulder. She didn't say anything for a while. Then:

"My grandmother used to say that leaving is a skill. That refugees are the only people who get good at it, and it's a terrible thing to be good at."

"She was right."

"She was right about everything," Sable said, "and it got her killed anyway. So." She shrugged. "Let's be terrible at it together."

* * *

At half past eleven they doused the lamp and waited in the dark, listening to Vess breathe. At quarter to twelve, boots on the quay — the watch changing, voices trading insults about the rain, laughter, the clank of the patrol boat's rail. Then the gap, exactly as promised, ten minutes of quiet with rain filling it like water fills a footprint.

They moved.

Wren first, hood up, leading them through alleys she navigated without a lantern, reading the town by touch and memory — a hand on a wall here, a pause at a corner there, once a full stop in a doorway while a drunk wove past singing something mournful. Kestrel came second with the chest strapped to his back, feeling its weight shift with each step, the journals inside shifting like sleeping animals. Behind him Sable, then Odell with Corren steadying his elbow, then Father Ivo murmuring something that was either a psalm or a list of everything that could go wrong.

The tannery district was ruins. The flood had done its work thoroughly — doors swollen off frames, a wall collapsed into a heap of bricks furred with gray silt, the whole district smelling of rot and abandoned industry. Wren picked her way through it to a low brick arch half-buried in a slope of mud and debris, barely taller than a man, choked with weeds.

"Culvert," she whispered. "Welcome to the fourth road."

She lit a single candle stub, cupped it, and held it into the arch. The light reached maybe ten feet and then gave up. Beyond it: blackness, and the sound of moving water, low and patient.

"Rules," Wren breathed. "One hand on the north wall at all times — that's your left, going in. The ledge is there when the water's deep; you'll feel it drop away under your feet and that's when you climb. Nobody speaks. If the candle dies, we freeze and relight. And if anybody hears the water rising—" she paused— "run. Don't be brave. Bravery is for places with exits."

Kestrel looked back once, down the ruined lane, at the last smudge of lamplight from the living town. Then he followed her into the dark.

The culvert was worse than she'd described and better than he'd feared. The water ran thigh-deep and cold enough to make his breath hitch, and the ceiling pressed down until he was walking bent, the chest scraping occasionally against brick. His left hand read the wall like a page: mortar, mortar, crack, mortar, then the sudden absence of floor — the ledge, exactly where she'd said, and he hauled himself up onto it and passed the warning back hand to hand until all six of them were strung along the narrow shelf like beads on a wire, inching through the dark above the black water.

Twice the candle guttered and died, and twice they froze in absolute darkness, breathing, listening to the tunnel drip, until Wren's flint coaxed the flame back. In those pauses Kestrel became aware of the others as pure sound: Odell's controlled breathing, too deliberate, an old man managing fear; Ivo's lips moving in silence; Sable's complete stillness, as if darkness were a country she'd been born in. Once, far behind them, there was a sound like a boot in shallow water, and they froze for a long five minutes before deciding it was the tunnel talking to itself.

They emerged, at last, through a grate of rotten iron bars into a night full of rain and river-smell, above the weir, on the north bank. Below them Vess lay scattered with lights, small and warm and finished with them. Kestrel set the chest down and stood a moment, water running out of his boots, looking back at the town that had sheltered his borrowed life.

"Emrys dies here," he said quietly. Nobody asked what he meant. Maybe they all understood: the name had been a mooring, and they had just cast off.

Wren was already studying the slope ahead. "Drover's path is up through the pines. Two hours' climbing and we're clear of the valley. By dawn we'll be somewhere no sweep reaches." She glanced at him. "By dawn we're ghosts."

They climbed. The rain thinned as they gained the tree line, and the pines closed overhead, dripping, dark, smelling of resin and cold. It was nearly the top of the first rise when Sable, walking beside him, suddenly stopped and caught his arm.

"Look," she breathed.

Below, back along the dark line of the river road, a lantern was moving. Not the harbor watch's rhythm — this light traveled fast and purposeful, swinging, searching, and behind it came a second light and a third, strung out along the bank like beads of fire. Heading for the tannery district. Heading for the culvert they'd crawled out of less than an hour ago.

One street behind. One hour behind. Close enough that Kestrel could imagine Halloway's voice carrying up through the rain, though no voice came — just the lights, patient as tide, sweeping the last safe place clean of them.

"They'll find the tunnel," Odell whispered.

"They'll find it empty," Kestrel said. "Move."

They moved, up into the pines, away from the lights, into mountains that did not care whose names they carried. And behind them, far below, the lanterns reached the broken grate and stopped, and one of them — Kestrel was sure of it, though he could not have said how — turned and looked up into the dark hills where six ghosts climbed toward a truth that had already begun to cost more than any of them had promised themselves it would.
