## 13. The Low Pass

The mountains did not care that we were running from something. That was the first thing I told them, standing at the treeline with the dawn still gray behind us and the Low Pass opening ahead like a wound in the world's skin.

"The mountain doesn't know your reasons," I said. "It doesn't know you're innocent or guilty, rich or poor, heir to anything. It only knows weight and weather and where you put your feet. Remember that, and it will let you pass. Forget it, and none of what we're carrying will matter."

Wren snorted. She had her pack cinched too tight and her chin up too high, the way people do when they're scared and want the fear filed under confidence. "Inspiring speech, Emrys. Very funeral."

"That's the idea," I said. "Funerals are honest."

Sable didn't laugh. She stood a little apart, looking up at the pass the way you'd look at a stranger who shared your face — wary, hungry, unwilling to admit either. The compass hung at her neck now, on its cord, and every few minutes her hand drifted to it without her seeming to notice. The needle held steady through all of it, pointing northeast into the gray teeth of the range, toward whatever waited in the erased country.

Brack said nothing, because Brack never said anything. He was already twenty paces ahead, reading the ground, his head tilted in that listening way of his. He'd been mute as long as anyone in Vess could remember, and he moved through terrain the way water moves through cracks — no wasted motion, no argument. When he paused and touched two fingers to the rock, then pointed east, I adjusted our line without asking why. Later I understood he'd heard meltwater moving under the scree, a hollow throat waiting to swallow someone. That was the first day. There would be others.

Father Ivo came last. Defrocked priests walk like men carrying furniture they can't put down, and Ivo was no exception. He was broad once, soft now, sixty if he was fifty-five, with ink-stained fingers and a satchel of books he refused to leave behind even when I told him the weight would kill him on the glacier.

"Then it will kill me doing something," he'd said, and buckled the satchel anyway.

We climbed.

* * *

The first day was kind, which I distrusted immediately. Kindness in mountains is usually a down payment.

The trail followed an old drover's road cut into the southern flank, switchbacking through pine and then larch and then the bare gold grass above the trees. Wren turned out to be stronger than she looked — smugglers carry other people's cargo for a living, and her legs had the low, patient power of someone who'd hauled casks up Vess's dock stairs since childhood. She complained constantly and kept pace perfectly, which I learned to read as her version of contentment.

Odell struggled. The cartographer was all wrists and eyes, a man built for desks, and by midday his breathing had gone ragged and his maps — wrapped in oilcloth against his back like a second spine — seemed to be dragging him backward down the slope. But he didn't ask to stop. Every time the trail bent and gave us a view north, he stopped anyway, not for breath but to look, pulling out his notebook and sketching ridgelines with hands that shook from effort rather than cold.

"You're mapping as we go," I said, dropping back beside him.

"Correcting," he said. "Every map I ever drew of this range is a lie by omission. I'm... annotating." His mouth twisted around the word. "You don't erase a country with one stroke, Mr. Emrys. You do it with ten thousand small corrections. I made most of them myself."

I didn't answer that. There wasn't an answer that helped either of us.

Sable climbed like she'd been born to it, which unsettled me more than I let show. Callowmere had been a mountain nation; the journals were full of terraced valleys and high-pasture summers, and something in her body remembered what her mind had never been taught. She found handholds I wouldn't have bothered pointing out. She read the wind on the rock faces before I did. Twice she paused, frowning at a bend in the trail, and said, "There should be a cairn here," and twice, when we cleared the bend, there was a cairn — toppled, moss-eaten, older than she was.

"Grandmother's stories," she said the second time, catching me watching her. Her voice was flat, defensive. "She talked about stone men marking the ways. I thought they were just stories."

"Most stories are maps somebody folded small enough to carry," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment, and I saw her decide not to ask what I meant, because she already knew, because everyone in this company was a folded map of somewhere they couldn't go home to.

We made camp that night in a hollow below the last trees, out of the wind, with a fire small enough to hide. Brack took first watch without being asked. Wren cooked — she'd brought spices, actual spices, smuggled in wax paper, and the smell of cumin in the thin cold air nearly made Odell weep. Sable sat with the compass open on her knee, watching the needle hold its stubborn line while the stars wheeled the other way.

And Father Ivo sat beside me at the edge of the firelight, and asked me the question I'd been waiting three days for him to ask.

* * *

"You believe this will help them?" he said quietly, so the words wouldn't travel past us. He nodded toward Sable, toward Wren. "The truth. Carried out of the mountains and laid before the world. You believe it heals?"

I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "I believe it's true."

"Yes. But that's not my question." He had a way of speaking that was gentle and relentless at once, like snowfall filling a valley. "I spent thirty years administering sacraments to people who had been lied to by their government, their landlords, their church — by me, sometimes, though God forgive me, I didn't always know it. And I learned something about truth, Emrys. Truth is not medicine. It doesn't work its cure simply by being administered. Truth is a blade. Whether it heals depends entirely on the hand that holds it and the wound it's opened."

"The coalition opened the wound," I said. "Twenty years ago. We'd just be naming it."

"We'd be *cutting it again.*" He leaned forward, firelight moving in the deep lines of his face. "Think about what we carry. Journals proving a famine was engineered. A girl who is the last living claim to a murdered throne. A map restoring a nation every chancellery in the borderlands has spent two decades pretending never existed. What happens when that reaches the lowlands? Do you imagine the coalition confesses? They will burn villages before they burn archives, my son. And the people in those villages will be people like these." He nodded at the sleeping shapes around the fire. "The dead of Callowmere cannot be killed again. The living can."

I let the silence sit between us for a while. Below us, far off, the river we'd pulled the chest from went on talking to itself in the dark.

"I buried four climbers," I said finally. "Eight years ago. On a route I'd mapped myself, led myself. The official record says my judgment failed. The record is wrong — but it took me years to learn *how* wrong, and those years ate me alive. Not knowing was worse than guilt. Guilt at least has a shape. Doubt has no edges. It gets into everything." I looked at him. "These people have lived twenty years inside a lie with no edges. Sable has lived her whole life inside it. If the truth costs blood — and it may, I'm not fool enough to promise otherwise — at least the bleeding will be in the open, where it can be stanched. Secrets bleed inward. Forever."

Ivo studied me with pale, tired eyes. "That's a guide's answer. Weight and weather and where you put your feet."

"It's the only kind of answer I trust."

"And yet." He smiled, and it was a sad, warm thing. "You didn't answer my question either. Neither of us believes truth heals. We believe it's owed. That's different. That's debt, not medicine." He drew a breath, and the firelight caught the old tonsure shadow on his scalp, grown over but never quite gone. "I'll carry it anyway. I only wanted you to know that I see what we're actually holding. Not a cure. A bill come due. And bills, Emrys, always name a payer."

He rose, groaned at his knees, and went to pray by the rocks — I could tell by the set of his shoulders, the slight bow of his head, the way his lips moved without sound. Doubt walked with him even there. I could see it in the way he prayed: not like a man certain of being heard, but like a man determined to keep speaking regardless.

I took second watch, and sat with the doubt he'd handed me, turning it over. He wasn't wrong. That was the trouble. Every mile north, the journals in my pack got heavier, and not from damp. Somewhere behind us, Reiss was climbing too — I felt it the way you feel weather coming in a bad knee. And somewhere ahead, the needle pointed at a valley full of people who had survived by being invisible, and we were walking toward them carrying everything that could make them visible forever.

Doubt came along. I let it. A good guide knows better than to pretend the mountain is empty of wolves just because he hasn't seen one yet.

* * *

The third day, the Low Pass tested us properly.

It began with weather — a front sliding off the high peaks at midmorning, turning the sky the color of dishwater, putting a fine cold rain into everything. Then the trail ended. Not faded, not washed out: *ended*, at a slide where half the slope had let go sometime in the last decade and dumped itself across the route in a fan of shattered rock two hundred feet high. Old maps showed the path continuing serenely through. The mountain disagreed.

"Detour?" Wren asked, rain streaming off her hood.

"There's no detour through the Low Pass. That's why it's low. Everything else is a wall until the high routes, and the high routes are glaciers, and the glaciers are eight days further and worse than this." I looked at the slide, reading it the way Odell read maps. Two hundred feet of loose talon over a core of bigger blocks. Crossable, in dry conditions, with light packs and luck. We had none of the three.

"We go over," I said. "Slow. Spaced out. Nobody moves until the person ahead is clear. Brack first, then me, then Sable, then Wren, then Odell, then Father Ivo."

"Why am I second-to-last?" Odell asked.

"Because you're carrying the maps and I want the man behind you young enough to catch you."

The crossing took four hours and cost us ten years each. The rock shifted underfoot like live things, plates grinding, and twice the whole fan shivered with a deep internal settling that stopped every heart on the slope at once. Brack found the line — of course he did, threading us along a seam of bedrock where the footing held — and I called the pace, and Sable moved through it like she was walking home, and Wren swore steadily and creatively in three languages, and Odell froze once, halfway, arms out, breath sawing, until I talked him through it one step at a time in the voice I used to use with terrified clients, calm and boring as bread. Ivo crossed last with his satchel hugged to his chest like a child, lips moving the whole way. Prayer or cursing. With Ivo it was often both.

At the top, on the far side, the rain stopped as if a tap had been closed, and the clouds tore open along the northern horizon, and we saw it.

The erased country.

It shouldn't have been visible. It was days away, beyond intervening ridges. But the air had been scrubbed clean by the storm, and there it lay: a long blue valley system running up into white peaks, folded and deep, with a thread of silver water glinting in its floor. No smoke. No roads. Nothing that would read as human from this distance — and yet I felt it, the way you feel a room is occupied before you see anyone. Something lived down there. Something had kept living.

Nobody spoke. Odell had his notebook out and was drawing with rain-wet fingers, fast, as if the view might be confiscated. Sable had both hands pressed flat over the compass at her sternum, and her eyes were shining, and she was crying without any of the noise or shame of crying, just water running down her face in the wind.

"That's it," she said. "That's the valley. From the dreams."

The needle, when I glanced at the compass hanging against her chest, trembled once — and steadied, straight as a struck nail, pointing down into that blue distance.

It was Father Ivo who broke the silence, and he surprised me. I expected more doubt. Instead he said, softly, "Lord, I have carried this question up the mountain and You have answered it with geography," and laughed at himself, a short wet laugh, and wiped his eyes with a sleeve already soaked through.

"What question?" Wren asked.

"Whether reopening the grave was worth it." He looked north a long moment. "I haven't changed my mind that it's dangerous. But look at it. Twenty years, and it's still *there*. Somebody kept it. Kept the terraces, kept the paths, kept—" he gestured at Sable, at the compass, at the whole improbable chain that had dragged six broken people over a mountain "—kept *this*. A grave stays shut because everyone agrees to stand on the lid. Someone down there never agreed."

Below us, at the toe of the slide, movement caught my eye — small, distant, wrong. I raised the battered spyglass I'd bought in Vess and focused on the scree field we'd just crossed.

Riders. Four of them, maybe five, working along the base of the slope where our trail from the south came up out of the trees. Dark coats. The disciplined spacing of men who'd done this together before. Even at this distance, one detail lifted my stomach into my throat: they weren't searching. They weren't casting about the way trackers do when they've lost the scent.

They were riding straight to the slide, dismounting, and beginning to climb. Our line. Our exact line, as if drawn on a map.

As if someone had given it to them.

I lowered the glass. Wren was watching my face, and whatever she saw there made her stop smiling.

"How much lead time do we have?" she asked.

I looked north, at the blue valley and the white peaks and the needle pulling at Sable's neck like a hooked fish, and did the arithmetic I'd hoped not to do for another week.

"Less than I'd like," I said. "Break camp. We move now, and we don't stop until dark — and after dark, we keep moving."
