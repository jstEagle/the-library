## 14. Reiss Ascending

The rain had followed them out of Vess like a debt of its own, and Davor Reiss let it fall on him without opening his oilskin hood. Behind him, six coalition troopers slogged up the river trail in gray silence, their boots sucking at the mud, their lanterns shuttered to coins of dim gold. Ahead, somewhere in the wet dark, four people were climbing into the mountains with a dead kingdom in a pack.

Reiss counted the lights of Vess shrinking below and thought: *I used to know these trails better than he did.*

Not anymore. Not for eleven years.

"Agent." Sergeant Vell drew alongside, rain streaming off the brim of her helmet. "Halloway's man says they took the low route past the tannery. Cart tracks washed out after that. We'll lose them by morning if we don't push."

"We won't lose them," Reiss said. "Kestrel Amberdale doesn't get lost."

Vell's face did something complicated — surprise, then the quick recalculation of a soldier who has just heard her superior use a blacklisted name out loud. "I don't know that name, sir."

"No," Reiss said. "You wouldn't."

He put his hood up after all, not against the rain but against whatever else might have shown on his face, and waved the column onward.

* * *

They made camp at midnight under an overhang of granite above the flood-scoured shallows, and Reiss sat apart from the troopers' small fire, turning a coal of memory over in his mind the way he'd once turned river stones as a boy.

Eleven years ago he had stood in a hearing room in Corvane with his right hand raised and told nine men in gray uniforms exactly what they had asked him to hear. *The guide abandoned the roped party when the weather turned. I saw him cut loose above the icefall.* He had said it in a steady voice. He had signed his name beneath it. And Kestrel Amberdale — who had carried Reiss himself, half-frozen and raving, down three thousand feet of the Greymantle icefield after the serac collapse — had sat in that room and never once looked at him with hatred. That was the part Reiss could not make peace with. Hatred would have been a payment. What Kestrel had given him instead was worse: a long, level look, the look of a man confirming something he'd already suspected about the world, and then silence.

Four climbers dead. One guide erased. One witness promoted.

Reiss had told himself the story the coalition preferred, the one printed in the finding: that the disaster was tragedy compounded by cowardice, that justice had been served, that the borderlands were safer with unstable elements removed from the mountains. He had repeated it so many times it had worn smooth as a pebble, and he had carried the pebble in his mouth for eleven years until he could almost speak around it.

Then Halloway's report had come in — *river salvage, sealed chest, Callowmere crest* — and the pebble had cracked straight down the middle.

Because Reiss knew what Callowmere meant. Not the schoolbook version, not the *failed state, economic collapse, regrettable famine* version taught to children in the coalition territories. He knew what the archives annexation order had actually said, the document he'd been required to read and initial as a junior officer twenty years ago, the one with the phrase *comprehensive administrative resolution* where any honest language would have written *make sure no one is left to contradict us.*

He had initialed it. He had been twenty-three years old and ambitious and he had initialed it.

"Sir?" Vell again, crouching at the edge of the firelight. "Permission to speak freely?"

"You will anyway."

"The men are asking what this is. Four fugitives and a chest. That's constabulary work, not a squad of six and an agent of the Interior Section." She paused. "And they're asking why we're following a smuggler's route instead of the main pass. You climbed that ridge yourself this afternoon to read the ground. You didn't send anyone."

Reiss watched the fire. "I sent you to ask questions instead," he said. "That was a mistake on my part. Go tell them it's a salvage recovery with political attachments, and that the political attachments are why they're being paid triple hazard."

"And the truth?"

"The truth is above your clearance, Sergeant, which is a mercy. Go."

She went. Reiss stayed by the fire a while longer, and if a man sitting alone in the rain could be said to pray, he prayed the way he always prayed: not to anything, just a list of names, spoken inwardly, in order of how badly he had failed them. Halric Voss. The twins, Anselm and Petra. Young Corwin Ashe, who had been nineteen. And last, always last, because last was its own kind of sentence: Kestrel Amberdale, alive, somewhere above him in the dark, climbing toward a truth Reiss had spent two decades helping to bury.

*Enviable*, he thought, and meant it, and hated himself for meaning it. The hunted slept badly, but they slept as themselves. Every step Kestrel took now was a step in his own direction. Reiss's steps belonged to the Interior Section, had belonged to it for twenty years, and there was no trail off that mountain because he had helped survey every side of it.

* * *

On the third day they found the campsite at Marrow Ford — or rather, they found what the river had left of it. Cold ash. A tent-peg hole. Boot prints in the clay going north, four sets, light and fast, and a fifth set that puzzled Vell until Reiss crouched and studied it.

"Horse, then walked," he murmured. "Someone joined them."

"A recruit?"

"Or a hostage. Or a friend." He rose, knees cracking, forty-one years old and feeling every one of them in the wet. "It doesn't matter. They're making for the Saddle of Veils. There's only one reason anyone goes to the Saddle of Veils."

"Which is?"

Reiss looked north, where the peaks stood up white against a bruised sky, range behind range behind range, and somewhere beyond them a valley that appeared on no map because he had personally delivered the order to remove it from every map.

"Home," he said.

That night, in his tent, he took out the thing he should have burned years ago and had never been able to: a page torn from the annexation file, the only copy he'd dared keep, listing the names of the Callowmere archivists who had been relocated during the *resolution*. Relocated. He'd been young enough then to believe the word. He was not young enough now. Below the list, in faded ink, someone had written a routing note: *surviving materials to be consolidated at the capital site pending final disposition.*

Pending final disposition. Twenty years, and the disposition was still pending, because the archive had never been found, because the valley had swallowed it, because somewhere in those mountains the last proof of what the coalition had done sat waiting like a loaded weapon.

And Kestrel Amberdale was walking straight toward it with a compass that pointed at it, if Halloway's informant inside the expedition could be believed — and Reiss no longer fully believed Halloway, which was its own quiet alarm bell ringing somewhere below his ribs.

He fed the page to the stove and watched it curl. It changed nothing. He knew every word by heart.

* * *

The storm caught them on the fourth day, a white wall rolling off the high peaks, and Reiss made the call to shelter rather than press — the safe call, the correct call, the call Kestrel would not have made, because Kestrel would have known these clouds by their smell and pushed through ahead of them. That was the difference between them, Reiss thought, hunched in a shepherd's hut with the wind screaming across the door gap. Kestrel read the mountains like scripture. Reiss read them like terrain to be controlled, and the mountains punished control.

In the hut's dimness, Vell sat sharpening her knife and watching him with those too-clear eyes of hers.

"You knew him," she said. Not a question this time. "Amberdale. Before."

Reiss considered lying. He was professionally excellent at lying; it was the trade he'd chosen over the one he'd loved. But the wind was howling and the truth had been rattling in his chest for days like a coin in a tin box, and he was tired, so tired, of the weight of it.

"He was my best friend," Reiss said. "We came up through the guides' guild together. He taught me to read a snowpack. I taught him to play cards, badly. When the Greymantle expedition went down, the coalition needed the disaster explained in a way that protected the survey contracts, and I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I gave them the explanation they needed. About him."

Vell's knife stopped moving. "You testified falsely."

"I testified *usefully*. There's a promotion in the difference." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry broken sound. "Four people died on that ice, Sergeant. The official finding blamed the guide. The actual cause was a load calculation error in the fixed-line placement — ordered by the survey office, against the guide's written objection. His objection is still in the file. I've read it a hundred times. He wrote that the anchor placements would fail under load. They failed under load. And I stood in that room and said he'd cut the rope."

The knife lay still in Vell's hands. Outside, the storm threw itself against the mountain as if trying to get in and end this conversation by force.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because in three days we're going to catch four exhausted people in the high country, and I'm going to be ordered to do things to them, and I need one person in this squad who knows what I am." He met her eyes. "So that when it happens, somebody remembers."

Vell looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw, she filed it away behind the flat competence of her expression, and returned to her knife. But before she turned away she said, quietly: "For what it's worth, sir — the men already think this mission is strange. Triple hazard for a chest recovery. They follow you because you've never lied to them about the mountains."

*Only about everything else,* Reiss thought.

He slept that night in full kit, boots on, the way he had slept on the Greymantle icefield eleven years ago, and dreamed of the sound a rope makes when it comes tight, and woke before dawn with the storm dying and the sky rinsed clean, and knew — the way he had once known weather, back when he'd been a mountaineer instead of an instrument — that the clear air would hold maybe two days.

Two days to close the distance. Two days to climb toward the Saddle of Veils and the four small figures that Halloway's last message placed a half-day ahead, moving slow, burdened, human.

He broke camp and pointed his squad upward, into the clean cold light, hunter and envier and penitent all in one gray coat, and behind him, far to the south, a pigeon beat its way out of Vess carrying a message he would not learn the contents of for two more days — a message that would change the shape of everything, though the mountains, indifferent as ever, kept that to themselves.
