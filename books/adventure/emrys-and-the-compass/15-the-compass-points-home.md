## 15. The Compass Points Home

The dream had always started the same way: a valley folded between two shoulders of mountain, green where everything else was stone, and a river running through it so clear you could count the coins thrown from the bridge. In the dream, Sable was always descending into it. Never arriving. Just descending — one step, then another, down a switchback trail cut into rock that hummed under her feet like a struck bell.

She'd had it since she was six years old, back when she still lived above the dye-works in Marrowgate with Gran and the smell of mordant and boiled wool. She'd wake up crying without knowing why, and Gran would sit on the edge of her cot in the dark and say, *It's only weather, little bird. Weather passes through.* But it wasn't weather. It was homesickness for a place she'd never seen, grief with no corpse to attach itself to, and by the time she was old enough to name the feeling she'd learned to keep it to herself, because in the borderlands you did not tell people you dreamed of Callowmere. You did not say that word at all.

Now she sat on a cold ledge three days into the mountains, holding a brass compass that pointed at her dream.

* * *

They'd made camp at dusk in the lee of a granite spine, a shallow scoop of stone that Odell pronounced "geologically unremarkable" and Father Ivo pronounced "a mercy." Wren had gotten a fire going small enough to hide in a cupped hand, and the mute tracker — Ash, though no one knew if that was a name or a description — had vanished upslope on watch as silently as smoke going out a window.

Sable hadn't wanted to stop. That was the strange part. Her legs ached from the day's climb, her boots had rubbed blisters she could feel with every heartbeat, and still some engine in her chest kept saying *further, further*, like a hand pulling at her sleeve. She'd caught Kestrel watching her when she said so, wearing an expression she couldn't read — not doubt exactly, but the careful face of a man measuring something against a memory.

It was after the fire burned down to coals that he took the compass out.

He did it the way he seemed to do everything now: quietly, deliberately, as if the mountains themselves might be listening. He'd been carrying it wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat since Vess, and Sable had begun to think of it as his heart — the thing he guarded, the thing all of this rotated around. He unwrapped it in the coal-light and held it flat on his palm.

"Look," he said.

She looked. The needle trembled, swung left, steadied. It didn't point north. She'd known that since the foundry, since the night Kestrel and Wren had turned up smelling of river water and trouble and asked her questions no sane person should answer. North was behind them now, somewhere over their left shoulders, and the needle ignored it completely. It pointed ahead and slightly east, into the massed black wall of the range, unwavering, patient, like a dog waiting at a door.

"It's done that all day," Kestrel said. "Since we crossed the scree field this morning. Before that it wandered. A degree here, a degree there. Today it locked."

"Locked onto what?"

He turned the compass slowly, and the needle stayed true to its bearing regardless of how the case moved. "Something fixed," he said. "Or someone who isn't moving fast."

The words landed in her stomach like swallowed stones. She thought of the journals stacked in Wren's pack, the ones they'd read by candle stub in the foundry's back room — the expedition logs, the supply manifests, the letters written in a careful clerk's hand about *the child* and *the line* and *where the blood must be kept*. She thought of the photograph: a girl of four or five with dark hair and a pale crescent birthmark below her left ear, standing on steps that led up to a building with columns. She thought of the mark under her own ear, which she'd spent her whole life explaining away — a stain, a burn scar, nothing, Gran saying *it runs in our family, don't fuss.*

"You know what it points at," she said. Her voice came out flatter than she intended.

"I have a guess."

"Say it anyway."

Kestrel looked at her across the coals. Firelight moved in his eyes, and behind it was something older and wearier, the look of a man who had already lost arguments with himself and was now just managing the terms of surrender.

"The journals talk about a sanctuary," he said. "A place the loyalists prepared in case the worst happened. Somewhere off every map, stocked and provisioned, meant to hold whoever survived. The compass was made to find it — or to find the person who'd be brought there. The last living heir of Callowmere." He paused. "And the needle hasn't wavered since we started climbing toward the high valleys. It's pointing at something in these mountains, Sable. And you've known where we were going before I did. Every fork in the trail, you've picked the right one like you were reading signs nobody else can see."

"That's just — I grew up in hill country. Marrowgate sits at the edge of the ranges. Anyone could—"

"You chose the ford at the split oak when the map said the low road," he said gently. "You told us the low road floods. It does. There's no way you'd know that unless—" He stopped himself. He was good at stopping himself; she'd noticed that early. A man practicing invisibility gets very good at not finishing sentences.

"Unless what?" she said, though she knew. She'd known since the photograph. Since the first night, really, when the compass had twitched in its case as she leaned over it, and Kestrel had gone very still.

"Unless part of you has been here before," he said. "Or was meant to be."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat up against the granite with her knees drawn up and watched the stars wheel, and the dream came whether she wanted it or not — but this time it came differently. This time it came with sound.

In the old dream there had never been voices. Just the valley, the river, the humming rock, her own descending feet. Now, hovering at the edge of sleep, she heard bells. Not the foundry bells of Marrowgate, which rang shifts and alarms, flat and functional. These were older, rounder notes, bronze with a singing tail, ringing some pattern she almost recognized the way you almost recognize a face across a market crowd. And beneath the bells, faintly, a language she didn't speak but understood the way you understand a lullaby — not the words, the intention. *Come home. Come home. It is time.*

She woke with her heart slamming and the whole camp asleep around her, and for one disorienting moment she didn't know where she was, only that she was lying on stone under open sky and that the bells were still fading in her ears, and that she was crying, silently, the way she'd learned to cry in the dye-works loft so Gran wouldn't hear.

Gran. That was the other thing she couldn't outrun out here. Every mile they climbed, she thought of her more — the bent back, the hands stained permanently blue at the knuckles from indigo, the stories she told in fragments and then snuffed like candles when anyone knocked at the door. *Before the bad years,* Gran would say, and then stop. *Your grandfather had land,* Gran would say, and then stop. *We were somebody,* Gran would say once, drunk on festival wine, and then never say it again, and in the morning pretend she hadn't.

*We were somebody.* Sable had thought it was the ordinary grief of poor people everywhere, the ache of people ground down by the coalition's tariffs and workhouses and loyalty papers. Everyone in the borderlands had a grandmother who used to be somebody. But Gran had hummed songs nobody else knew. Gran had cooked a festival dish every midwinter that no neighbor recognized, with honey and dark grain and salt, and called it by a word that wasn't in any language spoken in Marrowgate. Gran had flinched at the sight of coalition uniforms the way other people flinched at snakes.

And Gran had died with her hand pressed over Sable's birthmark, the winter Sable turned fourteen, whispering something too quiet to catch — or maybe not too quiet. Maybe Sable had heard it and buried it so deep she'd mistaken it for a dream: *If the needle ever finds you, little bird, don't run from it.*

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold. They broke camp in the blue half-light, and Kestrel set the pace, and the trail climbed through pine into bare rock where the wind had teeth. By midmorning they reached a saddle, and Sable stopped walking without deciding to.

Below them, opening northward between two ridgelines, lay a valley.

Not the valley. She knew that immediately, and the knowing hurt worse than any wrongness could have — because it was close, it was shaped right, it had the fold of mountains and the thread of a stream and a scatter of green, but it was empty. No bridge. No bells. No town at its heart with columns and copper roofs. It was the valley the way a portrait is a person: accurate and dead.

But the others were looking at her.

"What?" she said.

"You stopped," Wren said. "Like you hit glass."

Kestrel had the compass out again. The needle pointed down into the valley, steady as ever — but slightly west of the deepest green, slightly off from where her eye wanted to go. Toward a shoulder of the far ridge. Toward a wall of rock that looked, from here, like every other wall of rock.

"There," he said. "Not the valley floor. Higher. That cliffline." He glanced at her. "What did you see?"

"A town," she said, before she could stop herself. Then, defensively: "I see towns. I'm tired."

"You saw a specific town," Kestrel said. "Describe it."

She didn't want to. Saying it aloud would make it real, and if it became real then everything else became real — the journals, the photograph, the birthmark, the blood in her veins, the twenty years of coalition decrees erasing a nation that had apparently gone on existing inside her whether she consented or not. She stood at the edge of it the way you stand at the edge of cold water.

But the needle was pointing, and the bells were still ringing somewhere behind her ribs, and she was so tired of being a foundry girl with a stain on her neck and a dead grandmother's silences.

"Two towers," she said. "Copper roofs gone green. A bridge over the river with coins in the water — no. Not coins. Offerings. People throw things in and make wishes." She heard her own voice go strange and far away. "There's a stair cut into the cliff on the north side, and it hums when you walk on it. Like a bell. Like the whole mountain is a bell and the stair is where you strike it."

Nobody spoke. Wind moved the grass along the saddle.

"That's the sanctuary approach," Odell said at last, very quietly. He had his satchel open and was staring at his own hands. "The old survey fragments — the ones that were purged — mention a resonant stair. Acoustic signaling. The whole site was built to announce the heir's return. To the valley. To—" He stopped. "To anyone listening."

"To the compass," Kestrel said.

"No," Sable said. The word came out of her with a force that surprised them all, herself most of all. "No. I'm not doing this. I'm not the — I'm a foundry worker. I pour molds. I get paid by the piece. My grandmother dyed wool. Whatever your journals say, whatever that needle says, I didn't agree to be anybody's—"

"Symbol?" Wren said. "Nobody's asking you to be. We're asking you to walk."

"And where we're walking is toward soldiers and a kingdom that doesn't exist and a birthmark that's apparently a target!" Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it. "You want me to accept this? Fine. Here's my acceptance: whoever that child in the photograph was, she's dead. She died with Callowmere. What's left is a girl who knows how to survive, and surviving means not being found. So point your needle wherever you want. I'm going back down."

She took three steps toward the descent trail before her legs betrayed her.

Because the dream rose up — the full dream, the true one, the one she'd been carrying since she was six — and it wasn't the valley below them at all. It was deeper in. It was the cliffline Kestrel had pointed to, and the stair, and the doors at the top, and a woman's voice singing in a language of honey and salt, and it rose in her with such terrible sweetness that she stopped as if she'd walked into a wall.

Terror and belonging arrived together, exactly together, the way lightning and thunder are one event heard twice. Terror because accepting it meant losing the life she'd built out of refusal — the foundry, the piecework, the small hard competence of being nobody. Belonging because she had been nobody her entire life, and the dream had always known it, and the dream had always been telling her: *you were somebody before you were born, and you will be somebody after, and both of those somebodies are waiting.*

"Sable." Kestrel's voice, behind her. Careful. "I won't lie to you. Going forward may kill us all. Reiss is behind us. Halloway may be. The coalition will call you a pretender or a threat, and either way they'll want you managed. If you turn back, I'll take you as far as safety allows and I'll never speak your name to anyone. That's a promise."

She stood with her back to him, breathing the thin cold air.

"But I'll tell you what I know about running," he went on. "I've done it for ten years. Changed my name, buried my face, guided rich fools under a word that isn't mine. And I'll tell you the cost: you stay alive, and you stop existing. Every year the person you were pretending to be eats a little more of the person you actually are, until one day you reach for your own name and it isn't there." A pause. "You're fourteen years past the day that child in the photograph disappeared. Fourteen years of being a foundry girl. And the whole time, some part of you has been walking toward this saddle. You dreamed your way here, Sable. I just followed the brass."

She turned around. The wind pulled at her hair, and below her ear the birthmark felt suddenly warm, absurdly warm, like a coal cupped in skin.

"If I keep going," she said, "you tell me everything. Every page. No more reading ahead and deciding what I can handle. I'm not a child you're escorting. I'm the reason this expedition exists, if your journals are right, and I'll be treated like it."

"Agreed," Kestrel said.

"And when we find whatever's at the end of that needle — if it's ruins, if it's nothing, if it's soldiers — I decide what I am. Not the compass. Not the journals. Not the ghost of a kingdom. Me."

"Agreed," Kestrel said again, and this time something in his face shifted, some respect arriving late but arriving whole.

Wren grinned and shouldered her pack. "Well. That's settled. Can we please get off this exposed ridge before the weather decides to contribute?"

They descended toward the valley floor, then angled west along the stream toward the cliffline, and with every step the compass needle held its bearing and the humming in the rock beneath Sable's boots grew — faint, subsonic, felt in the soles of her feet and the roots of her teeth rather than heard. By late afternoon they stood at the base of the cliff, a sheer face of gray stone a hundred feet tall, featureless except for a vertical seam of shadow near its northern end.

Kestrel brushed away a curtain of ivy with his staff.

Behind the ivy: steps. Cut into the living rock, ascending steeply into the shadow, each tread worn smooth in the center by centuries of feet. Old carvings framed the entrance — a crescent moon over a bridge, the seal from the chest, worn nearly to abstraction.

Sable put her hand on the first step.

The mountain rang.

One clear bronze note, rising from the stone itself, rolling out across the valley and up the far ridges, echoing and echoing, and somewhere far above — impossibly far above, beyond the cliff, beyond the ridge — another note answered it. Then another. Then a third, different in timbre, closer, human, a voice lifted in a signal-call that had not been sung in twenty years.

Ash appeared on the rocks above them, moving fast, one hand raised in warning. From the valley behind them, faint but unmistakable, came another sound threading up the wind: hoofbeats. Many. And the thin metallic rattle of chain and plate that no amount of mountain silence could disguise.

"They've found our trail," Kestrel breathed. "That note carried for miles."

Sable stood on the ringing stair with her hand on the stone and the answering voice falling toward her out of the hidden heights, and she understood, finally and completely, that there was no path anymore that led back to the foundry, to Marrowgate, to the girl she had decided to be.

"Then we climb," she said.
