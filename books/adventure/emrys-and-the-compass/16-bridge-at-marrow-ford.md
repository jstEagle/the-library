## 16. Bridge at Marrow Ford

Wren had sold water in Vess long enough to know that rivers were liars. They looked slow and harmless from the bank, all glitter and patience, and then they took your cart, your stock, and once — nearly — your cousin Ferro, who'd leaned too far over the canal with a net and come up downstream with a broken arm and a permanent fear of fish.

The Marrow was no canal. The Marrow was a liar with teeth.

They heard it before they saw it, a low grinding roar that Odell mistook at first for wind through the gorge. Then the trees thinned, the ground dropped away, and there it was: a broad gray-green flood shouldering between granite walls, carrying whole pine trunks like matchsticks, spinning them under and swallowing them without a burp.

And where the bridge should have been, there was nothing.

Well. Not nothing. There were the stumps of it — two stone pylons standing midstream like broken teeth, and on the near bank a tangle of timber that had once been a span, now folded into the flood like a snapped wing. A rope railing trailed from the wreckage into the water, twitching in the current as if the bridge were still trying to swim.

"Bridge at Marrow Ford," Odell said, consulting his map with the particular dryness he reserved for the world failing to cooperate with his documents. "Marked as sound as of four years ago."

"Rivers don't read maps," Kestrel said. He'd already shrugged off his pack and was walking the bank, head down, reading the water instead. Wren watched him do it — the way he stopped talking when he worked, the way his whole face went quiet and serious, like a man listening to something the rest of them couldn't hear. It was the same look he'd had on the scree above Vess, the day she'd first decided he was worth following. Or worth robbing. She hadn't entirely settled between those two, even now.

"Options," Kestrel said finally. "We go upstream, we might find another crossing in two days. Maybe three. Downstream, the Marrow drops into the gorge and nobody crosses it until autumn."

"Two days," Sable said. "Reiss is what, one day behind us? Less?"

Nobody answered that, which was answer enough.

"So," Wren said, clapping her hands. "We swim."

"We don't swim," Kestrel said. "Swimming is how you die in water like this. The current holds you down and the cold takes your arms and legs, and then you're not swimming anymore, you're sinking with style." He pointed across the river, to where the far bank narrowed against a shelf of rock. "There's a crossing here. See how the water flattens? That's a bar of gravel under the surface. It comes within a meter of breaking. If we rope up and angle across, the current does half the work."

"And if it doesn't break?" Father Ivo asked mildly. The old priest stood with his hands folded in his sleeves, watching the river the way he watched everything — as if it might be about to confess something.

"Then it sweeps you off the bar and you get to find out how strong your faith is," Kestrel said. "Ivo, you'll be tied to me. Odell next. Wren—"

"I know, I know." She was already pulling off her boots and lashing them to her pack. "I'm lightest, I go last, if anyone goes under it's my job to be useless and mournful."

"It's your job to hold the rope and not let go," Kestrel said. "Even if it drags you in. Especially then."

Wren looked at the river. Cold came off it in a visible haze, like breath in winter. Somewhere under that gray-green surface the water was moving fast enough to fold a pine trunk in half. Her whole career — such as it was — had been built on a simple principle: never bet anything you can't afford to lose. Boots, yes. Coin, sometimes. Once, her freedom, and she'd won that bet by a hair.

This felt like a different kind of bet. This felt like betting people.

She noticed Tarn already at work.

The tracker hadn't spoken since they'd left Vess — hadn't spoken, Wren suspected, in years, for reasons nobody had pried out of him and nobody would. He communicated in gestures and glances and an occasional low click of the tongue, and somehow everyone always understood him anyway. Right now he was driving one of their pitons into a boulder at the top of the bank, testing it with his full weight, then rigging a belay line with quick, economical movements that made even Kestrel raise his eyebrows slightly — the mountain guide's version of applause.

Tarn caught her looking and gave her a look back. One corner of his mouth moved. In Tarn's language, that was a whole speech.

*What?* she mouthed.

He pointed at her, then at the rope, then made a flat gesture with his hand — *stay calm* — and then, infuriatingly, pointed at the river and mimed a fish.

"I hate you," she said warmly, and went to help him with the lines.

* * *

The crossing went wrong the way crossings do: slowly, and then all at once.

Kestrel went first with the lead line, angling out onto the gravel bar, and the water took him to the thigh, then the waist, and held him there while he fought sideways foot by foot, leaning into the current like a man pushing a door that wanted to close. Then Ivo, roped behind him, gray-faced and praying under his breath in a language Wren was fairly sure wasn't liturgical. Then Odell, who lost his footing halfway, went under for three heart-stopping seconds, and came up coughing with his map case held above the water like an offering.

"Save yourself!" Wren shouted. "Let the maps drown!"

"The maps," Odell sputtered, "are the entire point!"

Then it was Sable, and then Wren herself, and Tarn last with the heavy line coiled around his shoulders, carrying the bulk of their gear in a waterproofed bundle lashed tight — the journals among them, wrapped in oilcloth, because Kestrel had spent half the previous night re-wrapping them and checking the knots twice.

Wren hit the current and the cold punched the air out of her. It wasn't cold like winter cold; it was cold like being grabbed by something enormous and indifferent. The rope burned through her gloved hand, the gravel rolled under her boots, and the river shoved at her hips like a crowd trying to push past. Halfway across, her boot found the drop-off at the edge of the bar and suddenly she was floating, weightless, the world tilting, and the only thing in the universe was the rope in her fist and Kestrel's voice cutting flat and clear through the roar:

"Wren. Feet down. Find the bar. *Now.*"

She found it. She hauled herself along it hand over hand, gasping, swearing with a fluency that made Ivo glance skyward on principle, and then the far bank rose up and hands — Sable's hands — dragged her onto stone, and she lay there wheezing at a sky that seemed absurdly blue and calm.

"One more," Sable said.

Wren turned.

Tarn was mid-river.

He'd waited to cross until the gear line was rigged, and he'd crossed heavy — the bundle strapped high on his chest, the coil of spare rope over one shoulder, moving with that unhurried economy of his, feet finding the bar like he'd walked it all his life. He was two-thirds across when the river changed.

Wren saw it happen and understood it in the same instant, the way you understand a trapdoor opening under someone else. Upstream, where the gorge bent, something had given way — a jammed logjam or a slumped bank, some small failure in a system under too much pressure — and a slug of extra water came down the channel like a fist sliding into a glove. The river rose. Not much. A hand's width. Enough.

The bar disappeared.

Tarn went from waist-deep to swimming in a single stride. The current took him sideways, spun him, slammed him against the submerged edge of the bar, and Wren saw the bundle wrench at his chest strap, saw him reach for it instead of the rope — *reached for it instead of the rope* — and then both he and the bundle were rolling together in the gray-green chaos, downstream toward the gorge, toward the place where the Marrow dropped and nothing came back up.

"TARN!" Sable was screaming it. Kestrel was already moving along the bank, paying out the belay line, shouting something technical that the river ate alive.

Wren didn't remember deciding to run. She was just running, boots forgotten, pack abandoned, barefoot on wet stone, chasing the dark shape in the water. The bank ended at a granite spur that jutted into the current, and beyond it the water funneled hard toward the gorge throat. If he passed the spur, he was gone. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it.

Tarn surfaced twenty meters out. His arm was up — not waving, reaching. The bundle was gone from his chest; she could see it tumbling below him, a dark shape in the murk, oilcloth flashing. His other hand was tangled somewhere in the gear line, dragging him under, and his head went down, came up, went down again.

The rope. Kestrel had told her: *even if it drags you in.* But the rope was fifty meters back up the bank, and Tarn was right *here*, and the spur was right *here*, and Wren had grown up on canals where the difference between a living person and a dead one was often just whether somebody nearby was stupid enough to lean out.

She was stupid enough. She'd known it her whole life. She leaned out.

The spur's edge was slick, the current sucking at it, and she got one hand around a knob of granite and stretched the other arm out over the water until her shoulder screamed, and Tarn came around the bend of the current toward her — coming fast, too fast, a log with a face — and their hands met.

His grip closed on her wrist like a shackle. The weight of him hit her arm and her knees buckled and for one long, ringing second the whole world was her fingers on wet granite and the pull of a drowning man, and she thought, with strange clarity: *this is how it ends, drowned by a mute man in a river nobody's ever heard of, and Ferro will never let me hear the end of it.*

Then Kestrel was there. She never saw him arrive; he was simply suddenly beside her, flat on the rock, one arm locked around her harness strap, the other seizing Tarn's collar, and behind him Sable and Ivo and Odell in a chain, hauling, all of them screaming wordlessly into the roar. The river pulled. The chain held. And inch by obscene inch, like pulling a tree stump out of the earth, they brought Tarn up over the lip of the spur and onto stone, where he lay on his side and vomited half the Marrow back into the Marrow.

The bundle washed up ten minutes later, wedged in an eddy below the spur, oilcloth intact. Kestrel waded out on a rope and got it. Of course he did.

* * *

They made camp early, in a hollow above the bank, because nobody had the strength to argue with Kestrel about making good distance. Tarn sat wrapped in every blanket they owned, shivering, steam rising off him, refusing — with gestures — all suggestions that he rest lying down.

Wren sat beside him with a pot of the terrible tea Ivo brewed from needles and bark, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was easy, since only one of them could.

"You know," she said eventually, "when I signed on to this, it was a job. You understand that. A well-paid job, relatively speaking. Escort the brooding guide and the angry heir through the hills, keep my head down, collect my coin, disappear." She turned the tin cup in her hands. "That was the deal I made with myself. Head down. Coin collected. Disappear."

Tarn looked at her. His eyes were bloodshot and his lips were still faintly blue and he had the expression of a man patiently waiting for the punchline, because he'd learned, somewhere in whatever silence he carried, that most talk was just noise people made to avoid saying the true thing.

"I dove into a flood river today," Wren said. "For journals. For *paper*. Paper about a country that doesn't exist, belonging to a girl I've known three weeks, guarded by a man whose real name I learned last week." She laughed, but it came out wrong, so she stopped. "Head down. That was the rule. And today I looked at that rule and I jumped in a river anyway, and the worst part is—" She shook her head. "The worst part is it didn't even feel like a choice. It felt like the obvious thing. Like the only thing. When did that happen? When did you all become the thing I can't afford to lose?"

Tarn reached over. His hand was freezing and shaking, and he pressed it briefly over hers on the cup — a clumsy, crushing pressure, there and gone. Then he tapped his own chest, and pointed at her, and made the flat gesture: *same.*

"Yeah, well," Wren said, blinking harder than she meant to. "Don't do it again. Next time the journals can drown. Journals float. You don't."

He smiled — actually smiled, the whole mouth, a rare eclipse — and tapped the oilcloth bundle, now drying by the fire, and then tapped his chest again. *Worth it.*

"You're all insane," she told him. "Every one of you. The priest prays to a god for a dead kingdom, the mapmaker's doing penance for erasing it, the girl's inheriting a war she never asked for, and the guide—" She paused. "I don't even know what the guide's doing. I think the guide is trying to walk back into his own grave and fix it."

Tarn nodded slowly, as if she'd said something wise, which annoyed her, because she'd been aiming for funny.

Across the fire, Kestrel was re-wrapping the journals. Again. Checking knots. Sable sat beside him, holding pages steady while he worked, and the two of them had their heads bent together over the old handwriting, and the firelight made them look — Wren thought — like something out of one of Odell's older maps: two figures at the edge of blank space, standing where the cartographer had written *here be monsters* and been wrong about what kind.

She caught herself smiling at them and killed it immediately. Sentiment. Dangerous. She'd have to watch that.

* * *

It was Tarn who heard them first.

He'd gone out at dusk to check the back-trail — over everyone's objections, communicated entirely through his refusal to sit down — and he came back into camp at a pace that silenced every conversation before he'd taken his third step. He crouched by the fire, drew fast in the ash with one finger: a line for the trail, a circle for the ford, and then, downstream of the crossing, a series of small marks.

"What is it?" Sable asked.

Tarn looked at Kestrel. Drew again: the marks, repeated. Then he held up fingers. Counted them out, deliberate, grim.

Six.

"Lanterns?" Kestrel asked quietly. "On the far bank?"

Tarn shook his head. Pointed downstream — their side of the river. Then he touched his ear, and swept his hand forward through the air, and made a sound with his mouth, soft and rhythmic, like something knocking.

Boots. On stone. Coming up the valley.

Wren felt the warmth drain out of the evening all at once. Six men, on their side of the Marrow — which meant they'd crossed, which meant they'd had a crossing, which meant this hadn't been luck. Somebody had known the route. Somebody had known the ford.

"Halloway," she said, and the name came out flat and certain, because of course it was. The debt collector didn't drown. Debt collectors floated. They were built for it.

Kestrel was already kicking ash over the fire, his face unreadable, his hands moving with that terrible calm she'd seen exactly twice before. "Pack everything," he said. "Leave nothing. We move north in the dark, off the trail, and we don't stop until the Marrow's a rumor behind us."

In the darkness beyond the fire ring, Tarn stood listening, his head tilted, one hand raised for quiet — and Wren saw his fingers curl slowly shut, counting something in the black.

The knocking sound was getting closer.
