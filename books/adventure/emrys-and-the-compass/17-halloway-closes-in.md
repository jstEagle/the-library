## 17. Halloway Closes In

Halloway had learned long ago that the best hour for collecting was the hour before dawn. Debtors, like animals, were weakest then — half-frozen in sleep, their will still pooled somewhere below the surface, their bodies soft and unguarded. He had taken doors off hinges at that hour in nine towns. He had watched strong men cry at that hour, watched proud men sign away their grandmothers' graves, watched one ferryman in Doss try to swim away with his ledger tied to his back and drown in four feet of water because he had never learned to swim.

So it was at that hour that Halloway came up the goat track above the expedition's camp, alone, with a knife, a coil of oiled cord, and a pistol he had never once fired in twenty years of ugly work.

The pistol was theater. The knife and the cord were not. But even those were mostly for himself. Reiss's orders had been specific: recover the chest and its contents, and *manage* the bearers. Halloway knew what *manage* meant in the vocabulary of the man who paid him. It meant graves in soft ground, no names, no markers. It meant the mute tracker and the water-girl and the old priest and the cartographer all going into the earth because they had seen a brass compass turn the wrong way.

Halloway had been telling himself for three days that he was fine with that.

He was a collector. He collected. What was collected stopped being his problem. That was the whole architecture of his life, the load-bearing wall of it: *once the debt is settled, the debt is not mine.* He had built that wall brick by brick over twenty years, and he had never once looked behind it.

Until Vess. Until the chest. Until the night Kestrel Amberdale — or Emrys, or whatever name the man was wearing that week — had stood in his own doorway with a flooded chest at his feet and said *no* to a debt that would have broken any other man in the borderlands, and said it without anger, without fear, almost with pity, and Halloway had walked away from that doorway feeling something he had not felt since he was a boy: *small.*

Not because the man had refused him. Refusal was weather; Halloway had stood in it all his life.

Because the man had looked at him the way no one looked at him — not as a threat to be bargained with, not as a function to be paid, but as a man standing in a doorway holding someone else's leash. As if Kestrel had seen the leash. As if he had seen it and, worse, had seen that Halloway saw it too.

Nobody was supposed to see the leash.

* * *

The camp sat in a hollow below him, a scatter of low shapes against the last of the night: three small tents and a lean-to, a cold firepit, the mules hobbled among the scree. The sentry — the mute tracker, Bren, the one who never spoke and never slept as far as anyone could tell — was positioned at the mouth of the hollow with his back to a boulder, and Halloway watched him for a long quarter hour and confirmed what he already suspected: the man was awake, motionless, and facing the wrong way. Facing the trail, because trails were where trouble came from. He was not facing the goat track, because goat tracks were where goats came from.

Halloway had made a career of being the goat track.

He came down through the boulders without a sound he could hear himself, which was the only standard that mattered. The chest — the Callowmere chest, the reason for all of this, the thing worth more than every debt in Vess combined — sat under the lean-to between two packs. He could see the corner of it in the grey light, water-stained wood and brass fittings, sitting there like a headstone.

He was ten feet from it when the priest's tent flap moved.

Father Ivo did not come out. Halloway froze with his hand on the cord and watched the flap settle, and decided the old man had only turned in his sleep. Priests turned in their sleep. Priests were the one kind of man Halloway had never been able to make afraid, which was why he avoided them the way other men avoided dogs.

He crossed the last ten feet. He put his hand on the chest.

And Kestrel said, from the darkness behind the lean-to, "You should have taken the left fork."

Halloway went for the knife and the knife was not in his hand anymore, and he did not remember it leaving his hand, and that frightened him more than the cold ring of something hard pressed against the back of his neck. He had spent twenty years being the fastest thing in a room. Somewhere between Vess and this hollow, the man he had come to bury had become something else — someone who moved like the mountains had taught him personally, and hated him a little for the tuition.

"You were watching the trail," Kestrel said. His voice was quiet, conversational, terrible. "I watched you watching it. You always watch the front door, Halloway. You did it in Vess too. You came to my door at the hour before dawn because you think everyone's weakest then. You're right. But you always come the same way, and you always leave yourself one exit, and the exit is always the way you came."

"Kestrel—"

"Emrys." The pressure at his neck eased a fraction. "Around the others, Emrys. They know enough to be in danger. They don't need to know my name too."

Halloway turned, slowly, hands open. The grey light was coming up now, the first real light, and he could see the camp waking around him — the water-girl sitting up in her blankets with a knife already in her fist, the tracker Bren standing at the hollow's mouth with a sling that had not been in his hand thirty seconds ago, the priest emerging from his tent with his collar askew and his eyes going from Halloway to the chest to Kestrel with the unhurried look of a man reading a scripture he'd read before. Only the cartographer, Odell, still slept, snoring gently against a pack, a man who had apparently decided that if death was coming he would rather not be awake for it.

"You came alone," Kestrel said. It wasn't a question.

"Reiss wanted it done quiet."

"And you agreed."

Halloway said nothing. In the growing light, Kestrel studied him — the same flat, patient study he'd given him in the doorway in Vess, and Halloway felt it again, that awful sensation of being *read*, like a ledger.

"You agreed," Kestrel said again, softer. "Because you always agree. That's the arrangement, isn't it. Reiss forgives a little, you collect a little. How much do you owe him, Halloway? Or is it the other way — how much does he owe you, and calls it the other thing?"

"Shut your mouth."

"Twenty years," Kestrel said. "I've been watching you since Vess. You're good at the work. You're the best I've seen at it, and I've been on the wrong end of men like you before. But you hate the work. You hate it the way a mule hates the mill — you do it because stopping isn't in you anymore. And Reiss knows that. Reiss built that. He found you when you were nothing, gave you debts instead of wages, and called the leash a career."

"I said shut your mouth." Halloway's voice came out cracked, and he heard it, and hated it. The water-girl was watching him with something that wasn't fear. It was worse. It was recognition. He had seen that look before — on the faces of debtors who realized, mid-pleading, that the man holding their ledger was holding one of his own.

"Here's what I don't understand," Kestrel said. He crouched, unhurried, and turned the chest so the Callowmere seal faced the light — the crowned bridge, the eight-pointed star, the crest of a country that no longer existed on any map Halloway had ever collected against. "Reiss sent you to take this and bury us. Fine. That's his mistake, and it'll cost him. But you — you came up a goat track at the hour before dawn with a knife and a cord, alone, to kill five people in their sleep for a man who signs his orders *managed*. And you're standing there telling yourself that's just the work." Kestrel shook his head slowly. "It's not the work, Halloway. The work was Vess. The work was ledgers and doors and men crying over their grandmothers' graves. This is something else. This is you doing the thing you can't come back from, so that you can keep telling yourself you're the kind of man who does it."

The light was fully up now. Halloway could see the whole camp, and the whole camp could see him — a small, broad man in a good coat gone grey at the seams, standing over a dead kingdom's chest with a knife he no longer had.

"You don't know anything about me," Halloway said.

"I know you didn't fire the pistol."

Halloway's hand went to his coat before he could stop it.

"You carried it twenty years and never fired it," Kestrel said. "I've known men like that. The pistol's not for them. It's for the day they finally decide they're not that man anymore, and can't quite do it, and need to be reminded of what they're carrying." He stood. "So here's my offer. Not a bargain — I don't bargain with leashes. A trade."

"I don't make trades with dead men."

"You're not talking to a dead man. You're talking to the man Reiss is most afraid of, and you're going to help me prove it." Kestrel crouched again and pulled a journal from the chest — water-swollen, ink faded to ghosts, but legible. "You've read Reiss's orders. You've carried them. You know how he writes. Now look at this." He held the journal open. "Orders, Halloway. Dated twenty years ago. Signed by the same office Reiss reports to. The famine in Callowmere wasn't a famine. It was policy. And the disaster that got four climbers killed on the Vess glacier — the one they blamed on me — was ordered from the same desk. Reiss signed the testimony that buried me. He didn't just betray me. He was following the same leash you're on. He's been on it longer, that's all. He climbed it."

Halloway looked at the journal. He could not read most of it — the hand was cramped, the ink half-drowned — but he could read the letterhead, the seal at the top of the page, the office designation that he had seen at the top of every order Reiss had ever given him. The same office. The same chain. Twenty years of *manage* and *recover* and *quietly*, all of it running down a line that ended, apparently, in the drowning of a nation.

"That's not my problem," he said, and heard how it sounded, and heard that Kestrel heard how it sounded.

"No," Kestrel agreed. "It's not your problem. It's your *exit*." He closed the journal. "You want one thing, Halloway. You've wanted it so long you've forgotten it's a want and not a wound. You want the leash off. Not cut — cut means Reiss hunts you, and you know he would, you know what he does to loose ends. Off. Clean. A man who owes nothing, collects nothing, and is owed nothing. And here is the honest truth, the only thing I've said all morning that isn't strategy: I can't give you that. Nobody can give you that. You have to take it, the way everyone does, and it will cost you, the way it costs everyone."

"That's a hell of an offer."

"It's not the offer. The offer is this." Kestrel's voice dropped, and the whole camp seemed to lean in. "Reiss is coming up this mountain behind you with a squad, and when he's done with us he'll be done with you, because you've seen the orders now, or you will have, and men who've seen the orders get *managed*. You know it. You've managed men for smaller reasons. So: walk back down the mountain and tell him you found the camp empty. Tell him we went east through the low pass. Buy us two days. And when this is over — when the truth is out, whatever the world does with it — you come to the tribunal and you give them every order Reiss ever put in your hand. Every ledger. Every name. And a tribunal of exiles, a court that has no power except the truth, will put your testimony on the record. And the day that record exists, Reiss's leash doesn't just come off you. It comes off in *his* hands. He'll be holding it when it burns."

The silence went on a long time. Somewhere above the hollow, a chough called, twice, the way they did at first light.

"You're lying," Halloway said at last. "There's no tribunal. There's no record. There's five of you and a dead girl's kingdom and a compass that points at nothing. You're promising me a thing that doesn't exist."

"Yes," Kestrel said.

Halloway stared at him. "You admit it."

"I admit it doesn't exist *yet*. I'm not promising you it will. I'm promising you what I'll do: I'll stand up in front of whatever court the exiles can build, and I'll say your name and your testimony out loud, and I'll keep saying it until someone with power has to decide whether the truth or the coalition is heavier. That's all I can promise. It may be worth nothing. But it's worth more than what Reiss is holding, and you know it, because if it weren't you'd have used the cord on me by now instead of standing there listening." Kestrel held out the journal. "You've spent twenty years collecting other people's debts. This is the first one anyone's ever tried to hand *you*. It's yours. What Reiss owes you. Take it."

Halloway did not take the journal. He looked at it for a long moment — at the seal, at the drowned ink, at the office designation that had signed his life away in letters he'd never been permitted to read — and then he looked at the camp: the girl with the knife, the tracker with the sling, the priest with his ruined collar, the cartographer still snoring, and the guide with two names and no country, holding out a debt like a hand.

"You'll die up here," Halloway said. "All of you. He has thirty men."

"Probably," Kestrel said. "But not today, if you walk down and lie well. Can you lie well, Halloway? Twenty years of telling debtors the law is on your side — I'd think it's the one skill you've perfected."

Something moved through Halloway's face then that he could not have named and would not have wanted to. It might have been the first laugh he'd almost had in a decade.

He turned and went up the goat track, and at the top he stopped, and did not turn around, and said, in a voice pitched to carry no farther than the hollow: "The low pass has a watchpost. Reiss knows it. Go north of it, over the shoulder of the grey peak. There's a route. No one watches it because no one knows it." A pause. "I knew it because I collected a dead shepherd's debts there once, and his widow showed me, and I never told anyone, because telling anyone was never worth anything to me before."

"And now?"

"Now it's worth a tribunal that doesn't exist," Halloway said. "God help me."

He went over the crest and was gone.

Kestrel stood a long time looking at the empty track. The water-girl came up beside him, knife still in hand, and said, "You just made a deal with the devil's bill collector."

"I made him a promise I intend to keep," Kestrel said, "which is worse, for both of us." He turned the journal over in his hands, and his brow furrowed. "Odell. Wake up."

The cartographer snorted awake, blinked at the knife, the priest, the returning silence, and said, "Is it Tuesday?"

"Odell." Kestrel's voice had changed — gone flat and careful, the voice of a man standing on ice he didn't trust. "The route Halloway just gave us. Over the shoulder of the grey peak. You mapped that glacier twenty years ago, didn't you. You were on the survey."

Odell's face went very still, in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"Kestrel," the old man said quietly, "there are things about that survey you need to hear before we take one step toward that pass. And you're not going to like a single one of them."
