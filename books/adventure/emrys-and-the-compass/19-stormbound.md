## 19. Stormbound

The storm came down on them like a verdict.

Kestrel had seen it building all afternoon—a bruise-colored wall stacking itself over the western peaks, swallowing the sun in slow bites—and he'd pushed the party hard for two hours to reach the shelter he remembered from his mapping days: a limestone hollow halfway up the shoulder of Mount Ceren, wide enough for six people and a fire, its mouth angled away from the prevailing wind. They made it with perhaps twenty minutes to spare. Then the sky opened.

It was not snow so much as the mountain deciding to erase them. Wind screamed through the pass below with a sound like a train that never arrived, and the snow came horizontal, dry as ash, filling the air until the world beyond three arm-lengths was rumor. Kestrel stood at the cave mouth after dark was done, watching nothing, listening to the storm work, and felt the old familiar peace of it—the peace of being somewhere weather could not follow, of being, for once, exactly where he was supposed to be.

"You're smiling," Wren said behind him. She had her back against the wall, arms crossed, watching him with the flat suspicion she reserved for things she didn't understand yet intended to profit from. "Nobody smiles at that."

"Guides do."

"Guides who smile at storms have either been out here too long or are hiding from something worse." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. She went back to the fire, where Odell was drying map paper page by page over the flames with the tenderness of a man handling scripture, and where Father Ivo sat with his hands open on his knees, murmuring something that might have been prayer or might have been inventory.

Sable sat apart, as she always did, near the deepest wall of the cave where the stone still held the memory of summer. The compass lay open in her lap. She wasn't looking at it. She was looking at the fire, and her face had the particular stillness Kestrel had learned to read over the weeks since the foundry: not calm, but held. A woman keeping a lid on something boiling.

The mute tracker—Halat, though only Odell used the name—had taken first watch at the mouth out of pure habit, though there was nothing to watch but white. The priest had blessed the rations. The cartographer had cursed them. Everything was as normal as it ever got.

Kestrel banked the fire low, checked the rope lines twice, and settled against the stone across from Sable because it was the driest ground and for no other reason he was willing to admit.

* * *

The storm did not slacken by morning. It did not slacken by noon.

By the second day they had eaten into their margin, and Kestrel did the arithmetic he hated: four days' food at half-rations, five if he pushed it, and no way to know how long the storm would sit on the mountain like a hen on an egg. He told them plainly, because they deserved plainness: two more days, then they moved, storm or no storm, because dead men carried no truth anywhere.

Wren took the news the way she took everything, by turning it into leverage—"So if we starve, I get your boots"—and Odell took it by redrawing the route from memory, and Father Ivo took it by saying grace over a portion the size of a fist. But Sable set down her share untouched and looked at Kestrel across the fire with an expression he had been avoiding for days.

Not accusation. Worse. Patience.

He knew what was coming. He had known since the valley below Marrow Ford, when she'd watched him talk Halloway down with promises he might not keep, and asked nothing. Since the saddle, when Halat died holding the line and Sable had knelt in the bloodied snow beside the tracker's body and Kestrel had turned away to study the horizon because looking at grief felt like opening a door he'd nailed shut years ago. She had been patient the way deep water is patient under ice.

On the second night, with the wind dropping to a sullen roar and the others asleep in their cloaks, she finally collected.

"You were a guide," she said. "Before. Before Vess, before 'Emrys.' You guided the high routes. That's why you know this cave, this pass, every goat track between here and the capital. So tell me about it. Tell me about before."

"There's nothing to tell."

"Liar." She said it without heat, almost fondly. "You flinch every time someone mentions the Amberdale expedition. Odell goes quiet around you when maps come out. Father Ivo prays for four people nobody will name. I count things—it's what kept me alive in the foundry district. And I've counted: four climbers, one survivor, one name erased. Yours."

The fire popped. Out at the cave mouth, Halat shifted against the wind, a shadow among shadows.

Kestrel fed a stick into the flames and watched it catch. He could refuse. He had refused for seven years; he was very good at it, the way a man is good at holding his breath. But the cave was small and the storm was vast and Sable was the heir to a murdered kingdom sitting three feet away, asking him for the truth the way she'd never once asked anyone for anything—without bargaining.

"The Amberdale crossing," he said finally. "Five years ago. Six clients, four support climbers, me leading. A coalition survey contract—mapping the high glacier above the Serpent's Tooth for a new trade road." He heard his own voice go flat, recitation-flat, the voice of a man reading a report about strangers. "We were three days onto the ice when the weather turned. Not a storm like this. A whiteout that lasted nine days. We dug in. On the fourth day the cornice above our camp let go."

"And?"

"Four of my climbers died. Buried before anyone could shout. I dug for two days with my hands. Found three." He turned the stick in the fire. "The inquiry said I ignored warning signs. Said I pushed past a safe turnaround point to protect the contract. There was a testimony entered. Signed. It corroborated everything the coalition wanted on record—that the disaster was negligence, not the mountain, not the road project, not the fact that the survey data would have shown the road route ran straight through the last free Callowmere valley."

Sable had gone very still. "Who signed the testimony?"

"You already know."

"Davor Reiss."

"He was my friend. We'd climbed together since we were boys. He testified that I'd been warned twice and refused to turn back." Kestrel smiled, and it tasted like copper. "I was never warned. There was nothing to turn back from. But my name went on the blacklist, and blacklisted guides don't work, and men who don't work drink, and men who drink die quietly in towns like Vess. Except I didn't. I buried myself instead. Took a dead man's name and became a river guide for tourists who couldn't tell scree from shale. Seven years of being no one." He looked up. "There. Now you've counted everything."

Sable didn't say anything for a long moment. The storm filled the silence, pressing at the cave mouth like something alive and locked out.

"My grandmother," she said at last, "used to sing while she worked the forge. Songs in Callowmere tongue. When I was small I thought everyone's grandmother sang songs in a language nobody else spoke. She stopped singing the year I turned eight. The year a coalition census officer came to the district and started asking which families had 'old-border names.' After that she sang only when the hammer was falling, so the words hid inside the noise." Sable's hands closed around the compass, knuckles whitening. "She died two winters ago. At the end she got confused, thought I was my mother, and told me to run for the valley. *The green valley under the twin teeth,* she said. *Where the water runs backward.* I thought it was fever talking."

"It isn't."

"I know it isn't now. The compass led us to a valley that matches her fever-dream word for word." Sable laughed, short and bitter. "Do you understand what that's like? To find out the stories you were told were true? That the country they erased is real, that it has a valley and elders and people who survived, and that you're the—what did the journals call me—the *last living heir*?" She shook her head. "I'm a foundry worker. I pour iron. I'm good at it. Nobody ever handed me anything except wages and bruises, and now suddenly I'm an inheritance."

"Inheritances are heavy," Kestrel said. "That's how you know they're real."

"That's not comfort. That's just weight."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't comfort. Nothing honest is."

* * *

The third day of the storm, the wind changed.

Kestrel felt it before he heard it—a shift in the pressure against the cave mouth, the scream dropping half a tone—and he was on his feet with the barometer sense that had never left him, the sense that had kept clients alive and gotten him blamed for deaths anyway. "It's breaking," he told the others. "Tonight or tomorrow morning. Eat well tonight. Tomorrow we move."

That evening, with the fire built up properly for the first time in three days and morale rising like bread, Sable came and sat beside him at the cave mouth. Below them the clouds were tearing open in places, showing stars through wounds in the sky, and the snowfield gleamed blue-white under a moon fighting through.

"You didn't answer my question," she said.

"What question?"

"The one I haven't asked yet." She pulled her cloak tighter. "You told me about Amberdale. About the blacklist, the false testimony, the seven years of being Emrys. But here's what I counted that you didn't explain: you *like* being no one. You're good at it. Invisible men don't get hunted. Invisible men don't testify. And in a few days we walk into the ruins of my kingdom, and the journals go to a tribunal, and the truth comes out—and the moment it does, Davor Reiss's testimony gets examined, and your name gets cleared, or it doesn't, but either way *Emrys dies*. Everyone will know who you are. Every creditor, every coalition agent, every family member of those four dead climbers. You'll be visible for the rest of your life." She turned to look at him, moonlight catching the edge of her face. "So here's my question, Kestrel Amberdale. What will you do when the truth costs you the last hiding place you have?"

The question landed in his chest like a piton driven home.

He looked out at the mountains—at the peaks he knew better than his own face, the passes that had been his whole world before Vess, before the chest in the river, before a girl with a birthmark and a brass compass had walked into his life and refused to leave. He thought about the answer he'd have given a month ago: *I don't know.* The answer he'd have given a week ago: *nothing good.* 

But the storm had spent three days teaching him something. Confined in the stone with these people—Wren counting boots, Odell redrawing erased coastlines, Ivo praying over fists of food, Halat standing watch over nothing—he had felt something he hadn't felt since Amberdale. Usefulness without anonymity. Being *seen*, by five people who knew his real name, and not dying of it.

"When I pulled that chest out of the river," he said slowly, "I told myself I was curious. Then Halloway offered to wipe my debt clean if I gave it up, and I refused, and I couldn't tell you why. Then Reiss came to town and I ran, and I still couldn't tell you why." He exhaled, breath fogging in the cold. "I think I know now. I spent seven years hiding from a lie. And a lie only owns you if you stay hidden from it. The moment I stand up and say *my name is Kestrel Amberdale and here is what actually happened*—the lie stops owning me. Whatever comes after. Prison, ruin, the families spitting in my face. At least it'll be mine."

"That's not an answer. That's a speech."

He huffed a laugh despite himself. "No. It's not an answer. The honest answer is I don't know what I'll do when Emrys is gone. I've worn him so long I don't know what fits underneath." He met her eyes. "But I know what I won't do. I won't hand the chest back to the river. I won't let them bury Callowmere twice. If visibility is the price of the truth coming out, then I pay it. I've been paying worse prices for less."

Sable studied him for a long time. Then, unexpectedly, she reached over and pressed the compass into his hand—brass cold as the cave floor, the needle trembling, steadying, pointing southeast toward the valley they both now dreamed about.

"Hold it," she said. "Just for a minute. Grandmother said a thing once, in the forge songs. *The mountain doesn't ask who you were. Only where you're going.*" She shrugged, embarrassed, foundry-tough again in a blink. "I used to think it was about climbing. I don't anymore."

Kestrel looked down at the needle. Steady as a heartbeat. Pointing home—for her, maybe for all of them.

Behind them, from the depths of the cave, Odell's voice cracked the quiet, tight with something between awe and alarm: "Kestrel. You need to see this. The storm tore the cloud deck open—and there's smoke in the valley below us. Cook-fires. Dozens of them. Someone is camped on our route—and by the layout, they've been waiting there for days."
