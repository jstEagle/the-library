## 20. Reiss's Doubt

The rain had followed Reiss out of Vess for two days, and by the third he'd stopped noticing it. It ran off the brim of his oilskin hood and down the collar of his coat, and it made the mountain trail a gray ribbon of mud and stone that climbed toward the saddle country where, according to Halloway's last message, four fugitives and a dead man's chest were moving north.

He should have felt like a hunter. Instead he felt like a man walking behind his own funeral procession.

His squad was six strong: Voss and Merrick on point, the twins Brandt and Callow—no relation to the kingdom, though the coincidence had stopped being funny years ago—a young signals man named Petter who still laughed at his own jokes, and Sergeant Ilsa Vane, who had served with him long enough to read his silences. They moved well. They always moved well. That was the problem with soldiers; they did their work so competently that you could commit almost any crime and call it an operation.

"Camp site ahead," Vane said, drawing level with him. She nodded at a shelf of granite above the treeline, sheltered from the wind that came funneling up the valley. "We'll make the saddle by midday tomorrow if the weather holds."

"The weather won't hold."

"No." She glanced at the sky, where the clouds had gone the color of wet slate. "It won't. But we'll make the saddle anyway, if you want it."

*If you want it.* She left the question hanging there, the way she did when she wanted him to hear himself answer.

"I want it," Reiss said, and heard how flat it sounded, how much it sounded like a sentence being read aloud in a courtroom by someone who didn't believe the verdict.

They made camp. Petter got a fire going under an overhang, small and smokeless, and the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool settled over everything. Reiss ate his ration without tasting it and then did what he had been doing every night since Vess, since the moment he'd stood in that rented room above the chandlery and seen the salvage report with *Kestrel Amberdale* written across it in a clerk's careful hand.

He took out the folder.

It was not an official folder. Official folders came through channels, stamped and countersigned, and this one had come to him three nights ago through a courier who would not meet his eyes, with instructions from Director's Office liaison that it contained "supplementary background relevant to the Amberdale matter." He had told himself it was routine. He had told himself a great many things were routine.

Inside were copies of correspondence from twenty-two years ago—the year of the Amberdale expedition, the year four climbers died on the White Saddle glacier, the year a promising young guide's name was entered into the blacklist rolls and a promising young coalition officer named Davor Reiss signed the testimony that put him there.

Reiss had signed it because the evidence was laid out for him, complete and damning: Kestrel's route plan, altered; Kestrel's rope log, falsified; two witnesses among the porters who swore the guide had cut corners to save time. He had been twenty-six years old and hungry, and the directorate had handed him the case the way you hand a bone to a dog, and he had done his work competently, because that was the problem with soldiers.

He had never once, in twenty-two years, asked why the evidence had been laid out so completely. Who assembles a case that perfect? Only someone who built it.

The new folder answered questions he hadn't known how to ask.

The first document was a memorandum from the Borderlands Directorate to the Office of Internal Security, dated eleven days before the expedition set out. It concerned one K. Amberdale, mountain guide, noted for "exceptional route-finding ability in high terrain" and flagged for observation. Not suspicion of anything. Observation. The way you flag a mine shaft before you decide whether to collapse it.

The second document was the reason his hands had gone still on the page, three nights ago, in a way Vane had noticed and pretended not to.

It was a requisition order. Equipment, cold-weather grade, drawn against Directorate stores and issued to persons unnamed—"expedition support personnel"—in the week before the Amberdale party departed. Rope. Pitons. A quantity of pitons far exceeding any legitimate need, issued to men who were not listed on any expedition roster because they did not exist on any roster. Support personnel. Saboteurs. Men whose job was to go up the mountain ahead of six climbers and make the mountain kill them.

And beneath the requisition, in the margin, in the dry bureaucratic hand of some deputy director's secretary, a note that might have been about grain shipments or road repairs if you didn't know what you were looking at:

*Amberdale to be discredited regardless of outcome. If accident occurs, attribute to guide error. Subject's knowledge of eastern approaches makes him unsuitable for retention in civilian capacity.*

*Unsuitable for retention.* As if Kestrel had been a filing cabinet. As if four dead climbers had been a clerical expense, a rounding error in the cost of making sure one man who knew too much about the mountains would never again be trusted enough to lead anyone into them.

Because that was what it came down to, when Reiss sat with it long enough for the fire to burn low. The eastern approaches. The old Callowmere passes. Kestrel Amberdale had grown up guiding in country that the coalition had spent twenty years pretending wasn't there, and a man who knew erased valleys was a man who might someday find what had been erased. So they hadn't killed him. Killing him would have raised questions. They had done something more elegant. They had buried him alive in disgrace, in debt, in a border town under a borrowed name, and let the mountains themselves take the blame.

And Reiss had held the shovel.

"Davor."

Vane's voice. He looked up. She had come to stand at the edge of the overhang, her back to the fire, watching him with the particular patience of a woman who had decided hours ago to say something and was waiting for the right shape of silence.

"You've read that folder every night since Vess," she said. "You sleep worse than the twins snore. And today you set a pace that says you're trying to catch them or trying not to, and I can't tell which." She crouched, forearms on knees, close enough that the firelight found the lines around her eyes. "So tell me which."

"It's not your concern, Sergeant."

"Six people are following you into a storm toward a fight with a man you went to academy with. That makes it my concern." She waited. When he didn't answer, she said, quieter: "I carried your gear out of the Karst action when you took shrapnel in the shoulder. I've earned the version of you that tells the truth."

Reiss looked at the fire. Beyond it, the rain came down through the dark like static, and somewhere north of them, over the saddle, lightning flickered inside the cloud—a soft pulse, like something breathing under a blanket.

"When you were a soldier," he said, "did you ever follow an order you knew was wrong?"

"Everyone has."

"And what did you do with it?"

"Carried it." She shrugged. "Some things you carry until they get heavy enough that you either put them down or lie down under them. Which are you doing, sir?"

He almost laughed. It came out as breath, no sound. He closed the folder and held it a moment, feeling its weight—not the paper weight, the other kind—and then, because Vane had earned it, because someone had to know besides him, he passed it to her.

She read by firelight, turning pages slowly. He watched her face do what his had done: nothing, nothing, nothing, and then everything at once, a tightening around the mouth like a drawbridge coming up.

"This is genuine?" she said at last.

"The seal's right. The routing numbers check against the archive index. I had Petter verify them without telling him what they were." He paused. "He thinks we're auditing supply records."

"Clever."

"Cowardly," Reiss said. "But yes."

Vane set the folder on her knee and stared into the fire for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—flattened, careful, the voice of a soldier assessing ground.

"If this is real, then the Amberdale conviction was manufactured. Then the four climbers weren't lost, they were spent. And then whatever this man is doing now, running north with a salvaged chest and a girl the directorate wants 'managed'—" she used the word the way you'd spit out a bad tooth, "—then he isn't the criminal we were sent after. He's the loose thread. And we're the needle they're using to pull it shut."

"Yes."

"Then why are we still moving north?"

That was the question. He had been circling it for three days the way the compass needle in that cursed chest must have circled its target, and he still didn't have an answer he could live with, only answers he could survive on. Because orders were orders. Because a squad leader who turned his team around on a rumor of documents needed more than a rumor. Because if he was wrong, he'd be throwing away his career, Vane's career, five good soldiers' careers, on behalf of a blacklisted guide he had personally helped destroy.

And underneath all of it, honest and unbearable: because if he stopped now, he would have to stop being the man who had done it. And he did not know yet who he would be instead.

"Because I need to see it finished," he said. "One way or another."

Vane studied him. Then she handed the folder back and stood, and for a moment her silhouette blotted out the fire entirely.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the day you decide which direction you're actually marching—I'd rather be told before the others find out." She walked back to her bedroll, and that was the end of it, because she was a sergeant and sergeants understand that some decisions ripen like fruit and can't be picked green.

Reiss did not sleep. Near midnight the storm arrived in earnest, and he sat at the mouth of the overhang watching rain turn to sleet, and he made himself look at the last document in the folder one more time, because he was a thorough man and thorough men finish what they start.

It was the oldest item. A single page, undated, on letterhead he recognized from his earliest days in the service—the provisional authority, the transition government, the months after Callowmere fell. It was a directive concerning "residual cultural materials" in the former kingdom: archives, records, seals, instruments of state. The directive ordered such materials secured and removed to Directorate custody. And at the bottom, in a list of specific items to be recovered, was a line that made the sleet seem suddenly warm against his face:

*Item: navigational instrument, brass, of unusual manufacture, property of the royal house. Instrument is believed to indicate position of designated custodian. Recovery priority: absolute.*

*Designated custodian.*

Not heir. Custodian. As if the child in the photograph—as if whoever carried that blood—had been filed under logistics. An asset to be retrieved, or, failing retrieval, retired. Twenty years the directorate had hunted a compass and the person it pointed to, and the whole machinery of the borderlands—every checkpoint, every blacklist, every burned village rumor and erased map—had been built around that single absolute priority.

And Kestrel Amberdale, who knew the eastern approaches better than any living man, had pulled the compass out of a river.

Reiss sat with that until the sleet became snow and the snow began to muffle the world. Somewhere north, over the saddle, in weather that would kill unprepared men, four fugitives were climbing toward a truth that Reiss's own superiors had murdered four people to bury. His orders said bring back the chest and manage the bearers. His orders had always said things like that, in words like those, and he had always read them the way soldiers read them, as weather—as conditions to be endured rather than choices to be made.

But the needle doesn't point north, he thought. North is a convention. North is where everyone agreed to look so no one would look anywhere else.

He thought about Kestrel at nineteen, at the academy exchange year, teaching Reiss to read star paths because Reiss couldn't hold a heading to save his life. *You don't steer by where you've been,* Kestrel had said, laughing, tapping the chart. *You steer by the fixed point you trust.* And Reiss had trusted the directorate instead, for twenty-two years, and the directorate had turned out to be a false north all along.

Toward dawn, Vane emerged from the overhang and stood beside him without speaking. The snow had thinned to flurries. East, below the cloud deck, the sky was going the color of old pewter, and the ridge line of the saddle stood out black against it, sharp as a blade's edge.

"They'll cross today," she said. "In this visibility, we could take them on the descent. Clean. Over fast."

"We could."

"But?" 

Reiss watched the ridge. He thought about the requisition order, the pitons, the four climbers spent like ammunition. He thought about a boy's laugh on a chart table, and a word—*custodian*—written in a bureaucrat's hand, and a promise he had made to no one, in the dark, three nights ago, that he would not sign another name to another lie.

"Sergeant," he said, "when we reach the saddle, I want the squad dispersed wide. Skirmish interval. No contact unless they're armed first—and even then, warning shots. We're not taking anyone. We're going to watch, and we're going to learn what they're carrying, and then I'm going to decide something I should have decided twenty-two years ago."

Vane was quiet for a moment. Then: "And if headquarters asks why we didn't engage?"

"Then headquarters," Reiss said, "will have to explain to me why a requisition for saboteurs' pitons was signed by the Deputy Director of Internal Security—whose signature, incidentally, appears on my own field orders for this operation."

He saw it land in her face, the full weight of it, the whole inverted world: the hunter's leash and the hunter's master held by the same hand. Vane exhaled slowly, and something in her shoulders changed—the smallest loosening, a soldier stepping off a path she'd been marched down for years.

"Skirmish interval," she said. "Wide. No contact." She turned to wake the others, then paused. "You know what happens to us if this goes wrong."

"I know what happened to Kestrel Amberdale when it went wrong for him," Reiss said. "I'd rather share his road than keep mine."

Below them, far down the southern slope, a thread of smoke rose from a hollow out of sight—small, human, defiant. Someone down there was boiling water, warming hands, getting ready to climb. Reiss watched it for a long moment, this thin gray signature of the people he'd been sent to destroy, and felt the strangest sensation of his life: hope, pointed north.

Then Petter, at the signals kit, looked up sharply.

"Sir? Priority traffic from the Director's Office." The young man's face had gone pale in the gray light. "It's addressed to Sergeant Vane. Countermanding your command. Effective immediately."
