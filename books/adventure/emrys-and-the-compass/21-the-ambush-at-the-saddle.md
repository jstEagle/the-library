## 21. The Ambush at the Saddle

Wren had learned to read weather in the gutters of Vess — the way the drains sang before a downpour, how the fishwives' awnings sagged an hour before the rain came. Mountains spoke a different language, but Kestrel had been teaching her the grammar for weeks, and by the time they reached the high saddle she understood enough of it to know that something was wrong.

It wasn't the sky. The sky was clean, a hard blue scraped down to nothing by wind. It wasn't the snow, which lay in the usual wind-carved slabs along the ridge like unmade beds. It was the birds.

There weren't any.

For three days they had climbed through country that should have been loud with choughs and kestrels — actual kestrels, which made her smile every time — wheeling and scolding around any party foolish enough to cross their air. This morning the air was empty. Not quiet. Empty, the way a room is empty after someone has just left it.

She caught up to Corrag at the head of the line. The tracker moved the way water moves, without apparent effort or decision, and he had taught her more about reading ground than Kestrel had, because Kestrel explained things and Corrag simply showed you. She tapped his shoulder twice — *look* — and pointed at the ridgeline to the east, where a scatter of ptarmigan burst up all at once, white bodies against blue, fleeing something that hadn't made a sound.

Corrag was already looking. He'd been looking for ten minutes, probably. He held up one hand — *stop* — and then made the flat gesture that meant *down*, and one by one he pressed them into the lee of a rock spine below the saddle: Kestrel first, then Sable, Father Ivo with Odell's litter strapped between them, the cartographer still gray-faced and splinted from the crevasse, moaning softly when the motion jolted him. Wren went last, as always. She liked being last. Last meant you could watch everyone's back, and watching backs was the only honest work she'd ever found.

Kestrel crouched beside Corrag and studied the saddle through the battered brass spyglass. It was a shallow notch between two shoulders of black rock, maybe four hundred paces across, the only sane crossing for miles in either direction. On the far side the trail dropped into a hanging valley, and beyond that, if Odell's half-finished map could be believed, lay the route down toward the erased valley where Callowmere still breathed.

"Well?" Sable whispered.

"Too easy," Kestrel said. He said it the way other men said *there's a knife at your throat*. "The trail's been too good since the ford. Someone wants us to take this route."

"Reiss?"

"Reiss doesn't have enough men to hold every pass. But Halloway knew our heading before we left the river, and Halloway has friends." Kestrel lowered the glass. His face did the thing it always did when he talked about the men hunting them — went smooth and closed, like a door pulled shut on a lit room. "If I were setting an ambush, I'd set it there."

"Then we go around," Sable said.

"Around costs two days over the icefall. With Odell like this—" He stopped. Everyone stopped, in the way people do when the arithmetic is bad and nobody wants to finish the sum.

"I can walk," Odell said, from the litter, in a voice like wet paper. "I've walked worse."

"You haven't," Father Ivo said quietly. "But thank you for offering."

They argued for perhaps three minutes, huddled behind the rock, breath steaming, voices low. Wren didn't argue. She watched Corrag, because Corrag wasn't arguing either. He was lying flat against the stone with his cheek nearly touching it, eyes closed, the way he did when he wanted to feel the mountain instead of look at it. Then his eyes opened and he looked at Wren, and he touched two fingers to his own chest, and then pointed at the saddle, and then made the slow falling gesture that she had learned meant *men* — not soldiers, not enemies, just *men*, bodies, weight on the ground.

"How many?" she whispered.

He held up fingers. Four. Then, after a moment, turned his hand and added three more, but with a shrug — uncertain, possible.

Seven. Maybe seven soldiers on a saddle with a clear field of fire, and six of them, one of whom couldn't walk.

"We go around," Wren said.

Everyone looked at her. She almost never spoke first in these councils; she was the youngest, the least educated, the one whose opinions ran to *which roof is easiest to steal* rather than grand questions of justice and nations. But Kestrel nodded slowly, and Father Ivo murmured something that might have been gratitude, and even Odell managed a nod.

Then Corrag touched her arm.

He shook his head. He pointed east — not at the saddle, but at the lower slopes beyond it, the flanks of the mountain where the going was steep and blind. And then he made the gesture Wren hated most, the one he used only when he was certain: both hands flat, pressing down, pressing down. *They are already moving.* Not waiting. Coming around to find them, because whoever led those soldiers was no fool either, and had guessed that a guide like Kestrel Amberdale would never take the obvious crossing.

The choice, Wren understood with a cold drop in her stomach, had already been taken away from them.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do — not like the slow-motion horror of stories but like a dropped cup, all at once and irreversibly.

They tried to slip south along the rock spine, staying below the skyline, moving from cover to cover while Corrag chose each step. For twenty minutes it worked. Wren let herself believe it would keep working. That was her mistake, and she would carry it: she started planning what she'd eat in the valley below, whether the smugglers' caches Odell remembered might still hold anything worth taking, whether Sable would finally sleep a full night now that the compass pointed somewhere real.

The first shot cracked off the rocks above Kestrel's head and spanged away into the blue.

"Down! All of you, down!" Kestrel's voice, and then the world became noise — a second shot, a third, the flat ugly echoes rolling around the cirque so that it was impossible to tell how many rifles there were, only that the answer was *more than none*. Snow kicked up in little white geysers along their line of retreat. Someone screamed — Odell, she thought, or Ivo — and then they were running, bent double, along the spine toward a notch that might or might not lead anywhere.

Wren ran the way she'd run from constables and creditors all her life: low, fast, choosing ground without thinking. Behind her she heard Father Ivo gasping under the litter's weight, heard Odell's teeth grinding out curses, heard Sable shouting something — *the journals, the journals* — and realized the satchel had come loose in the scramble, lying in the open thirty yards back, its contents — twenty years of proof, the whole reason any of them were on this mountain — half-buried in windblown snow.

She turned to go back for it and Corrag's hand closed on her arm.

For a man who never spoke, he was incredibly loud with his hands. *No. Stay. I go.* He pointed at himself, then the satchel, then made the quick flicking gesture — *fast, faster than you*. And it was true, God help her, it was true; she'd seen him move. So she did the hardest thing she had done since leaving Vess, harder than the ford, harder than any lie she'd told: she nodded, and stayed down, and watched him go.

He went up the slope in that liquid way of his, using cover she couldn't even see, and the shooting stuttered as the soldiers lost sight of him. Thirty yards. Twenty. Wren found herself counting without meaning to, the way she'd counted seconds between lightning and thunder as a child, as if numbers could keep bad things at bay.

Ten.

Corrag reached the satchel, swung it over his shoulder, and turned back.

And stood up, fully, into the open — because between him and them, cutting off the retreat along the spine, came two coalition soldiers in gray, rifles leveled, boots crunching through the crust. They must have looped wide during the first volley, exactly the way Corrag would have done it, exactly the way Kestrel would have done it. Good soldiers. That was the worst part, Wren thought later. They were good at their work.

Everything after that happened in the space of a few heartbeats, and Wren's memory kept it in fragments she would return to for years, turning them over like stones:

Corrag, not slowing. Not diving for cover. Walking straight at the two soldiers with the satchel hugged to his chest like a child, like something born in the house rather than found in a river.

One soldier firing, and missing, because Corrag had begun to zigzag in a rhythm no drill instructor had ever taught, a rhythm that belonged to hunted animals and to no one else.

The second soldier firing, and not missing.

Corrag folding — not falling, folding, the way a coat folds when it slips off a hook — and the satchel skidding free across the snow, sliding, stopping at the lip of the slope above them.

And then Kestrel, who had been frozen — Wren saw it, saw the great guide struck motionless as though the bullet had hit him instead — Kestrel coming alive all at once, screaming a wordless sound, scrambling up the slope through fire that chewed the snow around him, grabbing the satchel, grabbing Corrag under the arms, dragging him down behind the rocks while Father Ivo crawled out into the open, into the open, the mad old priest, to lay hands on the wound and press and pray and press.

The shooting slackened. From the saddle came a whistle — two notes, a signal — and the soldiers withdrew, unhurried, confident. They didn't need to finish it. There were five of them left alive on the mountain, one of them bleeding out, and nowhere to go but down into country the soldiers knew better than they did.

Or so the soldiers believed.

* * *

Corrag died in the notch below the saddle, in a hollow of rock out of the wind, while the light went long and gold and mercilessly beautiful across the peaks.

Father Ivo worked over him for an hour, and Wren watched the priest's face change as he worked, watched hope drain out of it degree by degree like heat from a cooling stone. The bullet had gone deep, somewhere that hands and prayers couldn't reach. Corrag knew it too. That was the thing that broke Wren open: he knew, and he wasn't afraid, and he spent his last hour doing what he had always done, which was talking with his hands.

To Kestrel first. A long conversation, silent, urgent, Kestrel leaning close and reading every gesture, nodding, once gripping the tracker's wrist hard enough that the knuckles went white. Wren caught pieces of it — a pointing east, a repeated gesture she didn't know, a warning of some kind, a route or a danger or both. Whatever it was, Kestrel took it in like a man taking a debt into his body.

Then to Sable. He touched her birthmark, gently, the mottled star at her temple, and then drew in the frost on the rock beside him: a circle, a tower, a gate. Sable bent close and studied it and began, very quietly, to cry, and Corrag patted her hand twice, briskly, the way you'd pat a horse, and Wren understood that he was telling her *it's all right, it was always going to be all right, go and see it for me.*

Then, last, to Wren.

He beckoned her close. Up close his eyes were astonishingly calm, brown and steady, the eyes of a man who had spent his whole life listening and had finally, at the end, gotten the last word — or the last silence, which amounted to the same thing. He took her hand and pressed it flat against the stone beneath them, against the mountain itself, and held it there, and looked at her.

"What?" she whispered. "What are you telling me?"

He smiled. It was the only time she had ever seen him smile with his whole face. Then he lifted her hand from the stone and pointed it — at her own chest.

*You. You listen now.*

"That's not fair," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle of it. "That's not — I'm the thief. I'm the one who was going to rob all of you in your sleep, Ivo knows, Kestrel probably knows, I've been waiting for the right night for weeks. You want to leave the listening to *me*?"

He nodded. Yes. Exactly that. The worst person available, or possibly the best one; he didn't seem to think there was much difference anymore.

Then his hand went slack in hers, and Father Ivo closed the tracker's eyes with two fingers, and the wind came over the saddle and took the warmth out of the hollow very quickly, the way mountains do, without malice and without mercy.

* * *

They buried him at dusk because there was no time for anything better and no ground soft enough for anything deeper. Kestrel cut the grave into the scree with an ice axe, inch by inch, refusing help until Ivo physically took the axe from his hands. They laid Corrag in facing east, toward the erased valley, toward the home he had apparently been guiding them toward all along without anyone knowing it was also his. Ivo spoke the rites for the dead in the old border tongue, the words worn smooth as river stones, and then added, unprompted, in plain speech:

"He gave more than any of us. He gave it without a single word, which is more than the rest of us will ever manage." He looked up at the darkening sky. "God keeps accounts even when governments burn the ledgers. That one is paid in full."

Nobody spoke after that. There was nothing to say. Wren had imagined this moment, in the early days, in the abstract way you imagine your own funeral — the expedition broken, someone dead, the survivors standing around a hole in the ground. In her imagination it had always been survivable, even noble, death with a meaning attached, death in service of the truth and the heir and the great machine of the story they were all telling themselves.

This wasn't that. This was a mute man who had owed them nothing, killed by men who had never learned his name, for a satchel of paper. There was no rationalizing it. She tried, lying awake that first night in the notch while Kestrel kept watch, turning it over the way she turned stolen coins: *he died for the cause, he died so the journals would reach the tribunal, he chose it, he walked into the fire on purpose.* Every version rang false. He had died because good soldiers were good at their work, and because the world was built that way, and no amount of justice at the end of the trail would make it otherwise. The truth they carried didn't fix things. It just made you responsible for knowing.

That was the lesson, she supposed. That was what Corrag had pressed into her hand against the stone. Listening didn't save anybody. It just meant you couldn't pretend afterward that you hadn't heard.

Near dawn she got up and sat beside Kestrel at the watch point. He didn't look at her. Below, in the blue shadow of the hanging valley, small figures moved — the soldiers, regrouping, patient, certain.

"They'll come up the spine tomorrow," Kestrel said. "Maybe tonight. We can't outrun them with Odell. We can't outfight them." He paused. "Corrag gave me another way. Down the east face. A route he knew. It's not on any map, and there's a reason it isn't."

"How bad?"

"He wouldn't tell me in gestures. He just kept making the same sign." Kestrel's hand shaped it in the dark, absently, the way a man touches a scar. "Over and over. I think it means *no one comes back*."

Wren looked at the grave mound, at the axe-cut stone Ivo had set at its head. Then she looked east, where the first light was finding the peaks, gilding the impossible face of the mountain that stood between them and everything they had promised a dead man.

"Then we'd better be no one," she said.
