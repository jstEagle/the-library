## 22. What We Owe the Dead

Father Ivo had buried men before. He had said the words over soldiers in the border wars, over plague dead in the river towns, over a child once, in Vess, whose mother could not stop rocking long after the small body had gone cold. He knew the liturgy the way a mason knows his stones — by weight, by fit, by the sound each one makes when it settles into place.

He had never buried a man who had no name he could speak.

The tracker — they had all called him nothing but *the tracker*, or *him*, or, in Wren's case, *our quiet friend* — lay on a shelf of flat stone above the tree line, wrapped in his own cloak because the ground was too hard to dig more than two feet down. Ivo had dug those two feet with his hands and a broken tent stake while the others stood back, because this was his work and no one had offered to take it from him, which he appreciated more than they would ever know.

The sun came up gray behind the saddle where the man had died. Blood had frozen on the rocks in a fan shape, black now, like something painted there on purpose. Ivo found himself unable to look away from it. In the border wars he had learned to look at such things quickly and then not at all, and for twenty years that discipline had held. This morning it failed him.

*He died holding the line.* That was what Kestrel had said, afterward, in the flat voice of a man rationing his words like hardtack. *He saw them coming before any of us. He put himself between the guns and Sable.*

Ivo knelt at the head of the shallow grave and began the old office, the one from before the coalition standardized prayer into something safe and government-approved. *Out of the depths I cry to thee.* The words came out of him in Callowmere's old tongue without his deciding it, the way grief finds the oldest door in a house.

Behind him, someone shifted. He did not turn.

"Father." Kestrel's voice, low. "We need to move within the hour. Reiss's squad will regroup."

"I know."

"We can carry him if—"

"He stays here," Ivo said. "This is his ground now. The mountains don't give back what they take, and neither will I."

A silence. Then footsteps retreating down the scree, careful ones, the steps of a man who understood that some negotiations are lost before they begin.

Ivo finished the office alone. When he came to the naming — *receive thy servant into thy rest* — he stopped, because the servant had no name to receive. Twenty years a priest, defrocked or not, and he had never faced that particular poverty: a grave with no name cut into it, no name whispered over it, no name carried forward by anyone living.

Except.

He remembered then, suddenly, the way you remember a thing you have spent years refusing to remember. Three nights ago, in the cave below Marrow Ford, the tracker had sat across the fire from him, and Ivo had been mending the man's bootlace with waxed thread, an old habit, hands needing work the way lungs need air. And the tracker had taken a stick and drawn in the ash of the fire pit — slowly, carefully, as though the letters might be watched — four characters of the old Callowmere script.

*Ivo had read them aloud before he could stop himself.*

*Aldric.*

The man had not smiled. But he had nodded, once, and touched his own chest, and Ivo had understood: *that was my name, once, when there was a country for names to belong to.* And then Aldric had swept the ash smooth with his palm, erasing himself again, and Ivo had said nothing, because some confidences are given in silence and must be kept the same way.

Now there was no one left who knew it. No one but Ivo.

He looked at the cairn of stones he had raised, at the frozen blood on the rock above it, and he spoke the name aloud for the first time since the fire.

"Aldric of Callowmere. Servant of God, son of a murdered nation. Receive him."

The wind took the words east, toward the valley none of their maps admitted existed.

* * *

He nearly left that afternoon.

It happened the way these things happen — not as a decision but as a weather front, moving in off the high passes until the whole sky of him was gray. They had made six miles down the eastern flank, Sable silent and white-faced, Wren walking point with a ferocity that was its own kind of mourning, Kestrel carrying double packs without being asked. Odell limped on his strapped ankle and said nothing, which from Odell was a sermon in itself.

At the midday halt, Ivo sat apart on a fallen trunk and took out his breviary, the one book he had kept through the defrocking, and found he could not read a word of it. The letters swam. Every psalm seemed written for someone else — someone whose prayers still connected to anything, whose God had not gone as quiet as the tracker's voice.

*What are you doing here?*

The question arrived in his own voice, which was how he knew it was serious. Not the coalition's voice, not the bishop who had stripped his orders with bureaucratic gentleness ten years ago. His own.

*You are an old man walking toward a ruin with a pack full of other people's grief. You buried a man whose name you knew for three days. You will bury more. The girl is going to walk into a tribunal that has no legal existence, present evidence no court will hear, and you call this witness? It is a suicide with better scenery.*

And beneath that question, older and colder: *What did your faith ever save? Not the four climbers on the Amberdale route — you prayed for them, didn't you, the night the storm took them, and the storm took them anyway. Not Callowmere. Not your own vocation. You have been a chaplain to ruins your whole life, and now you propose to make a career of it.*

He closed the breviary.

When Kestrel came to check on him — the man had a habit of doing rounds like a sergeant, Ivo had noticed; guilt wears a uniform too — Ivo said, "Leave me the map case. I'll follow tomorrow. Or I won't."

Kestrel studied him. The guide had eyes like winter water, and Ivo had seen enough souls in confession to read the current under them: this man expected abandonment. Expected it the way a scar expects pressure.

"You're free to go," Kestrel said. "No debt in it either way."

"That's a lie, and we both know it. If I turn back, I'm a loose end on a mountain, and loose ends get collected." He almost smiled. "But thank you for the fiction. It's kind."

Kestrel crouched, forearms on knees, and looked out at the valley below rather than at him. "Why did you come, Father? Really. You've never said."

Ivo turned the question over and found it hollow on every side except one. "Because a woman in Vess asked me to hear her confession, twenty years ago, and what she confessed was that she had seen soldiers burn a granary and blame the famine on the king's hoarding. And I told her to report it to the magistrate. And she did. And she was hanged for sedition within the month." He heard his own voice go flat, matching Kestrel's. "I have been asking myself ever since whether telling her the truth would have saved her. Whether truth saves anyone. Whether it's just a beautiful instrument we play while the building burns."

"And?"

"And I don't know. That's why I'm leaving." He stood, joints cracking like green wood. "Or staying. I haven't decided. Go on ahead."

Kestrel rose too. For a moment he looked as though he might say something pastoral, which would have been unbearable, but instead he only said, "Camp's at the tarn below the ridge. There'll be food in the pot either way." And went.

That, Ivo thought, watching him go, is the most Christian thing anyone has said to me in a decade.

* * *

Evening came down blue and cold. Ivo did not go to the camp. He climbed instead — not far, just up the shoulder above the trail to a shrine he had noticed that morning and pretended not to notice: a niche in the rock face, old-carved, holding a stone figure worn featureless by weather. A wayside marker of the old kingdom. Callowmere's road-gods, or its saints; the distinction had never mattered much in the high country. Someone had left an offering recently — a strip of dried meat, a copper coin — which meant the erased valley's people still walked these trails, still tended their dead gods the way you tend a parent's grave.

He sat before it as the light failed and did the one thing he had not done in twenty years of priesthood and ten years of defrockment.

He prayed honestly.

Not the office. Not the beautiful machine of the liturgy, which had always let him hide inside its rhythms the way Kestrel hid inside a borrowed name. Just the raw thing:

*I don't believe you're listening. I don't believe you listened when Aldric died or when the woman in Vess hanged or when Callowmere burned. I think you may be a story we tell in the dark, and I think the dark may be winning. But if there is anything — anything at all — that persists past the reach of soldiers and archives and tribunals, then let it be this: that a man was named Aldric, and he chose where to stand, and he stood there. Let that not be erased. I can't do it. I'm too tired and too compromised and I stopped believing in miracles around the time I stopped believing in bishops. So you do it, if you exist. Or let me do it, if you don't. One of us has to.*

The wind moved through the pass. The little stone figure said nothing, wore no face, offered no comfort, and Ivo found — sitting there in the last light, cold to the bone — that this was somehow exactly right. Of course the god was silent. Of course the god was featureless. Faith that required answers was just commerce with better manners. What remained when the answers were repossessed — that was the actual thing. The bare floorboards of it.

And what remained, he discovered, was very simple.

*Someone has to see.*

Not save. Not fix. Not redeem — he had given up on redemption somewhere on the Amberdale route, though he'd never said so aloud. See. The woman in Vess had needed one person to see what she had seen, and he had sent her instead to a magistrate, to a system, to anything but his own eyes. Aldric had needed his name spoken, and Ivo had kept it like a secret instead of a sacrament. The journals in Kestrel's chest were not arguments. They were witnesses. Pages that had seen. The compass was not magic. It was attention, fixed and faithful, pointing at what everyone agreed to forget.

The whole expedition, he realized, was a single act of seeing, walking east on blistered feet.

And a tribunal of exiles in a ruined capital — lawless, unrecognized, powerless by every measure the world used — would be the first court in twenty years simply to *look* at the evidence. That was not nothing. That might be everything. Justice, Ivo thought, may begin as nothing more than attention that refuses to look away.

He got up. His knees protested. He ignored them, an old skill.

At the camp, the fire was low and the others were turning in. Wren looked up sharply as he came into the light — she missed nothing, that one; she had the eyes of a person who had grown up counting exits — and something in her shoulders loosened when she saw his pack still on his back.

"Told you," she said, to no one in particular. "Priests are like cats. They leave, they come back, they pretend it was their idea."

"It was my idea," Ivo said, and sat down, and accepted the bowl of barley she handed him.

Kestrel said nothing. But across the fire, the guide met his eyes for a moment, and Ivo saw there what he had seen in no mirror for years: the look of a man recognizing another man's decision to keep walking.

Sable sat a little apart, the compass open in her lap, its needle steady as a heartbeat, pointing east into the dark. Toward home. Toward the archive. Toward whatever waited for them in the ruins of a nation the world had agreed to forget.

Ivo ate his barley and felt the strangest thing settle over him — not peace, nothing so clean, but something adjacent to it. Purpose with the romance boiled out of it. Work.

Tomorrow they would descend toward the erased valley, and after it the glacier, and after it the gates. Some of them would die; he was a priest, he could do that arithmetic. And when they did, he would say the words over them, and he would use their true names, all of them, even the ones that cost — and when the tribunal convened in the rubble, he would stand where he stood and see what there was to see and refuse, this time, to look away.

Witness, he thought. So that's what you were for.

The needle trembled once in the firelight and steadied again, pointing east, patient as prayer.

Above the camp, on its shelf of stone, the grave of Aldric of Callowmere held its silence under the stars — seen, at last, and named, and not alone.
