## 23. The Erased Valley

The compass had been pulling us downhill for two days, and I had learned to stop fighting it.

That was the thing nobody told you about grief — that it could be a direction. For twenty years my grandmother's stories had been a weight I carried in my chest, shapeless and heavy, and now, walking down into a fold of mountains that Odell's maps called nothing at all — a blank space he'd inked over with the words *unverified terrain* and then, later, crossed out entirely — that weight had turned into a hand on my shoulder, steering me. I didn't know yet if I wanted to be steered. But my feet had decided before the rest of me did.

We came down through the last of the pine in the grey hour before dawn, when the light is more rumor than fact. Kestrel led, because Kestrel always led, moving with that economy that made the rest of us feel loud. Wren was behind him with the pack rope, and Father Ivo behind her, and Odell — who should not have been walking at all, not with the ribs the crevasse had cracked — rode the mule with his map case strapped across his chest like a splint. The mule was named Turnip, because Wren had named her, and Wren named everything after food when she was nervous.

I was at the rear. I had been at the rear since the saddle, since the tracker — since Ashen, whose name I only learned after we buried him — had died holding a line of rocks against men with rifles so that the rest of us could run. Rear was where you put the person the whole expedition was about. Rear was where you put the thing you couldn't afford to lose. I knew that's what I was now, and I hated it and I clung to it in the same breath.

"Smoke," Kestrel said, without turning around.

I didn't see it at first. Then I did: not a column, not a smudge against the sky, but a thinning of the air itself, a warmth in the cold, as if the valley below had exhaled in its sleep. Woodsmoke and under it something else — bread, maybe, or grain drying, or wet wool. Smells that meant people. Smells that meant morning happening to somebody on purpose.

"Odell," Wren whispered, "your map says there's nothing down there."

Odell's voice came dry and careful from the mule's back. "My map says a great many things that were purchased rather than discovered." He paused. "I drew that blank myself. Twenty-two years ago. I was told there was nothing there. I was paid to believe it."

Nobody said anything to that. There wasn't anything to say that Odell hadn't already been saying to himself for a week, in the flat voice of a man doing penance out loud.

The compass in my pocket was warm. It was always warm now — I'd stopped telling the others, because it felt like something that belonged to me, one small secret in a life that had been picked apart and inventoried by strangers. Kestrel had found it in a flooded river. Wren had nearly gotten us all killed stealing bread with it in her apron. Reiss had hunted it across three passes. And all that time it had been pointing at me, or past me, or — I still didn't know — through me, toward something it wanted found. Its needle trembled now against the glass like a pulse.

"Stay close," Kestrel said. "And whatever we see down there — hands visible. No sudden movements. These people have spent twenty years learning what strangers cost."

We went down.

* * *

The valley opened the way a secret opens — all at once, and too late to un-know it.

One moment we were in trees. The next, the forest simply stopped, cut in a clean line as if a giant had drawn it, and below us the land fell away into a bowl of terraced fields, green-gold in the early light, stitched up the slopes like a hem. Stone walls. Irrigation channels flashing silver. Roofs — slate, not thatch, slate cut in the overlapping fish-scale pattern I knew without knowing how I knew it — clustered along a river that ran the color of glacier milk. And over the roofs, on a shelf of rock above the settlement, a tower. Not a watchtower. A bell tower, slender and white-washed, with a roof like a folded hand.

I stopped walking.

I had seen that tower before. Not in life. In the dream — the one that had come to me since I was six years old, the one I'd never told anyone except my grandmother, and she had gone so still when I described it that I'd thought I'd said something forbidden. A white tower above green fields. A bell that rang in a pattern, not a peal. Water that ran uphill in channels before it ran down. I had woken from that dream with my cheeks wet for years and no idea why, and my grandmother had held me and said, in our kitchen in Vess, in a whisper, *Sable. Sable. Where do you see it?* And I'd said *I don't know, it's nowhere,* and she'd closed her eyes and said, *Nowhere is a place too.*

"Saints," Wren breathed beside me. "It's real. The whole — it's a whole town. It's a whole *town* that isn't anywhere."

"It's Callowmere," I said.

My voice came out strange. Flat. Like someone else's.

Kestrel looked back at me. He'd been doing that more lately — looking at me as if I were a route he was reading, checking for weather. "You're sure?"

"I've never been here." I heard myself say it carefully, the way you step across ice. "And I've been here every night of my life."

Nobody laughed. Nobody said *dreams aren't places.* Father Ivo took off his hat, which was the closest thing to agreement a priest could manage without committing to a miracle.

The bell rang.

Not the way bells ring in Vess — one great bronze complaint, tolling the hour. This was a pattern. Three notes, a pause, two notes, a pause, one held long. It rolled down the valley and up the terraces and into my chest, and my whole body answered it before my mind did, the way a dog answers a whistle, and I understood with terrible clarity that I was not hearing this bell for the first time. I was *recognizing* it.

The compass needle in my pocket stopped trembling and lay still as a dead thing — still, and satisfied.

"They've seen us," Kestrel said quietly.

They had. Figures on the terraces, still as herons. A man on the tower's gallery with something long and dark that might have been a rifle and might have been a rake. Doors closing along the nearest lane, unhurried, deliberate — not panic, but procedure. These people had drilled for us. For twenty years they had rehearsed the moment when strangers came down out of the trees, and now the rehearsal was over.

A voice called up the slope in a language I did not know and understood anyway — the way you understand a song from before you could talk. It was a challenge. It was also, underneath the challenge, a question, and the question was: *who are you, and what did you carry here?*

Kestrel raised both hands, palms out. "We come without soldiers," he called back in the border tongue. "We come with proof. We come with her."

He said *her* and every figure on the terraces turned, all at once, like a field of flowers finding the sun.

I wanted to run. I wanted to be back in the foundry in Vess with the hammers and the heat and my name that was just a name, Sable, girl who quenches iron, girl who is nobody's anything. I wanted my grandmother, who was four years dead and had known — she had *known*, she had sewn a birthmark-shaped charm into my first coat and told me never to show it to census men, and I had thought it was superstition, I had thought it was an old woman's fear, and it had been a map. She had been a map all along and I had let her die in a rented room thinking I'd never read her.

"Sable." Wren's hand found mine. Wren, who had attached herself to me in chapter one of this disaster because she'd thought I was a meal ticket, and who now gripped my fingers like I was the last solid thing on the slope. "Hey. Whatever happens down there — you're still you. The compass didn't make you. The bell didn't make you. All right?"

"All right," I said, and did not believe her, and loved her for saying it.

We went down into the valley.

* * *

They met us at the first terrace wall, and they were not what I had braced for.

I had braced for soldiers, or ghosts, or a starving remnant. Instead there were perhaps thirty people, and they looked — fed. Looked, in fact, like people who had spent twenty years getting very good at a very hard thing. An old woman at their center stood straight as a pike in a coat of undyed wool, her hair braided in the crown-pattern that made my hands ache with recognition. Beside her a younger man held the long thing, which was a rifle, old and oiled and loved. Behind them, children — there were *children*, whole rows of them, born in this nowhere, staring at me with the frank hunger of the young looking at a story walk out of its book.

The old woman spoke, and the young man translated into the border tongue, though I suspected the translation was for our benefit only.

"She asks," he said, "what you bring."

"Journals," Kestrel said. "Eleven of them. Written by the men who watched Callowmere be murdered and wrote down who held the knife. And a map being redrawn." He nodded to Odell on the mule. "And the heir."

The word landed on the terraces like a stone into still water. Ripples went through the crowd — a woman's hand flying to her mouth, a man gripping a wall, a child asking loudly *what's an heir* and being shushed in the old language.

"I'm not—" I started.

The old woman cut me off, not with words but with a look, and then she crossed the terrace, slow, deliberate, and stopped an arm's length away. Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and some herb I knew from my grandmother's kitchen, from a tin that lived above the stove and was never named. She looked at my face for a long time. Then her eyes went to my left hand, and I understood, and I hated that I understood, and I pulled off my glove anyway.

The birthmark. The splash of dark pigment across the base of my thumb and wrist, the shape my grandmother said was a *river and a mountain*, the shape I'd hidden under foundry gloves for nine years.

The old woman went down on her knees in the mud of the terrace path.

The whole settlement went down with her. Thirty people, folding to the earth in the fish-scale shadow of a tower from my dreams, and the sound they made was not cheering. It was something older and worse — a low, collective exhale, like a wound being unwrapped after twenty years, and I stood there with my bare hand in the cold air while strangers wept at my feet, and I thought: *this is what it costs. This is what it costs to be a country.*

"Get up," I said. My voice cracked. "Get *up*, all of you, I'm a foundry worker, I quench iron, I — I'm not —"

"You are," the old woman said, in the border tongue, in a voice like a door opening after a long winter. "You are, child. We kept the words alive so there would be someone to say them to. We kept the bell. We kept the fields. We kept *you*, or the hope of you, and now here you are with river-water on your wrist and your grandmother's face." She rose, joints cracking, and took my bare hand in both of hers. Her palms were a farmer's palms, ridged and hard. "My name is Maren. I was born in Callowmere. I have buried three children in this valley so that a fourth could be born free. Tell me your name."

"Sable," I said. "Just — Sable."

"Not just," she said. "Never just. But Sable will do, for now. Names are like bread. They rise."

* * *

They fed us. Of course they fed us. It was the first thing a besieged people do — feed the stranger, watch what he reaches for, watch what he wastes. We sat in a long hall with a smoke-blackened ceiling and ate barley and river fish and bread that tasted the way my grandmother's stories sounded, and around us the settlement went about its morning with the studied calm of people pretending a miracle hadn't happened before breakfast.

I watched them, and they watched me, and between the watching I learned the valley the way you learn a face.

The irrigation channels ran uphill — not magic, just engineering, a ram pump of wooden valves that Kestrel studied for a long silent minute and then said, quietly, "Callowmere built things like this. Before." Before. Before the famine. Before the border disaster. Before the coalition's engineers opened the sluices and the passes and let a kingdom bleed out on paper while the world's maps were paid to look away.

The children learned the old language in a schoolroom behind the bell tower, and through the window I heard them reciting something — not letters. A list. Names, I realized. A litany of the dead, or the lost, or both, recited every morning so that no one would be forgotten twice.

And in the corner of the hall, an ancient man sat by the fire with a slate, and on the slate, in careful chalk, he was drawing the fish-scale roof pattern, over and over, and beside each drawing a word. When he caught me looking he held up the slate. The word was *home*. In Callowmere script. He pointed at the word, then at me, then at his own chest, and his eyes were wet, and I could not look at him anymore after that.

"They've been waiting," Father Ivo said beside me, low. He said it not with wonder but with a kind of grief. "All this time. A whole generation, keeping a grave and a cradle in the same room."

"That's not waiting," Wren said, around a mouthful of bread. "That's stubbornness. Best kind." She looked around the hall, and I saw her doing what Wren did — pricing everything, exits and stores and choke points — but slower than usual, and softer, like the arithmetic kept coming out wrong. "Twenty years," she said. "No maps. No roads in. No roads out. They just... decided a country doesn't die if you refuse to bury it."

"It dies anyway," said Odell, from the end of the bench. He had not eaten. His ribs were bad and his face was grey and his voice was the flattest I'd ever heard it. "It dies every day a little. I know. I held the shovel." He looked at his hands. "This isn't a country. It's a wound that learned to farm."

"Odell," I started.

"No." He shook his head once, sharp. "Don't comfort me. I drew the blank space. I took the money. Every one of these people is a line I erased, and they fed me their barley anyway, because that's what Callowmere does — it feeds the man who buried it." He pushed the bread away and looked at me, and his eyes were the worst thing in the room. "Which is why you can't stay."

The hall went quiet around us. Not silent — a working settlement is never silent — but the talk lowered, the way a room lowers when it senses a wound being probed.

"Odell," Kestrel said, a warning in it.

"He needs to hear it, and she needs to hear it, and so does everyone in this valley who looked at that girl today like the war was over." Odell's voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "The compass pointed here. Fine. But the journals — the *proof* — that's not for this valley. That's for the world. For courts, for tribunals, for whatever's left of the idea that nations can be murdered and someone can be made to answer for it. And the moment that proof moves, soldiers move with it. And soldiers move *toward* the people who sheltered it." He laid his hand flat on the table, gently, like setting down something breakable. "She walks out of this valley carrying those journals, or this valley is a target. There is no version where she stays and the valley stays safe. I erased this place once. I know exactly what it takes to do it again."

Nobody spoke. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, the bell rang the mid-morning pattern — three, pause, two, pause, one held long — and my chest answered it again, traitor that it was.

Maren had come into the hall without my noticing. She stood at the far end, and I realized she had probably heard every word, and that she had let Odell finish, because that too was what Callowmere did — it let the man finish.

"You are right," she said simply. "All of it. We have known for twenty years that the day the proof moved, we would pay for it. We built the granaries deep. We built the paths that only we know." She came down the hall and stood over me, and put her hand — the farmer's hand, the hand that had buried three children — on my shoulder. "But hear me, foundry girl. We did not survive twenty years in a hole in the world so that the truth could stay in it with us. A secret kept in a valley is a seed that never meets the sun. It rots. It rots, and then the last of us dies, and the bell rings for no one." Her grip tightened. "So yes. You will carry it out. And yes, soldiers will come. And we will be here, and we will be ready, and we will be *Callowmere*, which is the only thing we have ever been able to be." She looked around the hall, at the ancient man with his slate, at the children, at the litany still faintly recited behind the bell tower. "But that is a reckoning for the elders, and for you, and it begins at moonrise. Until then—" and here her voice changed, softened, became almost young, "—come and see the tower. You've been dreaming of it since you were six. It's time you saw the inside."

I stared at her. "How do you know that?"

Maren smiled, and it was my grandmother's smile, and I nearly went to pieces on the bench.

"Because, child," she said, "the compass has been pointing here for twenty years. And every dreamer it has ever dragged across the mountains has stood in this hall and asked me the same question, in the same voice." She held out her hand. "Come. There is something at the top of the tower that your grandmother's grandmother carried up the night the capital burned, and it has been waiting for you longer than you have been alive."

I took her hand. Behind me, Wren said something to Kestrel — I heard my name in it, and *careful*, and *she's not ready* — and Kestrel answered, low: "No. But ready isn't a place. It's a direction."

The bell rang as we crossed the yard, three and two and one held long, and this time I rang with it, all the way up the tower stairs, into the light.
