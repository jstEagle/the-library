## 24. The Elders' Reckoning

The valley announced itself the way a held breath announces release.

One moment we were descending through cloud and scree, the compass steady in Sable's palm, and the next the fog tore open like old cloth and there it was below us: terraced fields stitched into a slope that no map admitted existed, slate roofs the color of the mountain itself, woodsmoke rising straight up in the windless morning. A river ran silver through the middle of it, and along the river stood houses built of stone and timber with carvings over their doors — spirals, mostly, and one repeated mark I recognized before I understood why my chest had gone tight.

The seal from the chest. The crest of Callowmere, cut into lintels, worn smooth by twenty years of hands touching them on the way in.

"Nobody's found this," Wren said beside me. Not a question. She'd said little since Marrow Ford, since the tracker's grave high on the saddle, and when she did speak her voice had changed — flatter, harder, like a blade laid down flat so it wouldn't cut by accident.

"That's the point of it," I said.

Sable hadn't spoken at all. She stood at the edge of the overlook with the compass open in both hands, and the needle didn't waver, didn't tremble, just pointed down into the valley with the certainty of a thing finally home. She was crying without any of the noise of crying. I'd learned that about her in the stormbound cave: she grieved the way stone weeps water, slow and invisible, everything absorbed and nothing shown until you noticed the whole surface was wet.

"It's real," she said. "My grandmother's stories. It's all real."

Below us, someone had seen us. Figures were moving between the fields, unhurried but purposeful, and two of them turned toward the terraces where an older man had stopped straightening up from his planting to look our way. Even at this distance I could see he wasn't afraid. That frightened me more than armed men would have. People who live hidden for twenty years learn fear like a language. This man had apparently decided, somewhere long ago, that fear was a dialect he'd stop speaking.

"We should go down open-handed," Father Ivo said. He'd washed the trail dust from his collar somewhere — I never learned how he managed cleanliness out of nothing — and he looked less like a defrocked priest than he had in weeks. "And we should be honest about what we've brought them."

"What do you mean?" Sable asked.

Ivo looked at me, and I took the question because it was mine to take.

"I mean," I said, "that for twenty years this valley has survived by not existing. And we're carrying proof that it exists. Journals. A map Odell bled over. Testimony from a coalition agent." I nodded back up the trail, toward where Reiss walked ten paces behind us, unarmed, watched by everyone. "We're not bringing them news. We're bringing them a decision."

Nobody answered that. There wasn't anything to answer with.

* * *

They met us at the valley floor, six of them, and the eldest was a woman named Maren Voss who walked with a stick she clearly used as an opinion rather than a support. She looked at each of us in turn the way a mason looks at a wall — checking for cracks — and lingered longest on Sable.

"You have Ondrej's jaw," she said. "And the birthmark. We knew the line survived. We prayed it stayed lost."

"You knew?" Sable's voice cracked on the second word.

"We hoped," Maren corrected. "Hoping is safer than knowing. Knowing makes you do something about it." Her eyes went to the pack where the journals rode, wrapped in oilcloth, and something moved behind her face — recognition, grief, and under both of them a kind of weary anger that had been aging in the dark for two decades. "You'll come to the meeting house. All of you. Even him." A tilt of the stick toward Reiss. "Especially him. If he's going to testify, we'll want to look at him while he does it."

* * *

The meeting house had been a granary once, or a temple — the carved beams overhead still showed wheat sheaves and seven-pointed stars beneath newer, plainer timber. Benches lined the walls, and every bench was full. Word traveled fast in a valley of three hundred people; word was probably the only thing that traveled fast there. They had lit lamps against the afternoon gloom, and the light fell yellow on faces that shared a family resemblance across the whole room, the way isolated communities do — the same strong noses, the same dark eyes, a nation compressed into one watershed and breeding true.

Maren sat at the head with four others she introduced only as "the elders," which told me they'd earned the title rather than inherited it. When we'd been seated — Reiss deliberately at the far end, alone, a fact nobody pretended not to notice — Maren nodded to me.

"The man called Emrys will speak first. Though I suspect that is not his name either."

It wasn't a trap. It was courtesy, of a kind — an acknowledgment that everyone in this valley lived under some arrangement with the truth. Still, I felt the old reflex rise, the smooth slide into the borrowed name, the practiced shrug of a man with no history. Twenty years of habit. I looked at Sable, who was watching me with the expression she'd worn in the cave when she asked what I'd do when hiding stopped working, and I set the reflex down like a heavy pack.

"My name is Kestrel Amberdale," I said. "I was a guide on the northern ranges. My name is on a coalition blacklist for a disaster I didn't cause, and I came here following a chest I pulled out of a river. That's the short version."

"And the journals?"

I unwrapped them and passed them forward, hand to hand, down the row of elders. I watched Maren open the first one and go very still.

"These are Halric Dane's hand," she said quietly. "He was chancellor of the archive. He got out with eleven crates the night the palace burned. We thought the crates were lost at the border." She looked up. "Where was this chest?"

"In the Vess river. Flooded out of wherever it had been hidden. Wedged in the shallows."

"Twenty years in the water," one of the other elders murmured, "and it surfaces now. On its own schedule."

Maren read. The room waited. I have sat through storms, through avalanche watches, through the long gray hours of waiting to learn if men buried in snow are alive, and none of it matched the silence of that room while an old woman read proof of a murder committed against everyone in it.

She closed the journal at last and laid her hand flat on the cover, the way you'd quiet a living thing.

"The famine was engineered," she said. "We always knew. The grain requisitions in the last year weren't incompetence — the quotas rose exactly as the harvests fell, and the roads out were closed by decree the same week the stores emptied. You cannot starve a kingdom by accident. But knowing and proving are different countries, and we have never had passage between them." She looked around the benches. "This is passage."

An old man near her — I later learned he was Tam Voss, her brother — spoke for the first time. "Then the question is whether anyone survives the journey."

"There are courts," Father Ivo began. "Tribunals in the lowland capitals, treaty bodies—"

"Treaty bodies," Tam repeated, tasting the words. "Signed by the same coalition that signed the famine order. You'd carry the truth to the murderers' own courthouse and ask them to convict themselves." He shook his head slowly. "Father, we have heard twenty years of stories about the world outside. In every one of them, Callowmere is a cautionary tale about what happens to nations that stand in the way of the border compact. The truth is not a weapon they've left lying around unguarded."

"Then why keep the archives at all?" Wren said. She was leaning forward on the bench, arms on her knees, a street argument coming even here. "Why carve your seal over every door if you're just going to sit on it? Either you're Callowmere or you're a rumor."

The silence after that had weight. Maren studied Wren for a long moment, and something almost like approval crossed her face.

"Because a nation is not its government," she said. "Callowmere is these fields. These children. The words we still teach them. The archives stay because forgetting is the second death, and we refuse to die twice." She turned back to me. "But you're asking us to risk the first death to prevent the second. Say it plainly, guide. What is it you want?"

What did I want? I had asked myself versions of that question since the river, and I'd never once given an honest answer. I thought about Halloway dead on a ridge road rather than give up our route. About Odell mapping with a shattered arm because a map restored was a life restored. About Reiss walking into this valley unarmed, carrying his confession like a man carrying his own execution order.

"I want the people who signed those orders to answer for them," I said. "In front of witnesses. Publicly. I know what it costs. I'm not pretending I don't."

"Do you?" Tam leaned forward. "Because I don't think you do. Let me make it concrete for you, guide. Soldiers came looking for this valley eleven years ago. A survey party, they said. Mapping the headwaters. We watched them from the ridgelines for nine days while they blundered around the wrong drainage, and then they gave up and went home. Nine days. That's how close it was — one good tracker among them, one lucky turn up the correct gorge, and there would be no meeting house, no fields, no children learning the words." His voice didn't rise; it lowered, which was worse. "Now imagine they come again. Not nine surveyors. Nine hundred. With the reason to find us written in their own archives, carried out of here in your packs. Every farm burned is a farm we cannot rebuild, because there is nowhere left to rebuild it. You would not be delivering justice. You would be delivering the address."

"They already have the address," Sable said.

Every head turned. She had been silent through all of it, the compass closed in her fist, and now she stood, and I saw the room take her in — the jaw, the birthmark, the bloodline walking upright in front of them.

"The coalition erased Callowmere from the maps because erasing it on paper wasn't enough. They starved it, they burned it, and then they spent twenty years making sure no one said its name. Do you think that stops? Do you think they've forgotten? They haven't forgotten. Forgetting is what they're afraid of." Her voice shook and she let it. "My grandmother died telling forbidden stories in a rented room in Vess. She never saw a field like yours. She never touched that seal." She opened her hand, showed them the compass. "This has been pointing at this valley — or whatever it points to beyond it — since before I was born. I didn't choose any of it. But I'm done letting other people decide what my grandmother's death was worth."

Maren looked at her for a long time. Then, softly, in a language I didn't know but understood anyway, she said something brief and old, and half the room answered it, and I realized I had just witnessed a prayer and a coronation argument happening in the same breath.

"You sound like your grandfather," Maren said at last, in trade tongue again. "Ondrej was stubborn past the point of survival too. It's genetic, apparently." She exhaled. "Here is our reckoning, then, and hear all of it. The elders do not forbid you to leave. We have no right, and forbidding would only prove Tam's point about impotence. But understand what you carry. Those journals are not paper. They are the match and the whole valley is the kindling. If the truth goes out and takes root in the courts, then everything suffered here finds meaning. If it goes out and dies there — dismissed, sealed, buried under procedure — then the soldiers will come anyway, because failed conspiracies burn evidence, and we are the evidence." She spread her hands. "There is no version of this where we remain safe. There is only a version where it means something."

Tam stood. "So we put it to you plainly, heir. Stay. Lead us. Build the nation here, in the only ground it has left, and let the dead kingdoms rest. Or walk out of this valley and pull every soldier in the borderlands down onto the last free Callowmere soil. Choose knowing that whichever door you open, we all walk through it behind you."

The lamps hissed. Somewhere outside, the river kept saying the only thing it ever says.

Sable looked at me. Not for permission — she'd made it plain in the cave that she was finished with permissions — but the way you look at someone who has walked a hard route before you, checking the footing. And I understood, with a clarity that felt like cold air at altitude, that whatever she chose, I had already chosen. Back in Vess, when Halloway offered to wipe my debt for the chest, I had refused without knowing why. Now I knew. Some debts you don't get to decline. You just pick which direction you pay them in.

Sable closed her fist around the compass and turned to the elders.

"Justice that stays in a valley isn't justice," she said. "It's just a secret with better manners. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry for what it costs you. But I won't lead a nation in hiding. I'll carry it out."

Maren closed her eyes. Tam looked at his sister, and his sister looked at the ceiling beams where the wheat sheaves were carved, and when she opened her eyes again they were dry and terrible and calm.

"Then you leave tomorrow, before first light," she said. "And you'll need more than courage, because I can tell you what's coming. Three days ago a coal train stopped at the far side of the pass. Unusual — that line carries nothing this season. Last night our watchers counted fires on the ridge above the north gorge. Campfires, laid in military pattern." She let that settle over the room like a dropped stone. "Someone knows the valley exists again. Whatever brought you here, heir, it brought them too. So you had best decide tonight exactly what you're willing to become, because by the week's end, every one of us will know."

Behind me, at the end of the bench, Reiss stood up slowly, and the sound of his voice — the first words he'd spoken inside these walls — was quieter than the lamps.

"I know that pattern," he said. "That's not a search party. That's a cordon. They're not looking for the valley anymore." He met my eyes across the room. "They're sealing it. And Kestrel — the order will have your name in it. It always does. They've been writing your name in their orders for twenty years, and I think it's time we found out why."
