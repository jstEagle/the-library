## 26. Halloway's Gamble

The map was a lie before the ink dried.

Halloway knew this the way he knew the weight of a ledger, the way he knew the particular silence a man made when he was deciding whether to lie to you. He had spent twenty-two years in the business of other people's debts, and he had learned that every debt was a story, and every story had a moment where it could still be told a different way. He was living in that moment now, and he hated it, and he could not stop.

He sat in the back room of the Salt & Cinder in Lowmark, the waystation two days south of the saddle where he'd put a knife to Kestrel Amberdale's throat and been talked out of using it. The room smelled of tallow and old smoke. On the table before him lay a surveyor's map of the high country, purchased from a dying man in Vess for three silver and a promise, and on that map Halloway's own hand was adding a route that did not exist.

A red line, climbing the eastern flank of the Sorrows, bending toward a pass that any real guide would have laughed at. A pass choked with seracs, according to the dying man, according to every rumor Halloway had bought in the last six days. A pass that would take Reiss and his eight soldiers three days out of their way and quite possibly bury them under a hundred tons of rotten ice.

Two days. Maybe three. That was what the lie was worth.

Halloway dipped his pen again and made the false route beautiful. That was the trick of it — he'd learned this from watching clerks forge receipts in the counting houses of Vess, back when he was a boy running errands for men who broke legs for a living. A forgery failed when it was too clean. It succeeded when it was worn, when it looked like a thing that had been carried and consulted and corrected. So he smudged the line with his thumb. He added a small annotation — *water source unreliable after midsummer* — in a different pressure of ink. He even added a tiny correction near the false pass, a crossed-out elevation, as if he had caught his own error and fixed it.

Then he sat back and looked at what he'd done, and felt nothing at all like a man.

*You're a small man, Halloway.* His mother had said it once, not cruelly, the way you'd note that a door was low or a winter was hard. She'd said it the day he'd taken his first collection job at fourteen, holding out his hand to a widow for six coppers, and she had been right, and he had built a whole life inside the truth of it. Small men survived. Small men kept their heads down and their ledgers balanced and their hands clean enough to eat with. Big men — men like Kestrel Amberdale, with their mountains and their causes and their terrible habit of saying no to people with power — big men died badly in ditches, and someone small like Halloway was always hired to inventory the ditch.

He had told himself that, in one form or another, for thirty years.

He told himself it again now, and for the first time in his life, it did not take.

* * *

Reiss arrived at dusk.

Halloway heard the squad before he saw them — the particular rhythm of coalition boots on packed earth, eight men walking like one man, and then Reiss alone in the doorway of the Salt & Cinder's common room, shaking rain off his coat, looking around the room with the flat gray attention of a man reading a ledger he already knew the totals of.

They had been friends once, Reiss and Amberdale. Halloway knew that too. He knew a great many things he wished he didn't; it was the occupational disease of debt collectors. You learned everything about a man because everything about a man was leverage, and leverage was the only currency that never devalued. He knew that Reiss had signed the testimony that blacklisted Amberdale twenty years ago. He knew that Reiss drank nothing stronger than tea on campaign. He knew that Reiss's superiors in the Border Compact's Directorate of Internal Stability had given Halloway his standing orders eleven years ago, along with a salary that arrived quarterly and a warning that arrived never, because it didn't need to.

*You are our eyes in Vess, Master Halloway. You will report on river salvage, on strangers, on anything bearing the Callowmere crest. You will be paid. You will not be noticed. If you are ever noticed, you will be disavowed.*

Eleven years of being disavowed in advance. There was a word for what he was, and the word was not *informant*. The word was *disposable*, and he had signed the paper anyway, because the paper had forgiven a debt that would have taken the rest of his life to work off, and a small man does not refuse a large kindness. A small man knows better.

Reiss crossed the room and sat across from him without asking. The soldiers took the far table and ordered nothing, which was its own kind of announcement.

"Report," Reiss said.

Halloway had prepared the report for two days. He had rehearsed it in the dark of his room, in the voice of a tired man doing a tired job. He laid the map on the table between them and turned it so Reiss could read it, and his hands were steady, and he was quietly astonished by how steady they were.

"They crossed the Sorrows here." He tapped the western flank, the true route, the one he'd promised Kestrel he'd feed to the wolves. "Moving slow. The priest is old and the cartographer's hurt — the crevasse fall, I told you. They made maybe eight miles a day."

"And then?"

"Then they turned east." He tapped the red line. "Here. Toward the Serac Pass."

Reiss looked at the line. Reiss looked at him. The lamplight moved on the agent's face and gave nothing back.

"Serac Pass is a graveyard," Reiss said. "Every guide in the borderlands says the same. The ice there hasn't moved right in forty years. No expedition has crossed it since before the Compact."

"That's what the dying man said." Halloway shrugged, a small man's shrug, practiced over decades. "But I didn't draw that line. They did. I watched them turn east from the ridge at Marrow Ford — I was two hours behind them, like you ordered. I can't tell you why a man who knows these mountains better than God would take his people up a death-trap. Maybe he's desperate. Maybe he thinks you won't follow. Maybe the compass—" He stopped himself. Careful. Careful.

Reiss's eyes came up. "The compass."

"The heir's compass. The one in the journals. Points to a fixed point, they say. Not north." Halloway let a little contempt into his voice, the contempt of a practical man for a fairy tale. "If Amberdale believes that thing, he's not navigating. He's being *led*. And a led man will walk anywhere, including up a mountain that wants to eat him."

It was good. He knew it was good the moment it landed, because he saw Reiss's jaw shift, the smallest movement, the movement of a man who had already considered the same possibility and now had it confirmed by a source he trusted. Eleven years of quarterly payments had bought Halloway exactly one thing, and it was not money. It was credibility. He was spending it now, all of it, in a single evening, on a red line drawn in forged ink.

"Serac Pass would cost us three days," Reiss said slowly. "If it's passable at all."

"If it's passable at all," Halloway agreed. "If I were you, I'd cut west and take the low route around the shoulder. It adds a day but you don't die."

"And lose a day while they gain the capital ruins." Reiss was silent a moment, and Halloway watched him do the arithmetic that Halloway had built the lie to force: *they are ahead, they are led by a compass I cannot predict, the direct route is a trap, therefore I must trust the map of a man I pay to be accurate.* "You're certain of the eastern turn. You saw it yourself."

"I saw it myself."

It was the first outright lie he had ever told Reiss, and it came out of him smooth as a coin across a counter, and Halloway thought: *so that's what it feels like.* Thirty years of collecting other people's debts and he had never once defaulted on his own. This was the default. This was the moment the ledger stopped balancing, and the strange thing, the thing he would turn over in the dark for the rest of whatever life he had left, was that it did not feel like falling. It felt like standing up.

Reiss folded the map. "Two days," he said. "If your route is wrong, Halloway — if I find them where you said they couldn't be — the Directorate will want to know why your quarterly reports have been so thorough and your field work so careless."

"I understand," Halloway said.

"And if your route is *right*," Reiss added, rising, "and we take them on the Serac, I'll note in my report that the recovery was accomplished through your intelligence. That has value. That has protection in it. You understand that too."

"I understand," Halloway said again, and meant it in a way Reiss could not have guessed, because protection was exactly what Halloway had stopped wanting. Protection was the cage. He had been inside it so long he had mistaken the bars for walls and the walls for a house.

Reiss paused at the door. He half-turned, and for a moment something moved behind the gray — something old and tired that Halloway recognized, because he saw it in mirrors.

"Twenty years ago," Reiss said, "I testified against a man who was my friend, because I was told the testimony was true and the alternative was the end of my career. I have spent twenty years being right." He looked at the folded map in his hand. "I find, lately, that I am tired of being right."

Then he was gone into the rain, and the squad folded around him, and the boots receded east, toward a pass full of rotten ice that no one was going to climb.

* * *

Halloway did not sleep.

He sat in his room with the lamp turned low and counted what he had done, the way he counted everything, because counting was the only prayer he'd ever learned.

Item: he had bought the expedition two days, perhaps three. Against eight soldiers and an agent who knew the border country like a scar, two days was nothing and everything. It was the difference between a knife at the back and a knife at the front. It was the difference between a fight and a fair fight.

Item: when Reiss discovered the lie — and he would, Halloway had no illusions, a man like Reiss always found out, it was only a question of whether he found out in time to matter — the debt would come due. Not the money. The other debt. The one the Directorate collected in rooms with no windows. Halloway had spent eleven years being invisible, and he had just, with one forged line of ink, made himself the most visible man in the borderlands.

Item: and yet.

He got up and went to the window. Rain. The street empty. Somewhere north, in mountains he had never loved and never climbed, a disgraced guide and a water-seller and a foundry girl and a broken cartographer and an old priest and a mute tracker were walking toward a dead kingdom with the truth in their packs, and one of them — the one with the blacklisted name and the terrible habit of saying no — had stood in the gray light of dawn with a knife against his throat and offered Halloway the only thing Halloway had ever actually wanted.

*Not money. Release. You want off the leash, Halloway. I can't cut it for you. But I can give you a reason to cut it yourself.*

He had thought, at the time, that it was a con. He had told himself: *this is what men like Amberdale do, they smell the leash on you and they talk to the dog.* He had walked away from that camp telling himself he'd take Reiss's silver and Reiss's protection and forget the whole conversation, the way he'd forgotten a thousand small shames, the way a small man forgets.

But the words had stayed. They had stayed the way a debt stays. And Halloway knew debts, and he knew that the only debts that ever truly ruined a man were the ones he refused to acknowledge — and that this one had been accruing interest for eleven years, since the first quarterly payment, since the first report, since the first stranger he'd fed to the Directorate's quiet machinery for nothing worse than owning a book with the wrong crest on it.

He had told himself he was a small man. He had believed it the way you believe a wall.

But walls, it turned out, were just stones that hadn't been asked to move.

Halloway took out a fresh sheet of paper. He did not write to Reiss — Reiss was beyond letters now, three hours east and climbing toward a lie. He wrote to no one at first. He just sat with the pen and thought about the shape of the next two days, and what a small man could build inside them.

Two days bought the expedition their lead. But two days was not enough, not against what was coming. He had seen the Directorate's mobilization orders pass through Vess three weeks ago — he reported on those too, had reported on them for years, columns of numbers that meant companies, that meant the border garrisons shifting like weather toward the high country. Whatever Amberdale carried out of those ruins, it would not be enough if the Compact's army arrived with him. The truth needed time. Time needed a man on the inside of the machine, feeding it wrong numbers.

Halloway knew the machine. He had been a gear in it for eleven years. And a gear, he now understood, turning slowly in the dark, could turn the other way.

He began to write. Not a confession — confessions were for men with time, and he had perhaps two days before Reiss found the Serac Pass empty and came back for him. A ledger. His last ledger. Every payment, every date, every order, every name — the Directorate handlers, the Vess garrison contacts, the standing orders regarding river salvage and the Callowmere crest, eleven years of it, in the neat hand of a man who had never once missed an entry. He wrote until the lamp burned low, and then he wrote by memory in the dark, because he had never once missed an entry.

Where it would go, he didn't yet know. To the exiles' tribunal, if there was one. To the world's courts, if any existed that would read it. To Kestrel Amberdale, if he lived, along with an apology so small next to the damage that it was almost funny. A small man's apology. A small man's ledger. A small man's first large act, committed in a rented room in a rain-soaked waystation, with ink on his fingers and soldiers on the road and no way back into the house he had built out of walls.

Near dawn, the rain stopped. Halloway sealed the ledger in oilcloth and hid it in the one place no Directorate man would ever search, because they had searched it a hundred times and found nothing but debts: the strongbox under his bed, beneath the stacks of other people's ruin, in a false bottom he had built the first year and never used.

Then he heard the horses.

He went to the window and looked down the Vess road, and his stomach went cold and quiet, the way it did before the worst collections. Riders. Not Reiss's squad — these wore the black-and-silver of the Compact's border garrison, six of them, moving fast, and at their head a man Halloway knew by reputation and by the quarterly reports: Captain Veyra of the Directorate's field section, the one they sent when a problem needed to stop being a problem.

She reined up below his window and looked up, directly, as if she had known exactly which room.

"Master Halloway," she called, in a voice that carried the way a verdict carries. "The Directorate would like a word about your reports. All of them. Come down."

Halloway stood in the gray light and did the last arithmetic of his small life. Two days. He had bought them two days, and the bill had arrived in one.

He picked up his coat, and — after a moment, after a breath — he picked up the pen as well, and the last blank page, and went down the stairs to meet the debt.
