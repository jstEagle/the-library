## 27. Reiss Alone

The telegram came at dusk, folded inside a diplomatic pouch that had no business reaching a field station this far up the border. Reiss read it twice by lamplight in the quartermaster's office at Fort Brannoch, while outside the sentries changed watch and the mountains went the color of old iron.

It was short. Coalition communications were always short; brevity, his instructors had said, was the courtesy of serious men.

*AGENT R-7. FIELD ASSESSMENT INCONSISTENT WITH DIRECTIVE OUTCOMES. RETURN TO VESS FOR DEBRIEF. TRANSPORT ARRANGED. COMPLIANCE EXPECTED.*

*Compliance expected.* Not *requested*. Not *ordered*, even — orders could be questioned, appealed, delayed by weather or wound. *Expected* was the word they used for men whose questioning days were over.

He read it a third time, though there was nothing new in it, and set it on the desk, and looked at his hands.

They were steady. That surprised him. For twenty years his hands had been the steadiest in any room — that was what they'd praised him for, back when they still praised him. *Reiss doesn't flinch. Reiss doesn't shake.* He had built a career on the stillness of his hands, and now, holding the paper that ended that career, they did not so much as tremble.

What shook was everything else.

He had known, of course. He had known since the archive at Marrow Ford, since the supply manifests with their neat columns of withheld grain, since the routing orders that showed the famine was not a failure of the coalition's benevolence but a feature of its design. He had known since he found his own initials countersigning the transfer of a certain mountain expedition's equipment — equipment that had been altered, sabotaged, returned to the field with its ropes aged and its pitons brittle, all to bury a guide who had seen too much of the border roads. He had known, and he had kept moving, because that was what he did. He kept moving. Doubt was a weight a man could carry if he never set it down to examine it.

But he had made mistakes. He saw that now, sitting in the lamplight with the telegram between his fingers. In the last two weeks he had written reports that were too honest — honest in the way a man is honest when he wants to be caught out, when some buried part of him is praying someone upstairs will read between his lines and say, *Davor, what happened to you?* He had flagged the Halloway discrepancy. He had noted, in his log of the saddle ambush, that the engagement had been *tactically unnecessary and disproportionately lethal*, which was the closest a coalition agent could come to writing *we murdered them* without the sentence being struck by a censor. He had requested access to sealed files from twenty years ago. Twice.

Someone upstairs had read between his lines. Someone upstairs had read them exactly right.

*Debrief.* In coalition usage, a debrief was where a career went to be taken apart. He thought of a man he had once processed in Vess, a customs clerk who had skimmed tariffs — a small sin, a human sin — and who had gone into the debrief room at the district office smiling and apologetic and come out two days later in a prisoner cart, his eyes already gone, already looking through the world at nothing. Reiss had signed the transfer order himself. He had not thought about that man in years. Now he could not stop.

They would be gentle with him at first. That was the method. They would sit him in a comfortable chair and call him by his first name and ask him to walk them through his reasoning, and they would be patient, so patient, nodding at everything, and the room would be warm and there would be tea, and all the while the door would stay shut and the questions would get smaller and smaller until they were asking him about a single afternoon twenty years ago, a single signature, and then they would stop asking and start telling, and by the time they were done telling, Davor Reiss would be whatever they needed him to be. A confession. A counter-testimony. A corpse with paperwork.

Or they would skip the chair altogether. The telegram said *transport arranged*. Arranged by whom? He checked the dispatch codes and felt the floor tilt: the transport was not coming from the district office. It was coming from the Interior Directorate. From the section that did not file reports, because its reports were the things that happened to other people.

He had spent twenty years learning what the coalition did to men. He had simply never believed it would be done to him. That was the arrogance of the faithful — he had thought his service was a wall, when all along it had been a queue.

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat in his quarters with the door barred — a formality; if the Directorate wanted him, a bar on a door was a courtesy they would extend to him out of respect for his record, right up until the moment they didn't — and he laid out his life on the bunk like a map he was reading for the first time.

The boy from the river district in Callowmere's capital, who had watched the kingdom starve and been told it was mismanagement, and believed it, because believing it was easier than the alternative. The young man who had crossed the border when the maps changed, who had been given a new language for old things: Callowmere became *the disputed territories*, refugees became *unregistered persons*, the famine became *the agricultural disruption*. The recruit who had been so good at the work because the work required exactly what he had: a talent for order, a hunger for certainty, and a heart he'd learned early to fold up small and put away.

And Kestrel. Kestrel Amberdale, who had been his friend when friendship was still something Reiss's heart knew how to do. He thought of the two of them at nineteen, rope-sharing on the Vess crags, Kestrel laughing at him for reading contour lines like scripture, Kestrel saying, *you can know a mountain from a map, Davor, but you can't trust it until you've stood on it* — and how Reiss had spent twenty years standing on maps, and how every one of them had been drawn by liars.

He had testified against that friend. He had signed a statement he knew — had known *then*, in the part of himself he kept folded small — was shaped like a knife rather than like the truth. He had told himself it was duty. He had told himself the expedition's failure was Kestrel's pride, and that four dead climbers needed someone to blame, and that the someone might as well be the man who survived, since the man who survived had already survived the worst thing that could happen to him. He had told himself so many things, in that folded-up voice, and the telling had worked for twenty years, and then a sealed chest had come out of a river, and the whole architecture of his telling had begun, stone by stone, to come down.

He got up and lit the stove and put the kettle on, because ritual was all that was holding him together, and while the water heated he did the arithmetic.

Option one: comply. Go to Vess. Sit in the chair. Perhaps he would walk out; the odds were not zero; his record was long and mostly distinguished. But if he walked out, he would walk out as a man who had been remade, and he would spend whatever remained of his life enforcing the lie he had finally seen clearly. And if he did not walk out — if the chair did what chairs did — then the last living man who could testify to the Interior Directorate's chain of command from the inside would be quietly erased, and Kestrel Amberdale would carry the blame for everything, forever, including the blame for things Reiss had done.

Option two: run. Desert. Go east over the passes, or south to the coastal states, and live out his years under a borrowed name in some town where nobody asked questions. He knew how that life went. He was looking at its finest practitioner. Kestrel had run that road for years and it had not saved him; it had only made him smaller, a man living in the negative space of himself. Reiss had spent two decades watching that experiment and he had learned its result: *you cannot hide from a thing you did. You can only delay the bill.*

Option three.

He did not let himself think it in words, not yet. He let it sit at the edge of his mind like weather on a ridge.

The kettle boiled. He made tea he did not drink.

* * *

At midnight there was a knock at his door. Two knocks, a pause, one more — the duty signal. He opened it to Sergeant Vael, a solid, incurious man twenty years his junior, holding a lantern.

"Transport's early, sir. Wagons at the south gate. They said to tell you — " Vael hesitated, which was unlike him. "They said no baggage. And they said to tell you the debrief officer is already aboard."

Reiss looked past him toward the south gate, where two wagons waited in the dark with no lamps lit. No lamps, on a coalition transport. No baggage, for a man being taken to a debrief that would decide his future. And a debrief officer already aboard, before the debrief had been scheduled, before Reiss had even confirmed receipt of the telegram — as if the outcome were not in question. As if the only thing being transported was the paperwork for a man who had already ceased to exist.

"Thank you, Sergeant," Reiss said. "Give me five minutes."

Vael lingered. "Sir — " He lowered his voice. "I processed the manifest for those wagons. There's no return leg filed."

The boy was frightened. Reiss saw it plainly: Vael had read the same absence in the paperwork that Reiss had, and had come to say it out loud because saying it aloud to someone else made it slightly more survivable.

"Go back to your post, Sergeant," Reiss said quietly. "You saw nothing. I was in my quarters. When they ask — and they will ask — you checked on me at midnight, I was packing, I left under my own escort. You did your duty."

"And did you, sir?"

Reiss held the boy's eyes. "Ask me again in a year," he said, "if there's a country left where the answer matters."

He closed the door and barred it, and then he went to work.

* * *

The stove was iron and old and it drew well. He fed it his service record first, page by page — twenty years of commendations, of successful operations, of men followed and files recovered and questions answered — watching the ink curl and go brown and then to nothing. It felt less like burning a history than like burning a costume. Then the telegram. Then his field journals, all of them except one: a small notebook, oilcloth-wrapped, that had lived for two weeks in a seam of his pack. In it he had written, in a cipher of his own devising that no coalition cryptographer had ever broken because no one had ever had reason to try — the chain of command. Names, dates, countersignatures. Who had ordered the famine's management. Who had signed the expedition sabotage. Who had ordered the saddle ambush. Who had signed the transport order — the one with no return leg — for Agent R-7.

Evidence was a strange thing to carry. It had weight without mass. He wrapped the notebook against his ribs, under his shirt, where it sat like a second heart.

He took his sidearm, and then, after a moment, took a second one from the weapons locker, and ammunition for both. He took the mountain kit he had never unpacked since Marrow Ford — the rope, the iron, the cold-weather shell. He had been a climber once, before the coalition taught him that mountains were terrain to be controlled rather than trusted. Kestrel had taught him the first lesson. The coalition had spent twenty years teaching him the second. He knew now which teacher he believed.

Then he sat down at his desk, and by the light of the lamp, with the stove ticking as it cooled, he wrote two documents.

The first was a full confession, dated and signed, of everything he had done: the false testimony against Kestrel Amberdale, the altered equipment manifests he had countersigned without reading closely because not reading closely was the luxury his rank purchased, the surveillance reports, the ambush at the saddle. He sealed it and addressed it to no one. It would go wherever the truth was being collected, if there was such a place. If there wasn't, he would carry it until he found one, or until he was dead, whichever came first.

The second was a short letter to Sergeant Vael, to be delivered after the wagons left, saying only: *The agent you escorted did not board. I cannot tell you more. Burn this.*

At the south gate, he walked past the wagons in the dark — close enough to hear the debrief officer's voice inside the second wagon, low and pleasant, talking to someone about the weather — and went out through the drainage culvert that he himself had flagged as a security gap in his first year at Brannoch and that no one had ever bothered to close, because the coalition's walls were always built against the enemy outside, never the man inside.

The culvert let out into the scree below the fort's western wall. Above him the stars were hard and cold. Ahead of him the mountains stood up against the sky like a verdict.

He had a head start of perhaps an hour, less if the debrief officer grew impatient. They would send trackers, and good ones. His face would be on every garrison wall from here to the coast by week's end. He was, as of tonight, exactly what he had spent his life hunting: a man with no name worth keeping, no country that would hold him, and one possession of value — the truth, folded against his ribs.

Davor Reiss stood in the scree and laughed, once, quietly, at the size of the joke. Then he turned north, toward the high country, toward the route he knew Kestrel would have to take, toward the ruins where all of this had started and where, if there was any justice left in the world, it might be persuaded to end.

He did not know if Kestrel would hear him out. He did not know if he deserved to be heard. He knew only that a hunted man's best hope was not distance.

It was arriving, unarmed, at the one place in the world where the truth was worth more than he was.

He began to climb.
