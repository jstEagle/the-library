## 28. The High Route

The glacier had a name once. Everything in these mountains had a name once; the coalition had simply decided the names were inconvenient and struck them from the survey rolls like debts written off a ledger. The maps Odell carried called it the Serac Field now, a surveyor's blandness, four syllables with no history in them. But Kestrel had learned its older name from a man who died on it, and he could not make his mouth say the new one.

*Vhalen's Tongue*, the old guides had called it — the ice that spoke, because in the deep cold of night the whole mass groaned and cracked like something dreaming aloud.

He stood at its toe at first light, breath smoking, and felt the years fall away all at once, the way snow slides off a tent fly in one clean shrug. Seven years ago he had stood on this same grey ice with six companions and a commission from the Border Survey Office, and he had been the best mountaineer any of them had ever seen. That was not vanity. It was arithmetic. He had read crevasses the way Odell read contour lines, had smelled weather two days out, had crossed serac zones at the exact hour when the sun's warmth had not yet begun to pry the ice apart. Four of those six companions had come down off the Vhalen's Tongue alive. The mountain had taken the other two, and the tribunal in Vess had taken the rest of him.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said beside him. "Where you stare at something like it owes you money."

"It does."

"Glaciers don't pay."

"This one already collected." He shouldered his pack and checked the rope coils without looking at them, hands moving through the old liturgy: knots, runners, pickets, the long steel screws they'd bought in the hidden valley from men who remembered when Callowmere's army had drilled on this ice. "Stay behind me. Step where I step. If I say *down*, you drop before you finish wondering why."

Behind them, Sable was helping Odell tighten the straps of a makeshift litter-sled they would drag across the ice — a frame of cut willow and pack canvas that let the cartographer travel flat, his splinted leg propped, his map case lashed to his chest like a breastplate. Father Ivo walked at the sled's head, staff in hand, saying nothing, which from Ivo was itself a kind of blessing. They were five now. They had been seven. Kestrel did not let himself think the number. Numbers were how mountains got you — you counted what you had instead of what you needed.

"Kestrel." Sable came up beside him, cheeks wind-burned, eyes on the white rampart rising above them in terraces of blue shadow. "You've been here before."

It wasn't a question. She had inherited her grandmother's talent for seeing straight through him.

"Yes."

"And people died here."

"Yes."

"Then why are we taking this route?"

Because every other route added nine days they didn't have, with Reiss somewhere behind them turned wolf and Halloway's false coordinates buying hours, not days. Because the low passes were watched. But those were reasons, and she deserved the truth under the reasons.

"Because I mapped it," he said. "Every crevasse, every serac, every slope angle. In another life I knew this ice better than my own heartbeat. And the mountain hasn't changed. Only I have." He looked at her, and found he could hold her gaze now, which was new. "I'm done letting it keep things it took from me."

Sable studied him for a long moment, then nodded once, the way her grandmother must have nodded over hard decisions, and went back to the sled.

They roped up at the moraine's edge. Kestrel led, then Wren, then Sable hauling the sled line, then Ivo. The ice took them gently at first, a broad highway of wind-polished blue, and for an hour it was almost easy, almost beautiful — the light coming down the valley in long gold blades, the peaks around them burning white, their shadows thrown enormous across the surface like the silhouettes of giants walking. Odell, flat on the sled, narrated in a voice thinned by pain and morphine-less discipline: *"Bear left of that medial moraine. On my old sheet there's an icefall here marked 'impassable' — I want to see if I was a coward or honest."*

"You were both," Kestrel said. "Everyone is, on ice."

The first hour was mercy. The second hour was the mountain remembering them.

The route bent east around the shoulder of a rock rib, and Kestrel felt the change before he saw it — a wrongness in the light, a hollowness under his boots, the faint drum-note of bridged snow where solid ice should have rung. He raised his fist. The column stopped. Ahead, the glacier split into a maze of crevasses running diagonal to their path, some narrow as a hand's width, some wide enough to swallow a house, all of them masked by snow bridges that the summer melt had been quietly undermining for weeks.

Seven years ago he had crossed this exact field with a man named Corvin Hale walking second on the rope. He could see Hale still — big, cheerful, always singing the wrong words to songs — stepping exactly where Kestrel told him to step, trusting him absolutely, right up until the bridge under his feet exhaled into nothing and the rope snapped taut and everything after happened very fast and ended very badly.

Kestrel knelt and drove his axe through the bridge crust, probing left, probing right, listening with his whole body. Hollow. Hollow. Solid — maybe. He probed again. Again. Then he uncoiled a length of cord, tied a stone to it, and tossed it across the gap he'd chosen. The stone skittered, caught, held.

"Wren. You're second. Watch my line and walk in my prints exactly. Not near them. *In* them."

"You've said that before."

"I'll say it again. Repetition keeps people alive."

She grinned at him — that quick, crooked grin that had gotten them through Marrow Ford and the saddle ambush and three days in a storm cave — and stepped out onto the bridge behind him.

He crossed the way he had taught a hundred clients to cross: weight slow, axe planted forward, never two points moving at once, the body balanced over the balls of the feet like water finding its level. Halfway across the first bridge he heard it — a sound he knew from nightmares, a soft structural sigh beneath the snow, the ice deciding whether to keep them. His heart slammed once, hard, against the memory of Hale falling. He kept moving. That was the whole secret of mountains and of grief: you kept moving while the ground decided.

They made the far side. Then the next field. Then the next. By midday they had crossed eleven bridges, and Kestrel had stopped hearing the old screams in the wind, which was either healing or exhaustion, and he had stopped caring which.

* * *

At noon they halted in the lee of a serac wall and ate cold food, and Odell asked to be propped upright so he could see the route ahead, and what he saw made him go quiet in a way that frightened Sable more than shouting would have.

"That col," Odell said finally, pointing with his chin at the notch between two peaks where the glacier drained upward toward the high plateau. "That's the pass your expedition was aiming for. Isn't it."

Kestrel chewed and swallowed and made himself look. The col sat in the sky like a door left open in a wall. Seven years ago he had stood below it with Hale's body cooling on the sled they'd improvised, and he had looked up at that door and understood that he could not lead the survivors through it, that his judgment — the thing everyone paid him for, the thing he had staked four lives on — had become a thing he could no longer hear over the noise of his own guilt. So he had turned them down. Down was safe. Down was retreat. Down was the decision the tribunal later used to hang the disaster around his neck: *Amberdale aborted the ascent in conditions his own notes called favorable, abandoning the survey.* As if turning back with dead men on your sled were a crime rather than the only sane act left in the world.

"That's the pass," he said.

"The survey was never finished."

"No. Two men died for half a map."

Odell was silent a moment. Then, in the mild, terrible tone of a man who has spent twenty years auditing his own sins: "When I erased Callowmere from the official sheets, I told myself maps are just tools, that ink isn't violence. I was lying, but I believed the lie completely. Is that what you did? Believe the lie that it was your fault?"

"I didn't believe it," Kestrel said. "I just couldn't prove otherwise, and a man who can't prove otherwise starts to *be* otherwise. That's how exile works. It doesn't argue with you. It waits."

Wren, who had been pretending to check her bootlaces, looked up. "And now?"

"Now the archives say otherwise. Now Reiss says otherwise. Now—" He gestured at the col, at the whole white immensity of it. "Now I get to finish the survey."

Ivo spoke for the first time in an hour. "Finishing a dead man's work is a fine thing. Just don't mistake it for resurrection. The mountain doesn't give back."

"No," Kestrel agreed. "But it can stop taking."

They moved again at one o'clock, into the steepest part of the route, where the glacier poured down from the col in a staircase of broken ice — seracs the size of chapels, leaning over the path like drunkards, held up by cold and habit. This was the hour Kestrel had dreaded since the valley, more than soldiers, more than the tribunal waiting in the ruins. Not because the icefall was deadly — though it was — but because he knew, with the certainty of a man returning to the scene of his own trial, that somewhere in this jumble was the place. The place where the rope had come tight. The place where he had cut it.

He had never told anyone about the cut. Not the tribunal, not the inquiry, not the priest who'd heard his confession in Vess and pronounced a penance of alms. When Hale fell into the crevasse and the second man, Aldous Renn, went over the lip after him, the rope had jammed in the crevasse edge and begun to saw against the ice, and Kestrel had understood with a clarity that still visited him at 3 a.m.: the rope would cut through, and when it did, it would drag the remaining three into the hole one after another like beads off a string. So he had put his knife to the line and severed it, and the screaming below had stopped, and the three above had lived, and he had carried the sound of that stopping ever since like a stone sewn into his coat.

The tribunal had never known. They blamed him for enough else. But he had convicted himself in a court with no appeal, and the sentence had been seven years of borrowed names.

Now he stood below the icefall and found the place. Of course he did. The mountain kept its landmarks the way the river kept its drowned. A leaning serac with a shattered brow, a crevasse mouth beneath it partially collapsed — smaller now, healed at the edges by seven summers, but unmistakable. He stopped the team ten meters short and stood looking at it, and the others, sensing something, waited.

"That's it?" Sable asked softly.

"That's it."

"What happened there?"

And because the storm cave had taught him that she would ask until he answered, and because the answer had grown lighter every time he set it down, he told her. All of it. The fall, the jammed rope, the sawing, the arithmetic of three lives against two, the knife. He told it in the flat voice of a guide describing terrain, because that was the only voice the story fit in, and when he finished, the wind filled the silence for a while.

"You saved three people," Sable said.

"I killed two."

"You *couldn't save* two. There's a difference, and I know it exactly, because I spent years not saving anyone. My grandmother died calling me by my mother's name and I couldn't even give her the comfort of answering to it. That wasn't killing. It was just the world being too heavy to lift. Your knife was the same. The world was too heavy. You lifted what you could."

Kestrel looked at her — this girl who had inherited a kingdom she never wanted and was learning, day by day, to carry it like a tool instead of a wound — and something in his chest that had been clenched for seven years loosened by a single degree. Not forgiveness. Forgiveness was a country he hadn't reached yet. But passage. Permission to walk through.

"Odell," he said. "Get your sheet out. We're going to do this properly."

* * *

What followed was the finest three hours of mountaineering Kestrel Amberdale had ever done, and there was no one to see it but the people who mattered and the mountain that remembered.

He led them through the icefall the way he should have led Hale's expedition — not boldly, not confidently, but *slowly*, with probes at every suspect bridge and anchors at every crux and turn-back times announced in advance and honored without discussion. He routed them along the rock ribs where the ice couldn't shift, took the serac zone in the coldest hour of the afternoon when the frozen bonds held tightest, and at the two places where the route hung over voids, he built running belays so good that a falling climber would have swung like a plumb bob and stopped within a meter. Wren handled rope like she'd been born to it, quick and economical, anticipating his calls before he made them. Ivo anchored the sled line with the patience of a man who had lowered bodies into graves and knew the sanctity of a well-set anchor. Even Odell, flat on the litter, contributed — calling angles from memory, correcting Kestrel twice, arguing cheerfully about whether a particular ice bridge had existed on the original survey ("It did." "It didn't, you romantic, that's a newer fracture—") until Kestrel realized he was smiling, actually smiling, on the Vhalen's Tongue, and let the smile stay.

Twice the mountain tested them. Once a serac calved three hundred meters upslope with a crack like the world's spine breaking, and Kestrel had them spread and sprinting along the escape line he'd pre-marked, and the debris thundered down across the route they'd vacated ninety seconds earlier, burying the old trail in house-sized blocks. Wren, breathless against the rock rib, stared at him. "How did you know?"

"I didn't. I scheduled us past that point early for exactly that reason. The mountain offers you odds. You take the good ones and don't ask for receipts."

The second test was quieter. At the upper icefall, probing a bridge, his axe broke through into air where his memory insisted ice should be. The route had changed. Seven years of shifting mass had rewritten his perfect map, and for one vertiginous moment he stood on the crust with the hollow singing under him and the old panic rising like floodwater — *you don't know this anymore, you never knew it, you were always a fraud* —

Then he breathed. Probed left. Probed right. Found the true line eight meters west, rerouted the team, and understood, standing on solid ice with his heart steady, that this was the whole lesson the glacier had been waiting seven years to teach him: mastery was never the absence of error. It was the speed and honesty of correction. Hale hadn't died because Kestrel lacked skill. Hale had died because ice moves, and luck is not a virtue, and blame is a currency the living mint to make sense of the senseless.

At the top of the last pitch, the glacier flattened into the broad white plateau that ran to the col, and Kestrel brought them onto it at dusk, in light like hammered copper, and stopped, and turned, and looked back down the route they had climbed.

From above, the icefall looked like what it was: a frozen waterfall, beautiful and indifferent, holding no grudge, keeping no list. The crevasse where Hale lay was invisible. Everything was invisible from the right distance. Perhaps that was mercy. Perhaps it was just scale.

Odell, propped on his elbows on the litter, was sketching — of course he was, the man sketched like other men breathed — and he held up the pad so Kestrel could see. A rough profile of the icefall, annotated in a cartographer's precise hand: *Route of K. Amberdale, second ascent, corrected and completed.*

"Second ascent," Kestrel said.

"You surveyed it first. Nobody who reads my sheets will ever know different." Odell tucked the pencil behind his ear. "Maps are promises, Amberdale. I broke a great many of them. Let me keep this one."

Below them, far down the glacier, something glinted that wasn't ice — a thread of metal-light in the blue dusk, there and gone, at the treeless moraine where no traveler had any reason to be.

Kestrel went still. He didn't reach for the glass. He didn't need to. He knew the shape of a following party by the rhythm of its glints, the spacing of its camps, and this rhythm was military: disciplined, patient, closing.

"They found Halloway's lie faster than we hoped," he said. "Or someone else has been climbing behind us since the saddle." He turned to the others, and the copper light made masks of their faces. "We rest four hours. Then we cross the col in the dark, on my rope, in silence. Whatever's behind us—" he looked once more down the long white throat of the glacier, where the light had flickered and gone, "—they'll want to take us on the ice. So we won't be on the ice when they arrive."

Above them, the col waited, a black notch full of stars. And beyond it, three days out, the ruins of a kingdom that had been waiting twenty years for someone to come home.
