## 29. Crevasse

The glacier had a sound, Wren decided, like a cathedral full of people breathing. Not wind — wind she knew, wind was honest. This was deeper, a slow groan under everything, as if the ice were turning over in its sleep and dreaming of the mountain it used to be.

She hated it immediately.

"Stay on the rope line," Kestrel said, for the fourth time that morning. He'd said it differently each time — flat, then gentle, then clipped — but the words never changed, and neither did his eyes when he said them. They went somewhere else when he looked at the glacier. Somewhere with bodies in it.

Wren adjusted her harness and said nothing. She'd learned on the low pass that Kestrel's instructions came pre-loaded with reasons you didn't want to hear yet.

Ahead of them, Odell was having the time of his life.

The old cartographer moved across the ice like a man walking through his own memory, which, Wren supposed, he was. Every hundred paces he'd stop, plant his staff, pull out his battered notebook, and sketch — quick, sure lines, the horizon translated into ink before the light changed. He'd been mapping since dawn, muttering coordinates, correcting twenty-year-old figures from memory.

"The old survey put the medial moraine forty meters east," he called back, not turning around. "It's drifted. Everything's drifted. Glaciers are just rivers too proud to admit it."

"Odell." Kestrel's voice carried that particular weight it got when he was about to say something he'd already said. "Rope line."

"I'm *on* the rope line, boy."

"You're at the end of it, and the end is where the rope stops mattering."

Father Ivo, roped between Sable and Wren, made a small sound that might have been a laugh or a prayer. The priest had gone quiet since Marrow Ford — quieter than his usual quiet — but there was a steadiness to him now, like a man who'd made a decision and was living inside it.

Sable walked with the compass open in her hand, though she didn't need it anymore. The needle had been pulling them up this valley for two days, steady as a heartbeat, and the closer they came to whatever waited at its center, the more Sable herself seemed to change. She stood straighter. She spoke less. She looked at the mountains the way other people looked at faces they were trying to remember.

The tracker — Tam, they'd finally learned his name, learned it too late to use it kindly — should have been walking point. That was the hole in their line now, the missing tooth. They'd buried him two days ago above the saddle, under a cairn Ivo built with hands that shook only after the last stone was placed. Nobody talked about it. Everybody thought about nothing else. Wren caught herself leaving space on her left where he used to walk, and hating herself for the habit of it.

So Kestrel took point himself, and the glacier opened ahead of them like a road someone had forgotten to finish.

* * *

They'd been on the ice three hours when the light went strange.

Not dark — worse. The overcast thinned into a flat white glare, sun behind cloud, so that shadows vanished and the whole surface became one seamless sheet of silver. Wren had seen fog do this on the Vess docks, erase the world down to arm's length. But here there was no arm's length. Here the erasure ran to the horizon.

"Whiteout coming," Kestrel said. "We rope tight. Ten feet between us, no slack. Odell—"

"I know, I know."

"No. Listen to me." Kestrel stopped, turned, and for once there was no patience in his face at all. "On the Vess trail, if you punch through a snow bridge, you fall six feet into a stream and get wet. On a glacier, you fall two hundred feet into blue dark, and the rope catches you against an edge that will cut you in half if we don't work fast and exactly right. So when I say rope tight, I mean *tight*, and when I say stop, your boots are welded."

The silence that followed had a texture. Wren watched Odell take it in — not the words, she thought, but the tone underneath them, the tone of a man describing something he'd seen done. Something he'd maybe helped dig out of.

"Understood," Odell said quietly, and shortened his distance without being told again.

They moved. The world shrank to ten-foot intervals: Kestrel's broad back, then gray-white nothing, then Odell's stooped silhouette resolving out of the glare like a figure drawn in pencil and half-erased. Wren counted her steps because counting was what she did when fear got loud. Four hundred and eleven. Four hundred and twelve.

At four hundred and thirty-one, the glacier spoke.

It wasn't loud. That was the horror of it — a soft whump, deep underfoot, like the ice clearing its throat. Then a crack ran past them, fast as a whip, off to the left, and the surface under Odell sagged.

Wren saw it happen the way you see things in nightmares: completely, clearly, and far too slowly to matter. The snow bridge — a skin of winter snow stretched over a gap in the ice, invisible in the whiteout, indistinguishable from solid ground — gave way beneath the old man's boots like a crust on fresh bread.

Odell dropped.

The rope snapped taut. Wren was thrown forward onto her knees, harness biting into her thighs, and the line went rigid from Kestrel at the front all the way back through her to Ivo and Sable, five bodies suddenly holding one falling one, and the falling one was screaming — no, wait, that was her, she was the one screaming, she realized distantly, and shut her mouth because every ounce of breath might be needed for holding.

"HOLD!" Kestrel's voice cut through everything. "Nobody moves! NOBODY MOVES!"

The line trembled. Below the broken bridge, out of sight, Odell's weight swung once, twice, and settled. The rope hummed like a plucked string. Snow sifted down through the hole where a man had been standing four seconds ago.

Wren's arms burned. Her harness creaked. Fifty feet ahead, Kestrel had thrown himself flat, anchoring the line with his body weight, boots dug in, and even from here she could see that his face had gone somewhere else entirely.

Somewhere with bodies in it.

* * *

Later — much later, when her hands had stopped shaking enough to hold a cup — Wren would try to reconstruct what happened next, and find that memory had already begun editing it, smoothing the terror into sequence. But some pieces stayed sharp as broken glass.

Kestrel crawling forward on his belly, inch by inch, testing every handhold before committing his weight, because the same hidden bridge could be under *him*. Reaching the lip of the hole. Looking down.

"Odell! Talk to me!"

A pause long enough to grow hair. Then, faintly, wobblingly: "I appear... to be hanging... in a church."

"Are you on the rope or is the rope on you?"

"I'm — my axe is set. In the wall. My weight's on the axe." A groan. "My shoulder disagrees with the arrangement."

"Can you climb?"

Another pause. When Odell answered, the flippancy was gone, and what was underneath it was worse. "No, boy. I don't think my shoulder will let me. And I don't think my arms have it in them regardless. I'm seventy-one years old and I'm sorry."

Wren heard it then, in the silence that followed — heard the ghost standing behind Kestrel's silence. Because she'd pieced the story together by now, the way you piece together anything on a long cold march: the expedition Kestrel had led at twenty-five, the four climbers who'd died, the official finding that he'd anchored wrong, belayed wrong, led wrong. She'd never known the details. But she knew this: whatever had happened up here, on ice like this, it had started exactly like this. Someone in a hole. Someone on a rope. Someone making decisions with seconds to make them.

And everyone deciding whether Kestrel Amberdale was the man who'd killed them or the man who'd tried to save them.

"Kestrel." Sable's voice, low, from the rear of the line. "What do you need?"

He didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice had come back from wherever it had been — scraped raw, but present.

"Wren. Crawl to me. Slow. Keep the rope off the lip — drape it over your staff, anything, the edge will saw through it. Ivo, anchor behind Sable, deadman the picket, bury it deep. Sable — you're our counterweight. If I go in, you hold, and you hold *low*, flat, understand? Nobody stands up. Standing kills."

"And me?" Odell called up, weakly.

"You," Kestrel said, "are going to keep talking. Anything. Tell me about the old survey. Tell me about your mother. Just don't stop."

"My mother," Odell said, "was a woman of ferocious opinions regarding turnips—"

Wren crawled.

* * *

The hole was wider than she'd expected — eight feet across, a ragged mouth punched through the glacier's skin, breathing cold up at them like a well. She reached Kestrel's side and looked past his shoulder, down.

Blue. That was the first thing. Blue going down forever, walls of ancient ice lit from within, beautiful the way dangerous things are beautiful. And fifty feet down, swinging gently at the end of the rope like a pendulum winding down, Odell — gray-bearded, one arm clamped useless against his chest, the other still gripping his axe where it bit into the wall beside him.

Their eyes met.

"Well," Odell said. "Hello, Miss Wren. Come to see the view?"

"Shut up and hang on, old man."

"That's the spirit."

Kestrel was already working. He moved with a speed that didn't look practiced so much as *remembered* — hands doing things his mind had clearly forbidden them to do for fifteen years, and doing them perfectly anyway. He built a second anchor while Wren held the lip: two screws driven into the ice at angles he checked twice, a sling threaded through, a pulley clipped in. His fingers were steady. Only his eyes weren't.

"Collapse risk?" Wren asked, keeping her voice level because someone had to.

"Lip's rotten. We can't haul from here — the load would cut the rope against the edge and drop him the rest of the way." He fed rope through the pulley, tested it, tested it again. "So we lower a loop to him, he gets his leg through it, and we haul from the second anchor, further back, away from the lip. It'll be slow. Every foot of rope has to pass over that edge protected."

"How long?"

"As long as it takes."

"Kestrel." She kept her voice down, under the wind, under Odell's distant commentary on turnips. "This is it, isn't it. This is what happened before."

His hands stopped. Just for a second. Then kept moving.

"Before," he said, "we had no second anchor. Before, the lip was worse. Before—" He exhaled. "Before, I made the call to haul fast instead of safe, because the man below was bleeding out, and fast was wrong. The rope cut. He fell the rest of the way." His voice didn't break. It just went very quiet, which was somehow worse. "Four times, Wren. I've been down there four times in my head, every night, for fifteen years. Today I get to do it right."

She wanted to say something — something about how it hadn't been his fault, how the archives had proven the route sabotaged, how Reiss's own testimony had been coerced. But she'd been poor her whole life, and poor people know when comfort is just noise. So instead she said:

"Then let's do it right. Tell me where to put my hands."

Something moved through his face — gratitude, maybe, or relief that she understood the shape of the gift she was offering. Work. Not absolution. Work.

* * *

It took four hours.

Four hours kneeling on ice in a whiteout, hauling hand over hand in a rhythm Kestrel called — one, two, *haul*; one, two, *haul* — while Sable and Ivo took turns on the counterweight and the rope sang over its protective sling and Odell rose out of the blue one agonizing foot at a time.

There were moments. There was the moment the sling slipped an inch and Odell dropped six inches with a cry and Wren's heart went with him. There was the moment, an hour in, when Odell's voice stopped answering, and Kestrel went white to the lips and lowered the loop another foot and said, in a voice like iron wrapped in wool, "Odell. Turnips. Now." And after thirty seconds, faintly: "...she believed, God help us all, that the secret of civilization was the correct preparation of root vegetables..." and Wren laughed until she cried, kneeling on a glacier, hauling a dying man out of a hole, laughing, because the alternative was screaming.

There was the moment near the end when Odell's head cleared the lip — blood-smeared, frost-rimed, one eye swollen shut — and he looked up at Kestrel crouched over him, and said, quite clearly:

"I read the journals, boy. All of them, that night in Vess, before you woke. I knew what they'd done to you before I ever signed on." He coughed. "I've spent twenty years drawing maps for men who paid me to leave things off them. Let an old coward say it plainly: what happened on your expedition wasn't your failure. It was theirs. This—" he gestured vaguely upward, at the rope, at the sky, at all of it, "—this is who you actually are. Always was."

Kestrel didn't answer. He gathered the old man out of the hole the way you gather something breakable, and held him against the ice, and for a moment — just a moment — he bowed his head until his forehead nearly touched Odell's shoulder, and Wren looked away, because some griefs are private even when they're public.

Then he stood up, and he was the guide again.

"Camp. Now. Before the light goes. Ivo — his shoulder's dislocated, maybe fractured, you're on him. Wren, hot water, melt snow, move. Sable—" He paused. Looked at her. At the compass in her hand, needle steady, pointing up-valley toward whatever waited for them all. "Sable. How far?"

She consulted it, though Wren suspected she already knew.

"A day," Sable said. "Maybe less. The needle's barely moving anymore. Like it's almost home."

* * *

They camped in the lee of a serac, a tower of blue ice leaning over them like a frozen wave deciding whether to fall. Odell lay in the middle of the tents, dosed on Ivo's last poppy tincture, his good hand still curled around his notebook even in sleep. Ivo sat watch over him, murmuring — prayer, or inventory, or both.

Wren found Kestrel at the edge of camp, re-coiling the rope. Coiling it slowly. Running his palms along its length like a man reading braille.

She sat down next to him without asking. That was their arrangement now, unspoken since Marrow Ford: she sat where she liked, and he pretended not to notice, and both of them preferred it to conversation.

"He's right, you know," she said eventually. "The old man. About who you are."

"Mm."

"Don't 'mm' me. I've watched you for two months. You're the worst liar I've ever met and the best guide I've ever seen, and tomorrow we walk into whatever that compass has been dragging us toward, and I need you to hear it plainly at least once: the thing they wrote about you isn't true. Whatever happens next. It isn't true."

Kestrel coiled the last of the rope. Set it down. For a long moment he looked up-valley, into the gathering dark, where the glacier bent around a shoulder of black rock and vanished — and where, a day away, a needle pointed at the end of everything.

"When he was down there," Kestrel said quietly, "hanging in the blue, I wasn't thinking about anchors. I was thinking: *this time*. This time the rope holds. This time everybody goes home." He shook his head slowly. "Fifteen years I've been carrying four graves up every mountain I climbed. Tonight I put down one of them. Maybe tomorrow—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to, because that was when Sable shouted.

She was standing at the edge of camp, compass in her outstretched hand, and the light was wrong — not the flat white of the whiteout but something warmer, flickering, coming from up-valley, from beyond the shoulder of black rock where the glacier bent out of sight.

Not firelight. Not sunlight.

Lamplight. Dozens of lamps, burning steady in windows that had no business existing in a valley erased from every map in the world.

"There's someone down there," Sable said, and her voice shook with something that wasn't fear. "Kestrel — there's *lights*."
