## 31. The Gates of Callowmere

The compass stopped trembling an hour before we saw the walls.

I had carried it so long that its small weight had become part of my body, a second pulse in the pocket of my coat, and when I pulled it out and saw the needle lying flat and still — not straining, not dragging, but *resting* — my first feeling was fear. For twenty years, I would learn later, for the whole length of its making, that needle had known where it was going. And now it had arrived, and it had nothing left to want.

"It's done," Kestrel said beside me. He said it the way a man says a prayer he doesn't believe in.

Ahead of us the trail dropped through a stand of black pines, and below the pines the valley opened like a held breath finally let go. I had dreamed this place. I want to be honest about that, because everything else in my life I have tried to be honest about since the foundry, and because the dream is the part that frightens me most. I dreamed it as a child in my grandmother's kitchen, before I knew the word *Callowmere* was dangerous, before she stopped speaking of it altogether. A bowl of a valley ringed in white peaks. A river the color of hammered tin. Towers. I dreamed towers, and I woke ashamed, because we were foundry people and foundry people did not dream of towers.

The towers were still there. That was the first thing. They were broken, but they were there — six of them along the eastern wall, sheared off at different heights like candles burned down at different rates, their upper stones tumbled into the streets below. The wall itself ran from the river to the cliffs and back again, a gray ribbon stitched with ivy and the pale scars of fire. Beyond it, inside it, the capital of Callowmere lay open to the sky. Roofs gone. Floors gone. Streets drifted knee-deep in the petals of twenty springs, because someone had planted flowering trees along the avenues and no one had ever been permitted to cut them down.

Nobody spoke. Behind me I heard Father Ivo murmur something in Old Callowish, the tongue my grandmother had used for curses and lullabies, and I heard Odell — who could barely walk, who had no business being on this mountain at all — breathing hard and saying nothing, which from Odell was its own kind of prayer.

Wren broke the silence, because Wren always breaks the silence, because silence is a door and she has never met one she didn't want to pick.

"Well," she said. "It's not on any map, and it's still *there*. I've been saying for years that maps are a scam."

"Twenty years," Kestrel said. "Twenty years, and the trees kept blooming."

He was looking at the gates. We all were, eventually. They stood at the end of the avenue where the river road entered the city, twin doors of bronze and black oak, taller than the foundry's smelter stack, and on them, green with age but unmistakable, was the seal.

I had slept with that seal pressed against my ribs for a season. The chest from the river, the one that started everything — the crest stamped into its lid, the crown over the mountain and the river running through. It was there on the gates, cast in bronze as big as a wagon wheel, and beneath it, in letters worn nearly smooth, a line of Callowish that my grandmother had sung to me before I could walk:

*The mountain remembers. The river carries.*

My grandmother used to end the lullaby there and tap my chest twice, over the heart. I had always thought it was just something old people did, the way they crossed themselves or touched iron. Standing in the shadow of the gates, I understood that it was a promise. Someone had made it to a child a long time ago, and the child's grandchildren had kept it without knowing what they were keeping.

"Open it," Odell said. His voice cracked on the second word. "Sable. If it's to be anyone — it should be you."

I looked at Kestrel. I don't know why. He wasn't my king and I wasn't his subject and we both knew the whole idea of such things was a corpse we'd been dragging uphill for a month. But he had pulled the chest from the river, and he had refused Halloway when refusing made no sense, and he had walked us through the low pass and the saddle and the glacier where his own ghosts had tried to bury him. When I looked at him he only nodded, once, and stepped back, and gave me the road.

The gates were not locked. They had not been locked in twenty years. They swung at my touch with a groan of hinges that hadn't been oiled since the fall, and the sound went rolling down the empty avenue like a bell tolling for someone whose name everyone had agreed to forget.

We went in.

* * *

A city that dies suddenly keeps its shape. That was the second thing I learned that morning, walking the avenue under the blooming trees. A city that starves slowly turns on itself, tears up its floors for firewood, pulls down its own walls in fury. But Callowmere had been erased — that was the word the coalition used, *erased*, as though nations were pencil marks — and erasure, it turns out, is a kind of violence that happens from the outside in. The people had been driven out or scattered. The buildings had simply been left to the weather, like a house whose family has died of fever.

We walked through streets where laundry still hung from a line, rotted to rags, the pegs still holding it. We passed a bakery with its oven cold and a wooden peel leaning in the corner. We passed a schoolhouse — I knew it was a schoolhouse because of the small chairs — and on its door someone had carved, in a child's uneven hand, the seal and the lullaby line, *the river carries*, as though even the children had understood they were leaving something behind that someone would someday come looking for.

Wren had gone quiet. That frightened me more than the ruins. Wren quiet was Wren doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"They didn't burn it," she said finally. "You'd burn a city you took in a war. This is — this is like they were *embarrassed* by it. Like they wanted it to look like it had never been lived in at all."

"That's what erasure is," Odell said. He was leaning on his staff, gray-faced, and I remembered what he'd confessed on the glacier, that his hands had drawn the maps that unmade this place. "You don't destroy the evidence. You destroy the *audience*. You move the people out, you strike the name, you redraw the roads to go around. The stones can stay. No one will know what they're stones *of*."

"I knew," I said.

Everyone turned.

"My grandmother knew. Half the foundry quarter knew, the old ones. They knew and they didn't say, because saying it got people disappeared, and because—" My voice caught. I made myself finish. "Because what was the point? Who would believe them? Who was left to believe them?"

"Us," Kestrel said simply. "Now."

The avenue ended at a wide square, and in the center of the square stood a building that even ruined was the grandest thing I had ever seen. It had been a hall of some kind — the archives, Odell said at once, his voice going strange, because he had *drawn* this city once, from the old pre-erasure surveys, and he knew its bones. The dome had fallen in on one side. The columns stood like men waiting for a parade that would never come. And across the fallen facade, half-buried in rubble, ran a frieze in bronze: the mountain, the river, the crown. The seal again. The whole city wearing its heart on its face, waiting twenty years for someone to read it.

And the compass — I had it out without deciding to take it out — the compass needle, which had lain still since the ridge, began to turn.

Not toward the hall. It turned in slow, lazy circles, left and right and left again, like a dog that has run all day and finally, at its own gate, has nowhere left to go. Round and round. It pointed at the columns, at the trees, at the sky, at nothing, at everything. Its purpose was spent. Whatever it had been made to find — a person, a place, a thread of blood and stone — it had found it, or it had brought us here to find the rest ourselves, and now it was only a piece of brass doing what brass does, which is nothing.

I held it up so they could all see. "It's finished," I said, and my voice did something I didn't authorize. It broke.

Wren put her hand on my shoulder. Kestrel looked at the spinning needle for a long time, and then he said, quietly, "It was never pointing at you. Or it was always pointing past you. Either way—" he stopped, and started again. "It got us here. That's all a compass ever does. The walking was ours."

I closed my hand over it. I didn't know yet that I would have to give it away. That was still ahead of me, in the square, in the days after — the tribunal, the choice, the strange lightness of setting down a thing the whole world had decided was my destiny. Standing under the ruined dome, all I knew was that my hand wanted to close around it and never open again, and that wanting that was exactly the kind of wanting my grandmother had warned me about, the kind that makes a person into a key instead of a door.

* * *

We found the archives on the second day, and I will tell it plainly, the way Odell insisted we record it, because plainness is a kind of reverence.

The collapse of the dome had not destroyed the vault beneath it. It had *buried* it — sealed it under forty feet of stone in a way that no soldier sent to burn records could have reached without months of work and a permission no one in the coalition would ever sign. Erasure, it turns out, has an allergy to its own evidence. They could not be seen digging here. So they had left the archive under the mountain, and the mountain had kept it, the way the mountain keeps everything.

It took us two days to cut down through the rubble to the bronze door, Wren and the mute tracker doing the dangerous work while Kestrel read the rock the way other men read weather, calling *left, no — there, the crack runs there, pull the slab now*. On the second evening the door stood open on a stair going down into dry dark, and when Father Ivo's lantern light swept the first chamber, I heard him make a sound I had never heard from a priest: a small, involuntary sob, like a man finding his own house still standing after a fire that took the whole street.

Shelves. Rows and rows of them, iron and stone, and on the shelves — ledgers. Thousands of them. Tax rolls and court records and shipping manifests and letters, the whole sedimentary record of a nation's ordinary life, filed and dated and waiting. Dust lay over everything like snow, undisturbed. No one had opened these doors since the gates closed.

Odell walked the first row with his fingers trailing over the spines, and he was crying without any attempt to hide it, and I found I could not look away from him, because this was the man who had erased this place, standing in the room where the erasure had failed.

"It's all here," he kept saying. "It's all *here*. The famine years — the grain requisitions — if the orders are here, if the signatures are here—"

"Then it's not a story anymore," Kestrel said. "It's a record."

That was the moment I felt the mourning arrive. Not when I saw the broken towers, not at the gates, not even at the spinning needle. It came in that dry dark room, in the smell of paper and stone, and it came as a strange, cold clarity: *we were too late and we were in time.* Too late for my grandmother, who had died with the lullaby in her mouth and the name unsaid. Too late for the four climbers on the glacier, for the tracker we buried at the saddle, for everyone the twenty years had eaten. And in time — barely, narrowly, by the width of a needle's swing — for the truth itself, which had waited under the rubble like a patient under anesthetic, alive the whole while.

I sat down on the vault floor among the dust of my own country and I grieved. Not gracefully. Wren held my hand and Father Ivo said the Callowish words over me, the ones for the dead, and Kestrel stood at the foot of the shelves like a guard, though there was nothing left in the world to guard it from but forgetting.

* * *

On the third morning, Kestrel came up the stair from the vault two at a time, which he had not done since the crevasse, and his face was the color of the river ice.

"You need to see this," he said to me. Only to me.

I went down. The lantern light lay on an open ledger, one of the last boxes of records, the final strata of the city's life — the evacuation years, the requisition orders, the paperwork of erasure. Kestrel's finger rested on a line of ink. I read it. I read it twice.

It was a transport manifest. It listed the families removed from the capital in the last month before the border closed, their destination, and the name of the officer who signed the order. And there at the bottom of the page, in a firm, bureaucratic hand, was a name I knew — not from the journals, not from the archives, but from a debt collector's ledger in a border town, from a man who had come to take the chest from the river.

The order to empty the capital had been signed by Halloway's principal. The man Halloway answered to. The man whose leash Halloway wore.

And beside the signature, countersigned in a different ink, was the seal of the coalition office that had also, twenty years later, signed the orders that ruined a mountain guide named Kestrel Amberdale and buried the truth of four dead climbers.

"It's the same hand," Kestrel said. His voice was very quiet. "The same office. The same *chain*. They didn't just erase Callowmere. They've been running the same machine ever since, and I—" He stopped. He looked at the ledger, and I watched a man understand that his exile had not been an accident of weather and bad luck but a line item, and I watched him decide, in the space of one breath, what he was going to do about it.

"They're going to come," I said. "When they learn what's down here."

"They were always going to come," Kestrel said. "The only question left is whether they come while this is still a secret — or after it's a *testimony*."

Above us, faint through forty feet of stone, I heard Wren's whistle — the sharp triple note she used for strangers on the approach. And beneath it, farther off, carried down the valley on the morning wind, the sound none of us had heard in a month of mountains and had hoped never to hear again:

Boots. Many of them. On the river road.

Marching toward the gates.
