## 33. Reiss at the Gates

Reiss left his pistol on the saddle of his horse, and his knife in the saddlebag, and the signal flare in the rocks where he'd cached his kit three miles back. He wanted them to see him do it. That was the whole point of arriving on foot, hands empty, walking the last half mile of the ruined causeway in full view of anyone watching from the broken walls of Callowmere's capital. If they shot him, let them shoot an unarmed man. It would at least be honest, and honesty was the one currency he had left that hadn't been counterfeit for years.

The causeway had been a grand thing once. He could read it in the stones even now, the way he'd learned to read a country from its infrastructure: the cut of the blocks, the drainage channels worn smooth by a century of boots, the iron rings where banners had hung. The coalition's engineers had mapped Callowmere's roads before they starved it. He had carried some of those maps himself, in another life, in a canvas case with a brass clasp, and he had never once thought about what the roads led to.

Now he walked one toward the people he'd helped bury.

The wind came down off the high ice and smelled of stone and old snow and, faintly, of woodsmoke — the expedition's fire, somewhere behind the near wall. His knee ached from the descent. His hands were shaking, and he let them. There was no one left to perform steadiness for.

At the breach in the outer wall, a figure stepped into the gap. Small, quick, a crossbow held low and professional. The water-seller girl. Wren. She'd tied her hair back and she stood like someone who had decided long ago that the world owed her nothing and she intended to collect anyway.

"Stop there," she said. "Hands out."

He stopped. He put his hands out. "I know you," he said. "You sold water on the Corso in Vess. Two coppers a jug, three if the buyer looked rich."

"Memory like that," Wren said, "and you still ended up on the wrong side of everything." She didn't lower the crossbow. "Kestrel said you'd come. He said you'd come unarmed or not at all. He said that like a man describing weather." Her eyes flicked over Reiss's coat, his boots, the absence of anything at his belt. "Turn around. Slow."

He turned. She went through him with brisk, impersonal hands — coat, lining, collar, the tops of his boots — and found nothing, and something in her posture shifted, not to warmth but to a kind of wary recalibration, the look of a woman whose arithmetic had just produced a number she didn't like.

"Empty," she said. "Either you're sincere or you're the best liar the coalition ever fielded."

"Both, once," Reiss said. "I'm down to the first one now. It's lighter."

"Walk. Ten paces ahead of me. If you run, I'll put a bolt in your leg and we'll talk about sincerity while you bleed."

He walked.

The capital of Callowmere opened up around him as he passed through the wall, and it stopped his breath in a way he hadn't anticipated. He had seen the photographs in the sealed files — the tribunal would want to know that, he decided, they would want to know every file he'd ever touched — but photographs were flat, and this was not. The city had died mid-sentence. A fountain stood in a square gone to weeds, its bronze figures green with twenty years of weather, still pouring nothing. A tram line rusted overhead, its wires sagging in loops. Shopfronts with their shutters down. A child's chalk hopscotch grid, faded to a ghost on cobbles, from a morning two decades gone.

He had signed the transit orders. Not the famine protocols, not the border operation — those had been above his level, deliberately, the way the machinery always kept its hands clean by distributing the dirt. But he had signed the orders that moved the census records out of this city and into a coalition archive, and he had signed them with a cup of coffee beside his elbow, and he had complained to a colleague that day about the weather.

The fire was in the shadow of what had been a courthouse or a ministry — a broad building with columns, its roof fallen in, its steps swept clean by someone recently. Sable sat on the steps, wrapped in a blanket, and when she saw him she stood, and the blanket fell, and for a moment the two of them simply looked at each other across twenty years and a ruined square.

He knew the face. Not hers — the photograph's. The child in the chest, the one the compass had been built to find. She had the Callowmere jaw, the dark hair, and when the light moved across her cheekbone he saw the birthmark, the small dark crescent that a whole apparatus of state had spent two decades trying to make sure never grew up.

"You're Reiss," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"My grandmother said that name." Her voice was level, which cost her something; he could see it cost her. "She said it the way you'd say the name of a disease. Davor Reiss, the man who signed the paper that took our street."

"I signed a great many papers," Reiss said. "That was the trick of it. No one signs the famine. Everyone signs something small."

Behind her, at the edge of the firelight, Kestrel stood up.

* * *

They had not spoken in eleven years, and the last time had been through the mesh of a tribunal anteroom, Reiss on one side of the glass having just finished testifying, Kestrel on the other, blacklisted, ruined, two weeks from vanishing off the face of the maps. Reiss had told himself for eleven years that the glass had been necessary procedure. He had told himself a great many necessary things.

Kestrel came down the steps slowly, and stopped an arm's length away, and looked at him with an expression Reiss had seen before — on the mountain, years ago, at the moment a rope came taut and a man's whole life hung on whether the other end held.

"You look terrible," Kestrel said.

"You look like a man using a dead man's name," Reiss said. "Emrys." He said it carefully, tasting the theft of it. "I pulled your file when I got to Vess. All of it. The real all of it, not the tribunal version. I've had three weeks to read what we did to you, and I want you to know I read every page standing up."

"Why standing up?"

"Because sitting down felt too comfortable."

Kestrel didn't smile. He looked past Reiss, at the breach in the wall, the empty causeway. "How many behind you?"

"None. Halloway fed my superiors a false route — the south col, I think, or something close to it. It bought you two days; you've spent one. When they find the col empty, they'll backtrack, and they'll find my burned orders in the cairn where I left them, and after that they'll stop looking for an expedition and start looking for a fugitive column. I'm the same category you are now. That's part of what I'm offering."

"And the other part?"

"The chain of command. Names, dates, signatures. Who ordered the famine protocols, who ordered the border operation, who ordered the sabotage of your expedition — because they did, Kestrel. I found the requisition trail. The piton stock your team used was recalled and replaced with brittle stock three weeks before you climbed. Someone signed that recall. I have the name. I've had it for nine days, and I've been deciding what to do with it, and the answer is I'm giving it to you. To all of you. To whatever this tribunal is that Ivo keeps talking about."

"You burned your orders," Kestrel said. "Wren told me. You went off-grid. Why?"

Reiss had prepared an answer for this. He had prepared several, ranked by persuasive force, because that was what he did, that was what he had always done — he was a man who arrived at conversations with the conversation already won in his head. But standing here, in the dead king's square, with the chalk hopscotch grid fading twenty feet away, every prepared answer died in his throat.

"Because they ordered my management," he said finally. "And I realized, standing there holding the paper, that I had signed papers exactly like it. Same font. Same signature block. Same calm little word — *managed* — doing the same quiet work. I burned mine. I never burned theirs. That's the whole of it. That's the confession. I'm not asking you to feel sorry for me. I'm asking you to use me."

* * *

They convened in what had been the archive's reading room — a vault below the fallen ministry, dry, candlelit, the expedition's salvaged documents spread across tables that twenty years ago had held a nation's records and now held its evidence again. Odell lay propped on cushions in the corner, his leg splinted, a half-finished map weighted with stones at his elbow. Father Ivo sat with his hands folded. Wren leaned in the doorway with her crossbow across her knees, ostensibly guarding the stairs, actually listening to every word. The mute tracker's absence was a shape in the room; no one sat in his usual place, and everyone's eyes touched that place and moved off it.

Reiss stood at the near end of the table, and they let him stand, and he understood that the standing was itself a condition — a man testifying stays on his feet.

He gave them the chain. He had rehearsed it on the mountain, walking alone at night, and it came out of him now in the flat, complete cadence of a man emptying a warehouse: the Deputy Minister for Border Affairs who authored the famine protocols under a food-security cover; the requisition officer who swapped the piton stock; the tribunal clerk who had steered the Kestrel inquiry away from the equipment and toward the guide's judgment; the archive annex in the coalition capital where the Callowmere census records sat in crates, catalogued, indexed, waiting. Names. Dates. Signatures. He gave them the recall order number for the pitons and watched Kestrel write it down in a hand that did not shake, and understood that the not-shaking was costing Kestrel more than shaking would have.

When he finished, the candle flames leaned in a draft from the stair, and nobody spoke.

It was Father Ivo who broke the silence, in his dry riverbed voice. "You've given us a map of the men who did this. Maps can be accurate and still be drawn by the enemy. Why should we believe the surveyor?"

"You shouldn't," Reiss said. "Belief is for churches. Verify me. Every name I gave you can be checked against the archives under this floor — that's why I came here and not to a newspaper in the lowlands. Your documents and my documents either corroborate each other or they don't. If they don't, hang me with a clear conscience. If they do, you'll need me, because I know how the coalition will come for you. I know the doctrine, the supply timelines, how their column commanders think. I am," he said, and the word tasted like ash and like relief at once, "the most useful piece of evidence in this room that can also talk."

"Or the most dangerous," Wren said from the doorway.

"Yes," Reiss agreed. "That's the same sentence."

Kestrel had been quiet through all of it, standing at the far end of the table with his arms folded, and now he came around the table's edge, slowly, until he was close, and the room went still around them the way air goes still before weather.

"Eleven years ago," Kestrel said, "you sat in a tribunal room and you testified that I ignored weather warnings. You knew I hadn't. You knew the warnings never reached us because the relay station had been closed — closed by a coalition budget directive, which you knew, because you'd signed the closure order yourself, two weeks before. You testified anyway. And then you came to the glass and looked at me, and you said—" his voice caught, just once, a hairline fracture — "you said *it was nothing personal.*"

"It was the most personal thing I've ever done," Reiss said. "That's why I said it. If I'd admitted it was personal, I'd have had to admit what I was. So I made it procedure. Procedure was where I lived. It had a bed and a salary and a clear conscience I rented by the month." He held Kestrel's eyes. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. Forgiveness is yours to give or not, and I've no claim on it. I'm asking you a narrower question. Can you use a betrayer as a witness? Because if the answer is no, I'll walk back down that causeway and take my chances in the lowlands, and I'll testify to a world that won't listen, and you'll have lost the only man in the coalition who can tell you exactly where the knives will come from."

The silence stretched. Sable had come down the steps into the vault at some point and stood at the table's edge, and it was she who spoke next, quietly, in the voice of someone reading her own inheritance.

"My grandmother had a saying. *A witness who was once the knife knows the shape of the wound.* She said Callowmere's last tribunal would need exactly that kind of witness, and that we'd hate needing it." She looked at Reiss. "She was right about both halves."

Kestrel looked at her. Then at Ivo. Then, last, at Wren, who gave a small, ungracious shrug that meant, in her language, *he's more useful alive and I hate that I think so.*

"Here's my answer," Kestrel said. "Not forgiveness. Forgiveness is a thing I might find someday on a mountain, by myself, where no one needs it for strategy. What I'm giving you is arithmetic. Your testimony plus these archives plus Odell's map plus Sable's blood — that's a case no court in the lowlands can bury. Minus your testimony, it's a case the coalition can call a fable told by exiles and a disgraced guide. So yes. You're a witness." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped to something that was not for the room. "But understand me, Davor. I'm not doing this because you suffered, or because you burned your orders, or because you read my file standing up. I'm doing it because it wins. If sentiment ever starts looking like the reason, I'll put you out myself, and I'll sleep fine."

"That," Reiss said, "is the first honest thing anyone in the coalition ever said to me. I'd forgotten what it sounded like."

Kestrel almost smiled. It didn't arrive. "Get some sleep. You testify at dawn, before the full tribunal. And Reiss —" he paused at the foot of the stairs — "the man who signed the piton recall. Say the name again."

"Corvane," Reiss said. "Deputy Quartermaster Aldous Corvane. And there's more, Kestrel. That's why I came unarmed and that's why I'd have come with nothing but the name in my mouth. Corvane didn't act alone. The recall order bears a countersignature from the coalition's Office of Interior Security — the same office, the same desk, that authorized your blacklist. They didn't just sabotage your expedition to kill four climbers. They sabotaged it to destroy the one guide whose testimony about the border passes could have contradicted their cover story for Callowmere. You weren't collateral, Kestrel. You were the target. The disaster was built for you."

The candle flames leaned again. Above them, far off toward the lowlands, so faint it might have been thunder, might have been nothing, came a sound none of them could name and all of them understood — the distant, patient noise of a column on the move.

Kestrel stood at the foot of the stairs, listening, and when he turned back to the room his face had gone the color of the glacier ice they'd crossed.

"Dawn, then," he said. "We'd better make the tribunal fast. I don't think we have until the verdict is comfortable."
