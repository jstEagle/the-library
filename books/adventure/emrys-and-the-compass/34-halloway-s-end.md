## 34. Halloway's End

They caught him on the third morning, in the grey hour when the mist still owned the valley floor and a man's footsteps made no sound worth hearing.

Halloway had known they were behind him for a day and a half. He'd felt it first as a wrongness in the birdsong above the tree line—too few thrushes where thrushes should be—and then as a smudge of woodsmoke where no honest traveler would bother with a fire. Coalition trackers. Not soldiers, not yet. The ones who came before soldiers, quiet as debt collectors, and Halloway had smiled at the thought, a dry cracked smile, because he knew their trade the way a fish knows water. They followed obligations. They followed what was owed. And Halloway, in the last week of his life, had become the largest outstanding debt on the coalition's ledger.

He had fed Reiss the false coordinates two nights ago, scratching them into a ciphered note and paying a drover three silver to carry it down to Vess. The drover hadn't asked questions. Nobody in the borderlands asked questions of a man with Halloway's face, a face like a closed ledger, all columns and no entries. The coordinates pointed to a high basin two valleys east of where Kestrel's expedition actually climbed, a place of scree and dead ends that would cost Reiss's squad two days to search and two more to admit they'd been fooled. Two days. It wasn't mercy, exactly. Halloway had spent thirty years learning that mercy was just interest paid in advance. It was arithmetic. He owed Kestrel Amberdale a debt that no ledger he'd ever kept could balance, and this was the first payment, made in the only coin he had left.

The coin was himself.

He'd known it would come to this from the moment he'd turned off the drover's road and started up into the hills instead of down toward Vess. A man who lied to Davor Reiss did not get to walk away. Reiss would send trackers, and the trackers would find him, because Halloway had never in his life been difficult to find. He had lived his whole existence in plain sight, in offices and counting houses and the grey anterooms of other men's power, a small man with a small man's talents: patience, arithmetic, and the ability to look at a human being and see only what they owed.

He sat down on a fallen birch to wait for them, and while he waited, he took out his ledger.

It was the one possession he'd carried for thirty years, a fat book bound in grey calfskin, its spine broken from use. Every debt he'd ever collected was recorded in it, in his tight, joyless hand. Names. Sums. Dates. Terms of forgiveness. He had forgiven very little. He turned the pages now with a thumb gone stiff in the cold, past the farmers and the ferry-keepers and the failed merchants of the borderlands, past the long sad column of Kestrel Amberdale's ruin—every loan, every refinancing, every cruel little tightening of the screw that had turned a mountain guide into a ghost named Emrys—and he looked at all of it the way a man looks at a house he has lived in too long and only now sees clearly.

Small. It had all been so small. Thirty years of sums, and not one entry worth the paper it was written on.

He heard them before he saw them, because they were good. A whisper of displaced scree, up-slope and to the left, where the mist was thinnest. Then nothing, which was worse. Halloway closed the ledger, tied its cord, and put it back in his coat, over his heart, where a man might carry a letter from someone who loved him.

They came out of the mist in a crescent, four of them, and a fifth behind with a crossbow. They wore no colors. They wore the borderlands the way fish wore water, waxed canvas and hooded wool, and their leader was a woman with a face like a hatchet and eyes that had already priced him.

"Halloway," she said. Not a question. Reiss would have described him: the calfskin ledger, the small careful hands, the way he never sat with his back to a door.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Halloway said. "You know my name and I don't know yours."

"It won't matter to you long." She came closer, boots silent on the needle-fall. "Davor Reiss wants the route the expedition took. The false coordinates you sent cost us two days. He wants to know what you actually know."

"And if I don't?"

The hatchet-faced woman didn't smile. "Then we take it."

Halloway nodded, as if considering a loan offer. He had sat across from desperate men his whole career and he knew the geography of this moment better than she did. She would hurt him, yes. She was very good at her work, and he could see the competence in the way the others spread out, cutting the angles, no wasted motion. But there was a flaw in her position, and the flaw was time. Reiss was two days east chasing scree. Every hour she spent with Halloway was an hour the expedition gained, and she knew it, and it made her impatient, and impatience was the one debt she carried that Halloway could spend.

"You should know," he said, "that I've already told you everything I'm going to tell you. The coordinates were accurate when I wrote them. My information has since depreciated."

"Where did they go, Halloway?"

"Into the mountains," he said. "Toward Callowmere."

The word landed among them. He watched it land. Even now, even after twenty years, the name made trackers flinch like men touched with a live wire. The coalition had erased a country and had never quite managed to erase the flinch.

The woman's hand came up, and the crossbowman shifted. "Last chance. The route."

Halloway looked up-slope, at the mist beginning to burn off the ridgeline, at the pale seam of the high country where a disgraced guide and a water-seller and a foundry girl and a handful of beautiful fools were climbing toward the ruins of a dead kingdom with the truth in their packs. He thought about Kestrel Amberdale's face the day he'd refused to hand over the chest, the first irrational choice, the one that had started all of it. Halloway had gone back to his office that night and opened his ledger and found, for the first time in thirty years, that he could not make the numbers come out. There was no column for what the coalition had taken. There was no interest rate that covered it.

He had spent his life collecting other people's debts. It had taken him until his fifty-third year to count his own.

"No," Halloway said.

It was a small word. He had imagined, in the sleepless nights since the false coordinates, that he would say it loudly, that it would come out of him like a shout, like a bell. But it came out the way he did everything: quietly, precisely, without ornament. No.

The woman exhaled through her nose. "Hold him."

They took the ledger first. Of course they did. Reiss would have told them: the ledger never leaves him. A hatchet-faced woman with competent hands paged through thirty years of Halloway's life while he watched, and he felt no shame, only a strange lightness, as if the book had been a stone he'd forgotten he was carrying. She found the Amberdale pages. He watched her find them, watched her lips move over the sums, and he wanted to tell her that she was reading it wrong, that the true ledger was the other one, the one no one kept, the one where a coalition had bought a famine and a border disaster and the ruin of an innocent man, and the balance outstanding was a country.

"Where is the route?" she said again, and when he said nothing, she nodded to the others.

Halloway had collected debts from men who wept and men who begged and men who laughed at him, and he had always believed, in the professional part of his mind, that he knew what pain was. He learned otherwise in the next hour. He learned that pain was not a number. It did not compound. It did not negotiate. It simply was, entire and total, and it burned away everything small in him—the sums, the columns, the thirty years of grey calfskin—until what remained was the one thing he had never expected to find in himself, because he had never had cause to look.

He would not tell them.

They asked him about the low pass. He would not tell them. They asked about the ford at Marrow, about the saddle, about the glacier. He had never been to any of these places, but they did not know that, and he let them believe he did, and he gave them nothing. Not a valley, not a river name, not a guess dressed up as knowledge. Twice he passed out, and twice they brought him back, because Reiss had ordered him alive, and their competence was his misfortune, and he bore it the way he had borne everything, patiently, one entry at a time.

Somewhere in the middle of it, he began to laugh. It was a terrible sound, wet and cracked, and it stopped them.

"What," the woman said.

"You're doing it wrong," Halloway said. "All of you. Your whole—" he coughed, spat blood into the needles, "—your whole machine. You erase the maps, you erase the names, you buy the famines and bury the witnesses. And then one small man with a ledger refuses you, and the whole machine stops. Two days, I cost you. Two days, and you'll spend a week wondering what else I lied about. That's the interest, madam. That's the interest compounding."

The woman looked at him for a long moment. He had the impression, absurdly, of a professional recognizing another professional, one collector acknowledging the rare client who could not be collected from.

"He'll want you alive," she said.

"He wants a great many things," Halloway said. "That's the trouble with men like Davor Reiss. They want. I'm done wanting. I'm square."

He was not square. He knew he was not square. The debt he owed could not be paid with a day of pain or a withheld route or even with the thing they were going to do to him when the woman finally understood that the ledger in her hands was the only confession he would ever give. But a man could face the balance honestly. A man could stand at the counter and say: here is what I owe, and here is my last coin, take it. He had spent thirty years teaching the borderlands that every debt comes due. It was only fitting that his own came due in the mountains, in the mist, in the grey hour that had long since burned away into an ordinary, blameless morning.

The crossbowman shifted his weight. The woman looked east, toward the empty basin Reiss was searching, and Halloway saw her do the arithmetic he wanted her to do: the time spent, the time lost, the small man who had already cost more than he was worth. He saw her decide.

"Nothing more to take," she said, to no one in particular.

"Then we're concluded," Halloway said. "I'll want a receipt."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she turned away, and the last thing Halloway Oyelaran saw was not her face or the crossbow or the mist on the ridgeline, but the pale seam of the high country to the west, where the mountains went up and up toward a valley that appeared on no map, toward a dead kingdom breathing, toward the people he had, in the only way available to a small man, chosen.

The ledger lay open on the ground beside him, pages fluttering in the wind off the ridge, thirty years of sums turning and turning as if someone were finally reading them.

* * *

Two valleys east, Reiss stood in a basin of scree and dead ends and knew, with the cold certainty of a man who had been lied to by a professional, that he was looking at nothing.

The coordinates were false. He had known it for half a day, had known it in the specific way one knows a forged signature—Halloway's cipher was perfect, the hand was perfect, and that was the tell. Halloway never did perfect work for free. Perfection was what he sold to people he was betraying.

Reiss stood at the head of the empty basin and felt something in his chest that he refused, for the moment, to name. He had ordered Halloway managed, and Halloway had instead chosen—chosen—to become a wall. A small, grey, unremarkable man had looked at the coalition's machinery and declined it, and the machinery had stopped, and Reiss was standing in the wreckage of two days, and somewhere to the west, Kestrel Amberdale was climbing toward Callowmere with a head start that a dead debt collector had purchased with his life.

"Sir," said the sergeant. "The trackers report they've got him. They're bringing him in."

Reiss looked at the scree, at the dead ends, at the beautiful useless basin. "No," he said. "They're not."

He was already turning, already calling for the squad to saddle up, already running the new arithmetic—the western valleys, the high route, the glacier that only one man alive knew how to cross. He told himself he was moving to intercept the expedition. He was very good at telling himself things.

But his hands, as he buckled the straps, were not steady, and the word he refused to name sat in his chest like a stone, and it was the same word that had been sitting there since Vess, since the testimony, since a friend's signature on a friend's ruin: *owed*.

Behind him, the empty basin filled with evening. Ahead, in the high country, a compass turned toward home, and the debt it carried was still growing.
