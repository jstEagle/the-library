## 35. The Tribunal of Exiles

Father Ivo had buried kings before. Not the crowned kind—the small ones, the men and women who had ruled their own lives with dignity until death took the scepter from their hands. He had said the words over miners and midwives, over smugglers and children, over the mute tracker whose name he had never learned and whose grave he had filled with his own hands three days past, high on the saddle where the coalition's rifles had found him.

But he had never presided over the birth of a law.

He stood in what had once been the great hall of Callowmere's high court, and he understood, with the particular ache of a man who had lost his faith and found it again in the worst possible place, that he was watching something be born in a tomb.

The hall had not collapsed the way the rest of the capital had. The coalition's engineers had been thorough with the palace, the granaries, the archives' upper levels—thorough everywhere except here, where the roof had held, sagging but intact, supported by stone ribs carved with figures Ivo could not identify. Kings, perhaps. Judges. Or simply the faces of people who had once walked beneath them and expected to be remembered. Dust lay over everything like a second snowfall, and through the shattered eastern wall the morning light came in at an angle, catching the motes and turning them into a slow, golden weather.

The survivors of the valley had come up through the pass in ones and twos, as they had done everything for twenty years—quietly, in small numbers, so as never to draw the eye of a map. Ivo had watched them arrive since dawn. Old men leaning on younger shoulders. Women carrying ledgers wrapped in oilcloth. A boy of perhaps twelve who walked with the deliberate gravity of someone who had been told, on the journey, that he was about to witness something that would be told about him afterward.

They arranged themselves along the walls of the hall in no particular order, and that, Ivo thought, was the point. No hierarchy. No throne. The Callowmere survivors had spent two decades learning what happened to nations that put their faith in thrones.

At the center of the hall stood a single table, salvaged from the rubble and scrubbed clean. Behind it sat seven elders, and Ivo recognized three of them from the settlement—the woman called Maren who kept the valley's seed stores and its memory in equal measure; the stooped former archivist whose hands shook unless they were holding paper; and the quiet man, Doran, who had lost two sons in the fall and had not spoken above a whisper since. The other four were strangers to Ivo, faces from other refuges, other hidden places—proof that the valley was not the last ember of Callowmere but only the brightest of several, banked and breathing in the dark.

Before the table, on a bench that might have been a pew in another life, sat the accused and the accused's evidence.

Kestrel sat with his hands flat on his knees, the way a man sits when he has decided not to fidget. Ivo had watched him for weeks now—through the low pass, through the storm, through the crevasse where Odell had fallen and Kestrel had gone down after him on a rope with his whole history knotted around his chest—and he had learned to read the guide the way one reads weather. What Ivo saw now was not fear. It was something more exhausting: a man who had spent years being no one, sitting in a hall of stone, about to become someone again, and knowing that the price of the exchange was everything he had.

Sable sat beside him, straight-backed, wearing a foundry coat she had refused to replace with finer things. The birthmark at her collar was covered—Ivo noticed she had buttoned the top button against the morning chill, and wondered if it was only the chill. The compass lay on the table before the elders, its brass casing dull with river years, its needle still and quiet for the first time since Ivo had known it. Its work, Sable had said, was done. It had brought them here. It had been pointing, all along, not at a person but at this: the sealed archive beneath the rubble, and the proof sleeping inside it.

And apart from them, on a stool with no back, his hands cuffed—not by the survivors' request, Ivo had learned, but by his own—sat Davor Reiss.

Ivo had prayed for that man's death. He was not proud of it, but he was honest enough to admit it, and honest enough to admit that the prayer had gone unanswered for reasons he was only beginning to understand. Reiss sat like a man in a confessional who had finally run out of sins to withhold. His uniform was gone. He wore a plain gray coat, and without the coalition's colors he looked smaller, older, ordinary—the kind of face that passes you in a market and is forgotten by the next stall. That was the horror of it, Ivo thought. The machinery of erasure was not built by monsters. It was built by forgettable men who chose, one signature at a time, to be useful.

* * *

Maren opened the proceedings without ceremony, and Ivo understood that the absence of ceremony was itself the ceremony.

"We are not a government," she said, and her voice carried in the ruined hall the way a stream carries in a canyon—thin, but finding every channel. "We are not a court, because a court requires a nation, and our nation was murdered. What we are is what remains. Seven of us, chosen by the settlements to hear what has been carried to us at such cost. We will not pretend to powers we do not have. We cannot sentence. We cannot pardon. We can only do the one thing the coalition has spent twenty years trying to make impossible."

She paused.

"We can establish what is true."

The word went through the hall like a bell through water. Ivo watched the faces along the walls—faces that had lived their whole adult lives in the conditional tense, *if we are found, if the soldiers come, if the valley burns*—and saw them try on, tentatively, the strange grammar of the declarative. *This happened. She is the heir. The famine was engineered.* Sentences without *if*. It was, he thought, the most dangerous language in the world, and every government since the first had tried to control it.

The archivist rose. His name was Pell, and his hands steadied the moment they touched the first journal—the water-stained expedition ledger Kestrel had pulled from the river in Vess, the chest's first fruit.

"These journals were written by four hands," Pell said. "Surveyors of the Callowmere border commission, in the last year of the kingdom. I knew two of them. I trained one." His voice caught, and he let it. "They document, in their own time and their own words, the grain quotas imposed by the coalition's trade authority in the two years before the famine. Quotas designed—here, and here, and here, in the margins, in their own arithmetic—to strip the border provinces of reserves while the roads out were closed for, quote, *quarantine*. They document the orders. They document who signed them."

He laid the journals down one by one, like a man laying out relics.

"Water has damaged them. Time has damaged them. But water and time are not the coalition's witnesses. They are ours."

* * *

Then they brought Kestrel forward, and Ivo found that he could not watch the guide. He watched the hall instead.

Kestrel spoke his name—not *Emrys*, not the borrowed ghost of Vess, but *Kestrel Amberdale*, and Ivo heard the name land on the survivors like rain on ground that had forgotten rain. Some of them knew it. Of course some of them knew it. The Amberdale disaster had been used for twenty years as a lesson, a warning, a stick with which the coalition had beaten the borderlands: *see what happens to those who go into the high country without leave, who ask questions, who remember.* Four climbers dead, and the guide who led them blamed, blacklisted, erased from the guild rolls.

"I led that expedition," Kestrel said, and his voice was steady in the way that ice is steady—load-bearing, and one crack from ruin. "I was blamed for it. I have spent nine years believing, most days, that I deserved the blame. I have since read the orders in your archive." He looked at Maren, not at the hall. "The route we took was chosen for us. The weather report that cleared us was falsified. The man who signed my censure—Davor Reiss—was made to sign it, and I have heard from his own mouth what was held over him. I am not here to be acquitted. I am here to say what I know, under whatever law you are building, and let it be weighed."

Maren asked him questions for the better part of an hour. Ivo listened to the answers and understood that he was hearing something he had not heard since he was a young priest in a functioning diocese: testimony. Not confession—that was private, that was between a soul and its God—but testimony, the public surrender of one's own account to be weighed against others'. The elders asked about the compass. Kestrel described pulling the chest from the flooded river, and Ivo watched a woman in the third row press her hand to her mouth, because—Pell had told them, and now the hall knew—the chest had been bound for the valley twenty years ago, entrusted to a courier who had drowned in the first purge, and the river had kept it, all this time, waiting for a disgraced guide with no nation and nothing left to lose to be standing in the right shallows.

"Did you know what you were carrying?" Maren asked.

"No. I refused to give it up before I knew, and I have asked myself every day since why. The best answer I have is that I was tired of being a man things were done to." He paused. "I don't offer that as virtue. I offer it as fact."

* * *

They called Sable, and the hall changed.

Ivo had seen crowds react to royalty, in the old days, before his diocese and his faith had both gone into receivership. He had seen the bent backs, the averted eyes, the theater of reverence. What happened in the great hall of Callowmere was not that. The survivors did not bow. They did not avert their eyes. They looked at her—openly, hungrily, the way starving people look at bread—and Sable stood there and took it, and Ivo saw the cost of it in her jaw, in the white line of her knuckles on the bench.

She told them about the foundry. About her grandmother's stories, told in whispers with the shutters closed. About the photograph of a child that had come out of the river chest with the journals, and the day Kestrel and Wren had found her with soot to the elbows and a birthmark she had spent her life hiding. She did not perform grief. She did not perform destiny. When Maren asked her whether she claimed the succession of Callowmere, Sable was silent for a long moment, and then she said:

"I claim the truth. The rest of it—" and here her voice did shake, once, and steadied, "—the rest of it I will carry as long as it's useful, and not one day longer. I am not a crown. I am a witness with a name that matters to you. Use me accordingly."

Ivo closed his eyes. *Thank you,* he thought, to no one, to whatever might be listening. *She understands it. She actually understands it.*

* * *

Then the hall had to decide what to do with Davor Reiss, and Ivo learned what it cost to build a law in a graveyard.

The survivors along the walls had not been silent during the earlier testimony, but they had been controlled. When Reiss stood, the control frayed. A man near the eastern wall—old, in a valley-woven coat—said, in a voice like a blade coming out of cloth: "That is the face that signed the censure. That is a coalition face. Why is it in this hall breathing our air?"

Doran, the father of the two dead sons, rose from the elders' table. Ivo had been told that Doran had not raised his voice in twenty years.

"Because we did not survive by becoming them," Doran said, in his whisper that somehow reached every corner. "They erased people. They erased names, and nations, and truth. If we erase him—if we decide in this hall, with no procedure and no weighing, that a man is a thing to be disposed of—then Callowmere truly died twenty years ago, and we are only its ghost wearing its clothes." He looked at Reiss. "You will testify. You will tell us the chain of command, the orders, the names, everything you know. And then these people will decide what you are worth. Not you. Not your masters. Them."

Reiss testified for two hours.

Ivo did not watch Reiss. He watched the hall absorb it—the names of ministers, the dates of the famine orders, the machinery of the border disaster laid out in the flat voice of a man reading an inventory of his own sins. He watched the old man in the valley-woven coat stop glaring and start listening, and he understood that something was happening in that hall that had not happened in the borderlands for a generation: an enemy was becoming evidence.

When it was done, Maren asked the hall—not the elders, the hall—"Does anyone here dispute that this man's testimony is true?"

Silence.

"Does anyone here claim that his testimony is therefore worthless?"

Longer silence. Then the old man in the valley coat said, grudgingly, "No. But I claim that truth from a traitor's mouth should be verified. Every name. Every order. Against the archive."

"Granted," said Maren, and wrote it down, and Ivo thought: *there. That is the whole thing. That is law. A claim, a challenge, a procedure, a record.* It had taken them twenty years of hiding and one morning in a ruin, and it was crude and unblessed and unratified by any living power, and it was more legitimate than half the tribunals Ivo had ever stood before in his vestments.

* * *

Last, they brought out Odell's map.

The cartographer could not stand; the crevasse had taken his leg below the knee, and he lay on a pallet near the table, propped on elbows, gray-faced with the pain of the carry up the pass. But his map was finished. Pell unrolled it with the tenderness of a man handling a psalter, and weighted its corners with stones from the ruined hall itself.

It was Callowmere. All of it. Every valley, every pass, the hidden settlements marked in Odell's small precise hand, the capital, the roads, the borders—the nation restored to paper, in defiance of twenty years of official blankness. In the lower margin, Odell had written the cartographer's confession: *Drawn from the surveys of the border commission, the testimony of survivors, and the memory of the undersigned, who helped erase it. Let the map stand as his correction.*

Maren read the inscription aloud. When she finished, she looked down the table at her six colleagues, and then out at the hall.

"Here is what we have," she said. "Journals. Testimony. An heir who claims only the truth. A betrayer turned witness. A map that un-erases us." She put her palm flat on the table, over the compass. "And a instrument that none of us can explain, that found its way through twenty years of river and ruin into the hands of a man with no reason to help us, and led him here. I am too old to believe in accidents of that size."

She looked at Ivo.

Ivo had not expected to be called. He had come to bury a tracker and had stayed to shepherd the living, and he had assumed—had assumed with the comfortable humility of a man who has renounced his office—that his part in this was over. But Maren was looking at him, and the hall was looking at him, and he understood.

"You are a priest of the old rite," Maren said. "The rite they suppressed. You have buried our dead and heard our people, and you hold no office and no nation and no debt to any of us. So we ask you, as a man outside our grief: tell us what you have witnessed. Not what you believe. What you have seen with your own eyes, from Vess to this hall."

Ivo rose. His knees ached. His faith, which he had spent ten years carrying like a stone in his pocket, wondering why he bothered, sat in his chest with an unfamiliar warmth.

"I have seen a debt collector die rather than give up a route he had no blood claim to," he said. "I have seen a mute man hold a line for people whose language he never learned. I have seen a cartographer finish his life's penance at the bottom of a glacier. I have seen a woman refuse a crown because she understood that a symbol without truth is just another kind of lie. And I have seen—" his voice went, and he let it go, and found it again, "—I have seen a hunter walk through the gates of the people he hunted, unarmed, in irons of his own asking, to say: *I was there. It happened. I helped. Here are the names.*"

He looked out at the faces along the walls.

"I was a priest of a church that taught me that law without mercy is a blade, and mercy without law is a fog. What I have witnessed in this hall is neither. It is what comes after both have failed: people deciding, together, in the open, to weigh what is true and act on it. I cannot bless it. My blessing is worth nothing; the church that issued it is gone or scattered. But I can witness it. And I do."

He sat down. In the silence that followed, the light through the broken eastern wall had moved, and lay now across the table, across the journals, across the map of the nation that was not on any other map, and across the compass whose needle had finally been allowed to rest.

Maren spoke the verdict of the seven elders, and it was not what Ivo had expected, and he saw by Kestrel's face that it was not what any of them had expected.

"The evidence is true," Maren said. "That is established. But truth locked in a valley is only a longer, slower erasure. The coalition will come for this place now—we all know it, we have known it since you came down the pass. So the tribunal rules this: the evidence does not stay here. It must be carried out. To the lowland courts, to the guild halls, to every chancery that ever stamped a map with Callowmere's absence. It must be carried by those who brought it, because they are the only ones who can speak it in their own voices."

She looked at Kestrel.

"You pulled the chest from the river. You will carry it back into the world. And you should know what that means: the moment you leave this valley, you are not a blacklisted guide anymore. You are the bearer of a nation's case against its murderers. There is no hiding after that. There is no *Emrys*. There is only your name, in the open, on every list they keep."

Ivo watched Kestrel take that in—the way a man takes in the size of a mountain he has agreed to cross. He watched Sable's hand move, once, toward the guide's sleeve and stop short of touching it. He watched Reiss, still in his irons, close his eyes as if the verdict had landed on him too, because it had: if the evidence went out, his testimony went with it, and there was no coalition left that would shelter him.

Outside the hall, far to the south and east, down where the lowlands began, Ivo imagined—could almost hear—the wheels of the coalition's response already turning. Soldiers mustering. Warrants drafted. A hunt beginning that would make the one from Vess look like a courtesy call.

The survivors of Callowmere had built a court in a ruin, and its first judgment was to send the truth into the teeth of the world that had killed it.

Ivo picked up his pack, and went to find Kestrel, because someone would need to hear the rites before they walked out of the only safe ground left—and because, he was discovering, witness was not a thing you finished. It was a thing you carried.
