## 36. Kestrel's Testimony

The tribunal convened at dawn in what had once been the Hall of Records.

Kestrel knew it was the Hall of Records because Odell told him so, tracing the outline on his new map with a finger gone gray and stiff from the crevasse. The building itself had been a shell for twenty years — roof fallen in one long slope of slate, walls furred with moss, the great arched windows empty of glass so that the morning light came through unbroken and lay across the floor like water. Someone had cleared the center of the hall, stacking rubble into rough benches arranged in a semicircle. On the seventh bench, elevated slightly above the others on a slab of dressed granite that must have been pried from somewhere deeper in the ruins, sat the elders.

There were nine of them. Kestrel counted twice. Nine elders of a nation that did not exist, wrapped in coats that had been mended so many times the original cloth was mostly memory, their faces seamed and patient as the mountains they'd hidden inside. At the center sat a woman older than the rest, her hair white as glacier flour, bound back with a cord. She had introduced herself the night before only as Maren, First Reader of the Archive, and she had looked at Kestrel for a long moment before saying, *You will speak tomorrow. Think carefully about which name you bring us.*

He had not slept. None of them had, really. The whole settlement had been awake in some way — he'd heard the hammers through the thin walls of the guest house, the defenders reinforcing the valley mouth, the low murmur of families moving children and stores down toward the deep cellars beneath the archive. Word had come at midnight from the watchers on the eastern ridge: dust on the border road. Soldiers. Two days out, maybe less if they pushed hard.

Two days. Enough time for a trial, if a trial could be held in the shadow of an army.

Sable found him an hour before dawn, sitting on the steps of the ruined hall with the compass in his hands. It hadn't pointed anywhere since they'd passed through the gates. The needle swung loose and idle now, a thing released from its purpose, and he found himself turning it over and over anyway, the way a man worries a coin.

"You should be resting," she said.

"So should you."

"I'm not the one on trial." She sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched. In the dark her face was just a shape, but he could feel her looking at him. "You're afraid."

"I've been afraid since Vess. Fear's not new." He turned the compass over again. "This is different. This is—" He searched for the word and hated how long it took to find it. "This is taking off armor in front of people who might shoot."

"Emrys is armor," Sable said. It wasn't a question. She'd known about the borrowed name almost from the beginning; Wren had told her, or she'd guessed, or she'd simply looked at him the way she looked at everything, straight through. "You've worn it six years."

"Seven."

"And underneath?"

He almost laughed. "Underneath there's a man who got four people killed. Or didn't. That's rather the point of all this."

Below them, in the dark of the valley, the first lamps were being lit along the paths to the hall. The settlement was coming to witness. Not everyone — most would stay with the defenses, or in the cellars, or simply keep their distance because hope was a dangerous thing to stand too close to — but enough. Enough that when Kestrel stood up, his knees would not be shaking alone.

* * *

They came into the hall as the light did.

Wren had appointed herself master of ceremonies by sheer force of personality, arranging the space with a smuggler's pragmatism: the journals laid out on a trestle table under an oilcloth, the photograph of the child propped against a stone, Odell's map weighted flat at the corner with four river-smoothed cobbles. Father Ivo had insisted on a small ritual — nothing grand, just a candle lit at each end of the table, "so the dead can see," he said, and no one argued with him. Even the mute tracker's absence had a presence; Wren had set his carved walking stick upright against the table leg where he'd have stood, and nobody moved it.

Reiss sat apart, at the edge of the semicircle, hands visible in his lap the way a man sits when he wants everyone to see he's unarmed. He'd been like that since the gates — quiet, watchful, wearing his defection like a wound he hadn't decided whether to show. He would testify after Kestrel. He'd said so plainly: *You first. You paid first.*

The nine elders took their bench. Maren settled last, folding her hands, and looked out at the hall with eyes that had clearly watched a great many things end and begin.

"We are not a court," she said, and her voice carried in the ruined acoustics without effort, the way old voices do when they've spent decades being listened to. "We have no power to bind any living government. What we have is record, and memory, and the obligation of both. Twenty years ago this hall held the laws of Callowmere. Today it holds what's left of them. We proceed."

She turned to Kestrel. "You will give your name."

And there it was. The whole chapter of his life balanced on two syllables.

He stood. His legs held, which surprised him. The hall was full of faces — Sable at the front, Wren beside her with her arms crossed tight, Ivo with his head bowed, Odell propped on a crutch near the map, refugees and defenders and elders, maybe two hundred people, every one of them a citizen of a country that had been deleted. Somewhere beyond the eastern ridge, soldiers were marching toward this valley with orders to make sure nothing here ever testified.

"Say it," Sable murmured. Not impatient. Almost gentle. *Say it and be done with the weight.*

"My name," Kestrel said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he let it crack, because hiding the crack would have been one more lie, "is Kestrel Amberdale. Of Thornbeck, in the Karst Marches. Guide, third grade, licensed by the Alpine Consortium. Blacklisted eleven years ago for the deaths of four climbers on the Whitcomb Expedition — Aldous Whitcomb, Petra Lindqvist, Tomas Ferro, and a young porter whose name the official record never bothered to include. Her name was Ansa. She was nineteen."

He heard the silence change. It wasn't shock — half these people had read the journals, had heard the rumors that had walked into the valley with him. It was something else. It was the sound of a room deciding to listen.

"For seven years I have lived under the name Emrys Vale," he went on. "I chose it because it was close enough to my own that I'd answer to it without thinking, and different enough that no blacklist clerk in three nations would connect it. I pulled boats for hire on the Vess river. I guided rich fools up easy trails. I kept my face down and my voice flat and I told myself that invisibility was survival." He paused. "I told myself that right up until the river gave me a chest with a dead kingdom's seal on it, and I discovered that the men who erased Callowmere were the same men who built the scaffold I was hanged on. Metaphorically. They never needed rope. A signature works just as well."

Maren raised one hand slightly. "Begin at the beginning. The expedition. Take your time. We have waited twenty years; we can wait an afternoon."

So he began.

* * *

He told it in order, because order was the only discipline he had left.

The Whitcomb Expedition, five years after the fall of Callowmere, commissioned to survey the high routes through the range that had once been the kingdom's northern wall. He'd been the guide. Reiss had been the coalition liaison attached to the party — officially a cartographic observer, officially a friend. Kestrel described the route, the weather window, the decision at the col that had put them on the north face traverse instead of the safe descent. He described it precisely, because precision was all he had, and because for eleven years the official record had been precise in exactly the wrong ways.

"The finding was that I ordered the traverse against conditions," he said. "That I ignored the storm signs. That four climbers died because of my judgment." He looked at his hands. "Here is what actually happened. The traverse was in the original plan. I argued against it that morning — Reiss was there, he heard me argue. But the commission's funding depended on completing the northern survey, and the man holding the purse was a coalition undersecretary named Corvane. You'll find his name in the journals too, though under a different title then. We went because we were told to go. And when the mountain did what mountains do, the report that came back had been written before we ever left base camp. My name was in it. My guilt was in it. Signed, witnessed, filed." He lifted his head. "Signed by Davor Reiss, who sits behind you, and who will tell you himself what he was promised and what he was threatened with. I won't do his confession for him. He owes you his own voice."

He saw Reiss flinch — a small thing, quickly mastered, but real. Good, Kestrel thought. Let it cost him. Confession that costs nothing confesses nothing.

Then the harder part. The part he'd rehearsed a hundred times in the dark and still couldn't say smoothly.

"I want to be clear about my own account," he said. "I did lead them onto that traverse. I was the guide. Whatever pressure was on the commission, the rope was in my hands, and when the cornice let go, it was my call that had put four people under it. For eleven years I haven't known how much of my guilt was mine and how much was manufactured, and I hid from not knowing. That's the truth of the exile. Not the blacklist. The hiding." His throat worked. "Ansa was carrying the spare rope coil. If I'd redistributed the loads at the col — I've replayed it ten thousand times — she might have been light enough to arrest. So yes. I buried myself under a false name because I believed I deserved it. And the men who framed me counted on exactly that. They didn't just need a scapegoat. They needed a scapegoat who'd agree to disappear. Guilt makes excellent cement."

Maren's voice, quiet: "And the chest?"

"The chest." He almost smiled. "The river gave it to me like a debt collector serving papers. Sealed, stamped with the crest — the double star and the broken crown. Inside were the expedition journals of Edran Voss, royal archivist, who apparently spent the last months of the kingdom doing what archivists do: writing everything down. The famine of the last winter wasn't blight. The grain shipments were diverted by order — Corvane's order, countersigned by three men whose names are now ministers and marshals. The border incident used to justify the intervention was staged; Voss names the officer who fired on his own garrison. And the final entry names a child smuggled out through the low passes with a wet nurse and a brass compass that doesn't point north."

He crossed to the table and picked up the compass. Held it up so the whole hall could see it turn idly in its case, pointing at nothing, at everything, at nothing.

"It points at the heir," he said. "It pointed at her through seven years of rivers and roads while I carried it like a drunk carries a bottle. And it led us here — not to a person, as it turns out, but to this. To the archive. To the proof that Voss hid beneath the capital before the gates fell, waiting for someone the compass could find." He set it down gently, next to the photograph. "I am not the hero of this story. I'm the courier. I broke the seal, I read the pages, I followed the needle, and I nearly got everyone with me killed doing it. The girl who sells rain navigated us out of Vess. The defrocked priest kept us from tearing each other apart. The mute tracker died at the saddle so the rest of us could walk. The cartographer fell into a crevasse and finished the first true map of Callowmere in twenty years with a broken arm. And the heir—" he looked at Sable, and made himself hold it, "—the heir refused twice to be what everyone wanted her to be, and then chose it herself, on her own terms, which is the only way inheritance has ever meant anything."

He stepped back from the table.

"That's my testimony. Under my own name. Kestrel Amberdale. Write it down however you like. Say it out loud if you want — I know the name means something bad in the lowlands. Let it mean something worse. I'm done renting a stranger's life."

* * *

The questions came for two hours.

Elders asked about the journals' provenance, the chain of custody from Vess to the valley — Wren answered those, crisp and unsentimental, describing seals and wrappings with a smuggler's exactness. They asked about the compass's behavior, and Odell, gray-faced on his crutch, explained the lodestone theory he'd been developing in the margins of his map. They asked Kestrel about the disaster three times, from three angles, and he noticed on the third pass that the story had changed weight in his mouth. Lighter. Not lighter as in less true — lighter as in carried by more than one pair of hands now.

Near the end, the eldest of the nine — a stooped man with a burn-scarred jaw — asked the question Kestrel had been waiting for.

"You understand what happens when you leave this valley. When this testimony leaves this valley. The coalition cannot permit it. They will come for the bearers of it, and they will come for us, and everything we have built in twenty years of silence stands in the path of your truth." His voice was not accusing. It was tired, and honest, and that was worse. "Knowing that. Would you still speak?"

Kestrel thought about lying. Some part of him wanted to say *yes, absolutely, without hesitation*, the clean heroic answer.

"No," he said instead. "If I'd known in Vess what it would cost — the tracker, the soldiers, this valley's exposure — I don't know that I'd have opened the chest. I'd like to tell you I would have. I can't swear it." He let that sit in the hall, uncomfortable and true. "But I know it now. And knowing it, I'd open it again. Because I spent seven years learning what silence costs, and it costs more. Silence doesn't protect anyone. It just decides who pays later, and it always bills the same people. You've been paying for twenty years." He looked around the semicircle of faces. "I'm done asking other people to pay for my safety. That's the whole of it. That's why the name comes off."

The burn-scarred elder studied him for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, the way mountains settle.

* * *

Maren called a recess while the elders conferred, and the hall emptied into the noon light like a held breath released.

Kestrel stood at the broken window arch, looking down the valley toward the east ridge, and Sable came to stand beside him. Below, the defenders were still working — he could hear the hammers, see the smoke of cook fires, ordinary life going on over the top of catastrophe the way it always did.

"They'll rule within the hour," Sable said. "Maren told me. The evidence goes out. All of it. Journals, map, testimony — carried to whatever courts will hear it, even if no court will."

"I know."

"And you volunteered to lead it out. Before they even asked."

He shrugged. "Somebody who knows the high routes. It's not bravery, it's logistics."

"Liar," Sable said, and there was a smile in it, small and fierce. Then it faded. "Kestrel. When you said your name in there — I watched your face. You looked like a man setting down something heavy and picking up something heavier."

"That's about the size of it."

"Does it feel different? Being yourself?"

He considered it honestly, turning the question over the way he'd turned the compass. "It feels exposed," he said finally. "Like standing on a summit ridge in lightning weather with a steel pike on your back. Every part of me says get down, get small, get invisible." He looked at her. "But ridges are how you get anywhere. And I'm done living in the valley of the map where nothing's written down."

Sable nodded slowly. Then she pointed, and his stomach dropped.

Down the valley, past the terraces and the timber barricades, a rider was coming up the track at a hard gallop — one of the eastern watchers, horse lathered, leaning forward in the saddle and shouting before he was even in earshot. The hammers below stopped. Faces turned. The sound reached them a moment later, thin and clear across the still air:

*Dust on the road. Banners. A column — a full column. They'll reach the valley mouth by nightfall.*

Kestrel felt the old reflex rise — *hide, get small, find the scree and vanish* — and put it down like a tool he no longer owned.

"Well," he said, and heard his own voice come out level, steady, his and no one else's. "They're early. Let's go give them a welcome written in their own signatures."

He turned from the window and walked toward the elders' bench, Kestrel Amberdale, in plain sight of everyone.
