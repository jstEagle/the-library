## 37. The Coalition Comes

Wren had sold water in Vess for six years, and in that time she had learned one law above all others: you always know when someone's about to take something from you. The hand that reaches for your cup. The smile that comes a beat too late. The way a rich man's eyes flick to the door before he robs you with a fair price.

She felt that way now, standing on the eastern rim of the erased valley at first light, watching the treeline across the gorge the way she used to watch the door of a tavern where she'd cheated the wrong man.

"They're there," she said. "I can't see them. But they're there."

Beside her, Reiss crouched with a strip of looking-glass held out on his palm, angling the dawn light down into the gorge. He didn't answer right away. He had a way of going quiet before he said something true — a habit she'd noticed somewhere on the high route and hated, because it meant the truth was always a small funeral.

"Two hundred, maybe two-twenty," he said at last. "Standard sweep formation. They'll send a probe up the goat track first — eight men, no more — to see if the valley's defended or just inhabited." He lowered the glass. "If the probe comes back with arrows in it, they settle in for a siege. If it comes back clean, they come all at once."

"And which do you want?"

Reiss's jaw worked. "I trained those men," he said. "Half of them, anyway. The officer down there — Voss, probably, he always drew the morning probes — I taught him to read terrain. Now I get to teach him what terrain does when it hates you."

Wren snorted despite herself. "Poetic. You always like that, or is it the altitude?"

"Kestrel says it's the altitude." He almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Get down to the terraces. When the probe comes, I want every soul below the second wall and not one voice above it. Can you do that?"

"I can do anything," Wren said. "It's my best quality."

* * *

The valley, when the coalition finally found it, did not look like a place worth two hundred soldiers.

That was the first miracle of it, and Wren understood now that it wasn't a miracle at all — it was design. From the rim, the Erased Valley looked like any other high fold in the mountains: a river, some terraced fields gone half-wild, a scatter of stone roofs with turf grown over them. Twenty years of careful, deliberate plainness. The Callowmere survivors had spent two decades teaching the mountains to lie about them, and the mountains had been a willing student.

But Wren had spent three days walking the settlement's spine, and she'd seen what the plainness hid. The terraces weren't just fields — they were stepped walls, dry-stoned by people who remembered how fortresses were built, each one chest-high and angled to give cover to anyone retreating uphill. The irrigation channels that fed the barley could be opened in a heartbeat to flood the lower paths into knee-deep soup. The goat track — the only route a squad could climb in formation — switchbacked through a defile barely wide enough for two men abreast, and above it, on both sides, the elders had spent the last week quietly stacking stones that had waited twenty years for a purpose.

"They were never hiding," Wren had said to Kestrel on the second night, watching an old woman with a woodcutter's shoulders walk the defile rim, marking positions with chalk.

"No," Kestrel had said. "They were waiting. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

Now, on the morning it came, Wren crouched behind the second terrace wall with a sling in her hand and a pouch of river stones at her hip — the same stones she'd skipped across the Vess as a girl, the same arm, and none of the same girl left in her — and she waited for the probe.

They heard it before they saw it: the small music of eight men trying to be quiet on scree. The settlement had emptied upward like water running backward, every soul — elders, children, the old tracker's widow with her husband's bow — folded into the terraces and the turf-roofed houses, invisible. Father Ivo had wanted to stand in the open and pray where the soldiers could see him. Kestrel had talked him down to the second wall, and Ivo had agreed with the expression of a man filing a complaint with heaven.

The probe came around the first switchback.

Wren saw them clearly then: eight men in coalition grey, moving well, rifles slung, the officer — Voss, it had to be, young and careful, checking his corners the way Reiss checked his — waving two men up toward the first terrace. Toward the barley. Toward the irrigation gate.

The gate opened.

Wren never saw who opened it. Later she learned it was the widow, alone, a lever and a lifetime of knowing her own valley. The channel water came down the track in a brown, cheerful rush, and the two lead soldiers went off their feet with a sound like startled geese, and that was the signal, and the mountain answered.

The stones came off the defile rim.

Not a rockslide — nothing so wasteful. The elders had stacked them in three deliberate curtains, and they released them in sequence, and the effect was not slaughter but geometry: the switchback below the probe simply ceased to exist as a path. Eight men found themselves on an island of scree with water rising around their boots and no route up and no dignity left. The officer, to his credit, got his men back down the track without a shot fired at them. Wren had a stone in her sling, drawn, aimed at the officer's knee — and she held it, because Kestrel's order had been absolute and she understood it: *not one death today that we can avoid. Every one we cause is a story they'll tell. Every one we don't is a story we will.*

The probe retreated. The valley exhaled.

"They'll be back by midday," Reiss said, materializing at her shoulder like a debt. "All of them. And they won't be careful this time."

"Good," Wren said, and was surprised to find she meant it. "Careful men are hard to rob."

* * *

They came at midday, and they came wrong.

Wren watched it happen from the second terrace with something close to pity. Two hundred soldiers in column on a track built for goats, compressed into the defile, and above them — on both rims, in the turf, behind stones their grandmothers had stacked — the defenders of a nation that officially did not exist.

Reiss had spent the whole morning arranging it, and Wren had watched him do it, and what struck her was not his competence but his grief. He knew these tactics from the inside. He knew that a column under arrow-fire bunches. He knew that the second officer in a column always pushes forward to restore order, and that the push forward is what kills him. He knew where the grenadiers would try to climb, because he had *been* the grenadier who climbed, twice, before they gave him a desk and a conscience. Every choice the coalition made below him, he had made himself, in another life, wearing the same grey.

"They're going to die for a map error," he said quietly, watching the column enter the defile. "Their briefing says this valley is empty. Their briefing is twenty years old. Somebody in the survey office struck it from the record and never told the soldiers." He shook his head. "We erased a place so thoroughly that we marched our own men into it blind."

The first arrow — the widow's, from the left rim, a bowshot of pure inheritance — took the column's front rank by surprise, and the column bunched, exactly as predicted, and the stones came down on the right, and the irrigation water came up from below, and for four hours the Erased Valley fought the coalition army to a standstill without giving the army a single body to avenge.

That was the trick of it. Wren saw it clearly by the third hour, crouched in the barley-smell with her sling arm aching: the defenders weren't trying to win. They were trying to *inconvenience*. Every rush of water, every curtain of stones, every arrow loosed at a rifle instead of a man was calibrated to cost the coalition time, pride, and certainty — and never blood. A wounded pride, Reiss said, reports itself. A dead comrade demands a massacre.

By late afternoon the coalition officer — not Voss anymore; a harder man had come up from the rear, and Wren didn't like his face — pulled the column back to the gorge rim and did the sensible, terrible thing. He sent for the mountain guns.

* * *

"They'll shell the terraces at dawn," Reiss told the war council that evening, in the longhouse, in lamplight, with the whole settlement's future sitting in the silence between his sentences. "Not to take the valley. To erase it properly this time. Guns don't care whose grandmother stacked which wall."

The elders looked at one another, and Wren watched a room full of people decide, together, not to be afraid — or rather, to be afraid and proceed anyway, which she was coming to understand was the only kind of courage there was.

"Then we don't fight the guns," said the eldest, a woman named Maren with hands like tree roots. "We leave."

"Leave?" one of the younger men said. "This is the last ground. You said it yourself."

"This is the last ground we *have*," Maren said. "It is not the last ground we *are*. We survived one erasure by becoming invisible. We will not survive a second by becoming rubble." She looked around the longhouse, and her gaze snagged on Kestrel, and on the packs at his feet, and on the brass compass glinting at Sable's throat. "Twenty years ago we buried the truth here and stayed with it. Now the truth is leaving. Perhaps it's time we followed it."

Wren felt the room turn. It turned on Kestrel, the way rooms always turned on him now, and she watched him stand — her guide, her mark, her meal ticket, her friend, though she'd have cut out her tongue before saying that last one aloud — and she watched him not flinch.

"The tribunal sits in the capital ruins," he said. "Three days' march down the high route, now that we know the way and the guns don't. If the settlement moves with us, the coalition has to choose between shelling an empty valley and chasing two hundred people across terrain I know and they don't." He paused. "I know which one I'd choose, if I were still the man who hunted me."

"You'd choose the people," Sable said quietly. "Because a dead valley proves nothing, but two hundred living witnesses walking out of a place that isn't on any map — that's a story even the lowland papers can't refuse."

"And the guns?" the young man asked.

"The guns will fire at dawn at a valley full of stones and barley," Reiss said. "And by the time they climb down to count the bodies, we'll be over the saddle and into country their maps say doesn't exist." He almost smiled. "They erased this valley. Tonight we make the erasure true — and we take everything that matters out of it first."

* * *

They moved at moonrise.

Wren walked near the rear of the column, because rear was where the money walked in her old life and rear was where the danger walked in this one, and she had learned the two were cousins. Two hundred souls on a mountain path in the dark — elders carried on litters, children dosed with poppy syrup so they wouldn't cry, the widow walking point with her dead husband's bow because someone had to and grief makes excellent eyes. Behind them, the valley they'd built out of nothing for twenty years stood empty under the stars, waiting for the guns.

Ahead of them, somewhere down the black throat of the high route, was the capital, and the tribunal, and the truth in their packs.

Wren found herself walking beside Sable, who carried the compass on its cord and kept touching it like a worry-stone.

"You're quiet," Sable said. "Quieter."

"I'm counting," Wren said.

"Counting what?"

"Everything I've ever stolen, sold, smuggled, or lied about. Trying to work out if any of it prepared me for this." She glanced back at the long line of the column snaking up the saddle, torches hooded, a nation on the move. "Verdict's still out. But I'll tell you what I've figured out. Back in Vess, I thought the strong were the ones who could take what they wanted. Coalition, creditors, rich clients — I thought they'd won something." She hefted her sling, the arm sore from a day of not killing anyone. "Turns out two hundred people with nothing but a true story and a home-field mountain can stop an army cold. That's the trade I've been looking for my whole life. I just had the exchange rate wrong."

Sable was silent a moment. "The compass is pulling," she said at last. "Hard. It's been pulling since sundown, like it knows."

"Knows what?"

"That we're almost there. That it's almost done." Sable's hand closed around the brass. "I used to think it was leading me somewhere. Now I think it's been leading *this* — all of it, the chest and the journals and Kestrel pulling it out of the river. Like the river was just another road, and the compass has been walking the whole time, and we've just been its feet."

"That's either beautiful or terrifying," Wren said.

"Both," Sable said. "That's the whole thing, isn't it? Both."

Behind them, far off, a sound rolled down the mountains — not thunder, though a fool would have called it thunder. The guns, firing at dawn's rehearsal, or at nothing, or at a valley that was already a memory. The column didn't stop. It didn't even quicken. Two hundred people who had already survived one erasure walked steadily into the dark with the truth in their packs, and Wren, bringing up the rear, turned once to look back at the glow on the horizon where her last safe place was being unmade, and found she felt no fear at all.

Only arithmetic. The soldiers had the guns.

But they had the compass, and the journals, and the witnesses, and the way.

She turned forward and walked.
