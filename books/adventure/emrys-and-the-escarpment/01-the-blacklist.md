## 1. The Blacklist

The trick to being nobody is to be excellent at something small.

I learned that the way I've learned most things—expensively. Ten years ago I was Wren Sandoval, certified lead guide, name on the brochures for the Escarpment Traverse, face in a magazine spread about the next generation of mountain professionals. Now I'm "Ren Salas," seasonal trail maintenance contractor, and my most important professional skill is that nobody remembers me five minutes after I pass them on a switchback.

It's a skill. Don't let anyone tell you it isn't.

* * *

The morning it all started up again, I was rebuilding a drainage crib on the Larkspur Cut-off, which is exactly as thrilling as it sounds. The trail runs along the shoulder of a hill that isn't tall enough to have weather and only just steep enough to have opinions. Water comes down it every spring like it's got somewhere to be, and every spring it eats the tread out from under the hikers' boots, and every fall somebody pays me to argue with water about hydrology using logs and gravel and spite.

I'm good at it. That's the humiliating part. Give me a slope that wants to fail and I can tell you where it'll fail first, what it'll take with it, and how much rock you need to move to make it sulk for another decade. It's the same part of my brain that used to read cornices and avalanche layers, just pointed at something too small to kill anyone. A career of diminished ambitions, executed flawlessly.

The crew was me and a kid named Dev who thought I was fifty-five because I told him I was fifty-five. He was twenty-two, built like a question mark, and he talked the entire time we worked, which I tolerated because he also worked, and in my experience that combination occurs in maybe one person out of ten.

"So did you ever do, like, real mountaineering?" he asked, prying a rotten stringer out of the crib wall. "Before this?"

"Before this," I said, "there was no this."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the best one available." I handed him the new cedar stringer. "Set it flush. If it rocks, the whole wall learns to rock, and then you've taught a hillside bad habits."

He set it flush. See? One in ten.

We were three hours into the morning when the radio on my hip crackled—the cheap VHF the district issues, the one that mostly picks up static and the lunch orders of people forty miles away. Then Marta's voice, from the district office, flat in the way that meant she'd rehearsed the sentence before transmitting it.

"Salas. You've got a visitor coming up the Cut-off. Says he knows you."

Nobody knows Ren Salas. Ren Salas has a PO box, a truck registered to an LLC with a name I picked out of a phone book, and a handshake that leaves no impression whatsoever. My stomach did the thing it does—not fear exactly, more like the body remembering a shape of trouble before the mind agrees to acknowledge it.

"Describe him," I said.

"Tall. Fifty-ish. Expensive jacket that's trying to look inexpensive." A pause. "He's carrying a folder."

Folders are worse than jackets. Jackets mean money; folders mean paperwork, and paperwork means someone had gone looking for me and found enough of a thread to pull.

"Tell him the trail's closed to visitors," I said.

"He says he'll wait."

"They always say that," I muttered, but Marta had already signed off, and Dev was looking at me with the bright incurious eyes of a man who has never once been reported to anyone by anybody.

"You know him?"

"No," I said, which was true, in the sense that I hadn't seen Cass Merrow in nine years and had arranged my entire life around never seeing her again.

* * *

Let me explain the arithmetic of exile, since apparently we're doing backstory now.

Ten years ago I led an expedition called Kestrel onto the Escarpment. Six of us went above the wall; four came down. The official record—and I use "record" loosely, because the concession holders wrote it—says that guide error put two climbers above the storm line when the window closed, that communications failed, that Wren Sandoval made choices. The inquiry took eleven days. The concession company provided the weather data, the radio logs, and the expert witnesses. The families got settlements with confidentiality clauses. I got a blacklist that covers every permitted range on the continent, a reputation that precedes me into rooms I've never entered, and a lifetime ban from the one piece of country I loved more than any person I've known since.

Here's what I remember: whiteout so total you navigated by counting steps. A radio that died on the second day, which I reported immediately and which the investigation later described as "consistent with conditions." And a retreat line—a fixed rope route we'd established on the descent—that wasn't there when we came back for it.

I said that last part out loud, once, during the inquiry. The concession lawyer smiled at me the way you smile at a dog that's barking at nothing, and the transcript records my statement as "unsupported speculation regarding equipment removal."

After that I stopped talking. Not literally—I still order coffee, I still tell Dev to set his stringers flush—but in the way that matters. You learn that the truth is a currency and yours has been counterfeited, so you stop spending it. You take jobs under names that aren't yours. You get very good at drainage cribs. You keep a rule: stay invisible. No summits, no permits, no ranges with concession signs at the trailhead. There are thousands of miles of unglamorous ground that nobody's blacklisted anybody from, and I have walked most of them, alone, competent, and forgotten.

It works. That's the thing nobody tells you about exile—it works fine. Grief doesn't need a landscape. It'll settle anywhere, like dust.

* * *

Cass Merrow came up the Cut-off at a pace that suggested she'd done her time on trails but had recently been spending it elsewhere—on stairs, maybe, or yachts. She was grayer than the magazine photos from a decade ago, leaner, wearing a shell jacket that cost more than my truck and had been scuffed strategically to pretend otherwise. She stopped six feet away, which I noted: she remembered I don't like people inside arm's reach on a slope.

"Ren Salas," she said, tasting it.

"Can I help you?"

"You rebuilt the Miller Creek bridges in '21. Double-stringer, offset abutments, after the flood took the originals." She said it like she was reading off a list she'd memorized. "There aren't twelve people in the province who build like that. I checked."

"Lots of good builders out there."

"Not with your left-hand splice. I watched a video a trail association posted. Four seconds of you lashing a rail." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "You always tied off left-handed. Even on Kestrel."

Dev, bless him, chose that moment to announce loudly that he was going to go check the upper culvert, and went, moving fast for a man pretending not to eavesdrop.

"Cass," I said, when he was out of earshot. "You should leave."

"I found you through a bridge video, Wren. Nine years of you being careful, undone by four seconds of lashing." She held up the folder. "And yes, before you ask—your current client filed a report yesterday. 'Contractor misrepresented qualifications.' Someone ran the name Ren Salas and found it belongs to a man who died in 2014 in Thunder Bay. Your employer is required to escalate identity discrepancies. By Friday there'll be a file with your photograph in it, and photographs get run against watchlists, and watchlists—" she tapped the folder "—have my family's name on the review board."

I kept my hands busy while she talked, squaring a stack of gravel bags that didn't need squaring. Busy hands buy time, and time buys composure, and composure is the only asset I've never lost entirely.

"What do you want, Cass?"

"I want to hire you."

"I don't guide."

"I know what you don't do. I've read the list. It's very thorough—you wrote half of it yourself, all those permit applications denying prior experience." She opened the folder and held out a single sheet. "Eleven days. Five clients. The Escarpment Traverse, end to end, before the concessions lapse on the twenty-eighth. After that the whole range closes to civilians forever and goes to extraction. This is the last legal window in history, and I need the one guide alive who knows that route better than the surveyors did."

The word *Escarpment* landed in my chest like a dropped tool. I looked at the sheet without taking it. Numbers. Dates. A fee with more zeroes than my last three years combined.

"No," I said.

"Wren—"

"The answer's no, and it was no before you finished the sentence. Find one of those twelve other builders. Find a guide with a clean file and a death wish."

"You're the only one who's been up there in a whiteout and come back."

"And I'm the reason two people didn't." I said it evenly, the way you recite a phone number you've dialed ten thousand times. "That's not a résumé line, Cass. That's the whole file. Ask your family—they helped write it."

Something moved behind her face then, quick and complicated, and for a half-second she looked less like money and more like the nineteen-year-old who'd sat across from me in a basecamp tent arguing about anchor systems until two in the morning. Then it closed over, smooth as lake ice.

"Your client reports you Friday," she said quietly. "Your false name unravels by Monday. You'll lose this job, and the next one, and eventually some clerk will cross-reference the old Kestrel file and you'll be right back where you started, except older and with worse knees." She set the folder on a stump between us. "Or you walk across the Escarpment one more time, on the last days it's legal, and you decide for yourself what happened up there. Because I don't think you believe the story anymore, Wren. I think you stopped believing it years ago, and you've been living in exile for a crime you're not even sure was committed."

She turned and went down the trail, expensive boots finding the tread without a slip, and I stood there among my gravel bags with the wind coming up the hillside and the folder sitting on the stump like something that might bite.

I didn't touch it. That's important. I want the record to show I didn't touch it.

I looked at it for a long time, though. Long enough for the light to change. Long enough for Dev to come back down the trail and stop dead when he saw my face.

"Boss?" he said. "You okay?"

"Yeah," I said, and picked up my shovel, and went back to arguing with water.

But that night, in the cab of my truck with the heater ticking and the rain starting, I took out the folder. Of course I did. I'm meticulous, not a saint.

Inside were the traverse plans, the permit dates, the client roster—five names, each one flagged in Cass's neat handwriting with notes like *medic* and *no digital footprint* and *asked twice if the trip was deniable*. Deniable. She'd assembled a crew the way you assemble a bomb: everything chosen to leave no trace of the maker.

And under all of it, one line in her hand, underlined twice:

*I know what they said about the radio. I don't think it broke.*

I sat in the dark with the rain on the roof and felt ten years of careful invisibility start, very quietly, to come apart at the seams.

Friday, my client's report would land. Monday, my name would unravel. And somewhere north of here, past the boundary marker I'd spent a decade not crossing, the Escarpment stood in its last legal season, keeping whatever it keeps.

I told myself I'd decide in the morning.

I already knew.
