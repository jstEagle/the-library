## 2. An Offer from Cass Merrow

The knock came at six in the evening, which was wrong on two counts. Nobody knocked at the bunkhouse in Kettle Falls—nobody came to Kettle Falls at all unless they'd taken a wrong turn on the logging road and needed a tire iron—and nobody who knew me would ever bother announcing themselves. They'd know I'd hear them coming a quarter mile out, boots on gravel, and be gone through the back window before they reached the porch.

I heard the vehicle first, actually. An engine with money in it, the kind of suspension that had never been asked to forgive a washboard road. It idled outside for a long moment, the way people do when they're checking their reflection in the dark glass of your life before they step into it.

Then the knock.

I set down the boot I was re-waterproofing and looked at the door the way you look at a weather report you don't like. Through the frosted pane: a silhouette, tall, hands in pockets, weight settled back on the heels. Patient. The posture of someone who has never once been kept waiting by anything that mattered.

There were exactly three people in the world who knew where I was, and I paid rent to one of them, and it wasn't her.

"Wren." Cass Merrow's voice came through the wood, unchanged by ten years, which felt like a personal insult. "I can see your light moving. Let's not do this in the hallway."

*Let's not do this in the hallway.* Ten years, and she still talked like the world was a corridor she'd been granted right-of-way through.

I opened the door anyway. That's the part I've turned over a thousand times since—the opening of the door, the small surrender of it. I could tell you I was curious. I could tell you I wanted to hear what she'd say. Both true. But underneath both there was something simpler and less flattering: after ten years of being no one, some animal part of me stood up when someone from the old life said my name.

Cass Merrow stood on my porch looking like an obituary for everything I used to want. Silver at her temples now, cut practical. A shell jacket that cost more than my truck. Behind her, the vehicle—a grey SUV with government-plate blankness—and beyond that, the ridgeline going blue with dusk, the Escarpment's southern shoulder just visible over the pines, the way it always was, the way it never let me forget.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look expensive."

"Both true." She glanced past me into the room. One cot, one stove, one table with a boot clamped in a vise and a tin of wax open beside it. She took it in the way she used to take in route conditions—fast, total, filed away. "Cozy."

"It's temporary."

"Everything about you is temporary. That's rather the point of you now, isn't it?"

I should have shut the door. I'm telling you honestly, as a professional assessor of risk, that shutting the door was the correct move, available, low-cost, reversible. Instead I stepped aside and let Cass Merrow into the only clean space I had left in the world, because ten years ago I had loved her the way you love a fire in wet country—gratefully, warily, knowing it could turn—and some sediment of that had apparently survived every flood since.

She didn't sit. Of course she didn't sit. She stood at my table and produced a document wallet from inside her jacket, laid it flat, and squared its corners to the edge of the table with one finger. Even her paperwork was disciplined.

"Coffee?" I asked, mostly to watch her refuse.

"No, thank you." She opened the wallet. "Eleven days from now, the concession permits on the Escarpment traverse lapse. They won't be renewed. The whole range goes into exclusive corporate tenure—extraction survey, then development. After that, civilians are done. Permanently. No appeals process, no grandfathering. Sealed."

"I read the notices."

"I know you read the notices. You read everything. You've built a whole life out of reading things and staying very, very quiet." She slid a contract across the table toward me. "Five clients. Full winter traverse, south col to the north gate, eleven days, standard fee tripled, plus a completion bonus that will let you stop pretending to re-waterproof boots for a living."

I didn't touch it. "You found me."

"Don't sound surprised. Finding people is most of what I do now."

"That's not what I mean." I looked at her. "Nobody finds me, Cass. I've spent ten years making sure of it. So either you got lucky, or you spent real money, or—" and here the cold started, the old familiar cold, climbing up from the stomach, "—someone told you."

Her face did something very small. Not guilt. Cass doesn't do guilt; she does accounting. But something adjacent to it, a ledger entry made reluctantly.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously."

"Then let's say I'm good at my job, and leave it there." She tapped the contract once. "The crew's already assembled. Four of them plus me. Medic, geologist, radio tech, and me. All experienced. All vetted."

"Vetted by whom?"

"By me."

"That's not reassuring, Cass. That's the opposite of reassuring. You vetting people is how the Kestrel Expedition happened."

The name landed in the room between us and neither of us moved. Outside, the light went from blue to grey. Somewhere down the valley a dog started up and quit again, embarrassed.

Cass recovered first, because she always did. "That's not fair."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Fairness wasn't in the report either."

"Wren—"

"The answer's no." I picked up the boot, unclamped it, began working wax into the seam with more attention than the task required. "Find another guide. There are dozens within a hundred miles who'd take triple rate to walk a ridge."

"There aren't," she said quietly, "and you know it. Anyone qualified is either employed by the concession holders or blacklisted by them. And anyone *not* qualified—" she gestured at the room, at me, at the whole architecture of my exile, "—well. You know what happens to underqualified crews on that mountain. You wrote the book on it. Literally. Your incident report is required reading."

"My incident report," I said, "is fiction with a signature on it."

"Yes," Cass said. "It is."

And there it was. Ten years of discipline, ten years of the rule—*stay invisible, stay small, stay hired for nothing higher than a day-hike and a smile*—and she'd walked through my door and put her thumb directly on the nerve in under four minutes. I put the boot down before I cracked the leather.

"Why are you here, Cass? Really. Not the contract. The reason."

She finally sat. That frightened me more than anything else she'd done, because Cass Merrow sitting down meant she'd decided this conversation might take a while, and Cass did not enter conversations she hadn't already priced.

"My father died in March," she said.

"I heard. Condolences."

"He left me his files." She said it flatly, but her hand had gone to the contract wallet, resting on it, and I understood suddenly that the contract was not the important paper in there. "Boxes of them. Concession correspondence going back thirty years. Survey records. Incident reports. Everything the family kept, and the family kept *everything*, Wren—you remember how he was."

I remembered Aldous Merrow. Chairman's chair, glacier-white hair, a handshake like a settlement agreement. I remembered him testifying, composed and sorrowful, about equipment failure and human error and the tragic loss of two climbers above the escarpment wall, and how his testimony had fit the official narrative so neatly, so *helpfully*, like a keystone dropped into place.

"I don't want whatever you think I want," I said. "I don't want an apology. I don't want restitution. I especially don't want to be part of whatever penance project you've constructed for yourself."

"It's not penance."

"Then what is it?"

"Mapping," she said. "Before it's sealed. Eleven days, five people, full traverse, full photographic and geological record of what's about to disappear behind a fence forever. That's the contract. That's all the contract says."

"And what does the contract *mean*, Cass? Because you've never once in your life done a thing that only meant what it said."

For a moment—one genuine, unguarded moment—I saw her tired. Actually tired, the way you get when you've been carrying boxes of your father's files around in your chest for months. She looked at the ridgeline through my window, at the shoulder of the Escarpment going dark, and when she spoke again her voice had lost its boardroom polish.

"They're going to pour concrete on the north approach by summer. Drill pads on the shoulder. There's a station up there—an old survey station, Kestrel-era—that will be bulldozed or buried or simply forgotten inside a locked perimeter. Whatever's in it. Whatever's *in* it." Her eyes came back to mine. "I need someone to lead us across who knows that mountain better than the maps do. There is exactly one person alive who fits that description, and the industry made sure she'd work cheap."

"The industry," I said. "Your father's industry."

"Yes."

"Which blacklisted me."

"Yes."

"Which is why I'm here, waxing boots under a false name in a town with one road."

"Yes," Cass said, and then, softer, almost gentle, which was worse: "Wren. I'm offering you the traverse. The actual traverse. The thing they took from you. One last time, legal for eleven more days, and then gone forever. You can hate me while you do it. I'll even arrange for you to have time to."

I looked at the contract. I made myself not pick it up. This is the discipline they taught me in the years after: want nothing, reach for nothing, because everything you reach for gets used against you. The paper lay there with its tripled fee and its completion bonus and its clean, innocent language—*recreational traverse, documented for posterity*—and every line of it was a hook dressed as a gift.

"No," I said again, and this time it came out steady. "No. Find your fence and stand behind it. I hope your mapping's beautiful."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and closed the empty folder, and I thought—relief, physical, flooding—it's over, she'll go.

She stood, buttoned her jacket, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame.

"One more thing," she said. "Not an argument. Just information, since you like having it." She turned halfway. "The logbooks from the Kestrel Expedition were never recovered. Officially, they burned with the high camp. Unofficially—" a small shrug, "—my father's files reference a survey station on the shoulder. Reference number 114-K. Still standing. Possibly still powered. And Wren—the last entry logged from that station, the night of the storm, the night Devi and Marisol died—" she met my eyes and held them, merciless, "—was a transmission receipt. Someone acknowledged their distress call. The radio worked. Somebody heard them, and logged hearing them, and let them die anyway. And then everyone agreed it was your broken radio."

The room went very quiet. The stove ticked. My hands, I noticed distantly, had gone white on the edge of the table.

"That's a lie," I said.

"It's a file," Cass said. "Numbered, dated, in my father's handwriting. I've read it forty times. I can recite it." She opened the door onto the cold. "I'm not asking you to come for me, or for the mountain, or for the money. I'm asking because in eleven days that station goes behind a fence, and whatever's in those logbooks dies with it, and you spend the rest of your life being the person the story says you are instead of the person you actually were."

She stepped off the porch. Gravel crunched. The expensive engine woke up.

At the vehicle she stopped once more and called back, not turning around, her breath hanging in the freezing air:

"Crew briefings start Thursday, seven a.m., the Kettle Falls diner. If you're not there, I'll assume the story about you was true after all."

Then she was gone, taillights shrinking down the single road, and I stood in my doorway with the cold pouring past me and the ridgeline black against the last grey light, listening to the silence where her engine had been.

I told myself I wouldn't go. I told myself it all night, through the waxing of three more boots, through the banked stove, through the long dark hours where the Escarpment stood invisible outside the window but present, enormous, patient, the way it had been for ten years.

Devi's laugh. Marisol checking the rope a fourth time because she always checked a fourth time. The storm coming down like a verdict. The radio in my hands, dead, dead, dead—or not dead, never dead, *logged*—

A transmission receipt. In his handwriting.

Thursday was three days away.

I got up, found the bottle I kept for emergencies, and poured one, and drank it looking at the mountain I had sworn off, sworn off, sworn off, until the glass was empty and the decision was made, though I'd be damned if I could name the exact moment it had happened.

Seven a.m., Thursday. The Kettle Falls diner.

God help me, I was going to hear the rest of it.
