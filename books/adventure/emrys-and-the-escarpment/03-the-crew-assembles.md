## 3. The Crew Assembles

The Kettle Falls diner at seven a.m. smelled like burnt coffee and industrial-strength bacon grease, which is to say it smelled like every trailhead diner I'd ever eaten breakfast in before a trip went wrong. I took that as an omen and ordered eggs anyway. If you're going to make a mistake, you should be fed while making it.

Cass was already there, of course. Cass Merrow had been early to everything since we were twenty-two years old, back when being early was a virtue instead of a tactic. She sat in the last booth with her back to the wall—old habit, or new paranoia, hard to say—and had claimed the table by spreading maps across it like a dealer laying out a hand she fully intended to cheat at.

"You came," she said.

"I said I would."

"You said it in a tone that meant you wouldn't."

"That's fair." I slid into the booth opposite her. "I'm here under protest. Consider this a professional consultation. I'm not committing to anything."

"Of course not." She pushed a cup of coffee toward me like she was baiting a trap, which she was. "You're just curious enough to be dangerous, and too proud to admit it. You haven't changed."

"Neither have you. Still buying people's breakfasts so they owe you."

"It works on you, doesn't it?"

I drank the coffee. It was terrible. I drank more of it.

"Here's how this goes," Cass said, all business now, the charm folding away like a map along its creases. "Four crew members. I've got them arriving through the morning. You talk to each one. If you don't like what you hear, we part ways friendly and I never bother you again."

"And if I do like what I hear?"

"Then eleven days, Wren. Traverse start to finish, before the concession lapses on the twenty-eighth. After that, the Escarpment stops being wilderness and starts being somebody's property line."

"And your crew. Who are they?"

She smiled, and it was the smile of a woman about to introduce me to four problems wearing human faces. "You'll see. One rule: don't tell them who you are."

I set down the cup. "Excuse me?"

"They know they're getting a guide named Ash. That's all they need."

"Cass. My name is on a blacklist with the Merrow family's signature somewhere near the bottom of it. If any one of these people googles—"

"They won't. They're not the googling kind." She leaned forward. "Wren. Think about who answers an ad for an illegal winter traverse posted from a burner phone. Think about *why* they answer it. Nobody on this crew is findable. That's the point. That's always been the point."

I should have stood up then. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them: I recognized the shape of the thing she was building, and I stayed anyway. Ten years of staying invisible, and the first time someone offered me a reason to walk back onto that mountain, my survival instincts folded like wet cardboard.

"Send in the first one," I said.

* * *

The first one was the medic.

She came in out of the cold like cold was something that happened to other people—tall, gray-streaked hair cut short for helmet compatibility, a duffel over one shoulder that she set down with the particular care of someone who knew exactly what was in it. She took the booth seat beside me rather than across, which told me she'd done field interviews before, or interrogations, and preferred to keep her hands where the other party could see them.

"Odile Fenn," she said. "You're the guide."

"Ash."

"Ash." She said it the way a doctor reads a chart—registering it, filing it, noting the discrepancy between it and my face. "Cass says you're good."

"Cass says a lot of things."

"She does." Odile signaled the waitress for coffee without looking. "Can I ask you something, guide to—whatever you are?"

"Ask."

"If someone on this traverse takes a fall into a crevasse at kilometer forty, hypothermic, maybe a femur, what's your call? Push for the road or shelter and stabilize?"

It was a test, but not the kind I expected. Not *are you qualified*. More like *will you lie to me*.

"Depends on the weather window," I said. "If we've got twelve hours of clear, we move. If the front's coming in, moving kills them faster than staying put. You stabilize, you dig in, you keep them alive until the mountain gives us a door. And if the mountain doesn't—" I shrugged. "Then we're having a different conversation, and I'd want you in it, because you'd know better than me what's survivable."

Odile studied me for a long moment. "That's the right answer," she said. "Most guides say 'we push.' Ego answer."

"What's yours? Why are you here?"

And there it was—the flicker. Small, fast, gone. She wrapped both hands around her coffee cup like it was something she was protecting rather than drinking.

"I worked emergency medicine in a place I'm not going to name," she said. "There was a triage decision during a disaster response. A committee reviewed it. The committee was wrong, and the review was political, and my license is currently in a state I'd describe as *contested*." She met my eyes. "I can practice medicine anywhere in the world except officially. So I practice it unofficially, in places with no paperwork. Like your mountain."

"You could lose more than a license if someone gets hurt up there."

"Yes," she said simply. "That's why I asked you the question first."

When she'd gone, I sat with it. A medic with a contested license. Unfindable. Deniable. I turned my cup slowly on the formica and didn't like the geometry of what was taking shape.

* * *

Tomas arrived mid-morning and filled the doorway the way large men do—not fat, just built on a scale the doorframe hadn't budgeted for, with a beard you could lose a small mammal in and the restless eyes of a man cataloguing everything, including me.

"Tomas Reyes," he announced, sliding in across from me uninvited. He looked at the maps still spread from Cass's setup, and his whole face changed—softened, hungered. "Is this the north shoulder? This is the old survey grid. Nobody's mapped this since—"

"Since Kestrel," I said, and watched him flinch.

So he knew the name. Interesting.

"Geologist," he said, recovering. "Structural. Glacial, mostly. I was with the university program until—" He waved a hand, a gesture that contained an entire career's worth of ruin. "Let's say my data collection methods became controversial."

"Controversial how?"

"I sample where I'm not supposed to sample." He said it without shame, which was either honesty or pathology, possibly both. "Protected land, concession blocks, active claims. There's rock under this range that nobody has ever gotten a core from because the permits cost more than a house, and I think—" he leaned in, voice dropping, and here was the zealot shining through the affable giant— "I think there's something under the escarpment. The geothermal readings alone. Warm rock where warm rock shouldn't be. Something's cooking under there, and everyone who might have looked the other way is about to own it outright."

"You want to core a mountain that's about to become private property."

"I want to core it *before* it becomes private property. After that, it's trespassing." He grinned. "Well. More trespassing."

"And if we get caught?"

"We won't get caught. That's why I'm asking about you." His eyes were sharp now, the affability peeled back. "Cass says you're the best winter route-finder she's ever seen, and she doesn't say things like that lightly. But she also says you've been off the big ranges for ten years. So which is it? Are you sharp, or are you a legend with rust on it?"

"Both," I said. "Rust is just iron being honest."

He laughed, big and easy, and I found myself almost liking him, which made the arithmetic worse. A geologist who samples illegally. A medic with a dead license. I was assembling a crew portrait and every face in it was a liability waiver waiting to happen.

* * *

Priya came in just after noon, and she nearly didn't come in at all—I watched her through the window do two full passes of the parking lot before committing. Small woman, wire-thin, radio equipment in a padded case she carried like a sleeping infant. When she sat down across from me she kept the case on her lap.

"Priya Nair," she said quietly. "Radio tech. Comms, telemetry, signal analysis." Then, before I could ask anything: "I need to know if there will be a satellite uplink on this trip."

"There's a satphone in the kit. Why?"

"Because I need to know if it's registered." Her hands were steady; her voice wasn't, quite. "Whose account it's billed to. What traffic it pings. Because I spent six years working signals intelligence for a government contractor, and I learned exactly how much a person's life can be reconstructed from their device metadata, and I am *not doing that again*."

"Doing what again?"

She looked at me for a long moment, deciding something. Whatever she decided, she decided it, because she kept talking.

"I flagged a pattern in intercepted traffic. Civilian traffic. I reported it up the chain instead of to a journalist." Her jaw tightened. "Nothing happened. Officially nothing happened. Unofficially, three people connected to that traffic disappeared, and my security clearance got suspended pending review, and I understood that the review would conclude whatever it needed to conclude. So I resigned before it did, and now I work cash jobs fixing radios for fishing boats, and I check every device I touch for trackers, because I assume I'm interesting to certain people." She lifted her chin. "Your employer hired me to run comms on a trip that doesn't officially exist. I said yes because a trip that doesn't exist leaves no metadata. Do you understand? For eleven days, I'd be *nowhere*. That sounds like heaven to me."

I understood. God help me, I understood completely. I'd spent a decade being nowhere myself.

"One condition," Priya added. "Whatever communications gear comes on this trip, I vet it. All of it. Including anything your employer provides."

"Noted," I said, and meant it, and filed it away next to a growing list of things about this expedition that smelled like smoke.

* * *

Last, of course, Cass. She waited until the diner had emptied to the two truckers who'd stopped listening to anything but their plates, and then she slid back into the booth across from me and read my face the way she used to read weather off the ridgeline.

"Well?" she said.

"Odile Fenn, unlicensed physician. Tomas Reyes, poacher of rock samples. Priya Nair, ex-spook who trusts nothing with a battery in it." I laced my fingers on the table. "Cass. Every single person on this crew is professionally radioactive. Every one of them has a reason to never testify, never file a complaint, never appear in a record. Do you know what that adds up to?"

"I know what you're going to say."

"Deniability." I kept my voice level. "This isn't a crew. It's a pre-built cover story. If anything goes wrong up there—if anyone dies, if the logs surface, if whatever you're actually after comes to light—there's no one left standing whose word counts for anything. A disgraced guide working under a false name. A doctor who can't practice. A scientist who steals. A tech who's paranoid. Cass, you didn't assemble a team. You assembled four people nobody would believe."

The silence stretched. Outside, the light was going flat and gray, the way it does before snow, and some animal part of my brain started counting hours to a storm that wasn't forecast yet.

"You're not wrong," Cass said finally.

"That's it? That's all you've got?"

"What do you want me to say, Wren? That I picked them because they're good? They *are* good—they're the best I could find who'd say yes. But yes, fine, they're also deniable, and so are you, and so am I." She spread her hands. "You want to know why? Ask yourself what's on that mountain that needs this much deniability. Mapping what will be lost—that's real, that's part of it. But it's not all of it. And I'm not telling you the rest in a diner."

"Then we're done."

"No," she said, and for the first time all day the polish cracked, and underneath it was something rawer and older and much more tired. "No, we're not done, because you already know half of it. You said the name yourself this morning—Kestrel—and I watched your face when you said it. You've spent ten years knowing something was wrong with that expedition and not letting yourself look at it directly. I'm offering you the chance to look." She stood, dropped bills on the table, shouldered her pack. "We leave Monday from the north access. Crew briefing Sunday night. Be there or don't."

She got three steps toward the door.

"Cass." She stopped. Didn't turn. "Why me? Really. Out of every blacklisted guide in the hemisphere—why the one person your family helped bury?"

A long pause. When she answered, she didn't turn around, and I was grateful, because I didn't want her to see my face either.

"Because you're the only witness left who was actually there," she said. "And because I need someone on that mountain who hates my family enough to check my math."

The bell over the door clattered. She was gone into the gray.

I sat there a long time with the cooling coffee and the maps she'd left behind, and I did the thing I'd sworn off doing, the thing I'd spent ten years not doing: I let myself remember. Not the verdict, not the headlines, not the hearing room with its water glass and its stenographer. Earlier than that. The morning we went in. Devi laughing. Marisol checking the rope a fourth time. The escarpment wall catching first light like a blade being drawn.

Two climbers went up that wall. Two climbers didn't come down. And the radio—

The radio in my hands. Dead, I'd always believed. Dead, dead, dead.

But Cass's father had led that expedition. And Cass had just told me, in her careful way, that her family had needed deniability once before.

Sunday night, then. Monday, the north access.

I finished the coffee, which had gone bitter as old guilt, and stepped out into the first flakes of the storm, and started walking toward a decision I'd already made.
