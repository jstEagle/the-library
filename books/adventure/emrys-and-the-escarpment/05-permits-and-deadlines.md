## 5. Permits and Deadlines

I learned to read contracts the way other people learn to read weather: early, obsessively, and with the assumption that something in there wants to kill you.

It started with my father, who ran a telecom repair shop in a town that no longer exists on maps that matter. Every year the utility company would send him a service agreement forty pages long, and every year he'd sit at the kitchen table with a red pen and a cup of tea gone cold, muttering things like *they've changed the indemnity clause* and *who reads this?* I'd ask him why he bothered, since he always signed anyway. He'd look at me over his glasses and say, "Because knowing what you're agreeing to is different from agreeing to it, Priya. The first one's a choice. The second one's just paperwork."

I was nine. I thought it was the most boring thing anyone had ever said to me.

Then I spent six years as a signals analyst for a company whose name I am contractually obligated—by a contract I did not read closely enough, which is its own kind of joke—to describe as a "communications solutions provider." What we actually provided was interception solutions, mostly for clients whose names appeared in the same documents as the words *mineral rights* and *concession zones*. I read a lot of fine print in those years. Most of it was written by people who assumed no one like me would ever look. When I finally looked at the wrong document, at the wrong time, and made the mistake of saying so out loud—I became unfindable too.

Which is how I ended up in a rented room above a closed bakery in Merrow's Landing, three weeks before the traverse, with a stack of concession documents spread across a card table, decoding the legal architecture of a mountain.

* * *

The Escarpment concession was, on its face, simple. A survey-and-exploration permit, granted forty-one years ago to Merrow Holdings—a name that made Cass go very quiet when I mentioned it—renewed on a rolling basis ever since. The permit covered the ridge line, the shoulder, the icefield, and the approaches. It gave the holder exclusive rights to conduct "geological assessment" within the boundary. It also, in Section 14, Subsection 3, gave the holder the obligation to *maintain continuous active operations*, failing which the concession would lapse and revert.

Revert to whom? That was the interesting part.

I sat up with the documents until two in the morning, cross-referencing the concession text against the provincial land registry, the extraction licensing framework, and a set of amendments that had been quietly attached to a highway infrastructure bill two years ago—because that's where you hide things, in bills about culverts. My tea went cold. My father would have been proud.

Here is what I found, and I want you to appreciate it the way I did, sitting alone in a cold room above a dead bakery, feeling the floor drop out from under a mountain I'd never seen:

When the concession lapsed—and it would lapse, automatically, in fifty-one days, because Merrow Holdings had already begun the process of *not* renewing it—the Escarpment would not become public land. It would not become protected land. It would become extraction land. The reversion clause routed the territory directly into a special extraction zone, a category created by that highway bill, with provisions I can only describe as elegant in their ruthlessness. No public access. No environmental review requirement—the review had been "pre-satisfied" by forty-one years of concession-era survey data. And, my favorite, the part I read four times to make sure I wasn't hallucinating: any records, findings, samples, or documentation originating within the zone boundary would become the proprietary property of the extraction licensee upon the zone's activation.

Anything found inside stays sealed.

Not *protected*. Not *preserved*. Sealed. Like evidence. Like a wound closed over something that's still in there.

I sat back and looked at the ceiling, where a water stain had spread into a shape like a mountain range, and I thought: *Cass knows this. Cass has to know this. So what exactly are we mapping, and for whom?*

* * *

Cass came by the next morning with bread from the only bakery in town that was still open, which felt pointed, and coffee in a thermos that had seen three decades of ridgelines. She spread her hands over my card table of documents like a general surveying a map she'd drawn herself.

"So," she said. "You found it."

"Found what? You'll have to be specific. I found several things. One of them is that your family's company has spent two years carefully arranging its own failure to renew."

"Carefully arranging—" She laughed, but it was a thin sound. "Priya. Merrow Holdings hasn't arranged anything. My father died eighteen months ago and the company's been a carcass ever since. The vultures are the ones who wrote the highway bill. They've been waiting for the concession to lapse for a decade. We're just—" she waved a hand— "the last obstacle before the door closes."

"The door closes in fifty-one days. And when it closes, everything inside becomes theirs. Every record. Every sample. Every logbook." I watched her face when I said *logbook*. I've spent my career watching faces for the micro-expressions that precede a signal, the tell that someone's transmitting even when they think they're silent. Cass's face did something very small and very fast, and then it did something else, which was become extremely calm, which is what trained people do instead.

"Which is why we're going in before it closes," she said. "To map it. Document it. Everything that's going to be lost—the glaciology, the geology, the survey history. A proper record, published, public, before the seal comes down. Once it's in the open, it's in the open. They can't un-publish a map."

"That's a beautiful idea," I said, and I meant it, and I want that on the record, because what I said next was also true. "It's also a beautiful idea that doesn't require five people and eleven days on a route that's rated beyond the season. A mapping expedition could run the southern approach in summer. Legal, cheap, safe. So why winter, why now, why the closed route, why—" I tapped the concession map, right on the shoulder where the old survey station was marked with a faded triangle— "why here?"

The silence went on long enough that I could hear the bakery below us, or rather not hear it, the absence of ovens, the absence of the town that used to need them.

"Because the southern approach doesn't cover the shoulder," Cass said finally. "And the shoulder is where the Kestrel survey data is. And the Kestrel survey data is the only complete geological record of the interior that exists. If we don't get it, the extraction consortium's version is the only version, and their version says the interior is barren except for one deposit they've already got claims staked on." She met my eyes. "I'm not lying to you, Priya. I'm just not telling you everything, and I'll cop to that. There are things I'll tell you on the mountain that I can't tell you in a rented room with thin walls."

I should have walked out then. I want to be honest about that too, since honesty is supposedly my one redeeming feature—the analyst who reads everything, the woman who blew up her own career by saying *this document doesn't say what they told us it says*. I should have stood up, folded my red pen into my pocket, and told Cass Merrow to find another radio tech.

Instead I looked at the map. At the boundary line. At the little triangle on the shoulder. And I thought about my father at the kitchen table, red pen in hand, signing anyway.

"Knowing what you're agreeing to," I said, "is different from agreeing to it."

Cass blinked. "What?"

"Nothing. Old family wisdom. I'm in. But I'm bringing my own comms plan, my own encryption, and a copy of every document on this table, in three places you'll never find." I smiled at her, and it wasn't entirely friendly. "The first one's a choice, Cass. I'm making it with my eyes open."

* * *

Wren Sandoval found me two days later, or perhaps I found them—we were both in the back of the outfitter's shop on the main street, and they were checking my gear with the expression of someone defusing a bomb they'd personally wired.

"This radio," they said, turning my primary unit over in their hands. "Where'd you get it?"

"Built it. Modified chassis, hardened against cold-soak failure. The battery management system is mine. It'll hold a charge at minus forty when the manufacturer's spec gives up at minus twenty-five."

"Uh-huh." They set it down with exaggerated care, the way you'd set down a snake you suspected of being friendly but weren't prepared to bet on. "And the antenna configuration on the ridge kit?"

"Redundant. Two independent paths, one low-band backup that'll bounce off the ionosphere if everything else dies. I can raise a station in the valley from the icefield."

Wren looked at me for a long moment. Up close they had the particular stillness of people who've spent years being careful, the economy of movement that comes from having learned that every motion is visible to someone. "You know what I did before I got blacklisted," they said. It wasn't a question.

"Guiding. High-altitude. The Kestrel route."

"I mean after." They picked up my backup radio, weighed it. "I spent ten years being the person nobody's radio could find unless I wanted to be found. So when I tell you that everything you've built here is findable—by anyone with the right equipment and the patience to listen—you understand I'm not insulting your work. I'm telling you what I know."

"Then I'll encrypt it."

"They'll break it, or they'll jam it, or they'll triangulate you and come up the ridge with better gear and worse intentions." Wren set the radio down. "The only secure transmission on that mountain is the one you don't make."

"Then what's the plan? Walk in silent and hope?"

"The plan," Wren said, "is to be boring. Boring is invisible. Schedules that don't match any filed itinerary. Check-in windows that look like routine chatter. And a protocol for when the chatter stops that doesn't involve anyone coming to look for us, because anyone who comes to look for us—" they paused, and something moved behind their eyes, some older weather— "isn't coming to help."

I thought about the documents on my card table. About Section 14, Subsection 3. About the words *proprietary property of the extraction licensee*.

"Someone's already coming to look for us, aren't they," I said. "That's why you're really here. Not for the mapping."

Wren's hands went still on the table. For a moment I thought they wouldn't answer, and I understood, in a way I couldn't have articulated yet, that this was the shape of the whole expedition: people telling the truth in pieces, each piece accurate, none of them complete, the full picture assembling itself somewhere above the tree line where it would be too late to turn back.

"I'm here," Wren said finally, "because Cass asked me a question I've spent ten years not answering. The mapping is real. The deadline is real. And if there's something else in the fine print—" they picked up my primary radio again, turned it over, and handed it back with a nod that I decided to accept as trust— "then you and I are going to want to know exactly what it says before anyone else does. Keep reading, Priya. Read everything twice."

"I always do."

"I know," they said, and there was the ghost of something that on a warmer person might have been a smile. "It's why you're on this crew. Cass didn't hire you for the radios."

* * *

That night I went back through the amendments one more time, because reading everything twice is not a slogan, it's a discipline. And at the bottom of Schedule C, in a subsection so buried it might as well have been geological, I found the enforcement provision.

Upon activation of the extraction zone, the licensee would be entitled to engage "authorized recovery and security personnel" to secure the zone boundary and "retrieve or render inert any proprietary materials remaining within the zone." Retrieve or render inert. I looked at those words for a long time. There was a version of me, a few years ago, that would have read them as bureaucratic filler. But I had spent six years in a room full of people who said *retrieve* when they meant *take* and *render inert* when they meant *make it so it never existed*, and I knew the dialect.

I also found the name of the security firm pre-approved in the schedule. I won't write it here. I'll just say that I recognized it, the way you recognize a voice through a wall—muffled, but unmistakable. The same firm whose intercept contracts had crossed my desk in another life. The same firm whose internal routing codes I had once known by heart, back when knowing them was my job and not my crime.

They had pre-approved the hunters before the door was even closed. You only do that when you know something's going to be in there worth hunting.

I sat with that until the water stain on the ceiling looked less like a mountain range and more like a closed fist. And here is the thing I keep circling, the thing I can't resolve, the thing I suspect I won't resolve until I'm standing on the ice with the wind trying to take my radio out of my hands: I still want the mapping to be real. I want Cass to be what she says she is. I want to believe that a woman would spend her inheritance and her family's name to put a public record on a mountain before the seal comes down, out of nothing but grief and principle. I want the expedition to be what it says on the contract.

But I've read the contract. All of it. Twice.

And the contract says: fifty-one days. Then the boundary closes, and everything inside it—every record, every secret, every body the mountain has been holding—becomes the property of people who pre-paid for the privilege of making sure it stays theirs.

Fifty-one days. Then we walk.

I packed my radios, encrypted my copies, and told no one what I'd found. Not yet. Not until I could watch Cass's face on the mountain, in the thin air, where the truth has a way of coming out at altitude whether you've scheduled it or not.

Besides—my father signed anyway. But he always knew what he was signing.

So do I.
