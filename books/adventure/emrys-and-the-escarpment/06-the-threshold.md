## 6. The Threshold

The weather window held the way a held breath holds—perfectly, and only for so long.

I was up before anyone else, which surprised no one except possibly Cass, who had apparently expected to be. She emerged from her tent at first light with her hair already braided and an expression that suggested she'd rehearsed emerging from her tent at first light. Some people don't climb mountains; they stage them.

"Good," she said, looking at the sky as if she'd ordered it personally.

"Morning," I said, and handed her coffee anyway, because I'm a professional.

The others came out in stages. Odile first, quiet and quick, checking her kit with the economy of someone who'd done it ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more. Priya next, yawning, radio strapped to her chest like a second heart. Tomas last, blinking into the dawn with his data logger already running, which meant he'd been recording since before he woke up, or possibly in his sleep. I wouldn't have bet against either.

"Day one," Odile said, and there was something in her voice—not excitement exactly. More like the sound a door makes when you open it onto a room you've only ever seen through frosted glass.

"Day one," I agreed, and pointed east, up the valley, toward a line on the landscape that no map drew but every guide in the province knew by heart.

Nobody said *the boundary*. Nobody had to.

* * *

We moved well for the first three hours, which is the kind of sentence that always makes me nervous when I write it in a trip report, because moving well early usually means you're spending something you'll want later. But the snow was firm under boot, the wind was a polite suggestion rather than a demand, and the five of us fell into a rope rhythm almost without discussion. Odile behind me, reading my pace like sheet music. Priya after her, chattering about signal propagation until the altitude shut her up. Then Tomas, then Cass at the rear, where I'd put her deliberately—last position, no line of sight forward, nothing to command but her own boots.

She hadn't argued about it. That worried me more than arguing would have.

The valley narrowed. The walls rose. And then we rounded a shoulder of grey limestone and there it was: the marker.

You'd think something that ruined a life would look like more than a steel post with a faded placard. CONCESSION BOUNDARY — PERMIT HOLDERS ONLY — VIOLATORS PROSECUTED. The paint had gone the color of old bone. Below the official text, someone had scratched something into the metal years ago—I'd never gotten close enough to read it, back when getting close enough would have been its own crime. Now I stood in front of it with four witnesses and a legal window measured in days, and I still couldn't make myself lean in.

"You've never touched it," Priya said. Not a question. She'd watched me stop two meters short like the post was electrified.

"I've never been allowed to touch it."

"And now?"

"Now I'm allowed for eleven days." I looked at the sky again. Still holding. "Which is not the same as welcome."

Tomas shouldered past me—he wasn't rude about it, just oblivious in the way of men who see the world as one continuous dataset—and ran his glove along the post's edge. "Corrosion pattern says this has been here twenty-plus years. Pre-dates the Kestrel closure." He photographed it from four angles. Then five. Then he crouched and photographed the base of it, and the snow around the base, and I understood that Tomas did not take pictures of things; he took custody of them.

"Tomas."

"Mm?" He didn't look up.

"When we move, we move together. Including cameras."

That got his eyes up. There was a flicker in them—not defiance, exactly. Possession. "Data doesn't weigh anything, Wren."

"No. But it draws attention. Every transmission you send, every device you power, is a signature somebody can read from a satellite or a relay station. From today until we're out, your loggers run local. Nothing goes up. Nothing pings home."

He stood slowly, brushing snow off his knee with great dignity. "Cass said full survey capability."

"Cass said a lot of things." I kept my voice level. "Ask her what she said about who leads on the mountain."

Behind him, Cass's face did something small and controlled. To her credit, she didn't contradict me. "Wren's right," she said. "Local storage only." And then, softer, almost pleasant, the way a knife is pleasant right up until it isn't: "We'll have plenty of time to argue about the data once we're across."

*Across.* As if the boundary were a river and not a wound.

* * *

We crossed at noon.

There was no ceremony, because I refused to allow one. No speeches, no photographs of boots straddling the line, none of the things I'd imagined doing for ten years during the long insomniac stretches when revenge wears the costume of fantasy. We just walked past a rusty post in the snow, and the mountain changed.

Not visibly. That's the thing nobody tells you about trespass—it doesn't announce itself. The rock was the same rock. The wind was the same wind. But I felt it in my sternum, a low hum like standing too close to a transformer, and I knew that somewhere behind us the world had quietly reclassified us. Guides become criminals. Clients become accomplices. Everything we did from this step forward would be read twice: once as mountaineering, once as evidence.

Odile fell into step beside me at the first rest halt. She didn't say anything for a while, just shared her thermos lid, which in medic language is a full paragraph.

"How are you doing?" she finally asked.

"Fine."

"Wren."

"It's strange," I admitted, because lying to a medic is a waste of good lying. "Ten years I built my whole life around the shape of that post. Stayed inside the lines. Took jobs that paid half what they should because they were inside the lines. And now I'm standing on the wrong side of it and the sky hasn't fallen and the mountain doesn't even care." I turned the tea in the lid. "It turns out the ban was never the mountain's idea."

"No," Odile said. "Bans rarely are." She looked up-slope, at the escarpment wall showing itself now between cloud layers, blue-grey and enormous and indifferent. "My hospital banned me from three floors once. Administrative decision. Had nothing to do with medicine and everything to do with a man whose family donated a wing." She took the thermos back. "Institutions ban people when the truth needs somewhere to live that isn't near the institution."

I stared at her.

"What?" she said. "I'm a medic, not a monk. I notice things."

* * *

The afternoon went sideways in the small ways afternoons do.

Cass found her voice around the second hour, and it turned out her voice sounded a lot like a concession holder's. "Take the high line here," she called forward, when we were still forty minutes from the point where the high line became relevant. "Wren, the moraine on the left will be faster."

"The moraine on the left is a rockfall chute," I called back, pleasantly enough that Priya, walking beside me, pretended very hard to be adjusting her pack straps. "But thank you for the suggestion."

"I've climbed this approach."

"In summer. Twenty years ago. In a party of twelve with a fixed camp and a cook." I let that sit for exactly one stride. "The mountain's changed since breakfast, Cass. It changes while you're looking at it. Trust the person whose job is noticing."

To her credit—she was accumulating credits all day, and I distrusted every one of them—she dropped back into silence. But I could feel her behind me like weather pressure, and I caught Priya glancing over her shoulder twice, wearing the expression of someone watching two dogs circle the same bone.

Tomas, meanwhile, had discovered that the geology improved dramatically past the boundary, which is to say he stopped walking whenever the rock got interesting, which was constantly. I lost him three times in ninety minutes. The third time, Odile found him fifty meters off route, chip hammering a sample out of an outcrop with the focused joy of a man who has forgotten other people exist.

"Fifty meters off route," Odile told him, "in avalanche terrain, alone, without telling anyone."

"I told Cass."

"Cass isn't the one keeping you alive."

He had the grace to look abashed, mostly, I suspected, because it was Odile saying it. He rejoined the rope without argument, but I noticed his sample bag had gotten heavier, and I noticed the way he checked its weight with his hand, the way a man checks a wallet in a crowd. Whatever Cass had promised him about this traverse, it lived in that bag now, and men guard their reasons the way dragons guard gold: jealously, and to the death of whoever comes close.

* * *

We made camp at 2,300 meters in a col sheltered from the west wind, and if you've never dug a tent platform into wind-slab at the end of a nine-hour day, let me tell you it is exactly as romantic as it sounds, which is not at all, and it is also the best feeling in the world, which is the whole contradiction of this profession in one sentence.

Priya got the antenna up and pulled a weather download through a relay she'd configured to bounce through three jurisdictions, which she explained was "technically not illegal in any single one of them," delivered with the pride of a woman who has thought about this a lot.

"Well?" Cass asked.

"Window holds another thirty-six hours. Then a system comes down from the northwest." Priya frowned at the screen. "It's fast. Faster than the models had it yesterday."

"Models are always wrong," Cass said.

"Sure," Priya said. "And sometimes they're wrong in the direction of *we should have turned around Tuesday*."

I let them talk and walked the perimeter instead, checking anchors, checking the slope above camp, checking the light dying on the escarpment wall to our north. Up close, in the last alpenglow, the wall looked less like geography and more like architecture—something built, tier by tier, by a patience larger than any of ours. Somewhere up there were two climbers who'd never come down. Somewhere up there was a storm with my name in its mouth. Ten years of my life had been spent walking circles around this view, and now I was inside the circle, and the strangest part was how ordinary it felt. How much it felt like work. How much I loved it, immediately, shamefully, the way you love a house you broke into.

Odile joined me at the edge of camp. We stood in the cold watching the wall go dark.

"It's beautiful," she said.

"It's a trap with good lighting."

"That too." She tucked her hands in her armpits. "Can I ask you something? You don't have to answer."

"People say that and then they ask anyway."

"Why did you say yes to Cass? Really. A man—" she corrected herself, "—a person with your record doesn't come out of hiding for mapping rights. What's up there that you need?"

The honest answers stacked up behind my teeth: vindication, grief, the scratched words on the boundary post I still hadn't read, the decade-old sound of a radio dying mid-transmission. What I said was:

"Eleven days ago I was invisible. Cass showed up and offered me the one thing I couldn't get by staying invisible." I nodded at the wall. "Answers. Or at least the ground where the questions are buried."

Odile considered that. "And if the answers are worse than the questions?"

"Then at least I'll know what I'm carrying."

She laughed softly, without much humor. "Spoken like someone who's never had to carry anything heavy."

"I've carried plenty."

"Everyone thinks they have. Until the wall." She tipped her chin at the escarpment, now just a blacker black against the night sky. "Get some sleep, Wren. Tomorrow we start uphill for real."

* * *

I was last into my tent and last asleep, which is standard, and I lay there listening to the wind test the fabric and thinking about thresholds—the way crossing one never feels like the thing itself. The real threshold isn't a post in the snow. It's whatever waits on the far side of it, patient, already knowing your name.

Around midnight, half-dreaming, I heard boots on packed snow outside. Soft ones. Deliberate. I lay still and tracked them by sound: past my tent, past the gear cache, stopping at the edge of camp where Odile and I had stood. A long silence. Then the scrape of something being set down on stone, and the boots returning, unhurried, to Cass's tent.

I gave it twenty minutes. Then I got up, clipped on my headlamp at its lowest setting, and walked to the edge of camp.

On the flat rock where the boots had stopped sat a small pile of stones. Balanced. Deliberate. A cairn, the kind climbers build for the dead, except there was nothing dead up here that anyone had ever admitted to.

Except that wasn't quite true, was it. Something had died up here ten years ago. It just hadn't been the climbers.

I stood there a long time, cold working through my jacket, looking at six balanced stones in the dark and understanding that Cass Merrow had crossed her own threshold tonight, and that whatever she was mourning—or planning—it had started the moment we stepped past the boundary together.

Above camp, the escarpment wall held the starlight and gave nothing back.

Tomorrow, we'd start toward it.
