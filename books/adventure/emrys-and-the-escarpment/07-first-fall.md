## 7. First Fall

I have a professional relationship with gravity. We've been colleagues for twenty years — I've set bones it broke, stitched skin it tore, and once, memorably, pulled a molar out of a snowboarder's jaw after he introduced his face to a chairlift tower at speed. Gravity and I understand each other. It does its work; I clean up after.

So when the cornice above the traverse let go on day three, my first thought — before the roar, before the white wall of it, before Wren's voice cracked across the slope like a whip — was not *oh no*. It was: *there you are. Right on schedule.*

* * *

The morning had been too good. That's the thing nobody tells you about mountains: they lull you. Two days of hard, clean travel under a sky so blue it looked lacquered, the crew moving like an actual unit instead of five strangers pretending, and me privately congratulating myself on having signed on with a guide who was, whatever the blacklist said, obviously competent.

Wren Sandoval moved through snow the way I imagine surgeons move through anatomy — total familiarity, zero sentiment. They'd read the slope all morning in that quiet way of theirs, stopping to probe, rerouting us twice for reasons they didn't bother explaining until Tomas asked, and even then the answer was just "loading" and a tilt of the head toward a slope I'd have skied happily.

Cass, meanwhile, walked point-adjacent like she owned the ridge's deed and mortgage both. She'd been giving orders since the boundary marker — where to rest, when to rope, which line to take — and Wren had been absorbing them the way sand absorbs water, silently, without argument, which struck me as either wisdom or a very long fuse.

Priya had named the cornices by breakfast. "That one's Gerald," she said, pointing at the overhanging lip of wind-packed snow capping the ridge above our route. "Gerald looks like he has opinions."

"Gerald is forty meters up and fifty meters left of our line," Wren said. "Gerald can have all the opinions he wants."

I remember that exchange because I remember thinking: *famous last words, but at least they're organized last words.*

Tomas was lagging, as usual, hunched over his data slate between steps, logging ice cores and snow stratigraphy with the devotion other men reserve for love letters. Priya had the radio harness chattering faintly against her hip. Cass was thirty meters ahead, breaking trail toward the shoulder below the col, moving well for a woman who claimed to be fifty-three and lied about it badly.

The route traversed beneath the ridge on a bench of wind-scoured ice, maybe two meters wide, angling down toward a crevasse field we could see glittering ahead — blue fractures in the glacier's skin, like veins in marble. Beautiful, if you were the kind of person who found beauty in things designed to kill you. I am that kind of person. It's why I get hired.

We roped in pairs. Wren put themselves on anchor duty, running belays off screws and flukes, and made each of us demonstrate our arrest position before we stepped onto the bench. When my turn came, they looked at me a beat longer than necessary.

"You've done this," they said. Not a question.

"I've cleaned up after this," I said. "Different job."

Something almost like a smile crossed their face. It was gone fast, but I caught it, and I filed it away, because in three days of travel I'd collected perhaps four expressions from Wren Sandoval and I was building a taxonomy.

That was at 10:40 in the morning. At 11:15, Gerald weighed in.

* * *

There was no dramatic warning. Cornices don't negotiate. One moment the mountain was silent in that particular high-altitude way, the silence that isn't silence at all but the sound of your own blood deciding whether to stay in your extremities — and then there was a crack, deep and structural, like the world clearing its throat, and the slope above us moved.

Not much. That's the horror of it. A section of the cornice lip calved and came down the rock band above the bench, and it wasn't huge — later, from camp, looking back up, I'd estimate it at maybe six tons of snow and ice, which sounds enormous written down and looked, in the half-second you had to perceive it, like nothing at all. Like weather. Like a decision the mountain had been making all along and finally got around to announcing.

It hit the bench thirty meters behind Cass, who was out of the runout, and it hit the bench directly above Tomas and me, and it took the bench with it.

I want to be precise here, because precision is my religion: the snow didn't carry me. The *bench* went. The whole shelf of wind-loaded snow we'd been walking on for an hour sheared along a buried weak layer and slid, and suddenly there was no ground under my left side, and I was going down-slope with it, and below the bench — visible now, horribly visible, because the snow that had hidden it was leaving — was a crevasse. Wide. Blue-black. Patient.

My training did what training does. I threw onto my uphill side, drove my axe, kicked. The rope snapped taut. And then physics introduced itself properly: Tomas, anchored above, took the catch, and the rope hauled me sideways out of the slide path even as the surface I'd been lying on became a river, and I swung — actually swung, like something in a bad action film — across open air over the crevasse, my boots passing over that blue dark by what I would later measure, because I measured it, because I'm a medic and measurement is how I keep from screaming, at less than a meter.

Less than a meter. My boot toes hung over the void like a signature over a blank check.

Then I stopped swinging, and I was hanging on the rope above the crevasse, spinning slowly, and the avalanche debris was settling somewhere below me with a long sighing sound, and the silence came back, and in it I heard Wren Sandoval say, very calmly:

"Nobody moves. Nobody talks except me."

* * *

Here is what flawless looks like, since I've seen it and can describe it: boring. That's the truth nobody puts in the brochures. A perfect rescue is almost insultingly undramatic. No heroics, no shouting, no cinematic last-grasp saves. Just a person doing eleven small correct things in order while everyone else holds their breath.

Wren anchored the rope to a deadman they placed in under a minute — I know it was under a minute because Priya, bless her compulsive heart, timed everything afterward and gave us a full debrief with timestamps, which is exactly the kind of trauma response I encourage in my patients. They rappelled to me on a separate line, checked my harness with two fingers without asking permission, clipped a second point, and said, "How are you?"

"Embarrassed," I said. My voice worked. Good sign.

"Medically."

"Pulse is fine. Hands are fine. I think my dignity needs evacuation."

"Dignity doesn't survive this route anyway," they said, and lowered me the remaining meters to a ledge inside the crevasse mouth, then followed, then rigged a haul system with a mechanical advantage I clocked at five-to-one and executed with the economy of someone who'd done it in worse places, which, given their history, I did not doubt.

They hauled me out in four minutes. Total time from collapse to standing on solid snow: nineteen minutes. Nobody hypothermic, nobody bleeding, nobody dead. In my professional opinion, that's not luck. That's ten thousand hours wearing a uniform nobody lets you wear anymore.

Tomas was gray-faced and shaking, still anchored above, still holding the rope he'd caught me on even though the anchor held it. Wren climbed to him, put a hand flat on his chest — not comfort, assessment — and said, "You kept your brake hand on. Most people don't." Then they let him shake for a while, which is also correct medicine.

Cass stood apart, looking up at the broken cornice, and her face was doing something complicated. I've spent twenty years reading faces for a living — pain hides in specific places, fear in others — and what I saw on Cass Merrow's face was not relief and not gratitude.

It was recognition. She'd known. Some part of her had looked at that loading, at that aspect, at this season, and had known, and had said nothing, and now the bill had come due one meter short of paid in full.

I filed that too. My file on Cass Merrow was getting thick.

* * *

The cost came due immediately, as costs do. My rope team's gear was scattered downslope or buried; more importantly, the bench route was gone, and the alternative line Wren scouted added half a day of terrain, and by the time we'd relocated camp to a protected hollow below the shoulder, the light was failing and everyone was running on fumes and adrenaline comedown.

Camp that night was quiet in the wrong way. Quiet like a waiting room.

I did my rounds — Tomas's rope burn, Priya's cold-nipped fingers, Cass's silence, which I treated as a symptom — and then I sat outside my tent with a stove, because sleep wasn't going to happen and pretending otherwise wastes fuel.

Wren came and sat across from me without being asked. We watched the alpenglow die on the ridge above Gerald's stump. For a while neither of us spoke, which I appreciated. People who fill silence with talk are usually afraid of what the silence contains.

"You counted," Wren said finally. "At the crevasse. You looked down and you measured."

"One meter," I said. "Maybe eighty centimeters."

"Most people don't look."

"I'm a doctor. Looking is the job. You look at the thing that can kill you, or it gets to surprise you."

Wren nodded slowly, turning their mug in their hands. "Kestrel taught me that," they said. "Look at the thing. Even if you can't fix it."

And there it was — the name, dropped into the firelight like a stone into still water. Ten days on this mountain and I'd heard it exactly twice, both times from Cass, both times followed by a silence with walls around it. I decided to spend some of the credit I'd earned at the crevasse.

"The Kestrel Expedition," I said. "You were on it."

"I led it."

"You were what, twenty-five?"

"Twenty-four." They said it evenly, but their hands stopped moving. "Two climbers died above the escarpment wall. Storm came in faster than forecast. Our radio failed. I made the call to push for the col instead of retreating, and when the retreat line collapsed behind us, I was the one who walked out and had to explain why two people didn't." A pause. "The explanation didn't go well."

"That's not how blacklists usually work," I said carefully. "Guides lose clients. It's awful, and it happens. They don't usually get erased from an entire range. Somebody wanted you gone specifically."

For a long moment I thought they wouldn't answer. The stove hissed. Somewhere above us, the mountain settled with a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

"Somebody did," Wren said at last. "Ask Cass. She knows more than she's told us. Ask her why the route rating changed last spring. Ask her why she needed a disgraced guide with Kestrel route memory instead of any of a dozen clean ones." They stood, dumping dregs into the snow. "She'll tell you eventually. She tells people things when she's calculated that telling will cost her less than hiding."

"And if she never calculates that?"

Wren looked down at me, and in the last of the light their face was unreadable, which I was beginning to understand was itself a kind of answer.

"Then somebody else finds out first," they said, "and it costs more than any of us brought money for."

They went to their tent. I sat with the stove a while longer, doing what I do: looking at the thing.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when the zipper of Cass's tent opened, and instead of turning in, she crossed the camp to where Wren sat on a gear crate, alone, checking the anchor lines one more time with a headlamp like a penitent counting rosary beads.

I should have pretended to sleep. Instead I lay in my bag with the door cracked six centimeters, because I'm a medic and eavesdropping is basically differential diagnosis.

"Cass," Wren said, without looking up. "Go to bed."

"We need to talk about today."

"Today's talked out."

"No." Her voice was low and steady, the voice of someone who'd rehearsed. "Today you pulled Odile out of a crevasse in nineteen minutes on a route I told you was standard for the season. And it wasn't. I need you to hear me say that, because you deserve to hear it from me and not from a document."

The lamp clicked off. In the dark I heard the crate creak as Wren turned.

"Say it again," Wren said quietly.

"The route was re-rated in March. Class four exposure, seasonal hazard advisory, cornice loading flagged red through May. I read the survey. I read it twice." A breath. "I hired you anyway, and I didn't tell you, because I knew if I told you, you'd refuse. And I need this traverse done before the permits lapse, Wren. I need it more than I've ever needed anything, and I used you to get it, and today it almost killed Odile, and I am—" The word she chose surprised me. Not *sorry*. "—ashamed."

Silence. Long enough that I counted my own heartbeats. Forty-two of them.

"Why?" Wren asked. Not angry. Worse than angry — careful. "Why this traverse? Mapping what will be lost, that's the pitch. Nobody crosses the Escarpment in winter to take pictures for a memorial. What's inside the concession that you need found before the company seals it?"

Another silence, and this one had weight, the kind of weight that shifts a whole expedition's center of gravity.

"When we're further in," Cass said. "Past the shoulder. There's a station on the next ridge — old survey station, Kestrel era. I need to see what's inside it. And I need you with me when I do, because whatever's in there, Wren..." Her voice frayed, just slightly, the first honest thread I'd heard from her in six days. "...it has your name in it."

I lay in the dark with my heart going, and I thought about a meter of blue air, and a woman who'd known the odds and rolled them anyway, and a name spoken into the night like something being dug up rather than buried.

Tomorrow we climbed past the shoulder. Tomorrow, apparently, we'd find out what the mountain had been keeping.

I just hoped, staring at the ceiling of my tent, that whatever it was keeping, it hadn't been keeping it for me.
