## 8. The Station on the Shoulder

I want to be clear about something before I tell you what happened at the station: geologists are not treasure hunters. We are not. What we are is people who have spent our entire adult lives being told *no* — no, you can't core that ridge, no, that permit's held by someone else, no, the data belongs to the ministry now, thank you for your interest. A geologist is a person trained to stand in front of a locked door and take notes on the keyhole.

So when Wren said *we leave it*, you can understand that my soul did a small, undignified thing. It rattled its cup against the bars.

* * *

We found the station on the fourth morning, on the shoulder below the eastern col, and we found it the way you find anything up there: by almost walking into it. The white of the building was the same white as everything else, and only the geometry was wrong — a straight line where the mountain had promised curves. Priya saw it first, because Priya sees everything that blinks or hums.

"Power source," she said, crouched with her receiver like a woman listening to a seashell that was talking back. "There's a hum. Low band. Something under there is *on*."

"Nothing's on," Cass said. "That station's been dead since before Kestrel."

"Then something dead is drawing current," Priya said, "and I'd like to meet it."

Wren stood apart from us with their binoculars up, doing the thing they do — that long, silent inventory, reading the slope the way Odile reads a chart. When they finally lowered the glasses they looked, for just a second, like a man seeing a ghost in a photograph he'd taken himself.

"I know this building," Wren said.

"You've been here?" I asked.

"No." A pause. "Kestrel's route log mentioned a survey hut on the shoulder. Half a line, no coordinates. It wasn't on any map we carried." They shouldered their pack. "Which means either the surveyors who built it didn't want it found, or somebody after them didn't."

Nobody had an answer to that, so we climbed to it, which is the mountaineer's answer to most questions.

* * *

The station sat in a shallow scoop of rock like a tooth in a jaw — prefab panels, corrugated roof half-shed on one side, a stub antenna wearing ten years of rime like a fur coat. The door was a steel slab with a wheel handle, the kind of door that says *the things behind me were meant to stay put*. There was no lock. There didn't need to be one. The lock was the Escarpment itself; the door was just a formality the weather enforced.

Wren went in first. Of course Wren went in first. That's not heroism, whatever Odile thinks — I've watched them long enough now to know it's accounting. Wren counts risk the way misers count coins, and they will not let anyone else spend from a purse they haven't audited themselves.

Inside: cold that wasn't quite cold. That was the first wrongness. Outside it was minus twenty-two and falling, and inside the station it was maybe minus five, which is impossible unless something is paying the heating bill. The air smelled of dust and machine oil and, faintly, of warm stone — that mineral breath you get near a thermal vent, like the mountain exhaling through its teeth.

Priya found the source in twenty minutes, because of course she did. Behind a bank of dead instrumentation, a floor panel, and beneath the panel a shaft lined with galvanized pipe, dropping into darkness, breathing heat. A geothermal tap. Someone had drilled into a fumarole system that appears on precisely zero surveys — I checked my own maps that night, twice, with growing professional humiliation — and run a heat exchanger off it simple enough to still be turning over a decade later, unattended, unmaintained, faithful as a monk.

"Nobody mapped this," I said.

"Nobody mapped a lot of things," Wren said.

It's a strange sensation, standing on ground that the entire accumulated apparatus of modern science has somehow failed to notice. It makes you feel either very small or very chosen, and I have never in my life been able to resist the second feeling. That's my flaw, if you're keeping a list. Cass keeps one for me, I'm sure.

* * *

The main room was a museum of an interrupted thought. Desks with papers pinned under glass weights, curled and brown. A wall chart of the escarpment face drawn in a hand so precise it looked printed, contour lines marching down the cliff like regimented ants. Core samples in wooden racks, labeled in faded ink: dates from eleven years ago. Two years before Kestrel. This place had been working long before the expedition that made my companions' names infamous ever laced a boot.

And there — along the far wall, in a steel cabinet with a stenciled number and a seal across the latch — the logbooks.

I felt them before I saw them clearly. I mean that almost literally. Every field scientist knows the feeling: the specific gravity of a room changes when original data is present. Copies are information. Originals are *evidence*. The cabinet held perhaps forty volumes, spine-labeled by season, and across each latch ran a paper seal stamped with a corporate crest I recognized the way you recognize a scar in a mirror — Merrow Consolidated. Cass's family crest, sitting on a shelf in a building her family's maps said didn't exist.

"Well," Cass said, from the doorway, in a voice I couldn't read at all.

"You knew about this place," Wren said. Not a question.

"I knew Kestrel referenced a station." Cass came in slowly, her headlamp beam moving across the wall chart like a slow finger. She stopped when it reached the upper contours — the face itself, the wall where two climbers had died while Wren Sandoval stood trial by reputation. Her light trembled. Just slightly. "I didn't know it was intact. I didn't know it was *warm*."

"The seals are Merrow's," Priya said quietly. She hadn't touched the cabinet. She was looking at Cass the way you look at a card player whose hand you can't see.

"They seal everything," Cass said. "It's not evidence of anything except bureaucracy."

"It's evidence of ownership," Priya said.

And Wren said, in the flat voice they use when they've already decided and are simply informing the universe: "We leave them."

* * *

Let me tell you about the argument, because there was one, and because I came out of it looking worse than I deserved, which is my other consistent talent.

Tomas wants them. That was the whole of my position, stated plainly enough. Forty volumes of primary survey data from a decade of unrecorded work on the most geologically slandered range on the continent — do you understand what that is? That's not a cabinet. That's a career. That's ten careers. That's the answer to questions I have been asking rocks since I was twenty-three years old, questions the ministry told me were closed, questions the concession holders told me were *proprietary*, which is the word powerful people use for *mine and you can't look*. Everything I have ever wanted to know is sitting behind a paper seal in a warm room on a shoulder of the Escarpment, and the man who found the way here — me, my routes, my readings, my stubborn little spectrometer that Priya teases me for babying — is being told to walk past it.

"We leave them," Wren said again.

"On what authority?" I asked, and immediately heard how it sounded, and said it anyway, because honesty is a compulsion with me and not always a virtue. "Whose? Ours? Yours? Because last I checked none of us owns this mountain, and the people who claim to want it sealed forever, and every hour those books sit here is an hour closer to a bulldozer."

"Tomas." Wren didn't raise their voice. They never raise their voice; they lower everyone else's instead. "Do you know why the seals are intact?"

"Because nobody's been here."

"Because nobody was *meant* to be here." Wren crossed to the cabinet but didn't touch it, stood looking at the crest the way you'd look at a snake you suspected was only mostly dead. "A station this size doesn't get abandoned with its records inside. Records get pulled. That's the first rule of closing a site — you burn it, box it, or ship it. Somebody chose to leave forty volumes of data in a powered building on a classified route, behind their own company's seal." They turned. "That's not abandonment. That's storage. And things that are stored are *checked on*."

The room got quiet in the way high rooms do, all the wind suddenly audible in the antenna wire outside, a thin mad violin note.

"You think someone's coming back for them," Odile said.

"I think someone never finished coming back for them," Wren said. "And I think we are standing in the middle of a story we've read exactly one page of. So no. We don't open sealed corporate archives in the middle of an illegal traverse with four days of weather margin. We photograph the cabinet, we mark the location, and if the world turns honest we come back with lawyers instead of ice axes."

"And if the world doesn't turn honest?" I asked. "If the permits lapse and the extraction crews move in? You said it yourself — anything found inside stays sealed. Forever. Those books become landfill under three hundred meters of tailings, and every question they answer dies with them, and I helped carry them to the grave myself. Is that the plan? Archaeology for machines?"

Wren looked at me for a long moment. There was something in their face I hadn't seen before — not anger. Recognition. Like they were looking at a younger version of someone, and grieving it.

"I know what it's like to be the person who takes the blame for what other people buried," they said. "I also know that the burial usually has a reason, and the reason is never good, and the people who dig first are the people who get buried deepest. You want to open that cabinet because it's *yours* — your discipline, your hunger, your whole career saying yes. Fine. But nothing in this room is yours. Nothing in this room is *ours*. It belongs to two dead climbers and a lie, and I am not going to let this crew become the next chapter of either."

I want to report that I argued better than I did. I want to report that I conceded gracefully, with dignity, a scientist honoring method over appetite. What actually happened is I stood there with my jaw tight and my hands in my pockets and hated Wren Sandoval for approximately ninety seconds with a purity I haven't managed since graduate school — and then I looked at the wall chart, at the beautiful doomed precision of it, at the contour lines running down the face where Kestrel ended, and I understood that Wren wasn't protecting the data from me.

They were protecting me from the data. From whatever it was that had made two sets of climbers' families mourn, and one guide's name into mud, and a corporation stamp its seal on the lid of all of it.

"We photograph everything visible," I said finally. "The chart. The racks. The instrument plates. If we can't take the library, we take the card catalog."

Wren nodded once. "Agreed."

Cass said nothing at all. I noticed that. I've been noticing it more lately — how quiet she goes whenever the seals come up, how her eyes move to the cabinet and away again like a tongue returning to a broken tooth. Priya notices too. Priya notices everything that blinks or hums, and Cass Merrow has been humming for days.

* * *

We worked until the light went amber and then blue. Odile catalogued the medical locker — stocked, incredibly, though the morphine vials had long since gone cloudy — and pronounced the station "the safest building in the range and therefore deeply suspicious." Priya wired a tap off the geothermal feed and charged our batteries to full for the first time since the trailhead, laughing like a child at a fountain of chocolate. I photographed the wall chart panel by panel, 214 frames, and if my hands lingered on the cabinet latch a moment longer than photography strictly required, well. Geologists are not treasure hunters. But we are not saints either.

It was Priya, at dusk, who killed the mood, because that is also among her talents.

She was at her receiver, running a sweep off the fresh charge, and she stopped laughing mid-breath. We all turned. She held up a hand — *wait* — and turned the gain down, then up, hunting through static with the patience of a woman tuning a piano in a hurricane.

"There's traffic," she said. "Low power, encrypted burst, directional. Coming from the south." She looked up at us, and her face in the lamplight was very still. "Somebody's talking on this mountain tonight. And it isn't us."

Outside, the wind had begun to change. I've spent enough seasons up high to know what that particular silence-before-pressure means, the way the sky stacks its clouds like a dealer stacking a deck. Wren stepped to the doorway and looked south a long time, toward the boundary we'd crossed four days ago, toward whatever was climbing up behind us through the dark.

"Storm coming," Wren said. "Three days, maybe more. We're pinned here." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to themselves: "Whatever's coming up that valley will be pinned too. Right on top of us."

The wind found the antenna wire and made it sing.
