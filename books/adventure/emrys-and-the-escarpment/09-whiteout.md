## 9. Whiteout

Here is what they don't tell you about storms at altitude: it isn't the wind you hate. It's the sound the tent makes between gusts, that soft nylon shudder, like something breathing just outside. Like something patient.

Day one, we told ourselves it was weather. Day two, we told ourselves it was bad luck. By day three nobody was telling themselves anything. We just sat in our respective nylon coffins and listened to the mountain try to decide whether it wanted us.

I'm Priya, by the way. Radio tech, insomniac, and the only person on this expedition whose job description includes the word "listen." Everyone else on this crew listens the way rocks listen — politely, and only to themselves. So when the storm pinned us at nine thousand feet on the shoulder of the Escarpment, it fell to me to hear what the mountain was actually saying.

What it said, at 0340 on the third night, was: *you are not alone up here.*

* * *

Let me back up, because the transmission makes more sense if you understand what three days in a tent does to five people who were already lying to each other.

Our camp was two tents and a cook shelter lashed into a wind scoop below a rib of grey rock. Wren had chosen the site in about ninety seconds, walking the perimeter with that flat-eyed competence that I'd initially found reassuring and had come to find slightly unnerving, the way you'd be unnerved by a chess computer that also did your taxes. Wren didn't choose sites. Wren *solved* them.

The storm arrived on schedule, because of course it did. Wren had called it two days earlier — "pressure's dropping, we'll get pinned at the shoulder, plan for seventy-two hours" — and had been so matter-of-fact about it that we'd all nodded along as if being trapped in a whiteout at altitude were a spa treatment we'd booked.

The rations math was the first thing to go wrong. Not catastrophically — Wren had built in a margin, because Wren built margins the way other people built grudges, deep and load-bearing. But the cornice incident had cost us a day, and a day at altitude is not a day. A day is fuel you burn just existing in the cold. Wren recalculated at breakfast on day one and quietly cut portions by a sixth, and nobody said anything, because everyone had done the same math and everyone had gotten the same answer and everyone was pretending they hadn't.

Odile took it best. She was our medic and had the metabolism of a hummingbird and the temperament of a wetland — everything flowed over her and she stayed green. She spent the storm days patching the blister on Tomas's heel, re-taping Cass's wrist, and teaching me to splint a forearm using a tent pole and my own bootlaces, on the theory that bored medics make mistakes and busy ones don't.

Tomas took it worst. Not loudly — Tomas didn't do loudly — but he had the specific restless misery of a man separated from his data. He'd brought a field kit that weighed more than my radio rig and half his body weight in sample bags, and being unable to *collect* anything was eating him alive. By day two he'd taken to chipping at the rock wall inside the cook shelter with a geological hammer, softly, like a man biting his nails.

"Tomas," Wren said, on the second morning, without opening their eyes.

"It's dolomite."

"It's our shelter."

He stopped. He started again an hour later, softer. Wren let it go, which I noted, because Wren letting things go was itself data.

And Cass took it somewhere in the middle, which was somehow the most unsettling place. Cass Merrow in motion was all certainty — she gave orders on the trail like the ridge had been deeded to her family, which, I eventually learned, was not entirely a joke. But pinned down, with nothing to command, she got quiet in a way that had edges. She'd sit in the cook shelter with her hands wrapped around a cooling cup of water — we were rationing fuel too, so no hot drinks, a deprivation Cass bore like a personal insult — and stare at the storm wall, and whatever was behind her eyes was not weather.

Wren and Cass orbited each other the whole time like two magnets in a box. Never quite touching, never quite apart. I'd clocked it on day one of the approach: these two had history, the kind with capital letters, and neither of them was going to say so. Fine. I'm a radio tech. Half my job is not commenting on what I overhear.

The other half is listening. So let's get back to 0340.

* * *

I had the radio in my tent because I never let it out of arm's reach, a habit from three years on a seismic survey ship where the radio was the only thing between you and becoming a statistic with an obituary nobody could write because nobody knew where you'd sunk. It was a good rig — Cass had paid for good, one of the few unambiguous facts about this expedition — and I kept it on a low-power scan cycle through the licensed bands, mostly out of professional discipline and partly because the static was better company than my own thoughts.

The storm made the bands a mess. Atmospheric noise like a waterfall, propagation garbage, the whole spectrum sounding like it had been put through a blender. I'd heard nothing but mush for three days.

Then, at 0340, inside the mush: a voice.

Not on our frequency. Adjacent to it, bleeding through, the way voices bleed when someone's transmitting high-power on a band they shouldn't be using. I sat up in my bag, heart doing something unscheduled, and turned the gain up and the squelch down until the static resolved into shapes.

"—copy, element two, hold at the—" burst of wash "—boundary marker, over."

Boundary marker. Our boundary marker. The one we'd crossed on day one, where Wren had gone still for a moment with their boot on the line like a man testing ice.

I grabbed my notebook and wrote down what I could pull out of the noise. Fragments. "Element two." "Hold." "Concession expires" — that part came through clean, which felt like the mountain underlining it. "Package" — twice. And once, unmistakably, a word that turned my mouth dry: "Merrow."

Then the voice was gone, swallowed, and the static went back to being just static, and I sat in the dark with my pen hovering over the notebook and the wind trying its zipper pulls one more time.

Element two. Hold at the boundary. Package.

You don't need a license to know what a recovery team sounds like. You just need to have spent enough of your life around people who are looking for something they intend to take.

* * *

I didn't sleep. At 0630, when the wind dropped to merely homicidal and Wren called a cook shift, I crawled into the cook shelter with my notebook and my radio and the specific posture of a person with news.

Wren took one look at me and stopped mid-motion, kettle in hand. "Report."

"Caught a transmission. Three-forty this morning. Adjacent band, high power." I read them the fragments. The shelter got very quiet, the way it does when five people are all breathing carefully.

"Distance?" Wren asked. Not *are you sure*. Not *could it be anything else*. Wren went straight to distance, which told me Wren had already accepted it, which told me Wren had been expecting it, which was its own small horror.

"Can't range it off one reception. But the power profile says vehicle-mounted or base camp, and the band they were using says they don't care who hears them."

"Which means they're not hiding," Odile said. She'd come in behind me, hair a static halo. "Why wouldn't they hide?"

"Because they think we're pinned," Wren said. "Because they think they have time. And because—" a pause, the kettle set down with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a decision being made, "—they don't think we have a radio."

"We have a radio," I said.

"We have a radio," Wren agreed, "because Cass insisted on the good rig." And Wren turned and looked at Cass, who had just come in from the tent line with an armful of snow for melting, and the look had weight to it, the kind of weight that bends things.

Cass set the snow down. "I insisted on the good rig because I don't cut corners on safety."

"You insist on a lot of things," Wren said.

"Here it comes," Tomas murmured, to his sample bags.

It came.

I want to be fair to both of them, because what happened next wasn't a fight, not really. It was two people who'd been holding a wire between them for ten years finally pulling it taut in front of witnesses. But I'm a radio tech, and fairness is not my department. Accuracy is. So here's the transcript, as close as I can reconstruct it, with the wind noise stripped out.

"You knew they'd come," Wren said. "You knew a recovery team was a possibility, and you didn't say it in the briefing."

"I said the route would be contested—"

"You said *permits*. You said *timelines*. You said we'd be off the range before the concession lapsed and nobody would be the wiser. You did not say anyone would come *after* us."

"Because I didn't know—"

"You suspected." Wren's voice never rose. That was the worst part. Wren angry was Wren precise. "You suspected, and you decided the risk was ours to carry and the information was yours to keep. That's not leadership, Cass. That's the same arithmetic your father used on Kestrel."

The silence after that had texture. Odile's hand had moved, very slightly, toward the med kit — not for supplies, I realized later, just for something to hold.

"My father," Cass said, and her voice did rise, finally, cracking on the second word like lake ice, "led six people into that range and brought four out, and spent the rest of his life—"

"Spent the rest of his life *what*?" Wren asked. "Go on. Finish it. You've had ten years to find the verb."

Cass looked at Wren for a long moment. And here's the thing I keep coming back to, the thing I've turned over maybe four hundred times since: she looked like she was going to answer. Like the whole story was right there behind her teeth, paid for, ready. Her father. Kestrel. Whatever actually happened up there above the wall, the thing Wren had taken the fall for, the thing that had gotten two people killed and one person erased from the profession.

Then the wind hit the shelter like a fist, the whole structure cracked and leaned, and we all grabbed for the poles, and when the gust passed, Cass's face had been reassembled into the boardroom version.

"When we're off the mountain," she said. "You get the whole of it when we're off the mountain. Not pinned in a storm with a recovery team inbound. I'm not having that conversation at nine thousand feet."

"You've been having it at nine thousand feet for ten years," Wren said. "You're just the only one who could hear it."

Cass picked her snow back up. Her hands were steady. Her jaw wasn't. "Melting rotation's yours," she said to me, and went out into the white.

* * *

After that, the day arranged itself into the particular misery of a storm camp with a countdown on it.

Wren ran the numbers aloud, once, in that flat guide voice that made even arithmetic sound like a verdict. Storm forecast to break — if it broke — in thirty-six hours. Rations to that point: adequate. Rations past that point: a negotiation. Retreat: two days back down terrain we'd need visibility to cross. Onward: three days to the next viable camp, through ground Wren described, after a pause, as "unmapped."

"Unmapped meaning what," Tomas said, "unsurveyed, or unmapped meaning Kestrel mapped it and the maps were confiscated?"

The pause before Wren answered was long enough to be an answer. "Both."

"Great," said Odile, cheerfully, in the tone of a woman filing it under *later*. "Priya. Your recovery team. If they're holding at the boundary, and the storm's pinning us, it's pinning them too, yes?"

"Unless they've got shelter we don't know about," I said. "Which, given that they're the ones with the concession paperwork, they might."

"Then we're all just sitting in the weather," Odile said, "waiting to see who moves first."

"Nobody's moving in a whiteout," Wren said. "Not us, not them. Whiteout kills the competent and the incompetent at the same rate." A beat. "The difference is the competent stay put."

It was the closest thing to comfort on offer, and we took it.

The hours after that I mostly spent on the radio, because it was the one useful thing I could do. I mapped the interference patterns, logged every fragment the storm let through. The recovery team — if that's what they were — transmitted twice more, both times brief, both times in that clipped procedural cadence that I now know better than my own heartbeat. "Element one, confirm." "Hold position." "Package integrity." Nobody said *climbers*. Nobody said *people*. We were the package, or we were in the way of the package, and I honestly couldn't decide which was worse.

Tomas sat with me for an hour of it, uncharacteristically quiet, watching me log fragments in my notebook.

"You could raise them," he said at one point, not looking at me. "If you wanted. Ask who they are."

"I could," I agreed.

"You're not going to."

"No."

"Why not?"

I thought about it. The honest answer was that the radio had told me everything I needed: *hold at the boundary, concession expires, package, Merrow.* The other honest answer was that some transmissions, once you answer them, can't be unheard. I settled on: "Because right now they don't know our position, and the only thing keeping that true is that nobody on this mountain is talking. I'd like to keep it that way."

Tomas nodded slowly, and I got the distinct sense he was filing my answer somewhere in his vast internal archive, next to the sample bags and the chipped dolomite, for later examination.

* * *

Third night. Wind down to a scream instead of a roar, which meant the front's back edge, which meant, if Wren's forecast held, maybe twelve hours of grace.

I was on the 0200 radio watch, wrapped in my bag in the cook shelter because my tent had developed a personality and it was a bad one. The storm was dying the way storms do, grudgingly, in long diminishing fits. Between two of them, on the adjacent band, the voice came back one more time.

Clearer, now. Closer, or the atmosphere had simply stopped lying.

"—element two, be advised, weather clearing oh-six-hundred, resume advance. Confirm visual on target camp by—" wash "—or fall back to the marker. Over."

*Target camp.* Not "the climbers." Not "the party." *Target camp.* Confirm visual.

I wrote it down. I checked my watch: 0217. Weather clearing oh-six-hundred. Resume advance.

Then, because I am a professional and not merely a frightened person with an antenna, I did the thing I should have done on the first night: I ran a proper bearing on the signal, using the loop antenna and the two fixed peaks I could just see through the shelter's vent gap whenever lightning lit the cloud from inside. It wasn't precise. It was a wedge, not a line. But the wedge pointed down-range, back along our own route, toward the boundary marker.

Behind us. Exactly where Wren had said, in that voice that never rose, that they would be.

I sat with the notebook open on my knee for a long time, listening to the storm finish dying, doing the math I didn't want to do. If they advanced at oh-six-hundred, and we broke camp at first light, and the terrain between here and the unmapped ground was what it was —

They wouldn't catch us. Not on the trail. Not if we moved clean.

But we weren't going to move clean. We were going to move tired, on short rations, into terrain our guide called *both*, with a crew that had spent three days in a nylon box learning exactly how much they trusted each other, which was to say: exactly as far as they'd been told, and no further.

At 0530 I ducked into Wren's tent without asking, which on this expedition was roughly equivalent to disarming a trap with your teeth. Wren was already awake — of course Wren was already awake — sitting up in the bag with the route notes open, watching the tent walls go from black to bruise.

"Clearing by oh-six-hundred," I said. "They're advancing. Bearing puts them at the boundary or past it." I handed over the notebook. "And Wren — they're not calling us climbers. They're calling us the target."

Wren read the fragments twice. I watched their face do something I hadn't seen it do before: not fear, exactly. Recalculation. The chess computer, re-solving the board.

"They'll move fast once the weather lifts," Wren said. "Vehicle support to the marker, then boots. Eight, maybe ten hours behind us if we're clean." A pause. "We're not going to be clean."

"Then what are we going to be?"

Wren looked at me, and for a second the guide mask slipped and I saw underneath it something older and tireder and, strangely, more alive than anything I'd seen on this whole trip.

"We're going to be off-trail," Wren said. "Wake the others. We break camp in forty minutes — and Priya? Nobody touches that radio again until I say. From here on, the only thing we broadcast is nothing."

Outside, the wind was falling away into silence, and the silence was worse. It sounded exactly like something patient, just outside, waiting for us to move.
