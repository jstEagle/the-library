## 10. Cass's Version

The storm arrived the way my father used to arrive at dinner: late, loud, and with no intention of leaving until it had ruined everything.

Three days pinned in tents at nine thousand feet. Three days of nylon snapping like applause for a performance nobody asked for. Priya had rigged the radios to a trickle charge off Tomas's solar panel, which meant we spent our waking hours listening to static and each other breathing. Odile counted calories out loud in a voice that made rationing sound like a threat. Wren checked anchors twice a day and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of weather.

I'd been waiting three days for someone to ask me about Kestrel.

Nobody did. That was the thing about this crew—Wren had assembled them, or I had assembled them for Wren, and either way they'd all been chosen because they knew how not to ask questions. It was a skill I'd grown up around. My father's dinner table was full of people who could hold a secret the way other men held a fork: competently, without thinking about it, until one day you noticed your whole life had been organized around the grip.

On the third night, the wind dropped just enough that the tents stopped screaming and started muttering. I unzipped into the vestibule of the big tent where Wren sat mending a crampon strap by headlamp, hands moving with that infuriating economy of theirs, and I decided the silence had gone on long enough.

"You want to know," I said. "You've wanted to know since I said the word *Kestrel* in your kitchen. So ask."

Wren didn't look up. "I'm working."

"You've been working on that strap for an hour."

"It's a bad strap."

"Cass." Odile's voice from the smaller tent, muffled, amused. "Let them mend."

"I'm going to tell it anyway," I said. "So you can mend or listen. Your call."

That got the headlamp raised. Wren's face in the light was the same as it had been ten years ago at the inquiry—careful, closed, watching me the way you watch ice you don't trust. Ten years older. Ten years more careful. I'd done that. Part of it, anyway. I could itemize my contributions if pressed.

"Tomas, Priya—you should hear this too," I called. "It concerns everyone who's standing on this mountain because of me."

They came. Of course they came. Curiosity is the one ration nobody rations.

I sat back against my pack and told it.

* * *

"My father didn't climb," I began. "People assume he did, because he built Merrow Alpine Outfitters and put his name on half the huts in these ranges. But Aldous Merrow never went above the treeline if he could help it. He was a businessman who happened to sell mountains to people. He used to say climbing was a product with terrible margins and excellent marketing."

Tomas shifted. "Charming man."

"He was worse than charming. He was sincere. That's what made him dangerous. He believed every word of the company line—that access was a public good, that the concession system protected the ranges from worse fates, that Merrow stood for stewardship. And when Kestrel happened, he believed so hard in the story that the truth never stood a chance."

"Kestrel was his expedition," Wren said. Not a question. They'd known. Of course they'd known—they'd been on it.

"His last one. His legacy project. Twelve years of permits, four years of planning, and a route above the escarpment wall that two previous surveys had written off as unprotectable. He wanted to prove it could be done safely. Not climbed—*safely*. There's a difference, and it's the whole story." I pulled my gloves off, flexed fingers gone stiff with cold even inside the tent. "He hand-picked the team. Senior guides, a surveyor, a doctor. And two apprentices. Junior guides, cheap, hungry, grateful to be included."

"Wren Sandoval," Odile said quietly.

"And Dev Raskin," I said. "Who doesn't get to be a footnote anymore, so let's say the name properly. Dev Raskin, twenty-four years old, best young ice climber in the southern ranges, and dead above the escarpment wall along with Marcus Hale, who was fifty-one and should have known better, except the person who should have known best was my father."

The wind gusted against the fly. Nobody moved.

"The official record says storm. Whiteout, radio failure, the retreat line buried under new snow, two climbers separated and lost. It says the guide of record—" I looked at Wren, and made myself keep looking— "made a judgment call to push the summit window and got caught. It says the inquiry found procedural failures but no criminal negligence. It says Merrow Outfitters settled civil claims generously and voluntarily suspended high-altitude programming as a mark of respect."

"That's the version that ended my career," Wren said. Flat. No heat in it, which was somehow worse than heat.

"Yes. And here's what I know that isn't in any version." I'd rehearsed this in hotel rooms and airport lounges for a decade, and it still came out of me like something being surgically removed. "Two weeks after the funerals, my father came home early. I was nineteen, home from university, and I heard him in the study talking to our lawyer and the concession administrator—Grieves, the old one, before the current crowd bought him out. The door was thick but the house was old. I heard enough.

"They weren't discussing grief. They were discussing exposure. The retreat line—the fixed ropes and wands that were supposed to be the escape route off the upper wall—had been pulled. Early. Before the storm. Pulled on my father's direct order, because the survey team had found something on the shoulder and he didn't want the route documented until Merrow controlled the mineral rights underneath it. Pulling the line would force the government survey to reroute away from the strike. It was supposed to be temporary. A week, maybe two. Then the front came in forty hours ahead of forecast, and two climbers went looking for a retreat line that wasn't there."

Priya had gone very still. "Pulled. On purpose. To hide a mineral strike."

"To control one. There's a difference, and it's also the whole story." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "My father spent the rest of his life telling himself those were different things. That he hadn't killed anyone; he'd made a business decision, and the weather had killed them. He believed it the way bedrock believes in itself. When the inquiry started asking inconvenient questions about the line, the answer was ready-made: the storm took it. Storms take lines. Everyone knows storms take lines. And there was a junior guide whose field notes showed she'd—" I corrected myself. "*They'd.* Whose field notes showed the team had discussed pushing past the turnaround point. One signature on a waiver, one ambitious note in a margin, and suddenly the story had a villain who wasn't a Merrow."

Wren's hands had stopped moving on the strap. "You're saying he framed me."

"I'm saying he didn't have to frame anyone. That's the elegant horror of it. He just declined to correct the record. You'd pushed the window—I'm not going to insult you by pretending you hadn't, you told me yourself once, drunk, in Innsbruck, that you'd wanted the summit so badly you could taste it. The inquiry only needed a reason, and you handed them one, and my father handed them a pen." I swallowed. "Dev's family got a settlement and a story. Your family got a blacklist. And the strike stayed exactly where he wanted it: undocumented, unmapped, and unclaimed by anyone but us."

Tomas spoke carefully, the way geologists do when they're about to say something about money. "And now the concession lapses. Which means the strike passes out of Merrow hands regardless."

"Now the concession lapses," I agreed. "My father died three years ago believing he'd kept it all. The current board doesn't even know the full history—they think the Escarpment closure is environmental theater. But the recovery team coming up behind us? That's not environmental. Somebody over there has found fragments of the old survey. Enough to be frightened of."

"So why you?" Odile asked. "Why hire a blacklisted guide to walk into your family's crime scene?"

"Because I'm the only one left who knows where everything is buried." I said it plainly. "Literally. The station on the shoulder, the caches, the original survey logs—my father burned most of the paper copies, but Kestrel ran on redundancy, it was doctrine. Field logs triplicated. One set home, one set to the concession office, one cached on the mountain. The mountain set is still up here. I've spent ten years and most of my inheritance trying to get to it legally. Permits denied, petitions denied, the closure zone expanded twice. The mountain keeps what the mountain keeps—unless someone walks in and takes it back."

"And the crew," Wren said slowly. "Odile lost her license over a triage call. Tomas was fired for refusing to sit on data. Priya—"

"Priya broadcast a corporate frequency log she shouldn't have had," Priya said, not looking up from her radio. "Allegedly."

"Everyone here is deniable," I said. "If we're caught, we're a group of disgraced individuals with no affiliation, no sponsor, no paper trail back to Merrow. If we succeed, the records come out and the company that destroyed them is finished. I designed it that way. I'm not proud of it. I'm telling you because you deserve to know you were hired as much for your liabilities as your skills."

Silence, except the wind doing its slow arithmetic against the fabric.

Then Odile, dry as chalk: "Well. At least the pay makes sense now."

* * *

Wren didn't speak for a long time after the others crawled back to their tents. They sat with the mended strap across their knees, turning it over, and I recognized the gesture because I'd seen my father do the same thing with a pen when a number didn't work.

"Say it," I said finally. "Whatever it is. Say it."

"You waited ten years." Wren's voice was level. "Ten years, and you couldn't have walked into the inquiry room and said *the line was pulled*?"

"On whose evidence? Mine? I was nineteen, I'd overheard a conversation through a door, and the moment I opened my mouth, my father's lawyers would have made me a liar in front of six journalists before lunch. Or worse—a Merrow. Do you know what a Merrow's word does in that room? It's radioactive. Anything I touched would have been contaminated by association. Including you." I heard my own voice climb and forced it down. "I needed proof that didn't pass through me. Physical proof. Logs, instruments, dates. Something the lawyers couldn't burn. That's what's on this mountain, and that's why you're here, and yes—it took ten years, and every one of them I knew what my name was costing you."

"Do you?" Wren set the strap down. For the first time all trip, they looked at me straight, and there was nothing careful in it at all. "Because from where I sat, Cass, the cost was mine. The hearings were mine. Every trail job under a fake name, every client who Googled me mid-route, every winter wondering if this was the year the industry finished forgetting I existed—that was mine. You got to feel guilty in nice hotels."

"Yes," I said. "I did. And I'm not going to stand in a tent at nine thousand feet and tell you my guilt was heavy too, because compared to yours it wasn't, and you'd see through it, and you'd be right to."

That landed somewhere. I watched it land. Wren looked away first—not in defeat, I thought, but the way you look away from a view that's too big to take in all at once.

"Why tell me now?" they asked. "Why not at the trailhead? Why not in your kitchen?"

"Because at the trailhead you'd have turned around." I smiled, and it hurt. "And because down there, it's a confession. Up here, it's a briefing. You needed to hear it at altitude, where it's actionable."

Wren almost laughed. Almost. "You really are your father's daughter."

"No," I said. "That's the point. I'm everything he wasn't willing to be. Which is a witness."

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, when the storm had settled into its long grinding shift and the tents creaked like an old ship—I heard movement outside. I unzipped far enough to see Wren standing at the edge of the camp, headlamp off, facing into the white where the escarpment wall would be if the cloud ever lifted. Just standing there. Reading a mountain neither of us could see.

I thought about going out. I thought about all the things I might say that would make it worse, which at this point was most of them.

Instead I lay back down and listened to the wind, and I understood—for the first time, really understood, in my body instead of my briefs—that whatever we found in the logs, it wouldn't be absolution. Not for my father, who was beyond it. Not for me, who'd helped bury the story simply by inheriting the silence. Maybe not even for Wren, because some verdicts arrive too late to matter to anything but paper.

But proof isn't for the dead. Proof is for the living, so the living stop lying to themselves.

In the morning, Priya shook me awake with her hand on my shoulder and her radio log open on her knee, and her face told me before she said a word.

"Weather break tomorrow," she said. "Six hours, maybe eight. And Cass—we've got a problem. Someone's been transmitting on our backtrail. Short bursts. Encrypted." She turned the log toward me. "Somebody on this mountain is talking to the recovery team."

Behind her, in the grey light, I saw Wren already awake, already watching me.

And I understood that the briefing was over, and the mountain had begun taking testimony.
