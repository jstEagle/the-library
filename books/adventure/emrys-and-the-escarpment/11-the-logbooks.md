## 11. The Logbooks

I want to say it was curiosity. That's the version I'll tell if anyone ever asks, the version that makes me sound like a scientist instead of a thief. Curiosity is a respectable word. It has a lab coat on. But the truth is uglier and simpler: I've never once in my life walked past a closed box without wanting to know what was in it, and Wren Sandoval knew that about me before I knew it about myself.

That's why they ordered the logbooks left in the station.

Not because they were dangerous—though God, they were dangerous—but because they knew me. Ten minutes in that derelict survey station and Wren had read the whole room: the geologist with the hungry eyes, the man who'd already pocketed three rock samples from a mountain he had no legal right to touch. *We leave it,* they said, in that flat guide's voice that isn't a suggestion so much as weather. *All of it. We came to map, not to dig.*

And I said yes. I said yes the way you say yes to a dentist.

* * *

Here's the thing about the station on the shoulder: it shouldn't still be warm.

That's what got me, that first hour, before the storm closed in. Odile found the geothermal tap—a hairline fissure venting heat into the building's foundation, some accident of engineering that nobody had ever mapped—and suddenly the whole place made a horrible kind of sense. Ten years of abandonment, and the instruments still ticking. The diesel smell still faint in the air. The kettle on the stove still sitting where somebody set it down in a hurry, the morning somebody walked out of here and never came back.

Kestrel equipment. I knew the era from the fittings—the old Merrow Consolidated survey stamps, the serial numbers from a procurement batch that had been quietly written out of the company's public records. I'd spent two years of my career studying this range's geology from a hundred kilometers away, working off satellite data and secondhand cores, because this was the one formation on the continent no one was allowed to sample. And here it all was. Sitting in the dark. Keeping itself warm for us.

Wren posted watch rotations and we settled in for the blow. The storm arrived around dusk like it had an appointment. By full dark the windows were white and the wind was trying to pry the roof loose, and there was nothing for any of us to do but sit in a warm dead man's office and not think about the sealed crates in the back room.

I thought about the sealed crates in the back room.

* * *

Priya slept first, then Odile. Cass sat up with her tea going cold, staring at the wall like it owed her money. Wren took second watch by the window, a shape against the white, so still they might have been part of the mountain already.

I told myself I was just going to look at the crates.

The back room had been a survey office once: drafting table, map cabinet, a wall of sample drawers with little brass labels. The crates were stacked under the table—four of them, steel-banded, stenciled with the Kestrel bird. The bands were the kind you cut, not the kind you unbuckle. Somebody had wanted these shut. Somebody had wanted them shut *specifically*, and had gone to the trouble of making sure that anyone who disagreed would have to work at it, and would know, while they worked, exactly how much they weren't supposed to be there.

I had a multi-tool in my pocket. I'd had it in my pocket since the threshold marker, when Wren crossed the line of their own banishment and something in their face made me understand, for the first time, that this trip was not about mapping.

The bands came off easier than they should have. That's the detail that still wakes me up—the ease of it. Ten years of silence and the steel gave on the second notch, like the crates had been waiting.

Field logbooks. Six of them, canvas-bound, water-stained at the corners. And under them, wrapped in oilcloth, a seventh thing: a core sample log, the official kind, with the assay results typed on carbon paper in that beautiful old-fashioned font that makes everything look true.

I told myself I'd read one page. Just to confirm provenance. Just to know what we were leaving behind. There's a scientific argument for that—there's a scientific argument for everything, that's what I love about science, it will hold your coat while you do whatever you were going to do anyway.

I read one page.

* * *

The first three logbooks were what you'd expect. Survey notes. Route descriptions. A hand-drawn stratigraphic column that got more excited as it went—the handwriting loosening, the margins filling up with underlinings. Whoever wrote them—initials R.M., a neat, patient hand—had been watching the formation tell its story and could barely keep his composure on the page.

*Vein structure continuous across four sampled faces. Grade exceeds anything in the published literature for this class of deposit. If the eastern extension holds, this is not a mine. This is the mine. This is the one they will fight wars over.*

I did the arithmetic in my head, the way you do, the way I can't not do. Grade times tonnage times the price of the metal in a world that's running out of it. The number that came back had eleven digits. Maybe twelve. A mineral strike worth more than the GDP of several of the countries I've worked in. Enough to buy the concession a thousand times over. Enough to explain why Merrow Consolidated had spent a decade and an unknown sum of money making sure no one ever came back to check.

Enough to explain why the permits were lapsing, and why the land was being sealed, and why a recovery team was—though I didn't know that yet—already packing to come up behind us.

I should have stopped there. I know that now. There is a particular kind of knowledge that behaves like a crevasse: the bridge holds fine until you're standing on it.

The fourth logbook was dated eleven days before the accident.

The handwriting had changed. Still R.M., but tighter now, pressed hard into the paper, the letters of a man writing fast because he was afraid of running out of time.

*Merrow's orders are to close the eastern face and pull the survey team down. I asked why. He said the assay results were inconclusive. I have the assays. They are not inconclusive. He said the retreat line on the col route was "under review." We have been on this mountain nine weeks. The retreat line has been fine for nine weeks.*

*Something is wrong. The company does not seal a billion-dollar discovery. The company seals people.*

* * *

I sat down on the floor of that back room with the crate lid across my knees and the storm screaming over the roof, and I read the rest of it in one sitting, the way you read about a car crash, the way you cannot not read.

The fifth logbook was the last one R.M. finished. The entries were daily now, short, clipped. The survey team had been split—half ordered down to the station, half kept on the eastern face "to secure equipment." R.M. had been sent to the station, which he understood, with the terrible clarity of a man writing his own autopsy, was a way of separating the people who knew the numbers from the people who knew the route.

*Fixed rope on the col retreat line inspected this morning. Anchors sound. If anything happens on the face, the way home is the way home.*

That entry was dated the day before the storm.

The sixth logbook was not in R.M.'s hand. The writing was hurried, slanted, written in pencil, written—I could tell from the grit pressed into the pages—in a tent, in wind, in the last hours of somebody's life, or very nearly. It was a route log. Times, positions, weather. And then, dated the night the storm hit Kestrel:

*Retreat line gone. Col anchors cut. Not failed—cut. Clean cuts, both anchors, the kind you make with a kit. Six of us on the face. The rope was our way down. Someone took the rope.*

*We are going to try the east chute. If anyone reads this: the anchors were cut from above, and the only people above us were ours.*

*Tell the truth. Tell it loud. Don't let them make it weather.*

Two climbers died above the escarpment wall that night. The official report called it a storm casualty, a broken radio, a route pushed past its margin by an overreaching guide. I'd heard the story the way everyone in the profession had heard it, secondhand and smoothed over, a cautionary tale with a name attached. The name attached was Wren Sandoval.

The name in this book was not.

The seventh thing—the oilcloth bundle—was the core log. Full assay series, eastern extension, dated, signed, countersigned. The numbers that made R.M. write *this is the one they will fight wars over.* And clipped to the back of it, a single typed memo on Merrow Consolidated letterhead, one paragraph long, no signature. I read it four times. It doesn't get better with repetition.

*Concession value is contingent on the deposit remaining unconfirmed. Confirm nothing. The survey incident provides the necessary pretext to suspend all independent access. See that the guide is held responsible. The route will be closed by season's end. — M.*

*The guide.* Not a name. A function. A line item. Ten years ago somebody read that memo and went back to work, and a guide I have spent two weeks walking behind—a guide who crossed their own banishment line without hesitation and who ordered us to leave these crates shut, who knew, God help them, they knew what was in here—lost everything they had to a sentence with no signature.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for the candle to burn down a finger's width. Long enough for the cold to find me through the floor, though the building was warm. Some kinds of cold come from inside.

Here is what I want you to understand about what I did next, because it's the part I keep turning over: I closed the logbooks. I put them back in the crate. I put the lid on and I pressed the cut bands flat so it would look—so it would *be*—as if the crate had never been opened.

And it didn't work. That's the horror of it, the real horror, the thing they don't put in the ethics lectures. You can't close a book and have it be unopened. The knowledge doesn't go back in the box. I was carrying it now. I would carry it up the rest of this mountain and down the other side and into whatever came after, and every person on this crew would carry it too, whether they ever read a word of it or not, because I was never going to be able to look at Wren Sandoval the same way again, and Wren was going to see that in my face the way you see weather coming.

I heard the floorboard behind me and I didn't even flinch. I think some part of me had been waiting for it.

Wren stood in the doorway with the storm-light behind them, and their eyes went from me, to the crate, to the band I was still holding in my fist like a confession. They didn't shout. I almost wish they had. What they did was worse. They crossed the room, took the band out of my hand, set it on the drafting table with a small precise click, and said, quietly:

"How much did you read?"

"All of it."

"Say it out loud."

And I did. I said the numbers, and I said the cut anchors, and I said the memo, and I watched ten years land on Wren's face all at once—not surprise, that was the terrible thing, not surprise but *confirmation*, the look of a person who has spent a decade being told they're crazy and has just been handed the receipt. They sat down slowly on the sample drawers, like their legs had been renegotiated, and for a long moment the only sound was the wind working at the roof.

Then the door to the main room opened and Cass Merrow stood there, and I watched her see the open crate, and I watched her see Wren's face, and I watched her understand—faster than I did, much faster—that the thing her family had buried on this mountain had just come up out of the ground.

"We need to talk," she said. "All of us. Now."

Behind her, out in the main room, Priya looked up from the radio with an expression I didn't understand yet, and wouldn't, until the storm broke.

"First," she said, "you need to hear this."
