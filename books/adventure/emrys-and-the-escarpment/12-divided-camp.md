## 12. Divided Camp

The logbooks sat in the middle of the tent like something that had died there.

Tomas had laid them out on his sleeping pad with the reverence of a man arranging relics—three bound volumes, canvas covers gone grey and soft with a decade of cold, their spines cracked where he'd forced them open against Cass's explicit instruction and my own. The pages were furred at the edges from damp that had gotten in before the geothermal tap dried the air, and the ink had bled in places, but the writing was legible. Meticulous, even. Survey handwriting, the kind drilled into people who know their notes might be the only thing that survives them.

I hadn't touched them. I'd read every word over Tomas's shoulder by headlamp, and I still hadn't touched them.

Outside, the wind had dropped to that post-storm murmur that sounds almost like mercy if you don't know better. The snow was settling into the crust it would keep for a day or two, firm enough to travel on, loud enough to broadcast every step to anyone listening. Somewhere south of us, behind two ridgelines and a day of whiteout, the recovery team was either digging out or moving. We'd heard nothing since Priya's radio caught the garbled transmission. That silence was worse than static.

"Say it again," Odile said. She was sitting with her back against the gear wall, arms crossed, medic's posture—the stance she took when she was bracing for bad news instead of delivering it. "The part about the retreat line. Slowly."

Tomas adjusted his glasses, which he did when he was buying time. "Page forty-one. Kestrel's route log, day nine." He turned the book toward her, careful not to smudge. "They fixed anchors across the upper icefield on the descent line. Standard practice—fixed lines so a tired party can retreat fast. The entry says the lines were *pulled*. Not storm-damaged. Not buried. Pulled, on the orders of—" he tapped the initials, "—M. Merrow. Marcus Merrow. Cass's father. Two days before the storm hit."

"Why would you pull your own retreat line?" Priya asked. She already knew. We all already knew. She asked anyway because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be dealt with.

"Because the survey wasn't finished," Tomas said. "Because they'd found the strike—the iridium-platinum body under the shoulder—and Merrow didn't want anyone else coming up to check. A pulled retreat line means the mountain's closed. It means *nobody goes up, nobody comes down*. It buys you a season of exclusive access." He looked around the tent. "It also means that when the weather turned, the climbers above the wall had no way down except the face. And the face in a storm is a coffin."

"And Wren took the blame," Odile said quietly.

"Wren took the blame," Tomas agreed, "for a broken radio and a missed window and two dead climbers, when the actual decision—the one that killed them—was made in a base camp tent over a satellite phone, by a man who wanted his mineral strike kept quiet until the concession paperwork cleared."

The stove ticked as it cooled. Nobody moved.

I found that I was looking at my own hands, which is what I do when I need them to be doing something other than shaking. Ten years I'd carried this. Ten years of false names and low-stakes trail jobs and clients who reported me because a blacklist follows you like a smell, and the whole time, the truth had been sitting in a locked box on a shoulder of rock four miles from where I slept last night. Guarded by a geothermal tap nobody mapped. Waiting.

I should have felt relief. That's what I'd imagined, all those years—relief, vindication, the great unclenching. What I actually felt was closer to grief. Because the dead were still dead, and no page number changed that, and because the man who'd done it had died rich and respected three years ago with a mountain named after his family's company on the maps.

"Okay," Cass said.

She'd been silent since Tomas opened the books an hour ago. Silent in a way I recognized—she'd gone still the way people go still when they're standing somewhere structurally unsound and don't want to shift the load. Now she unfolded herself from her corner and crouched by the logbooks, and I watched her look at her father's initials for a long moment before she spoke.

"Okay," she said again. "We have maybe thirty-six hours before that recovery team reaches this camp, less if they're good. We have data worth—" she exhaled, "—billions. We have proof of corporate manslaughter, evidence tampering, and a cover-up that reached into the permitting office. And we are five exhausted people on the wrong side of a closed concession with one working radio and a guide who's legally banned from the very ground we're standing on." She looked up. "So we decide. Now. Together. What happens to these books."

"Sell it," Tomas said immediately. "That was always on the table. There are buyers—a dozen consortiums would pay eight figures for the survey data alone, before you even get to the criminal evidence. We split it, we disappear, none of us ever works again unless we want to."

"You'd be selling it back to them," Priya said. "Whoever buys it buries it. That's how this works. You don't get rich off a secret like this—you get *hired*. Or you get gone."

"Then publish it. Fine. Publish it." Tomas spread his hands. "Anonymous drop, full scan, every page. Let the world choke on it."

"That's the recovery team's worst case," Priya said. "Which makes it our most dangerous one. They're not coming up here for exercise, Tomas. They're coming because those books are the only thing standing between the company and its permits. People who kill to protect a mineral strike don't negotiate with whistleblowers. They *lose* whistleblowers."

"So burn them." Odile said it flatly, and we all turned. She shrugged. "I'm serious. Look at the board. Sell: someone gets rich, truth dies. Publish: we probably die, truth maybe lives. Burn: we walk away, everyone lives, truth stays dead—but so does the money, and the strike gets sealed with the concession anyway when the permits lapse next month. The mountain closes. Whatever's under it stays under it. Two climbers stay dead, and nobody else joins them." She met my eyes. "Wren. You've been quiet. You've had ten years to think about this. Tell me burning it isn't the sane option."

And there it was. My name on the table with everything else.

I made myself look at the logbooks. At the neat, damning handwriting. At the margin note on page forty-one in Marcus Merrow's hand—*lines pulled per directive, advise no further ascent traffic*—written in the same tidy script that had once signed off on my guiding certification, back when he was on the regional board and I was twenty-four and grateful.

Here is the thing about being blamed for something you didn't do. You build your whole life around the shape of it. You learn to move through rooms without being seen. You take jobs under other names and you become very, very good at small things—knot checks, water crossings, keeping nervous people calm—because big things are no longer offered to you. And after enough years, the injustice stops being a wound and becomes a load-bearing wall. Take it out and you have to ask what the house is standing on.

If I burned those books, I stayed guilty. Officially, permanently, in every record that mattered. But I walked out. We all walked out. That was the trade on offer, and it was not a contemptible one, and I will not pretend I didn't want it. God, I wanted it. I wanted to be a person who got to choose survival over principle and call it wisdom.

But I'd read page forty-one. And page fifty-three, where the second climber's last transmission was logged—*radio works, requesting descent clearance, denied*—and understood that the radio I'd been blamed for breaking had worked fine, right up until the moment someone decided it should stop mattering. They had asked permission to come down. Someone had said no. And then the world had spent ten years saying *Wren Sandoval's fault*, and I had said nothing, because who listens to a blacklisted guide?

Vindication. That was the word for what the books held for me personally, and I had learned to be suspicious of words that felt that warm. Vindication was a fire and I was cold and I knew exactly how fires like that burned through the people carrying them. Ask Cass's father. He'd wanted security for his family and a future for his company, and every step toward it had been reasonable, and reasonable, and reasonable, until reasonable ended above the escarpment wall with two climbers asking to come home.

Power doesn't usually corrupt by making you evil. It corrupts by making each next thing make sense.

"I don't get to decide this alone," I said finally. "That's the first thing. Whatever we do, we all carry it, so we all choose it. But I'll tell you what I see, and then I'll tell you what I want, and those aren't the same thing."

I turned the logbook gently, so the light caught the margin note.

"What I see: three options, and every one of them costs. Selling feeds the machine that killed two people. Burning keeps us safe and keeps the lie official forever—with my name on it. Publishing might bring the whole rotten structure down, and might just as easily bring a helicopter crash down on five people who deserve better." I looked at each of them. "What I want is simpler and stupider. I want my name back. I've wanted it for ten years, and now it's sitting here in ink, and I'm telling you honestly: I don't trust myself with it. That's why I'm not deciding."

Silence. Then Cass laughed—not her boardroom laugh, something rawer.

"There it is," she said. "Ten years, and you're the only person in this tent who thinks that's a weakness."

"It's not a weakness," Odile said. "It's the only reason I trust you."

"We vote," Priya said. "All five. No proxies. And whatever it is, we commit before the weather breaks, because a divided camp on open snow with hostiles inbound is how everybody dies."

So we voted.

Tomas for sell. Priya for publish. Odile, after a long look at me, for burn. Cass—

Cass hesitated. I watched her father's initials and I watched her face and I understood that she had known about the books' existence longer than any of us, that this whole expedition had been built around them, and that her vote was going to cost her something no one else in the tent could price.

"Publish," she said. "Anonymously. All of it. My family built its name on this lie. I'm not leaving it standing under mine."

Three to two. Publish.

Tomas stared at the floor. Odile closed her eyes briefly, then nodded once, like closing a chart. And I sat there with the strangest sensation in my chest—ten years of weight shifting, not lifting, just *shifting*, finding new footing—and thought: now we find out what it costs.

Priya was already pulling the logbooks toward her scanner, already talking frequencies and burst transmissions and dead drops, when her radio crackled.

Not static. A voice. Clear, close, and courteous as a hotel concierge.

"—camp on the shoulder, this is Meridian Recovery One. We can see your tents. We'd like to talk."

* * *

Nobody breathed for a second. Then Priya killed the stove light and we killed the conversation and the five of us sat in the dark listening to the radio hiss, and I did the math I didn't want to do: they could see the tents, which meant optics, which meant they'd been watching long enough to count us, which meant the storm that saved us had ended, and the day we'd lost to Odile's crevasse was a day we no longer had.

"They're a mile out," Priya whispered, reading the signal strength. "Maybe less. Moving in the clear."

Cass reached over and put her hand flat on the logbooks, the way you'd steady something alive.

"Well," she said. "They have opinions about our vote."

Outside, in the blue hour before dark, a single figure crested the near ridge, walking slowly, openly, hands visible. Coming to talk.
