## 13. The Recovery Team

I have a theory about fear, and it's this: fear is a triage problem. Your body is a field hospital with limited beds, and terror is the patient who screams loudest while the actual hemorrhage happens quietly in a tent you forgot to check. I learned this in residency, in the ER, in the year I spent doing aid work in places whose names I still don't say at parties. The screamer is rarely the one dying. The quiet one in the corner, the polite one, the one offering you tea—that's the one you watch.

So when the voice came through Priya's radio on the fourth morning, smooth as poured cream, asking—not demanding, *asking*—to speak with our expedition lead, every clinical instinct I owned stood up and turned to face the tent wall.

"Good morning," the voice said. "I hope I haven't caught you at breakfast. This is Halloran, calling on behalf of Meridian Resource Group. We understand you've had difficult weather. We'd like to help."

*We'd like to help.* In my experience, the four most expensive words in any language.

Priya had the radio cupped in both hands like a small injured bird she wasn't sure she wanted to save. She looked at Wren. Wren looked at the radio the way you look at a snake that has gotten into your boot and is, for the moment, being reasonable.

"Tell him we're fine," Wren said. "Tell him we're recreational."

"I already told him that yesterday," Priya whispered. "He laughed."

"He *laughed*?"

"He said, and I'm quoting, 'Tell Dr. Ferreol her suturing technique is very good. The helicopter medic was impressed.'"

I felt that sentence go into me like a probe. Somewhere out there, behind the white curtain of the storm that had pinned us for three days, there was a helicopter, and in that helicopter there was a medic, and that medic had been looking at us through magnification or a thermal lens or God knew what, and had *graded my work*. I am not an easy person to unsettle. I once held a man's aorta closed with my thumb for forty minutes in the back of a truck that was not, technically, a vehicle anymore. But being watched without knowing it—that does something to the spine that no trauma bay ever taught me how to brace for.

"Put him on speaker," Wren said.

"No," said Cass, from the corner where she'd been sitting very still for three days, conserving herself the way she conserved everything. "Absolutely not. We don't negotiate on an open channel."

"We're not negotiating," Wren said. "We're listening. There's a difference, and the difference is that listening costs nothing."

Cass's jaw did the thing it did when she was deciding whether a fight was worth the calories. She nodded once, a queen granting a border adjustment.

Priya switched the speaker on. The tent filled with the sound of wind—not our wind, a cleaner, higher wind, the kind that lives at altitude where the air has been thinned of everything but itself, and beneath it, the faint hum of machinery that did not belong to any world I wanted to live in.

"—still with me? Wonderful. Dr. Ferreol, Ms. Merrow, Mr. Reyes, Ms. Chandra. And of course, Mr. Sandoval." A pause, delicately measured, the length of a man enjoying his own omniscience. "Or do you prefer Wren? The old expedition reports are inconsistent. Some say Sandoval, some say nothing at all, which I understand was rather the point."

I watched Wren's face while he said it. I'm a doctor; watching faces is half my job, and I'm good at the other half too. What crossed Wren's features was not fear. It was older than fear, and quieter. It was the look of a person hearing their own name spoken in a room they'd spent ten years making sure they'd never be in.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Wren said into the radio, voice level as a frozen lake. "You know everyone. I don't know you."

"Halloran. I said that. Recovery and Site Integrity, Meridian Resource Group." A small laugh, self-deprecating, rehearsed. "I know how it sounds. 'Recovery.' Like we're coming to take something. In a sense we are, but only what was never yours to begin with. Survey materials. Company property. The kind of paper that, frankly, would bore you all to tears."

"Then it should be easy to leave it with us," Tomas said. He was sitting against the tent wall with his data slate hugged to his chest, and I noticed—and I would remember noticing—that his arms were wrapped around it the way you hold a child, not the way you hold a rock sample.

"Mr. Reyes." Halloran's voice warmed another degree, and the warmth was the worst thing yet. "The geologist. Excellent work on the strike mapping, by the way. We've been reading your published data for years. You have an eye. It would be a shame if an eye like that got associated with... irregular activity. You're a young man. Careers are long. Mountains are not."

The silence in the tent had texture. I could feel Tomas's breathing change beside me—short, high, the breathing of a man doing arithmetic with his whole life.

"You're threatening him," Cass said.

"Heaven forbid." Genuine-sounding horror. "I'm *informing* him. There's a difference, and I suspect your guide just explained it to you." A pause. Wind. The machine hum. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, because I respect you all enough to be direct, and because the weather is going to break tomorrow and we will all be very busy after that."

I want to record the offer exactly, because the exactness is what made it monstrous.

Extraction by helicopter, all five of us, within the hour of the weather lifting. Transport to Meridian's southern facility. Hot food, real beds, medical attention—I felt him smile at that, felt it through the radio, the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck—and full legal immunity for the trespass. The concession violation, the permit breach, all of it, dissolved. In exchange: the survey materials. The logbooks. Everything recovered from the station on the shoulder. And a mutual nondisclosure agreement, standard boilerplate, binding for a term of—here he actually chuckled—*"well, let's say in perpetuity, for simplicity."*

"And if we decline?" Wren asked.

"Then you're five civilians on a closed concession after the permits lapse, in deteriorating conditions, without a filed flight plan, in possession of stolen corporate property." The voice was almost gentle. "I don't make that happen, Mr. Sandoval. The mountain does. I just fail to prevent it. It's remarkable, honestly, how much trouble this range causes people. It caused your expedition a great deal of it, ten years ago. Two climbers, wasn't it? Above the wall. Terrible business. The reports were very thorough."

*The reports were very thorough.* I have treated a lot of wounds in my life and I know a blade when I hear one. That sentence had been sharpened on both edges: it said *we know what happened* and it said *we know what was said about you* and it said *we know which of those things is true*, all in six words, delivered with the courtesy of a maître d'.

Wren's hand was resting on their knee, and I saw the tendons stand up in it, one by one, like something deciding whether to surface.

"Your medic," Halloran continued, conversational now, the knife going back in its sheath so we'd all relax, which is the trick, that's always the trick, "is better than the one I've got. I mean that as a compliment and a professional assessment. Dr. Ferreol, if your companions are foolish enough to refuse my offer, I'd ask you to think about your own obligations. You have a duty of care. That duty does not extend to dying for someone else's conscience."

And here is the part I am not proud of, and I'm writing it down anyway because a record that flatters its keeper is worthless: for one full second—maybe two—I considered it.

Not the whole offer. I never once thought about handing over the logs; I didn't even know what was in them, not really, only that Wren had ordered them left in the station and that the order had the weight of a scar behind it. But the *extraction*. The helicopter. The thought arrived whole and warm: *I could go home. I could sleep. I could stop counting calories and fuel and hours of daylight.* I have been unfindable for three years, and let me tell you what unfindable costs. It costs you your mother's voice on the phone. It costs you a mailbox with your name on it. It costs you the right to be looked for, which turns out to be half of what being loved means.

One second. Two. Then I looked across the tent at Priya, who was twenty-six years old and had burned her own life down to be here because she believed maps belonged to everyone, and at Tomas, whose eye Halloran had just appraised like livestock, and at Wren, who had carried the weight of two dead climbers for a decade without once, in all the weeks I'd known them, asking anyone to carry it with them—and my second was over. My duty of care was exactly where it had always been. It was in this tent. It had gotten into the tent the way cold does, through every seam, and it was mine now.

"Tell your medic," I said, taking the radio from Priya, "that my duty of care is present and accounted for, and that if his team comes up this mountain in this weather, he's going to need me. Because he will. Meridian flies its people in whatever serves the schedule, I've seen how you operate, and this range in a post-frontal system will hand him fractures and frostbite and a cardiac event or two, and he will be one man with a litter and no shelter. So here's *my* offer. You leave us alone, and when your people inevitably break themselves on this mountain, I'll treat them. Free of charge. That's my NDA: you get your people back alive, and I don't bill you."

The silence on the channel went on long enough that I checked the radio was still transmitting.

"Well," Halloran said at last, and there was something in his voice that hadn't been there before—not anger, nothing so useful as anger. Recalculation. "That's a generous counteroffer, Doctor. I'll convey it." Another pause, and then, softly, almost kindly, the way you speak to someone you've already decided about: "You should know that Mr. Sandoval's reputation preceded him up this mountain by about ten years. Everyone on my team has read the Kestrel file. Everyone knows what he did. I mention it only so you understand the company you're keeping, and the company you're keeping it for."

"You mention it," Wren said quietly, "because it's the only weapon you've got that doesn't require a helicopter."

"Mr. Sandoval. It has been a genuine pleasure." And the channel closed with a click so small and so final that it sounded like a bone setting.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while. The stove hissed. Snow ticked against the fly in the small dry sounds of a storm that was, as Halloran had promised, running out of argument.

"He knows about Kestrel," Tomas said finally. "The actual file. Not the public report. The *file*." He said it the way you'd say *the body*. "Which means—"

"Which means Meridian has known the truth for ten years," Cass said. Her voice was flat and careful, a person walking on ice she'd told everyone else was safe. "And buried it. And now they're worried we've found what they buried."

"You knew the route was beyond the season," Priya said. Not accusing—worse, wondering. "You said so. You told Odile that night. Why would you bring us up here if you thought—"

"I brought you up here," Cass said, "because the window existed. Because the route was possible, barely, for the right team. And because whatever's in those logs dies with the concession in—" she checked her watch, a small absurd gesture, the aristocrat's tic, "—nine days. After that, no court, no journalist, no family member of anyone who died up here can ever see a single page. It gets fenced and strip-mined and the truth gets processed into tailings with everything else." She looked at her hands. "I have spent my whole life inside my family's version of Kestrel. I wanted the real one."

"You wanted it badly enough to let Halloran know exactly where we'd be," Priya said.

"I have never contacted Meridian."

"You don't have to. Your name opens doors you don't knock on."

I let them go at it for a while, because someone had to hold the space open and I've learned that crews, like patients, sometimes need to hemorrhage a little before you can suture anything. Wren said nothing at all. Wren was looking at the tent wall, at the weather, at some interior horizon I couldn't see, and I realized I was watching a person do the thing I'd watched surgeons do when the scan comes back and it's the diagnosis they feared and the one they expected, all at once, and now the only question left is what to cut.

"Odile," Wren said, without turning. "How many days of food if we stop eating like people and start eating like a problem to be solved?"

I had the numbers before they finished the sentence. I always do. "Seven. Eight if we cut to minimum and accept that everyone loses weight they'll want back later. Nine if the weather lets us move and moving burns less than shivering."

"And the icefield route off-trail—the one I described at the shoulder. Realistically."

I looked at them then. Wren's face was calm in a way I recognized from the cornice day, the day Odile Ferreol fell into a crevasse and was pulled out of it by hands that did not hurry and did not miss. Calm like that isn't the absence of fear. It's fear that has been given a job.

"Realistically," I said, "it's the only route Halloran hasn't mapped. It's also the route where the map is ten years old and written by a dead expedition, and you're the only one who's read it."

"Then we break camp tomorrow at first light," Wren said. "We take the icefield. And we take the logbooks."

Every head in the tent came up at that, including Cass's, including mine. For six days Wren had ordered those logs left where they lay, and the order had been the one thing in this expedition that felt like bedrock.

"You said they were poison," Tomas said.

"They are." Wren finally turned around, and what was in their face made my chest hurt, because it wasn't vindication and it wasn't resolve. It was grief, ten years old and freshly reopened, being converted in real time into the only fuel it would ever burn as. "But Halloran just told us something. He told us Meridian would rather five of us die quietly on a closed concession than let those pages reach a courthouse. Which means the pages are worth exactly as much as he says they're worthless." They picked up their ice axe from where it leaned against the tent pole, and held it across their knees like a promise. "We're not carrying them out to sell. We're carrying them out so that the next people Halloran's employers bury don't have to dig us up first."

Outside, the wind dropped a register, the way it does when a storm is deciding. I lay back against my pack and did the inventory I always do—airway, breathing, circulation, crew—and for the first time since the radio had purred its cream-smooth greeting, every number came back acceptable.

Then Priya, who had been quietly sweeping the emergency channel out of habit, sat up so fast she cracked her head on the tent pole.

"Guys," she said, and her face had gone the color of the snow. "The helicopter. It just filed a flight plan. It's not waiting for the weather to break."

She turned the radio toward us, and through the static, faint and getting fainter, I heard rotors.

"They're coming up *tonight*."
