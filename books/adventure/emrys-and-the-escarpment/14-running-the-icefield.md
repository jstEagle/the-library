## 14. Running the Icefield

The thing nobody tells you about being a guide is that half the job is deciding what not to tell people. The other half is lying about the time.

"We move in ten minutes," I said, and started packing while the crew was still chewing. Ten minutes meant twenty. Twenty meant they'd eat, drink, argue, and still believe we were ahead of schedule. A guide's clock is a kindness. You wind it backward until people stop looking at it.

We'd broken camp in the gray hour, before the storm fully spent itself, because the recovery team's tracks were two hours old and freshening by the minute. Priya had caught their check-in on the radio at dawn—polite, procedural, a man's voice reading coordinates like a grocery list. Our coordinates. Or close enough. They were sweeping the icefield in a line abreast, the way you search for a body, and we were the body.

"Which way?" Cass asked. She stood with her arms crossed, breath streaming, looking east toward the mapped route like it owed her money.

"Not east."

"The route—"

"The route is where they expect us. The route is a funnel with their name on the bottom of it." I pointed north, at the icefield itself: six kilometers of crevassed, unmapped, unloved glacier that ended, if my memory was worth anything, at the shoulder below the wall. "Kestrel went that way. Off-trail. Nobody's crossed it since because nobody's stupid enough."

"And we are?" Odile said. There was no fear in it. That was the worst part. She'd already decided, and she was just giving me the chance to talk myself out of it.

"We are desperate," I said, "which is different, and I'd argue better. Desperate people pay attention."

Tomas said nothing. He'd been quiet since the logbooks, quiet the way a man is quiet when he's carrying something heavy and doesn't want anyone to offer to help. He checked his harness twice, cinched his pack, and looked north with the expression of a geologist reading a cliff face—half appetite, half dread.

Priya folded the radio into its case with exaggerated care. "I can keep the handset cold. If they transmit, I'll hear it."

"If they transmit, we'll already know," I said. "Sound carries on ice. You'll hear them before the radio does." I looked at each of them in turn. Cass, Odile, Tomas, Priya. Four people who had, for reasons ranging from noble to unforgivable, put their lives in my hands on unmapped ice. "Rules are simple. Rope spacing as drilled. Nobody steps where I don't step. If I say down, you're down before you finish wondering why. And if the ice talks—"

"Ice talks?" Priya said.

"It groans. Cracks. Whistles through the moulins. It's not conversation, it's geology having opinions. You listen anyway." I clipped into the front of the rope. "Let's go be stupid."

* * *

The first hour was the easy kind of terrible.

The icefield's surface was wind-scoured sastrugi, frozen waves of it, hard as pavement in the cold and treacherous as glass where the sun touched. We moved in the shadow of the serac line to keep the surface firm, and I navigated by a memory ten years old and half-rotten: a black rock band on the left margin, a crevasse field that bent like a river delta, a serac shaped—I swear to this day—like a hunched old man. Kestrel's route had been logged in a notebook that burned with everything else, and what remained lived in my legs rather than my head. Guides don't remember routes. Their feet do.

"Left margin," I said, more to myself than anyone. "Stay off the center. The center drains."

"Drains where?" Tomas asked.

"Down." I didn't elaborate, because the honest answer was *into a plumbing system nobody has ever mapped, through holes that open and close like mouths*, and I needed these people walking, not thinking about that.

The black band was there. That was the moment the fear in my chest changed flavor—from the generic kind, the kind that rides along on any serious terrain, to the specific kind, the kind that means *you actually remember, and the ice hasn't moved enough to lie to you*. Ten years is nothing to a glacier. Ten years is a long time for a moulin.

We crossed the delta crevasses in the early light, and I'll give the crew this: they'd learned. Cass read the snow bridges before I called them, Odile moved like she'd been born on rope, Priya kept her spacing without being told twice. Even Tomas, burdened by whatever was in his pack and his conscience, placed his feet where I placed mine. For an hour I forgot to be afraid, which on a glacier is the most dangerous hour there is.

Because the icefield doesn't like being forgotten about.

It was Priya who heard it first—a sound under our boots, low and resonant, like someone blowing across the top of a bottle the size of a cathedral. She stopped. I stopped. The whole rope went taut and still.

"What is that?" Odile whispered.

"Water," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "Moving water. Under us."

In winter, on a healthy glacier, there is no moving water. Everything is frozen to the bedrock. But this icefield sat on the geothermal tap that had kept the Kestrel station powered all these years, and I had known that, had *filed it away* as a fact about the station and not a fact about the ice, and now the ice was correcting my paperwork. Warm ground meant meltwater. Meltwater meant moulins. Moulins meant holes in the world, hidden under snow bridges that had no business holding anything, and we were standing on the drainage.

"Spacing," I said quietly. "Widen it. Probe everything. We go slow."

"Slow is what they'll catch," Cass said.

"Then they can catch us alive." I didn't look at her when I said it. I was already probing, driving my axe shaft through the snow in a grid, listening with my boots. *Slow is what they'll catch* hung in the air anyway. She was right. The sweep line behind us wasn't slowing down for geology.

We crossed maybe four hundred meters of the drainage zone in ninety minutes, and every meter of it was the worst kind of work—the kind where your body does something repetitive and your mind does something else entirely, and the something else is a countdown. Probe. Step. Probe. Step. Priya's radio hissed once, twice, and each time my heart performed a small unauthorized maneuver.

Then the icefield tried to eat Odile.

* * *

It happened the way it always happens, which is to say it didn't happen at all until it happened all at once.

There was no warning sound. The bridge over that moulin had been probed, had held my weight, had held Cass's weight, had held Odile's weight for exactly one and a half steps. Snow bridges don't fail by strength. They fail by fatigue, and fatigue doesn't announce itself. One boot planted, the second boot planted, and the world opened beneath her like a mouth deciding, after long consideration, to swallow.

I heard the crack. I heard her go. And my body did the thing ten years of this work builds into the spine: I dropped, axes self-arrest, rope wrapped twice around the shaft, and I was falling forward into the arrest position before the conscious part of me had finished the sentence *oh no*.

The rope came tight. The world became a diagram: Odile's weight, my weight, the anchor of friction across the lip, the physics of it all expressed in the creak of nylon. She'd gone in to the waist. The moulin's throat was blue-black below her, polished walls curving down into a dark that had no bottom that I cared to imagine, and meltwater sound came up out of it like breathing.

"I'm okay," she called up, and her voice was steady, which told me she was not okay, because the truly okay people scream first and get calm later. "I'm okay. Rope's caught on something. I can't—there's a lip, I'm hung up on a lip."

"Hang up is good. Hang up means you're not at the bottom." I looked back down the rope. "Cass. Anchor. Now. Deadman placement, two meters back, bury it deep. Tomas, on the rope with me. Priya, radio silence, and if you hear boots, you tell me in words of one syllable."

They moved. That's the thing I'll remember about this crew until I die—they moved. No frozen panic, no shouting over each other. Cass built the anchor like she'd done it a thousand times, because she had, whatever else was true about her. Tomas got on the line behind me and we ran the counterweight, easing Odile's hang-up off the lip millimeter by millimeter while she fed me information in a medic's flat, precise voice: *left boot wedged, right leg free, no pain, no blood, harness loaded, don't rush it.*

"I'm rushing it," I told her. "I'm just rushing it carefully."

The extraction took forty minutes. It took forty minutes because the last ten meters of it I did on my belly, crawled out over the moulin's throat on a spread-eagle of axe and body, close enough to see the water sheen far below and the way Odile's face had gone the color of the ice, and I talked to her the whole time—not rescue procedure, just talk, garbage talk, about the weather and the awful sastrugi and how the old man serac looked less like a hunched man every year, more like a man lying down, and she laughed once, one sharp bark of a laugh that echoed down the blue throat beneath her, and then her harness came free of the lip and we hauled and she came up over the edge like a diver surfacing, and we lay there in a heap on the snow, four of us tangled in rope, breathing.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Odile said, into the ice, "I heard it. Under me. The water."

"I know."

"How deep?"

I looked at the hole. The blue went down and down and the light didn't follow. "Deeper than the rescue," I said, which was true, and kind, and a lie in the way all the kind ones are.

* * *

We lost the sweep line at midday by the simple expedient of going where no sweep line would believe we'd gone.

The near-miss changed something in the crew, or maybe it finished changing something that had been changing since the station. Nobody suggested the mapped route again. Nobody asked how much farther. When I called the next rest, they drank and ate with the efficiency of people who had finally accepted that the mountain's schedule was the only schedule, and that my clock, wound backward though it was, was the honest one.

Cass sat beside me on a sastrugi ridge, sharing the wind-shadow of my pack. Below us the icefield spread out, white to the horizon, unbroken except where the drainage zone scored it with subsurface rivers we'd never see.

"You knew," she said. "About the water. You knew the ground was warm and you still brought us across it."

"I knew the station was geothermal. I didn't connect it to the ice. That's on me."

"You could have taken us around. Added half a day."

"Half a day is where they catch us. And around meant the serac zone, and I don't trust ten-year-old seracs." I took a mouthful of snow, let it melt. "Every option out here is a bad option, Cass. My job is picking the bad option that lets everyone go home."

"That's not what you said at Kestrel Col, ten years ago."

The words landed like a dropped axe. I didn't turn. "I don't know what you mean."

"You said the same thing. Odile doesn't know that. Tomas doesn't. But I was there, Wren. I read the transcripts they buried. *Every option is a bad option; my job is picking the one that lets everyone go home.* Your own words, in the incident record, used to prove you'd made a fatal judgment call." She was quiet a moment. "They took the sentence you said to keep people calm and they turned it into the noose."

I looked north, at the shoulder coming up out of the ice haze, black rock against white, exactly where my feet said it would be. Ten years ago I had stood on that shoulder with a broken radio in my hands and two climbers somewhere above me in the storm, and I had said that sentence, or something like it, to four terrified people, because it was true then too.

"Then let's make it true one more time," I said. "Shoulder's four hours. We cross the drainage's upper margin, high line, and we're off the icefield before dark. After that it's rock, and rock doesn't eat people."

"Rock doesn't," Cass agreed. "People on rock do." She stood, shouldered her pack, and for a second she looked like her father must have looked, and then the resemblance passed, and she was just a tired woman in a cold wind. "Wren. Whatever's at the shoulder—"

"Move," I said, not unkindly. "We'll get there faster than the conversation will."

* * *

The high line along the drainage margin was the finest guiding I have ever done, and I say that with no pride, because the finest guiding is invisible. It's the client who says afterward, *that was easy, wasn't it?* It's the crew that crosses a crevasse field threaded with hidden moulins and arrives at the far side believing they were never in danger, because the danger was spent in advance, in the probing and the pacing and the hundred small decisions that never got announced.

I probed every step for three hours. I read the ice the way you read a person you've known too long—the sag of a bridge that's tired, the hollow note of snow over air, the faint blue stain in the pack where meltwater had once risen and frozen, marking a throat below. Twice I turned the rope ninety degrees for no reason I could articulate, and both times, twenty meters on, we found a hole the size of a church door under two inches of crust. Priya started calling it *Wren's sense of doom* and asking for it by name.

"It's not a sense," I told her. "It's arithmetic. Warm ground plus old ice plus gravity equals holes. I'm just doing the math faster than you can see the problem."

"Can you teach the math?"

"Ten years," I said. "And a near-miss you don't walk away from." I didn't say the rest of it, which was that the math had been taught to me by the Kestrel route, by the storm, by the two who didn't come down, and that every step I took on this ice was a step I owed them. That I had spent a decade guiding easy trails under a false name because I could not bear to do this—the real work, the work I was made for—without them. And that today, with a recovery team at my back and a moulin's breath still in my ears, I had finally stopped guiding away from something and started guiding toward it.

The shoulder rose to meet us in the last light. Black rock, wind-scoured, honest. Below and behind, far out on the white, something glinted—sun on glass, maybe, or a lens. Priya saw it too. She raised the radio, listened, and her face did the thing faces do when good news and bad news arrive in the same envelope.

"They've stopped," she said. "Their sweep line. They've stopped moving."

"Camp?" Cass asked.

"Camp," Priya said. "Or they've found something."

Tomas went very still. I watched his hand drift toward his pack, toward the weight inside it, and I watched him stop himself, and I filed that away too.

We made the shoulder as the light died, and I put the crew in the lee of the first rock band, and I stood a moment looking north at the wall above us—the escarpment face, blue ice and black stone, the place the mountain keeps what it keeps. Somewhere up there was a cache with a radio in it that had never been broken. Somewhere up there was the truth, and below us, on the icefield, four or five armed men were camped in our tracks, and somewhere in my own crew was the reason why.

"First watch is mine," I said. "Sleep. Tomorrow we climb."

Nobody asked what we were climbing toward. That, more than anything, told me they'd finally stopped being clients.

They were crew.
