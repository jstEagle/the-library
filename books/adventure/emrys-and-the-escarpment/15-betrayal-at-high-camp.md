## 15. Betrayal at High Camp

I want to tell you that I hesitated.

That's the version I'd write if I were a better woman, or a worse liar. The truth is I didn't hesitate at all. I sat in my tent at High Camp with Priya's radio in my lap and my father's name in my mouth like a stone I'd been carrying so long I'd grown a socket for it, and I keyed the transmit button, and I told a stranger where to find us.

Three short bursts. Grid reference. That's all it takes to sell a person. Not even a whole person—just their coordinates.

* * *

Let me back up, because you deserve the architecture of it, and because if I don't explain it now I never will.

My father, Aldous Merrow, led the Kestrel Expedition. Everyone who knows anything about the Escarpment knows that much; it's on the commemorative plaque at the trailhead, bronze letters gone green with weather: *In memory of the climbers lost above the wall, and in honor of those who brought them home.* My father was one of the ones who came home. He came home and he built Merrow Concessions out of the mountain's grief, and he taught me two things before he died: that the Escarpment was ours to protect, and that some truths are heavier than the people asked to carry them.

He said that second one the winter before the end, when his hands had started shaking too badly to pour whiskey. He wasn't confessing. I want to be clear about that. He was instructing. There's a difference, and the difference is that a confession asks forgiveness and an instruction asks obedience, and I gave him obedience because I was thirty-one years old and he was dying and I loved him, God help me, I loved him the way you love a mountain—without any expectation that it loves you back or cares what it does to you.

So when Cass Merrow, CEO of a shrinking company, hired Wren Sandoval to guide an illegal traverse, she told herself a story. The story went like this: *find the proof, clear your family's name of the worst of it, protect the concession, save the route.* A clean story. Stories are always clean until they meet terrain.

The problem with the story was Wren Sandoval.

* * *

Here is what I actually knew, going in. I knew the Kestrel logs existed because my father kept a locked drawer his whole life and got drunk enough exactly once, in my presence, to say the words "the station still has power" and then look at me like he'd handed me a live grenade. I knew the official record—that the retreat line failed, that the storm closed in, that Sandoval made a judgment call above the wall and two climbers paid for it—was load-bearing. If it fell, the concession fell, because the concession's founding charter cites Kestrel's "tragic but sound leadership" as its moral basis. You can't have a permit built on a lie once the lie is documented. Lawyers are very clear about this sort of thing.

And I knew—I've known since I was old enough to read a room—that my father hadn't come home from Kestrel a hero. He'd come home a man rehearsing. Every time someone mentioned the expedition he gave the same speech with the same pauses, and I used to think that was grief. It took me twenty years to understand it was testimony. Memorized. Vetted. Possibly written by lawyers.

So yes. I hired the scapegoat to dig up the grave. I told myself I wanted exoneration—for Wren, obviously, but also for the dead, whose names had been spent like currency for a decade. And I did want that. I need you to believe both things are true, because they are, and that's what makes this unforgivable rather than merely evil. Evil would be simpler. I could live with simple.

What I didn't plan for was eleven days on a rope team with them.

* * *

You spend that long roped to a person, you learn their weight. Not their body—their *weight*. What they carry, how they shift it, when they lean. Wren Sandoval carries guilt like a climber carries a rack: distributed, checked, absolutely non-negotiable. They never once said *I didn't do it*. In ten days they never said it. They just moved through the mountains with the particular economy of someone who has nothing left to prove to themselves and everything left to prove to the ice, and somewhere around the third day I realized that this was worse than innocence. Innocence protests. This was a person who had made peace with being the villain of a story they didn't star in, because the alternative—being nobody at all—was colder.

And I thought: *my father did this.* Not the storm. Not the mountain. The after. The ten years. He watched this happen and let it happen and built a company on top of it, and then he died in a warm house with everyone saying what a pillar he was.

That's when the story changed. That's when I understood that if the logs came out whole—if they came out with everything in them—then it wouldn't just be the deaths that were exposed. It would be the cover-up, and the cover-up had my name attached to it through blood and through silence, and Merrow Concessions would not survive it, and neither, honestly, would I. Not the way I'm built. I'm built the way my father was built. We're people who can carry heavy truths as long as we get to choose which end of them we hold.

So I cut a deal. Three nights ago, while the whiteout had us pinned and everyone was watching Wren, I used Priya's backup handset—the one she keeps stowed in the gear cache for emergency relay, the one she thinks only she knows about—and I contacted the recovery team myself.

Not to surrender the logs. Let me be precise, because precision is all I have left. I offered them Tomas. His data, his sample bags, his little hoard of strike-zone coordinates—he'd already been sniffing around a sale, anyone with eyes could see it, and the recovery team's leader, Voss, already had his name. I gave them confirmation. In exchange: the crew walks. All of us. No charges, no blacklisting, no questions about permits lapsed mid-traverse. And the logs stay buried—not destroyed, buried, sealed into whatever archive corporations keep for things that cost billions and buy silence.

Everyone goes home. That was the deal. That was the deal I told myself was mercy.

Except.

* * *

Except that Voss, being thorough, asked about the guide. And I heard myself say—"the guide is a separate matter"—and I heard how it sounded, and I said it anyway. Because Wren Sandoval walking free with knowledge of what's in those logs is a loose end my father's ghost cannot afford, and because there is a part of me, the part I inherited along with the jaw and the stubbornness, that needed one person to pay. Ten years ago the mountain was given a payment and it took the wrong ones. Two climbers instead of one guide. My father spent a decade knowing the ledger was wrong and doing nothing, and I told myself I was correcting it—*give them Sandoval, the original debt, close the account*—and every word of that is true and every word of it is filth, and I have never in my life despised anyone the way I despised myself at 4 a.m. in a frozen tent, whispering coordinates into a radio like a prayer said backward.

Tomas gets blamed. I know that already. His tent's been odd for days, he's been sleeping with his pack half-packed, and when the recovery team shows up—and they will show up, tomorrow probably, the weather's breaking—everyone will do the arithmetic and land on him. He won't even be wrong to feel guilty. He did try to open the logs against orders. He did hoard. He's a man who makes himself easy to accuse, which is maybe why I chose him, though I'll never admit that under oath.

But it was me. It was me, and here is the part I keep circling like a bird over a cornice: I would do it again. That's the discovery of this trip, the real survey, the finding underneath the finding. Put me back in that tent with that radio and that stone in my mouth and I key the transmitter again. Because I am my father's daughter, and we Merrows do not burn the archives—we curate them.

* * *

Tonight the wind dropped around dusk the way it does before a hard cold snap, and the sky cleared to that brutal high-altitude black with stars like nailheads, and I crawled out of my tent to check the anchors and found Wren already outside, standing at the edge of camp looking north toward the col.

They didn't turn around. "Weather window opens at dawn," they said. "Six hours of stable air, maybe eight. We move at first light."

"The face?" I asked. I meant the escarpment wall, the Kestrel line, the route that killed two people and ruined one more.

Wren was quiet long enough that I counted their breaths—four, five, six. Then: "It's the way out. So it's the way."

No accusation in it. That's the thing about Wren that has undone me more thoroughly than any shouting could—they don't accuse, they just *see*, and standing there in the starlight I felt completely, comprehensively seen, like a specimen pinned under glass, and they still didn't know. They stood next to me for another minute watching the col glow faintly under snow-light, and then they said goodnight and went to melt snow for the morning's water, methodical as ever, and I stood alone with the radio in my chest pocket weighing four ounces and four hundred pounds.

Odile passed me on her way to her tent. She paused. She looked at me the way she looks at a wound she hasn't decided whether to debride.

"You should sleep," she said.

"I will," I said, and we both knew it was a lie, and we both let it stand, because that's what this crew does—we let each other's lies stand, politely, at altitude, where lies freeze solid and hold weight right up until they don't.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless. Perfect guiding weather. Wren had us breaking camp in forty minutes flat, efficient, gentle with the rookies, checking Odile's harness twice without making a point of it, and I watched them work and thought: *this is the best guide on the mountain and I sold them.* Thought it plainly, no anesthesia. And then I did the thing I've been doing my whole life, the Merrow inheritance, the family trade: I filed the feeling away in the drawer with the lock, next to my father's whiskey glass and the plaque at the trailhead, and I shouldered my pack, and I clipped into the rope behind Priya, and I smiled at Tomas—who was looking at me with something naked and hopeful in his face, poor man, waiting for absolution I had no right to give—and I said, "Big day."

And then, from the north, thin and getting thinner, we all heard it at once: the chop of rotors.

Coming up the pass. Coming for us.

Wren turned, and I watched their face do the arithmetic—distance, wind, fuel range, the works—and then their eyes came to me, and stayed there, and I understood with total clarity that they already knew. Maybe not the details. But the shape of it. Guides read terrain, and I am terrain, and I have just developed a very obvious fault line.

"Sandoval," I started, and my voice cracked on the second syllable like lake ice.

"Don't," they said. Quiet. Almost kind. "Save your breath. You're going to need it for the wall."

And they turned and pointed up at the escarpment face, pale and enormous in the early light, and began, calmly, terribly, to brief us on the climb—as if the helicopter behind us were just weather, as if I were just another client, as if the rope between us were still a rope and not a sentence I haven't finished serving.
