## 16. The Wall

The storm found us at dawn, the way storms do on the Escarpment—no theatrics, no warning shot across the bow. Just a thickening in the west, a smell like iron filings, and then the sky simply closed like a ledger.

I stood at the mouth of my tent and watched it come, and I thought: *of course*. Of course the only way off this mountain runs up the face where two people died. Of course the weather turns now. The Escarpment has never once done me the courtesy of being merely difficult. It insists on being meaningful, which is worse.

Behind me, camp was waking badly. Priya had been up half the night with the radio, tracking the recovery team's chatter as they picked their way through the pass below us. Odile was repacking the medical kit for the third time, which is what she does instead of sleeping. Tomas sat alone by the stove, hands wrapped around a mug he wasn't drinking, wearing the particular gray look of a man who has read something he can't unread. And Cass stood at the edge of the col, looking up.

Looking up at the wall.

From below, in flat light, it didn't look like much—a sweep of ice and dark rock, maybe four hundred meters, stitched with ramps and ribs. That's the trick of it. Mountains lie to you from underneath. It's only when you're on them that they tell you the truth, and by then you've already agreed to hear it.

"That's where they died," Cass said. Not a question. She'd said some version of it three times since we'd reached the col, each time like she was testing whether the words had changed.

"Below the second band," I said. "Kestrel tried to retreat down this line when the storm hit. The radio was dead—" I stopped myself. *Was silenced.* Ten years of saying one sentence and now the whole grammar of my life had changed overnight. "They were cut off. They went down the face because it was the only direction left."

"And now it's the only direction left for us," Priya said from the tent flap. She'd heard everything; her voice had the flatness of someone conserving energy for things that mattered.

"The recovery team will reach this col by tomorrow evening," I said. "Maybe tomorrow morning if they push. They have sleds, satellite comms, and a helicopter on standby in the pass. We have four hundred meters of bad ice and whatever we're carrying." I let that sit. "So. Everyone eat something. Then we talk."

* * *

We talked in my tent, all five of us packed shoulder to shoulder among the frost feathers, the stove hissing like something resentful. I laid out the route on the map even though I didn't need the map—I'd been carrying this face in my head for ten years, the way you carry a stone in your boot.

"Here's the honest version," I said. "No sales pitch. The face is rated hard in summer conditions. In these conditions it's beyond anything any of us should be climbing. There are two pitches of water ice over rock that may or may not be bonded—the cold has been deep enough that it probably is, but *probably* is not a word I love on a cliff. Above that, a snow ramp to the upper band. If the wind hasn't stripped it, it's a walk. If it has, it's an hour of exposed traversing on verglas, roped, in weather you can't see your own boots in."

Nobody spoke. Good. They were listening, which meant they hadn't yet started lying to themselves.

"There is no bail-out once we commit past the first band. Down-climbing that ice in a storm isn't realistic. So understand what I'm actually telling you: some of us may not make it." I looked at each of them in turn. "I won't dress that up. I've buried people off this mountain. I know exactly how it happens—not dramatically, usually. Someone gets tired. Someone makes one small error at the wrong moment. The mountain doesn't need to be cruel. It just needs you to be human."

Odile nodded slowly. "And the alternative?"

"The alternative is we wait here, the recovery team arrives, and we find out how polite men with rifles handle civilians holding billion-dollar evidence. Or we go back down the way we came, through terrain they've already mapped and are watching." I folded the map. "Those are the options. I don't recommend either one. I recommend the wall, but I don't *recommend* it, if that makes sense. I'm telling you what it costs."

"It makes sense," Tomas said quietly. He hadn't looked at me directly since the logbooks. "Wren. If we leave the logs here—if we cache them and come back for them legally, after—"

"After the concession lapses, everything inside the boundary belongs to the extraction consortium," Priya said. "Including cached evidence. You know that. You decoded the same permits I did."

"I know what I hoped," Tomas said.

There it was. Hope, that expensive luxury. I almost envied him.

"Cass," I said. She'd been silent, watching me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. "You called this traverse a mapping expedition. You knew the wall was the exit. Say what you think."

She took her time. Outside, the wind had begun to work at the tent fabric in long, patient tugs, like something learning our shape.

"My father stood at the base of this face and made a choice," she said finally. "He chose the company over his climbers. I've spent twenty years telling myself I would have chosen differently." She looked up at the wall through the open flap, at the gray slab of it vanishing into cloud. "This is my chance to find out. That's all I've got. I'm not going to dress it up either."

* * *

We voted the way you vote on mountains: out loud, one at a time, no abstentions, no pressure. I went last.

Priya first. She didn't hesitate, which surprised me until I thought about it. "I've spent six years making sure other people's voices get through," she said. "I'm not staying somewhere mine can't." She looked at the wall and, I noticed, didn't flinch. Radio techs know better than anyone that a signal is just a decision to keep transmitting.

Tomas next. He turned his mug in his hands for a long moment. "I became a geologist because rock tells the truth eventually," he said. "Even when nobody wants it to." A breath. "Yes. The wall."

Odile: "If someone falls, you'll need me above them, not below them waiting." Which was Odile for *yes*, and also Odile for *I've already decided who I am on this mountain, and it isn't the person who waits.*

Cass: "Yes." Nothing else. Her father's daughter, choosing differently, or trying to.

Then me. And here's the thing I didn't say out loud, because there are some truths you carry privately or they stop working: I wanted to climb that wall. Not for the logs, not for vindication, not even for escape. For ten years I'd been the guide who lost people on this face, the name on the blacklist, the cautionary tale told in permit offices. The wall and I had unfinished business, and I was tired of pretending otherwise.

"Yes," I said. "We rope in threes. Two ropes. Odile with me, Cass and Tomas with Priya anchoring the middle. We climb light—everything nonessential stays cached under the cairn, marked. If we make the top, we send coordinates to no one and come back for it ourselves, on our terms."

"And if we don't make the top?" Tomas asked.

"Then the mountain keeps it," I said. "Along with everything else it keeps."

* * *

We climbed out of the col at eleven hundred hours, into wind that arrived not as gusts but as a single sustained shove, as if the atmosphere had decided against us personally. The first two hundred meters were miserable but honest—steep snow over rock, front-pointing, tools biting well. My body remembered this work the way it remembers nothing else useful: without hesitation, without grief.

It was at the base of the first ice pitch that the weather showed us its teeth.

The snow thickened until the world contracted to the radius of our headlamps. Spindrift came down the face in smoking ribbons. I placed a screw, tested it, placed another, and led us up through ice that rang hollow in places—that unbonded sound I'd warned them about—and solid in others, and there was no system to it, no logic, just the mountain deciding moment by moment what it would hold.

At the crux, hanging on two tools with my heels dangling over two hundred meters of nothing, I heard Odile below me, breathing in the rhythm I'd taught her at the crevasse—*in for four, out for four*—and I understood suddenly why guides do this. Why I'd never managed to quit, even disgraced, even exiled. Not for the summit. For this: the sound of someone trusting you enough to breathe on your count while the world tries to kill you both.

"Good," I called down, and my voice was swallowed instantly, but I felt her hear it anyway.

The pitch took ninety minutes that felt like nine hours. When I pulled over onto the ramp above and brought the ropes up, five figures huddled together on a shelf of snow barely wider than a dining table, I did a count out of pure animal habit—one, two, three, four, five—and felt something in my chest unclench that I hadn't known was clenched.

All of us. Still all of us.

Cass caught my eye across the rope. Her face was rimed white, lips cracked, eyes fierce and exhausted and alive. Ten years ago her father had stood on this mountain and chosen paper over people. She was here, freezing, terrified, having chosen the other thing—or at least still choosing it, hour by hour, which is the only way anyone ever chooses anything.

"Upper band next," I shouted over the wind. "One hour if the ramp holds. Stay tight."

Above us, the wall disappeared into the storm. Below us, somewhere in the white, the recovery team was climbing toward our empty camp. And ahead—through the murk, faint and impossible—I could see it: a flicker of orange, high on the ridge, moving against the wind.

Not a bird. Birds don't fly in this.

A flare.

Someone was signaling from Kestrel Col.
