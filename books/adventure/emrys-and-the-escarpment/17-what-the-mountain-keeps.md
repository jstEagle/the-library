## 17. What the Mountain Keeps

I became a doctor because I like problems with edges. A wound either closes or it doesn't. A pulse either returns or it doesn't. People think medicine teaches you humility, and maybe it does, but what it really taught me was a kind of arithmetic: you can't save everyone, so you triage, you rank, you let some problems bleed while you work the ones you can fix. It's a cold little system, and I built my whole self on it, and I want you to understand that before I tell you what I did on the escarpment wall at four in the afternoon with the wind screaming through the col like something being skinned.

Because the arithmetic said *keep climbing*. And we didn't.

* * *

The wall had been under us for six hours by then.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had left. Six hours of Wren Sandoval doing things with rope and ice that I still can't describe without my hands shaking—not from fear, exactly, but from the particular awe of watching someone do the one thing they were made for, after a decade of everyone insisting they were made for nothing. Wren climbed the way I imagine a surgeon who's been struck off climbs back into an operating theater: with a fury so controlled it looked like tenderness.

We were a rope team of four by then. Cass, Wren, me, and Priya, roped in that order, with Tomas behind me hauling the gear sled on a second line. The recovery team—four of them, plus their pilot—had made contact at dawn, and Wren had made a decision that still impresses me: instead of running, we climbed. The face where Kestrel died was the one place their machines couldn't follow and their politeness couldn't reach. "They can hike," Wren had said, racking a carabiner with a click that sounded final, "but they'll have to hike *up*."

So up we went. Pitch after pitch. The rock was black and rotten in places, welded in others, and the weather was doing that mountain thing where it pretends to be a rumor. My world narrowed to boots, holds, Wren's voice calling commands up and down the line in a register I'd only ever heard in operating rooms—flat, total, no room in it for anything but the next move.

I was on a stance the size of a dinner plate, clipping through, when Priya's radio crackled.

I want to say I heard it and understood immediately. I didn't. What I heard was a smear of static, and then a voice I recognized—Halloran, the recovery team's leader, the courteous man with the list of our names in his pocket—except the voice was wrong. Stripped of its courtesy. Saying words that came through in pieces:

*—mayday, mayday—* static *—down, repeat, we are—* static *—pass—fire—*

Then nothing. Then, faintly, a second voice, a woman's, screaming a name.

Priya looked at me. I looked down the rope at Wren, three pitches below, a fleck of red jacket against all that gray.

"Wren," I said into the radio. "You need to hear this."

* * *

Here's what I know about Wren Sandoval, and I knew it before the mountain confirmed it, because you learn it in the first hour of any crisis with them: they do not panic, and they do not hesitate, and the space between those two things is where they live. Wren took the report the way a surgeon takes a scan. Quiet. Complete. Then they looked up the face, and then they looked back down the way we'd come, toward the pass, where the weather was assembling itself into something with intent.

"The helicopter's down," Wren said, when Priya relayed it a second time, clearer. "In the pass. Behind us."

"Then our problem solved itself," Cass said. I heard the relief in her voice and hated it a little, and understood it a lot. Those people had chased us for three days. They'd offered to buy our silence with money on one side and a shallow grave on the other. Halloran had said Wren's name like he was reading it off a warrant.

Wren didn't answer right away. They stood on their stance with the wind pulling at their hood, and I watched them do the thing I'd watched them do at every hard moment since the station: not decide, exactly, but *arrive* somewhere they'd apparently already been.

"Four people," Wren said. "Maybe five. Wreck site, fuel, open ground, and that front coming in off the icefield will hit the pass in—" they checked the sky the way other people check a watch, "—two hours. Three if it's lazy."

"So?" Cass said. "Wren. *So?*"

"So they're dead in three hours," Wren said, "if nobody goes."

The rope went quiet. Wind, and the small creak of our anchors, and somewhere below us the gear sled ticking against rock.

"They hunted us," Cass said. "They had rifles. They offered to make us disappear. Odile—" and here she turned to me, and I felt it like a hand on my collar, because Cass Merrow knows exactly where people keep their soft spots, "—you triaged worse than this in field hospitals. You know the math."

I did know the math. That's the thing. I knew it better than anyone on that rope. Four or five casualties, exposed site, incoming weather, a two-hour traverse back down and across, and a climb still ahead of us with a storm on its heels. Every variable said *leave them*. Every variable said the mountain had finally done our arithmetic for us, and the answer was clean.

But I'd spent three days listening to Halloran be polite, and I'd noticed something about politeness like that: it's what people use when they've already decided you're not a person. And I thought about what it does to you, out here, to be not-a-person. I'd been not-a-person once myself, in a county hospital that lost my license paperwork for eleven months and treated me like a piece of equipment that had wandered in off the street. I remembered what the cold of that was like. It doesn't matter how good you are at your work. If everyone has agreed you're disposable, you start to believe the paperwork.

"Wren," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "I'll go."

Cass stared at me. "Odile."

"Somebody has to," I said. "And I'm the only one who can actually treat burns and crush injuries, and we both know it. You want to do the math? Fine. Here's mine. If we leave them, we're exactly what they said we were. We're just cargo that ran." I looked down the rope. "And I'm not spending the rest of my life being that."

Wren was already unclipping.

* * *

I'll give the devil her due: Cass fought it for exactly ninety seconds, and then she stopped fighting, because Cass Merrow is many things but she is not a coward, and she heard it in Wren's silence—heard that Wren was going down into that pass whether the rest of us came or not, and that a rope team doesn't get to vote on its leader's soul.

"You're giving up the wall," Cass said. "The storm closes the col. You understand that. Whatever window we had, we're spending it on *them*."

"I know what I'm spending," Wren said. "That's what spending is."

Tomas, who had been silent through all of it—Tomas, who had opened the logbooks against orders and put us all in the crosshairs, and who had been carrying that like a stone in his chest for two days—spoke for the first time in hours. "I'm coming with you," he said. "I know the icefield approaches. I mapped half of them."

"You mapped them from satellite," Wren said.

"I mapped them *well*," Tomas said, and for once there was no hunger in his voice, no data-lust, just a man trying to hand over a debt.

Priya said she'd hold the high stance with the gear and the logs—someone had to, and her shoulder was wrecked from the fall anyway. Cass said nothing for a long moment. Then: "I'm coming too."

Wren looked at her. Really looked. "You hate these people."

"I hate what they are," Cass said. "That's not the same thing. And my family built the machine that sent them. So." She shrugged, and the shrug was the least convincing thing I'd ever seen her do, and I loved her a little for it. "I'm coming."

* * *

The traverse down and across to the pass took two hours and ten minutes, and I remember almost none of it as climbing. It was more like being *carried*—by Wren's route-finding, by the rope, by the sheer momentum of a decision already made. Wren moved us across terrain that should have taken four, cutting corners I'd have sworn were suicidal until we were through them and I realized they'd been reading the ice the way I read a chart: old fractures, wind loading, the faint sag of a snow bridge that hadn't failed *yet*. Twice Wren stopped us, walked ahead unroped for thirty meters—thirty meters that aged me a year each—and came back and said, "Left. The right side is hollow," in the tone of someone reporting a lab result.

We came around the shoulder of the pass at 16:40 and saw the wreck.

I've seen aircraft accidents before. I want to tell you they're always dramatic, fire and twisted metal and that cinematic silence. Mostly they're not. Mostly they're just wrong—a machine lying in a landscape that has no room for it, like a sentence in a language you don't speak. The helicopter sat on the pass floor canted fifteen degrees, its tail boom snapped, one skid buried, the fuselage scorched along the top where the fire had tried and failed to take hold at altitude. The rotor blades were folded against the rock like a broken bird's wing.

And there were people around it. Three of them, upright. One on the ground with someone kneeling over him. One not moving at all, off to the side, laid out flat and covered with a survival blanket that was already half torn away by the wind.

The upright ones had rifles. Of course they did. Two of them swung toward us, and I watched the exact moment the recognition hit—that half-second where Halloran's people understood who was walking out of the storm toward them, and what that could not possibly mean, and then what it did mean. One of them actually raised his weapon partway, out of pure reflex, and then lowered it, and I watched a man's whole posture collapse as he understood that the gun had become the most useless object on the mountain.

Wren walked up to them, stopped ten feet out, and pulled back their hood.

"Report," Wren said.

It was such a guide's word. Not *what happened*, not *are you okay*—*report*. The word you use when you've taken command of a scene, and it landed on them like a rope thrown to a drowning swimmer. The younger one—the one with the rifle, whose hands were shaking—actually started talking. Pilot dead on impact, he said. Or the impact plus the fire; hard to say. Halloran had a femur fracture, open, plus burns on his arms. The woman screaming on the radio earlier—Voss—had a dislocated shoulder and a head lac and had been the only one with any first-aid training, which was to say a weekend course six years ago. The fifth, a man named Ibarra, had gone to check the tail section for the emergency beacon twenty minutes ago and a snow bridge had dropped him into a crevasse. They had him on the surface. He wasn't breathing well.

"Ibarra's dying," the young one said, and his voice cracked on it. "He's dying and we can't—we don't know how to—"

"Show me," I said.

* * *

I'm not going to give you the clinical details. Not because they're gruesome—though some were—but because they're not the point, and because Ibarra, whoever he was, deserves better than to become an anecdote in someone's adventure story. What I'll tell you is the shape of the next hour.

I worked. That's the whole of it. I worked the way I've worked a hundred times, in places with better light and worse odds, and the mountain stood over us and threw wind at us and I knelt in the lee of a broken machine and did the job. Cass held the IV bag. That's what I remember most, maybe: Cass Merrow, who had cut every deal and kept every secret, standing in the wind holding a bag of saline above a stranger with both arms raised like an offering, not letting the wind knock it, not complaining, not saying one clever thing. Tomas dug a wind wall out of the wrecked snow with a shattered rotor blade. Wren organized the survivors the way they organized everything—quietly, completely, until the scene belonged to them.

Halloran woke at one point. Concussion, shock, the femur. He looked up at me, and then past me at Wren, and his face did something I'll keep for a long time.

"You came back," he said. It came out as an accusation.

"Don't talk," I said.

"We had orders to—" He stopped. Swallowed. "We were going to leave you on the wall."

"I know," I said. I tightened the traction strap. "That's the difference. Hold still."

And here's the cost, because I promised you the cost, and I don't get to round it down.

The hour we spent in that pass was the hour the weather spent coming over the icefield. When we finally loaded Halloran into the rescue litter and started moving—because we couldn't stay; the pass floor was going to be a wind tunnel by dark—Wren stood and looked up at the col and I watched them do the arithmetic they'd been doing all along, the one they'd known the answer to since Priya's radio first crackled.

The col was closed. The window was gone. The wall, the final climb, the route out—the one honest door off this mountain—had shut while our hands were full of other people's blood.

Wren didn't say it. They just looked up for a long moment, and then down at Halloran, and then at us, and I saw them set down something they'd been carrying for ten years—no, that's not right. I saw them *pick up* something. Vindication, maybe. The col, the cache, the truth at the end of the route. Whatever Wren had been climbing toward since the station, it was on the other side of a face that was now wearing a full storm, and we had four casualties, no helicopter, and maybe ninety minutes of usable light.

"There's an old route," Wren said. "Kestrel's retreat line. It goes around the shoulder instead of over the col. Longer. Lower. Through terrain nobody's touched in ten years." A pause. "It passes the cache."

Cass went very still. "The final cache."

"The radio," Wren said. "The original. The one that was never broken." They looked down the pass, at the wreck, at the people we'd just spent our escape on. "They'll need to know why. All of it. So do you. And this is the only way to it now."

The wind rose. Somewhere above us, the mountain took the col back into the cloud, the way it takes everything, eventually.

"Can we make it?" Priya's voice, thin over the radio from the high stance.

Wren shouldered their end of the litter.

"We're about to find out," they said. "Rope up. All of us."

And that's how the eight of us—the hunted, the hunters, and the doctor in the middle—started moving into the dark together, toward the place where the mountain had kept its oldest secret, and where I already understood, in the way you understand a prognosis before the tests come back, that not everyone roped into my team was going to see the other side of it.
