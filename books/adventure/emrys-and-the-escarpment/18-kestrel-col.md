## 18. Kestrel Col

The col looked exactly the way I remembered it, which was the cruelest thing about it.

Ten years is long enough for a mountain to forget you. Avalanches redraw the slopes, seracs calve and collapse, a whole icefall can rearrange itself like furniture in a house where nobody lives. But Kestrel Col just sat there between its two shoulders, a saddle of wind-scoured snow about as wide as a tennis court, holding its little bowl of sky, unchanged, indifferent, waiting.

I'd spent a decade telling myself I never wanted to see it again. Turns out that's not the same as not knowing the way.

We came up from the east in the last grey light of afternoon, six of us moving like survivors of something, because we were. Odile on point with me, Priya behind, then Tomas, then the two recovery-team men we'd pulled out of their own wreckage two days ago—Voss limping on a strapped ankle, Delgado moving fine and saying nothing. Cass brought up the rear. She'd been bringing up the rear since the helicopter went down in the pass, quiet in a way that wasn't like her, and I hadn't decided yet whether that quiet was grief or arithmetic.

The wind at the col came out of the west at maybe forty knots, steady, the kind of wind that doesn't gust so much as insist. It had polished the snow down to blue ice in patches, and it had scoured one corner of the saddle clean down to old rock—a shelf of dark stone with a lip of granite, sheltered from the prevailing flow.

I stopped when I saw it.

Not because I recognized it. Because my body recognized it before my head did, the way you flinch from a doorframe you walked into once, years ago. My legs simply stopped, and my stomach dropped through the floor of me, and I stood there in the wind like an idiot while five people stacked up behind me and asked what.

"Give me a minute," I said.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about the things you bury: they don't decompose. You put them down deep and pile the years on top and tell yourself the weight will finish the job, and then some wind comes along, ten years later, and scours off every layer you laid down, and there it is. Whole. Waiting. Patient as geology.

The cache was under a cairn I had built myself.

I knew the stones. That sounds insane—I know how that sounds—but I'd built a thousand cairns in my life and this one I'd built with hands that wouldn't stop shaking, stacking rocks in a storm while two of my clients lay dead above me and the third sat against his pack staring at nothing. You don't forget the stones you stack with shaking hands. They're crooked in a particular way. These were crooked in that way.

"Tomas," I said. "Pry bar."

He handed it over without a word. He'd been quieter too since the logbooks—since he'd read aloud, in a voice that kept cracking, what Kestrel had actually found up here. The strike. The retreat line cut on purpose. The word *deliberate* sitting in a surveyor's flat handwriting like it was a note about soil samples.

The cairn came apart stone by stone. Underneath: a duffel bag, canvas, army-surplus, the fabric bleached grey and stiff as plywood but intact. Frozen into a block. I knelt over it and worked at the zipper with bare fingers until Odile put her hand on my shoulder and said, "Wren. Let me. Your hands are done."

I let her. That's a thing I've learned late in life: let people help even when the help is for something only you can face. She worked the zipper free with a knife tip and hot water from her bottle, and the bag opened like a mouth.

Inside, wrapped in a groundsheet gone brittle as autumn leaves: the radio.

* * *

You have to understand what that radio meant. It was the whole case against me, in one object.

Kestrel Expedition, ten years ago. Seven of us above the escarpment wall when the front came in early and hard. We retreated. Somewhere in the retreat, the radio—the satellite unit, our only link—failed. Dead. No mayday, no weather updates, no coordination with base. Two climbers, Halloran and Ibarra, went back for a dropped rope anchor above the wall and the mountain took them while we stumbled down blind. When we got out, the inquiry asked one question over and over: why didn't you call for support, for a heli evac, for anything? And the answer, my answer, was: the radio broke. And no one believed me, because the radio, recovered later from the cache where I'd left it, showed impact damage to its casing. Broken. Consistent with a fall. Consistent with negligence, with a guide who'd dropped his lifeline and lied about it.

My license went. My name went on a list that gets quietly passed between outfitters and insurers. *Do not hire.* I became a ghost who walked other people's dogs up other people's hills under other people's names.

And here it was. The radio. Sitting in a bag I packed myself, in a cache I built myself, at a col I reached before the deaths, not after—which mattered, though it took me a moment to understand why my memory was screaming at me.

Because I hadn't carried it down. I'd left it here. We'd cached heavy gear at the col on the ascent, standard practice, and the radio had been in the cache because on the retreat we'd planned to come back through the col and retrieve it. Except the storm changed the plan, and the retreat went south instead of east, and the radio stayed.

Which meant the damage to it—the damage the investigators documented, the damage that convicted me—happened after I last touched it. After. While it sat under my crooked stones.

Or someone opened the cache and made sure of it.

My hands were steady now. Funny how that works. Ten years of shaking and the shake finally stops at the exact moment it's too late to do me any good.

"Wren?" Odile again. "What is it?"

"It's the Kestrel radio," I said. "It's been here the whole time."

Tomas made a sound low in his throat. Priya stepped forward, and Voss and Delgado exchanged a look that I pretended not to see—two corporate recovery agents watching evidence walk around in the open air. Let them look. There wasn't a company left to recover it for. There was barely a company, period; the logbooks had seen to that.

Priya turned the unit over in her gloved hands. Her breath caught.

"Wren. Look at the battery compartment."

* * *

The casing damage was real. A crack across the housing, a dent near the antenna mount—genuine impact trauma, the kind the investigators' photos had shown. But the battery compartment told a different story. The latches were intact. Unscratched. And inside, when Priya eased them open with the delicacy of a surgeon defusing something, the battery pack sat snug and seated, terminals clean, contacts bright.

Bright. After ten years in a frozen cache, the contacts were bright, because they'd been coated in dielectric grease. Applied recently. Applied by someone who understood electronics and wanted the unit preserved.

"This radio works," Priya said. Her voice had gone strange and flat, the way it did when she was reading bad news off a wire. "It works right now. This isn't a broken radio, Wren. This is a maintained radio. Someone has been servicing it."

"Servicing it," Tomas repeated. "In a sealed cache on a closed concession."

"Someone with access," Priya said. "Someone who came up here."

And then the arithmetic finished, all of it, every column, and the sum landed in my chest like a dropped rock.

Cass Merrow's father had led Kestrel. Cass Merrow's family had held the concession for thirty years. Cass Merrow had spent a decade, by her own telling, digging into what happened—her words, trapped in a tent during a whiteout, sympathetic and halting and half-true. She'd hired me, she said, to find proof. Proof of the cover-up. Proof that the retreat line was cut, that Halloran and Ibarra were written off as weather, that the mineral strike below the wall was worth more than two lives and could be protected better if the route died with them.

She'd sent me up here to find proof.

She'd known exactly where the proof was.

"You've been here before," I said. I didn't turn around. I watched Priya's hands on the radio and I spoke to the col, to the wind, to the crooked stones. "To this cache. More than once. You kept the radio alive."

Behind me, the wind filled the silence where an answer should have been.

"Cass." Odile's voice, gentle and terrible. "Is that true?"

* * *

When I finally turned, she was standing apart from the group, the way she'd been standing for two days, and her face had come apart. Not dramatically—that was the awful part. No collapse, no wailing. Just the slow structural failure of a person whose load-bearing story has finally given way. She looked like a ridge in thaw.

"I was nineteen," she said.

Nobody moved. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"The summer after Kestrel. Dad sent me up with the survey crew—they still ran surveys then, before the extraction contracts. Maintenance runs, they called them. Checking the caches, checking the instruments." She laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "Checking the radio. That was my job. Every season. Keep it cold, keep it greased, keep it working. I didn't ask why. I was nineteen and my father had just buried two climbers and ruined a guide, and I thought—" Her hands opened, empty. "I thought we were preserving evidence. For the truth. Eventually. Someday. I thought someday we'd bring it down and it would clear everything up."

"And instead it cleared you up," I said. "And buried me deeper."

"Yes." No flinch. I'll give her that; she never flinched. "For fifteen years I told myself it was for the truth. Then Dad died and I read his files—all of them, not the curated ones—and I found out the truth had a price tag on it. The strike. The buyers. The permit renewal contingent on the route staying closed and the Kestrel question staying closed with it." She looked at the radio in Priya's hands like it was a heart on a tray. "So I hired you. And yes, Wren, I knew where things were. I knew this col. I knew if anyone could get a crew up here and find what needed finding, it was the guide my father destroyed. I told myself that was justice. Sending you to dig up your own vindication."

"And the permits lapsing? The extraction land? Was that justice too?"

"That was leverage. Pressure. A deadline to force everyone's hand—including mine." Her voice cracked on the last word, finally, one hairline fracture. "I had a deal, Wren. Before the helicopter. Before the wreck. I was going to hand over the logs, take the fall for the trespass, protect the family name as best I could, and leave you and the others out of it. That was the plan. That was always the plan."

"And the radio?"

"I don't know," she whispered. "I swear to God, Wren, I don't know who ordered it silenced. I only know I kept it alive. All those years. It was the one true thing I had."

* * *

Here's the thing I want to get right, and I'm going to try, because it matters more than the radio or the logs or the billion-dollar strike sleeping under the wall.

I should have hated her. Some part of me did inventory and found the hatred missing, and reported the discrepancy with something like alarm. Ten years of exile, of false names and dog-walking jobs and motel ceilings, of a list with my name on it passed hand to hand like contraband—and the woman who'd kept the instrument of my ruin oiled and running stood in front of me crying into the wind, and what I felt was not hate.

It was recognition.

Because I knew what it was to carry a dead expedition. I'd carried mine for a decade, and mine at least had the decency to be a lie I could examine. Hers was worse. She'd carried hers as a duty, a filial maintenance schedule, greasing the terminals of a lie every summer with her own hands, telling herself it was service to the truth. That's not betrayal. That's a hostage situation that lasts fifteen years and starts to feel like a marriage.

"Halloran," I said. "Ibarra. Say their names."

She lifted her head. "Tom Halloran. Reina Ibarra."

"They died because the retreat line was cut. Because someone decided the strike was worth more than the climb out. And the radio—if it had worked, if we'd called down—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I let it. "A heli could have reached the upper shoulder inside the window. I did the math a thousand times. A thousand times, Cass. They'd have lived."

"I know."

"The radio was never broken."

"No."

"So the whole inquiry, the whole verdict, the whole list my name's been on—it was built on a machine somebody kept alive in a box so it could stay broken on paper forever."

"Yes." She wiped her face with the back of her glove, a rough, angry gesture, furious at her own tears. "And I helped. Nineteen years old, greasing the latch. So go ahead. Whatever you're going to do. Do it."

What I was going to do was stand there for a long moment with the wind pushing on all of us, looking at this woman who was both the architect of my ruin and its most faithful prisoner, and understand that the mountain had brought me here not for vengeance and not even for vindication, but for this: to watch the last locked door in the whole rotten structure swing open, and find that behind it was just a person. Young once. Complicit. Sorry in the only currency that can't be counterfeited, which is the kind of sorry that costs everything to say out loud.

"Voss," I said. "Delgado. You two are witnesses. Both incidents—Kestrel and this traverse. Everything you saw. I want it on record that you heard it from her mouth, freely given."

Voss shifted his weight off his bad ankle. "We're not exactly—"

"You're the only disinterested parties on this col," I said. "Congratulations. It's a promotion."

Something almost like a smile crossed Delgado's frost-burned face. "Witnessed," he said.

* * *

We buried the radio properly that evening—or rather, we didn't bury it at all.

That was Priya's idea. "Evidence stays evidence," she said, wrapping the unit in fresh insulation, logging its condition, photographing it against the col's skyline so no one could ever claim it had been tampered with after recovery. "But it flies out with us. It goes to the inquiry. It testifies." She patted the wrapped shape like a teammate. "Ten years on the bench. Time to play."

Tomas built a new cairn over the empty cache—not to hide anything, but to mark it. He's a geologist; he thinks in strata. "Layers," he said, setting each stone straight and true, none of them crooked. "This is where the record was kept. People should be able to find the place."

Odile checked everyone's frostnip and fed us melted snow and didn't say much, which from Odile meant she was filing everything away in the part of herself that would later become diagnoses and, occasionally, miracles.

And Cass sat on the edge of the col with her back to the wind, looking west toward the escarpment wall, where the light was going copper and then violet and then gone, and I sat down beside her because somebody had to and because, God help me, I wanted to.

"My father used to say the mountain keeps what it keeps," she said. "I thought he meant the bodies."

"He meant the truth," I said. "Mountains are honest. It's the paperwork that lies."

She huffed something that was almost a laugh. "What happens to me now?"

"I don't know," I said, and it was the truth, and it was strangely comfortable to say. "The inquiry decides. The families decide. Halloran's sister is still alive, you know. I found that out three years ago and couldn't make myself call her. Maybe now I can. Maybe now there's something to say besides *I'm sorry* and *it was the weather.*"

"She'll want the radio."

"She'll get the radio."

Below us, far down the eastern approach, a light blinked. Once. Twice. Steady rhythm, wrong color for a beacon, wrong place for a camp.

Priya was already reaching for her receiver. "That's a signal lamp," she said. "Somebody's climbing toward us."

Of course they were. The mountain keeps what it keeps—but apparently it also sends up whatever's left.

I got to my feet, and my knees, ten years older than the ones that had fled this col, held fine.

"All right," I said. "Everybody up. Looks like the descent starts tonight."
