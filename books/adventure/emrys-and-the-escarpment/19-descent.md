## 19. Descent

The storm didn't end. It just got tired of us.

That's the only way I can describe it—somewhere around dawn on our third day pinned at the wreck site, the wind dropped from a scream to a mutter, and Wren stood at the tent door like a woman listening for a knock she'd been dreading, and said, "We move now, while it's sulking."

Nobody argued. You don't argue with the person who just turned a climb around for the people trying to arrest you. You pack your gear and you try not to look at them too long, because looking too long at a thing like that makes your chest do something inconvenient.

We were nine now. Five of ours, and four from the helicopter—two of them walking wounded with the particular gray-faced stoicism of men who'd been polite and armed right up until the mountain swatted their machine out of the sky, and two of them not walking at all. Odile had spent the storm doing surgery-grade improvisation in a tent that smelled of jet fuel and antiseptic, and she came out of it looking like she'd aged a decade and would bill for every year.

The pilot was the worst. Compound fracture, internal bleeding Odile didn't like the sound of and couldn't rule out. His name was Aris. He'd told us that somewhere in the second day, in the flat voice of a man who'd decided that if he was going to die among enemies, they were at least going to know who was dying at them.

"He can't be hauled," Odile said at the briefing. Not a briefing, really—five people and two survivors huddled in a vestibule while snow ticked against nylon. "I mean he *can*, but every hour of transport costs him. Sled, improvised, over broken ground, in this." She didn't finish the sentence. She didn't have to.

Wren looked at the storm, then at the wreck, then at the map they'd drawn in the snow with a ski pole.

"Then we don't haul him far," Wren said. "We take everyone to Kestrel Col. There's shelter there—the old cache hut, if it's still standing. We stage from the col, get a line out on Priya's radio, and bring him down after."

"And if the col hut is gone?" That was Voss, the recovery team's second, the one with the courteous voice and the eyes that never stopped calculating. His arm was splinted. He'd stopped pretending to be in charge around hour six of the storm, which I'll grant him: he adapted fast.

"Then it's a long night," Wren said. "But it's the same night either way. The col is the only ground that makes sense."

Voss looked at Wren for a long moment. I watched him do the arithmetic—every hour with us was an hour his employer's secrets sat in a pack on a stranger's back—and I watched something else arrive behind the arithmetic, something more tired.

"Lead on," he said.

* * *

I want to tell you what hauling a sled through a storm on the shoulder of the Escarpment is like, and I can't, really, because my hands were full and my head was down and the world had contracted to a strip of rope and the boots in front of me. But here's what I remember:

I remember Wren setting the pace like a metronome. Not fast. Never fast. Fast is how people die on ice, and Wren had told us that on day one, and by day nineteen we'd all had it carved into us. Step. Breathe. Step. The rope between us was less a line than a nervous system; you felt every stumble travel down it like a word passed hand to hand.

I remember Odile singing. Low, tuneless, half under her breath—some medic's litany, a checklist set to a rhythm to keep her counting. Airway. Bleeding. Warmth. Airway. Bleeding. Warmth. Aris was on the sled between Cass and Voss, and every time the sled lurched, Odile's song got one note louder.

I remember Tomas crying without any noise at all, just the shaking of his shoulders under the pack, and the way he kept one gloved hand pressed flat against the side of his rucksack where the logbooks rode, like a man checking a wound.

And I remember Cass.

Cass Merrow, who had cut a deal with the people hunting us, who had planned to hand over everything but Wren—Cass had spent the storm not speaking. Not to Wren, not to any of us. She'd hauled her share and more, taken the worst rope positions, volunteered for every miserable task, and said approximately forty words in three days. I'd caught her once, at night, sitting at the tent wall with the wreck's red beacon light strobing across her face, and she wasn't praying. She was listening. Like she was trying to hear the exact frequency of what she'd done.

Now, on the rope, she kept looking back at Wren. Not the way you look at someone you've wronged when you want forgiveness. The way you look at a fixed line you don't trust yourself to deserve.

The col appeared out of the murk around noon, a notch in the ridge like a door someone had left ajar, and the cache hut—God, the hut was still there. Half-buried, door gone, but the walls stood, Kestrel-era corrugated steel gone the color of old bone, and when we got the survivors inside and got the stove going, the whole thing filled with a light that was almost, almost warm.

Wren stood in the doorway a long time, looking at the walls. I knew what they were seeing. I'd been on the radio when the transmission came through at Kestrel Col's twin, up-route: the original radio, never broken. Deliberately silenced. The last piece of the machine that had chewed up two climbers and spat out Wren Sandoval's name as a chew toy. Cass's father's machine.

Cass knew it too. You could see it land on her there in the hut, in the light of a stove her father's company had probably funded, and I watched her make a decision the way you watch someone step off a edge.

* * *

She did it while Odile was changing Aris's dressings and Tomas was melting snow and Voss was asleep—actually asleep, which either meant trust or total collapse, and with Voss I genuinely couldn't tell.

Cass took her pack off, unbuckled the top compartment, and brought out the logbooks. Both of them. Wrapped in her spare shirt, which I noticed, because you wrap things in your spare shirt when you've stopped believing you'll need to be warm more than you need them safe.

She carried them to Wren, who was re-coiling rope by the door because Wren's hands needed work the way other people's hands need sleep.

"Cass." Wren didn't look up.

"I'm not going to explain," Cass said. "I've been explaining for nineteen days and none of it has made me less of what I am. I cut a deal. I was going to give them the logs and keep your name out of it, and I told myself that was mercy. It wasn't. It was just the version of betrayal I could live with." She held the books out. "Take them. No conditions. Not even the condition that you forgive me. Especially not that one."

Wren put down the rope.

For a second I thought they were going to refuse. Wren Sandoval, the person who had turned a climb around for their own hunters, the person who had led us off the map and across the icefield and up the wall of their own worst memory—Wren stood there with their hands at their sides and I swear the whole hut held its breath, stove ticking, storm muttering at the walls, four injured strangers watching from their bedrolls like an audience that had paid too much for its tickets.

Then Wren took the logbooks.

"Thank you," Wren said, and it came out rough, like something hauled up from depth. "For the no-conditions part. That's the only thing you could have given me that was worth anything."

Cass nodded once, and went back to her pack, and that was it. No absolution, no scene. Some transactions are too large for ceremony.

I found out later—much later, in the spring, over beer—what Wren did with the books that night. They didn't read them. They put them in the stove-side dry bag, next to the flares, in the position of highest priority, which in Wren's grammar meant: *these are the cargo now. Everything else is ballast.*

* * *

It was Voss who found the problem, which is the kind of irony the mountain deals in.

He'd been awake an hour, sitting up with his splinted arm cradled like a pet, and he'd been watching Aris. Watching the way Aris's breathing had gone. I'd been on the radio for two of those hours, running through frequencies with the patience of a woman praying to a machine, and I got a carrier wave—a real one, a helicopter out of the north, a survey flight that owed Cass's family nothing and me a favor from a job I'd done under a false name two years ago—and I was so busy shouting coordinates into the static that I missed it.

Odile didn't. Odile never misses it.

"His pressure's falling," she said, in the voice she used for weather. "He's bleeding inside. I can slow it here. I can't stop it here. He needs a hospital in—" she looked at the storm, did the math, hated the answer— "six hours. Maybe eight."

The survey flight couldn't land at the col. Too narrow, too much crosswind. They could lower a litter at the wreck site—the wreck site, down the shoulder, two hours' travel through the storm's dying convulsions—and only at first light.

"Somebody has to be down there with him," Odile said. "To hold the litter steady, to talk him through it. Two somebodies, ideally. And they'd miss the main descent. They'd be on this mountain another full day, past the permit lapse, past everything."

The hut got quiet in the way huts do when a question has been asked that nobody wants to answer first.

"I'll go." It was Cass. Of course it was Cass. "It's my family's mountain. My family's mess. My—" she stopped, recalibrated. "I'll go."

"I'll go with her." Voss. Sitting up straight, splint and all. "He's my pilot. My responsibility. And frankly, Ms. Merrow, you're the one person on this mountain my employer will never stop looking for. Someone should be there to make sure they find you *alive*."

Cass looked at him. Something passed between them that I didn't have the security clearance to read.

That left one more, and everyone knew it, because Aris couldn't be moved to the litter site and stabilized by two people with four hands between them, not in wind, not in the dark, not with a litter on a winch line.

"I'll go," said Odile.

"No." Wren said it fast, and then slower. "No. I need you on the descent. If anything happens to the group on the way down, you're the only one who—" Wren stopped. Looked at their own hands. "I can't do the arithmetic that spends you."

"Then we're one short," Odile said gently.

And that's when Tomas stood up.

I want to be fair to Tomas here, because the easy version of this story—the version the inquiry will print—is that Tomas was the one who opened the logbooks, Tomas the data-hoarder, Tomas who couldn't leave sealed things sealed, and therefore Tomas was the one who stayed behind on the mountain. Cause, meet consequence. Clean.

It wasn't like that. I watched his face when he stood up, and there wasn't a trace of penance in it. There was something simpler and much harder to look at.

"I'm the strongest climber left who isn't needed on the rope," he said. "And I—" he laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "I've spent nineteen days wanting to *know things*. This is the first time knowing something will actually help anyone." He looked at the logbooks in Wren's dry bag, and then away. "Let me be useful instead. Just once. Just usefully."

Nobody argued. I think we'd all run out of arguments somewhere around the icefield, and what was left was just people, doing what the moment required, and discovering that we were—God help us—good at it.

* * *

They left at first light, the three of them, Cass and Voss and Tomas, down the shoulder toward the wreck, hauling Aris on the sled with Odile's instructions written on a strip of tent fabric in marker: doses, intervals, the warning signs, in handwriting that shook only a little.

I stood at the hut door and watched them go until the murk took them, and I want to tell you I felt noble about it, solidarity of the rope and all that, but mostly what I felt was the specific grief of the radio operator, which is: you send people out and then you sit in one place and *listen*.

So I listened. I worked the frequencies. And at 09:40 the survey flight confirmed the litter site, and at 10:15 they had Aris on the line, and at 10:22 Odile's voice—relayed, broken, half static—came through my headset saying *he's stable, he's stable, get him out, get him out*, and I put my head against the radio housing and cried like the machine had personally told me a joke.

Aris went out at 10:40. The flight couldn't come back for the others until the next weather window.

The next weather window was thirty-one hours away.

* * *

We descended without them. That's the sentence, and it's true, and it was awful. Wren led us down the south flank on the old Kestrel descent line, the one from the logbooks, the one that had never been in any permit filing, and the storm's last convulsion chased us the whole way like a dog that had decided we were its pack and didn't approve of us leaving.

We came out of the cloud at midafternoon two days later, onto the lower moraine, and the light down there was the ordinary golden light of the ordinary world, and it was so obscene in its ordinariness that Halvard—quiet Halvard, who hadn't said a full sentence since the wall—sat down on a rock and laughed until he cried.

The radio crackled at dusk. Tomas's voice, thin with distance and exhaustion, but steady:

"Priya. Cass says—tell Wren—she's staying till the flight. Voss too. They're fine. The wreck's—Priya, the wreck site is where the retreat line was cut. There's hardware in the ice. Anchors. Kestrel anchors, ten years old, right where the logbooks said. Cass found them. She's—" static, long, ugly. "She's sitting with them. She says somebody should."

I relayed it to Wren word for word. Wren was checking the rope coils, and their hands didn't stop, but they went very still, the way water goes still right before it freezes or right before it moves.

"Tell her," Wren said finally, "that the permits lapse at midnight tomorrow. Tell her that after that, nobody on this mountain is trespassing. Tell her—" and here Wren's voice did the rough thing again, the depth-thing, "—tell her the mountain keeps what it keeps. But people get to come home."

I sent it. All of it. And from the shoulder of the Escarpment, through the static and the dying storm, Cass Merrow's voice came back one last time that night, faint as a memory of a memory:

"Tell Wren the logbooks were never mine to give. But I gave them anyway. And Priya—" a pause, wind noise, the beep of Odile's monitor in the background, keeping time for all of them, "—when this is over, someone should write it down. All of it. Even the parts about me. *Especially* the parts about me."

I looked at Wren. Wren looked at the mountain, where the storm was finally, grudgingly, letting go.

"Midnight tomorrow," Wren said. "We cross out before the permits die. All of us. Even the ones still up there—especially them." A breath. "And then, Priya, we tell everyone."

The radio hissed. Somewhere above us, in the last of the light, three people sat with a wreck and a decade of buried truth, waiting for a helicopter, while below, the whole world waited to hear what we'd carried down.
