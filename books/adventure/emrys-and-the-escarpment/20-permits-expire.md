## 20. Permits Expire

The concession boundary was a line of orange survey stakes driven into the scree, half of them snapped by wind, all of them older than my disgrace. I'd crossed it once before, ten years ago, going the other direction, with two dead men on my conscience and a company lawyer's business card in my pocket. This time I came down out of the storm with eleven living people, three of them strangers who'd tried to ruin us, and no paperwork at all.

The clock mattered. Priya had done the math at Kestrel Col, fingers too cold to work the keypad, so she'd recited it twice and made Odile repeat it back like a medic confirming a dosage. The permits lapsed at midnight, local time. After that, everything inside the boundary—every stake, every cache, every body the mountain was still holding—became the property of whatever consortium held the extraction lease. Trespassers would be prosecuted. Evidence would be *sealed*.

We came down off the moraine at 11:40 in the evening, twenty minutes to spare, and then the mountain decided we hadn't suffered enough and gave us a crevasse field between us and the stakes.

"Of course," Odile said. She said it the way other people say prayers.

I went first. There's a version of this where I tell you I felt nothing, that the icefield was just geometry and I was just a machine for reading it, and some nights I'd even believe myself. But the truth is my hands were shaking, and not from the cold. Every bridge I tested with the probe, I heard the radio under it—the one that never broke. *Deliberately silenced.* My father's voice saying *the set's gone, Wren, we're blind.* Ten years of believing I'd failed to fix what couldn't be fixed, because a man with a concession to protect had put a screwdriver through the transmitter and told everyone it died of natural causes.

I probed snow. I listened to the mountain lie about being solid. And I thought: *they knew. They always knew.*

"Wren." Cass, behind me, roped in second. Her voice had changed since the col. It was smaller now, or maybe just honest-sized for the first time. "You want me to take point?"

"No."

She didn't argue. That was new too.

We crossed the last bridge at 11:52. Eight minutes. Tomas nearly went down at the far lip—he'd been running on fumes since the helicopter crash, hauling wreckage survivors on a litter he'd improvised from a tent pole and guilt—and Priya caught his pack strap and swore at him in three languages until he found his feet again.

At 11:58 we stood among the broken orange stakes. At midnight, technically, legally, administratively, we stopped being trespassers.

Nobody cheered. We were too tired to cheer. But Priya sat down right there in the snow, inside the boundary one minute and outside it the next, and started laughing—a low, cracked, unhinged sound that went on long enough that Odile crouched beside her and checked her pupils.

"I'm fine," Priya said. "I'm fine. I just spent nine days committing crimes so that at exactly twelve-oh-one they'd stop being crimes, and it worked. Do you understand how stupid the law is? Do you understand that it *worked?*"

"It worked because you read the fine print," Cass said.

"It worked because Wren got us over a crevasse field in eight minutes," Priya said. "Let's be accurate about our miracles."

I looked back the way we'd come. The storm had closed behind us like a door. Somewhere up there was the pass, and the wreck of the recovery team's helicopter, and the man who'd chosen to stay with it—Halloran, the recovery team's comms officer, who'd waved us off with a broken arm and a level voice and said someone had to keep the injured ones company until relief came, and that it wasn't going to be us, because we had living people to get home. He'd been right. That didn't make it easier. Nothing about him had been easy; he'd pointed a rifle at us four days ago and shared his chocolate with us yesterday.

"You're thinking about Halloran," Odile said quietly. She'd fallen into step beside me without my noticing, which is either a medic's training or a friend's instinct, and I wasn't sure which frightened me more.

"I'm thinking he's still up there."

"He knows the protocol. He built the shelter before we left. He has heat, signal fire, and three men who owe him their lives." She paused. "He also knows you turned around a summit push to dig them out of that wreck. Whatever else happens, that story leaves this mountain with him."

I didn't answer. Some debts you can't pay and can't refuse, and mercy is usually both.

* * *

We made camp an hour beyond the stakes, in the gray country below the escarpment where the land stops pretending to be dramatic and starts being merely beautiful. Flat meadows under old snow, a frozen creek, wind-stunted pines. Ordinary ground. Ground where nobody had ever died for a mineral strike or a stock price.

Tomas built the stove. His hands were steady now, which surprised me until I understood: he'd opened the logbooks against my orders, and he'd carried what was in them across the whole high route, and somewhere in the last two days the carrying had burned the trembling out of him. You don't shake when you've already seen the worst thing.

"They'll send people," he said, to the stove more than to anyone. "The consortium. Once the survivors report in. They'll want the logs."

"The logs are mine," Cass said.

Everyone looked at her. She was sitting against her pack with her boots off—Odile had insisted, frost-nip on two toes—and she met every stare without flinching, which took a kind of courage I was in no mood to admire.

"You surrendered them," I said. "No conditions. Your words."

"And I meant it." She turned her hands palm-up, empty. "But I want to say the rest of it out loud, once, where everyone can hear, because I've earned the right to be suspected and I don't get to skip the hearing. If I could buy my father's name back with those books, I'd have done it. Three days ago I tried. I cut a deal, I signaled the team, I planned to hand over everything but Wren." She said it flat, no performance in it. "And then I watched Wren turn a climb around for the people hunting us, and I understood that I'd inherited my father's arithmetic—that some lives count and some are rounding errors—and I am done doing that math. So. The logs are Wren's. Whatever Wren decides. I'll testify, publicly, under my own name, if it comes to that. Merrow included. Let the family name burn with the rest of it."

Silence. The stove hissed. Somewhere downslope a branch cracked under snow-load, loud as a shot, and nobody reached for a weapon, which told me how far we'd come from the whiteout, when every sound had been an accusation.

Priya spoke first. "For the record: I still think you should have told us about the deal before the deal."

"Yes."

"But for the record also: you're the only person on this mountain who ever came clean about anything, including the people who were right the whole time." She glanced at me. "Present company excepted. You were right and quiet about it, which is somehow worse."

"Thank you," I said.

"It wasn't entirely a compliment."

"It was entirely accurate, though."

That got the first real laugh out of the camp in days—thin and exhausted, but real. Even Cass smiled, briefly, like a woman testing whether her face still worked.

* * *

The survivors came down to us at dawn.

We heard them before we saw them—three figures picking along the creek line, moving like men who'd learned humility from a helicopter. Their leader was a broad, gray-templed woman named Voss, and she walked into our camp with her hands visible and her weapon slung backward, which in her profession probably counted as a bouquet of flowers.

"Sandoval." She said my name carefully, like a word in a language she'd studied but never spoken. "You know who I am."

"I know what your invoice looks like. Voss Recovery Solutions. You pulled two clients of mine out of a whiteout in '19 and charged them the price of a house."

"That was a good job." She looked at our camp—at the litter, the bandages, Odile's med kit spread open like a field altar—and something in her jaw shifted. "This one's better. You want to tell me how a blacklisted guide runs a five-person crew across the Escarpment in winter, evades my team for six days, and then turns around mid-ascent to dig my people out of a wreck?"

"Badly, mostly. With help."

Voss crouched by the stove and warmed her hands, uninvited, and I let her, because her three survivors were already sitting with ours sharing water like it was the most natural thing in the world, and because I'd learned something on the icefield about the difference between enemies and people with worse employers.

"Here's my situation," she said. "My contract terminated at midnight. Not suspended—terminated. The consortium's lawyers called my satellite phone at oh-six-hundred and informed me that any personnel remaining inside the former concession are independent actors, and that my insurance doesn't cover independent actors." She almost smiled. "They crashed my helicopter, stranded my crew, voided my coverage, and they did it all from an office at sea level. I've been in this business thirty years. That's the coldest thing I've ever seen on a mountain, and I once recovered a body from the north couloir in February."

"What do you want, Voss?"

"Two things." She held up fingers. "One: transport. My people need to get off this range and I need a radio relay, and your tech—" she nodded at Priya "—has been reading our frequencies for days anyway, so let's not pretend."

"One condition," Priya called over. "I keep copies of everything I intercepted."

"Done. Two:" Voss lowered her voice, though there was no one left to hide from. "When this goes wherever it's going—the inquiry, the press, whatever Merrow's planning—I'll corroborate. All of it. The cutoff line on the retreat route. The order to seal the station. The instruction I was given at briefing: *recover materials, limit witness exposure.*" She said the phrase like it tasted bad. "I didn't know what it meant when I took the contract. I know now. My people heard the logbooks read aloud at your camp two nights ago while we were waiting to see if you'd let us freeze. We talked about it all night." She looked at me straight. "Ten years ago somebody fed you to this mountain to protect a number on a balance sheet. I'm not going to be the next link in that chain. I've got a daughter. I'd rather she inherit a difficult mother than a comfortable liar."

I didn't say anything for a moment. Exhaustion does strange things; I found I was near tears and filed it under wind-chill.

"Your transport," I managed. "There's a ranger strip at Miller Flats, forty klicks east. Priya can raise them. Tell them eleven hikers, three of yours, one stretcher case. Tell them the truth about how many and leave out everything else. Truth travels better when it travels light."

Voss stood, offered her hand, and I shook it. Her grip was careful, deliberate—the handshake of a woman making a record.

"For what it's worth," she said, turning to go, "the blacklist file had your name flagged *indefinite review*. I've seen those flags before. They don't expire. They wait." She shrugged into her pack. "Somebody built that file to last, Sandoval. Whoever it was will be watching the inquiry very closely. People who bury things deep tend to dig fast when the ground opens."

She walked down the creek line with her three survivors, and I watched until the ground folded them away, and I noticed that my hands had started shaking again.

* * *

Cass found me at the edge of camp an hour later. I was doing the thing I do instead of sleeping—checking rope coils, checking the sky, checking things that were already checked—and she stood beside me in the wind and didn't speak for a while, which I grudgingly respected.

"Priya raised Miller Flats," she said finally. "Aircraft tomorrow morning. Weather holding." A pause. "You haven't slept."

"I'll sleep when the range is behind me."

"You said that at the threshold. Day one."

"I say a lot of things on mountains. It's the only place I'm honest."

She almost laughed, then didn't. "What are you going to do with them? The logs."

I looked west. The Escarpment stood above the tree line in the morning light, enormous and indifferent, wearing its storm like a scarf. Somewhere on that face was a cache with a silenced radio in it, and two graves the mountain had kept for ten years, and a truth that had cost more to carry than to learn.

"Not sell them," I said. "Not bury them. And not hand them to a lawyer, a journalist, or a Merrow." I glanced at her. "Sorry."

"Don't apologize. It's the most sensible thing anyone's said in weeks." She wrapped her arms against the cold. "Then what?"

"There's a version of this where I walk into the inquiry board and clear my name in front of cameras, and it feels wonderful for about a day, and then I'm *the Kestrel survivor* for the rest of my life, and every client I ever guide gets booked by people who want the story instead of the summit." I tested a coil of rope that didn't need testing. "I spent ten years learning to be invisible. Turns out I'm good at it. Maybe the records just... surface. Anonymously. Let the documents testify. Documents don't get tired, and they don't want credit."

"And you?"

"Me?" I looked down the valley, east, toward ordinary ground, toward spring, toward the improbable fact of eleven people alive who had no reason to trust me and did anyway. "I was thinking about a small trip. Low stakes. Open permits. Good company."

Cass studied me for a long moment, and whatever she saw, she had the grace not to name it.

"When the inquiry comes," she said, "they'll want a source. Someone will figure it out. Someone always does."

"Then someone will," I said. "But it won't be today, and it won't be me, and tonight—for the first time in ten years—nobody is looking for me at all."

I said it and then heard it, really heard it, and had to stand very still for a moment with my hand flat on a boulder like I was taking its pulse. No warrant. No blacklist flag. No recovery team on my frequency, no client filing reports, no name in anyone's file worth chasing. Just tired bones, a borrowed stove, and a camp full of people arguing amiably about who got the last of the coffee.

Freedom, it turns out, doesn't feel like flying. It feels like putting down a pack you'd forgotten you were wearing.

It lasted until Priya came out of the tent holding the radio handset, her face doing something complicated.

"Wren," she said. "Miller Flats is on the line. There's a problem with the passenger manifest." She swallowed. "They're asking for you by name. And they're saying the inquiry board convenes Thursday—and someone's already given them yours."
