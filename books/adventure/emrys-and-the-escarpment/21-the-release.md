## 21. The Release

The news broke on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays are the day the world does its accounting—rent due, permits filed, the small bureaucratic machinery grinding through its weekly rotation. If you're going to detonate a decade of corporate cover-up, you might as well do it when the clerks are already at their desks.

I heard about it the way I hear about most things now: late, secondhand, and over a bad cup of coffee. Odile had the radio going in her clinic's back room while she restocked gauze, and Priya was cross-legged on the floor with a laptop older than some of my clients, refreshing a feed she pretended not to care about.

"Merrow Concessions," Priya said, without looking up. "Suspended. All of them. Pending inquiry."

Odile didn't stop wrapping gauze. "Say the whole thing."

"'Pending formal inquiry into the Kestrel Expedition incident of ten years prior, following the public release of survey records, expedition logs, and internal correspondence.'" Priya read it flat, like a weather report. Then she looked up at me. "That's us. That's your handwriting in those logbooks, Wren. The copies we made."

"I have distinctive handwriting," I said. "Very slanted. Guilty-looking."

Nobody laughed, but nobody was supposed to yet. It was that kind of morning—the kind where something enormous has happened and everyone is standing very still around it, afraid that moving will scare it off.

The records had surfaced over the previous seventy-two hours, seeded across four continents through channels that would take investigators months to untangle, if they ever bothered. Scanned logbooks. Radio transcripts. A concession map annotated in a dead man's hand showing where the retreat line had been cut—not lost, not storm-damaged, *cut*, with a maintenance order signed three days before the climb. Internal memos weighing the cost of a rescue against the cost of a scandal. And at the bottom of it all, like a signature on a confession: the original Kestrel radio, photographed in its cache at Kestrel Col, functional, silenced by someone who had decided two climbers were more expendable than a permit.

The photographs were mine. Everything else had been laundered through enough relays to make Priya proud.

"You know what happens next," Priya said. "There'll be an investigation into who released it."

"There always is." I drank my coffee. It tasted like burnt pine needles, which meant Odile had made it, which meant I should have made it. "Investigations into leaks are how organizations admit the leak mattered."

"And if they trace the scans? The geotags, the metadata—"

"Tomas scrubbed it twice. You scrubbed it once more after he did because you didn't trust him, which was correct." I set the cup down. "They'll find nothing. That's rather the point of doing it properly."

Odile finally sat down across from me, elbows on the table, and studied me the way she'd studied my frostbitten fingertips on the descent—clinically, patiently, waiting for me to tell the truth about how bad it was.

"How does it feel?" she asked.

I considered lying, considered deflecting, considered one of my better jokes. Instead I said: "Lighter. Which worries me. Light things blow away."

* * *

For the first week after the news broke, I did what I do best: I disappeared.

Not dramatically. No bus stations, no burned bridges. I just went back to work—a trail-clearing contract up in the eastern foothills, low stakes, honest labor, the kind of job where nobody asks a guide named Wren Sandoval for anything because as far as the world knew, Wren Sandoval was still blacklisted, disgraced, and finished. The irony was thick enough to spread on bread. Ten years of exile had taught me invisibility so thoroughly that even vindication couldn't find me unless I let it.

The foothills work was simple. Blowdown removal, drainage repair, re-benching a section of tread that spring melt had chewed to soup. My hands knew it. My body settled into it like water finding level. There's a particular peace in swinging a mattock from dawn until your shoulders quit complaining and start negotiating, and I took full advantage, because I could feel the other thing circling—the interviews, the inquiries, the inevitable moment when someone with a press badge connected an anonymous leak to the only crew that had crossed the Escarpment before the concessions lapsed.

On the fourth day, a supply runner brought word up the trail along with my resupply: the inquiry had gone public. Hearings scheduled. Three Merrow executives had resigned; a fourth had retained counsel described in the coverage as "aggressive." The recovery team's survivors—all seven of them, including the leader who'd once known my name and reputation and now owed me his life—had submitted corroborating statements. Uniform, detailed, and damning.

And there was a letter, the runner said, left at the trailhead office. For me.

I opened it sitting on a stump with sawdust in my hair. Cass's handwriting, still sharp, still impatient, the letters of a woman who wrote like she climbed—fast, committed, no rest points.

*Wren.*

*You won't answer my calls, so I'm writing this the old way, which I suppose is fitting.*

*The hearings start next month. They'll want testimony. I've given them everything except you—I've kept your name out of it as long as I can, but the timeline makes our traverse obvious, and obvious people get subpoenaed. When it comes, go quietly. Say less than you want to. Trust me on this; I've spent my whole life watching people say exactly what they wanted to in rooms like that, and it never once helped them.*

*I also want you to know what I've done with the company. What's left of it. The concessions are dead, the extraction leases are frozen, and the name Merrow is worth approximately nothing, which is the most honest valuation it's ever had. But the guiding side—the permits for the open ranges, the training programs, the equipment—none of that was part of the rot. I'm rebuilding it. Small. Clean. And I want you to run the mountain program.*

*Before you refuse—and you're going to refuse, I can feel it from here—consider that I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to consider that the best revenge on people who weaponize mountains is teaching other people to love them honestly. You'd be good at it. You're the best I ever saw, and I've watched you be the best since we were nineteen and you out-read me on a slope I'd been climbing since childhood.*

*Come build something. Or don't, and keep clearing trails under other people's names, and be free that way instead. Both are answers. Only one of them gets you back above treeline where you belong.*

*Cass*

I read it twice. Then I folded it into my jacket pocket, picked up the mattock, and worked until dark, because that's what I do with feelings too large to carry—they get set down and picked up later, when the light is better.

She wasn't wrong about any of it. That was the problem. Cass Merrow had spent her whole life being half-right in ways that cost other people everything, and I'd learned, at considerable expense, to tell the difference between her sincerity and her strategy. Usually they arrived wearing each other's clothes.

But the offer itself—that was real. I could feel its weight, the same way you feel the weight of a rope you haven't clipped into yet.

* * *

Priya found me at the end of the week. She didn't call ahead; she just appeared at my campsite at dusk with her own tent and a bag of groceries, on the theory—which she announced immediately—that I was constitutionally incapable of feeding myself properly when left alone.

"The hearings are a circus," she reported, handing me onions like tribute. "Cass testified yesterday. Six hours. She gave them the whole architecture—the cover-up, the maintenance order, her father's signature. She named herself as complicit in the concealment. Voluntarily."

I stopped chopping. "Voluntarily."

"She waived protection. Said, and I quote, 'I grew up inside the lie; you may as well have the tour.'" Priya shrugged. "Her lawyers are reportedly inconsolable. One of them was seen drinking from a flask at nine in the morning, which frankly feels earned."

"What does she get out of it?"

"That's the thing." Priya crouched by the fire, poking it with a stick in a way that suggested she had opinions about fire management and was choosing restraint. "Nothing. Strategically, it's ruinous. She's exposing herself to criminal liability to burn down whatever's left of her family's name. Tomas thinks it's penance. Odile thinks it's control—she'd rather destroy it on her terms than watch it get dismantled. I think—" She paused. "I think she told you once that she hired you to find proof. Maybe proving it was only half of it. Maybe the other half was deciding what to do once it was found, and she needed ten years and one winter on the Escarpment to figure out that the answer was *all of it*. Every document. Even the ones that hang her."

I thought about the letter in my pocket. *Both are answers.*

"She offered me the mountain program," I said.

Priya's eyebrows went up. "And?"

"And nothing. I haven't answered."

"You're going to say no."

"I'm going to say *not yet*," I corrected. "There's a difference. No is a wall. Not yet is a door I'm leaving unlocked while I decide whether I like what's on the other side."

She considered this with the seriousness she gave all things, including snack selection. "What's on the other side?"

"Honestly? Her." I gestured with the knife. "A company with Merrow money in its foundations. Good intentions stacked on top of bad history, like a hut built on a moraine—you can live in it, but you'll always hear the ice shifting underneath. I spent ten years learning what it costs to be somebody else's convenient story. I'm not eager to become somebody else's redemption arc."

"And if it's not that?" Priya asked quietly. "If it's actually just... a clean thing, built by a broken person, and she's offering you the part that isn't broken?"

I didn't have an answer for that. So I handed her the cutting board and told her to make herself useful.

* * *

The subpoena came eleven days later, delivered by a process server who hiked four miles to find me and was visibly regretting his career choices by the time he reached my camp. I signed for it, offered him water, and watched him limp back down the trail with the expression of a man who would be telling this story badly at parties for years.

The hearing itself was anticlimactic, which is the highest compliment I can pay any bureaucracy. A room in the provincial capital, wood-paneled, over-warm. Lawyers in clusters. Cass at the witness table looking like a woman who had finally put down something heavy and was discovering how much lighter her spine felt. When my turn came, I told the truth in the flattest possible language: I had been hired to guide a traverse; I had found a station; I had found logbooks; I had copied certain documents; I had no knowledge of their subsequent distribution. Every word true. Every word useless. The investigators understood perfectly well what had happened, and I understood perfectly well that they understood, and we all agreed silently to respect the craft involved.

One of the junior counsel caught me in the corridor afterward. Young, earnest, the kind of person who still believed institutions could be repaired from inside.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the release changed everything. Without those records, the concessions would have converted quietly and the Kestrel families would have gotten nothing. Now there are three criminal referrals and the extraction leases are frozen indefinitely." She hesitated. "Whoever did it did the right thing."

"Whoever did it," I agreed, "sounds like someone with excellent handwriting."

She almost smiled. Then: "There's talk of recognizing the traverse crew. Publicly. Search-and-rescue wants to study Wren's icefield route; the mountaineering council is talking about reinstatement. Your name's going to come back, whether you want it or not."

I stood in that warm corridor and felt the old reflex rise—the flinch, the calculation of exits, the decade-deep habit of *stay invisible, stay alive*—and then, slowly, it settled. Because the thing underneath the fear had changed. Ten years ago, my name coming back meant the blame coming back. Now it meant something else entirely, and I was going to have to learn to stand in the open with it, the way you learn to stand in wind again after a long time indoors.

"Tell them thank you," I said. "And tell them I'll come down from the hills when I'm ready."

* * *

I went to see Cass in early spring, before the thaw finished, before anything was decided. Her office—if you could call it that—was a rented room above a gear shop in the valley town, stripped down to a desk, two chairs, and a topographic map of the open ranges pinned to the wall with actual pins, because apparently some Merrows resisted digitization on principle.

She stood when I came in. We looked at each other across ten years and one winter and everything either of us could say, and then she said, "You look terrible," and I said, "You look worse," and that was the whole ceremony. Some friendships—or whatever ours was, that genus-defying organism—are built entirely on insults functioning as greetings.

"I got your letter," I said.

"I know. You didn't answer."

"I'm answering now." I sat down uninvited, which she deserved, and pulled the letter from my jacket, creased soft from rereading. "Here's my answer. Not yet."

Something flickered across her face—hurt, quickly mastered, and beneath it, grudging respect, because Cass had always known exactly what she was dealing with in me, even when she was using it.

"Not yet," she repeated.

"Not yet. Here's why." I leaned forward. "If I take that program now, I take it from you. From Merrow money, Merrow guilt, Merrow need to fix something fast. And every good thing we built would sit on top of the question of whether you hired me to guide a traverse or to absolve you. I'd never know. You'd never know. And that uncertainty would rot the foundation, same as the last rot did."

"And later?"

"Later, when the hearings are done and the name Merrow means whatever it's going to mean—later, if the offer still stands, make it again. Not as penance. Not as partnership-with-a-conscience. As two people who survived the same mountain building a thing that belongs to neither of us." I held her eyes. "Can you wait that long?"

Cass was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the thaw dripped off the eaves in a slow irregular rhythm, like the building counting something.

"I waited ten years to hire you," she said finally. "I can wait six months to be refused twice." Then, wryly: "You realize this is the most generous rejection I've ever received."

"It's not a rejection. It's a reservation."

"Same thing, in your handwriting." She almost smiled. Almost. "Where will you be?"

"Around," I said. "Above treeline somewhere. Doing it honestly."

* * *

I walked out of the valley the next morning with a pack I hadn't fully unpacked since the Escarpment, because apparently some part of me had known I wouldn't be staying anywhere long. The foothills trail took me up through bare alder and old snow holding on in the shadows, and by midday I was above the last fence, on open ground, under a sky doing its enormous indifferent blue thing.

My radio crackled. Priya, checking in on schedule, because Priya checked in on schedule the way glaciers calved—inevitably and on geological principle.

"Any word?" she asked.

"They posted the hearing schedule. Criminal referrals proceeding. Extraction leases frozen through the inquiry." I paused at the ridgeline, looking north toward the Escarpment, a white line on the horizon like a scar that had finally stopped aching. "And the council sent confirmation. Reinstatement. Full certification, retroactive clearance of the blacklist. It's official. I'm employable."

"Congratulations. How does it feel?"

I thought about it honestly, the way Odile had taught me to answer that question—no jokes first, truth first, jokes after if they fit.

"Like taking off a pack after a long carry," I said. "The shoulders don't believe it yet. They keep bracing for weight that isn't there. But it's not there. It's actually not there."

"And the company? Cass?"

"Reserved. Not accepted." I started walking, easy rhythm, the trail rising gently toward a pass I'd been meaning to look at for years. "Some things you don't take back the moment they're offered. Some things you let prove themselves."

"Spoken like a woman who's about to be very busy this summer," Priya said, and I could hear the smile in it. "Because Odile says if you're reinstated, she's in. Whatever you build. And I'm in, obviously, someone has to run comms before you kill yourself on some unmapped—"

"Priya."

"—and Tomas sent a message, he says he's done selling data forever, he wants to do *field science*, ethical field science, and he knows that sounds like an oxymoron but—"

"*Priya.*"

Silence. Wind. Somewhere below, meltwater starting its long journey downhill.

"We're not building anything yet," I said. "But ask them to be ready by June."

The radio clicked off, and I climbed.
