## 22. Homecoming

The trail out of Miller's Hollow doesn't have a name. It has a condition: mud. Six weeks of spring melt had turned the lower switchbacks into a brown river with delusions of being a path, and by the third hour my boots had achieved the consistency and moral standing of wet bread.

"Your famous route-finding," Odile said behind me, "is it always this glamorous?"

"This is the glamour part," I said. "Wait until lunch."

I was guiding again. That sentence still sat strangely in my mouth, like a word in a language I'd studied but never spoken aloud. Not because I'd forgotten how—my hands remembered everything, the rope coils, the way you read a client's breathing two switchbacks back to know who'd struggle at the col—but because for ten years I'd been someone who couldn't say it. *Guide.* The word had been taken from me the way they take a tooth: quickly, with anesthetic that wears off later.

Now I was leading four clients up a hill that couldn't kill anyone if it tried. A retired schoolteacher named Bess who kept misidentifying every bird as "some kind of hawk." Her nephew Dev, twenty-three, here because his therapist said mountains. A quiet couple from the coast, Hal and June, who held hands on scree like it was a dance floor. Open ground, permitted ground, honest ground. The kind of trip I used to take before Kestrel, before the blacklist, before any of it.

Odile walked sweep. Priya ranged ahead with the radio, ostensibly checking weather, actually enjoying the fact that nobody was shooting at us.

* *

You should understand what spring means after a winter like ours.

The Escarpment traverse ended in February, eleven days after we crossed the boundary marker, when we came down off the ice hauling three injured strangers from a corporate recovery helicopter and one set of logbooks that weighed less than a sandwich and more than a decade. We crossed out of the concession nine hours after the permits lapsed. Nobody stopped us. There was nobody left to stop us—the recovery team's survivors signed statements from hospital beds in Verrall, and their leader, a courteous man named Anselm whose name I will apparently be hearing in depositions until I die, corroborated everything before his lawyer could finish coughing.

The records went public in April. Anonymous channels, Priya's specialty, though she denies it with the specific vigor of the guilty. Concessions revoked pending inquiry. The Kestrel Expedition entered the public record not as my failure but as what it always was: a company decision dressed as weather. Two climbers, Sefu Adeyemi and Lena Brandt, cut off above the wall so their findings would die with them. The radio in the cache at Kestrel Col, never broken. Deliberately silenced. I had carried the weight of that broken radio for ten years, and it turned out it had never been broken at all. It had been *kept*. Somewhere under all the grief there was room for anger, but honestly, mostly what I felt at the col was something closer to vertigo—the ground of my whole life shifting under me, and me discovering I could stand on the new ground just fine.

Cass Merrow did not come home with us. She stayed at the wreck site through the storm with the recovery team's wounded, holding the position until relief came, and then she surrendered the logs to me without conditions, which is the closest thing to an apology her family has produced in three generations. She faces questions now. Inquiries. Possibly charges; possibly nothing, which would be its own verdict. She wrote to me once, a letter I haven't answered, offering—of all things—a partnership. A guiding company rebuilt on honest ground. *For now,* I told her, which is more than she probably deserves and less than she maybe earned. For now.

Tomas took the mineral data to journalists instead of buyers, which surprised everyone including Tomas. He says it was the moulin that changed him. Nearly going into the earth does something to a man's spreadsheet priorities. I believe him about sixty percent, which for Tomas is a strong showing.

And me? I went back to work. Small trips. Open trails. Clients who sign waivers written in plain language and mean them.

The wilderness forgives nothing—I want that on the record, because everyone keeps telling me I've been given a second chance, as if the mountain had opinions and had revised them. The mountain has no memory and no mercy and no interest in my rehabilitation. The cornice that nearly took Odile last winter would take her just as happily this spring. What changed wasn't the wilderness. What changed was that I stopped asking it for forgiveness, which had always been a stupid thing to ask of a landform, and started asking people for company, which turns out to be much easier to get.

* *

We made the high meadow by early afternoon, where the snowline still held in white ribbons down the north faces and the first glacier lilies were pushing up through the melt like small yellow insurrections. I called the lunch stop, and Bess immediately identified a Clark's nutcracker as "some kind of hawk," and Dev laughed for the first time in two days, and I felt the particular satisfaction that is the entire salary of this job: people arriving somewhere they didn't think they could get to.

Priya dropped beside me on the rock, unwrapping a sandwich with surgical intent.

"Weather holds till tomorrow evening," she said. "Then a front off the coast. Nothing dramatic."

"Nothing dramatic," I repeated. "Say that slower. Savor it."

"I'm allowed to enjoy a boring forecast. I've earned boring forecasts." She chewed. Watched the clients. Bess was showing Hal how to skip a stone across a tarn, badly. "You know what I keep thinking about? The station. The geothermal tap. Somebody built that place to run forever, and nobody ever came back. All those years, the lights on, the logs waiting."

"That's stations for you."

"It's us, too, though. Isn't it?" She said it lightly, but Priya doesn't say anything lightly; she just wraps heavy things in light paper. "We were running on old power for a long time. You especially. And somebody finally climbed up and opened the logs."

I looked at her. Ten years of exile, and the person who finally read my record straight through wasn't a review board or a lawyer. It was a radio tech eating a sandwich on a rock, who'd decoded permit law in a tent during a whiteout and then spent three days listening to me and Cass argue about the past and never once told either of us to shut up, though we'd both earned it.

"You're getting philosophical," I said. "Altitude?"

"Guilt, probably. I still feel bad about the garbled transmission. Three days I could've given you warning."

"Priya. If you'd caught it earlier, we'd have run earlier, and we'd have missed the cache at the col. The storm put us exactly where we needed to be pinned." I shrugged. "That's not forgiveness. That's just arithmetic. The mountain forgives nothing. But sometimes it deals you the right cards for terrible reasons."

She bumped her shoulder against mine, which from Priya is a sonnet, and went to tell Bess that no, that was definitely not a hawk.

* *

Odile found me at the col.

Not a dramatic col—a gentle one, a saddle between two forested humps with a view down the valley and, far to the north, a line of white that might have been the Escarpment or might have been cloud. I was pretending to check the route while actually looking at it. Old habit. You don't stop reading a range just because you've been banned from its best chapters.

"There it is," she said, coming up beside me. No greeting; Odile considers greetings redundant between people who've shared a crevasse lip.

"Might be."

"It's not. I've got the bearing memorized." She did, of course. Odile memorizes things the way other people breathe. "How does it look from here?"

"Small."

"Liar."

"Small," I insisted, "and very far away, and entirely someone else's problem until the inquiry lifts the access moratorium, at which point it'll be everyone's problem, and I will guide it again or I won't, and either way—" I stopped. She waited. Odile is very good at waiting; it's half her profession. "Either way I don't need it to say yes to me anymore. That's the whole thing. That's the entire discovery of my fifty-first year. The mountain was never going to absolve me. I was waiting on a verdict from a jury of granite."

"And now?"

"Now the verdict's in from the only court that mattered." I nodded back down the trail, where our four clients were strung out below us, laughing about something, Dev carrying Bess's pack without being asked. "They walked out with me. That's the whole judgment. Case closed."

Odile was quiet a moment. Then: "Cass wrote again. Second letter. She showed me—well, she told me. Same offer. Company, permits, her name off the door entirely if I want."

"What did you tell her?"

"Told her I'd think about it." She smiled, thin and real. "Which means I'll think about it for about six months and then tell her no, unless you say otherwise, which you won't, because you're constitutionally incapable of telling anyone what to do outside of an emergency."

"I give excellent non-emergency advice."

"You gave Bess advice about her divorce for forty minutes yesterday. Unprompted."

"She asked what kind of hawk that was. It evolved."

Odile laughed—that short, startled bark she pretends isn't a laugh—and we stood together at the top of the world's most modest pass, looking north at the white line that had taken everything from me and, in the end, given back more than it took, though I'd never say that within earshot of it. Mountains are vain enough already.

* *

We camped at the lake. Honest camp, legal camp, fire ring already established by some previous generation of travelers, and I taught Dev and Hal the difference between a deadfall and a live snag while June and Bess argued amiably about whether the sunset was pink or "pink-adjacent."

After dark, when the clients were zipped into their bags and the fire had burned down to a red argument among the coals, Odile and Priya and I sat up with the last of the tea. This had become a ritual on every trip since February. We didn't call it anything. Rituals you name tend to die of self-consciousness.

"Do you remember," Priya said, "in the whiteout, day two, when Wren said the storm was 'character-building'?"

"I remember," Odile said, "Wren saying a lot of things in that whiteout that I've chosen to forgive."

"The tents were trying to leave. Collectively. As a group decision."

"The tents showed poor judgment and were counseled accordingly."

I let them have it. This was the shape of the thing now: not the mountain, not the vindication, not even the truth, though the truth was good and I wouldn't trade it. The shape was this—two people who knew exactly what I'd done and hadn't done, what had been done to me, what I'd been willing to burn and what I'd refused to burn, sitting with me in the cold drinking bad tea and reciting my worst material back at me like scripture. Belonging, it turned out, wasn't a place cleared of the past. Every place is full of the past; that's what places are made of. Belonging was people who carried it with you, who knew where all the bodies and the logbooks and the broken-not-broken radios were buried, and chose the trail anyway.

"Cass called me today," Priya said suddenly. "Before we lost signal."

The fire popped. Neither of them looked at me, which meant they'd discussed this.

"And?" I said.

"Inquiry's wrapping up. Anselm's cooperating fully—apparently almost enjoying it, which tells you what he thinks of his former employers. Charges likely against the concession board, maybe not against Cass. And the moratorium on the high routes lifts in the fall. Provisional permits. First come." She paused. "She wants us to put in for the Escarpment traverse. Guided. Legal. Ours, if we want it. She says—" Priya's voice did something careful around the next words— "she says she won't come. She says the route should belong to the people who walked out."

I looked into the coals for a long time. North, past the black trees, the Escarpment was somewhere under the stars doing what it always did: keeping what it keeps, forgiving nothing, needing nothing from me.

"Fall's a long way off," I said finally.

"That's not a no."

"That's not a no." I wrapped my hands around the cooling cup. "Ask me again at the trailhead tomorrow. Ask me somewhere with mud in it. Decisions made at altitude don't count—they're just the altitude talking."

Odile snorted. "You're going to say yes."

"I'm going to sleep on it," I said, "on open ground, legally, with witnesses, which is more than I ever managed before, and tomorrow I'm going to walk four civilians safely down a muddy hill, and that—" I tipped my cup toward the dark, toward the white line in the north, toward the whole indifferent magnificent world— "that's the job. That was always the job. Everything else was weather."

Priya smiled and banked the fire. Odile headed for her tent, pausing at the flap.

"For the record," she said, "it's a yes. We already voted."

"You can't vote on my—"

"Two to one. Goodnight, Wren."

I sat alone by the embers a while longer, laughing quietly at absolutely nothing, while somewhere north of us the Escarpment held its snow and its secrets and its long memory of everyone who ever crossed it—and for the first time in ten years, I fell asleep on a mountain without asking it for a single thing.
