## 1. Debt of Clear Skies

The trick to landing a plane you can't afford to land is to do it like you mean it.

Felix Fairweather brought the *Kestrel* down through the last gray rags of a dying squall line, wheels touching Port Calloway's crushed-coral strip with a chirp so clean it would have made her old instructor weep. Not that anyone was watching. The airfield was one windsock, two fuel drums, and a customs shed whose roof had been patched with tin that sang in the offshore breeze like a man humming to keep himself brave.

She rolled out long, let the tail come down gentle, and taxied straight past the shed to the far corner of the field, where a stand of salt-burned casuarinas threw shade over a patch of grass gone brown at the edges. There she killed the engine and sat for a moment in the sudden quiet, hands still on the yoke, listening to the airframe tick as it cooled.

"Never land twice," she said to the windshield. It was the closest thing she had to a prayer.

The rule was simple, and she'd built three years of her life on top of it: no field twice, no harbor twice, no face twice. Keep moving ahead of the arithmetic. Somewhere behind her, scattered across four hundred miles of coast like bad weather, were eleven creditors who each believed—with reason—that Felix Fairweather owed them money she did not have. Eleven men and women with ledgers, and worse, eleven men and women with friends. The Vane Syndicate held six of those ledgers now, having bought them cheap from smaller lenders the way gulls buy fish from fishermen, and the Syndicate's collectors had a reputation for being unfailingly polite right up until they weren't.

She climbed out into heat that hit her like a wet towel. The *Kestrel* was a twenty-year-old Fairchild utility bird, high-wing, honest, ugly, its paint faded to the color of weak tea, its port-side strut wrapped in enough safety wire to knit a sweater. She loved it the way you love a dog that's bitten three people but never you. It had carried her through two seasons of cloud-seeding contracts, a year of flying mail up the Kessari delta when the boats wouldn't run, and one memorable afternoon hauling a prize bull that had opinions about turbulence. It had never once left her stranded.

It had also never once made her money. That part was on her.

Felix pulled the cover over the canopy, wedged a chock under the wheel with her boot, and walked toward the customs shed with her collar up and her cap low, moving like a woman with somewhere boring to be. The shed's interior smelled of kerosene, mildew, and the particular staleness of paperwork nobody had filed since the last monsoon. A fan turned overhead with the enthusiasm of a dying thing. Behind the counter, a customs officer looked up from a radio he'd been fiddling with, took in her flight jacket, her sun-cracked knuckles, the oil stain on her sleeve, and visibly decided she wasn't worth standing up for.

"Fairweather," she said, sliding her logbook across the counter. "Transit. Fuel if you've got it. Overnight, maybe two."

He stamped without reading. That was the other half of the rule: be forgettable. Be the kind of pilot customs officers stamp without reading. She paid for fuel in cash counted out slow, from an envelope that got noticeably thinner doing it, and asked, casual as anything, whether there was work going. Survey flights, mail runs, anything.

"Calloway's dead this time of year," the officer said. "Monsoon season. Nobody flies into the Kessari headwaters but fools and scientists." He said it like the two categories were adjacent rather than identical, which, in Felix's experience, they were.

"Fools pay better," she said, and he almost smiled.

* * *

The boarding house was called the Meridian, which was optimistic, since the only meridian it occupied was the line between damp and soaked. Felix took the room at the back, third floor, facing the drainage canal—a room chosen specifically because its window overlooked nothing worth guarding and its stairs had a back exit onto an alley that smelled of fish heads and diesel. She'd learned to read buildings the way she read clouds: not for what they were, but for what they were about to do.

She showered off three days of cockpit sweat, ate a plate of rice and smoked fish that tasted mostly of smoke, and sat on the bed with the ledger.

Not a real ledger. A page in her logbook's back flap, where she kept the arithmetic she couldn't afford to look at too often. Eleven debts. She added them the way you touch a bruise—quickly, wincing, to confirm it still hurts. The total hadn't changed since Tavira, which meant interest was quietly eating the edges of it like tide eating a sandbar. Two thousand, nine hundred and forty. Give or take the give-or-take.

Against that: forty-one in cash, a quarter tank of fuel, and a plane worth maybe eight hundred if she sold it, which she would do roughly never.

Outside, the first real rain of the evening came down the canal in slanting sheets, drumming the tin roof of the neighbor's cookhouse, turning the world beyond the window into watercolor. Monsoon season. Six weeks of it, minimum. Every honest pilot on the coast was grounded or gone south, chasing dry-season work, and every dishonest one was—

Someone knocked on her door.

Two knocks. Even, unhurried, friendly. Nobody friendly knocked on Felix Fairweather's door. Friendly people waited until morning; only collectors and constables worked after dark, and constables announced themselves.

She was off the bed and silent before the second knock finished, heart going hard and flat, eyes finding the window. Third floor. Drainage canal below—not a drop, a plunge, but the roof of the cookhouse was close and its slope was shallow and she'd jumped off worse things, though admittedly not while carrying everything she owned.

"Miss Fairweather," said the voice through the door. Pleasant. Male. The voice of a man who introduced himself at parties. "I know you're awake. The rain's too loud for sleeping, and besides, I watched your light go out about ninety seconds ago, which is very nearly a confession."

Felix stood barefoot on wet floorboards and considered her options, all of which were bad, and then considered the voice, which was wrong. Collectors didn't talk like that. Collectors talked like men reciting numbers at you through a door because the door was the last thing between you and the numbers.

"Who is it?"

"Bright," the voice said. "Juniper Bright. The Vane Syndicate sends its regards, and also its optimism." A pause, and then, cheerfully: "That's my job title. Optimist. I'm told it confuses people, which I've decided to take as a compliment."

"You're early," Felix said, because her mouth had always moved faster than her judgment. "I'm not due in Calloway for another—" she checked her mental math, hated the answer, "—six hours."

"I know. I caught the fast boat." He sounded delighted about it, as though beating her to port had been a game they were both playing and he'd won fair. "May I come in? Or we can do this through the door, but I'll warn you, I have a document with me and reading documents through doors gives me a headache."

Every instinct she had said *window, cookhouse roof, alley, fly at dawn*. But the window meant running, and running meant the pattern continuing forever, eleven debts becoming twelve, becoming twenty, the arithmetic compounding in the dark behind her like weather building on a horizon. And something about the voice—the genuine, unweaponized good humor of it—made her want, just once, to hear the whole sentence before she started sprinting.

She opened the door.

The man in the hallway was perhaps thirty-five, thin as a heron, dressed in a linen suit that had been white once and was now the color of old bone, rain-spotted to the knee. He had a face like a friendly ferret and hair that the humidity had defeated entirely. Under one arm: a leather folder, dry, protected by an oiled cloth, because of course the Syndicate's paper stayed dry even when the world didn't. He beamed at her.

"There she is," he said. "The famous Felix Fairweather. Do you know, I've read your file? All of it? Twice? You're my favorite account."

"That's the least flattering thing anyone's ever said to me."

"It gets better." He glanced past her at the room, at the rain-blurred window, and made a small apologetic gesture. "May I? Just for a moment. I promise I'm the best-dressed threat you'll encounter this month."

She stepped aside because refusing would have cost more energy than it saved. Bright entered, looked around the room with the frank appraisal of a man pricing furniture, noted the packed bag by the door, the chock of the escape route, and nodded as if she'd confirmed something pleasant.

"You were going to leave tonight," he said. "Or dawn. Fly south along the coast, refuel at Sarnholt, disappear for another season. Am I close?"

"You're a collector. Guessing's your job."

"Optimist," he corrected, settling onto the room's single chair uninvited, folding his long frame into it like a deck chair opening. "And no, guessing is the *collectors'* job, and they're very good at it, which is why I don't do it. I do something much harder." He laid the leather folder on his knee but didn't open it. "I find people who are good at something, and I offer them a chance to be paid for it."

Felix leaned against the wall by the window, arms crossed, keeping the escape route at her back. "I'm good at owing money. If that's the skill, you're late. Already got eleven of those offers."

"You're good at weather." He said it simply, no flattery in it, and somehow that was worse. "Specifically, you're good at monsoon weather, which puts you in a category of approximately nobody. I've spent three weeks asking around the coast about pilots who could fly the Kessari headwaters in season, and your name came up from four separate people, two of whom are terrified of you and one of whom is apparently still angry about a bull."

"The bull was fine."

"The bull sued."

"He lost." Despite herself, despite everything, the corner of her mouth twitched. Bright saw it and his grin widened, patient as a fisherman feeling a take on the line.

"Here's the shape of it," he said, and finally opened the folder, turning it so she could see—not thrusting it at her, just letting it exist in the space between them. "An expedition. Scientific. Seven people, going upriver into the Kessari basin during full monsoon. They need a pilot for weather support—reconnaissance flights, storm tracking, supply runs when the river turns hostile. One season. Contract pays eighteen hundred, plus per-flight bonuses, plus—" and here he tapped the folder, once, lightly, "—upon completion, the Vane Syndicate will consider your existing obligations settled. All of them. Eleven accounts, closed. Clean slate. Fresh sky."

The number landed in her chest and sat there glowing. Eighteen hundred was nearly two-thirds of the debt on its own. Settled meant settled. She'd seen Syndicate settlements honored before—it was the one thing they were scrupulous about, because a lender who welched couldn't lend—and the thought of waking up in spring owing nobody, owing *nothing*, was so foreign it took her a moment to even imagine the shape of it.

Which was exactly why she didn't trust it.

"What's the catch?" she said. "There's always a catch. Nobody pays a debt-ridden pilot hazard rates to babysit scientists out of charity."

"No catch," Bright said. "A risk. Different animal entirely. The expedition's sponsor wants weather support during the worst flying season on the coast, in a river basin where the storms build taller than anything you've seen, and they want it badly enough to pay for it. That's not a catch, Miss Fairweather. That's just Tuesday, for someone with your résumé." He tilted his head. "The catch, if you insist on one, is that you'd have to stop running. Sign a contract, stay in one theater of operations for a season, be findable. I understand that's difficult for you."

The rain thickened against the window. Down in the canal, water ran brown and fast, carrying a dead frangipani branch toward the sea.

"And if I say no?"

Bright shrugged, and for a moment the good humor thinned enough to show the machinery underneath—not menace, exactly, but the calm of a man who knew the tide was working for him. "Then I wish you a pleasant flight south, and the collectors resume their schedule, and in about four months they find you wherever you've landed twice." He stood, brushing off his ruined trousers, and held out the folder. "But you won't say no. Not because of the money. Because I've read your file, and underneath all that debt and all that running, you're a pilot, and pilots hate being grounded more than they hate anything. Even us."

She took the folder. It was heavier than paper should be—contracts, schedules, a manifest, insurance riders, pages of fine print dense as a thundercloud. She felt the weight of it in her hand and understood, with the clarity that sometimes arrives a half-second too late to be useful, that whatever happened next, this was the moment the season turned. Stay or run, sign or burn, the woman who walked into Port Calloway tonight was not the woman who would walk out.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"I don't read the fine print." She flipped the folder shut before he could react. "You heard me. I've learned that fine print is where hope goes to get itemized. So here's how it works: you tell me the truth in plain words, I sign the front page, and whatever's on the back stays between you and your lawyers. Deal?"

Bright stared at her, and then he laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of him, bright as a lightning flash.

"Oh, I *like* you," he said. "That is a terrible idea and I accept completely."

He produced a pen from inside his jacket, because of course he had a pen, and clicked it open, and outside the rain came down harder on the tin roofs of Port Calloway, and Felix Fairweather—who had survived three years by never signing anything, never staying anywhere, never trusting a smiling man with a dry folder—took the pen, turned to the signature line, and began, deliberately, not to read.
