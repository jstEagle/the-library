## 2. The Manifest

The contract arrived at dawn, delivered by a boy who couldn't have been more than fourteen, who knocked on my door at the Hotel Delmar with the particular knock of someone who has been paid not to care what's behind it.

I took the envelope, tipped him a coin that was probably worth more than his week's wages, and watched him go down the corridor whistling. Then I stood there with the envelope in my hand for a long moment, feeling its weight. Contracts from the Vane Syndicate had weight. Everything they touched did—money, paper, people. They were cheerful about it, too, which was the worst part. Most lenders wanted you to feel the knife. The Syndicate smiled while they slid it in, and thanked you for holding still.

I'd met Bright again the evening before, in the back room of a tea house that smelled of cardamom and mildew, and he'd laid it out the way he always laid things out: like a man showing you a map of a country you'd already decided to move to.

"One season," he'd said, tapping the table with one finger. "Weather support for a scientific expedition going up the Kessari. You fly, you read the sky, you keep them alive. The Syndicate holds the insurance interest on their equipment, which means we hold your contract, which means when you're done—" he'd spread his hands, delighted, "—the arithmetic works out. Clean slate, Felix. Imagine it."

I could imagine it. That was the problem. I could imagine it so vividly it hurt—waking up in a bed I owned, in a town where nobody knew my name or my balance, with a plane whose liens belonged to me alone. I could imagine it the way a drowning woman imagines air.

So now I sat on the sagging bed of the Hotel Delmar with the envelope open and the morning light coming through shutters that didn't quite meet, and I made myself do the thing I'd promised myself I wouldn't do.

I read it.

Not all of it. Not the parts that mattered. But I read the manifest first—the list of souls I'd be responsible for keeping breathing—and that part, at least, I read like a pilot reads weather. Carefully. Twice.

* * *

There were seven names.

Dr. Marcus Anselm, expedition lead. Geophysicist, per the attached summary, though the summary had the flat, over-rehearsed tone of something written by a committee. *Atmospheric anomaly survey, upper Kessari basin.* Anselm had published papers I half-remembered skimming in a waiting room somewhere years ago—something about standing wave systems, about storms that behaved less like weather and more like architecture. The kind of scientist who got funding because he was right too often to ignore and wrong too politely to punish.

Six scientists under him, plus a doctor folded into the count. Dr. Priya Nair, physician and field researcher, listed with credentials long enough to wrap around the page twice. A hydrologist named Okafor. Two atmospheric techs, brothers by the sound of their surname—Kestrel, which no one is born with, so either adopted or invented, and I liked them already for whichever it was. A cartographer, Mireles, whose job description included the phrase *ground-truthing of unverified tributaries*, which meant the maps upriver were lies and everyone involved knew it.

And then, last on the list, with a line item all her own:

*Ilsa Voss — botanist / field naturalist. Route authority.*

Route authority. I turned the page back and looked at that phrase for a while. On an expedition roster, that title usually went to a guide—a local man with river mud in his veins, someone who'd run the Kessari since before he could swim. It did not go to botanists. Botanists knew plants. Plants didn't tell you where the sandbars had moved after the spring flood, or which channel ate barges in the wet season, or where the river narrowed into water that moved faster than it looked.

But the more I read of Voss's file, the less sense she made as a botanist and the more sense she made as something else. Her publications were sparse and strange—monographs on riparian fungi, on seed dispersal patterns in flood forests, on the root architecture of trees that grew in places trees shouldn't grow. Buried in the citations, though, were references to her as a consultant on river navigation for two previous surveys, both of which had come back incomplete, and one of which had come back short a man.

Her birthplace was listed as a settlement that hadn't appeared on any map printed in the last forty years. I checked. I actually got up, dug through my flight bag for the battered river atlas I kept for reasons I told myself were professional rather than sentimental, and checked. The place wasn't there. The atlas showed a bend in the river and nothing else, the cartographer's equivalent of a shrug.

So this woman came from nowhere, knew the river better than the maps did, and had been given authority over the route by a committee of scientists who presumably trusted her because she kept being right.

I set the manifest down and looked out the shutter gap at Port Calloway waking up below—fishermen mending nets in the gray light, the harbor cranes swinging their slow arcs, the smell of diesel and brine coming up through the floorboards. Somewhere across town, a debt collector named Hobb was drinking coffee and checking his list, and my name was on it, and my plane was tied down at the municipal strip with a lien on it like an anchor.

One season. Seven strangers. A river full of unverified tributaries and a guide from a ghost town.

It should have been enough to make me walk away. I want to be honest about that—I've had time to be honest about most things now, and the truth is that every instinct I owned stood up that morning and voted no. The Kessari in monsoon season kills pilots with better planes than mine and worse debts. The Syndicate doesn't offer clean slates; it offers longer ropes. And a botanist who outranks the maps is either a miracle or a warning, and the river has never once in recorded history produced a miracle.

But here is what I've learned about desperation, and I learned it early and thoroughly: it isn't a feeling. It's a discipline. It's the practiced art of not looking at the things that would stop you if you looked. Every pilot who ever flew into weather she shouldn't have flew into it by degrees—first ignoring the buildups on the horizon, then the green tint in the light, then the radio chatter about hail, until the only thing left to look at was the wall itself, and by then looking was beside the point.

Willful blindness is a survival strategy. Nobody tells you that. They tell you ignorance is bliss, which is a lie told by comfortable people. What ignorance actually is, when you do it deliberately, when you choose it the way you choose a heading—it's armor. You don't read the fine print because the fine print can't hurt you if you never know it's there. You sign the contract because the version of the deal you allow yourself to see is the only one you can survive seeing.

I picked the contract back up.

* * *

The document itself was beautiful, which should have been my second warning after Bright's smile. Real paper, heavy stock, the Syndicate's seal embossed in silver at the top—a stylized wind vane, naturally, pointing in every direction at once. Their lawyers wrote like poets. I'd seen their work before, on the loan that had started this whole descent: clauses that curved and doubled back like a river channel, terms that seemed generous until you read them a second time and realized generosity was just the shape the trap had been folded into.

I read the first page. Compensation: substantial, front-loaded, with a completion bonus that would clear my principal and most of the interest besides. Term: one monsoon season, defined precisely, ending on a date certain. Duties: weather reconnaissance, supply runs, medical evacuation capability, radio relay. All standard. All survivable.

Then page two began, and I felt the old familiar pull—that downward drag, like current taking hold of a hull. *In the event of equipment loss attributable to contractor negligence...* *Contractor acknowledges that all data, observations, recordings, and materials generated in the course of service remain the exclusive property of...* *In the event of contractor incapacitation, the Syndicate reserves the right to...*

I stopped reading.

I want to be precise about this moment, because later—much later—people would ask me how I could have signed what I signed, and the honest answer is that I did it on purpose. I held the pages up to the window light and saw, faintly, the watermark of things I didn't want to know. Data rights. Salvage claims. Indemnification language thick enough to stop a bullet. Somewhere in those paragraphs was probably a clause that made me legally dead if the river took me, and another that made me legally theirs if it didn't.

And I folded the pages closed and pressed my thumb to the ink pad Bright had thoughtfully included, and I signed.

Felix Fairweather. My mother's name for my father's stubbornness, written in my own hand, giving away pieces of myself I refused to count.

Here's the trick of it—the trick I performed on myself that morning, smooth as any con Bright ever ran: I told myself the fine print didn't matter because I intended to survive. Whatever they'd hidden in there applied to failure, to death, to incapacity, to negligence, and I was none of those things. I was Felix Fairweather, and I had been reading monsoon skies since I was nineteen years old, and I did not lose planes and I did not die and I did not fail, and therefore the contract's dark corridors would simply never be entered. I would walk through the bright rooms only, take the money at the end, and burn the key on my way out.

Every person who has ever been ruined by a contract has told themselves some version of this. We are all, in the end, excellent forecasters of everything except ourselves.

I signed the second copy. I initialed the corners. I put the documents back in their envelope, sealed it, and set it on the table by the door for the boy to collect, and then I sat very still on the edge of the bed and listened to the building breathe around me—the pipes knocking, someone's radio playing a song about the sea, the gulls arguing over the fish market—and I let myself feel, for exactly one minute, the weight of what I'd done.

Then the minute ended, because that was the arrangement I'd made with myself, and I got up and started packing.

* * *

Packing for the Kessari in monsoon season is a negotiation with reality, and reality drives a hard bargain. I laid everything out on the bed like a surgeon laying out instruments: charts, three sets, including the old atlas with the ghost town missing. Flight suit, patched twice. Rain gear rated for weather that hadn't been invented yet. A tool roll, spare spark plugs, a water separator filter, and the small brass compass my father had given me that pointed true north and, on bad days, seemed to point at trouble. Medical kit. Rations for a week beyond the planned schedule, because the planned schedule was a fiction and I preferred my fictions reinforced. A paperback novel I wouldn't read. A photograph I wouldn't look at.

Last, the logbook. I flipped through it out of habit—years of entries in my own cramped hand, airports and altitudes and winds aloft, the whole biography of a woman who had mistaken motion for freedom. Near the back, the entry from two days ago: *PCY municipal, 14:20. Full stop. Fuel.*

Full stop. I'd written it without thinking, and then I'd lain awake all night thinking about it, because in twenty years of logs I had never once written those words. Full stops were for other people. Full stops were for pilots with hangars and hometowns and creditors who weren't hunting them. I landed, I fueled, I left before anyone could ask questions, and my logbook read like a man running from the law instead of a woman running from arithmetic.

This season, if I survived it, the log would finally say something different. That was the deal. That was the whole deal, the only deal, the deal beneath the deal: one season of flying for the enemy, and then a life with periods at the ends of its sentences.

A knock at the door. The boy again, for the envelope. I handed it over, watched him tuck it away with the solemnity of a courier carrying state secrets, and gave him another coin he didn't need, because tipping well was the only luxury I'd kept and I defended it like territory.

Then I stood in the empty room with my bags packed and the day wide open, and I let myself think about Ilsa Voss.

Not the file—the file was paper, and paper lies by existing. I thought about the shape of her. A woman from a place that wasn't on maps, hired by scientists to tell them where the river was, given authority over a route that killed professionals. There were only two kinds of people who got that kind of trust: liars very good at their work, and truth-tellers whose truth was so strange that ordinary categories broke against it. I'd met both kinds. I'd been both kinds, depending on the year.

I found I was hoping—actually hoping, which was dangerous, hoping was a leak in the hull—for the second kind. I found I wanted the river to have a priestess instead of a con artist. I found that some part of me, the part that had become a pilot because the sky was the only honest witness left, wanted the Kessari to mean what Voss's file implied it meant: that there were still places the maps surrendered to, still knowledge that lived in blood and mud instead of databases.

And underneath that hope, quiet as ballast, sat the other thing. The thing I'd noticed in the file and filed away without examining, because examining it would have required reading the fine print of my own instincts, and I'd sworn off that practice until further notice.

The Syndicate held the insurance interest on the expedition's equipment. That was how Bright had framed it—casual, administrative, a detail. But the Syndicate didn't insure research equipment out of civic virtue. They insured things they expected to own, or things they expected to lose money on unless they controlled the outcome. Either way, seven scientists and a botanist from a ghost town were not, to the Vane Syndicate, people. They were exposure. They were a position. And I had just signed a contract making me the weather between that position and the sky.

I picked up my bags. I locked nothing, because there was nothing in the room worth locking and because locking doors was for people who planned to come back.

Outside, Port Calloway had committed fully to the morning—heat rising off the cobblestones, the harbor glittering like spilled coins, a trawler coming in loaded with the night's catch and the crew's arguments. My plane waited at the municipal strip, tied down and fuelled, and I walked to it the way you walk toward anything you've decided not to think about too closely: steadily, with your eyes on the middle distance, humming a little.

The preflight took an hour. Oil, fuel, control surfaces, the slow ritual of touching every part of the machine that would hold my life together above the clouds. When I finished, I sat in the cockpit with the canopy open and the river visible away to the north—a brown ribbon threading into green haze, running up toward mountains that owned the monsoon and lent it out at interest rates worse than the Syndicate's.

Seven names on a manifest. One of them from nowhere. One contract with corridors I'd refused to enter.

I keyed the radio and called in my flight plan—VFR north along the valley, first leg of the ferry run to the expedition's staging camp at the river mouth. The tower cleared me with the bored efficiency of a controller who hadn't looked at my name or cared to.

I brought the engine up, checked the mag drop, and rolled out onto the strip with the morning sun full in my eyes.

Later—weeks later, when the radios went silent and the handler told me to wait and my gut told me to fly—I would think back to this morning, to the moment before takeoff, and I would realize that I'd known even then. Not the specifics. Not the storm, not the valley, not the voice. But the shape of it. The way you sometimes feel a buildup forming over the horizon hours before it shows, in the pressure behind your ears, in the way the birds go quiet.

I'd known, and I'd signed anyway, and I'd packed my bags, and I'd flown north into the green haze toward a river that kept its secrets the way I kept my debts:

Absolutely, and with interest.
