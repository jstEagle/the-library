## 3. Monsoon Rules

The first thing I taught them was that the monsoon doesn't hate you.

They were seven scientists and a doctor crammed into the covered deck of the *Kestrel's Daughter*, my plane lashed down on the barge that would carry us upriver while I sat on an ammunition crate like it was a lectern. Rain drummed the canvas overhead in a rhythm I'd been hearing since I was nine years old, when my mother taught me to count seconds between lightning and thunder with her fingers wrapped around mine.

"Everyone wants to think of the monsoon as an enemy," I said. "It isn't. It's a landlord. It has rules, and if you follow them, it mostly leaves you alone."

Dr. Anselm, expedition lead, a tall man with the patient face of someone who'd spent decades being ignored by funding committees, raised his hand like we were back in school. "And what are the rules?"

"Glad you asked." I held up fingers. "Rule one: the river is always wider than it looks. What you see is maybe sixty percent of what you get. The rest is under your feet, waiting." Rule two: never trust a sky that's too beautiful. "The prettiest evenings out here are the ones building the biggest argument for tomorrow. Rule three—" I looked around at their faces, sunburned and earnest and already half in love with the adventure of it all, "—when I say run, you don't ask why. You run."

"And rule four?" That came from the botanist, Ilsa Voss, without raising her hand. She was sitting apart from the others, knees drawn up, watching me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. Not skepticism exactly. More like a woman checking another woman's arithmetic because she already suspected the answer.

"There is no rule four," I said. "Three rules is all anyone's ever needed."

"Four," she said quietly. "The storm tells you what it's going to do before it does it. Most people just aren't listening."

The deck went quiet except for the rain. Anselm glanced between us like a man watching two dogs circle. I smiled the smile I keep for passengers—warm, competent, entirely closed—and said, "Well. When you've flown as many seasons as I have, Voss, you learn there's no point listening to something that's going to do what it wants regardless."

She didn't smile back. She just looked at me a moment longer, the way you look at a lock you're deciding whether or not to pick, and then she unfolded herself and went to check her specimen cases, and the moment passed, and I told myself I hadn't just been corrected in front of my own class.

I'd signed a contract whose fine print I hadn't read. I was starting to suspect the fine print had signed me back.

* * *

We made good time the first week. The barge ground its way up the Kessari against a current gone brown and muscular with the season, and I flew the *Daughter* ahead each morning in low, lazy arcs, reading the river for the pilot of the barge—a laconic man named Oduya who communicated almost entirely in nods—and scouting landing strips that were less strips than suggestions. There were three of them marked on the charts: Kessari Bend, a missionary clearing; Two Sisters, a gravel bar that dried only in the driest months and was currently a rumor; and Fort Halvard, the last outpost of anything resembling authority, four hundred miles up, where the maps gave up and started drawing trees instead of roads.

I flew the way I always fly in monsoon country: early, low, and paranoid. Up before dawn while the air was still cool and honest, back down before the afternoon convection started cooking. The mornings were glass. The river unspooled below me like something generous, braided channels glittering, fishing camps sending up threads of smoke, kids waving from dugouts as I passed over at two hundred feet. Every day the land got greener and wilder and less interested in me, and every day I felt the debt collectors' phone calls getting fainter, the way a bruise stops throbbing when you finally stop pressing on it.

One season of weather support. That was the deal Bright had offered on the tarmac at Port Calloway, his smile bright as new chrome. Fly for the expedition, keep them alive, log everything, report everything. One season, and the Vane Syndicate's arithmetic changed in my favor. I hadn't asked what they wanted logged beyond weather. Willful blindness—I'd told myself it was a survival strategy, and mostly I believed it, the way you believe a rope bridge will hold right up until you're standing on it.

The radio check-ins were the ritual that held the days together. Eleven hundred hours, ship's time, I'd key the set in the *Daughter*'s cabin and raise base camp at the river mouth, and a cheerful voice named Prentiss—young, competent-sounding, probably bored out of his skull—would read back our position and conditions.

"*Kestrel's Daughter*, this is Calloway Base. Position logged. Conditions?"

"Calloway Base, *Daughter*. Conditions are—" and here I'd look out at whatever the sky was doing, which for six straight days was nothing much, warm gray cumulus stacked up like clean laundry, a few showers wandering the hills well off our line of travel. "—generous. Say again, generous. River's high but behaving. Team's healthy. Morale's disgusting."

"Copy disgusting morale," Prentiss would say, deadpan, and somewhere behind him I could hear the base camp laughing, and for eleven hundred hours every day I let myself believe that this job might actually be the easy money it was dressed up as.

Anselm took to joining me for the check-ins, standing in the cabin doorway with coffee he'd sweetened past the point of flavor. He liked to ask questions after Prentiss signed off. Good ones, actually—the kind that reminded me scientists weren't all useless in the field.

"The rain on the western ridges," he said one morning, nodding at a gray smudge forty miles off. "It hasn't moved in three days. Is that normal?"

I looked. He was right—it hadn't moved. A stationary shower complex, parked over the same fold of highlands, dumping itself empty and refilling like some patient machine. I'd noticed it too, filed it under *monsoon being monsoon*, and moved on.

"Orographic lifting," I said. "Air hits the ridge, goes up, rains out, does it again tomorrow. It'll sit there all season. It's furniture."

"Furniture," he repeated, tasting the word. "You make it sound so domestic."

"That's the trick, Doctor. You want to survive out here, you stop treating weather like an event. It's not an event. It's a place. Some places are safe and some places will kill you, and the whole skill is knowing which room you're standing in."

He laughed, and I laughed, and it was almost easy. Almost camaraderie. I remember thinking, that morning, banking the *Daughter* back toward the barge with the sun coming up gold through the rain haze, that I could get used to this. A season of flying a river that wanted to be kind. A paycheck that erased a debt instead of deepening one. People who waved at airplanes like airplanes were good news.

That's the thing about the calm before the season turns. It doesn't feel like a hold-your-breath pause in a story. It feels like arrival. It feels like *finally*.

* * *

Ilsa Voss started appearing at my shoulder on the fourth morning, and by the seventh I'd stopped pretending to mind.

She didn't talk much. She'd climb up onto the wing root while I did my pre-dawn walkaround, silent as a cat, and stand there while I checked the surfaces and drained the fuel sump, and then, just as I was about to tell her to go drink her coffee, she'd say something like: "The wind's backed two points since yesterday. Northeast now. That's wrong for this stretch, isn't it? Should be southeast until the bend."

And she'd be right. Every time.

I started testing her. Little games, just for me. I'd look at the sky and form my own call—*afternoon buildups over the eastern hills, maybe a squall line by dark, nothing organized*—and then wait to see what she saw. And she'd say, conversationally, to no one in particular, "Those clouds over the east are stacking. By dark there'll be a line. We should tie down double tonight." Or: "That haze isn't smoke, it's inflow. Something's pulling air hard from the south. Big rain north of us by morning."

Right. Right. Right. Four days running.

On the fifth day I caught her doing something I couldn't explain. We were moored below Two Sisters—which turned out to exist after all, a gravel bar the size of a church, half drowned but holding—and the evening sky was doing its beautiful, lying thing: towering cumulus lit up orange and rose along their western faces, gorgeous, postcard-perfect, the kind of sky tourists photograph right before the weather ruins their vacation. The rest of the team was on the bank taking pictures of each other in front of it.

Ilsa stood alone at the bow, facing away from the sunset, looking east into the gathering blue-gray dark, and she had her mouth slightly open, and her head was tilted, and I swear to God she was listening. Not to anything I could hear. To the air itself.

"What are you doing?" I asked.

She didn't startle. She'd known I was there, obviously. "Counting," she said.

"Counting what?"

"Thunder." She held up a finger. Waited. And far off to the east, so faint I might have invented it, came a sound like a door closing in a very large house. Another finger. "Every ninety seconds. Same bearing. Same strength." She lowered her hand and looked at me, and in the last light her eyes were the color of the river. "Furniture, you'd say."

My stomach did something complicated. "Orographic lifting," I said, but it came out thinner than I meant it to.

"Mm." She turned back to the east. "Except the ridge is thirty miles inland, and that thunder is over open water on the chart. There's no hill there, Felix. So what's lifting the air?"

I didn't have an answer. I want to be clear about that, because for the rest of this story people assumed I knew things earlier than I did—I didn't. I had a botanist who read clouds better than I did and a patch of thunder sitting where no thunder should sit, and I had a contract I hadn't read, and I put all three of those things in the same drawer in my head labeled *deal with later*, and I locked it, because that drawer is how I've stayed alive this long.

"Tie down double tonight," I said instead, and walked away, and heard her soft laugh follow me across the deck—not mocking, worse than mocking. Knowing.

* * *

Fort Halvard came and went in an afternoon of fuel drums and mosquito coils and a district officer who stamped our papers without reading them because reading them would have constituted effort. The last radio relay tower in the world stood behind his office, leaning, wrapped in vines, and Prentiss's voice came through it thin and crackling but clear enough:

"*Daughter*, Calloway. Be advised, you're approaching the edge of reliable coverage. Beyond Halvard, expect degraded comms. Scheduled check-ins may skip. Don't panic unless we tell you to panic."

"Copy," I said. "Define 'don't panic.'"

Long pause. Static breathing. Then Prentiss, cheerfully: "Upriver of Halvard, the schedule is a courtesy, not a promise. If you miss a window, we assume weather. If you miss three, we assume interesting. Over."

"Interesting," Anselm repeated beside me, delighted. "What a wonderful word for it."

"It's a terrible word for it," I said. "Interesting is what weathermen say when they're glad it's happening to someone else."

But I was smiling when I said it, and so was he, and the whole expedition was riding that golden first-week feeling where everyone is still their best self and the river seems like a collaborator. Even Ilsa unbent a little that night at Halvard. She shared out dried fruit from her own stores and told a story about a field season in some delta I'd never heard of, where the river had risen four feet overnight and floated their entire camp away intact, tents and tables and all, and set it down gently two miles downstream, "arranged," she said, "more neatly than we'd left it."

"Rivers don't arrange things," said the doctor, Okonkwo, around a mouthful of fruit.

"This one did," Ilsa said, and there was something in her voice—not fear, not wonder, something older than either—that made the fire pop loudly in the silence, and everyone laughed a beat too late, and the subject changed.

I lay awake that night in the *Daughter*'s cabin listening to the rain start up on the canvas and thought about the thunder with no hill under it, counting itself out every ninety seconds over open water, patient as a metronome. Thought about a botanist who listened to air. Thought about fine print.

Then I did what I always do with thoughts that cost more than I can pay. I rolled over, and I slept, and in the morning the sky was glass again, and the river ran green-gold and wide, and the check-in came through right on schedule, Prentiss's voice warm as a lamp:

"*Kestrel's Daughter*, Calloway Base. Conditions?"

I keyed the mic and looked out at the long bright water and the clean-stacked clouds and the country going soft and wild ahead of us, and I said the truest thing I'd say for weeks:

"Calloway, *Daughter*. Conditions are generous."

Behind me, unheard, unsaid, the season turned over in its sleep.
