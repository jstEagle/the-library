## 4. Static on the Third Week

The river mouth at dawn looked like someone had spilled pewter across the world.

Felix stood on the corrugated roof of the fuel shed at base camp, a mug of reconstituted coffee going cold in her hand, and watched the Kessari open its mouth into the sea. The water came down brown and heavy with silt, pushing against the tide in a slow argument neither side ever won. Out past the sandbars, fishing boats sat low and patient, their crews mending nets that would be useless by noon. The monsoon didn't care about schedules. It would come when it came, and everything on this coast lived or died by how well it read the sky.

She'd been reading skies for twenty years. She was good at it. It was the only thing she'd ever been good at that anyone had been willing to pay for, and lately even that was a maybe.

Twenty-one days. The expedition had been upriver for twenty-one days, and today was check-in day.

* * *

Base camp was less a camp than an apology for one: two sheds, a generator that coughed like a two-pack-a-day man, a radio mast lashed together with guy-wire and optimism, and her plane — the *Kestrel*, twenty-six years old, held together by safety wire, prayer, and Felix's own hands. She slept in the plane's shadow in a hammock strung between the landing gear struts, because it was the only place she felt like herself anymore. In the hammock she wasn't a debtor. She was a pilot between flights, and every pilot between flights is one good decision away from being somebody new.

The radio schedule was simple. Every seventh day, 0800 local, the expedition's field set would crackle up on the agreed frequency. Thirty seconds of position, weather, morale. Then silence for another week. Ilsa Voss had designed the protocol herself, back in Port Calloway, tapping the manifest with one finger while Felix pretended not to study the fine print of her own contract. *We're scientists, not chatterboxes,* Ilsa had said. *Thirty seconds is plenty to say we're alive.*

Felix had thought that was charming, in a grim way. People who needed fewer words were usually people who trusted themselves more than they should.

At 0740 she was already at the radio, headset around her neck, watching the second hand crawl around her father's old watch. At 0800 exactly she keyed the mic.

*Kestrel Base to Kessari Expedition. Scheduled check-in, day twenty-one. Over.*

Static. The good kind, though — the honest kind, the hiss of atmosphere doing what atmosphere does. She could hear the river in it, almost. She waited ninety seconds and tried again.

Nothing.

That was fine. That happened. Solar interference, battery conservation, a wet connection, a field set dropped in a creek. She made herself a list of benign explanations and worked through it methodically, the way she'd been taught, the way she did everything — because method was cheaper than panic and panic cost interest.

By 1000 she'd tried eleven times. By noon she'd stopped counting and started walking the perimeter of the camp, which was what she did instead of pacing, because pacing was admitting something was wrong and walking was just exercise.

The backup window opened at 1600. The protocol allowed for it — if the primary window was missed due to weather or equipment, the field team would transmit during a four-hour stretch beginning sixteen hundred. Ilsa had explained this too, in her flat, careful voice, and Felix had nodded along without really listening, because three weeks ago nothing about this job had felt real enough to listen to.

Now it was real. Now every hour had weight.

* * *

At 1530 the generator died mid-cough, and Felix spent forty minutes elbow-deep in its guts with grease to her elbows and a word she wouldn't repeat in front of her mother lodged behind her teeth. She got it running at 1612, filthy and furious, and was at the radio by 1615 with the headset clamped so tight her ears ached.

*1600 hours, day twenty-one. This is Kestrel Base calling Kessari Expedition on backup window. Anyone receiving, please respond. Over.*

Static.

She let the frequency run open and listened to the river breathe through it. Somewhere under that hiss there should have been voices. There was nothing. Just the long, indifferent shush of a planet that didn't know she existed.

She thought about the roster. Seven names. Dr. Marcus Anselm, team lead, a climatologist who talked about pressure gradients the way other men talked about old lovers. Ilsa Voss, botanist, who knew the river better than the maps did and never once explained how. Two hydrologists, a geologist, a doctor, a graduate student named Pell who got airsick every time Felix ferried them the first leg up and apologized the whole way as if it were a moral failing. Seven people, thirty seconds a week, and now nothing.

They weren't her people. That was the thing she kept coming back to, turning over like a stone looking for what was underneath. She'd flown them upriver, taken the Syndicate's advance, and signed on to sit at the mouth of the river for a season playing relay station. Her job was the plane and the weather calls. Their survival was their own department. If she made it personal, personal was how she'd end up — personally broke, personally dead, personally buried in some unmarked ledger in Bright's cheerful little book.

And yet.

She looked at the sky. Old habit, older than debt. The ceiling was high, scattered cumulus building over the interior heat, nothing organized, nothing mean. A flyer's sky. A sky with a door left open in it. Three days ago a squall line had come off the coast and she'd have called the route suicide. Today the weather was handing her a gift and daring her to take it.

Her gut said fly. Her gut had been saying fly since 0800, quietly at first, then louder, and Felix Fairweather had survived twenty years of storm work by learning exactly one lesson over and over again: the gut is the instrument that doesn't break. Gauges fail. Radios fail. Contracts fail. The gut is the last thing standing, and hers was screaming.

But flying meant fuel, and fuel meant drawing attention, and attention was the one thing she couldn't afford. Not with Bright's people holding paper on her. Not with the Syndicate monitoring this contract the way a pawnbroker monitors a shop full of other people's watches.

Which was, of course, exactly when the satellite phone rang.

* * *

"Bright." She answered on the third ring, keeping her voice level, leaning against the radio mast where the reception was best. "You're up early."

"I'm always up early, Ms. Fairweather. Optimism keeps its own hours." Bright's voice came through warm and unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once been inconvenienced by anything, including conscience. "How's our river?"

"River's fine. Rain Tuesday, clearing yesterday, building again this afternoon. Textbook."

"And our scientists? Chatty as ever?"

Felix watched a heron stalk the mudflat below the shed, one deliberate step at a time, hunting things that hadn't yet decided to hide. "Check-in's late," she said. "Backup window's open now. Could be solar activity. Could be batteries. Could be they're busy."

"Could be," Bright agreed pleasantly. Silence stretched out on the line, comfortable for him, corrosive for her. He was letting her hear him think. That was a technique. Everything Bright did was a technique; the smile was just packaging. "Here's my thinking, Felix. May I call you Felix? I feel we've known each other long enough."

"You hold my debts. Call me whatever gets you through the day."

"Ha! See, this is why I like you." Papers rustled somewhere on his end, or he wanted her to think they did. "My thinking is this: wait it out. Twenty-four hours. These academic types lose track of time upriver — you give them a river and a budget and they forget calendars exist. Tomorrow, same windows. If nothing, then we discuss options. Fuel is expensive, aircraft are expensive, and frankly, Ms. Fairweather, your margin for expensive is—" he paused delicately, "—thin."

"My margin is my business."

"Your margin is our business. That's what the paperwork says, and you did sign the paperwork." A pause, and then, softer, almost kind, which was worse: "Look. Nobody's lost up that river who matters. Seven researchers and some instruments. The instruments are insured. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Whatever's happened up there, the arithmetic already works. Don't make it complicated by being heroic. Heroics are for people whose losses are their own."

The line clicked off before she could answer, which was also a technique. Bright never argued; he stated, and then he left you alone with your own agreement forming in your head like mold.

Felix stood there with the phone hot against her ear and the static still hissing out of the radio behind her, and she understood that she had just been given an order dressed as advice, and that following it would be the easiest thing in the world, and that ease was the most dangerous currency she dealt in.

* * *

Night came down over the river mouth like a lid. The frogs started first, then the insects, then the endless wet machinery of the mangroves settling into themselves. Felix sat in the *Kestrel*'s cockpit with the dome light off, running the numbers she'd promised herself she wouldn't run.

Fuel: three-quarters tanks, enough for the run upriver and back with reserve, if the weather held and she didn't have to dogleg around anything ugly. Airframe: sound, mostly. Weather: a window opening tomorrow before dawn, closing by early afternoon when the interior heat cooked up its daily storms. And the schedule: twenty-four hours, Bright had said. Wait it out.

Except waiting was a currency too, and she was spending it every hour she sat here, and the exchange rate was terrible.

She thought about seven names on a manifest. She thought about Ilsa Voss's flat gray eyes and the way she'd looked at the river in Port Calloway — not like scenery, not like a workplace, but like something she recognized. She thought about the thirty-second check-ins, week one and week two, routine as sunrise: *Position confirmed. Weather noted. All well.* Ilsa's voice both times, clipped and calm, signing off with the expedition code phrase — *Kessari clear* — like a woman closing a door softly behind her.

Week three: nothing. Not even static shaped like a voice.

People who were alive found ways to signal. That was the arithmetic Bright didn't want done. Dead radios meant dead men or broken men or men who didn't want to be heard, and all three of those possibilities had teeth.

Felix pulled the logbook onto her knees and wrote by dome light, because writing made decisions real: *Day 21. No contact, primary or backup window. Handler advises standdown 24 hrs. Weather window AM. Gut says go.*

She stared at the last three words for a long time. Then she added: *Gut has never been wrong about weather. Question is whether people are weather.*

She closed the book. Outside, the river ran dark and enormous toward the sea, carrying everything it carried — silt, seeds, secrets, maybe seven sets of gear and no people at all — down to an ocean that kept every confidence.

She'd give Bright his twenty-four hours. She'd honor the letter of it, because breaking contract in writing was how you ended up owing more than money.

But she'd spend those hours preflighting the *Kestrel* by lantern light, checking every rivet and wire, loading the survival kit, filing the flight plan in her head. And when the window opened tomorrow before dawn — his twenty-four hours expired, her hands clean, her conscience technically intact — she was going to point the nose upriver and fly into whatever was waiting there.

Because staying invisible had been the plan, and plans were cheap, and somewhere up that river seven people had gone quiet in a way that quiet shouldn't.

The static hissed on behind her, patient as the river, saying nothing, saying everything.

Felix killed the dome light and began, in the dark, to count the hours until morning.
