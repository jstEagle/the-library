## 5. Flying Into the Wall

The handler's voice was still in her ear when Felix pulled the chocks herself.

"Wait it out, Fairweather. That's not a suggestion, that's arithmetic. You go upriver and vanish, the Syndicate writes off a plane and a pilot, and neither one costs us what you think it does." Bright's voice had that sunny patience he wore like a uniform. "Sit tight. They'll check in."

"They've missed two windows," Felix said.

"And sometimes people miss windows. Radios drown. Batteries die. Scientists get excited and forget they have employers." A pause, and then, softer, the voice he used when he wanted to sound like a friend instead of a ledger: "You've got forty-one thousand in outstanding balances, Lena—Felix. Sorry. Force of habit. You want to add a broken airframe to that? Because I'll tell you how we account for broken airframes. We don't forgive them. We itemize them."

She looked at the Albatross sitting on the mud apron at the river mouth base camp, her battered STOL with the patched cowling and the engine she'd rebuilt twice with parts bought from men who didn't ask questions. Forty-one thousand. She'd done the arithmetic too, every night for three years, lying in whatever bunk or hammock or back seat the season gave her. The number never got smaller. It only got more patient.

"Bright," she said. "If they're dead, waiting doesn't help them. If they're alive, waiting might kill them. Either way, waiting helps exactly one party, and it isn't me and it isn't them."

"It's the party holding your paper," Bright said cheerfully. "Think about it overnight."

"I'm thinking about it now," Felix said, and hung up on him, which she would pay for later, and climbed into the cockpit before the part of her that survived on caution could catch up with the rest of her.

* * *

The Kessari at dawn was a brown serpent swollen fat with the monsoon, sliding through drowned forest, its banks gone soft and speculative. Felix had flown rivers in flood on three continents and she knew their moods: the sullen brown of heavy load, the white lace of current over submerged snags, the way the water ate islands whole and spat out new ones downstream. This river was doing all of that and none of it bothered her.

What bothered her was the sky upriver.

She'd spent the evening before with the synoptic charts spread across the mess table, weighting the corners with coffee mugs while the expedition's junior meteorologist—a nervous, brilliant kid named Okafor who'd been left at base camp to run communications—traced the pressure fields with a grease pencil. Everything said what monsoon seasons always say: diurnal convection, afternoon buildups, squall lines marching off the hills in the late hours, all of it disorganized, seasonal, honest weather. Weather with rules.

But the last two satellite passes had shown something at the top of the valley system, two hundred kilometers upriver past the last relay station, that Okafor kept circling without naming. A cell that hadn't moved in six weeks. A storm parked over the same coordinates through nineteen days of steering winds that should have dragged it apart or shoved it along. He'd called it a data artifact. He'd called it terrain anchoring. He'd called it, once, quietly, when he thought she wasn't listening, *that's not possible*, and then he'd erased his grease-pencil circle and redrawn the chart like a man tidying a room where something frightening used to be.

Felix had spent enough years around impossible things to know they usually announced themselves early, politely, in ways you could still walk away from.

She walked away from this one at 0540, into a sky the color of wet slate, with the Albatross loaded to eleven percent over gross because she'd stripped out everything that wasn't fuel, a raft, a machete, flares, and the emergency kit. No radio operator's blessing. No flight plan filed beyond a scribbled note on Okafor's clipboard: *UPRIVER, BACK BY DARK OR DON'T WAIT.* She'd signed it with a smiley face, because Bright's people would read the report eventually and she wanted them to know she'd gone laughing.

The first hour was ordinary flying, which is to say it demanded everything she had. Rain hammered the windscreen in pulses. The river vanished and reappeared below her like a card trick. She flew the valleys between buildup cells the way she'd learned in the delta country years ago—reading the light on the water, feeling the gusts arrive through the stick a half-second before they hit the airframe, treating turbulence not as an enemy but as a language she happened to speak fluently.

At the second hour the language changed dialect.

She felt it before she saw it: a wrongness in the way the air leaned against her, a pressure gradient that didn't match anything on her eyeball scan of the horizon. Then she saw it, and her hands went cold on the yoke even though the cockpit was ninety degrees and dripping.

A squall line stood across the river valley ahead of her, wall to wall, from ridge to ridge—and it was rotating.

Not the broad, lazy circulation of a big mesoscale system, which she'd seen and survived. This was tight. Organized. The cloud bases were lowering into a smooth, collared structure that looked less like weather and more like architecture, and the rotation ran *backward*—counterclockwise where everything in this hemisphere, in this season, in this atmosphere, had any right to run clockwise. She throttled back, put the nose down, and watched it through the rain-blurred glass with the particular attention of a woman trying to find the innocent explanation before the guilty one finished forming.

Terrain anchoring, she told herself. Valley flow deflection. There are places where mountains twist the wind into shapes—

The line built upward as she watched. Not gradually, not the slow cauliflower swelling of ordinary convection. It rose like something inhaling, the tower stretching toward the tropopause in visible surges, and each surge came with a flicker of lightning inside the cloud that didn't flash so much as *pulse*—regular, rhythmic, spaced almost evenly, like a heartbeat seen through skin.

Okafor's artifact. Nineteen days stationary. Rotating wrong, building wrong.

"Felix," she said aloud to herself, because saying things out loud was how she held onto the sane version of events, "you can turn around. Right now. Nobody would ever know. Bright would be delighted. You could log it as recon-complete, weather-avoided, pilot-judgment-excellent."

The Albatross droned on northward. Her hands hadn't moved on the yoke.

Because here was the thing she couldn't say to Bright, couldn't put in a log, barely admitted to herself: seven people were up there somewhere past that wall, and one of them was a botanist named Ilsa Voss who read clouds better than Felix did and had known it, had let Felix teach anyway, had sat beside her in the co-pilot seat during the training flights watching the sky with an expression Felix had only ever seen on gamblers watching a wheel they'd already rigged. If anyone on that expedition had understood what was building above the valley, it was Ilsa. And if Ilsa had understood it and said nothing—

Then whatever waited behind the wall wasn't an accident.

Felix banked south, looking for a gap in the line. There wasn't one. She banked north. The line bent with the valley and closed again, patient as a hand closing. She climbed to twelve thousand feet, which for the Albatross at gross-over was less flying than begging, and the tops of the cells rose to meet her, and the lightning pulsed inside them in that same terrible rhythm, and she understood that the storm was not going to offer her a polite way through or around.

It was going to make her choose.

"Okay," she said. "Okay, you ugly miracle. Your rules."

She picked her hole the way she'd pick a route through a canyon: the lowest cloud base, the widest rain shaft, the sector where the inflow looked weakest. Northeast quadrant, just west of the river bend where the maps showed an old logging cut and, six kilometers short of the expedition's last known camp, a long gravel bar the survey charts swore was dry-season usable. It was not dry season. But the Albatross had landed on worse than wet gravel, and Felix had landed on worse than the Albatross.

She went in at treetop height, rain hitting the windshield sideways, wipers useless, flying half by instrument and half by the memory of the river's shape from the training flights. The turbulence hit in fists. Something in the tail section began to buzz—a loose panel, she hoped, and told herself it was a loose panel, and flew. The rotation was above her and around her now; she could feel the air spiraling into the storm on her left wing like a hand slowly closing, and the lightning pulses were no longer inside the cloud but crawling down its flanks in bright branching ladders, and they were *evenly spaced*, and no natural storm she had ever met in eleven years of chasing had ever been evenly spaced about anything.

The gravel bar appeared out of the rain like a rumor.

She flared long, touched down on the first third, and the wheels hit standing water and the plane hydroplaned, slewed, caught a wheel in soft mud at the bar's edge and swung hard left. Felix rode it, full opposite rudder, engine to idle, and the Albatross spun a lazy half-circle and stopped with the left main gear buried to the axle and the propeller tips bent into green tulips against a fallen log.

Silence, except the rain.

She sat there for ten seconds, hands locked on the yoke, listening to the engine tick as it cooled, taking inventory of herself the way she took inventory of aircraft after a bad landing. Fingers, toes, ribs, skull. All present. All complaining. Good enough.

"Well," she told the bent propeller, "you always said you wanted to retire near water."

* * *

She gave herself twenty minutes to do what could be done. That was the discipline, the old discipline, the one thing the debt years hadn't taken from her: fix what's fixable in the time you have, then move. She hauled the raft kit, the machete, the flare gun, the med pouch, and three days of ration bars into a waterproof duffel. She drained what fuel she could from the lines into a jerry can—not for the plane, for fire, if it came to fire. She wrapped the emergency beacon in the duffel's bottom and then, after a moment, took it back out and stashed it under the pilot's seat instead.

Because if the Syndicate came looking for the plane, she didn't want them finding a beacon telling them where *she'd* gone.

That thought arrived fully formed and chilled her more than the storm had. When had she started planning around her own creditors in a rescue situation? She stood in the rain beside her crippled airplane, halfway up a drowned river, at the edge of a forest that breathed, and realized the answer was: years ago. Somewhere between the second loan and the fourth, staying invisible had stopped being a tactic and become a reflex, and now here she was, hiding a rescue beacon from the people paying for the rescue.

"Past the point of return," she said, and found she meant three different things by it, and shouldered the duffel, and walked into the trees.

* * *

The flooded forest was a cathedral with the floor removed. Kapok and fig trunks rose out of brown water that varied from ankle-deep to chest-deep without warning, and the canopy knitted overhead so completely that the rain arrived not as rain but as a slow, perpetual dripping, a thousand small taps instead of one drowning roar. Felix moved by compass and by the river's sound, keeping the current's voice on her left, cutting through hanging lianas with the machete in strokes she made herself pace—one, two, three, breathe—because the alternative was to rush, and rushing in floodwater was how experienced people died stupidly.

Two hours in, she found the first sign: a plastic sample vial wedged in a root crotch, labeled in neat block letters. *KV-114 / SEDIMENT / A. OKAFOR WAS HERE.* She turned it over twice. Okafor was at base camp. Which meant either someone else had borrowed his labels, or the team had come downriver this far and gone back, or—

Or the labels had traveled some other way. She pocketed the vial and didn't finish the thought.

Three hours in, the water shallowed onto a rise of ground where the forest broke around a stand of bamboo, and she allowed herself ten minutes on dry(ish) land to eat a ration bar and rewrap the blister already opening on her heel. While she chewed, she listened. Monsoon forest has a soundscape like a crowded room—insects, birds, water, wind—and she'd spent enough nights in it to hear when the room went quiet.

The room went quiet.

Not everywhere. Just to the north, in the direction of the storm, a swath of silence maybe a kilometer wide, moving—she was almost sure it was moving—slowly closer. No birdsong in that band. No insects. Just the drip of the canopy and the mutter of the flooded river, as if the forest itself were holding its breath around something passing through.

Felix had grown up around storms the way other kids grew up around dogs. She knew their body language. This was not the hush before a squall, which is electric and brief. This was the hush of attention. Of something turning to look.

She got up, cinched the duffel, and kept walking north, faster now, straight toward the silence, because the camp lay that way and because—she admitted it only to the inside of her own skull—because the silence frightened her less than the thought of Ilsa Voss standing in the middle of it, unhurt, unafraid, having known all along.

The light was failing when she reached the edge of the old logging cut, and the first thing she saw was the boot print.

Bare feet, actually—the print was bare, human, pressed deep in the mud of the trail as though its owner had been carrying weight—and it pointed north, up the cut, toward the valley, toward the pulsing glow that had begun to show through the trees where the storm's lightning lit the underside of the clouds in slow, rhythmic flashes. Evenly spaced. Patient. Like a lighthouse. Like a doorbell.

Felix stood over the footprint for a long moment, rain dripping off her chin, machete loose in her hand.

Then she followed it.
