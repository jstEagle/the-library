## 6. What the River Kept

The rain let up an hour before dawn, as if the sky had finally gotten bored of her.

Felix lay in the wreck of the *Kestrel's* cabin with her knees drawn up under the emergency blanket and listened to the forest drip. Every sound was wrong here. In Port Calloway she could sleep through dock horns and Syndicate muscle kicking in doors; out here the silence between drips had a texture to it, a held-breath quality that made her count her own heartbeats like a nervous accountant. The river ran somewhere below the slope, high and brown and muscular, carrying whole trees sideways. Somewhere in that current were the pieces of her landing gear, and the last honest parts of her career.

She'd come down through a squall line that rotated against every law she'd spent fifteen years respecting, and the ground had caught her the way ground does—without mercy but without killing her either. The Kestrel was done. Nose crumpled into a termite mound, one wing folded like a broken finger, propeller bent into a shape that looked almost apologetic. She'd walked away from it at midnight with a flashlight, a machete, a flare pistol, and forty hours of rations, telling herself the same lie she told herself about her debts: *this is temporary.*

Now the light came up gray-green through the canopy, and she could see how far she still had to go.

Anselm's last coordinates put the expedition's forward camp on a bluff above a drowned side-valley, maybe six kilometers northeast as the crow flew—except nothing flew today except things with too many wings, so Felix walked. The flooded forest took her in to the waist in places, black water warm as bathwater and opaque as ink, full of things she chose not to think about. She moved from trunk to trunk, testing each grip before trusting it, and the whole time the forest watched her with the patient attention of something that had eaten better-prepared people.

Six kilometers took her five hours. By the time she smelled woodsmoke—or thought she did, or wanted to—her hands were shredded and her boots had become a rumor of boots, held together by mud and stubbornness.

Then the trees opened, and there it was.

* * *

The camp sat on a shelf of high ground above the drowned valley, and it was intact.

That was the first thing. Not burned, not scattered, not torn apart by animals or water. The two big dome tents stood staked and zipped, guy-lines taut, rain flys still buttoned down. The mess tarp was rigged over the cooking area exactly the way the field manual specified, corners tucked, drainage trench cut on the downhill side. A line of sample bags hung from a drying rack, labeled in neat block letters. Someone's coffee mug sat on the crate beside the stove, half full, a skin of mold across its surface like a small green continent.

Felix stood at the treeline for a long moment with the machete low at her side, doing the arithmetic of abandonment.

Camps that get hit by floods look hit. Camps that get abandoned in panic look exploded—zippers ripped, gear trails, the archaeology of fear. This camp looked *paused*. It looked like seven people had stepped out to look at something and meant to come back before the coffee went cold.

"Hello!" Her voice came out rusty. "Camp Kessari! Felix Fairweather! Anyone?"

The forest answered with birdsong and dripping leaves. Nothing else.

She went in slow, machete up, checking the tents the way you check a room that might be holding a snake. Empty. Bunks made or unmade in the ordinary way of bunks—sleeping bags thrown back, a paperback splayed face-down on a pillow, someone's reading glasses folded on top of it. Personal effects everywhere. Nobody packs for a hike and leaves their glasses.

The food stores stopped her cold.

Under the mess tarp, sealed crates and mylar sacks stacked three deep: rice, lentils, oil, salt, a case of vitamin bricks, water purification tabs enough to treat a small lake. Untouched. Not raided, not rationed-down, not carried off. She pried one crate open and the plastic seal inside cracked with a sound like a knuckle—factory fresh. Seven scientists don't walk away from six weeks of food. Starving people don't leave it. Even polite, orderly, rule-following academic types, even people leaving in a hurry, take the food. Food is the first thing anyone takes.

Felix crouched over the open crate and felt the dread change shape in her chest.

Because this wasn't disaster. Disaster she knew how to read. She'd pulled bodies out of flood zones and crash sites for a decade; disaster had a grammar, and she was fluent in it. This was something else. This was a camp where nobody had been hurt, nobody had starved, nobody had packed—and everybody had left.

*Deliberate,* said a voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly like Bright. *This looks deliberate.*

"Shut up," she told it, and kept moving.

* * *

The equipment shed was a third tent, bigger, aluminum frame, and it was where the story turned strange.

Inside, the expedition's science lived in careful rows: sediment corers, water samplers, a disassembled weather mast, laptops in padded cases, hard drives in waterproof boxes, notebooks in ziplock bags. All of it dry. All of it organized. And all of it, as far as she could tell in one sweep of the flashlight, *working order*. Nothing smashed. Nothing sabotaged. Whatever had ended this expedition, they hadn't taken it out on their instruments.

Except the radios.

There were four of them—the expedition's HF base set and three portables, per the manifest she'd skimmed and hadn't read. She found all four lined up on the folding table like patients prepped for surgery.

Every one of them was dismantled. Not broken—*dismantled*, with a precision that raised the hair on her arms. Back panels removed and set aside, screws sorted into little piles by size. Batteries disconnected, leads coiled neatly beside each unit. Antennas unscrewed and laid parallel. On the portable units, someone had gone further: pulled the speaker drivers, lifted the microphone elements out of their handsets, and arranged them in a row on a square of clean cloth, like specimens. Like evidence being preserved rather than destroyed.

Felix picked up one handset and turned it over. The mic capsule was gone. Not torn out—unfastened, four tiny screws backed out and set in their pile, the cavity plugged with a twist of cloth to keep the dust out. Whoever did this had taken their time. Had used tools. Had, God help her, been *careful*.

You smash radios when you're panicking. You smash them when you're angry, or scared, or trying to keep someone from calling for help in the next ten minutes. You do not spend an hour of your life removing screws in size-ordered piles unless you are making sure those radios never work again—ever, anywhere, by anyone—and you want them kept in a condition where they *could* be made to work again someday, once it's safe, once it's allowed.

It was vandalism performed like an apology. Or a promise.

Felix set the handset down very gently, the way you put down something that might be listening.

Her eyes went around the shed again, slower now. The hard drives. The notebooks. All still here. If someone wanted to bury a discovery, the radios were the wrong target—you burn the data, not the phones. Unless the data wasn't the danger. Unless the danger was the *talking*. Unless whatever they'd found upriver, whatever had pulled seven trained scientists out of their tents and into the flooded forest without their glasses or their food—

Unless it heard them coming.

"That's insane," she said aloud, just to hear how it sounded.

It sounded insane. It also sounded like the only theory on the table, because the alternative theories were all worse.

* * *

She found the boot print twenty minutes later, on the far side of camp, where the bluff dropped toward the drowned valley.

A single print pressed into the clay at the edge of the waterline, deep-heeled, sharp-edged—a proper expedition boot, the kind Ilsa Voss had worn in Port Calloway, Felix realized, the kind half the team wore. Facing away from camp. Facing into the water.

One print. That was the thing. One.

She circled, looking for its partners, and found instead a scatter of smaller marks—bare feet. Several sets, overlapping, blurred at the edges by seepage but unmistakable: human feet, bare, walking out of the shallows and up onto the bank. The bare prints came *out* of the water. The booted print went *in*. And neither came back.

Felix stood at the waterline with the rain starting again, fine and warm, beading on the back of her neck, and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing since the mess tarp.

Seven people. No bodies. No struggle. Radios surgically silenced, food untouched, data left behind. Bare feet walking calmly out of a flooded forest, and one shod pair walking in. This wasn't an accident that had happened to the expedition. This was a decision the expedition had made—together, or nearly together—about what the world got to know.

They hadn't lost contact with base camp.

They'd cut the line.

And the backup window, the missed check-in, Anselm's silence—it wasn't a distress call she'd answered. It was a door she'd knocked on that had been locked from the inside.

The rain thickened. Out over the drowned valley, maybe eight kilometers north, the light had begun to go strange—not storm-dark, not evening-dark, but a deep bruised indigo that pooled in one place on the horizon like ink under ice. She'd seen it yesterday from the air and told herself it was a stationary cell anchored on the highlands. Standing storms happened. They lasted days, sometimes weeks.

But standing storms didn't have rain shadows you could set a watch by. Standing storms didn't crawl lightning down their flanks in patterns too regular to be chance. And standing storms, as far as Felix Fairweather knew, had never once made seven scientists dismantle their own voices screw by screw and walk barefoot into the water to join them.

The print filled slowly with rainwater, going soft at the edges. Whatever had walked in that boot was hours ahead of her now, maybe more, moving through country where she couldn't follow and wouldn't survive if she tried.

But the barefoot tracks—the ones that came *out*—those led somewhere too. Upstream along the bluff, toward the indigo stain on the horizon, toward the thing the valley was keeping.

Felix tightened the machete lashing on her hip, checked her flare pistol, and looked north at the storm that hadn't moved in months.

"Okay," she said quietly. "I'm coming anyway."

Far off, so far it might have been thunder and might not have been, something answered.
