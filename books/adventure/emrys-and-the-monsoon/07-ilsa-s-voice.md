## 7. Ilsa's Voice

Three weeks before Felix Fairweather landed a broken airplane in the flooded forest, Ilsa Voss stood on the ridge above the drowned valley and heard the storm say her name.

Not her name, exactly. Not yet. What she heard first was the river itself, played back.

She had climbed alone, the way she climbed everything, leaving the others at the lower camp with instructions to rest and a lie about surveying drainage lines. The truth was simpler and worse: she'd been awake for two days listening to the barometers, and the barometers were lying. All four of them, in different instruments, agreed on one impossible fact. The pressure over the valley had not changed in eleven days. Not risen, not fallen. Held, like a held breath, while the monsoon wheeled and broke and rebuilt itself all around that one column of still air.

She reached the ridge at dawn, or what passed for dawn under a sky like bruised slate, and looked down into the valley the maps called the Kessari's old oxbow, and saw the storm.

She had read the satellite passes a hundred times. A supercell, anchored, stationary, feeding on inflow that should have carried it east days or weeks or months ago. On paper it was a curiosity, an error in the data, a storm that had decided to be a mountain. From the ridge it was something else. It was a cathedral. A tower of cloud rising out of its own rain, green-black at the base, lit from within by lightning that came not in flashes but in pulses—regular, patient, like the light in a hallway where someone is walking toward you.

The rain fell in a perfect ring around it and did not fall on the valley floor. She could see the drowned trees standing in the flooded oxbow, silver with water, and above them the cloud's underside turning slowly, a carousel of dark, and between her and it, a corridor of clear air as deliberate as an aisle.

Ilsa Voss had spent twenty-two years studying how weather ignored people. That was the whole discipline, really: the atmosphere's magnificent indifference, its refusal of meaning. She had built her career on the comfort of that refusal. Weather did not care. Weather could not care. It was the one thing in the world that could not be flattered, bribed, or betrayed, and she had loved it for that the way other people loved God.

So when the wind off the valley lifted, turned, and came up the ridge to meet her—when it moved against every gradient she knew, a soft warm exhalation that smelled of rain and crushed leaves—and when, in the hiss of it, she heard the rhythm of her own breathing returned to her, she did what any honest scientist does with an impossible observation.

She assumed she was wrong.

She stood there until her legs ached and the light changed, and she catalogued every mundane explanation: pareidolia, wind through the drowned trees, an echo off the cloud base, her own exhaustion composing music out of noise. She wrote them all in her field book. She underlined none of them. Then she climbed down to camp and said nothing, and made breakfast, and watched her six colleagues eat and argue and complain about the humidity, and understood that she was now carrying something that would not fit back into the world they all still lived in.

* * *

The confirmation came on the fourth day, and it came through the radio, which was how she would remember it afterward—the irony of that, the cruelty of it, that the machine she would later order dismantled was the machine that taught her what she'd found.

Dr. Anselm had been running the daily atmospheric sounding from the lower camp, sending up the balloon with its little sensor package, reading the numbers back over the radio to the data recorder because the recorder's screen had cracked in the humidity. Routine. He stood in the clearing with the handset, reading: temperature, dewpoint, wind shear at altitude, the storm's inflow velocity. And Ilsa, sitting under the tarp with her charts, heard the wind off the valley change.

It came up through the trees in a long slow surge, and this time she was not alone to doubt herself, because everyone stopped. The porters looked up. Anselm lowered the handset mid-number. The wind moved through the camp like something arriving, and in it—under it, threaded through it—was Anselm's voice.

Not words. The cadence of words. The rise and fall of a man reading numbers aloud, played back through the wind at half speed, softened, the way a voice sounds through a wall, through water, through years. *Dewpoint... twenty-six... point... four...* The wind carried it up the slope toward the valley, toward the storm, and then it faded, and the ordinary rain-noise resumed, and the camp stood in the dripping silence the way people stand in a church.

"It's an echo," said Okonkwo, the geologist, too quickly. "Off the—there's a cliff formation, off the valley wall, it—"

"There's no cliff formation," Ilsa said. Her own voice sounded foreign to her. "The valley is a bowl. It doesn't return echoes. It absorbs them."

"Then it's—" He gestured at the sky, at everything, and ran out of geometry.

Anselm, to his credit, did not run out of anything. He raised the handset again and read the next line of the sounding, and the wind came again, and again it carried his voice back—slower, softer, and this time Ilsa was certain, with the terrible certainty she had spent her whole career refusing, that the playback had changed. It had caught the new numbers. It was not a recording of the last reading. It was listening, and answering, and the answer came a half-beat late, the way a reply comes when the listener is thinking.

"Stop," she said.

Anselm lowered the handset.

"Not on the radio. Not on any radio. Not again." She heard her own voice come out flat and cold and did not recognize it as hers. "Pack the instruments. All of them. Now."

* * *

They argued for six hours under the tarp while the rain rang on the canvas like a struck thing, and Ilsa won the argument the way she won most arguments, by being the one willing to say the unforgivable thing out loud.

Okonkwo wanted to transmit. Of course he did. A stationary supercell that responded to acoustic input was the discovery of the century; it rewrote convective dynamics, it rewrote the boundary between weather and— he kept almost saying the word *behavior* and catching himself, and every time he caught himself Ilsa loved him a little and feared him a little more. He wanted the satellite phone. He wanted the uplink. He wanted to be first.

"And then what?" Ilsa said.

"Then we publish. Then the world—"

"Then the world comes." She let the silence do a lap of the tarp. "Think about who comes, Okonkwo. Think about what a thing like this is *worth*, and then think about who in this world has the money to want it. You want to publish a living storm system to a planet that weaponizes weather in simulation because the real thing won't obey? We are sitting on the only object on Earth that can't be modeled, can't be owned, can't be predicted—and your plan is to tell everyone where it is."

"It's science," he said. "It doesn't belong to us."

"No," Ilsa said. "It doesn't. That's exactly why it can't be reported. The moment it's reported it belongs to whoever arrives with the most equipment. You know this. You've watched it happen to every river, every forest, every genome, every language on this planet. Discovery is not neutral. Discovery is a door, and doors only open one way."

Anselm sat with his hands folded, and when he finally spoke it was the question she'd been waiting for, the one that mattered. "How long have you known?"

The rain ticked. Ilsa weighed the truth and found it, for once, lighter than the lie.

"Since the ridge," she said. "I heard it breathing with me. I came down and said nothing because I thought I was mad. Then Anselm's numbers came back through the wind and I knew I wasn't mad, and I've spent four days trying to find the version of this where telling the world doesn't destroy it, and there isn't one. I've run it every way. There isn't one."

"You can't know that."

"I can know it the way you know a load rating," she said. "You don't test it past the point of failure. We are past the point of failure. Every hour we sit here with a working transmitter, we're gambling with something that has no replacement."

"And the radios," Anselm said quietly. "What exactly are you proposing?"

Ilsa looked at the equipment crates. Two HF sets, the satellite phone, the VHF handhelds, the data uplink. Forty thousand dollars of communication gear that had carried them safely upriver and now, in her arithmetic, was the most dangerous cargo in the watershed.

"I'm proposing we drown them," she said.

* * *

She did not order it done the way a commander orders. She did it the way a surgeon schedules: privately, in pairs, with reasons tailored to the listener, and she has never decided since whether that makes it worse or only more honest.

To Okonkwo she gave the science: the storm responded to radio-frequency and acoustic signatures, and every transmission was a stimulus they hadn't cleared. That was even true. She had watched the cloud's lightning cadence shift within minutes of the first HF check-in, three weeks ago, back when the storm was still a hundred kilometers south and they were reading it as coincidence. It heard them. It had been hearing them the whole way up the river, and it had come to rest above the valley the way an animal comes to rest near a sound it can't stop listening to. Every broadcast was a call it might answer by moving.

To the porters she gave the practical: the storm was unstable, transmissions attracted lightning, the ridge strikes were evidence enough. Mostly true. Mostly.

To Anselm she gave the truth, or the shape of it, and he took it the way he took everything, heavily, with both hands, and asked for one night to think. In the morning he said: "I'll help. But I want it on record—my record, in my own book—that the data was preserved. Everything. The soundings, the recordings, the barometric logs, the acoustic captures. If we destroy the ability to report this, we keep the ability to prove it. Someday. If it ever becomes safe."

"Nothing about it will ever be safe," Ilsa said.

"Then the data will wait," Anselm said, "as long as it has to. That's not a compromise, Ilsa. That's the whole job. We're not the owners. We're the custodians. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

She agreed because she had to agree, and because some cold honest part of her knew he was right, and she has thought about that bargain every day since, because it was the first fracture—not the breaking of the radios, which was easy, which took Okonkwo and the youngest porter one gray afternoon at the river's edge, the sets opened with screwdrivers, the boards snapped, the batteries spiked with saltwater, the whole sad expensive lot of it weighted with stones and lowered into the brown floodwater while the storm stood on the horizon and its lightning pulsed, slow and even, like something sleeping near a sound it loved.

The fracture was that she let Anselm keep the data.

She told herself it was the price of his help. She told herself custody was containment. She told herself a great many things in the weeks that followed, standing her watches on the ridge, learning the storm the way you learn a dangerous animal—by its hours, its moods, its appetites. She learned that it responded to voices the way a dark room responds to a lamp. She learned that if you spoke to it from the ridge, the wind would carry your words down into the valley and hold them there, and that on still evenings, in the clear corridor beneath the cloud, you could hear them again—your own sentences, softened, slowed, kept. She learned that it did not distinguish between a greeting and a distress call, a prayer and a coordinates fix. It kept everything. That was the horror of it, and the holiness, and she could not yet say which frightened her more.

She learned one other thing, in the third week, on the night the barometers finally moved.

The pressure over the valley shifted—not much, a single hectopascal, a breath—and the lightning's patient pulse stuttered, and the storm's attention swung south, downriver, the way a face turns toward a door. Ilsa stood on the ridge with the rain starting at last to fall inside the rain shadow, and she looked down the dark line of the Kessari toward the coast, toward the world of transmitters and uplinks and men with money, and she understood that something down there was talking about her storm.

She didn't know yet that it was a check-in window gone silent. She didn't know yet that a pilot named Felix Fairweather was arguing with a Syndicate handler at a river-mouth camp, or that the argument was going the way arguments go with people who have nothing left to lose.

She only knew that the storm had heard something, far away, and turned toward it—and that whatever came up that river would find the radios drowned, the camp empty, and a barefoot trail leading into the flooded trees, because she had made sure of the trail herself, walking it at dusk, planting it like a message: *gone, all gone, nothing here but water.*

She stood in the rising rain and said, to the valley, to the thing that kept every voice: "No one is coming. Forget them."

And the wind came up the ridge, soft and warm, and carried her voice down into the dark, and kept it.
