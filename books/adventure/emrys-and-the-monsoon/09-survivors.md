## 9. Survivors

The rain shadow was the first wrong thing.

Felix had been walking for six hours through forest that drowned and surfaced and drowned again, water to her knees in places, leeches finding every gap in her boots, and then—abruptly—the rain stopped. Not thinned. Stopped. She stood on a ridge of black stone with water streaming off her sleeves into air that was simply dry, and above her the storm turned like a lighthouse beam seen from underneath, its flanks lit from within by lightning that pulsed in slow, even intervals. Four seconds apart. She'd been counting since the crash site. It never varied.

Beyond the ridge, the land sloped down into a bowl of shattered limestone, and at the base of it, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging roots, there was light.

Not firelight. Too steady, too white. Generator light.

Her hand went to the machete she'd taken from the abandoned camp—not because she expected to use it, but because holding something with an edge made the walking feel less like surrender. She descended sideways, testing each foothold, and the smell reached her before the cave did: woodsmoke, yes, but also coffee, and under that something almost domestic. Soap. Someone down here was washing things.

The cave mouth was tall enough to walk into upright, which felt like an insult after the day she'd had. Inside, the passage opened into a chamber the size of a chapel, and the white light came from two LED panels rigged to a battery bank, and beneath them, on folding camp chairs arranged around a table made of equipment crates, four people were playing cards.

They looked up at her the way you look up when a waiter arrives. Mildly. Without alarm.

Felix stood in the entrance dripping river water, mud to her thighs, a machete in one hand and six hours of terror on her face, and a woman with gray-streaked hair tied back in a knot said, "You'll want the chair by the heater. The other ones wobble."

* * *

It took twenty minutes before anyone told Felix their names, and longer than that before anyone seemed to understand why she'd come.

There were four of them: Dr. Miriam Anselm, the expedition lead, sixty-ish, with the forearms of someone who'd spent decades hauling core samples; the gray-haired woman, who introduced herself as Tomás Okonkwo, geophysicist, and corrected nothing when Felix got the pronouns wrong twice; a young seismologist named Priya Raghavan who kept glancing toward the cave mouth whenever the thunder rolled through; and a quiet man called Dele who had been the expedition's cook and, from the state of the table, still was. He put a tin mug of coffee in Felix's hands without being asked and it was, absurdly, good coffee.

"Seven," Felix said. "The manifest says seven. Where are the others?"

Anselm set down her cards. "Ilsa took two of them deeper. Toward the eye. And three went downriver."

"Downriver? When?"

"Eleven days ago." Anselm's voice was level, but her hands weren't; she folded them on the crate-table as if pinning them there. "We sent them for help. They should have reached the second checkpoint by now."

"They didn't reach anything." Felix heard her own voice go flat, the way it did when she read weather reports for crashes she'd have to identify. "Base camp's been dark for three weeks. No check-ins, no backup window. The Syndicate told me to wait it out. I didn't."

"The Syndicate." Anselm repeated the word like she was tasting it. "You're contracted to the lenders?"

"I'm contracted to whoever pays. Right now that's your expedition's insurance bond, which is underwritten by—" She stopped, because Anselm's expression had changed, and not the way faces usually change. It was the way a barometer changes. A slow, total shift in pressure. "You knew. You knew who was holding paper on this trip."

"I suspected." Anselm looked at the storm-light flickering faintly at the cave mouth. "Which is part of why we're having this conversation in a cave instead of on a radio."

* * *

The strangeness of them accumulated slowly, the way floodwater does—you don't notice the rise until it's at your chest.

They were healthy. That was the first thing. Three weeks past their last contact, in monsoon country, and they were tanned, well-fed, moving easily. Dele had built a smoke rack. Priya had a garden going in a hollow of wet stone—actual greens, actual beans, growing in soil that shouldn't have supported anything this far into a flooded valley. Tomás had repaired the generator twice and read the same battered paperback four times and said so cheerfully.

"You've been resupplied?" Felix asked.

"By what?" Tomás laughed. "There's nothing up here but us and the weather."

"Then how—"

"We eat what the valley gives." Anselm said it plainly, without mysticism, which somehow made it worse. "Fish come up the flood channels. The soil in the rain shadow is the richest I've ever sampled. It rains everywhere else and here it doesn't, and everything that needs water grows at the margin, and everything that needs dry grows in the center. This valley is the most habitable place on the river, Felix. That's not an accident. That's architecture."

"Architecture by what?"

Anselm didn't answer directly. She stood, took a lantern, and led Felix deeper into the cave system, past the sleeping alcoves, past a wall where someone had taped up hand-drawn charts, to a narrow throat of stone at the back where the air moved cool and constant. Through a crack in the rock, maybe forty meters of winding passage away, they could hear it: the storm. Not muffled. Distinct. Every thunderclap arriving with the clarity of a struck bell.

"It comes through the limestone," Anselm said. "The whole cave system is a resonating body. We hear it better in here than outside." She paused. "And it hears us."

"Hears—"

"When we first sheltered here, after the radios, Priya sang to keep her nerve. Old songs, whatever came into her head. And the lightning intervals shifted." Anselm held up four fingers. "Four seconds. It had been seven since we arrived. It stayed at four for the whole hour she sang, and went back to seven when she stopped. We've replicated it eleven times since. Speech works too. Music works best. It isn't random response—it's attention. The way a person turns toward a voice in a dark room."

Felix set down her coffee because her hands had decided they couldn't be trusted with it. "Dr. Anselm. What you're describing is a weather system with auditory processing. That's not a thing. That is not a thing that exists."

"No," Anselm agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. And yet."

"And you've been sitting down here for three weeks instead of reporting it because—"

"Because of exactly what you just did." Anselm's eyes were very steady. "You walked into this cave six hours ago and your third question was about ownership. Your insurance bond. Who holds paper. You couldn't help it—it's how you survive, I don't blame you—but think about what happens when the people who hold my paper find out there's a mind over this valley. Think about what a debt collector does with a god."

* * *

She argued with them until midnight, and lost.

That was the honest shape of it. Felix laid out the case the way she'd lay out a flight plan: the missing three downriver, possibly dead or worse; the Syndicate already decoding fragments of the early transmissions—she'd heard Bright say so herself, that cheerful vulture; the expedition declared overdue, which meant search parties, which meant more boats, more planes, more noise moving up the valley whether these four liked it or not. Staying hidden wasn't hiding. Staying hidden was just letting other people find you first.

Tomás nodded along reasonably and then said, "All true. And we're still not leaving."

"Why?"

"Because you can't evacuate a colleague."

"A colleague. It's a storm."

"It's a storm that has learned four songs," Priya said quietly, from her corner. She hadn't spoken much all evening, and when she did, everyone listened. "It hums them back during the four-second intervals if you listen through the limestone. Badly. Like a child. Three weeks ago it couldn't do that at all." She hugged her knees. "I know what it sounds like when I say it out loud. But I've spent every day of my career listening to the earth make noises, and I'm telling you: something down here is learning, and it learns faster when we're kind to it, and I am not going to be the person who walks away from that."

"Kindness," Felix said. "You want me to fly back through a squall line that rotates backwards and tell the Syndicate the survivors declined rescue because they're being kind to the hurricane."

"We want you to tell them we're dead," Tomás said.

The words dropped into the room and sat there.

"That's what Ilsa wanted from the beginning," Anselm said. "No signal, no report, no coordinates. Let the expedition die on paper. Seven names, closed file. Whatever lives in this valley stays unmeasured, unmapped, unowned." She spread her hands. "I disagreed with her methods—Ilsa destroyed our radios while we slept, which is theft and mutiny and probably the only reason any of us are alive—and I sent three people downriver against her wishes because I believed the world deserved to weigh this discovery. I still believe that. But I no longer believe the world will weigh it fairly. Not with your Syndicate holding the scale."

"So what do you believe now?"

Anselm was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the thunder came through the stone, four seconds, four seconds, patient as a heartbeat.

"I believe I owe those three people I sent downriver a debt I can't pay if they're dead," she said finally. "And I believe that if they died carrying word of this place, then the file is already open, and hiding is theater. So: help me find out what happened to mine, and I'll decide what happens to yours."

* * *

Felix slept badly on a foam pad that smelled of mildew and woodsmoke, and woke once in the deep night to a sound she couldn't place—low, wavering, rising and falling. For a foggy moment she thought someone was crying. Then she realized it was coming through the limestone itself, from the direction of the valley floor, and that it was the storm: a long tonal moan riding under the thunder, almost melodic, almost familiar.

Priya was awake too, sitting against the far wall with her arms around her knees.

"What is it doing?" Felix whispered.

Priya listened for a while. "It's practicing," she said. "That's the third song. The one I sang yesterday." Her face in the dim generator light was young and old at once. "It's getting better."

In the morning, Felix found Anselm at the cave mouth, watching the tower of cloud stand fixed against the spinning sky, and told her the truth she'd been carrying since the abandoned camp: the dismantled radios, the barefoot print leading into the flooded forest, the surgical care of it all. "Your botanist didn't run," Felix said. "She escorted. Two of your people went with her willingly, or convinced enough to fake it. If I'm going to help you find your three downriver, you need to tell me where Ilsa would go, because I have a feeling the answer to everything in this valley is standing right over it."

Anselm looked up at the storm for a long time.

"She'd go to the eye," she said. "Ilsa always said the middle of a thing is where it keeps its reasons." She turned back, and for the first time since Felix had arrived, the expedition leader looked her age, and tired, and afraid. "Pack light. And Felix—if we find her, let me talk first. She trusted me once, before the discovery. I'd like to see if there's anything left of that woman inside whatever she's become out here."

Above them, unhurried, the lightning counted out its four seconds, and somewhere in the stone beneath their feet, the storm practiced its third song.
