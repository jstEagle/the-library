## 10. Bright Ideas

The rain at the river mouth came down like a debt collector—patient, inevitable, and personally invested in your discomfort.

Bright stood under the tarpaulin awning of base camp with his shoes off, because wet leather was a sin against good sense, and watched the Kessari shove brown water past the moored barges as if it had somewhere better to be. Behind him, inside the comms tent, three of his people hunched over a decoding rig that cost more than the entire expedition they were eavesdropping on, and one of them was laughing.

Bright did not like laughter in the work tent unless he understood its cause. He ducked inside.

"Something funny, Pem?"

Pem Oduya was twenty-six, wire-thin, and had the particular genius of being able to hear structure in noise the way other people heard melody. She'd been pulling fragments out of the expedition's early transmissions for nine days—weak signal, atmospheric scatter, half the packets shredded by the monsoon itself—but tonight she'd finally cracked the compression scheme the scientists were using, and she was laughing at her screen like it had told her a joke.

"Oh, it's funny," she said. "It's funny the way finding out your neighbor has been sitting on a gold mine is funny." She turned the monitor toward him. "The expedition's telemetry headers. They've been broadcasting their instrument readings this whole time, embedded in the check-ins. Weather data, mostly. Barometric pressure, wind vectors, electrical activity."

"Weather data," Bright repeated. "We hired a weather pilot to support a weather expedition. I'm shocked."

"Not weather data." Pem tapped the screen. "Look at the station coordinates. Then look at where the readings are coming from."

He looked. The expedition's route ran upriver along the mapped stations—the standard survey grid, every hydrology outfit in the delta used some version of it. But for the last six weeks of transmissions, the instrument cluster hadn't moved. It sat at a single set of coordinates deep in the drowned valley country, and the readings attached to those coordinates were—

"They're wrong," Bright said.

"They're impossible," Pem corrected, delighted. "Barometric differential of eleven millibars over four hundred meters, sustained. That's not a gradient, that's a wall. Wind shear values that would shred any aircraft we own. And the electrical activity—" she pulled up a graph, a jagged spine of lightning-strike counts, "—is periodic. Not random. Periodic. Storms don't do schedules, Mr. Bright. This one does."

Bright pulled a folding stool closer and sat, which was what he did when something deserved his full attention. The Vane Syndicate had sent him upriver because he was good at three things: reading people, reading numbers, and knowing which was lying. Right now the numbers were telling him something extraordinary, and the person who'd found it was grinning like a child at a fair.

"Talk me through it," he said. "Like I'm an optimist."

"You *are* an optimist. That's literally your job title." Pem spun back to her keyboard. "Okay. A supercell is a heat engine. It needs fuel—warm moist air feeding in at the bottom, cold air exhausting out the top—and it needs to move, because it eats through its own fuel and has to travel to find more. A storm that stays put dies in hours. Days if it's lucky and the geography feeds it."

"And this one?"

"This one has been sitting on the same coordinates for—" she scrolled through reconstructed logs, "—at least four months, based on the earliest fragments. Possibly longer; the satellite archive shows a persistent convective cell over that valley going back to last dry season. Everyone wrote it off as sensor error because a stationary storm that size is a contradiction in terms." She looked up at him. "It's not dying, Mr. Bright. It's not even hungry. The energy budget doesn't balance unless you assume it's drawing power from somewhere we can't measure."

"Or somewhere we haven't thought to measure."

"Or that." She hesitated, and for the first time the grin flickered into something more careful. "There's one more thing. The voice traffic. Most of the expedition's audio is garbage—we get maybe one word in ten through the interference. But there's a pattern in what survives." She put on her headphones, queued a clip, and turned the speaker volume up.

Static. A man's voice, shredded: "*—responding—it turned when Anselm—*"

More static. A woman's voice, tighter, angrier: "*—shut it down, all of it, before—*"

Then a third fragment, and this one made the hair on Bright's forearms stand up despite the humid heat: a chorus of overlapping voices, distorted almost past recognition, but rhythmic. Almost conversational. Like wind through a canyon, if the canyon were trying to say something.

"That last one," Pem said quietly, "isn't from any radio we can identify. It's bleed-through. Something up there is transmitting on the expedition's frequency, or close enough to it, and whatever it's saying, it sounds like voices."

* * *

Bright slept badly, which was unusual for him. He'd built a career on the principle that most problems looked smaller at dawn, and most of the rest could be solved with breakfast and a smile. But he lay in his bunk on the barge *Vane's Fortune* listening to the rain hammer the deck plates and turned the fragments over in his mind like stones in a hand.

The Syndicate's business model was simple, and Bright could recite it in his sleep because he'd helped write it: *everything is an asset, and every asset has a price.* Land, cargo, reputations, secrets, people. The Vane family had started generations ago lending money against grain harvests in a port town nobody remembered, and they'd learned early that the most profitable collateral was the kind that didn't know it was collateral yet. Information was best of all. Information could be sold repeatedly, to multiple buyers, none of whom knew about the others.

A storm that held still. A storm that spoke.

He got up at 3 a.m., dressed, and walked the wet deck to the comms tent, where Pem was still working—she worked the way rivers flowed, continuously and without apparent effort—and asked her the only question that mattered.

"If this is real, what's it worth?"

Pem took off her headphones. She didn't ask which part he meant. That was why he liked her.

"The weather market alone," she said. "Insurance, agriculture, shipping—a system that can be steered, or even just predicted at that resolution, is worth more than the shipping industry it protects. Militaries would pay obscene amounts just to deny it to each other. And that's before anyone figures out how it works." She paused. "If it responds to sound—if it can be *addressed*—then someone, someday, gives it orders. And whoever owns that capability owns the sky."

"Over their territory."

"Over everyone's territory. Weather doesn't respect borders, Mr. Bright. That's the whole point. That's the whole *product*."

He stood there in the dripping dark of the tent, watching the graph of lightning strikes march across the screen in its patient, metronome rhythm, and felt the rare, clean joy of a man who has just realized the size of the thing in front of him.

"Pack it up," he said. "We're going upriver."

* * *

The message from Head Office arrived by courier launch two days later, sealed, because the Syndicate never trusted radio for anything that mattered. Bright read it twice in his cabin while the rain kept up its drumming.

*Fragmentary intelligence suggests anomalous meteorological phenomenon in upper Kessari watershed. Expedition status unknown; assume compromised. You are granted full salvage authority. Secure the phenomenon or proof thereof. Expenditure ceiling raised. Personnel losses acceptable within reason. Note: Fairweather, F., remains contracted as aviation support. If contact is made, assess her reliability. If unreliable, assess her as liability.*

Bright read the last line three times. *Assess her as liability.* In Syndicate language that meant exactly what it sounded like, and the fact that Head Office had put it in writing meant they wanted deniability later, which meant they expected this to become a story someone told.

Felix Fairweather. He'd found her in Port Calloway, three weeks from ruin, and hired her with a smile and a contract whose fine print she'd deliberately skipped—he'd watched her skip it, watched her make the choice to not-know, and filed that away as useful information. She flew like a woman with nothing to lose, which made her either the best pilot he'd ever employed or the most dangerous. Possibly both. She'd gone upriver with seven scientists and come back down with nothing but silence, and now Head Office wanted him to decide whether she was a tool or a problem.

He folded the letter, fed it to the cabin's little alcohol stove, and watched it curl into ash. Then he went topside and started making lists, because optimism without logistics was just wishing.

* * *

They left for the upriver run five days later: two barges, forty personnel, and enough equipment to outfit a small war. Bright had argued the manifest himself over a crackling satellite call—generators, winches, a portable met tower, hardened sensor arrays, cages of recording gear rated for electromagnetic extremes, and a machine shop that could build anything the sensor arrays couldn't survive. He'd also requested, and received, twelve men whose contracts described them as *site security* and whose manner suggested they'd done site security in places where sites needed securing aggressively.

The river changed character as they went up. The lower Kessari was wide and sluggish, a brown road through drowned farmland, but by the third day the banks closed in, the current quickened, and the rain stopped being weather and became climate—an ambient condition, like gravity. The crew grew quiet. Even the security men stopped joking. There was something about traveling deeper into a monsoon instead of waiting for it to pass that wore on a person, Bright observed, like being slowly convinced the world had always been underwater and always would be.

On the fifth day, the scout boat reported wreckage on the eastern bank: the expedition's forward supply cache, torn open, crates scattered, everything soaked but nothing looted. No signs of violence. No signs of people at all.

On the seventh day, they passed the place where Felix Fairweather's plane should have been able to land, and Bright stood at the barge rail looking at the squall line stacked across the horizon ahead of them and understood, for the first time viscerally, what the numbers had been trying to tell him.

It didn't look wrong, exactly. That was the horror of it. It looked like a thunderstorm—a big one, a serious one, the kind of wall of cloud that made pilots turn around and pray. But it was *organized* in a way storms weren't. Its flanks were too clean. Its rotation, visible even from here as a slow corkscrewing of the cloud base, was too steady, like a thing spinning on bearings rather than boiling on convection. And it simply did not move. The barges traveled all day, and the storm stayed exactly where it was, hanging over the drowned valley like a lamp over a table.

Pem came to stand beside him at the rail, her instruments cradled against the spray.

"It's bigger than the fragments suggested," she said. "The energy flux is off the scale. Mr. Bright—there's no natural mechanism for this. None. Whatever's holding it there, it isn't geography."

"What do you think it is?"

She was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked against her equipment cases.

"I think," she said finally, "that we should be very careful about letting it know we're here."

Bright smiled at her, because smiling was his job and because the alternative expression wasn't professional. But he gave the order anyway: engines idled after dusk, no generators, no radios above minimum power, no shouting on deck. Forty people crept up a river toward a storm, whispering.

That night, anchored in a bend of black water with the storm's distant lightning strobing green through the rain curtains, Bright sat in his cabin and drafted his report to Head Office by lamplight.

*Phenomenon confirmed visually. Scale exceeds projections. Expedition fate unknown; recommend treating survivors as sources, not principals. Fairweather status unresolved. Requesting clarification on containment parameters: do we secure the phenomenon itself, or the knowledge of it? These may require different methods.*

He considered the second question longer than the first. The Syndicate had salvaged ships, cargoes, companies, occasionally reputations. They had never salvaged a piece of the sky. Somewhere below the professional calm, Bright was aware of a feeling he rarely permitted himself: the sense of standing at the edge of a map, where the careful annotations stop and the blank paper begins.

He sealed the report for the return courier and stepped out onto the deck for air.

The storm filled the northern sky, silent at this distance, its internal lightning pulsing in that steady, scheduled rhythm—bright, bright, pause; bright, bright, pause—like a lighthouse. Like a signal. Like something keeping time for an audience it believed in.

Pem's warning sat in his chest alongside the excitement, two passengers who refused to share a seat. *Careful about letting it know we're here.*

But that was the trouble with salvage, Bright thought, watching the light pulse across the water. Sooner or later, you had to knock.

Downriver behind them, at the mouth of the Kessari, a battered single-engine plane that shouldn't have been airborne in this weather lifted off the flooded strip at base camp and turned north, alone, into the wall of rain—flying straight toward everything the Syndicate had come to take, and carrying the one thing neither the Syndicate nor the storm yet knew about: a pilot named Felix Fairweather, who had already decided, somewhere over the black water, that she wasn't going home empty-handed.

Bright learned about the departure an hour later, from a very nervous radio operator, and stood for a long moment in the rain with his smile fixed in place like a hat in high wind.

"Well," he said, to no one in particular. "Isn't that interesting."
