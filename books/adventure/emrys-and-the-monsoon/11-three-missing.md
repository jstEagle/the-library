## 11. Three Missing

The cave smelled of woodsmoke and wet limestone, and Dr. Tomas Anselm had spent nine days pretending that was enough.

He sat near the entrance with a tin cup of river water gone lukewarm in his hands, watching Felix Fairweather sleep. She'd arrived half-dead two days ago—mud to the collarbone, a gash above her ear crusted brown, hands shaking so badly she'd dropped her cup twice at dinner and laughed about it both times, a bright brittle laugh that fooled no one. The team had taken to her the way drowning men take to driftwood. Even Priya, who trusted nothing with wings since the radios died, had started sitting close enough to touch her sleeve.

That was the problem. They were all reaching for something solid, and Anselm was about to tell them the ground was made of paper.

Felix stirred against the wall where she'd wedged herself between two supply crates. Her eyes opened without transition—asleep to fully awake, no fog between—and found him across the fire. Storm chaser's reflexes, he thought. You don't survive weather by sleeping deeply.

"You're up," she said.

"I'm up."

"Then either the storm's doing something new or you've been rehearsing whatever face you're making." She sat up, wincing, and came to sit beside him at the mouth of the cave. Beyond the overhang, the rain fell in its endless gray curtain, and beyond the rain—the thing they did not look at directly anymore, the way you don't stare at the sun—rose the tower of cloud, fixed and patient, lightning crawling its flanks in slow regular pulses like breath.

Anselm turned his cup in his hands. "I need to tell you something before you go back downriver."

"Go back? I just got here."

"And you'll want to leave faster after I say it. So I'd rather say it now, while your legs still hurt too much to run."

She looked at him then, really looked, and he saw her decide not to joke. It was one of the things he'd noticed about her in two days: she was fluent in deflection but capable of setting it down like a heavy pack when the trail got steep.

"Three weeks ago," he said, "I sent three people downriver. Okafor, Lindqvist, and Marisol. In the raft we'd cached at the second bend. With a message for the outside world."

The rain filled the silence for a moment. Somewhere behind them in the cave, Priya turned over in her sleep.

"Against whose wishes?" Felix asked quietly.

"Dr. Voss's." He heard how it sounded—Ilsa's name in his mouth had become a weight he carried differently than the others carried it. "You met her manifest. Botanist. Cartographer's daughter. She knew the river better than our charts did, and I thought that was charming. A woman who read water the way I read pressure gradients." He laughed, without humor. "I should have understood what it meant. Someone who knows a landscape that well has usually been listening to it longer than everyone else."

"Felix," he said, and stopped, because saying her name was easier than saying the rest, and he was tired of taking the easy road. "We found it on day eleven. The standing cell. Forty kilometers of supercell that hasn't moved since the satellite record began—which is wrong, structurally impossible, it should have collapsed or drifted within hours. We set up instruments. And by the third night Ilsa had already figured out what took me another week to believe."

"That it listens."

He looked at her sharply. "She told you?"

"She told me the wind answers back. I shouted into it yesterday, walking in. Something shouted my own cadence back at me." Felix's jaw tightened. "I assumed exhaustion. Then I assumed a lot of other things. None of them held."

"It responds to voice. To patterned sound. Not like an echo—" he held up a hand before she could interrupt— "not like an echo. Like attention. You speak into the inflow and the turbulence reorganizes around what you said. The first time, we thought it was coincidence. The tenth time, we stopped thinking anything and just stood there feeling *seen*." His hands had begun to shake; he wrapped them around the cup. "And Ilsa said, very calmly, 'Take down the radios.'"

"She ordered the equipment destroyed."

"She dismantled every transmitter with surgical care while we slept. Antennas coiled neat as hair. Batteries buried. When I confronted her she didn't deny it. She said—" and here Anselm closed his eyes, because he had replayed this conversation so many times it had worn grooves in him— "she said, 'Tomas, the moment this reaches a radio tower, it stops being a discovery and becomes an asset. And assets get owned. Do you want to find out what a weapons program does with a storm that answers?'"

"And you disagreed."

"I believed science survives sunlight. I still mostly believe it. But Ilsa had done something I hadn't: she'd imagined the specific future. Syndicates. Militaries. Salvage crews with cages of recording equipment." He glanced sideways at Felix. "People like whoever paid for your fuel."

Felix didn't flinch, which told him she'd already done her own accounting of who she worked for. Good. That would make the rest survivable.

"So I did it in secret," he said. "Okafor, Lindqvist, Marisol. Our strongest, our fastest talker, and our best rafter. I gave them the data—raw, uninterpreted, honest—and I sent them downriver to the relay station at Kessari Mouth with instructions to transmit everything. I told them if anyone asked, they were carrying botanical samples for quarantine." He set the cup down because his grip had gone unreliable. "They left at dawn on day nineteen. The raft had fuel for four days' running. The river was high but manageable. It should have taken six days."

"How long ago?"

"Nineteen days."

Felix was quiet for a long moment. He watched her do the arithmetic he'd done a hundred times, the same numbers yielding the same terrible sum. Nineteen days. No check-in. No raft sighted. And Felix herself had flown through a squall line that shouldn't exist, building wrong, rotating wrong—

"The storm," she said slowly. "It's been swelling. Building off its anchor. You think—"

"I don't know what I think." His voice cracked and he hated it. "I know that three days after they left, the cell grew thirty percent. I know that the rain shadow shifted and flooded the second bend, where their put-in was. I know that Ilsa stood at the cave mouth that morning and watched the water rise and said nothing to me, and her saying nothing was worse than any accusation. Because I couldn't tell whether she'd known it would happen, or whether she simply thought we deserved whatever the river decided." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I keep landing on the first one. I keep landing there because the alternative is that I killed three people for nothing."

* * *

Felix let him sit with it. He was grateful for that; the ones who rushed to comfort always wanted the wound to be smaller than it was.

When he could speak again, she said, "There are seven of you on the manifest. I've counted five here. You, Priya, Devi, Okonkwo, and Halloran. Where are the other two?"

And there it was. The second confession, the one that had been sitting in his chest like swallowed glass.

"Ilsa took them. Chen and Adeyemi. Nine days ago, upriver. Toward the eye."

"Toward the—" Felix turned to look at the storm full-on, and Anselm watched her do what he'd trained himself out of weeks ago: meet the thing's gaze. Lightning pulsed along the cloud base in that slow, deliberate rhythm, and even from here, even through the rain, he felt the old vertigo, the sense of a vast attention turning its face toward them the way a person turns toward a whisper in a quiet room.

"There's a path," he said. "Through the drowned forest, along the ridge spine. Ilsa found it—or it found her, I've never been sure which. It leads into the calm center. The eye. She called it—" he searched for the phrase, remembering Chen's report before they left, the young geologist's voice shaking with something that wasn't fear— "she called it a room. Still air. Sound behaves strangely inside. Voices persist. You can hear things that were said there days ago, as if the air keeps them." He looked at his hands. "Chen said Ilsa walked in and the storm went quiet around her. Not calm. Quiet. Like it was listening to only her."

"Why would she take people deeper? If her whole position is seal it, hide it, protect it—why bring witnesses into the archive?"

"Because she needs them," Anselm said. "Or because she believes they're safer inside her argument than outside it. I honestly don't know anymore. Chen went willingly—he'd started dreaming in the storm's pulse rhythm, he said, and it frightened him less than it should have. Adeyemi..." He hesitated. "Adeyemi followed Ilsa the way sailors follow lighthouses. I think she believed Ilsa understood something the rest of us refused to. Maybe she was right. That's the hell of it. Every fork in this story, I chose the reasonable branch, and the reasonable branches keep ending in floodwater."

Felix pulled her knees up, staring out at the rain. "So the team split. Downriver versus deeper in. Report it versus bury it."

"Not exactly." He shook his head. "It's worse than a split. Splits have sides. This is more like—" he groped for it— "like a fault line running through each person individually. Priya wants to leave, but she won't abandon me. Devi thinks Ilsa is right but hates her for being right. Okonkwo hasn't spoken in four days except to ask whether the storm can hear us in here, and I don't have an answer that helps him sleep. And Halloran—" he exhaled— "Halloran keeps the fire going and pretends we're a camping trip that ran long. Everyone is loyal to someone and truthful to no one, including themselves. Including me." He turned to face her fully. "Including you, pilot. You flew against orders to get here. Whose orders matter to you—the Syndicate's, or yours? Because I promise you, when your employers learn what's upriver, and they will, that question stops being rhetorical."

* * *

She didn't answer right away, and Anselm respected the silence. Outside, the monsoon hammered its steady anvil rhythm on the world. The storm's pulse rode beneath it, slower, deeper, like a second heartbeat under the first.

Finally Felix said, "The three you sent. Okafor, Lindqvist, Marisol. What do you want me to do about them?"

"Nothing. There's nothing to do." The words cost him; he paid them anyway. "Nineteen days. If they made it past the flood, they'd be in Port Calloway by now, and the whole coast would be crawling with hydrologists and journalists and men in unmarked boats. Instead the sky above the middle river is building itself into something none of my models can hold. Either they never made it past the bend, or—" he stopped.

"Or they talked," Felix finished softly. "To someone. On a radio somewhere downriver. And whatever heard them is still hearing them."

Anselm felt the cold move through him that had become familiar these past weeks, the cold of hypotheses he wished were wrong. "Ilsa warned me. Not with threats—with physics. She said, 'Every transmission is a bell. You don't get to ring a bell once.' I thought she was being mystical." He laughed, a dry broken sound. "She was being literal. She's always literal. That's what makes her terrifying."

"So we assume the worst. Assume the message went out. Assume someone decoded fragments." Felix's eyes had gone hard and distant, calculating winds. "Then it's not a question of whether the outside comes looking. It's when, and how armed."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me this now, two days after I crash-landed, because...?"

"Because you're the only one of us who can leave," Anselm said plainly. "And because I owe you the truth before you choose. I sent three people into that river carrying the truth, and the truth hasn't come back. I will not send a fourth." He reached into his shirt and drew out the oilskin packet he'd kept against his sternum for nineteen days, through flood watches and Ilsa's silences and Halloran's cheerful denial—his field notebook, and folded inside it, the raw data. Pressure series. Acoustic spectra. The pulse intervals, annotated in his own hand, going back to day eleven. Everything Okafor would have carried, duplicated.

He held it out.

Felix looked at it without taking it. "What is it?"

"The truth. Uninterpreted. Heavy." His arm didn't waver. "I don't know yet whether it should go downriver or stay buried—that fight is still alive in me, and I'm no longer sure which side I'm on. But I know I can't be the only one holding it. I've seen what happens to the only one holding something. Ilsa is up in that eye right now becoming the only one holding something, and every day she's up there she gets further from being a person I could argue with." His voice dropped. "Take it. Or don't. But know that whichever you choose, you're choosing a side in a war that hasn't declared itself yet."

For a long moment Felix didn't move. Then, out past the rain curtain, the storm changed.

Anselm felt it before he understood it—a shift in the pressure against his eardrums, a faltering in the great slow pulse. Both of them turned. Along the cloud base, the lightning had stopped its patient breathing. The flanks of the tower were *moving*, columns of cloud detaching and reattaching, and beneath the monsoon's drumming came a new sound, low and vast, rising from the direction of the valley like a note held on an instrument too large to see.

Priya sat bolt upright in the dark of the cave. "Doctor," she said, in a voice stripped of everything but fact. "It's never done that before."

Felix was already on her feet, the oilskin packet forgotten between them on the stone, and Anselm watched her face as she listened to the deepening sound and understood, as he understood, that the choice he'd just laid at her feet had just been overtaken by events.

"It's answering something," she said.

"No," Anselm whispered, the cold flooding back through him, colder and more certain than any hypothesis he'd ever held. "It's *remembering*. And Ilsa is up there showing it what to remember."
