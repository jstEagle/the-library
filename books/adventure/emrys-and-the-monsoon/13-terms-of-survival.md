## 13. Terms of Survival

Ilsa Voss had spent thirty years learning how to disappear from a landscape without leaving a mark, and in all that time she had never once considered that the hardest person to hide from would be a debt-ridden pilot with mud to her knees and a look in her eyes like a woman who had already made up her mind about something terrible.

Felix Fairweather stood at the mouth of the cave, dripping river water onto stone that hadn't seen direct rain in four months. Behind her, the storm filled the sky over the drowned valley like a cathedral that had decided to become a fist. Lightning crawled its flanks in slow, deliberate spirals—too regular, too patient—and Ilsa watched Felix watch it, and knew the pilot was doing the same arithmetic she had done three weeks ago: *that is not weather. Weather does not wait.*

"You've been up here two days," Ilsa said, not turning from the rain-shadow view. "You found Anselm's people. You found the camp. And you didn't radio anyone."

"The radios are dismantled."

"Yours too."

"My plane's a wreck and my radio went through a squall line sideways." Felix came further into the cave, boots squelching, and stopped an arm's length away—the distance of someone who understood that proximity was a negotiation. "But yes. Mine too. On purpose."

Ilsa turned then. She let herself study the woman properly: the salt-cracked hands, the flight jacket gone gray at the elbows, the way her weight stayed on her back foot even standing still, as if every room were a runway she might need to leave. A runner. Ilsa had read the file Bright's people kept on her—read it twice, once for the debts and once for the pattern. Felix Fairweather did not stay anywhere. Not towns, not contracts, not trouble.

Which meant the offer had to be built exactly right.

"You shouted at it," Ilsa said. "At the storm. Yesterday, on the ridge above the valley. I watched you."

Felix's jaw tightened. "You were watching me."

"I watch everyone near it." She said it flatly, without apology. "What did it do?"

A long silence. Rain hissed against the rock outside, a curtain of sound that ended precisely where the cave began, as if the storm had drawn a line around this place and honored it.

"It answered," Felix said finally. "I shouted—just nonsense, testing—and the wind came back with the same rhythm. Same cadence. Like..." She shook her head, hating the word before she said it. "Like an echo that thought about it first."

"Yes." Ilsa moved to the cave's rear wall, where her field kit sat open among instruments she had stopped trusting weeks ago. "That's what it does. That's what it *is*, if you want the truth of it. And now you understand why there are no radios in this valley."

* * *

She told him the story first—or rather, she told Felix the story of telling Anselm, because that was the version that mattered. Three weeks ago, sitting in the last intact tent while the standing supercell burned lightning off its western flank in patterns that repeated every eleven minutes, Ilsa had laid out the data and watched Dr. Anselm's face go through wonder and arrive somewhere else entirely.

"He wanted to transmit immediately," she said now. "Coordinates, spectral readings, the acoustic response profiles. He said the world needed to know. He used that phrase—*the world needs to know*—like it was a key that opened doors instead of one that broke them."

"And you disagreed."

"I've watched things get known, Pilot." Ilsa pulled a battered notebook from the kit—not her official log, the other one, the one with no institution stamp—and set it on the crate between them. "I was in the Congo basin when a pharmaceutical consortium 'discovered' a fungal compound the local healers had tended for two hundred years. Within eighteen months the healers couldn't afford to walk their own forest. I was in the Andes when a glacier got measured, and measuring became monitoring, and monitoring became a mining company's water rights claim filed before the first paper was even published." She tapped the notebook. "Anything measured gets owned. That is not cynicism. That is the entire history of science funding."

"So your plan is what? Keep it secret forever?" Felix gestured at the storm through the cave mouth. "It's a supercell anchored over a valley like a—like a lighthouse. Someone was going to find it eventually. Satellite passes. Shipping lanes. It doesn't exactly hide."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "It doesn't hide. But there's a difference between something being seen and something being *known*. Right now, if a satellite caught it, it would be logged as an anomaly—a mesoscale curiosity, a footnote. There are dozens of unexplained convective features in the tropical belt. But if we transmit what we know—if the acoustic data reaches a database—" she met Felix's eyes, "—then it stops being weather. It becomes an asset. Something that responds to human attention can be trained to respond to human commands. You understand who pays for that kind of research. You owe them money."

The words landed the way she'd intended, precise as a scalpel laid flat on a table. Felix flinched, but she didn't deny it, and Ilsa gave her credit for that.

"The Vane Syndicate," Felix said quietly. "You know about my debts."

"I know Bright decoded fragments of the expedition's early transmissions before I killed the relay. I know what he'll do with the rest if he gets it." She opened the notebook to a page dense with her own handwriting—waveform sketches, timing intervals, the eleven-minute pulse. "This storm has been listening to this watershed for longer than we've been a species, as far as I can tell. The drowned valley down there—it's not a floodplain. It's an archive. Every voice that ever carried across that water is still in it, somehow, held in the pressure fields, in the charge differentials. I don't have the physics for it. Neither does Anselm. But I know what happens when powerful people learn there's a library that never forgets."

"They'll want to read it."

"They'll want to *own* it. Reading is just ownership with better manners." Ilsa closed the notebook. "So. My plan. We seal the valley. The expedition reports a routine loss—an equipment failure, a flooded camp, seven scientists evacuated early, nothing found. The anomaly stays a satellite footnote. And I stay."

Felix stared at her. "Stay. Here. Alone?"

"With it." Ilsa felt the old calm settle over her, the one that had carried her through war zones and epidemics and the slow bureaucratic murders of wilderness everywhere. "Someone has to be its warden. Someone has to make sure no one else comes upriver with generators and cages. I'm fifty-eight years old, Pilot. I have no children, no institution that will miss me past a memorial plaque, and a body of work that will read as eccentric rather than criminal. I am the cheapest possible price for keeping this thing free."

"That's insane."

"Almost certainly."

"You'd die up here."

"Everyone dies somewhere. Most of us choose badly." Ilsa almost smiled. "At least mine will be well-informed."

* * *

But the plan was only half of it, and they both knew it, and Ilsa had been circling the other half since she'd watched Felix shout into the wind two days ago and seen the storm lean toward her like a plant toward light.

Because that was the problem she hadn't solved. The warden could guard the door, but Felix Fairweather had walked in through the wall—had flown through a squall line that should have killed her, had crash-landed, had trekked forty kilometers of flooded forest on stubbornness and spite alone. A woman like that didn't leave valleys. She left *people*, sure, and institutions, and debts—but places, the ones that got under her skin, those she came back to. Ilsa had read the file. Port Calloway. The Kessari. The pattern of returns disguised as coincidences.

And a returning witness was a leak that no amount of sealing could stop.

"There's a second part," Ilsa said.

"I figured."

"Felix Fairweather died in the squall line upriver." She said it plainly, watching the pilot's face for the flinch, and got instead a stillness so complete it was almost professional. "The Syndicate knows you flew against orders. They know your plane went down short of the camp. When you don't come back, they'll write you off as another monsoon statistic—another debtor conveniently erased. No body, no salvage worth the fuel. Bright will mourn you for exactly as long as it takes to pour a drink."

"And then what?" Felix's voice was careful. "I live in a cave with you?"

"You leave. Downriver, on foot, the way smugglers do it—there's a route along the eastern escarpment, dry season paths, three days to a fishing settlement where nobody asks names. From there, anywhere. A new name costs two hundred dollars in Port Calloway and less than that further south. Your debts die with your identity—the Syndicate can't collect against a woman who doesn't exist. Your record, your licenses, whatever wreckage you left behind in whatever life you were running from—" Ilsa spread her hands. "Erased. All of it. In exchange for one thing."

"Silence."

"Silence. You never speak of the storm. Not to Bright, not to some journalist in ten years, not drunk at a bar, not on your deathbed. The valley keeps its archive, and you keep your life, and neither of us owes anyone anything ever again." Ilsa leaned forward. "I know what you're thinking. You're thinking it's a bribe. It isn't. A bribe buys a lie. This buys a truth you simply decline to tell—which is the only freedom anyone actually has."

Felix was quiet for a long time. Outside, the storm pulsed its slow eleven-minute heartbeat, and Ilsa watched the pilot listen to it without meaning to, the way you listened to surf or a lover's breathing, involuntarily, completely.

"What did you hear?" Felix asked suddenly. "In the eye. When you went in. Everyone comes out of there changed—I saw it on Anselm's people. What did it give you?"

Ilsa hesitated. This was not part of the negotiation. This was the part she had promised herself she wouldn't do—the part where she treated the pilot as a person instead of a variable.

"My mother," she said. "Singing. In a kitchen in Turin, forty-six years ago. A song I haven't heard since before she died." She looked out at the storm. "It wasn't a recording. It was the kitchen. The steam, the radio underneath her, my own small feet on the tiles. It holds everything, Pilot. Everything anyone ever said within reach of that water. Do you understand now why it can't belong to anyone? Because whoever owns it owns every grief and every promise this watershed ever held. There is no cage big enough for that which isn't a prison for all of us."

Felix's hand had drifted to her jacket pocket—to something inside it, Ilsa noticed, some small object the pilot touched the way other people touched rosaries.

"And if I say no?" Felix asked. "If I fly out of here tomorrow and tell Bright everything?"

"Then I will have been wrong about you," Ilsa said, "and the world will get what it always gets." She held out the notebook anyway—the data, the waveforms, the whole shape of the discovery. "But I don't think you will. I think you've spent your whole life paying other people's prices, and this is the first time anyone's offered you a clean one."

Felix looked at the notebook. Looked at the storm. Her fingers stilled inside her pocket.

Before she could answer, a sound rolled up the valley from downriver—distant, wrong, mechanical. Not thunder. Thunder didn't idle.

Engines. Barges, coming up the Kessari, and behind them, faint under the storm's pulse, the whine of generators spinning up.

Bright had arrived.
