## 14. Salvage Rights

The barges came upriver on the third morning after Ilsa's offer, riding a brown flood that had swallowed the landing at Kessari Bend whole. Felix heard them before she saw them—diesel engines working against the current with the patient, arrogant confidence of machinery that has never once been told no.

She was crouched in the treeline above the drowned valley's southern rim, halfway through her second day of pretending to scout while actually memorizing the storm's drift patterns, when the first barge nosed around the bend. Then the second. Then a third, low and black-hulled, stacked with crates lashed under tarps and ringed by men in matching yellow rain slickers who looked less like laborers than like an audience waiting for something to begin.

On the lead barge's deck, beneath a canopy rigged from salvage parachutes, stood a folding table, a thermos, and Bright.

Even at four hundred meters, even through monsoon haze, Felix could see him waving.

* * *

She told herself she wouldn't go down. She told herself it for a full hour while she watched the crews unload generators the size of coffins and begin running cable toward the valley rim with the brisk efficiency of people who had been promised this would be easy. She told herself it while she catalogued their equipment through Anselm's battered binoculars: winches, antenna masts, and—her stomach dropped when she identified them—acoustic isolation cages, the kind used for recording cetaceans, each one lined with foam and fitted with its own microphone array on a gimbal mount.

They weren't here to salvage wreckage. They were here to interview a storm.

By noon she'd counted eleven crew, two of them clearly Syndicate enforcers doing a poor job of looking like technicians, and one woman with a surveyor's laser who kept pacing off distances to the valley floor like a realtor measuring a lot. The rain fell on all of them equally and none of them seemed to mind. That was the thing about the Vane Syndicate that had taken Felix years to understand: they weren't afraid of weather because weather, to them, was just another invoice waiting to be issued.

She went down because not going down was itself an answer, and answers, given too early, closed doors. It was the oldest rule of her trade. You never refused a contract you hadn't read; you signed it blind and survived long enough to learn what you'd agreed to.

Bright met her at the waterline with two tin cups and the expression of a man greeting a sister he genuinely liked.

"Felix Fairweather." He handed her a cup. Steam rose off it—real coffee, Syndicate-imported, the kind that cost more per kilo than her plane's annual inspection. "You look terrible."

"I've been told the river diet does that."

"River diet." He laughed, warm and easy, and for a moment she remembered why she'd almost liked him in Port Calloway, back when his offer had looked like a rope instead of a noose. "You found them, then. The expedition."

"Found what's left of them."

"Four survivors, one doctor who talks to clouds, and a botanist playing hermit." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't move from her face. "Yes, we got your position reports. Well—" he smiled, "—we got *some* of them. The ones you wanted us to have, I'd guess. You always were careful about which frequencies you sang on."

Felix drank the coffee. It was excellent. That was the other thing about the Syndicate: everything they touched was high quality, right down to the traps.

"You came upriver fast," she said.

"Opportunity travels light." He turned, gesturing at the operation behind him with the pride of a man showing off a garden. "Generators, recording arrays, mobile analysis suite. Do you know what we're looking at up here, Felix? Because I'll be honest—I didn't believe the decoded fragments at first. A standing supercell, anchored to fixed terrain, responsive to acoustic input? That's not meteorology. That's *infrastructure*. Somebody built a power plant out of sky and forgot to send us the bill."

"Nobody built it."

"Everything's built by someone." He said it cheerfully, the way he said everything. "The question is only whether we own it or it owns us. And between us—" he leaned in, confiding, warm, "—I'd rather it be us. Us is kinder. Us has safety regulations."

* * *

He made the offer over lunch, seated at the folding table under the parachute canopy while rain drummed the fabric overhead like polite applause. Around them the crews worked: cages being positioned along the rim at measured intervals, cable spooled toward the generators, the woman with the laser calling out coordinates in a bored voice.

"Double forgiveness," Bright said, sliding a slate across the table. On it, a number. Felix stared at it. It wasn't just her debt. It was her debt plus the interest plus the penalties plus the collection fees—the entire architecture of ruin the Syndicate had spent three years building around her life, erased twice over, leaving a surplus. Enough to recondition the plane. Enough to fly somewhere the monsoon never reached.

"For what?"

"Reconnaissance. Flight support. You know the airspace now better than anyone alive—you've proven that. We need telemetry from inside the storm's circulation envelope. Wind vectors, pressure gradients, charge differentials." He ticked items off on his fingers, pleasant as a man ordering dinner. "You fly the pattern, our instruments do the listening, and in two weeks you walk away richer than clean."

"And if I say no?"

Bright spread his hands, and for the first time the warmth in his face went still—not cold, exactly, but *level*, like water finding its plane. "Then you're a witness, Felix. And witnesses are a liability category. Not yours specifically—it's nothing personal—but the Syndicate has procedures for liability categories, and the procedures are boring and final." He sipped his coffee. "But you won't say no. You want to know why?"

"Because I'm desperate."

"Because you already tried to say no once." He nodded upriver, toward the standing storm, invisible beyond the ridgeline but present anyway, the way a loaded weapon is present in a room. "You flew against orders. You burned your fuel reserves getting here. Whatever you think you're protecting up there, you've already spent your exit buying access to it. People don't walk away from sunk costs, Felix. They double down. It's the most reliable law in economics and I have never once seen it broken."

* * *

She walked the perimeter that afternoon, ostensibly inspecting their equipment placement, actually mapping it. The cages sat in a crescent along the southern rim, microphones aimed at the valley like a congregation facing an altar. Cable ran everywhere, bright yellow against the mud, converging on the generator bank. Two enforcers shadowed her at a distance of thirty meters, close enough to be a message, far enough to be deniable.

And she saw it then—saw the whole shape of it, the way you finally see a squall line's true structure only after you're committed to flying through it.

Both sides needed her.

Ilsa needed her silence, or failing that, her help sealing the valley. Bright needed her eyes, or failing that, her absence made permanent. The survivors needed a pilot to get home. The storm—she thought of it now as *the* storm, capitalized in her head, a proper noun forming whether she consented or not—needed, as far as she could tell, nothing except to be left alone, which was the one outcome nobody was offering.

Which meant neither side could let her leave. Which meant every door out of this valley had quietly become a door into someone else's plan.

She stopped at the edge of the rim, where the ground fell away into the drowned forest, and let the rain hit the back of her neck. Below, the valley lay green and flooded and secret. Above, past the ridgeline, the storm stood on its anchor, patient as a cathedral.

There had been a moment, years ago, over the Corvane Sea, when her engine had quit at nine thousand feet and she'd had ninety seconds to choose a place to put down. She remembered the strange clarity of it—the way fear burned off like fog and left behind a flat, workable calm. *Pick the field. Fly the field. Everything else is weather.*

She picked the field now.

* * *

She found Bright at dusk, packing his slate into a waterproof case, and told him yes.

His smile bloomed exactly on schedule, and she hated how much relief she felt watching it, because relief was data and data was leverage and she'd just handed him a reading.

"Sensible," he said. "First flight tomorrow, oh-six-hundred. Weather permitting." He glanced at the sky, and they both laughed at the joke, because the alternative was acknowledging that the weather up here did not permit anything—that it *decided*, and that they were all currently negotiating with something that had never once answered.

"One condition," Felix said.

"Name it."

"The survivors stay out of it. No interviews, no transport manifests, no names in any report. They're science personnel caught in a weather event. That's the whole story."

Bright considered her, head tilted, and she watched him decide that the request cost him nothing—which meant he hadn't yet understood what she was protecting, which meant she still had one card he couldn't see.

"Done," he said. "You know, Felix, this is why I like you. Everyone else upriver wants something impossible. You just want terms." He offered his hand. "Welcome aboard. Again."

She shook it. His grip was dry and warm and completely without malice, which was somehow worse than the alternative.

That night she lay in the lee of her wrecked plane's wing, listening to the generators throb down at the camp and the rain fall through the dark, and she took stock the way she always took stock before a flight she might not come back from.

One repaired radio, hidden in the tail section, forty percent functional. One copy of Anselm's raw data, sealed in wax and oilcloth inside her survival vest. One storm that had spoken her dead brother's name back to her in a voice made of wind. One botanist somewhere on the ridgeline, watching these same lights, drawing her own conclusions about the new arrivals.

And one morning flight, tomorrow at oh-six-hundred, in which Felix Fairweather would take the Syndicate's money-flight straight into the heart of the thing they wanted owned—and see, for the first time from above, what the storm did when the machines started talking to it.

She closed her eyes. Somewhere below, in the valley, in the dark, the rain changed rhythm—just slightly, just briefly—like something turning over in its sleep.

At oh-five-forty, before first light, before the generators' morning cycle, a single microphone cage at the western rim began transmitting on its own. Nobody noticed until Bright's technician pulled the overnight logs and found eleven minutes of audio: wind, rain, and underneath both, unmistakably, a human voice saying the same word over and over.

Felix's name.
