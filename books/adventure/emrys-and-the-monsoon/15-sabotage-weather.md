## 15. Sabotage Weather

The Syndicate worked the way water worked—patiently, everywhere at once, finding every low place. By the third morning after Bright's barges tied up at the drowned landing, they had transformed the rain shadow below the cave into something between a research station and a siege camp. Generators throbbed in canvas shelters. Floodlights burned through the monsoon dark. And everywhere, on every tripod and mast and crate, cages of recording equipment: microphones in wire housings, barometers in armored cases, antennas folded like sleeping insects waiting for a signal to wake them.

Felix stood at the treeline with a tin cup of river tea going cold in her hands and counted what Bright had brought ashore.

Twelve generator sets. Nine instrument towers, half-assembled. A crane barge anchored mid-channel with a winch that could lift a small building. And men—maybe forty of them—moving with the cheerful competence of people who had been told this was easy and believed it, because Bright only hired people who believed things were easy until the moment they weren't.

"You're up early," said a voice behind her, warm as fresh bread.

Bright came down the trail with his hands in his pockets, rain beading on his immaculate oilskin. He never seemed to get wet properly. Even his boots looked dry. Felix had decided it was some kind of trick of accounting—the man simply refused to register losses, including to weather.

"Somebody has to watch you people work," she said.

"We're not working yet." He stopped beside her, following her gaze to the crane barge. "We're setting up. There's a difference. Work is when you know what you're doing."

"Then what do you call all this?"

"Optimism." He smiled at the camp like a father at a school play. "Mr. Vane wants readings by month's end. Frequency signatures, pressure differentials, whatever the eggheads say matters. We put the instruments in the right places, we let the storm talk, and we go home rich." He glanced sideways at her. "You could help with the placement. You're the only one here who's actually flown inside it."

"I flew *into* it," Felix said. "There's a difference."

"There always is." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on her a beat too long, and she felt the old Syndicate arithmetic running behind them: assets, liabilities, salvage value. "Offer stands. Double forgiveness. Think about it."

He walked back down toward the generators, whistling something cheerful and minor-key, and Felix stood in the rain and thought about instruments.

That was the thing about weather equipment that most people never understood: it wasn't just measurement. It was *invitation*. A microphone didn't record a storm; it gave the storm a reason to organize around it. She'd seen it a hundred times in smaller ways—a sounding balloon seeding a cell, a radar dish drawing lightning like a lit window draws moths. The expedition's own radios had been destroyed for reasons Ilsa still hadn't fully explained, but Felix was starting to build a theory, and the theory had teeth.

The storm listened. Bright wanted to make it listen harder.

She drained the cold tea and went to find out where he planned to put his ears.

* * *

The answer, by noon, was everywhere.

Instrument towers staked along the valley rim. Hydrophones in the flooded forest. A ring of microphones at the base of the slope below the standing storm, close enough that the first gust front would hit them before it hit anything alive. Bright walked the line with his site engineers, gesturing with both hands, sketching circles in the air, and Felix trailed him at a respectful distance with a clipboard she'd borrowed from nobody in particular, playing the consultant she'd half-agreed to be.

It was good cover. It was also unbearable.

"Here," Bright said, planting a flag in the mud at the mouth of a ravine that funneled straight down from the cloud base. "This is the throat. Everything drains through here. Put the primary array right in the channel."

Felix looked at the ravine, then up at the sky above it, and did the math she'd been doing since she was nineteen years old and broke in a hangar in Port Calloway.

The ravine channeled more than sound. It channeled air. Every outflow from the storm's western flank would compress through that gap and come out the other side moving fast enough to peel paint. Any array set in the channel would take a hundred-knot beating within hours of the next pulse cycle—and she knew the pulse cycles now, better than anyone, because she'd spent two weeks watching the storm breathe while everyone else watched their instruments.

"Bad placement," she said. "You'll lose the array in the first gust front."

"Then we anchor it better."

"You'll lose the anchors too." She crouched, drew in the mud with a stick. "Look. Outflow comes off the flank here, funnels through, accelerates. But—" she moved the stick upstream, to a shelf of ground behind a granite shoulder, "—put it there, and the rock breaks the flow. You get clean readings, protected air, and your masts stay vertical."

Bright studied her sketch. His smile didn't waver, but something behind it recalculated. "You're sure?"

"Am I sure about wind?" Felix let a little of her real exhaustion into her voice, the weariness of a woman who'd bet her life on forecasts too many times. "I'm the surest person on this river."

He laughed and waved his engineers over, and the flag came up and moved fifty meters to the shelf behind the granite.

And that was how it began.

Not sabotage—not exactly. Nothing she could be caught doing, nothing that would stand up in any court Bright's lawyers could convene. Just weather advice. Just the accumulated instinct of fifteen years of flying into things other pilots flew around, offered generously, freely, and always slightly wrong.

She told them the fog banks off the oxbow lakes burned off by mid-morning, so they scheduled their barge crossings for dawn—and the fog that week hung on till past noon, because she'd read the moisture profile herself and knew the river was warmer than the air and had three days of evaporation banked. Two days of crane work lost to soup.

She told them the afternoon convection built over the eastern ridge, so they should run their cable-laying along the western shore—and then she watched the sky all morning, waited for the upper flow to shear just so, and flagged down the crew chief at eleven to warn him, sincerely, apologetically, that the ridge cells were drifting west early today, better move. They moved. The cells went east. Half a day, gone, chasing weather that had politely declined to exist.

Each time, she apologized. Each time, she made the apology cost her something visible—a furrowed brow, a bitten lip, a night spent "rechecking" charts. Bright thanked her every time, warmly, and every time she caught him watching her afterward with that recalculation flickering behind the smile, and she knew the ledger was turning against her, page by page. An optimist who kept losing money started reading fine print.

So she slowed down. Made her errors rarer, smaller, deniable. Let the camp settle into believing the river itself was against them—which, to be fair, it was—and let Bright's schedule slip from month's end into a haze of revised projections.

And every night, while the generators throbbed and the floodlights carved cones out of the rain, Felix sat in the wreck of her own plane and committed a crime that required no weather skill at all.

* * *

The radio had come out of the expedition's stores, one unit the team had missed—or, she suspected now, one unit Ilsa had allowed to survive, tucked under a false bottom in a supply crate like a coin sewn into a refugee's coat. Felix had found it on her second day scouting the abandoned camp, and she'd carried it out wrapped in her spare shirt, heart hammering, certain that Bright's people would search her bag at any moment.

They never had. The Syndicate searched for things worth stealing. A broken radio wasn't worth stealing.

Broken was the operative word. Ilsa's people had dismantled the camp's radios with surgical care, but this one they'd merely killed—coils pulled, crystal cracked, wiring cut in three places with a blade so precise it might have been surgery's rougher cousin. Repairing it meant fabricating parts from what the crash site and the camp ruins offered: a crystal salvaged from a dead transponder, wire stripped from her plane's burnt harness, coils hand-wound around a pencil stub by lamplight while rain hammered the fuselage overhead.

Four nights of work. Her fingers were too big for it; she knew that the way she knew her landings were too hard. She did it anyway, winding and unwinding, testing continuity with a multimeter cannibalized from the plane's panel, swearing at the solder joints in three languages she'd picked up from mechanics on three continents.

On the fifth night, the radio breathed.

Just a hiss, at first—carrier wave, no signal, the electrical equivalent of a pulse. Felix sat back in the cockpit and let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for three weeks. One working radio. Range maybe sixty kilometers in this terrain, less in weather. Not enough to reach Port Calloway. Enough, if she chose her moment, to reach the coastal relay station at the river mouth—if it still stood, if anyone still manned it, if the Syndicate hadn't bought the operator.

One thread out of the web. Thin as spider silk. But a web is only silk until something pulls.

She was re-soldering the final joint—hands steady, breath held, the whole cockpit lit by a shielded lantern—when the fuselage door opened and the rain came in with it, and Dr. Anselm climbed into the wreckage of her aircraft without asking permission, the way he seemed to do everything.

Felix's hand closed over the radio. Too late. His eyes had already found it, the open case, the exposed guts, the pencil-stub coil still warm.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Rain drummed on the aluminum skin. Somewhere across the water, a generator changed pitch.

"Coil's wound too tight," Anselm said at last. "You'll drift off frequency in humidity."

Felix exhaled slowly. "It's held so far."

"It won't hold through a real transmission. Heat expands the former." He crouched beside her, and up close she could see how much weight he'd lost since the cave—hollows under his cheekbones, hands that trembled faintly unless he made them stop. "Wrap it in wax. Candle wax. Seal the whole assembly."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're doing it badly," he said, "and because I've been waiting five nights to see if you'd finish."

Her stomach dropped. "You've been—"

"Watching. Yes." He settled onto the jump seat across from her, folding his long frame into the space with the ease of a man accustomed to cramped field quarters. "You think Bright doesn't notice his schedules slipping? He notices. He blames the river. I blame you—I've watched you read clouds since the day we met, and no one reads clouds that well by accident." He nodded at the radio. "Ilsa would have destroyed that. If she'd found it first."

"Would you have told her?"

Anselm was quiet for a moment. Outside, the floodlights swayed in a gust, shadows wheeling across the cockpit glass like slow birds.

"I sent three people downriver to get help," he said. "Against her wishes. You know that."

"I know they never arrived."

"No. They didn't." His voice stayed level, but his hands, in his lap, had gone still in the way of hands gripping something invisible. "I don't know what happened to them. Neither does she. That's the part that keeps me awake—that I don't even know which of us was right. Maybe the river took them. Maybe the storm did. Maybe Ilsa's people—" He stopped. Started again. "The point is, I gambled with lives to bring the world the truth, and the world didn't even get the chance to refuse it. So now I'm gambling differently."

He reached inside his coat and brought out a package wrapped in oilcloth, bound with cord, dense as a brick. He set it on the chart table between them.

"What is it?"

"The raw data. Everything we recorded before the radios came apart. Pressure series, acoustic profiles, the response curves—all of it, unprocessed, the way science is supposed to travel." He pushed it an inch toward her. "Ilsa thinks data is a weapon that fires in every direction. She may be right. But I spent thirty years believing that the cure for bad information is good information, and I am too old to change my religion twice in one season."

Felix didn't touch it. "If I take this, and I transmit it, and the Syndicate intercepts it—"

"They won't. Your coil will drift; use the wax. And transmit in short bursts during the gust fronts, when the static covers you." He almost smiled. "You taught me monsoon rules in week one, Ms. Fairweather. Storm noise hides everything. I assumed you knew that applied to voices too."

She stared at him. "You want me to send it."

"I want someone to send it who isn't me. If I transmit, and Ilsa learns of it, the split becomes permanent, and this team dies of it—one way or another. But you—" he studied her with those tired, clear eyes, "—you're already outside every circle. You owe the Syndicate nothing they can collect. You owe Ilsa nothing she'll admit. You're the only person on this river whose betrayal costs anyone nothing." He rose, ducking under the cockpit frame, and paused at the door with the rain streaming past him. "Except yourself, perhaps. Consider whether that's a price you can pay."

Then he was gone, down into the dark, and Felix sat alone with the oilcloth package and the humming radio and the sound of her own heartbeat loud in the wrecked cockpit.

* * *

She wrapped the coil in wax that night, melting candle stubs over the assembly until it gleamed like something organic, like a seed pod. She tested the frequency drift against the multimeter. Held steady. Anselm's fix was good—of course it was; the man calibrated glaciers for a living.

Then she sat with the package in her lap for a long time, listening to the camp across the water wind down, generator by generator, light by light, until only the storm remained, breathing its slow pulse over the drowned valley.

Transmitting the data meant choosing. Not between Syndicate and expedition—she'd stopped believing in that choice weeks ago—but between the world that measured and owned, and the world that got to keep its secrets. Ilsa's valley or Bright's ledger. Silence or signal.

But Anselm had reframed it, and the reframing wouldn't leave her alone: the truth, sent honestly, in short bursts, hidden in storm noise. Not sold. Not seized. Just *said*, once, into the static, the way you'd tell a friend something true at a funeral—quietly, and without expecting the world to handle it well.

She thought about the eye. About the voice that had spoken her name. Some things survived only by being told to no one. But some things died that way instead, and she was done pretending she could tell the difference from the outside.

Dawn came gray and dripping. Across the water, Bright's crews were already stirring, and Felix saw the crane barge swinging its load toward the ravine—the throat, the original placement, the flag quietly moved back while she slept. Bright had made his own calculation. The optimist was done trusting the weather woman's advice.

Fine. She had a radio that worked, data worth dying for, and a gust front due off the western flank by mid-morning—she could feel it building in her teeth, the way she felt all the big ones.

Storm noise hides everything.

Felix sealed the oilcloth package inside the radio's carrying case, slung the strap over her shoulder, and went out into the rain to find her window.
