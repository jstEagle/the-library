## 18. One Voice Enough

The storm had left its anchor at dawn, and by noon it was forty kilometers downriver and still walking.

Felix stood on the flooded levee at Old Kessari Landing with the microphone in one hand and Anselm's data case strapped across her back like a child she'd promised to carry out of a fire. Behind her, the barge crews were screaming. Ahead of her, the sky had come down to meet the water.

She'd seen squall lines before that looked like walls. This wasn't a wall. Walls ended. This thing spanned the whole valley from ridge to ridge, a bruised cathedral of cloud dragging a skirt of rain so dense it looked like gray smoke pouring off the bottom of the world. Lightning moved through it in slow, deliberate pulses—left flank, right flank, crown—like something breathing in its sleep. Or like something dreaming, and not gently.

"Fairweather!" Bright's voice carried over the generators, bright as a coin flipped into the air. He was wading toward her through shin-deep water, hands raised, smile intact even now, even here. "Whatever you're thinking, think about the arithmetic! That plane won't fly in that! You'll die for nothing!"

"That's the nice part," Felix said, not turning around. "It's not nothing."

She'd spent the night doing the math nobody else would do. The storm was drifting at eleven kilometers an hour, following the river's course the way a dog follows a scent trail. At that rate it reached the delta towns in thirty hours. After the delta came the coast. After the coast came everything.

You couldn't fight it. You couldn't outrun it. But Ilsa had told her the truth in the eye of it, three days ago, standing in that impossible room of still air: *it listens*. It had been listening to this watershed for longer than there had been words to listen to. It kept what it heard. And when Felix had shouted into the wind, the wind had shouted back—not mimicry, not echo. *Answer.*

So you didn't fight it. You called it home.

And to call something home, you needed its own voice.

* * *

The Otter sat where she'd beached it two nights ago, half its left float stove in from the crash landing, engine cowl dented, one wingtip scarred where a tree had tried to keep her. She'd spent the hours since then with a hammer, a sheet of aluminum, and the particular fury of a woman with no other options. The float wouldn't hold forever. It didn't have to hold forever. It had to hold long enough.

Anselm met her at the plane. He'd insisted on coming down to the water, though every step cost him—the man had been living on cave air and river fish for weeks and it showed in the way he moved, like a house settling.

"You understand," he said, "that if this works, we lose it."

"I know."

"Not damaged. Not studied. *Lost.* Everything it is, everything it kept—" His voice caught on the word *kept*, because they both knew what the valley held. Voices. Years of them. The drowned valley was an archive, and archives don't survive being found.

"If I do nothing," Felix said, "we lose the delta instead. Thirty thousand people who never asked to live downstream of a miracle." She cinched the data case strap tighter. "I'm a debt collector's problem, Anselm, not a philosopher. I pay what I owe."

He pressed something into her hand—a second microphone, expedition-grade, wired to a battery pack. "In case the first fails. And Felix." He looked up at the storm, and for a moment his face was naked with something that wasn't fear. Longing, maybe. Grief. "Tell it we're sorry. For the machines. Tell it some of us knew better."

"I'll tell it whatever gets it home."

* * *

Takeoff was sixty meters of flooded field and a prayer with the throttle pinned. The damaged float slapped the water like a flat stone refusing to sink, once, twice, three times—and then the Otter staggered into the air trailing spray, and Felix hauled it up and away from the trees with her teeth gritted hard enough to ache.

Below her, the world was ending politely. The river had shrugged out of its banks in the night; whole forests stood waist-deep, their crowns making dark islands. She could see Bright's barge strung with lights, the salvage crews' generators humming, the cages of recording equipment glinting under tarps—everything the Syndicate had brought to measure the unmeasurable, now scrambling like ants whose hill had been kicked. A helicopter was trying to lift off from the barge deck and thought better of it, settling back down as the gust front hit.

Her radio crackled. Bright's voice, warm as ever, threaded with static.

"Felix. Last offer, and I mean it as a friend. Turn around, land on the barge, and I tear your file in half in front of you. Every cent. Every name you've used. Clean slate, and a bonus besides. All you have to do is let us handle the asset."

"The asset," Felix said, "is listening to you right now. Think about what you're teaching it."

Silence on the channel. Then, quieter, almost human: "You're going to kill yourself for a fairy tale."

"No," she said. "I'm going to fly into one. There's a difference, and it's the only difference that's ever mattered to me."

She switched the radio off. Some conversations you finished by leaving.

* * *

The rain wall took her at four hundred feet.

It wasn't like flying into weather. It was like flying into a place. One moment there was gray light and driving rain; the next, the world simply changed its mind about existing. Visibility went to nothing. The altimeter spun. Hail the size of grapes hammered the windshield, and the Otter bucked like a horse hearing a rattlesnake, and Felix flew by feel and faith and twenty years of monsoon seasons written into her hands.

Left float groaning. Right engine running hot. She climbed when the air told her to climb and descended when it shoved her down, riding the storm's structure the way she'd ride any cell—except no cell she'd ever flown had *structure* like this. The convection organized itself around her in spirals too clean to be natural, corridors of relative calm threading between cores of violence, and after ten minutes of white-knuckle survival she understood, with a lurch, that the corridors were opening *for her*. The storm was reading her the way she read clouds. It felt the plane. It felt her.

*I see you,* the air said, in the language of pressure and wind shear. *What are you?*

"I'm the one who's sorry," she said aloud, alone in the roaring dark, feeling ridiculous and desperate and certain all at once. "I'm the one who came to bring your voices back."

The hail stopped. Just like that, between one heartbeat and the next, as if a door had closed somewhere overhead.

Then the lightning started crawling the flanks again—that slow, deliberate pulse, left, right, crown—and Felix realized she was being *escorted*.

* * *

Ilsa came out of the rain like a ghost with a propeller.

One moment Felix was alone in the gray; the next, a battered amphibian slid off her left wing, close enough that she could see Ilsa Voss's face through the other cockpit's wet glass—set, grim, eyes fixed on her with an expression Felix had last seen on a woman defending a door with her body.

Ilsa keyed her radio. Felix, against every rule of the flight, keyed back.

"Don't do this," Ilsa said. Her voice was stripped raw, no lecture in it, just plea. "Turn south. Fly out ahead of it and let it go. It will find new country. New rivers. It will survive us, Felix—it has survived everything. But if you take it back to that valley and the Syndicate follows—"

"They can't follow what they can't find, and they can't find what I burn."

"You think fire stops them?" Bitterness cracked through. "I burned my research once. Ten years ago. Do you know how long it took them to stop looking? They never stopped. They just got patient. That's what the Syndicate *is*, Felix—patience with money behind it. As long as the storm exists, they will hunt it. The only mercy left is to let it leave."

"And the delta?"

Ilsa was silent for three full seconds. In a storm, in two aircraft, that silence was a canyon.

"The delta is a price," she said finally. "I've done this arithmetic my whole life. Protect the many by hiding the one. Hide the one or the one becomes a weapon, and weapons always find their war." Her plane drifted closer, wingtip almost touching. "Come with me. Both of us fly south. We let it go and we say nothing, and in fifty years someone finds a strange weather pattern over empty mountains and writes a paper nobody believes. That's the best ending available. Take it."

Felix looked at her—at this brilliant, broken woman who had ordered radios destroyed to keep a wonder safe, who had chosen erasure as love—and felt something twist in her chest that wasn't quite anger and wasn't quite pity.

"You heard it too," Felix said. "In the eye. You heard the voices. You heard—" she stopped, because she'd heard her own name in there, spoken by a dead man, and some things stayed private even at the end of the world. "It's not a treasure, Ilsa. It's not a weapon. It's a *listener*. And you want to sentence it to solitude forever because being known hurt you once."

"You don't know what I—"

"It replayed my dead brother's voice to me," Felix said, and the words came out steady, which surprised her, because nothing had been steady in years. "Three days ago. In the calm. And I wanted to stay in that room until I died. So yes—I know exactly what you're protecting yourself from." She rolled her wings level, holding formation. "But it doesn't belong to us. Not to the Syndicate, not to science, and not to your guilt. It belongs to the valley it grew out of. I'm taking it home, and then I'm going to stand between it and everyone who comes with a cage. Starting with you. Turn back, Ilsa. Please. Don't make me do this twice."

For a long moment the two planes flew together inside the breathing dark, engines harmonizing, rain streaming past both windshields like time itself pouring down the glass.

Then Ilsa peeled away, banking south, and her voice came through one last time, quiet enough that Felix almost missed it under the roar:

"When it asks you who you are—tell it the truth. It'll know if you lie. It always knows."

And she was gone into the rain, and Felix flew on alone.

* * *

The eye opened ahead of her like a held breath.

Even drifting, even wounded loose from its anchor, the storm kept its center—a column of calm a kilometer wide, walled in by rotating cloud, lit from within by that slow pulse of lightning. Felix dropped through the turbulence at the edge and into stillness, and the silence rang in her ears like the aftermath of a bell.

Below, the drowned valley scrolled past: treetops rising from brown water, ridgelines, the black thread of the old river channel. Forty kilometers behind her, the anchor point waited—an amphitheater of hills where the storm had stood for months, where the archive lay drowned and dreaming. She could see the damage already: the storm's departure had left the valley's microclimate collapsing, fog boiling off the warming water, the whole delicate system it had sustained beginning to unravel. It wasn't just homesick. It was dying by inches, out here, cut off from what fed it.

Which meant she wasn't calling it home out of mercy. She was calling it home because home was its *body*.

Felix unbuckled the data case, set it on the seat beside her, and opened it. Anselm's raw recordings—weeks of them, captured before Ilsa destroyed the radios, before the team scattered. Field recordings made in the eye itself: the storm's own answers, played back through the expedition's equipment, layered and strange. Voices the valley had kept. Rain-sounds shaped like speech. A language made of weather remembering.

She wired the microphone to the Otter's external speaker system with shaking hands. Checked it twice. Three times.

On the barge, far behind and below, she could imagine Bright's crews scrambling, generators roaring, the helicopter finally lifting off with its cages of sensors—chasing a god with a butterfly net. Let them come. By the time they arrived, either this would be over or it wouldn't matter anymore.

She keyed the mic.

"Hello," Felix said, and her voice rolled out beneath her into the still air, absurdly small, a single human sound offered up into a cathedral of storm. "I brought your voices home."

She pressed play on Anselm's recorder.

The speaker crackled—and then the valley spoke.

Rain-voices, dozens of them, braided together: an old man laughing, a woman singing half a song, children shouting in a language older than maps, the creak of boats, the murmur of markets, a hundred years of the watershed talking to itself—all of it recorded in the eye, all of it given back now, spilling out of the little plane into the storm's listening silence.

The lightning stopped pulsing.

The whole rotation of the storm seemed to lean inward, a billion tons of cloud tilting toward one small aircraft like a face bending close to hear a whisper. The walls of the eye began to move—not violently, but with the slow grandeur of a decision being made—and Felix felt the air pressure shift around her wings, felt the storm gathering itself toward her the way a crowd gathers toward a stage.

Somewhere below, faint through the stillness, she could hear the helicopter coming. Somewhere above, the cloud ceiling lowered like a lid.

Felix took a breath, put her hand on the microphone, and began to answer the only question that had ever mattered.

"My name is Felix Fairweather," she said, "and I need to tell you the truth—"

The eye closed around her like a room, and the storm, for the first time in a hundred years, waited to hear what a human being would say next.
