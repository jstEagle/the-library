## 19. The Long Answer

The storm had swallowed the sky whole.

Felix flew into it with her hands light on the yoke and her heart hammering somewhere up near her teeth, the microphone wired to the radio in her lap like a bomb she was choosing to carry. Below her, the Kessari ran brown and swollen, a vein of floodwater two miles wide in places, and above her the cloud deck came down to meet her like a ceiling that intended to become a floor.

She had flown into weather her whole life. She had never flown into something that was *listening*.

"Emrys," she said aloud, testing the name against the engine noise, and the turbulence around her wings shifted—not calmed, not worsened, but *shifted*, the way a sleeping animal shifts when it hears a familiar footstep. Her skin prickled under her flight jacket.

Behind her, strapped into the jump seat because there had been no arguing with her, Ilsa Voss said nothing. She had tried everything else—grabbing the door frame at the airstrip, shouting over the props, finally climbing aboard with the grim resignation of a woman who understood that if the plane went down, she'd rather go down arguing than waiting. Now she just watched Felix's hands on the controls and waited for the moment she'd try to take them.

"You understand what you're doing," Ilsa said. It wasn't a question. It hadn't been a question for twenty minutes.

"I'm answering it."

"It doesn't need an answer. It needs *silence*. That's what I've been trying to tell everyone from the beginning—" Ilsa's voice cracked, and Felix heard underneath it not fanaticism but exhaustion, three weeks of living inside a decision too large for any one person. "Anything you say to it becomes part of it. Anything it says back becomes evidence. You're about to hand the whole watershed a witness."

"No." Felix banked through a curtain of rain so dense it turned the world the color of wet slate. "I'm about to hand it *mine*. There's a difference. Bright wants to record it. Anselm wants to measure it. You want to hide it. Nobody's asked it what it wants, Ils. Somebody has to ask."

"And you think that's you? A debt-ridden bush pilot who can't read a contract?"

"A debt-ridden bush pilot," Felix said, "is exactly who should be talking. I've got nothing left worth protecting. That makes me the only honest voice in this valley."

Lightning walked down the storm's flank ahead of them, and Felix saw it again—the pattern, the terrible regularity of it, forks branching in sequences that repeated every forty seconds like a pulse, like breathing, like something enormous counting. Anselm's data sat in a waterproof case between her feet. Three weeks of recordings from the drowned valley: voices pulled out of rain, out of wind, out of the standing stillness at the center where sound behaved like memory. Voices of people long dead. Voices of the river itself, if the river had ever learned to speak.

And one voice she knew better than her own. One voice that had said her name in the eye of that impossible calm, in a cadence only one person alive had ever used, and that person had been dead six years.

*Felix-girl. You're flying too low again.*

Her father. In the archive. In the storm. Which meant the storm didn't just listen—it kept. It held what it heard the way a valley holds an echo, and everything spoken under the monsoon, everything wept or shouted or whispered along ten thousand miles of watershed, had been pouring into it for longer than anyone could guess.

That was the truth she'd realized alone in the drifting dark: Emrys wasn't responding to humans. It was listening to all of them. It was the archive of everything the valley had ever said, and if Bright got his generators and his cages of equipment up here—if anyone measured it, mapped it, claimed it—then every dead voice, every secret the water had carried, became salvage. Property. Weapon.

So she was going to give it one more voice. Freely. On the record, in front of the only witness who mattered.

The eye opened ahead of her.

* * *

It was worse than she remembered. Or better. The wall of cloud parted like theater curtains and inside was the room—that hollow of still air she'd found once before, miles across, floored with the flooded valley and walled with rotating gray, lit from within by lightning that moved too slowly, like light through honey. Her instruments spun uselessly. Her compass gave up. She flew by eye, by feel, by the strange gentle pressure of the air itself, which seemed to be steering her as much as she was steering it.

Down below, tiny with distance, she could see them: Bright's salvage crews on their barges, floodlights cutting cones through the dusk, generators humming, cages of recording equipment gleaming on the decks like livestock ready for market. And further up the shore, a scatter of lights that would be Anselm and the others, watching. Everyone she owed. Everyone who owed her. All of them looking up at the same impossible sky.

Ilsa leaned forward. "Whatever you're going to do, do it fast. The drift is taking it toward the coast. Every hour it moves, it gets closer to towns, and the Syndicate gets closer to an excuse."

"I know the arithmetic, Ils."

"Do you?" Ilsa's voice was quiet now, almost gentle. "Because I've done nothing but arithmetic for three weeks. Seven minus three missing. Six radios minus six radios. One valley divided by everyone who wants it. And none of it adds up to anything except that somebody has to lose. That's what discovery is, Felix. It's deciding who loses."

"Then watch," Felix said. "Maybe nobody does."

She leveled off at the center of the eye, killed her radio transmitter's encryption, keyed the microphone, and opened the channel to everything—to the storm, to the barges below, to any receiver in range, because a confession whispered isn't a confession at all.

"This is Felix Fairweather," she said. "Broadcasting in the clear. No codes. No coordinates. If you're hearing this, you're hearing the last thing I've got."

Below her, the floodlights swung upward. She imagined Bright's face, that cheerful professional smile faltering for the first time. Good. Let him hear it come.

* * *

She started with the archive.

Anselm's recordings played through the plane's radio, out into the still air of the eye, and the storm took them the way dry ground takes rain. The voices of the drowned valley rose around her—fishermen calling catches across black water, a woman singing to a child in a language the maps had forgotten, men arguing prices in a market town that had been underwater for sixty years, laughter, grief, a dog barking at thunder, generations of the ordinary music of human lives, all of it preserved in pressure and wind. The lightning slowed its pulse to match the cadence of the voices. The walls of the eye seemed to lean in.

Beside her, Ilsa had gone very still. "It's *playing them back*," she breathed. "It's harmonizing. It knows them."

"It remembers them," Felix said. "There's a difference, and it's the whole point."

Then she keyed the mic again, and gave it hers.

"My name is Felix Fairweather. Not Fairweather-the-pilot, not Fairweather-who-owes. Felix Mara Fairweather. My mother named me after her sister, who drowned in the Vell when I was four, and I have been afraid of deep water my entire life, which is a funny thing for a woman who lives on rivers."

The storm's slow light pulsed once, twice. Listening.

"I owe the Vane Syndicate eleven thousand four hundred marks, and I have owed money to someone since I was nineteen years old, because my father taught me to fly and forgot to teach me that flying costs more than it pays. He died in a squall line over the Tessin with a load of medical supplies he was flying for free, because that was the kind of man he was—generous in the air, broke on the ground. Six years ago your rain took him, or some rain did, and I hated the monsoon for six years. I flew around it. I flew over it. I flew through it for money I didn't even want, because moving was easier than stopping."

Her voice cracked and she let it crack. What was the point of a clean confession?

"Three weeks ago I came up this river to pay a debt, and I found you instead. And in your eye, you played me a voice. My father's voice. Saying my name. And I don't know if you kept him from the day he died or if you keep everyone, always, every voice the valley ever gave you—but either way, you gave him back to me for ninety seconds, and no one has ever given me anything like that without wanting something in return."

The eye was changing around her. She felt it before she saw it—the rotation easing by fractions, the walls of cloud drawing breath instead of spinning, the drift that had been carrying the whole system downriver slowing like a great ship coming about.

"So here's what I want to give back. Freely. No contract, no fine print—I learned my lesson about fine print." She laughed, wet and short. "Here's the truth, Emrys. Everything they want from you is a lie. Bright down there wants to own you, and owning means caging, and caging means the voices stop. Anselm wants to know you, and knowing means measuring, and measuring means the voices become data. Ilsa wants to hide you, and hiding means silence, and silence is just another way of making the voices stop. They're all the same plan wearing different clothes. But me—I don't want anything. I'm the only one up here with empty hands. So I'm telling you the only thing I have left: my name, my debts, my fear of deep water, my father, my whole small broke frightened life. Take it. Keep it with the fishermen and the singers and the rest. Add me to the archive. That's all I'm offering. Just—remember me honestly."

For a long moment, nothing. The eye held its breath. Below, the floodlights of the barges made small yellow scratches on the dark, and Felix thought absurdly of moths against glass.

Then the storm answered.

Not in words. In weather. The rotation unwound like a fist opening. The drift reversed—a degree, then five, then decisively, the whole vast mass of cloud swinging back upriver with a groan of wind that Felix felt in her sternum through the airframe. Lightning flared in the old pattern, the forty-second pulse, steady as a heartbeat finding its rhythm again. And the rain began—not the drowning sideways deluge of the past week but a straight, warm, generous fall, monsoon rain the way it was supposed to fall, falling on the valley like a blanket being drawn up over a sleeping child.

"It's going home," Ilsa whispered. She had both hands pressed flat against the window, and she was crying without any apparent awareness of it. "You called it home. With the truth. Of all the—" She laughed, a broken sound. "Three weeks I spent planning erasure, sealing, silence. And the answer was just *telling it honestly*. Truth offered freely. No faction planned for that. No faction would even think of it."

"That's because factions don't have empty hands," Felix said. She put the plane into a slow turn, riding the settling air, and looked down at the valley as the storm folded itself back onto its anchor like a bird onto its nest. "Nobody plans for the thing that costs them everything and buys them nothing."

The monsoon broke around them as they climbed out—not ended, never ended, the season had weeks yet to run, but broken open, eased, the pressure that had been building for a month releasing all at once into ordinary rain. Behind them the standing storm settled over its drowned valley, fixed and breathing and full of voices, and Felix did not look back, because some things you survive by seeing once and holding forever.

On the radio, cracked with static and distance, a voice she recognized—Bright, broadcasting in the clear now too, because the whole valley had heard her confession and there was no pretending otherwise. His voice sounded smaller than she'd ever heard it.

"—Fairweather, do you copy. Felix. What did you *do* up there."

Felix keyed the mic. Smiled at the dark.

"Told the truth, Bright. Try it sometime. It's free."

She flew south through the soft rain, toward base camp, toward the barges, toward the arithmetic that would start tomorrow—the reports, the inquiries, the men with clipboards asking her to repeat what she'd seen for the record. Let them ask. She already knew how that story would end: with her sounding like a madwoman, with the evidence washed downstream, with her debts dissolving into the flood along with everything provable.

But tonight, for eleven thousand four hundred marks' worth of reasons and one that couldn't be priced at all, Felix Fairweather flew home lighter than she had ever been, carrying the only cargo that mattered—unrecorded, unowned, and true.

Behind her, far upriver, the storm kept her name.
