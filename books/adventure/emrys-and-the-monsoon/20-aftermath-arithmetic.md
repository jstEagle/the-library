## 20. Aftermath Arithmetic

The flood took three days to leave Port Calloway and a week to stop talking about.

Felix watched it go from the second-floor window of the harbormaster's office, where she'd been given a cot, a blanket that smelled of diesel, and the particular hospitality reserved for people who had no other place to be. The river withdrew the way a drunk withdraws from an argument—slowly, sullenly, leaving wreckage everywhere and denying responsibility for any of it. Barges sat aground in the market square like beached whales. A fishing skiff hung from the roof of the customs house, tangled in its own anchor line. The mud came up everyone's shins and stayed there, drying into a gray crust that made the whole town look like it had been dipped in concrete and left to set.

Her plane was fine. That was the joke of it. The *Kestrel*—battered, debt-encumbered, held together with safety wire and stubbornness—sat on the high ground of the old military strip where she'd left her before flying into the storm, untouched by water, needing only fuel and a new propeller blade. Everyone else's world had drowned. Hers had survived out of sheer orneriness.

She'd stopped finding that funny around day two.

The Syndicate's people arrived on day four, and they arrived angry, which for the Vane Syndicate was almost unprecedented. Felix watched them from the harbormaster's window: six figures in dry, expensive raingear picking through the wreckage of their salvage operation at the river mouth, where the flood surge had deposited everything they owned in a tangle forty meters inland. Generators filled with silt. Recording equipment fused into single objects that had once been cages of instruments. Bright's barge lay on its side against a collapsed pier, its cargo hold open to the sky like a ribcage, nothing inside it but brown water and one floating crate of what had been, according to the manifest Felix had glimpsed, archival-grade storage media.

All of it blank now. All of it soup.

Bright stood at the center of it with his hands in his pockets, and even from this distance Felix could read his posture—the careful stillness of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. He'd been cheerful when he found her in Port Calloway half a season ago. Cheerful when he offered her the job. Cheerful all the way upriver, cheerful through the storm, cheerful while his crews hauled generators through flooded forest toward a living storm system they intended to own. She had never once seen him not smiling, and she'd assumed the smile was armor.

It wasn't. It was inventory. And right now the inventory read zero.

She went down to meet him because avoiding him would have been worse. That was another thing the monsoon had taught her: you can't outrun weather, only move through it.

* * *

"Felix." Bright turned as she picked her way across the mud, and the smile came back up like a drawbridge—but slower than before, and it didn't reach anything above his cheekbones. "You're alive."

"Last I checked."

"We were worried." He said it smoothly, but his eyes were doing something else entirely—moving over her, cataloguing, looking for the thing he'd come to collect. "You flew into a rotating supercell in a single-engine aircraft. People don't generally come back from that."

"People don't generally come back from a lot of things," Felix said. "I've made a career of the exceptions."

"And did you?" He gestured vaguely upriver, north, toward the drowned valley hidden behind three days of broken weather. "Come back from it? Or just come back?"

She'd rehearsed this on the flight down. Not the words—she'd learned during the debrief with the coastal authorities that the words didn't matter, that no arrangement of them survived contact with a listener who hadn't seen it. What she'd rehearsed was the flatness. The delivery. Say it like a flight report. Say it like numbers.

"There's a standing storm over a valley two hundred kilometers upriver," she said. "It hasn't moved in months. It responds to sound. Spoken voices, specifically—it holds them, plays them back. The expedition found it, tried to protect it, split apart over what to do about it. Your salvage crews' machinery woke it up and it started drifting downriver toward the coast. I flew into it and talked it home. Then the monsoon broke and the flood took your equipment. That's the whole story."

Bright listened with the patience of a man watching a horse he intended to buy. When she finished, he nodded slowly.

"That's remarkable," he said.

"I know how it sounds."

"No, I mean it. It's a remarkable piece of work." He crouched, picked a handful of mud off a ruined instrument housing, let it crumble between his fingers. "You understand my problem, though. My crews saw fog banks appear exactly where they needed to stall our operations. They saw gust fronts arrive on schedule, like appointments. They saw a woman with a radio transmitter repairing equipment she claimed was beyond saving. What they didn't see—" he stood, brushed his hands clean, "—was a storm that talks."

"The flood took your recordings."

"The flood took everything." For the first time, something honest moved behind his face—not grief exactly, but the naked relief of a man whose failure had been witnessed by no one. "Every sensor log. Every spectral analysis. Three weeks of data, and the river ate it in an afternoon. Do you know what I have left?" He spread his arms at the devastation around them. "Mud. I have mud with opinions."

"You could ask the survivors."

"I did ask the survivors." His smile thinned. "Dr. Anselm gave me forty minutes of the most courteous nonsense I've ever heard. Something about hydrological anomalies and seasonal pressure cells. The botanist—Voss's colleague, the one who came downriver—cried, and then recited rainfall statistics until I left. Your scientist friends, Felix, are either the worst liars I've ever met or they genuinely believe the boring version. I honestly can't tell which is worse for me."

"And me? Where do I fall?"

Bright looked at her for a long moment. Somewhere behind him, a crew chief cursed at a generator that would never run again, and the sound carried flat across the mudflats.

"You," he said finally, "are the interesting case. Because here's what I know. I know the expedition's early transmissions mentioned an anomaly—I decoded fragments before the flood, enough to know there was something worth salvaging upriver. I know you flew into weather no sane pilot would enter, alone, without orders, and came back. And I know that whatever's up there, you want it kept quiet, because every word out of your mouth since you landed has been engineered to make me stop asking questions." He paused. "Which means it's real. Which means it's valuable. Which means—"

"It means nothing you can bank, Bright." She stepped closer, and let him see it—whatever it was in her face that had changed upriver, in the eye, in the long silence after. "You have no data. You have no witnesses who'll corroborate you, because the ones who could are committed to the boring version and the ones who won't be are staying up there. You have a destroyed barge, a dead contract, and a pilot telling ghost stories. Take that to Vane. Tell him there's a storm that remembers voices, and your proof is that a debtor with a crashed reputation swears it's true. See what he says."

Something flickered across Bright's face then—the first genuine expression she'd ever seen him wear. It looked almost like respect, if respect could be bitter.

"He'll say you're insane," Bright said.

"Yes."

"He'll say I'm insane for entertaining it."

"Yes."

"And the debt?"

And there it was. The whole architecture of her life, laid bare in three words. Felix had spent years under that number—the real sum, the compounding interest, the cheerful men with clipboards who appeared wherever she landed twice. She had imagined this moment a hundred ways: begging, bargaining, running. She had not imagined it standing in the mud with nothing to offer and nothing left to lose.

"What about it?" she said.

Bright studied her. Then he laughed—a short, exhausted sound with no performance in it at all.

"You know what the funny thing is, Felix? Debts are instruments. They're claims on future value. We hold paper against you because you have a skill set, an aircraft, and a pattern of behavior we can predict." He kicked a sodden crate; it broke apart. "But a woman who flies into impossible storms and comes back with stories nobody believes isn't an asset. She's a liability with a propeller. Every hour we spend collecting on you is an hour we spend listening to testimony that makes us look like lunatics. Vane doesn't invest in madness. Vane invests in returns."

"So I'm written off."

"You're *voided*." He said it precisely, like a man closing a ledger. "Not forgiven—we don't forgive, it sets precedents. Just... uncollectible. There's a difference. Forgiveness means we were kind. Voided means you became more trouble than the principal was worth." He shrugged. "Congratulations. It's the only exit we offer and almost nobody qualifies."

Felix felt it land somewhere below her ribs—not joy, not yet, more like the moment after a hard landing when you realize the gear held. Free. The word felt foreign in her mouth, a currency she'd stopped believing existed.

"And if I talk?" she asked. "If I stand on a dock in some port town and tell everyone about the storm that listens?"

Bright was already turning away, wading back toward his ruined operation, but he answered over his shoulder, and the smile was back—real again now, in its way, because he'd finally found the shape of the loss and could wear it properly.

"Then you'll be the crazy pilot lady," he said. "There's one in every port. Nobody lends money to her either."

* * *

The official inquiry lasted nine days and concluded in eleven minutes.

They held it in the customs house because the courthouse was full of river water, and they convened it with the grim efficiency of administrators who wanted very much to be done. Seven expedition members had gone up the Kessari. Four came down it. One remained unaccounted for—Ilsa Voss, last seen heading deeper into the flood zone—and the coastal authority had neither the boats nor the appetite to go looking.

Felix testified last, after Anselm's careful hydrological account and after the young botanist's tearful corroboration of everything Anselm said. She told the truth. All of it, in order: the standing storm, the voices in the eye, the sabotage, the drift toward the coast, the flight into the moving wall of cloud with a microphone and a dead man's name in her throat. She told it plainly, without embellishment, because she had discovered that plainness was the most damning thing she could do—embellishment could be caught, but simple truth, told flatly, simply sounded like delusion wearing a straight face.

The magistrate listened with his chin on his fist. When she finished, the room was quiet except for the drip of a leak somewhere behind the paneling.

"Pilot Fairweather," he said at last. "You're describing a meteorological impossibility."

"I'm describing what happened."

"You're describing a storm that *remembers*."

"Yes, sir."

He exchanged a glance with the clerk. In the gallery, someone coughed. Felix recognized the look passing between the officials—she'd seen it her whole life, aimed at gamblers and prophets and anyone whose account of the world exceeded the world's allowance. Pity with paperwork attached.

"Extreme weather events," the magistrate said carefully, "can produce disorientation. Hypoxia. Auditory hallucination is well documented at altitude and under stress." He made a note. "We'll record the expedition's loss as a weather casualty with four survivors recovered. The anomaly will be logged as an unverified atmospheric phenomenon pending further study." A pause. "Pending further study" was the phrase bureaucrats used to bury things politely. "You're fortunate to be alive, Pilot."

"Yes," Felix said. "I am."

That was the verdict, in essence: she was fortunate, and she was unreliable, and the two facts canceled each other out so cleanly that no one would ever come looking. Anselm walked out beside her afterward, blinking in the gray light, and said quietly, "You told them everything."

"Everything."

"And they believed none of it."

"Not a word."

Anselm nodded slowly, and there was something in his face that wasn't quite relief and wasn't quite grief—the expression of a man watching a door close that he'd spent months trying to hold open. "Ilsa would say that's the best outcome possible," he said. "I keep waiting to feel that it's a tragedy instead. I'm not sure I do anymore." He stopped at the bottom of the customs house steps, mud sucking at his boots. "The data's safe?"

"Safe."

"Then we're done." He offered his hand, and she shook it. "Fly carefully, Felix. Though I suppose you don't anymore. Carefully, I mean."

"No," she agreed. "I don't think I do."

* * *

She spent the evening doing arithmetic of her own, sitting on the *Kestrel*'s wing with a logbook on her knees and a lamp hooked to the strut.

The numbers were strange to look at. Zero debt. Not paid—voided, which was different, which meant she hadn't earned her freedom so much as become worthless to the people who owned her. Her hands should have shaken with relief. Instead she kept writing figures that wouldn't resolve: fuel costs for the ferry runs downriver, still owed to the surviving expedition members, one trip each, promised. A new propeller blade. Airframe inspection after the storm flight—she'd find things bent in places she didn't want to know about. The total came to more money than she had, which was familiar, but for the first time in years the deficit was hers. Chosen. Owed to herself and to promises she'd actually made, rather than to cheerful men with clipboards.

It was, she decided, the best balance sheet of her life.

Below the numbers, in the private section of the log where she kept things no examiner would ever read, she wrote the other entry. The one that wasn't arithmetic at all:

*Storm holding position over valley as of departure. Stable. Listening. Left it what it asked for.*

She stared at that last line a long time. *What it asked for.* As though the thing had wants. As though she'd negotiated. But that was what it had been, in the end—not a battle, not a rescue, but a conversation conducted in the only language either party trusted: names, debts, confessions, truth offered freely. She had given the storm her real self, and it had settled onto its anchor like a dog lying down at a familiar voice, and the monsoon had broken around it, and the river had risen and erased every trace that any of it had happened.

Except in her. Except in the logbook. Except upriver, where a botanist named Ilsa Voss was keeping watch over a hollow of still air that remembered the dead.

Felix closed the book. Out past the strip, the harbor lights were coming on through the last of the ground mist, and the air smelled like clean rain and drying mud, and somewhere south a plane engine turned over—some other pilot, some other debt, some other season.

She had one more ferry run to make. The last survivor, the young botanist, wanted to go home to the coast, and Felix had promised, and promises were the only currency she dealt in now.

But as she stowed the logbook and checked the tie-downs, her eyes kept going north, upriver, into the dark where the clouds would be stacking themselves over the drowned valley, patient as a held breath. Next season was seven months away. The flight plan was already forming in her head—she could feel it assembling itself the way weather assembled, pressure building behind her sternum.

Some debts voided. Some kept.

She'd pay this one in person.
