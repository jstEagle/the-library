## 21. Downriver

The first thing the river gave back was a body of water where a landing strip used to be.

Felix stood on the wing strut of the Kestrel, ankle-deep in brown water that had swallowed the gravel bar she'd touched down on six weeks ago, and watched the monsoon finally do what monsoons were supposed to do: move on. The storm behind her—Emrys, though she hadn't said the name aloud yet, not even to herself—sat on its drowned valley like a cat returned to a favorite chair, rumbling contentedly, going nowhere. The sky south of it was scrubbed clean and blue in a way that felt almost obscene after what they'd all just lived through.

"You're sure it'll hold?" Dr. Anselm stood at the water's edge with his pack at his feet, looking older than his file photo by about fifteen years. His hands had stopped shaking two days ago. His voice hadn't quite caught up.

"The plane? It held together getting me here. It'll hold together getting you out." She patted the fuselage like it was a horse that might spook. "The river's another question. But the gauge at Serrat Falls dropped four inches overnight. We've got a window."

"And if we don't take it?"

"Then we wait for the next one, and the next one after that, and Bright's people find something to salvage out of this flood besides mud." She looked at him until he nodded. "We go today."

Four passengers. That was the arithmetic now. Seven had gone up the Kessari; four were coming down. Anselm. Okonkwo, the hydrologist, who hadn't spoken above a whisper since the night the storm swelled off its anchor and Felix talked it back home. Reyes, the geologist, who talked constantly, compulsively, as if silence might let something in. And Priya Chandrasekar, the doctor, who had set Felix's dislocated shoulder on the cave floor by lantern light and never once asked how she'd gotten hurt, which Felix understood was its own kind of mercy.

Three weren't coming down. Two had gone deeper with Ilsa Voss—Tomas and Wren, gone up into the rain shadow toward the eye, toward whatever Ilsa had decided the valley owed them, which was nothing, which was everything, which was silence. And one—Dev Anand, the expedition's radio technician—had gone downriver three weeks ago with Anselm's blessing and Ilsa's fury, trying to reach help, and the river had taken him somewhere between the falls and the coast. They'd found his pack snagged in a strainer of fallen bamboo. Just the pack.

Felix carried that arithmetic in her chest like ballast. Four up, four down, and every seat empty on the return legs would be a small grave she'd have to fly over.

"Chandrasekar first," she said. "She's got family in Port Calloway, and the longer she sits in this valley the harder it'll be to get the story straight."

"The story," Reyes said from the shade of the wing, "being that we found nothing."

"That we found *nothing*," Felix agreed. "A standing supercell over a drowned valley that answers voices—that's not a story, Reyes. That's a confession. Nobody confesses their way out of a Syndicate debt."

Anselm flinched at the word Syndicate. Good, Felix thought. Let it sting. He'd signed the same contract she had, the one with fine print neither of them read, and the fine print had teeth on both ends.

* * *

Priya cried for the first eleven minutes of the flight and then went very still, watching the Kessari unspool beneath them—the way it braided and split around islands that hadn't existed in the survey charts, the way whole forests lay on their sides in the flooded lowlands like men asleep in a field.

"It looks so ordinary from up here," she said finally.

"Everything does. That's the trick of altitude. It's why pilots make terrible witnesses."

"What will you tell them? At Calloway?"

Felix flew for a while before answering, threading a gap between two thunderheads that were doing honest, honest work—building, raining, dying, the way clouds were supposed to.

"I'll tell them the truth shaped like a lie. Equipment failure. Radio mast came down in a squall, camp flooded, team consolidated upstream on higher ground, comms down for the duration. It's even mostly true. The squall was real. The flooding was real. Higher ground was real." She glanced over. "I'm just leaving out the part where the higher ground was under a living storm that remembered my dead brother's voice."

Priya looked at her for a long moment. "You have a brother?"

"Had." Felix adjusted the throttle more precisely than it needed adjusting. "He's how I learned to read weather. He's also why I owe the Vane Syndicate more money than this plane is worth. One led to the other. There's a story there too, and nobody's hearing that one either."

They came into Port Calloway at midafternoon, and the town rose out of the haze the way it always did—tin roofs, fish smoke, the harbor cranes standing like patient herons. Priya wept again on final approach, quietly, both hands pressed flat against the window glass.

The officials met them at the airstrip: a regional administrator named Corvo with sweat rings under his arms, a public health officer, and a man in a gray suit who introduced himself as being "with the licensing authority" and whose shoes were too clean for anyone who worked near rivers. Felix recognized the species immediately. Not Syndicate—Bright's people dressed better and smiled more—but the same genus. Government was just another lender. Everyone wanted collateral.

"Dr. Chandrasekar," Corvo said, shaking her hand with elaborate gentleness, as if she might be contagious or holy. "Thank God. We feared—" He let the sentence die, professionally. "And the others?"

"Consolidated upstream," Felix said. "Camp site flooded. They're on high ground with supplies. Comms are down—I'll be running a shuttle."

"A shuttle." The gray-suit man wrote something down. "Through active monsoon conditions."

"Through *my* monsoon conditions," Felix said, and smiled the smile she'd learned from Bright himself, the one that said *I am cheerful and I am ruthless and you do not want to audit me.* "I flew support for this expedition for six weeks, sir. I know this river's weather better than your licensing authority knows its own paperwork. You want those scientists home, you let me fly."

It took two hours of forms, tea, and strategic incompetence—Felix misplacing documents, misunderstanding questions, developing an accent thicker than the one she'd grown up with—but by evening Priya was in a guesthouse bed with clean sheets and a phone line to her sister, and Felix was walking back to the strip through blue dusk with the gray-suit man's business card in her pocket, destined for the first drain she passed.

At the drain, she paused. The card said MERIDIAN OVERSIGHT DIVISION. She turned it over twice, then let it go. The water took it spinning.

"Sorry," she told the drain. "Nothing personal. It's just that everything I know is either worthless or priceless, and I can't afford either."

* * *

Okonkwo next. He weighed almost nothing when he climbed into the passenger seat, as if the valley had been slowly unmaking him, and he spent the entire flight with his forehead against the window, looking back.

At the halfway mark—a fuel cache on a bluff above a tributary, a forty-gallon drum and a hand pump Felix had staged before the season started—they sat on the drum lids eating dried mango and watching the southern horizon, where Emrys sat on its anchor like a mountain that had decided to become weather instead.

"It's still there," Okonkwo said.

"It's still there."

"After everything. After the machines, after Bright's barges, after—" He stopped. Started again. "In the cave, when it began to drift, I thought: this is what we did. All our instruments, all our noise. We taught it to move. And then your voice on the radio, playing those recordings, calling it home, and it *went* home, and I keep thinking—what does that make us? What does that make *it*?"

Felix chewed her mango. Downriver of them, a fishing boat dragged its nets through the gold light, and the fishermen waved at the plane on the bluff, and one of them shouted something cheerful about the season breaking early.

"It makes it a neighbor," she said finally. "A neighbor nobody can introduce at a party without starting a war. So we don't introduce it. We wave across the fence. We keep our music down."

"You sound like Ilsa."

The words landed harder than he probably intended. Felix looked away, at the storm, at the smudge of it on the horizon, and thought about Ilsa Voss standing in the shallows of the drowned valley two days ago, dry clothes, dry eyes, a woman who had made her decision so completely that there was no grief left in her, only architecture.

"Somebody has to stay," Ilsa had said. "Not to guard it. To *witness* it. There's a difference, Felix. A guard wants to keep people out. A witness keeps faith with what's there. When the next survey comes—and one will come, in five years, ten—someone needs to have been here the whole time, so the valley has a history that isn't a hole. So it reads as boring. Boring is armor."

"And your life?" Felix had asked. "Your career? Your name on the discovery?"

"My name on the discovery is a target painted on a storm," Ilsa said. "I'd rather be anonymous and right than famous and the reason they cage it." She'd almost smiled then, the first time Felix had seen anything cross her face that wasn't resolve. "Besides. Someone has to teach it not to answer strangers. It takes years. It takes patience. It takes exactly the kind of stubborn woman who orders radios destroyed to protect a secret she believes in."

Felix had wanted to hate her for it—for Tomas and Wren, for Dev, for the whole shape of the choice. She still did, some days, in the hour before dawn. But standing in the shallows, watching the lightning crawl Emrys's flanks in its slow, patient, almost-contented patterns, she couldn't find the hatred anywhere in her body. Only the recognition. Ilsa was doing with her whole life what Felix was doing with her testimony: burning the evidence so no one could weaponize the truth.

"Yeah," Felix said to Okonkwo, shouldering the fuel hose. "I guess I do sound like Ilsa. Don't tell her. She'll be insufferable."

Okonkwo laughed—a cracked, rusty sound, the first one she'd heard out of him—and it occurred to Felix that this was part of the job too. Not just flying them out. Flying the weight off them, pound by pound, leg by leg, until what remained could pass through a customs checkpoint without setting off any alarms, including the ones inside themselves.

* * *

Reyes talked the whole flight down, which was how Felix knew he wasn't okay.

He narrated the riverbanks, the cloud types, the geology of every cutbank—strata, uplift, alluvium—as if the landscape were a hostage he needed to keep talking to. Felix let him. She answered in monosyllables and flew the plane, and somewhere past Serrat Falls, in the long gold slant of late afternoon, he ran out of geology and finally said the true thing:

"I keep hearing it."

Felix kept her eyes on the horizon. "Hearing what?"

"In the eye. Before you came. The calm center—it plays voices back. You know that. You were in there." His hands were flat on his knees, pressing down, keeping himself in the seat. "I heard my mother. She died when I was nineteen and I heard her say my name like I was ten years old and late for dinner. And I know—I *know*—it was just the storm replaying something, some pattern, some echo of me wanting it. But Felix, it didn't feel like an echo. It felt like attendance. Like something had taken attendance and my mother's voice was on the roster."

The river bent south beneath them, wide and slow and glittering, carrying its silt down to a delta where a million people slept tonight without knowing what hung on the horizon north of them, patient as a lighthouse.

"Here's what I know," Felix said. "I heard a voice in there too. Somebody dead. Somebody I loved. And I've spent every hour since deciding whether it was a miracle or a machine or a mirror, and I'll tell you what I've concluded." She banked gently around a rain shaft, giving it the respect it deserved. "It doesn't matter. Whatever it was, it's not for sale, it's not for study, and it's not for telling. Because the minute it's data, it's property. And the minute it's property, it's Bright's. And I would rather fly this plane into that storm forever than hand my dead brother's voice to the Vane Syndicate."

Reyes was quiet for a full minute, which for Reyes was a geological epoch.

"They'll ask us to sign statements," he said at last. "Inquiry boards. Insurance. Meridian, whoever they are."

"They will."

"What do we say?"

"We say the storm was anomalous and unstable and it collapsed after the monsoon broke, as storms do. We say the valley flooded and the equipment was lost. We say we're grateful to be alive and we'd rather not discuss it further, and then we cry a little, because that part's easy." She glanced at him. "Can you do that?"

He looked out the window at the ordinary, beautiful, lying sky. "My mother's voice is in that valley," he said. "And I'm going to spend the rest of my life knowing the most important discovery in human history is sitting on a hillside being babysat by a botanist, and I'm going to say *collapsed, as storms do.*"

"Yeah," Felix said. "Welcome to the expedition."

* * *

Anselm came last, and he brought the data.

They were on the bluff at the fuel cache, the last stop before Port Calloway, and he handed her the case the way you hand someone a newborn: two hands, both arms, an expression that dared her to drop it. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were the expedition's raw notebooks—six weeks of readings, sketches, sound measurements, Ilsa's own field notes in her cramped upright hand, everything the storm had been before it became a secret.

"Bright's people burned the copies," Anselm said. "What they could find. This is the original record. Every observation we ever made."

"Why are you giving it to me?"

"Because you flew into a wall of rotating weather against direct orders to find us. Because you talked a living storm into coming home. And because—" he hesitated, and Felix watched a man decide to trust someone for the second time in one season, which she knew from experience cost more than the first, "—because Ilsa trusted you enough to stay. She doesn't trust easily. If she thought you'd sell this, she'd have put you in the ground beside the radios."

"She tried to stop me mid-flight."

"She tried to stop you because you were flying into a hurricane with a microphone," Anselm said mildly. "That's not distrust. That's triage."

Felix weighed the case in her hands. Six weeks of wonder, wrapped in oilcloth. Enough to make a hundred careers. Enough to start a war. Enough, if the wrong person photographed a single page, to put coordinates on a map that would never come off it.

"What am I supposed to do with it?"

"Keep it somewhere no auditor will ever look," Anselm said. "You're good at invisible. You've been invisible your whole life—it's why you're alive and Dev isn't. Keep it invisible. And someday, when the world gets wiser or Ilsa gets tired or the storm decides to introduce itself, someone will need to know it was real. That we saw it. That it sang to us." He looked north, at the horizon, at the smudge. "Truth doesn't have to be published to be true, Felix. It just has to be kept."

She stowed the case under the cargo net, behind the emergency raft, in the one compartment she'd never once opened in front of another soul. Then she shook Anselm's hand, and he climbed aboard, and they flew the last leg into Port Calloway through a sunset so clean and generous it felt like an apology.

The officials were waiting again. Corvo, the health officer, a new gray suit—this one a woman, shoes only slightly less clean. They walked Anselm through the same gentle questions, and Felix stood beside him and listened to him deliver the story they'd built together—flood, consolidation, equipment loss, gratitude—and she added her corroborating details in the flat competent voice of a contract pilot who had seen nothing worth seeing, and the woman from Meridian wrote it all down, and Felix watched her write it and thought: *you are transcribing a masterpiece and you will never know.*

Afterward, on the dark airstrip, Corvo shook her hand.

"Four flights, four survivors," he said. "The Syndicate's people were asking about you in the harbor bars, you know. Something about outstanding obligations."

"Were they." Felix smiled her Bright smile. "Funny. I don't owe the Syndicate anything. Ask them to produce a ledger. Ask them to produce *evidence.*" She let the word sit there between them, fat and meaningless. "I hear the flood took a lot of records this season. Barges, generators, filing cabinets—all washed downriver. Terrible thing, weather."

Corvo blinked at her, decided he didn't want to know, and wished her safe travels.

* * *

The last flight was hers alone.

She took the Kestrel up at dawn with an empty cabin and a full fuel load and pointed the nose north one more time—not to the valley, not today, but along the edge of it, the long way home, a detour no flight plan would explain and no official would think to question. The morning air was rinsed and cool. Below her the Kessari ran high and fast, hauling the last of the flood seaward, and ahead, fixed on its drowned hillside, Emrys stood in the new day and grumbled its low continuous grumble, shedding an occasional dignified bolt, going nowhere.

She keyed the radio once, briefly, on the old expedition frequency—the one Ilsa had ordered destroyed, the one frequency in the world that was now theirs alone.

"Valley, this is Kestrel. Shuttle complete. Four delivered. All quiet downriver." A pause. The wind moved against the canopy, steady, attentive. "The data's safe. The story's buried. Do what you do, Voss. I'll check in next season."

Static answered her—not dead static, not empty static, but the rich textured hush of open air over open country, and beneath it, just at the threshold of hearing, something that might have been wind moving through a drowned forest, or might have been a voice, or might have been nothing at all, which was the only answer she'd ever get and the only one she needed.

She turned south for the coast, alone in the cockpit with an empty seat beside her and a case of impossible truth riding in the dark behind her head, and she realized her hands were steady on the yoke for the first time in six weeks. No debt collector waiting at the field. No handler in her ear. No ledger with her name in it. Just sky, and fuel, and the long bright road of the river running home below her.

Free, she thought. Actually free. It fit strangely, like a coat cut for someone else.

She was thirty miles south of the valley, climbing through the last cool layer, when the radio crackled—not the expedition band, but the civil channel, a tired dispatcher's voice reading traffic from the coastal stations. Most of it was routine. Tide tables, fish quotas, a grounded ferry.

Then, in the middle of the list, flat as a stock quote:

*"—advisory from Meridian Oversight, all licensed operators: survey contracts pending for upper Kessari basin, geological and meteorological assessment, bids close end of quarter. Repeat: upper Kessari basin. Assessment teams to mobilize within ninety days—"*

Felix flew in silence for a long moment, watching the storm shrink in the mirror behind her, small and patient and utterly unaware that the world had already begun turning its paperwork toward it.

"Ninety days," she said to the empty cockpit. "Okay, Voss. Ninety days."

She pushed the throttle forward, and the Kestrel dove for the coast, and behind her the storm stood on its hill and waited, the way it had always waited, for someone to speak first.
