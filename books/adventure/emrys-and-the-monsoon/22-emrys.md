## 22. Emrys

The hangar at Port Calloway smelled the way it always had—engine oil, salt rust, and the sweet rot of canvas that had given up arguing with the weather. Felix sat on an upturned crate beneath the wing of the *Kestrel's Regret*, her logbook open on her knee, and for the first time in eleven years she was in no hurry to be anywhere else.

The plane looked worse than she remembered. That was fair; she probably did too. The port-side strut she'd replaced with salvage steel after the crash-landing still held a faint bend, a permanent apology written into the airframe. The fabric over the third rib of the left wing had been patched so many times it looked like a quilt made by someone with more courage than thread. But the engine turned over that morning when she'd tested it—turned over with a cough and then a clean, stubborn roar—and the mechanic who'd come to look at it had whistled low and said, *Whoever flew this thing through what you say you flew it through, they were either very good or very lucky.*

Felix had smiled and said nothing about which one she'd been.

Outside, the monsoon was breaking up the way it always did by late autumn: ragged, apologetic, spending its last big storms on the coast like a gambler down to his final chips. The sky over Port Calloway was the color of pewter going soft at the edges, and somewhere out past the breakwater the sea was working itself into a slow gray fury that meant nothing to anyone but pilots and fishermen.

She'd been grounded four months. Not officially—the aviation board had found no cause to suspend her, mostly because the official account of her last flight read like a drunkard's diary. *Pilot reports severe weather anomaly upriver. Pilot reports weather anomaly responsive to voice. Pilot requests immediate evacuation of Kessari Valley. Request denied pending verification.* They'd asked her, gently, if she'd been drinking. She'd said no. They'd asked if she'd hit her head. She'd said yes, actually, quite hard, and watched their faces relax with relief, because a head injury explained everything and required nothing.

The medical review had cleared her. The debt review had been more interesting.

* * *

The Vane Syndicate's letter had arrived in September, on cream paper, in the cheerful round hand she'd learned to dread. She'd opened it expecting a summons, a lien, maybe Bright himself grinning on her doorstep with a contract and a pen.

Instead it was two paragraphs long.

*Dear Ms. Fairweather,* it began. *Following our internal review of assets lost during the Kessari River operation, the Syndicate has determined that outstanding balances associated with your account are unrecoverable under current circumstances. Your obligations are hereby voided. We thank you for your service and wish you clear skies.*

No signature. Just the Syndicate's seal—a stylized wind vane, pointing in every direction at once—and below it, in smaller print, the sentence that told her everything: *This determination is final and not subject to appeal.*

They couldn't collect against a woman with no proof of anything. Her testimony sounded like madness; her flight logs had been sealed as evidence and then quietly unsealed and then quietly lost, all within six weeks; Anselm's data—the raw readings she'd carried out of the valley sewn into the lining of her jacket—had gone into the flood surge along with Bright's generators and cages of recording equipment, dissolved into the same brown water that had swallowed the barges. The Syndicate had salvaged nothing. Worse than nothing: they'd salvaged a story so implausible that repeating it made *them* sound crazy, and the Syndicate could survive many things but not sounding crazy. So they'd cut her loose the way you amputate a finger to save the hand.

She should have felt triumphant. Some nights, drinking cheap rum on the seawall, she almost did. Mostly she felt the way the valley must have felt after the storm settled back onto its anchor: quiet, and heavier for it.

Because here was the arithmetic nobody warned you about. Freedom purchased with credibility spends strangely. She was free to fly anywhere, work anywhere, tell anyone anything—and everything she might tell was worthless. If she stood in a tavern and described a living storm that listened, that answered, that kept the voices of the dead folded in its calm center like letters in a drawer, she'd get exactly what she'd gotten from the aviation board: kind eyes, a gentle tone, and a form asking about head injuries.

Some things you survived by telling everyone. Those were the easy ones—shipwrecks, crashes, debts, betrayals. You put them into words and the words carried them away and other people's sympathy did the rest.

And some things you survived by telling no one.

* * *

The logbook was new—she'd filled the old one past its last page somewhere over the drowned forest, and this one she'd bought in Port Calloway from a stationer who sold her the good paper because she'd paid cash and looked like she'd cry if he didn't. She'd started it the way she started every logbook: date, aircraft, route, remarks. Clean columns. A pilot's tidy little fiction that a life can be entered line by line.

But tonight she wasn't logging a flight. Tonight she was doing something she'd never done in eleven years of keeping records: writing the truth where only she would ever read it.

*Private entry,* she wrote. *Not for any authority, board, or syndicate, present or future.*

Then she stopped, pen hovering, and looked out through the hangar doors at the darkening water.

What do you call a thing like that? The scientists had called it *the anomaly*, which was what people called anything that refused to fit. Anselm had called it *colleague*, half-joking, in the cave beneath the rain shadow, with the reverence of a man who'd spent his whole career studying weather and finally been studied back. Ilsa had never named it at all—that was the point of Ilsa, the whole architecture of her: a thing unnamed could not be owned, catalogued, weaponized, or sold. *Anything measured gets owned.* Felix could still hear her saying it in the eye's still air, calm as a woman discussing crop rotation, offering erasure like a gift wrapped in silence.

But Ilsa had stayed upriver. Ilsa had chosen to become part of the secret, a warden in a drowned valley, and Felix had chosen otherwise—chosen to walk out, ferry the survivors home one by one, lie gently to officials with her hands steady and her heart hammering, keep Anselm's choice and Ilsa's choice locked behind her teeth. Four of the seven home safe. Two gone deeper, by their own will, into the green country under the standing storm. One—the third who'd gone downriver for help—never accounted for, and Felix had flown that stretch of river twice in the autumn looking, knowing even as she burned the fuel that the river keeps what it keeps.

The storm had let her go. That was the part she circled back to, night after night, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. In the eye, with the archive playing and her own voice added to it—her real name, her real debts, her real confession, spoken aloud to the wind like a woman testifying at a trial with no judge—the storm had slowed. Turned. Settled back onto its anchor while the monsoon broke around it like a held breath finally released. It had heard her. Not her instruments, not her transmissions, not her usefulness. *Her.* The whole ragged truth of her, offered freely, the one thing no faction had planned to give.

And in that still hollow of air where sound behaved like memory, before she'd spoken, the storm had spoken first. Her name—in a voice she hadn't heard in nine years, a voice that belonged to a man who'd taught her to fly in a grass strip outside Meridian and died in weather she'd have known how to read, if she'd been older, if she'd been better, if she'd been there.

*Emrys.*

Her father's name. Spoken back to her out of a storm's calm heart, in his own voice, warm and slightly amused, the way he'd said it when she was small and he wanted her attention: *Emrys, look at the sky. Emrys, tell me what the clouds are doing.* She'd never told another living soul. There was no one alive who'd recognize it. The expedition's records—if any fragment survived—would show a pilot named F. Fairweather. The name Emrys existed nowhere except in her memory and, apparently, in the archive of a watershed that had been listening longer than any of them had been alive.

So that was the name. Not the anomaly. Not the asset. Not the discovery, the phenomenon, the opportunity. *Emrys.* Because whatever else it was—and it might be older than the river, older than the valley it drowned, older than names—it had answered her with the one word that proved it wasn't just weather. Weather doesn't know your dead. Weather doesn't hand you back the voice you've spent nine years failing to forget.

Naming it felt dangerous. Naming it felt necessary. Ilsa would have disapproved—Ilsa would have said a name is a handle, and a handle is for carrying, and anything that can be carried can be delivered to someone who'll pay for it. But Ilsa had her valley and her vigil, and Felix had a logbook and a long winter, and a secret kept entirely silent curdles into something rotten. It needed to live somewhere. It needed, at least once, to be said out loud, even if the only listener was paper.

* * *

She dipped the pen and wrote.

*I name the storm Emrys. It stands over the drowned valley of the upper Kessari, anchored where it has anchored since before the maps, and it listens—to the river, to the forest, to the voices of everything that has ever spoken inside its reach. It answered me with my father's voice. It accepted my confession and let the monsoon break. It is not a weapon. It is not an asset. It is not mine, or the Syndicate's, or science's, or Ilsa Voss's, though she guards it now and God help anyone who comes to take it from her.*

*I will not report it. Not because I doubt what I saw—I doubt nothing, not anymore—but because the moment it is measured it is owned, and the moment it is owned it is aimed. Let the official record show a pilot who hit her head. Let the Syndicate write off their losses. Let the aviation board file my incident report in the drawer where impossible things go to be forgotten. The truth is safer as madness than as cargo.*

*But I know. And I'm going back.*

She sat with that last line a while, watching the ink dry, listening to the wind worry the hangar roof. Then she turned the page and did the thing she'd been circling all evening, the thing that had brought her down to the hangar with the good pen and the good paper instead of staying in her rented room above the chandlery with the bad lamp and the cheaper rum.

She filed a flight plan.

Not with the authorities—she'd done that properly, in triplicate, that afternoon, because she was done being a pilot who cut corners; corners were how the Syndicate had gotten its hooks in her in the first place. The official plan was boring on purpose: *Kestrel's Regret, single-engine, coastal survey, Port Calloway to Meridian and return, VFR, three days.* Nobody would look twice. Nobody ever had.

But in the private log, beneath the naming, she wrote the real itinerary:

*Departure: first week of the dry season, when the river drops and the old landing strips surface like bones. Route: upriver. All the way. Cargo: spare parts for a radio Ilsa will pretend not to need, coffee she will pretend not to want, and letters from four people who made it home and cannot say why they're writing to a woman who doesn't exist in any manifest. Passenger: none. Witness: one.*

Anselm had taken a teaching post in the capital, lecturing on mesoscale dynamics to students who thought he was being poetic when he described storms as systems that "integrate information." He'd shaken Felix's hand at the dock in a grip that lasted one second too long and said, *You know where to find me if it ever changes its mind.* The botanist, Pell, had gone back to cataloguing orchids and sent Felix a pressed flower from the rain shadow, no note, which was its own kind of message. The doctor had cried on the ferry and thanked her and never once asked her to confirm that any of it had happened, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for her all year.

And Ilsa—somewhere upriver, in the green drowned country, keeping her watch. Felix had seen her exactly once since, from altitude, in October, on the unofficial flight she'd told the board was a coastal survey. The valley had appeared through a gap in the cumulus the way cathedrals appear: suddenly, impossibly, all at once. The storm stood where it had always stood, vast and patient, lightning crawling its flanks in those patterns too regular to be random. And on the ridge below it, small as a punctuation mark, a figure had stood in a clearing and raised one arm—not waving, Felix had decided. Saluting. Or warning. With Ilsa it had always been both.

Felix had waggled her wings—two dips, the old bush-pilot signal for *message received*—and turned south, and hadn't told a soul, and had slept better that night than she had in years.

* * *

The harbor light came on outside the hangar, a yellow bloom in the blue dusk. Somewhere down the quay a fisherman was arguing cheerfully with his son about tide tables, their voices carrying across the water the way voices do when the air goes cool and honest. Felix closed the logbook and held it in both hands and felt its weight settle into her palms—not the weight of paper, the weight of custody.

Eleven years she'd defined herself by motion. Keep moving, never land twice, never sign anything you've read, never carry anything heavier than fuel. It had worked the way a tourniquet works: brilliantly, and only for as long as you could stand the loss. The Syndicate had bought her desperation with a smile; the expedition had bought her skill with a contract she hadn't read; the storm had bought nothing at all, because you can't buy what a person gives freely, and in the eye she had finally understood that everything that mattered in her life had been given freely—her father's lessons, her love of weather, her confession, her choice to go back.

She was still broke. The *Kestrel's Regret* needed a fabric job she couldn't afford and a propeller balance she'd keep putting off. Grounded, broke, and free: that was the ledger now, and for the first time in her adult life the books balanced.

She stood, tucked the logbook inside her jacket—over the heart, where Anselm's data had ridden out of the valley—and walked to the hangar doors. The last light was going off the water. Far to the north, past the river mouth, past the drowned forests and the ridges and the hundred miles of country no map got right, a single towering cloud caught the sunset and held it, burning rose-gold at the top long after the sky around it had gone gray.

It shouldn't have been visible from Port Calloway. Nothing that far upriver was visible from the coast; she knew the geometry, she'd have sworn to it in any court. But there it stood, fixed and patient, holding the light like a lantern in a window.

Felix stood in the doorway a long time, watching it, and then she raised her hand—not waving, not saluting, something that was both—and spoke into the cooling air, quietly, the way you speak to someone across a room when everyone else has gone to bed.

"Message received," she said. "I'm coming home."

The cloud held the light a moment longer. Then the dark took it, gently, the way the river takes everything it means to keep.

She went to find a wrench. Three days of coastal survey first—she'd filed the plan, and she was done cutting corners—but the dry season was coming, and the strips would be surfacing like bones, and somewhere north a storm that knew her father's voice was waiting to hear how the story ended.

It wasn't ending. That was the thing she'd finally learned, sitting in the eye of it with her whole life spoken aloud: stories that matter don't end. They just change pilots.

Felix Fairweather rolled up her sleeves and went to work on her airplane, humming, in the hangar smell of oil and salt and canvas, under a sky she no longer pretended to understand.
