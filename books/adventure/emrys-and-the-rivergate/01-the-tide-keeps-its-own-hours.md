## 1. The Tide Keeps Its Own Hours

The tide kept its own hours, and Ada Eriksen had learned to keep them too.

Not the tidy hours printed in the almanac at Halvard's chandlery—those were for tourists and oyster boats—but the real ones, the ones you learned by standing in cold water up to your shins twice a day until your ankles remembered what your head refused to. The Rivergate estuary ran on a rhythm older than any clockmaker's ambition, and it did not care that it was October, or that the season had folded itself away like linen in a chest, or that Ada was the only person left on this stretch of coast who had nowhere better to be.

They woke before dawn in the boathouse because the cold woke them. That was the arrangement: no alarm clock, just the damp seeping through the tarpaulin they'd strung over the old boat cradle, finding the gap between sleeping bag and dignity. The boathouse belonged to a man named Pell who wintered in Torquay and asked no rent so long as Ada kept the padlock oiled and the windows boarded against storms. It smelled of tar and rope-rot and the ghost of outboard fuel, and after four months Ada had stopped noticing, which felt like a kind of belonging, or its cheapest imitation.

They sat up in the dark and listened to the water.

The Rivergate moved beneath the floorboards—literally beneath; the boathouse stood on pilings over a slipway that hadn't launched anything but eel traps in thirty years—and at this hour it made a sound like slow breathing. In and out. The estuary filling, the estuary emptying. Ada had grown fluent in it the way their mother had once been fluent in dead languages, and the thought arrived uninvited, as it did every morning, and was set down again, as it was every morning, like a stone carried to the same pile and added to the same cairn.

Ingrid Eriksen had been gone eleven weeks now. Not gone-gone—she'd been gone-gone since Ada was nineteen, off to some dig or other conference or other river valley, postcards with museum logos, a motherhood conducted by correspondence. This was a different kind of gone. Three weeks ago her last message had come from somewhere upriver, a text with no punctuation: *found something at the source don't wait for me.* And then nothing. No calls returned. The university said she'd taken leave. The police, when Ada finally worked up the courage to file something that wasn't quite a missing-person report because Ingrid would have hated the fuss, had looked at Ada with the particular pity reserved for adult children of difficult parents and said, gently, that Dr. Eriksen was a grown woman with a history of extended fieldwork.

Ada dug shell middens instead of arguing.

* * *

The middens were the town's oldest shame and Ada's steadiest income. Along the low cliffs south of the harbor, where the salt grass gave way to exposed clay, the prehistoric refuse heaps lay banded in the earth like dark veins in pale stone—thousands of years of oyster suppers, periwinkle feasts, the ordinary meals of people whose names had washed out of memory entirely. The Heritage Trust—that was the *Rivergate* Trust, different animal entirely, though people confused them constantly and the confusion suited somebody—paid a modest fee for salvage excavation before winter storms took another meter of coastline. Ada had the only pair of hands in the valley that knew how to read a midden without destroying it, a skill inherited, like everything else, from Ingrid.

So they knelt in the clay at first light with a hand trowel and a dustpan brush, working a section that had slumped in the last spring tide, and let the work do what work does: occupy the hands so the mind can go where it needs to without supervision.

Oyster. Oyster. Cockle. Charcoal lens—a cooking episode, maybe four hundred years back. A fragment of pottery, grit-tempered, the rim worn smooth by someone's thumb long before anyone thought to write down the name of this river. Ada turned it in their fingers. The clay held the faint curve of a fingerprint—not the maker's, pressed into wet clay, but a user's, worn into the surface over years of handling. Somebody had held this pot the way Ada held their thermos. Somebody had eaten beside this same gray water when it was younger and the villages upstream still had names on maps.

"You're talking to yourself again," Ada said aloud, because there was no one to hear it, and the wind took the words out over the flats and did nothing with them.

That was the shape of the days now. Dig until the light went thin. Walk into town once a week for supplies, keeping to the back streets, avoiding the window of the bakery where Mrs. Trelawny held court, because Mrs. Trelawny's gossip had teeth and Ada's family was the softest thing in the valley to bite. *That Eriksen woman, always digging where she shouldn't. And now the child's gone strange too, living in that boat shed like a hermit, no wonder, runs in the family, the mother never could leave things buried.*

Ada had heard a version of it through the chandlery door three weeks ago and had walked out without the rope they'd come for. Bryn Halvard had chased them down the quay with the rope and taken no money for it, which was its own kind of unbearable kindness.

"Your mum," Bryn had said, not asking a question, watching Ada with those flat gray eyes that missed nothing on the water and pretended to miss everything on land.

"Is fine," Ada had said. "Working."

Bryn had nodded slowly, the way she nodded at weather she didn't believe, and gone back to coiling line, and Ada had walked home along the seawall hating how easily lying came lately. It was becoming a skill. Like the trowel work. Like reading tides. Everything was becoming a skill except the one thing they needed, which was knowing what to do about a mother who had sent a message from the mouth of the river and then dissolved into it.

* * *

The tide was wrong that afternoon.

Ada noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a song you know by heart—not immediately, but as an itch, a small dissonance in the body before the mind catches up. They'd finished the midden section by two o'clock and walked the tideline north toward the cliffs below Harrow Point, checking storm damage as much as anything, boots sinking in the wet sand, gulls arguing overhead about something eternal.

And the water was too far out.

Not dramatically. Nothing a tourist would question. But Ada had spent four months learning this beach the way you learn a face, and the wrack line—the tangle of kelp and driftwood marking the high tide's reach—sat a good eight meters beyond where it should have sat for this hour of this day, given the moon, given the season. The pools that usually never emptied, the ones that held blennies and stranded shrimp all summer, lay open to the air, their anemones folded shut like fists.

An anomalous low tide. Meteorological, probably. Pressure systems did strange things; a deep low offshore could pull the sea back like a held breath. There'd be a rational explanation and Ada could produce it if asked, could cite the barometric records, could give a little lecture in their mother's crisp cadence.

But nobody was asking. And the beach lay exposed further than Ada had ever seen it, all the way to the base of the cliffs where the water never went, where the guidebooks said the rock fell sheer into six fathoms.

Ada stood at the edge of the exposed zone for a long time, wind pushing their hood back, and felt the itch become something else. Curiosity, yes—but underneath it, older and less comfortable, the specific hunger that had gotten them disowned-by-silence from half their mother's world: the need to look at the thing everyone agreed not to look at. Ingrid had called it *the digger's disease*. She'd said it laughing, once, before the silences started. *We can't walk past a closed door, either of us. It's why we're good at this and bad at everything else.*

The base of the cliffs at Harrow Point was a closed door.

Ada walked toward it.

The going was slow—the exposed seabed was treacherous with weed-slick rock and old oyster clusters that could open a boot sole like paper—and the light was already leaning gold and long, which meant the window would close soon. Whatever pulled the sea out would, by some mechanism Ada chose not to examine too closely, bring it back. The tide keeps its own hours. You borrow time from it at interest.

Fifty meters from the cliff base, Ada saw the marks.

Boot prints. Recent—the edges still sharp, the sand not yet slumped into them. Two sets, actually, one larger, one smaller, both heading toward the cliff base and both coming back. Someone else had walked this impossible beach recently, during whatever window this anomaly opened, and had gone deliberately to the place where the water never went.

Ada's pulse picked up in a way that had nothing to do with exertion. They crouched and read the prints the way Ingrid had taught them to read a trench: weight distribution, stride length, direction changes. The smaller set paused often—turned side-on, looking up, looking around. Cautious. The larger set walked straight, purposeful, like someone who'd been here before.

Or like someone following instructions.

The cliff base, when Ada reached it, was not what the geology survey promised. The survey showed solid limestone. What Ada found, behind a curtain of hanging ivy and storm-tangled blackthorn, was a seam in the rock—a vertical fissure widened by something that was not natural erosion, its edges too regular, tooled, cut by hands that had wanted a doorway here and had made one, however many centuries ago. And at its base, revealed by the retreating sea for perhaps the first time in living memory, a mouth.

A cave mouth, low and dark, breathing cold air that smelled of stone and salt and something else—something organic and old, like the inside of a sealed room, like the air in a tomb before the first lamp disturbs it.

Ada crouched at the threshold. The cave floor was dry here, above the normal reach of even the lowest tides, and dry floors preserve evidence. There were more boot prints inside, fading into shadow. And on the rock beside the entrance, at the height of a standing person's shoulder, carved into the limestone with a confidence that made the hair rise on Ada's arms:

A mark. A spiral, cut deep, wound tight at the center, opening outward like water turning. Below it, worn shallower but unmistakable, a second mark: a horizontal line crossed by three vertical strokes.

A toll sign. Ada knew it from their mother's monographs—from the Bronze Age trackways chapter she'd written before Ada was born, the one with the photograph of a marker stone from a drowned valley in Norway. *Toll-markers,* Ingrid had written. *Threshold charges. Passage here is permitted, but not free. Admission charged by legend.*

Someone had charged tolls at this door when the sea stood elsewhere. Someone had believed this place mattered enough to guard it, price it, make you pay in story or silver or oath to pass.

The wind shifted. Far out across the flats, the water had begun, very quietly, to come back.

Ada should have noted the location, photographed the mark, retreated, reported it properly to the Heritage office, done everything in the correct order like a professional. That was the sensible sequence. That was what Ingrid would have done—no. That was what Ingrid would have *said* she'd do, while her eyes went bright and her feet stayed exactly where they were.

Ada clicked on their headlamp and stepped through the door.

* * *

Inside, the passage sloped down and then leveled, the ceiling rising enough to stand after ten crawling meters, and the sound of the sea fell away behind them like a coat shrugged off. The air was cold and utterly still. The headlamp beam found walls that were not natural either—flattened in places, dressed, and marked. More spirals. More tally-strokes. Rows of them, some faded to whispers, some still sharp-edged, receding into the dark beyond the reach of the light.

Ada walked slowly, cataloguing by habit, naming by training, and feeling beneath the training something that had no professional vocabulary at all. It was like walking into a sentence begun thousands of years ago and never finished. The marks weren't random. They had grammar. The spirals clustered near the entrance; the tallies deepened with distance, as if the cave recorded journeys, payments, passages—one stroke each? one traveler? one year?—a ledger written in stone by people who had understood something about this place that the maps had forgotten.

The passage turned. Beyond the turn, the floor changed—sanded smooth, swept clean within recent memory—and Ada's stomach tightened, because swept floors mean visitors, and visitors mean the prints outside had come from here, and here was not abandoned.

Against the wall, hung on a rusted iron bracket that had been set into the rock with molten lead, someone had left a lantern. Old pattern, brass and glass, decades of corrosion green-black on the metal. Ada lifted it carefully and turned the base to the light.

Stamped into the brass, crisp as the day a die struck it: an interlocked R and G, crowned with a wave motif.

The Rivergate Trust crest.

Ada stood very still in the cold dark, holding a piece of the present inside a place that should have belonged only to the past, and felt the two timelines grind against each other like tectonic plates. The Trust owned the water company, the ferry licenses, half the waterfront, the flood defenses, the archives. The Trust did not lose lanterns in caves. The Trust did not *have* business in caves. The Trust had maps, and the maps said there was nothing here but sheer rock and six fathoms of sea.

Unless the maps were part of the point.

The headlamp flickered—battery dying, or the cold—and in the stutter of darkness Ada heard it: a distant, rhythmic sound from deeper in. Soft. Regular. Like water dripping into a pool. Or like footsteps, far off, unhurried, someone else down here in the dark who had every right to be and no reason to expect company.

Ada killed the lamp entirely and stood blind in the absolute black, heart slamming, listening.

The sound continued. Drip. Drip. Drip. Patient as tide.

Far behind, muffled through the turn of the passage, came a longer, lower note—the sea, returning, finding its voice in the entrance chamber. Ada checked their watch by feel and did the arithmetic and did not like the answer. The window was closing faster than it had opened. Anomalous tides, their mother had written in a margin once, are debts the sea collects suddenly.

Ada turned back the way they'd come, moving fast now, and the beam steadied, and the toll-marks slid past in the light like witnesses saying nothing. At the entrance chamber the incoming water was already sheeting across the low rocks outside, silver in the last daylight, and Ada waded out thigh-deep, gasping at the cold, and climbed, and reached the wrack line, and turned to look back.

The fissure in the cliff was already swallowing the sea, black water pouring into the cave mouth with a sound like a throat clearing. In twenty minutes there would be no cave. In twenty minutes there would be nothing but the survey's sheer rock and six honest fathoms, exactly as the maps promised.

Except Ada had seen the marks. Had held the lantern. Had heard the dripping in the dark that might have been water and might not.

They stood on the rising beach, soaked to the waist, shaking—with cold, mostly—and understood that the restless thing that had lived in their chest for eleven weeks had finally found a door and put its shoulder against it.

Because there was one more thing, the thing they'd almost missed in the failing light, the thing now replaying behind their eyes with terrible clarity: the boot prints inside the cave had not all been old. One set, the smaller one, the cautious one, had been pressed into swept-clean sand that no tide had touched in years.

And the tread pattern—Ada knew it, had bought the same boots at the chandlery in September because they were the only ones in stock—

was their mother's.

The sea came in, and covered the door, and kept its own hours. Ada stood in the darkening and watched it, and did not move for a long time.
